## Abstract

## Background
Noncommunicable diseases are closely linked to health inequalities and disproportionately affect socially vulnerable populations, leading to higher morbidity and mortality rates as well as poorer health outcomes. In alignment with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) on health and well-being, the health situation in the Brazilian Amazon stands out due to the region’s numerous vulnerabilities. In this context, educating primary healthcare professionals has emerged as a key strategy for promoting a more integrated approach that includes addressing social determinants of health and tackling health inequities. This study explores training processes for the dissemination of the SDGs among primary healthcare professionals.

## Methodology
This qualitative study is guided by the social theory of situated learning. Individual online interviews were conducted with 25 out of 116 health professionals working in primary healthcare settings in four states in the Brazilian Amazon region. They were part of a public initiative to internalise the SDGs in the Amazon (TED no. 57/2021 between the Ministry of Health and UFAC). In addition, technical reports written by participants regarding their professionals’ interventions were explored. The data were analysed via thematic analysis.

## Results
A total of 25 interviews were conducted, and four themes emerged from the thematic analysis: (1) Convergence of Agendas, (2) Situated Learning in Coparticipation, (3) Faces of Vulnerability, and (4) Dynamics of Political Influence. The course participants developed intervention projects, focusing mainly on targets 3.8 and 3.4 of SDG 3 and on synergies with SDGs 2, 5, 8, and 16. The professionals demonstrated a multidimensional view of vulnerability, described as complex and related to the lack of access to resources and services, manifesting in health services as work overload. The municipal political context was critical, cited both as an obstacle and as a success factor in the actions.

## Conclusion
The results highlight the importance of permanent health training in the implementation of the SDGs in contexts of great vulnerability and inequality, such as the Brazilian Amazon, showing that the permeability of these actions faces obstacles such as a lack of infrastructure and political influence.

## Background
The progress made with the Millennium Development Goals, which focused mainly on reducing inequalities between countries, fighting poverty and promoting development, established a basis for advancing the post-2015 Agenda. The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) aim not only to reduce inequalities between countries but also within them, committing to ‘leave no one behind‘ [ , ].
The 17 SDGs seek to promote transformative actions in favour of sustainable development, characterised by their integration, indivisibility and comprehensiveness in economic, social and environmental dimensions [ , ]. In this context, monitoring inequities became a priority in health after 2015, as spearheaded by SDG 3, which was aimed at “Ensuring healthy lives and promoting well-being for all at all ages”. However, health targets also permeate other goals on the agenda, covering diverse topics such as nutrition, sanitation and air and water pollution control [ ].
Despite the ambition and global dimension, a political strategy has been adopted whereby it is the responsibility of each country’s government to determine priorities, governance structures, monitoring of results and forms of financing so that policies can be adapted to ensure that progress on the SDGs is taking place in a way that promotes equity [ , ].
Brazil is a middle-income country with high levels of income inequality [ ] and regional inequalities [ ]. Although there have been improvements in health outcomes since 1990, these improvements have not sufficed to eliminate health inequalities, which are generally greater in the states of North and Northeast than in South and Southeast [ ]. Located in northern Brazil, within a unique socioenvironmental context, the Amazon region is one of the Brazilian regions that suffers the most from health vulnerability [ ].
The Brazilian Amazon covers an area of 5.1 million km², accounting for 60% of the national territory [ ]. However, its vast expanse contrasts with a low population density, housing only 13.7% of the Brazilian population [ ]. Created as a political concept aimed at regional planning and development, the Amazon has been subdivided into the Eastern Amazon and the Western Amazon. The Eastern Amazon comprises the states of Pará, Mato Grosso, Amapá, Tocantins, and part of Maranhão, while the Western Amazon includes the states of Amazonas, Acre, Rondônia, and Roraima. The latter shares borders with Bolivia, Colombia, Peru, the Republic of Guyana, and Venezuela [ ]. Despite the specific characteristics of each state, the Amazon region faces similar structural challenges. Life expectancy in the Brazilian Amazon was 76 years in 2023, a value below the national average. Access to healthcare is one of the main challenges, characterized by a low density of medical facilities and professionals per capita. In 2009, the average distance between a municipal seat and a healthcare facility offering emergency services under the Unified Health System (SUS) was 17 km, decreasing only slightly to 15 km in 2019 [ ]. In the rest of the country, this average distance was 55% shorter, approximately 10 km. Additionally, premature mortality due to non-communicable chronic diseases in the Brazilian Amazon increased by 10.8% between 2013 and 2023, rising from 237 to 262 premature deaths per 100,000 inhabitants in the 30-to-69-year age group [ , ].
The reduction of health inequities is one of the central challenges for achieving the 2030 Agenda in Brazil. In this context, the Unified Health System (UHS) plays a fundamental role in ensuring universal and free access to health services, aligning with the principle of equity advocated by the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Created by the 1988 Federal Constitution and supported by legal frameworks such as Law 8.080/1990 and the National Primary Health Care Policy, UHS is organized in a decentralized manner, with participation from different levels of government and a strong emphasis on Primary Health Care (PHC), which serves as the main entry point to the system [ ].
Among the strategies adopted to expand coverage and reduce geographic and socioeconomic barriers to healthcare access, the Family Health Strategy (FHS) stands out. Through multidisciplinary teams, it focuses on prevention and continuous follow-up of the most vulnerable populations. However, persistent challenges remain, such as underfunding, a shortage of qualified professionals in remote areas, and regional inequalities in resource distribution. Studies indicate that despite significant progress in recent decades—including expanded vaccination coverage and responses to public health emergencies, such as the COVID-19 pandemic strengthening PHC and improving SUS governance are essential to ensuring the effectiveness of health policies and reducing inequalities in the country [ , ].
The 2030 Agenda, especially through SDG 3, reinforces the importance of promoting conditions that reduce health vulnerabilities. These vulnerabilities involve various levels and forms of susceptibility. They may result in suffering, illness, or even mortality. This condition is shaped by a set of social and individual factors, which influence both people’s relationship with the problem and their access to the resources needed to address it [ ].
Among the health problems most impacted by conditions of vulnerability are chronic noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), a group of illnesses that can affect individuals throughout their lives and are associated with genetic, physiological, environmental, and behavioural factors. NCDs include hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular diseases, and various types of cancer. These conditions have become increasingly prevalent worldwide, especially in developing countries. In Brazil, national studies indicate that NCDs are responsible for more than 70% of deaths and account for a significant proportion of years lost due to disability and reduced quality of life [ , ].
NCDs predominantly affect socially vulnerable populations, resulting in increased morbidity and mortality and unfavourable health indicators. Identifying and preventing risk factors is therefore essential for addressing NCDs in low- and middle-income countries, facilitating the planning and effective targeting of interventions [ ].
One of the ways to promote the 2030 Agenda in the field of health in Brazil is to decentralise credits between public administration bodies and entities to promote the execution of programmes, projects or activities included in the budget of the decentralising unit [ ]. One of these programmes at the national level is the permanent education process for primary healthcare professionals, who are key players in the success of health goals [ , ].
Permanent Education in Health and Continuing Education are distinct concepts, although often confused. Permanent Education in Health, which emerged in the 1980s and was widely disseminated by PAHO, seeks not only the updating of knowledge but also a critical reflection on professional practice and the work process, aiming to promote significant and lasting changes. On the other hand, Continuing Education is more associated with the sequential and cumulative acquisition of technical-scientific knowledge, often in formal formats such as postgraduate courses. While Continuing Education focuses on technical updating, permanent Education involves continuous and reflective training, integrated into the daily practice of professionals, with an emphasis on transforming health practices [ , ].
Permanent education for health professionals provides space for reflection on practice, promoting transformative and participatory interventions in the workplace. This approach can be an essential tool for strengthening the dissemination of the SDGs among primary care professionals [ ]. When applied in a meaningful way, permanent health education becomes a valuable resource for identifying local needs, adjusting interventions and ensuring that professionals are prepared to address the specificities of primary care [ , ]. This study seeks to contribute to understanding how professional training can strengthen the implementation of the SDGs, training professionals to face the specific challenges of this region and promoting more inclusive and equitable health practices.

## Methodology

## Study design, setting and sampling
We conducted a qualitative study with purposeful sampling to investigate the processes of permanent training for implementing the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) among primary healthcare professionals in the Brazilian Western Amazon. This study was part of a Permanent Health Education (PHE) programme established through an agreement between the Federal University of Acre and the Ministry of Health (TED No. 57/2021). The PHE project proposed the support of universities in raising awareness, organising, strengthening and developing strategies for the internalisation of the 2030 Agenda. The permanent health education programme initially involved 116 participants from the states of the Western Amazon (Acre, Amazonas, Rondônia and Roraima) and had a total workload of 80 h in hybrid mode. Among the proposed activities, professionals were instructed in developing health intervention actions adapted to the context of their municipalities. Professionals who agreed to take part in this study (n = 25), regardless of whether they had completed the full course load, had their contributions included, as noncompletion could also reflect the difficulties in disseminating the SDGs in the Amazon region. ## Data collection
Data were collected through interviews with the participants and document analysis of the technical reports on the PHE programme provided by the Ministry of Health. The interviews were conducted individually online, which is a strategic choice to overcome the challenges of mobility in the Amazon region. A guide with guiding questions for the interviews and a script for analysing the technical reports was developed based on the Guidelines and Instruments for Monitoring and Evaluating the National Policy for Permanent Education in Health [ ]. The interviews followed a conversational format, in which the guide served as a mental framework for the interviewer, allowing the questions to be adapted according to the context and progress of the conversation without a rigid sequence [ ]. All the interviews were recorded and transcribed via ‘TL; DV’ software and the transcripts were reviewed by the team to ensure their reliability. The interviews took place between October 2023 and February 2024 and lasted between 25 and 30 min. To ensure consistency in the procedures and the quality of the data, the interviewers received training, which included handling equipment, probing techniques and reviewing the guiding questions. The documentary analysis of the technical reports from each state was carried out in two stages: a critical analysis, with an exhaustive reading of the sources, and an analysis of the key points extracted from the Guidelines and Instruments for Monitoring and Evaluating the National Policy for Permanent Education in Health [ , ].

## Data analysis
The study used the thematic analysis technique to examine the data via a retroductive coding scheme that brought together reflections and interpretations based on the theoretical framework and the contextual aspects of the research scenario. The stages proposed by Clarke & Braun [ ] and the systematic process proposed by Green et al. [ ] were followed in the operationalisation of the analysis, with the stages of immersion in the data, generation of initial codes, search and review of themes, naming and production of the report. Coding and analysis were conducted simultaneously during data collection. The process of immersion in the data and analysis was guided by the social theory of situated learning [ – ], providing an interpretation of the learning aspects incorporated into everyday activities in the context of health services. All the transcripts were read and reread two or more times for an in-depth understanding of the data, with observational notes and occasional comments.
The data were coded via a combination of sentence and paragraph units, according to the participants’ responses, and the support software used made it easy to review the context of the statements whenever necessary. For saturation, no new codes emerged from the 21st interview onwards, although each new piece of information contributed to revealing the complexity of the reality studied [ ], so all the data were coded to ensure that the opinions of all the participants were included. ‘Atlas.ti.24’ software was used to organise the data; manage the documentary analysis, the transcripts and the codes generated from the analyses; and help visualise the data via relationship diagrams.

## Ethical considerations
The study was approved by the ethics committee of the Federal University of Acre. CAAE: 76675223.3.1001.5010. Informed consent was obtained from all health professionals who participated in the study. To preserve the privacy and anonymity of the participants, each transcript was given a unique identification number linked to a separate file that held the corresponding identification information. The text fragments of the interviews presented in the results were subtitled by a code consisting of a numeral, followed by the acronym of the participant’s state of origin.

## Results

## Profile of the study participants
Of those enrolled in the PHE programme, 25 agreed to take part in the interviews. The operationalization of the training defined its completion with the presentation of intervention actions by the participants. Among the interviewees, seven attended the in-person stage but, due to the challenges mentioned, did not complete and/or present their health intervention. However, regardless of the completion of activities, their accounts were considered, as they highlighted that the in-person stages, tutor support, and analysis of the local context for intervention planning contributed to the construction of learning related to the SDGs. This process, in turn, can impact the work environment, in line with the principles of Permanent Health Education.
The participants (6 men and 19 women) held management positions in health surveillance [ ], nurses [ ] and nursing technicians [ ]. Most of the professionals were trained nurses [ ], but at the time of the interview, they held management positions. In the PHE programme, as part of the activities, 18 health intervention actions were started, 16 had all the stages recorded in the PHE technical reports, and 2 projects had their execution cancelled due to local political issues. The greatest number of health interventions involved topics such as hypertension, diabetes and mental health (Table  ).
 The results of the study are presented in 4 main themes (Fig.  ).
 

## Theme 1: “I thought nobody saw these diseases”: convergence of agendas
The purpose of the training programme was to establish a relationship between the SDGs, NCDs and the local health context, with interventions based on concrete data and the local needs of the municipalities. The participants reported that even though they worked with surveillance and care related to NCDs, the SDGs were perceived as distant concepts.
“Before, we used to talk about the SDGs, but very far from the ministry’s manuals, we did not have the practice of truly correlating them with the day-to-day process.” 01AC.
“First, it took away that thought I had that nobody sees these diseases, the SUS [National Health System] policy does not see these patients, it does not see the hypertensive, it does not see the diabetic, that patient who is chronic and needs the service. This made me realise that there is still a real focus on the health/disease process within your municipality, within the service you provide.” 02RO.
The convergence between the global agenda and the national agenda in the intervention projects is demonstrated in the network of relationships (Fig.  ). The projects are integrated into different public health themes and linked to multiple SDG targets, the main approach of which is within SDG 3 (health and well-being) and its specific targets, such as Target 3.4 (reduction of mortality from noncommunicable diseases), Target 3.8 (universal health coverage), Target 3.d (reduction of health risks) and links with targets related to violence prevention, mental health promotion and immunisation.
 The synergy relationship refers to the direct and indirect relationships between the projects and the goals of the 2030 Agenda. Synergistic relationships were found with SDGs 2, 4, 5, 8, 10, 11, 16 and 17, indicating that public health interventions generated cross-cutting effects on various sustainable development goals. The interviewees referred to the perception of synergy in the development of the actions.
“When you do a project like this, we have to open our minds; if we’re going to talk about hypertension and diabetes, it is not just hypertension and diabetes. It is knowing if that individual has housing conditions, if that person has some level of education.” 01AC.
Owing to the convergence between the agendas devised since the creation of the national agenda, the interventions also covered the three groups of targets of the NCD plan, which include targets for NCDs, accidents, violence and their risk factors.

## Theme 2: “because there’s no school better than experience!“: learning based on coparticipation
The interviewees had a positive response to the learning process during the course, with many reporting that they did not know the SDGs until the PHE project was carried out. They emphasised that the course was especially useful in planning community intervention projects, an area in which they had previously faced difficulties.
“Mainly in terms of doing the project. Building the project and putting the project on paper, because I had that difficulty. So for that, for me, it is very important.” 03RO.
The main points raised about learning were the support offered by the trainers, teamwork and practical implementation of the interventions. The interaction between the professionals took place in small groups, with an average of four course participants. However, the involvement of other professionals not enrolled in the course, such as Community Health Workers (CHWs) who collaborated in designing the interventions, was also noted.
“I never thought on my own. I spoke to the CHWs and proposed working with crops and vegetables. However, as they know the clients better, they said, ‘No, that will not work here, we’d better think of another proposal’. Therefore, we adjusted to their reality.” 02RR.
One of the proposals of the PHE programme was for the course participants to become multipliers of this knowledge. The participants felt ready to share the practices they learned, and the creation of new projects in other sectors was already being considered. However, the high demand for work was cited as a factor that hindered this multiplication and interfered with the frequency of participation in the course.

## Theme 3: “this is just the tip of the iceberg”: faces of vulnerability
When asked about the concept of vulnerability, the interviewees presented a multidimensional view, describing it as a complex condition that manifests itself in various ways in society. According to them, vulnerability is associated with a lack of guarantee of basic rights to welfare, social and economic needs, social exclusion and difficulties in accessing services such as education and health. It also involves unfavourable environmental conditions and risk situations.
The professionals highlighted various conditions of social and environmental disadvantage that affected their intervention actions. One of the main challenges reported was the instability of the internet connection, making it difficult to access distance learning. Many professionals mentioned that the connection in their municipalities is often unstable, and this technical limitation was one of the reasons why some participants expressed a preference for exclusively face-to-face PHE. Difficulties with the internet were widespread. In one of the situations reported technical problems during a presentation required a venue change, resulting in an improvised environment for the activity and jeopardising essential aspects of learning, such as communication, quality and presentation time.
“If you have a good internet connection, you could watch the course on the phone. The lack of internet had a big impact on my participation and that of my colleagues. On the day of our first project presentation, we had to go to a rented building. When we got there, the power went out. The computer was dead, and it was crazy.” 04AM.
In addition to the technical limitations, the interviewees pointed to the accumulation of functions and demands caused by the shortage of health professionals, which prevented them from dedicating themselves fully to the online training activities during the course. These conditions were also linked by participants to poorer performance in prevention indicators. As an example, in the ‘Previne Brasil’ programme, it was difficult to adequately monitor NCDs and adhere to protocols by age group in regions with a high incidence of these diseases.
Dropouts, among other challenges in PHE training during the course, were often attributed to contextual issues, such as socioeconomic characteristics. For most of the participants, for example, the salary they received was lower than the national salary floor of 4,750 reais. According to the nurses interviewed, the salary was less than 50 per cent of the amount guaranteed by law:
“Nurses here earn 2,200 reais [380$]. We know it’s very little; it’s very little for someone with responsibilities, a family, and a lot of work. However, we endure because of our dedication to the profession.” 07RO.
In addition, as all the states in the Western Amazon have a large international border region with neighbouring countries (Peru, Bolivia, Colombia and Venezuela), the issue of immigration was cited as an aggravating factor in existing vulnerabilities, owing to the low coverage of public policies in Brazilian municipalities in this region. Limited resources and infrastructure have become even more restricted due to the increase in the number of immigrants, which has overloaded the health system. Although there are diplomatic reception operations, these initiatives have not been permeable enough to meet the needs of these people or adapt the local planning of the corresponding public policies. In the intervention actions carried out by the study participants, even though immigrants were covered, monitoring actions were limited given the housing instability and constant mobility of these populations, among other limitations.
“Each project, in its way, impacts with the issue of vulnerability. As we have limited financial resources, we work with education, guidance and the dissemination of knowledge. I believe that all the groups have this potential.” 01RO.
Vulnerability in the health system is also reflected in the overload of specialised units, such as mental health units, that can result in critical situations:
“When we discussed the issue of domestic violence in one unit, the nurse showed us a pile of cases in the queue. There were battered women, battered children, and people who had attempted suicide, all waiting to be seen by a single psychologist and a therapist. Patients had been waiting for months. The lack of services makes people vulnerable.” 02RO.
The conditions of vulnerability experienced by the communities served permeated the professionals’ speeches. Many intervention projects have addressed the prevention and management of NCDs, carrying out actions to encourage healthy eating; however, these initiatives often come up against the reality of food insecurity for a public that, in some cases, lived in a situation of hunger. When asked about the possibility of extending the knowledge acquired in the project to other aspects of the social vulnerability that affects these communities, the interviewees emphasised the need to expand these initiatives to other groups and regions:
“This is just the tip of the iceberg. There is much to explore. Even this project, we’re only implementing in one team, but it needs to expand to others.” 03AC.
In addition to social and structural issues, environmental factors also pose challenges. During the period of training and carrying out the intervention actions, some municipalities experienced severe periods of drought, which made it difficult for professionals to move around (in this case, by the river) and access the PHE, and in some cases, the intervention actions had to be adapted to address these limitations. In some regions, difficulties in accessing communities in inland villages led to the use of alternative strategies, such as radio broadcasts, to reach the local population.

## Theme 4: “it depends on the political return”: the dynamics of political influence
The policy significantly influenced the intervention actions and the PHE training process, both by providing support and by presenting challenges. The involvement and commitment of public managers, such as health secretaries and mayors, were decisive for the continuity and success of the actions. An example of this is the municipality in the interior of Roraima, where owing to political issues involving the mayor and commissioned positions, the participants were prevented from continuing with the intervention project despite having completed the first stage of training:
“We had some management problems here in the municipality, which caused some uncertainties about these management issues. So I drew up a project, but we have not managed to implement it yet because of these issues.” 01RR.
In the view of the participants, the importance that some managers attach to projects is often linked to the political benefits they can obtain, such as building up an electoral base. One of the participants noted that if the project does not have a political or financial counterpart, support tends to be limited:
“If the project is something that is not going to bring financial benefits to the municipality, it is difficult to get support. Not that there is not, but it is hard to get that full support.” 07AM.
Direct communication between course participants, health secretaries and mayors was a crucial strategy for guaranteeing the support needed to carry out some actions. In many cases, mayors offered logistical and financial support, recognising the importance of the projects for the community. One example was given by a course participant:
“I invited the secretary and he took part so that he would have an idea because we were going to do some actions that needed logistical support, so they had to be aware of it and we had no problem with that. He was very collaborative and took part in both actions.” 05RR.
Despite political support in some cases, frequent changes in management and high turnover of positions, as seen in municipalities in the interior of Rondônia, have had a negative impact on the progress of interventions. The lack of continuity in administrations has led to important initiatives being interrupted or abandoned. In addition, “politicking” (political action based on particular interests to the detriment of collective interests) was mentioned as an obstacle that prevented the full application of the knowledge acquired during training. Frustration is expressed as follows:
“Here, in the municipality, there’s a lot of ‘politicking’, and this politicking ends up making a wall for us to be able to apply what we have learned, but whenever I’m given a break I go there and apply it, my boss always says ‘let us go slowly’, I said, well if I can do my best in my job, I’m not going to do my worst.” 07RO.
During the conversation, the interviewees noted that, in their perception, public managers with an academic background in health recognised the importance of the intervention projects and provided all the necessary support. He emphasised the difference in the commitment of these managers compared with others.
“Our health secretary and the mayor are nurses, so they understand the importance of caring for those who care, and this has ensured significant support for us.” 01AM.
Overall, local politics strongly influenced the progress and execution of the intervention projects. When managers were aligned with the needs of the communities and committed to the objectives of the training, progress was visible and successful. However, in scenarios where politics interferes negatively, many projects face significant barriers, reinforcing the importance of consistent political support aligned with the population’s health needs.

## Discussion
The partnership between higher education institutions and health services, utilized in the training program, is a strategy for strengthening the Brazilian health system. This articulation is outlined in the National Policy on Permanent Education and highlights the importance of integrating education, service, and community. This cooperation contributes to the implementation of public policies, the expansion of equitable access to healthcare, and the training of critical, autonomous, and socially committed professionals [ , ].
Realising the progress required by the SDGs and the national agenda through the NCD plan requires effective action on a local scale. However, theme 1 points to the lack of in-depth knowledge and the distance between agendas and health services. These reports reveal worrying fragmentation, as it limits implementation and isolates local actors. When responsibility and experience are fragmented, local participation, which should be the guiding principle, is weakened [ ].
The perception of the participants that NCDs are not properly addressed in health policies is in line with the analysis of the Voluntary National Reports (VNRs) presented at the High-Level Political Forum on Sustainable Development, which revealed that the citations of indicators referring to NCDs in the VNRs increased proportionally with the income classification of the states [ ]. This means that in low- and middle-income countries, such as Brazil, citations are less frequent than they are in high-income countries. This pattern can be attributed to a combination of factors, such as the limited technical capacity to monitor a broader set of indicators and the prioritisation of more urgent issues, such as infectious diseases and maternal and child health, which historically demand more attention in the health systems of these countries [ , ].
In addition, low- and middle-income countries face a double burden of disease, with challenges related to both infectious and noncommunicable diseases. This reality, coupled with resource and infrastructure limitations, tends to reduce the focus on NCDs in national reports. This may reflect selective prioritisation, driven by local urgencies, but may contribute to the lack of a more integrated and comprehensive approach to the prevention and control of these diseases [ ].
A brief analysis of the latest Brazilian VNR and the Luz Report, which is organised by national civil organisations, shows that of the thirteen targets of SDG 3, only target 3.1 (reducing maternal mortality) showed satisfactory results [ , ]. Target 3.4, which aims to reduce premature deaths from NCDs by one-third, was classified as ‘falling behind’, with an increase of 1.9% compared with 2016, reaching 371/100,000 inhabitants by 2022 [ ]. The NRW indicates that some targets, including 3.4, have also been affected by the COVID-19 pandemic, which has worsened the NCD scenario [ , ].
Given the sense of distance and invisibility reported by the interviewees about the SDGs and actions aimed at NCDs, the PHE project stands out as a factor that minimises this perception by bringing the agendas closer to the daily practice of the health service. During the PHE, the activity of drawing up intervention projects allowed local actors to choose indicators according to the specific realities of each municipality. This contributed to building systemic thinking, which may have had a positive influence on the various synergies found between the SDGs.
Methodologies that favour systems thinking improve the decision-making process, especially in the face of complex and interconnected challenges that need to be addressed with locally available resources [ , ]. The use of systems thinking, as highlighted in the study by Tan et al. (2019) [ ], reinforces the importance of a place-based approach, where the coproduction of case studies allows local actors to tackle complexities, develop new understandings and strengthen interventions aimed at achieving the SDG targets.
A central feature of the systems approach is coproduction between stakeholders [ ], which can be observed in theme 2, where learning takes place in the dynamic interaction between activities, context and actors involved in the PHE. According to Lave and Weger, learning is situated in the form of coparticipation, and the participants’ positive perception of their experiences suggests that the PHE offered a favourable context for learning [ , ]. This reinforces the fundamental role of academic institutions in implementing the SDGs, promoting the collaborative generation of knowledge and facilitating dialogue between multiple actors, such as the government, the private sector, the academic and scientific community, civil societies and the general public [ ].
Under this theoretical lens, the skills acquired through the learning process are highly interactive due to the productive process [ ]. In this sense, the participants reported that, through the activities developed in the PHE, they were able to “build a project” or even “know which paths to take” to work with the SDGs in conjunction with NCDs. Therefore, the professional acquired the ability to execute by actually becoming involved in the process, something that situated learning theory (SLT) calls Legitimate Peripheral Participation.
The learning process takes place within a framework of participation [ ]. In the Amazon region, the involvement of CHWs, as mentioned in theme 2, is essential, as they are the main link between the community and medical services, especially in river-based family health teams, which serve populations intermittently [ ]. These professionals are close to the community daily, organise medical appointments and facilitate communication between the residents and health services. This means that learning was mediated from the perspective of the coparticipants, including the work teams, even if they did not participate directly in the PHE. Although the learner (the course participant) is the one who is most transformed by increased participation in the process, learning is shared between all those involved and is a collective effort and not an isolated action [ ].
One of the aims of the PHE was to disseminate the knowledge built up, with the course participants acting as “multipliers”, which, according to the SLT, forms a community of practice. In this community, practices become an integral part of a group that develops over time through the continuous pursuit of a shared goal. These communities in action can add substantial value to an organisation, helping it drive strategy, introduce new service lines, solve problems quickly and transfer best practices [ , ].
The formation of communities of practice can be jeopardised when participants do not feel confident in their ability to act as multipliers or when they face contextual barriers that make it difficult to disseminate practices. Theme 3 provides insight into the different types of vulnerabilities experienced by participants and the local community, which can limit the impact of these practices. The expression ‘tip of the iceberg’, mentioned during the conversation with the interviewee, refers to the potential of the health practices implemented. However, the vulnerabilities observed are so vast and complex that the findings also seem to represent only the surface of an even greater reality.
Health vulnerability, especially in its programmatic aspects, stands out as the main limiting factor for the full development of PHE, the spread of the SDGs and national programs such as ‘Previne Brasil’. In the participants’ discourses, this programmatic vulnerability is evident, reflected, among other aspects, by the precarious internet connection and lack of professionals. Programmatic vulnerability reflects the ineffectiveness of policies, programs and infrastructure, which directly affect the ability to provide adequate conditions for development and well-being in health services [ , ]. Internet connection problems in the workplace compromise access to information, resources, essential tools and health training processes in the Western Amazon region [ ]. Poor connection quality, limited coverage and high prices characterise internet access in the northern region of Brazil. These problems result from the lack of adequate infrastructure, which requires significant investment, making it a low-cost initiative for the private sector and consequently affecting the provision of telecommunications services in the region [ ].
Nursing professionals, including technicians and assistants, make up the largest health workforce in Brazil, but the country faces challenges such as inequity in the distribution of these professionals. The national average number of nursing professionals is 13.5 per 1,000 inhabitants, but the Amazon states are below this average [ , ], and the same phenomenon can be observed with the number of doctors, where the Western Amazon states of Acre and Amazonas have the lowest densities in the country [ ].
The low density of professionals in the Amazon region is influenced by various factors, such as great geographical distances and difficult access to remote areas, which make travel time-consuming and costly. In addition, the precariousness and inadequacy of the infrastructure in health centres discourage professionals from staying [ ]. The low pay of nurses and other professionals also contributes to this lack of motivation to work in the region, limiting their ability to take part in professional training and dedicate themselves fully to health interventions. The WHO, through evidence-based recommendations, noted that a combination of salaries and sustainable financial incentives can be effective in attracting and retaining professionals in rural and remote areas [ ].
The conditions reported by the interviewees about the communities they serve show that health inequities reflect the inequities present in society [ ]. In the Amazon basin, these disparities are especially marked because of the region’s unique sociospatial dynamics. A clear example is the health care of riverside populations, which are systematically disregarded in public health policies, even with the need to address access barriers and population distribution challenges in a differentiated territory [ , ].
Other studies carried out in the region have shown that health services face similar challenges to those observed during PHE, such as accessibility [ – ]. Other studies also report socioeconomic conditions, such as income and schooling [ – ], or challenges particular to the region, such as communication with indigenous communities [ ]. These factors reveal the complexity of the social determinants of health, which directly affect the quality and scope of the services offered.
Because it is a border region, the Western Amazon also faces challenges arising from migration processes. Although migration flows in Brazil are diverse and not generalizable, the last decade has seen an increase in migratory volume, with a greater presence of people from the Southern Hemisphere [ ]. Since 2015, Venezuelan migration has intensified due to the severe humanitarian crisis in their country, particularly in Roraima [ ]. This phenomenon presents specific challenges, such as linguistic and cultural barriers, which hinder access to basic services, including healthcare. Immigrants, like the Warao Indigenous people, who do not speak Portuguese, face serious communication difficulties with healthcare professionals, further exacerbating their vulnerability. The shortage of community interpreters and the lack of preparedness among healthcare professionals to handle the cultural and linguistic diversity of these groups contribute to their marginalization and exclusion [ ].
Brazilian legislation, such as the Refugee Statute and the Migration Law (13.445/2017), along with international treaties, guarantees fundamental rights to migrants, including access to healthcare [ ]. However, the implementation of these laws faces significant obstacles, such as a lack of infrastructure and adequate resources. Adequate attention to the needs of migrant people, particularly regarding access to healthcare services, requires a commitment from public authorities and an approach that is sensitive to the cultural needs of these groups [ – ].
A broader understanding of health vulnerabilities in the Amazon region can be seen through the perspective of the model proposed by Dahlgren and Whitehead (1991), which presents a multi-layered approach to understanding the social determinants of health. The model suggests that health is not merely a reflection of individual factors, such as behaviour and genetics, but is also shaped by broader conditions, including education, work environment, living conditions, and public policies [ , ].
In the case of the Amazon, factors such as unequal access to healthcare services, a shortage of qualified professionals, inadequate infrastructure, and the specific socioeconomic conditions of riverine and Indigenous communities exemplify the various layers of determinants that directly impact the health of the local population. These often interconnected determinants create a cycle of vulnerability that reinforces the health disparities observed in the region, highlighting the need for more robust intersectoral actions to promote equity.
The circumstances of the social determinants of health are shaped by the distribution of money, power and resources at the global, national and local levels, which are influenced by political choices [ ]. The political context shown in theme 4 reveals that political instability and “politicking” were mentioned as barriers that prevented important projects from continuing in the local health context, showing that the success of health interventions is often linked to the opportunism of electoral interests.
Considering the multiple layers that affect health provides an analytical lens for public policies aimed at mitigating the adverse effects of these inequalities. Firstly, interventions should address behavioural and lifestyle factors, recognizing that risk behaviours are often shaped by deeply rooted cultural norms. Changing these behaviours requires more than isolated actions targeted at individuals; it demands population-wide policies that create and sustain conditions for communities, especially the most vulnerable, to take an active role in their improvements [ ].
The observed political dynamics show that while social determinants are fundamental to understanding health inequities, it is also necessary to consider political determinants. These factors create structural conditions and social drivers that shape the health of populations. It is no coincidence that certain communities have higher rates of premature mortality or that women from some populations face a higher risk of pregnancy-related death [ ]. Factors such as poverty, high job turnover, inadequate transport, food insecurity, medical deserts and other community attributes associated with social vulnerabilities expose these populations to an increased risk of such adverse outcomes [ , ].

## Limitations of the study
As this is a qualitative study, it has the inherent limitations of this method, characterised by its particularistic nature, which makes it difficult to generalise to a wider set of conditions beyond those observed in the study itself. However, the knowledge generated may have conceptual implications that go beyond the data collected and may help to reflect on peculiar aspects of the implementation of the SDGs in other remote regions of the world. The study focused on the contributions perceived by the participants in the short term, but we recognise that assessing the implications for concrete changes resulting from permanent health education actions is also essential in the medium and long term.

## Conclusion
The results of the study highlight the strategic importance of PHE for the internalisation of the SDGs in the context of the Brazilian Western Amazon. PHE has proven to be a potential means of sensitising and training Primary Health Care professionals to implement actions that promote health inclusively and sustainably. Despite structural barriers such as a lack of resources, limited internet access and high workloads, the professionals showed commitment and adapted the practices learned to local realities, reinforcing their capacity for resilience and innovation in the face of regional challenges. The active participation of the course attendees in the creation of intervention projects aligned with the SDGs proved to be effective, broadening their understanding and application of global and national health agendas, the formation of communities of practice, and promising ways to strengthen local capacities. In addition, political support and institutional stability emerged as crucial factors for the continuity and success of interventions. Thus, public policies and strategies for health professional training must consider not only the technical aspects but also the local sociopolitical conditions that can facilitate or hinder the implementation of health practices. It is essential to guarantee adequate working conditions and institutional support so that health professionals can continue to train and implement interventions that converge with priority health agendas for human development, such as the 2030 Agenda.
