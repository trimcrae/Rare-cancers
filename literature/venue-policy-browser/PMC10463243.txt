VRIC attracts vascular clinicians, surgeon-scientists, and basic science researchers to a common arena to facilitate the exchange of basic and translational science to stimulate and inspire participants to study and find solutions to vascular conditions. The theme of the conference this year was “Structural and Immune Cells in Vascular Disease.” Abstract sessions focused on venous disease, vascular regeneration, stem cells and wound healing, aortopathies, and the role of the immune system in atherosclerosis. A moderated translational science panel included talks from Dr Chiara Giannarelli and Dr Bhama Ramkhelawon. Recipients of Society for Vascular Surgery partner grants and National Institutes of Health K08 awardees presented their progress reports, and the Resident Research and VRIC Trainee Awardees were acknowledged. The Alexander W. Clowes Distinguished Lecture, entitled “Co-stimulatory Immune Checkpoints in Atherosclerosis: Novel Immunotherapeutic Targets to Combat Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease” was given by Dr Ester Lutgens. VRIC continues to foster interdisciplinary collaborations across the translational field of vascular disease.

The Vascular Research Initiative Conference (VRIC), previously known as the Research Initiatives in Vascular Disease Conference, is an annual conference organized by the Society of Vascular Surgery (SVS). Since its inception in 1986, this conference has successfully brought together some of the greatest minds in vascular research to provide a forum for discussion of high quality, relevant, and innovative research. , This year set a record for in-person attendance, with 135 individuals present from across the world. VRIC offers an opportunity for vascular surgeon-scientists, including trainees and junior faculty, as well as more experienced researchers, a venue to showcase their research. This year’s conference focused on “Structural and Immune Cells in Vascular Disease.”
Since 2009, VRIC has coincided with the American Heart Association’s (AHA’s) Vascular Discovery Conference, a partnership that has paved the way for the development of interdisciplinary collaborations focused on solving specific issues in vascular surgery research. Evidence of the importance of this partnership continues to be present, with abstracts being presented at both VRIC and Vascular Discovery sessions, either as podium presentations or as a part of the poster sessions. There was an impressive showing of both SVS and AHA members throughout each conference session.

## Scientific sessions
The VRIC scientific sessions are designed to facilitate interactions between attendees and presenters to foster the evolution of vascular research. Each session was moderated by a team of two vascular surgeon-scientists representing the SVS and Arteriosclerosis, Thrombosis, and Vascular Biology (ATVB) program committees, which included Luke Packard Brewster, MD, PhD, from Emory University School of Medicine; Andrea Obi, MD, from the University of Michigan; Ulf Hedin, MD, PhD, from Karolinska Institutet; Kevin Southerland, MD, from Duke University School of Medicine; Kathryn Howe, MD, PhD, from the University of Toronto; Nicholas Leeper, MD, from Stanford University; Bhama Ramkhelawon, PhD, from NYU Langone Medical Center; and Jean Marie Ruddy, MD, from Medical University of South Carolina.

## Venous disease and novel devices
Kiran Kumar (University of Michigan) started off the abstract presentations, presenting data that showed a role for TLR4 signaling in stimulating the resolution of venous thromboses; however, that monocyte and macrophage expression of the fibrinolytic protein urokinase occurs via a TLR4-Myd88-independent pathway, which may represent an exciting therapeutic target to augment resolution of venous thromboses. Sriganesh Sharma (University of Michigan) presented a potential therapeutic target to mitigate a known complication of venous thrombosis: post-thrombotic syndrome. Because NR4A1 is a suppressor of monocyte and macrophage activity and plays a role in epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition and tissue fibrosis, the group studied its role in post-thrombotic vein remodeling; loss of NR4A1 results in impaired vein wall remodeling following venous thrombosis, and agonism of this receptor may be of therapeutic benefit in mitigating post-thrombotic syndrome.
Having previously demonstrated the utility of cyclic peptide LXW7-coated grafts to promote endothelization of prosthetic materials via capture of endothelial cells and their progenitors, Dr Kwong’s group (University of California Davis) set out to develop its utility for in vivo application. Using bilateral iliac interposition grafts in Yorkshire pigs as their large animal model, they found a significant increase in graft patency at 6 weeks post-implant in the LXW7-coated interposition grafts compared with uncoated controls, suggesting an exciting potential for LXW7 coating as an adjunct for prosthetic grafts to increase patency in bypass applications.
Dr Bhasin’s group (Emory University) took a different approach to studying vein-graft patency, using single-nuclei and spatial transcriptomic investigations of canine cephalic vein conduits to elucidate the effect of saline distension during graft harvest. They demonstrated a genomic response among specific cellular subtypes in distinct anatomic regions with relative increases in expression of genes associated with remodeling, cell proliferation, and mesenchymal transition, supporting the hypothesis that acute injury caused by over-distension of the vein during harvest may contribute to vein graft failure.
Next-generation RNA sequencing has allowed for the study of genetic variations associated with different disease states. Using this technique, Dr Xiaochun Long’s group (Augusta University) characterized a previously undescribed metabolically active vascular smooth muscle cell phenotype consisting of proliferative and matrix-organizing qualities while still retaining contractile abilities, which becomes active in the vein wall following arteriovenous fistula (AVF) creation. The Vazquez-Padron lab (University of Miami) utilized this technology to create a comprehensive single-cell atlas of cells involved in vein transformation following AVF.
Dr Judit Csore (DeBakey Heart and Vascular Center) presented a proof-of-concept study in which artificial intelligence-assisted methods were utilized to characterize the plaque components of atherosclerotic lesions in peripheral artery disease based on high-resolution magnetic resonance imaging histology. Using a convolutional neural network, they were able to successfully distinguish plaque composition in ex vivo samples, which suggests a potential application of artificial intelligence-based technologies as an adjunct in surgical planning.

## Vascular regeneration, stem cells, and wound healing
Epigenetics was a hot topic in this year’s presentations. In the first abstract session, Dr Chris Audu (University of Michigan) discussed that derangements in the activity of plasmacytoid dendritic cells in diabetics, which exist at an epigenetic level via the histone demethylase JARID1C, may be a major contributing factor in the poor wound healing demonstrated by diabetic patients by driving the adaptive immune response toward a chronic inflammatory phenotype characterized by pathologic Th17 T-cell responses. Dr Tyler Bauer (University of Michigan) demonstrated an increase in the histone methyltransferase SETDB2 in cells from diabetic wounds, which contributes to decreased levels of col1A and periostin, which are crucial to fibroblast migration. Both presentations outlined the potential role of altered interleukin (IL)-6 expression contributing to chronic inflammation and poor wound healing.
Neointimal hyperplasia is a well-described mechanism that plays a role across a spectrum of vascular diseases. Jonathan Jung, PhD (Northwestern University) presented his data on activation of the free fatty acid receptor-3 (FFAR3) by microbe-derived butyrate, concluding that this interaction may stimulate cell cycle progression and modulate immune-related transcriptional pathways, promoting endothelial cell migration and proliferation, yet limiting neointimal hyperplasia after arterial injury. Dr Weichang Zhang (Yale University) showed that the endothelial glucocorticoid receptor, a negative regulator of vascular inflammation, may also serve as a physiologic regulator of venous remodeling following AVF creation by limiting endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition and neointimal hyperplasia.
Several groups focused on the myopathy that accompanies peripheral arterial disease. Dr Southerland’s group (Duke University) utilized single-cell RNA sequencing to identify a population of fibroadipogenic progenitor cells that are responsible for the fatty infiltration that contributes to progressive muscle dysfunction in patient with chronic limb-threatening ischemia. Dr Ulka Sachdev’s group (University of Pittsburgh Medical Center) investigated the role of the AIM2 inflammasome in this process and found that specific inhibition of the AIM2 inflammasome improves function of myoblasts in patients with peripheral artery disease (PAD) and may represent a novel therapeutic target to improve function in this population.
Biliverdin reductase B (BLVRB) was found by Dr Hedin’s group (Karolinska Institut) to be a viable biomarker for intraplaque hemorrhage in carotid atherosclerosis, which may provide prognostic information for patients with carotid disease and help guide management decision-making.

## Atherosclerosis and role of the immune system
Rodrigo Meade (Washington University) presented data showing that manipulation of fatty acid synthase, both through selective targeting with a drug and via liver-specific knockdown, improves serum lipid profiles and aortic atherosclerotic plaque deposition as well as inflammation within deposited plaques. Like their other investigations of epigenetics in vascular disease, Dr Gallagher’s group (University of Michigan) identified a single nucleotide polymorphism upstream of the gene that encodes the histone demethylase JMJD3, which causes an increase in transcription of JMJD3 and is associated with elevated blood pressure as well as an increase in contractile smooth muscle gene expression.
Next-generation RNA sequencing technology was used to identify and describe the novel human-specific long noncoding RNA, INKLIN, and its role in inflammation and vascular disease. RNA sequencing analysis of endothelial cells in culture was used by Dr Orr’s lab (Louisiana State University) to augment their in vivo studies of Nck 1, identifying this signaling adaptor as a potential target to reduce atherosclerosis and associated inflammation without inhibiting adaptive angiogenesis in ischemic tissue. Dr Yarui Diao’s group (Duke University) utilized this technology to compare cell populations between ischemic and nonischemic muscle samples and create a single-cell atlas, which will serve as a valuable resource for future studies of chronic limb-threatening ischemia.
Nanotechnology utilization was described in several promising ways, aimed at treating atherosclerosis while limiting off-target effects. Dr Vina Chhaya (University of Virginia) showcased her work with GW3965, a liver X-receptor agonist, modified with a short peptide that was noncovalently co-assembled with ApoA1-targeted peptide amphiphile nanofibers to create a specific, atherosclerotic plaque-targeted drug delivery system. She demonstrated a reduction in atherosclerotic plaque burden in male mice compared with control with markedly less hepatocyte injury than in mice systemically treated with the liver X-receptor agonist. Interestingly, she found no difference in plaque burden in female mice across treatment groups.
Sharika Bamezai (University of Michigan) used anti-atherosclerotic nanotechnology targeting CD47 in the form of macrophage-specific single-walled carbon nanotubes loaded with a chemical inhibitor of SHP-1, finding decreased vascular inflammation in the carotid arteries of the treatment group as well as several differentially expressed genes, and reduction in off-target effects associated with global CD47 inhibition. At the early time point of 3 months, only fatty streaks were observed in the major arterial beds, however, limiting the conclusions to be made about atherosclerotic plaque burden.
Dr Ali Hakim (University of Nebraska) presented a novel PAD model in Ossabaw swine that more completely recapitulates aspects of the pathophysiology of human PAD and claudication and shows promise for use in the development of new invasive and noninvasive therapies and interventions for PAD. A novel model for the tracking of extracellular vesicles in atherosclerotic disease was presented by Dr Kathryn Howe’s group (University Health Network, Toronto). In this model, transgenic mice were induced to produce green fluorescent endothelial cell-derived extracellular vesicles, which were successfully detected in circulating blood and within atherosclerotic plaques. The group plans to continue to develop this model to ultimately assess the role of extracellular vesicles in atherosclerotic plaque development, which at current is poorly characterized.

## Aortopathies
The last of the four scientific sessions focused on abdominal aortic aneurysms (AAAs) and aortic dissections. Dr Gallagher’s group (University of Michigan) presented their work investigating endoplasmic reticulum stress response in the development of AAAs, demonstrating that activation of the endoplasmic reticulum stress response is critical to regulation of vascular smooth muscle cell apoptosis in AAAs and identifying this pathway as a potential therapeutic target.
Several groups discussed varying roles of the immune system and inflammation in the development of aortopathies, including a presentation by Dr Ucuzian’s group (University of Maryland) about the role of exercise in modulating the inflammatory and growth factor pathway dysregulation in both aortic dissection as well as AAAs, which he discussed in further detail in his K08 update. Dr Alan Daugherty’s group (University of Kentucky) presented their work with HMGB1, a DNA-binding nuclear inflammatory molecule that they found to be upregulated in both murine and human aneurysmal disease. They additionally demonstrated that knockdown with an antisense oligonucleotide resulted in attenuation of AAA formation in hypertensive mice. Dr Humraaz Samra (Indiana University) presented a phase I trial (ARREST trial) in which mesenchymal stromal cells were delivered to patients with small AAAs in order to increase circulating IL-10 and shift the T cell polarization toward an anti-inflammatory Treg phenotype. Their findings suggest that systemic administration of allogenic mesenchymal stem cells is safe and may be of clinical utility in reducing AAA expansion.
Dr Bo Liu’s group (University of Wisconsin-Madison) discussed using CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing technology alongside pharmacologic inhibition to demonstrate a positive correlation between Ripk3 expression, a mediator of smooth muscle cell necroptosis, and aneurysm size, with hope to soon prove a causal relationship and between Ripk3 gene editing, smooth muscle cell necroptosis, inflammation, and aneurysm growth. Dr Tarek Aridi (Beth Israel Deaconess) presented his data on the anti-inflammatory gene A20/TNFAIP3, showing knockdown of this gene to be protective against AAA development by promoting smooth muscle cell proliferation and preserving the integrity of the extracellular matrix, with validation of their findings in human aortic smooth muscle cells.
Next-generation RNA sequencing was again seen in the final scientific session, with Dr Derek Klarin’s group (Stanford University) using single-nucleus RNA sequencing to assess expression of genes that may link ICA with over vascular disease, finding several loci to be associated with ICA that are shared with atherosclerotic and aneurysmal disease, independent of the genetic predisposition hypertension, which is considered to have a causal relationship with ICA development.
Dr William Warner closed out the scientific presentations showing the Paulsen Group’s (Dartmouth College) early investigations in using spatially tracked ultrasound to provide real-time 3D reconstruction and localization of an endovascular stent relative to a preoperative computed tomography image. Though the concept has only been utilized thus far in a gelatin phantom model, it shows promise as a cost-effective and radiation-free alternative to conventional endovascular techniques.

## Awardee report and translational panel
Dr Raul Guzman (Yale University) presented this year’s SVS Foundation awards, with the Resident Research Award being presented to Dr Sneha Raju (Toronto General Hospital Research Institute), and the VRIC Trainee Awards going to Sharika Bamezai (University of Michigan), Dr Tyler M. Bauer (University of Michigan), Rodrigo Meade (Washington University), and Dr Humraaz S. Samra (Indiana University).
Following the trainee awards presentation, Areck A. Ucuzian, MD, PhD (University of Maryland), a recipient of one of the SVS partner grants with the National Institutes of Health K08 awardees, presented his progress report. He described the benefits of aerobic exercise in maintaining the vessel structure in mouse models of aortopathy. In contrast to the theoretical risk of worsening aortic disease with the increase of systemic blood pressure and heart rate during aerobic exercise, Dr Ucuzian demonstrated that moderate aerobic exercise reverses aortopathy, prevents chronic aneurysmal degeneration, and confers a significant survival benefit as a result of the modulation of gene dysregulation and inflammation by aerobic exercise. This work suggests moderate exercise programs maybe be a useful therapeutic modality and has important implications for the overall health and quality of life of vascular patients with aortic disease.
This year’s translational panel featured presentations from Chiara Giannarelli, MD, PhD, and Bhama Ramkhelawon, PhD. Dr Giannarelli (NYU Langone Health) described deep phenotyping in atherosclerotic disease, from both in situ and dissociated human specimens obtained from the operating room. Her work suggests the possibility of deep phenotyping as a path toward precision medicine. Dr Ramkhelawon (NYU Langone Medical Center) discussed the structural triggers of macrophages and implications for the development of aortic aneurysmal disease. She demonstrated amplification of CD16-positive cells in patients with AAAs, suggesting that they may be a useful biomarker for aneurysmal development. Additionally, she described the significance of platelet-macrophage interactions and showed the therapeutic implications of this relationship, finding a reduced incidence of aneurysm formation following treatment with anti-platelet agent eptifibatide.

## Distinguished lecture
Among the highlights of VRIC each year is the Alexander W. Clowes Distinguished Lecture. Dr Clowes was a Professor of Surgery at the University of Washington as well as a world-renowned vascular surgeon-scientist whose ground-breaking work has had an unmeasurably great impact on the field of Vascular Surgery. This year’s invited speaker, Dr Esther Lutgens, is a physician, molecular biologist, and professor of vascular immunopathology at Amsterdam University Medical Center who has been recognized for her work investigating the role of co-stimulatory molecule interactions, specifically of the cell-type specific actions of CD40-CD40 L signaling and the identifications of signal transduction pathways relevant to atherosclerosis. Throughout her talk, she painted an intricate picture of atherosclerotic disease as a spectrum of lipid-driven immune disease, elegantly comparing the variable presentations of atherosclerosis to artist Wassily Kandinsky’s Farbstudie—Quadrate mit konzentrischen Ringen ( ). 
Dr Lutgens described her early work with the CD40-CD40 L dyad, finding global knockouts of either CD40 or CD40 L in apolipoprotein E-deficient mice led to less atherosclerosis and a favorable plaque phenotype with increased fibrosis and less inflammation. Although these findings suggest CD40 or CD40 L would be a convenient therapeutic target, she explained that long-term broad immune inhibition has significant untoward side effects, underscoring the importance of specific, targeted therapy. Dr Lutgens then went on to outline the intricate pathway from drug development to clinical translation, specifically through manipulation of CD40-TRAF6 signaling. Starting initially with mice with mutations at the CD40-TRAF6 binding site, Dr Lutgens’s team has since elucidated a role of this interaction in recruitment and differentiation of monocytes/macrophages and has developed a small molecule inhibitor (TRAF-STOP) that binds to the TRAF6 binding site on CD40, preventing this interaction. This small molecule has been shown to be safe in a study of nonhuman primates and offers an exciting look at the potential future of targeted immune checkpoint therapy for treatment and prevention of atherosclerotic disease.

## Poster competition
Following the scientific sessions, there was a poster competition that was moderated by Andrea Obi and Mohamed Zayed. Unlike your typical poster session, presentations were assigned to one of five categories, which were aneurysm/matrix metalloproteinases, endothelium, peripheral vascular disease, thrombosis/inflammation, and vascular biology. Each presenter was given time to present their work to and field questions from a panel of judges who then scored each presentation, and the presenter in each group with the highest score was selected as the winner. This year’s winners were Dr Jonathan R. Krebs (aneurysm/matrix metalloproteinases), Dr Sneja Raju (endothelium), Dr Jordan Stafford (peripheral vascular disease), Dr Li Guo (thrombosis/inflammation), and Dr Katherine Hekman (vascular biology).

## Conclusion
VRIC, orchestrated by the SVS Basic and Translational Research committee and held in conjunction with the AHA’s Vascular Discovery meeting, offers a venue for vascular surgeon-scientists to present their work and provoke scientific discussions in the context of a large scientific community. This meeting fosters scientific curiosity, collaboration, and growth and allows for surgeon-scientists at all levels to continue their meaningful contributions to the field of Vascular Surgery.
