## Abstract

## Background
Driven by increased injection opioid use, rates of hospitalisation for infective endocarditis, an infection associated with injection drug use, are increasing. In the USA, 1 in 10 hospitalised patients for opioid use disorder-associated infective endocarditis (OUD-IE) die in the hospital and 1 in 20 have a patient-directed discharge. Emerging models of care reveal opportunities for healthcare systems to meet the complex care needs of these patients. We characterised promising practices of staff who care for these patients and identified areas for improvement.

## Methods
We conducted a qualitative study with 1-hour semistructured virtual interviews between October 2021 and March 2022. Participants included 26 healthcare staff who care for patients with OUD-IE at the Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania. We used thematic analysis of interviews guided by an abductive approach. Interviews were digitally recorded and transcribed and analysed using NVivo software.

## Results
Interviews were characterised by three major themes: (1) care rooted in interdisciplinary collaboration; (2) managing OUD and its sequelae in a setting not designed to treat OUD; and (3) clinician needs and barriers to change.

## Conclusions
These findings highlight the facilitators of high-quality treatment for patients with OUD-IE, as well as the key areas for improvement. Findings add context to the complexity that both the healthcare staff and patients navigate during and following hospitalisation for OUD-IE. Needed changes include training staff to talk with patients about preparing for a return to drug use following hospitalisation, and changing discharge facilities’ practices that hinder access for patients with OUD-IE.

## WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC
Patients hospitalised with opioid use disorder-associated infective endocarditis (OUD-IE) have complex needs that are often unmet during hospitalisation. There are emerging models of care for patients with OUD-IE.

## WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
This study documents a model of hospital care for patients with OUD-IE and identifies key areas for improvement.

## HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY
Increased staff training about how to have conversations about a return to drug use following hospitalisation.
Changes in discharge facilities’ practices that hinder access for patients with OUD-IE.

## Introduction
Driven by increased opioid use, rates of hospitalisation for injection-related endocarditis are increasing. In the USA, nearly 13 000 patients were hospitalised in 2016 for opioid use disorder-associated infective endocarditis (OUD-IE), and these patients exhibit distinctly poor outcomes; while more recent data are not available, the number of hospitalised patients with OUD-IE in 2023 is likely higher than it was in 2016. One in 10 die in the hospital and 1 in 20 have a patient-directed discharge (PDD; discharge ‘against medical advice’). When these patients experience a PDD, they are likely to be readmitted 50% of the time, compared with 21% for other discharge types in a 30-day readmission. Patients with OUD-IE amass more than $700 million in annual inpatient charges and require lengthy hospitalisations to provide the 2–6 weeks of intravenous antibiotics needed to treat the infection.
Twenty per cent of patients with OUD-IE are readmitted for recurrent OUD‐IE, likely due to continued injection opioid use. Even though the average length of hospitalisation for patients with OUD-IE is 19 days, healthcare staff tend not to address OUD during the hospital stay. Only 6–8% of patients with OUD-IE are connected to evidence-based treatment for OUD by discharge, despite the finding that it can reduce mortality by 50%. While emerging models of care, like those in San Francisco and Boston, reveal opportunities for healthcare systems to meet the needs of patients with OUD-IE, widespread implementation of such interventions is lacking, in part because many models have not been documented or evaluated. In this analysis, we characterised promising practices of healthcare staff regarding the care of patients with OUD-IE and identified areas for improvement in the treatment of these patients.

## Methods

## Setting
The Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania (HUP) is part of the Penn Medicine Health System and is a 776-bed, urban academic medical centre in Philadelphia. Almost 46% of HUP’s patients have private insurance, 34% have Medicare and one-fifth have Medicaid or are uninsured. Its large, nationally ranked cardiothoracic (CT) surgery programme accepts complex cases from across the region. HUP does not have an addiction consult service providing substance use disorder (SUD) treatment for hospitalised patients; however, beginning in 2018, the CT surgery postoperative unit began holding weekly interdisciplinary meetings (called ‘huddles’) to coordinate the care of patients with SUD.

## Participants
We purposively sampled HUP staff greater than 18 years of age who cared for patients with OUD-IE during the last 6 months, stratified by professional role. Staff invited to participate in interviews included physicians, advanced practice providers, pharmacists, bedside nurses, certified recovery specialists, social workers, clinical managers and leadership. Participants were compensated $25 for their time. All participants provided informed consent.

## Procedures
One author (RF) conducted and recorded qualitative interviews between October 2021 and March 2022. A qualitative methods expert (JC) trained her. Interviews were conducted virtually via a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA)-compliant virtual platform. Interviews followed a systematic, semistructured guide ( ) that asked participants to share their perspectives on (a) identifying OUD among patients with infective endocarditis; (b) success of related healthcare efforts; (c) use of evidence-based OUD treatment; and (d) recommendations for improving healthcare delivery to these patients. We collected participants’ demographic information after completing the interview. Interviews averaged approximately 1 hour.
 

## Data analysis
Interviews were audio recorded, professionally transcribed and imported into NVivo V.1.6.1 software. The coding team (RF, AS) independently coded three interviews, and met to reach consensus on a comprehensive coding scheme. The few disagreements in coding or themes were resolved through discussion with expert guidance (JC). The remaining interviews were single coded (AS). New data did not alter the codebook after the 13th interview, suggesting thematic saturation. Once coding was complete, the coding team (RF, AS) synthesised the codes into an overarching explanation using an abductive approach.

## Results
A total of 26 interviews were conducted. Most participants were female (77%) and white (96%). On average, participants had worked on their unit for 6 years and practised for 12 years ( ). The majority of participants (77%) worked on a CT surgery unit. Three themes emerged: (1) care rooted in interdisciplinary collaboration; (2) managing OUD and its sequelae in a setting not designed to treat OUD; and (3) clinician needs and barriers to change. ## Theme 1: care rooted in interdisciplinary collaboration
Participants reported that care for patients with OUD-IE undergoing CT surgery was necessarily interdisciplinary. Aspects of this model of interdisciplinary care included: (1) huddle as an interdisciplinary communication tool, (2) well-defined roles and (3) benefits of interdisciplinary collaboration for patients and clinicians.

## Huddle as an interdisciplinary communication tool
The care team used a ‘huddle’ as a communication tool. During these 30 min weekly huddles, the interdisciplinary care team discussed and formulated plans for patients with SUDs. One participant shared how the huddle functioned: ‘Every Wednesday at noon there’s a conference with social work and the primary nurse and the care team and psychiatrists about discharge barriers, specifically for the endocarditis population with IV drug users’ (14). Another participant stated: ‘It’s like, what is this patient’s goal, what is the discharge plan, and how are we all working together to make this as safe as possible?’ (22).
Participants noted that the huddle facilitated an interdisciplinary process for developing and refining treatment plans. One participant stated: ‘Once the patient is identified [as having SUD], we talk about them once a week [in the huddle]. Then pharmacy, psychiatry and pain will come together and formulate the plan for that patient’ (5). Another participant shared: ‘Discussing [patients with OUD-IE] in a multidisciplinary venue makes it so much easier when determining what is the best medication to use, what does the patient really want?’ (19).

## Well-defined roles amidst interdisciplinary collaboration
Participants reported having well-defined roles on their team. For example, one participant stated, ‘Usually we get psych on board pretty early on, not just for assistance with the meds that we don’t prescribe as often, but because they’re going to follow them to titrate certain medications and make suggestions for withdrawal medications as well’ (11). Participants also discussed how some team members are better suited for a particular task, which revealed more fluid role definition. One nurse practitioner stated: ‘Sometimes we try to educate [the patient] as well as get our colleagues in psych to give them a little [education] as well as sometimes we have excellent pharmacists on the team who will get involved and explain to patients’ (12).
Participants also talked about the role of peer recovery specialists on the care team. One participant stated: ‘We’ll often consult [peer counselors] when the patient is wanting and open to some more support or if it’s a patient that’s new to [medication assisted treatment] and is thinking about it and wants a little more information, kind of experiential information on it’ (7). Social workers were primarily responsible for discharge planning, which is especially complicated given the frequent need for both intravenous antibiotics and medication for OUD (MOUD). One participant shared: ‘Social work is heavily, heavily involved… in getting them out of the hospital. The setting that they need to get IV antibiotics is the path that starts this whole conversation’ (14). Another participant stressed that discharge planning included many responsibilities such as ‘finding a transportation system that would get the patient to the methadone clinic every day. Or getting the patient insurance coverage to be able to pay for the Suboxone’ (1).

## Benefits of interdisciplinary collaboration for patients and clinicians
Several participants brought up the positive impact of an interdisciplinary approach to care for patients with OUD-IE. As one participant shared, ‘[I]f [the care team is] on the same page and everyone says the same thing [to the patient], I think it really builds trust and they know that we’re doing what’s best for them’ (15). Another participant shared a similar viewpoint:
I think that’s part of what the huddle really helped is getting a unified plan in place amongst all the providers of what’s realistic for the patient to expect so we didn’t have four or five different people going and presenting different possibilities and options that may or may not pan out, which led the patients to get frustrated, maybe contributed to like leaving AMA [against medical advice]. (7)
Participants also found that communication with other team members during the huddles promoted clinician education about OUD. One participant shared their experience: ‘It was for me like education and familiarity, hearing information from the providers, from psychiatry, from pharmacy has been incredibly helpful just in understanding my assessment with the patients for their substance use history’ (7). Another participant stated: ‘We don't always signpost education, but being in a huddle, talking about patients, using the right kind of language, I think people will learn from that’ (22). Participants also shared how the huddle reduced stigma towards patients with SUD. As one participant noted, ‘One thing that our huddles do– they’ve humanized the person. I may not even know the patient or have seen the patient personally, and I can find myself getting invested in the person’s story’ (19).

## Theme 2: managing OUD and its sequelae in a setting not designed to treat OUD
Endocarditis, for which there is a well-established treatment protocol, provides a complex challenge when paired with navigating the treatment of OUD alongside intravenous antibiotics to treat endocarditis. Pain management provides its own set of challenges. While participants succeeded in managing the pain of patients with OUD-IE and connecting them with proper postdischarge treatment, harm reduction education was limited.

## Managing pain within the context of OUD and endocarditis
Participants noted that managing pain and withdrawal is a salient aspect of care for patients with OUD-IE. Pain management is challenging due to the nature of endocarditis, sometimes causing joint, muscle and/or chest pain. Pain management challenges are compounded when CT surgery is required. When patients have OUD in addition to endocarditis, pain management becomes even more complicated because (a) patients have a higher tolerance to pain medications, (b) patients may experience opioid-induced hyperalgesia and (c) stigma may cause clinicians to delegitimise the pain patients report and view their behaviours as drug seeking. 
Participants reported that managing OUD-IE required a patient-centred approach. Listening to the patient emerged as a key strategy to manage pain in these difficult situations. As one participant stated, ‘It’s always engaging [with] the patient [and] laying out the different options, and seeing what best works for them’ (1). Another participant described their discussions with patients: ‘[Patients] often know more than some of the providers. I say what have you been on? What has worked for you before? Are you interested in one of them?’ (20).
Some participants found that pain management benefited from a collaborative approach among the care team. Most participants were trained to manage pain related to endocarditis, but not OUD. Participants found that working with an interdisciplinary team helped them learn how to manage both concurrently. One participant stated:
Coordinating [narcotics] with psychiatry, pain management, that’s a big part of our role, too, is just a collaborative approach to their care with those teams, but we have to have set that up and make sure it’s done. And then, especially if they need methadone clinic post-op and to be discharged then it’s also social work is a really big factor in their care as well. (15)
One common strategy was to involve pharmacy:
Yeah, we have a very tight connection with pharmacology…And it’s been very helpful to be able to have that because they do have the tools and they see all these patients, they see the medications that they’re on, so they can definitely offer insight on treating patients. And we’ve actually had a few success stories involving co-managing these patients, especially opioid use disorders, patients on Suboxone, on methadone and even ketamine where we’ve actually developed protocols in certain patients while working with pharmacy. (25)

## Longer term management of OUD
Participants found that social workers led efforts to connect patients with postdischarge OUD treatment. Participants viewed this connection as a step towards recovery for patients with OUD. Notably absent from discharge planning, however, were conversations about how to stay safe should the patient return to drug use. One participant stressed this idea:
Where I think we still lag is in encouraging people to use safe practices when using. Narcan is now seen as lifesaving, so there’s a different stigma around that. But clean needles, where to go [to get them], I’m not sure a lot of physicians discuss that with patients, but we should. (19)
Another participant similarly stated: ‘I feel like [the discharge education to patients is] just like, don't use. Which is– the patient knows that they should do that, obviously, but that’s not how it should be presented’ (13).
Many participants expressed that discharge education related to OUD is better than it used to be but still has room for improvement. One participant stated: ‘I think we are much better at it than we used to be, because when we first started prescribing Narcan [at discharge], physicians wouldn’t even prescribe it, because they were afraid…now I think that people understand we need Narcan’ (19). Such conversations are important, even for patients planning for recovery, because a return to use is often part of the recovery journey and/or patients may feel like they need to tell their care team that they are planning for postdischarge recovery to receive needed medical care, even if they are not invested in recovery.

## Theme 3: clinician needs and barriers to change
Participants identified current policies that hinder best care practices for patients with OUD-IE. Four categories emerged: (1) hospital regulations and policies, (2) external barriers associated with discharge, (3) education and training and (4) recommendations.

## Lack of addiction services
Participants reported disjointed and lacking inpatient resources for treating the addiction and psychological needs of patients with OUD-IE. As one participant, who was aware of plans to launch an addiction consult service at another Penn Medicine Health System hospital, noted, ‘We have all these guidelines [but many people are not] comfortable using them. You have to know who to call when you aren’t comfortable using them…that’s why we want this more addiction consult service so people know exactly where to go and get help’ (4). Participants found the lack of resources to severely limit care delivery, especially given that most patients with OUD-IE are in the hospital for approximately 6 weeks. One participant expressed: ‘We don't have good addiction medicine counseling. We don’t have an addiction medicine service that can come and consult, and create the proper links post discharge, or even manage medication’ (21).

## External barriers associated with discharge
Participants recognised external barriers related to discharge for patients with OUD-IE, such as facilities with admission practices that deny these patients care. Discharge facilities accept few patients on MOUD, if any. One participant shared: ‘Sometimes we can get people to a [skilled nursing facility]. Sometimes we can get them to a nursing home. [But] that has become increasingly difficult. Part of that is stigma, and places in Pennsylvania are allowed to refuse people for a substance use disorder’ (3). While discrimination may be at the root of (these) policies, one participant mentioned that it could be out of the facilities’ control:
[W]e always say on the provider side, oh, this is about stigma, but at the end of the day, if you’re not a licensed methadone clinic it’s illegal for you to distribute, and if you don’t have an X-waivered physician, it’s illegal to give the Suboxone unless these things are for pain. (3)
As a possible solution to discharge challenges, one participant noted the possibility of opening an in-house discharge facility that could accept patients with OUD-IE, stating:
We had talked about using space [in downtown Philadelphia] to admit patients [needing MOUD and IV antibiotics], get them out of HUP. I'm not sure why we haven't moved on it. When you think about it, six to ten patients every week, and they need six weeks of treatment, it adds up. (21)
Many participants noted additional discharge challenges, such as poor transportation and social support. One participant discussed the challenge of accessing medication after discharge, including transportation and insurance. Another participant addressed new barriers that emerged because of COVID-19: ‘Facilities really don’t want to be transporting people in and out of their facilities so much because every single time they leave it’s an exposure’ (3).

## Education and training
Several participants expressed the need for more education in medication management, OUD-IE treatment and patient interaction. One participant stated: ‘A lot of my questions have arisen from wondering what I can order for patients. So, pharmacological things…I want to make sure that I am not going to hurt the patient by ordering something that maybe I don’t fully understand’ (14). Another participant highlighted clinicians’ unawareness of medication management policies: ‘There’s no reason why you can't start methadone in MOUD during an inpatient stay. There’s a misinterpretation of existing guidelines, and then over application of existing guidelines that make people leery of doing that’ (26). While participants did their best to tailor treatment for each patient, they noted their limited knowledge about OUD and the relatively limited offerings for OUD-IE treatment, which include only MOUD for OUD and intravenous antibiotics for endocarditis. One participant shared:
The HUP teams don’t have as much experience or knowledge if someone is using like a bundle a day what that translates for opioids. [I]f they’re not in a place where they want to go on Suboxone or methadone, how to give them the equivalent is a challenge for some of the team. (20)
Many participants noticed a lack of training regarding appropriate patient interaction. One participant noted: ‘I think we need to start with, number one, trust matters. People leave AMA, not just because of withdrawal, but because of all these other reasons. Here are some basic strategies you can take to help build rapport with your patient and make them feel safe’ (22). Another common remark made was the need for training in handling challenging patients. One participant expressed: ‘Right now nurses we know want more tools for managing episodes where they’re hearing racism from patients, racist requests for staff or other things’ (2).

## Recommendations
We compiled recommendations based on the interviews with clinicians caring for patients with OUD-IE ( ). Recommendations include strategies for improving daily interactions with patients, such as avoiding the use of dehumanising language, listening to the patient, letting the patient know they can return to the hospital, and system-level recommendations such as developing an in-house discharge option for patients with OUD-IE needing intravenous antibiotics. This in-house discharge option would allow patients with OUD-IE needing both MOUD and intravenous antibiotics to be discharged to a Penn Medicine facility able to provide both services.

## Participant recommendations for team members caring for patients with OUD-IE
Use an interdisciplinary model of care.
Use consult teams (ie, pharmacy, pain management, psychiatry) as appropriate.
Avoid use of dehumanising language.
Listen to the patient.
Let the patient know they can return to the hospital if needed.
Have conversations about how to stay safe if drug use continues.
Provide the appropriate supplies and resources for discharge.
Develop an in-house discharge option for patients with opioid use disorder-associated infective endocarditis (OUD-IE) needing intravenous antibiotics.

## Discussion
This qualitative study characterised promising practices of healthcare staff in one hospital regarding care delivery for patients with OUD-IE and identified opportunities for improvement. One salient finding was that participants found the interdisciplinary approach of the CT surgery team facilitated high-quality, evidence-based endocarditis and OUD treatment. At the same time and consistent with evidence, opportunities for improvement emerged, specifically talking with patients about a potential return to drug use and addressing discharge facilities’ practices that hinder access for patients with OUD-IE.
Participants shared that interdisciplinary care occurred at several levels. On one level, consultations from pain and psychiatry services bridged knowledge gaps of how to treat patients with OUD. More intensively, the weekly 30 min CT surgery huddles led to growth and learning because they allowed the care team to listen to how others—including those with a noted commitment to or training in treating patients with OUD—thought about their patients. These interdisciplinary models of care focused on managing pain and withdrawal, something that many participants shared they knew was important but lacked the training and resources to execute well. Interdisciplinary models of care can also improve information sharing, empowerment and a culture of collaboration. The huddle provided a space for people to connect about caring for patients with SUDs, which proved vital to its success.
HUP has had success in caring for patients with OUD-IE. Despite evidence suggesting slow progress to destigmatise SUD in clinical settings, participants reported that the CT surgery team at HUP has made progress in destigmatising OUD. These efforts include learning from experts and others' experiences treating patients with OUD. For example, participants reported that emphasising the importance of non-stigmatising language—which happens during the huddles—helps to humanise patients with OUD. While these approaches are embraced by many, some members continue to hold stigmatising views of OUD. Participants reported taking patient complaints of pain seriously, recognising that needed doses of pain medications for patients with OUD are higher than for patients not tolerant to opioids. Additionally, HUP surgeons’ willingness to operate on patients with OUD, including for repeat occurrences of endocarditis, is something that many other providers will not do. 
While HUP’s efforts represent developments in caring for patients with OUD, areas for improvement remain. Social workers provide patients with information on treatment planning and MOUD, which is necessary but not sufficient. Consistent with other evidence, participants reported in their interviews that patient-centred conversations about how to stay safe should a return to drug use occur following discharge should be added to recovery-oriented discharge planning. Evidence suggests that some people with OUD will continue to use drugs following discharge, yet participants reported that members of the care team often overlook the possibility that individuals with OUD-IE will use drugs again. The lack of these conversations may send the message to patients that recovery is the only acceptable option for them and/or that receiving medical care is contingent on them pledging recovery, even if they are not invested in recovery. Participants highlighted that further education and training about how to have conversations with patients about drug use, the aetiology of addiction and clear guidelines on medication management would facilitate clinicians’ ability and willingness to have such conversations, which is consistent with other evidence supporting the need for such programmes to improve the quality of care for patients with OUD. 
Participants identified policies that hinder care for patients with OUD-IE, and the need to review these policies. Discharge facilities disproportionally will not admit patients with SUDs. Many discharge facilities report that they cannot distribute methadone, lack staff with addiction training or cannot safely store MOUD. Participants emphasised their uphill battle to find appropriate aftercare for their patients. While some legislative work is underway, more work is needed. The Penn Medicine Health System recently launched an addiction consult service at another hospital, which could improve hospital-based care for patients with OUD. Addiction consult services are among the most effective hospital-based SUD interventions for initiating MOUD and facilitating discharge planning. In line with this, hospitals have proposed a collaborative care model incorporating addiction specialists emphasising a short-term follow-up and planning for outpatient continuation. This intervention, however, has its own barriers including clinician stigma and lack of knowledge around evidence-based addiction treatment as well as a lack of resources for the intervention. 
Several study limitations should be mentioned. Participants who chose to participate were more likely to be interested in providing care for OUD-IE, which may present selection bias. The study sample is mostly white women, and the study is focused on a single site that happens to be a large academic centre. Some setting characteristics, such as use of huddles and the lack of an addiction consult service, may be institution specific; thus, it is not clear how these findings apply to other settings. Additionally, a direct measure of quality before and after implementation of the interdisciplinary approach would have strengthened our results.

## Conclusion
This study examined care for patients with OUD-IE in one hospital; it unearthed some promising practices and some distinct challenges. To meet the needs of this high-risk and expensive—admittedly small but growing—population, unified efforts are needed to document, collect data on, and compare existing models of care. We present these findings as a first step in pursuit of this goal by detailing a promising interdisciplinary model of care for patients with OUD-IE. The efficacy and generalisability of this model needs to be tested. Areas for improvement must also be addressed, such as ensuring that conversations about preparing for a return to drug use occur. We hope that others will share their promising models for caring for patients with OUD-IE, and we expect that unifying efforts will have broad implications for hospital care delivery for patients.
