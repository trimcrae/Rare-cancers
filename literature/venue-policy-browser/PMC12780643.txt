## ABSTRACT

## Introduction
The school‐to‐work transition (SWT) is complex for young people. The postpandemic era, characterized by rapidly evolving economies and technologies, has augmented the complexity of negotiating such pathways. Despite this, young people—understood here through the lens of emergent adulthood—are often positioned as wholly responsible for navigating their own pathways beyond school.

## Methods
A pragmatic case study approach was used to analyze qualitative data collected from three SWT partnership programs (UniHub, Australian School‐based Apprenticeships [ASbAs], and Regional Learning Pathways [RLPs]) aimed at young people (2018–2023) in regional areas of Tasmania, Australia. For each program, participants were interviewed (N = 6 [UniHub]; N = 23 [ASbAs]; N = 44 [RLPs]) and surveyed (N = 350 [UniHub]; N = 21 [ASbAs]), and data were thematically analyzed to identify the role of boundary‐crossers in SWT partnerships.

## Results
Boundary‐crosser work emerged as a key component of the sampled SWT partnerships. Enabling features of boundary‐crossing work in partnerships include accessibility, support contextualized to the region and individual—like reducing barriers to equity—and using clear communication approaches. This role redistributed responsibility in the SWT to a partnership between schools, pathways, and young people.

## Conclusions
Whereas literature and policy underscore the economic and developmental importance of SWT, the contribution of this article is research and data that nuance understanding of how SWT partnerships can share the responsibility of the SWT by designing in the program a boundary‐crosser role. The concept of boundary‐crosser work in SWT partnerships offers a novel approach for policymakers in policy and practice to rethink how the SWT is supported.

3.1

## Boundary‐Crossing Work and Boundary Objects in SWT Partnerships
We present key findings on the features of SWT partnerships that include and enable boundary‐crossing work. These findings are situated within two assumptions: (1) the SWT is a boundary and (2) partnerships in the SWT are boundary‐crossing work. 3.1.1

## Program Delivery Is From a Familiar, Trusted Place
An enabling boundary object for all three cases was physical (i.e., not virtual) spaces from where the SWT partnership was administered. One RLPs facilitator observed, “for people to feel comfortable around further education it needs to be first off in their space and place.” A UniHub participant shared the benefit of “having the UniHub open more often as my questions can be spur of the moment and easily forgotten.”
Both the UniHub and RLPs initiatives are delivered from within the day‐to‐day context of the participants. The UniHub is conducted from the school campus and is aligned with school place, time, and students' routines. The RLPs is delivered from trusted community hubs—like a library or neighborhood house. An RLPs facilitator reflected that being based in trusted community centers enabled participants to feel comfortable to engage with the program.
In comparison, ASbA did not have a dedicated space for program delivery, and participants were split between home, school, and the apprenticeship location. All ASbA participants reported that the lack of dedicated program space made it difficult to clarify program expectations (Table  ).
 
3.1.2

## Reduction of Financial and Transport Barriers to Participation
The SWT often incurs financial burden associated with increased independence, which can be an additional boundary. While all sampled programs were free for participants, there were indirect short‐ and long‐term financial consequences of participation.
Perceived future financial security was important to EA participating in the sampled partnerships. Program design that provided financial support to participants enabled program engagement. For example, the UniHub model assists prospective university students with applying for higher education places, with 89% of UniHub participants who applied to the local university between 2018 and 2024 going on to enroll. Similarly, ASbA participants were motivated to complete the program to gain more job opportunities (85%), a job (80%), and a qualification for part‐time work (65%).
However, when participation in SWT partnerships added short‐term financial burden to EA, engagement was lower. While ASbA pays participants for their apprenticeship hours, program limits on workable hours deterred some participants from completing the program “two dropped out… because… your hours get changed to minimum 10 and maximum 15 hours… [they] wanted to have hours over 20, so it wasn't enough work for them… So that's why they dropped out, because the hours were limited.” Another ASbA participant highlighted the relationship between financial pressure and “…life situations. If they had to go and get a full time job to support their family… As it works out at the moment once tax comes out I'm a little under $9 an hour so it's not really… it kind of makes you wonder if it's worth it.” In alignment with this, fifty percent of industry stakeholders and 60% of school staff agreed financial difficulties were a barrier to participation in ASbA.
3.1.2.1

## Managing Transport Barriers to Participation
In RRR areas like Tasmania, where there are few public transport options, organizing transport to and from school, higher education, or work can add additional barriers to the SWT. Transport between home, school, and apprenticeship location was particularly challenging for ASbA participants. One hundred percent of ASbA participants lived regionally, with a participant describing how the partnership design did not account for transport: “I didn't have a license to get into town, so I had to walk and catch the bus, and walk a bit more. That was hectic… so I had to get on a school bus—because I was still going to school I could—but if I was out of school I wouldn't have been able to catch that bus.” An ASbA industry stakeholder agreed transport impacted on engagement “… some people… are travelling an hour and a half to get to their traineeship spot with buses… even if you get concession rates, it adds up.” The impact of transport on ASbA participation was disputed by school staff and industry partners supporting the program. For example, 95% of school staff and industry stakeholders (combined) agreed access to reliable transport was vital to the success of ASbA, but 60% of industry partners and 36% of school staff disagreed transport difficulties were a barrier to participation. A school staff member shared, “ultimately, if the young person really wants to do something and transport's involved, they will find a way around it.” This was echoed by an industry stakeholder: “I disagreed that transport difficulties would be an issue because I think people have the same access to public transport no matter what [their] socioeconomic [status].”
The design of the UniHub and RLPs programs planned for material (practical transport support like buses) and nonmaterial (scheduling and program design) boundary objects associated with transport. The RLPs and UniHub were also split between a core program location (schools, libraries, neighborhood houses) and industry or campus locations. However, program design facilitated transport between program sites. The RLPs design included additional allocation of funding for participants to use for transport and child‐care (where services were available). Further, the UniHub model design accounted for transport being an additional boundary to participation: both programs covering costs for campus and industry visits.

3.1.3

## Consistent Communication With Participants to Foster Relationships
For all cases, consistent communication approaches connected participants with the partnership, thereby enabling young people to engage with their SWT. Both the UniHub and RLPs programs had clear management and delivery of communication structures. Contextually appropriate communication boundary objects included phone and email (RLPs and UniHub), and social media groups with participants (RLPs). UniHub also used regular, face‐to‐face events, excursions, and 1:1 mentoring sessions. A UniHub participant shared, “personal support is really encouraging. It's nice to have people who genuinely care about your future and remember you.”
In comparison, consistent communication roles were not embedded in the ASbA partnership. Participants lacked clarity on who their support people were and whether they were located at their school or workplace. Staff members—responsible for delivery and management of the program—described poor communication and poor relationships with participants: “You could be blithely going along for 6 months thinking everything's fine, and then you get that training report mid‐year and suddenly find out they've been doing nothing… We do quiz the students, like, ‘And how's it going?’” Reflecting on what could improve program delivery and participation, a staff member shared, “… someone who's totally devoted to that student at that time…just to sit down and listen to the student and see what they need.”
ASbA was aimed at supporting Aboriginal students throughout the SWT. Contextually appropriate cultural communication was needed:… a lot of these kids who are only probably now discovering their culture and what that background means to them, if they had someone, a strong mentor at work, someone that they could look up to and pair up with, I think that would make a huge difference…it would have to be someone who understood how these kids think.
(Aboriginal school staff member)

Eighty percent of surveyed industry stakeholders and 64% of surveyed school staff agreed that supporting Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander students with a dedicated cultural mentor would lead to improved success for ASbA participants who identify as First Nations (see Rudling et al.  ).

3.1.4

## Opportunities for Exploring SWT Possibilities
Across all cases, participants valued experiencing potential pathways for their SWT. An RLPs participant reflected on their renewed: “confidence in the future I guess because now I've got more sources, I mean the source to get the information, where to get the information from and where to go and things like that.” This was extended by a UniHub participant: “it's helped me a lot with understanding uni and stuff.” An AsBA participant shared EA: “have to be introduced to their interest and see if they actually do like it.” For another Unihub participant, the experience of participating was valuable, regardless of whether it led to a direct pathway: “I loved attending sessions put on [by] the UniHub, sometimes if only to rule out something about my future.”
Wide‐ranging experiences helped participants to explore their SWT. For example, an RLPs participant described how a guest industry speaker: “was really interested in what each of us individually was looking to do, and she was giving people pointers and contacts.” For a UniHub participant, campus visits helped them to visualize their pathway: “I love music… it also showed me that going into a university pathway for music may be more useful and enjoyable than I assumed.” The data highlights the intrinsic value of experiencing possible SWT pathways. However, for some ASbA participants, limited placement possibilities or opportunities that were misaligned with their SWT were demotivating. Participants were divided on the value of transferable skills (like resilience and independence) the traineeship offered their SWT. The formal partnership underpinning ASbA led to some young people approaching ASbA as a pathway and some other young people approaching ASbA as an experience with inherent value to their career development.

## Discussion
4.1

## Conceptualizing Boundary‐Crosser Work in SWT Partnerships
Here, we introduce our conceptual model of boundary‐crosser work in SWT partnerships. We synthesize and interpret our findings to discuss how boundary‐crossers can redistribute the responsibility for the SWT from being primarily placed on the EA (Schoon and Bynner  ; Redmond et al.  ) to a shared responsibility (Bell et al.  ). Boundary‐crosser work can broaden understandings of the SWT partnership by drawing attention to the interplay of agency and structure occurring during the EA life‐stage (Blokker et al.  ). Such boundary‐crossing work is enabled by SWT partnerships that provide opportunities to explore SWT possibilities, deliver the SWT partnership from a trusted space, reduce financial and transport barriers, and use consistent communication approaches. Figure  illustrates our conceptual model of boundary‐crosser work within SWT partnerships.
 In a challenge to Beck's ( ) responsibilization of young people in the SWT, Figure  shows multiple responsible stakeholders in the SWT: schools and community organizations, the boundary‐crosser, the EA, and the pathway. The color shading depicts how the varying degrees of responsibility work in partnership across the SWT (see Shergold et al.  ). The EA is at the foundation of this partnership. Their grounded position represents how, for the EA, the SWT involves entering unfamiliar territory, often without the requisite skills, and that it can be challenging to “see” oneself in the unknown (Suchman  ; Blokker et al.  ; Hamilton  ).
In this depiction, the boundary‐crosser extends the EA's agency in the SWT (Rudd and Evans  ). The top of the “boundary‐crosser” oval sits at the highest point of the diagram to illustrate that the boundary‐crosser has both institutional and school authorization to “see” and “move” across institutional boundaries to help young people navigate their SWT (Flynn et al.  ; Akkerman and Bakker  ). The boundary‐crosser work coordinates the four stakeholders into a partnership (see Akkerman and Bakker  ; Kelly  ). This pictorial representation of the role of boundary‐crossers in SWT partnerships redistributes the responsibility of the transition from a sole undertaking to a shared one (Schoon and Bynner  ) that accounts for the EA's agency, as well as offering structure from the partnership program (Blokker et al.  ).
In recognition that EA are gaining independence in multiple domains simultaneously (Hamilton  ), the arrows reflect how the SWT can be iterative (Carabelli and Lyon  ; Helms Jørgensen et al.  ). It also depicts how participants in the sampled initiatives valued broad experiences that helped them to discern their interests. Viewing the SWT in this way is aligned with recommendations by Shergold et al. ( ) that the SWT occurs across a multistage life cycle and draws on scholarship that attaining work can be a gradual and extended process for EA (Lehmann  ; Grossi et al.  ).
Our conceptual model broadens the policy framing that young people themselves “construct” their SWT (see Schoon and Bynner  ; Redmond et al.  ; Kelly  ). Instead, it reflects policies that reimagine the SWT as a cycle and a partnership (Shergold et al.  ; Tasmanian Government  ). This framing underscores the importance of SWT partnerships that connect EA with pathways that go beyond discipline‐specific interventions (Flynn et al.  ) or programs aimed at specific cohorts (Ngai et al.  ) so as to strike a balance between structure and agency (Hays  ) and support EA to explore their possible futures (Rudd and Evans  ). Our conceptual model of boundary‐crosser work in SWT partnerships is supported by boundary objects, which we discuss in the following section.

4.2

## Enabling Boundary Objects of Boundary‐Crosser Work in the SWT
There are many boundaries in the SWT: social, economic, and individual contexts (Blokker et al.  ; Tasmanian Government  ). Our conceptual model of boundary‐crosser work in SWT partnerships is enabled by boundary objects that help to redistribute the burden of responsibility of the SWT as a shared undertaking between the EA and the boundary‐crosser. The sampled partnerships highlight how the following “operational” (Flynn et al.  ) and “object” artefacts (Akkerman and Bakker  ,  ) enable boundary‐crossing work in SWT partnerships: opportunities for exploring SWT possibilities, program delivery from a familiar and trusted place, reduction of financial imposition and transport challenges, and consistent communication that fosters relationships. These attributes offer structure to the SWT but also reallocate the onus of responsibility (see Schoon and Bynner  ; Cohen‐Scali et al.  ) between the stakeholders in the transition (Wood et al.  ; Arnett  ) to extend agency (Rudd and Evans  ).
4.2.1

## Program Delivery Is From a Familiar, Trusted Place
EA experience a paradoxical reduction in age‐appropriate supports simultaneous to their increased responsibility and independence, which can be a boundary to the SWT (Blokker et al.  ; Tasmanian Government  ). Physical (i.e., not virtual) spaces for program delivery that are embedded in the EA's context (such as school or local community venue) are an example of an effective boundary object that links EA with the SWT (Tasmanian Government  ). For example, RLPs are embedded in the regional community with the program run from selected trusted community venues. Participants felt familiar with the location, which reduced the unknowns associated with participation. Similarly, the UniHub program includes a physical school office, which aligned the program in both place and time (school hours) with students' established routines. These places act as “boundary objects” (Akkerman and Bakker  ) that connect discrete systems and create a sense of familiarity for the EA, leading to a sense of safety and trust, which is vital for making pathways possible (Lang et al.  ).

4.2.2

## Reduction of Financial and Transport Barriers to Participation
Across the cases, program participants' ability to engage with the SWT partnerships was bound by their socioeconomic context (Rudd and Evans  ) and whether the partnership program design enabled participation by accounting for these circumstances. Financial imposition—including transport issues—associated with participating in the SWT partnership dissuaded engagement, especially for EA in RRR (Kilpatrick et al.  ; Corbett and Roberts  ; Napthine et al.  ). Partnerships that did not plan for these factors (Bell et al.  ; Shergold et al.  ) tended to overresponsibilize participants (Schoon and Bynner  ). The case studies reveal that contextually appropriate boundary objects—like designing for transport—enable EA to engage with their SWT (Star and Griesemer  ; Akkerman and Bakker  ,  ). The identification of such barriers by the partnership program is vital to implementing boundary objects that are responsive to place (Tasmanian Government  ) and empower young people to overcome inequities in material needs (Rudling et al.  , pp. 151–152; see further Rudd and Evans  ). Overresponsibilizing EA to mitigate barriers to participation that they may be experiencing evades the responsibility of the SWT pathway initiative in inclusive design (Lehmann  ; Kelly  ).

4.2.3

## Consistent Communication With Participants to Foster Relationships
Establishing consistent communication approaches is important to reducing the responsibility attributed to EA in the SWT partnership. This approach positions the boundary‐crossers in SWT partnerships as the key duty‐bearers for effective communication, rather than perceiving the EA in terms of “individual pathologies and deficits” (Kelly  , p. 24).
Communication approaches can be an enabling boundary object when done consistently (Star and Griesemer  ). Communication approaches that act as a bridge across partnership stakeholders are consistently applied and are appropriate to all intersecting contexts (Akkerman and Bakker  ; Star and Griesemer  ), which can be challenging for boundary‐crossers when SWT pathways tend to be highly individualized (Akkerman and Bakker  ). The sampled cases suggest how establishing clear communication processes reduces the onus of risk and responsibility on the EA (Akkerman and Bakker  ; Kelly  ). For instance, partnerships with consistent communication had clear roles and responsibilities, such as authority for a person to act as the boundary‐crosser (Andreason  ; Flynn et al.  ), thereby creating structure in the partnership (Hays  ). However, the RLPs show how agency was also protected in the deliberate use of contextually responsive communication channels like phone, email, and private social media groups. In the UniHub program, agency was also enabled by an open‐door policy, which enabled the boundary‐crosser to establish consistent and accessible communication channels, which was vital to maintaining partnership roles and responsibilities. In contrast, the ASbA partnership suffered when nobody was identified as a coordinator who could operate across boundaries (Akkerman and Bakker  ).

4.2.4

## Opportunities for Exploring SWT Possibilities
SWT partnerships have the responsibility to extend EA's agency and opportunity as they pursue their SWT. EA transitioning beyond school are also exploring their futures (Wyn and White  ; Moshman  ), but many EA—especially those in RRR—experience a bounded agency in the SWT (Rudd and Evans  ; Corbett and Roberts  ) due to reduced access to opportunities and fewer available resources. Therefore, making possible SWT pathways visible to EA and connecting EA with options that are beyond their immediate horizon is essential (Lang et al.  ; Suchman  ). Within SWT partnerships, boundary‐crossers can fulfill this need by familiarizing EA with a range of SWT possibilities, to both demystify the SWT (see Creed et al.  ) and to enable EA to imagine pathways beyond their perceived and real limits (Rudd and Evans  ).
For early school leavers, opportunities to build confidence—too often undermined during their school experience—are essential to support meaningful transitions beyond school (Creed et al.  ; McGregor et al.  ; Mortimer et al.  ). For example, the RLPs initiative activities drew on participants' interests and skills to tailor opportunities. Such connections can make EA feel validated, thereby increasing the likelihood of subsequent success (McGregor et al.  ). This reflects the point made in previous research with EA (Bishop and Willis  ; Bryant and Ellard  ) that successful pathways are not only about instrumental outcomes (such as further study or work) but also about support for more deeply personal development, such as cultural identity as an Aboriginal person for participants of the ASbAs.
More traditionally, SWT initiatives scaffolded EA toward their preferred pathway through broadening their knowledge (see Grosemans et al.  ). The role of boundary‐crossers to broaden horizons is especially vital when school curriculum offers are shaped to suit local industry needs, therefore “acting as a kind of funnel” (Lang et al.  , p. 11). Focusing on possibilities, rather than being constrained by current probabilities, is core to a hopeful approach (Giroux  ; Te Riele  ). This illustrates the importance of supporting EA to explore and question their preferred work pathway (Grosemans et al.  ) and challenges assumptions that adulthood is a calculable destination (Wyn and White  , p. 96).
Deciding a postschool pathway is often an iterative process (Carabelli and Lyon  ), and EA value varied experiences (Te Riele  ). There is a danger of purporting an overly instrumentalist view of the SWT that does not value the exploratory and developmental context of the SWT (Ball et al.  ; Bryant and Ellard  ). Boundary‐crossers, then, can provide vital structure in SWT partnerships for EA (Te Riele  ).

4.3

## Study Strengths and Limitations
In this article, we aimed to extend thinking around EA's transitions to meaningful careers and further education by considering the support that can be provided by boundary‐crossing roles and boundary objects in SWT partnerships. Our analysis of three SWT partnerships highlights the disjuncture between school and postschool options and the essential role of boundary‐crosser work (see Akkerman and Bakker  ) in bridging these systems and supporting EA in their SWT. Our attention to the experiences of EA experiencing disadvantage is useful to other similar studies. However, the scope of this article is limited to indicative insights from only three initiatives in one Australian jurisdiction using a pragmatic design, and none of the data are longitudinal.
Future empirical work—such as impact evaluations with mixed method or quasiexperimental design—would offer and complement our qualitative investigation with quantitatively measurable insights into the impact of boundary‐crosser work in SWT partnerships (see Gopalan et al.  ). Future empirical work could also draw links between other demographic factors, like gender and socioeconomic status, and the influence of boundary‐crossers in pathway partnerships. As a result, the findings are not intended to be generalizable but rather to point to a constructive avenue both for practice (to recognize the role and potential benefits of boundary‐crossing work in partnerships) and for future empirical research on the impact of partnership programs on youth transition (to apply the lens of boundary‐crossing).

4.4

## Implications
Partnerships are vital because the SWT involves EA crossing several social and developmental boundaries: the shift from student to worker, accumulating capital, commencing a process of life course development, and a developmental transition to adulthood (Blokker et al.  ). Wrapped around these phases are the “boundary conditions” of the SWT: neighborhood context (i.e., socioeconomic status and remoteness), career barriers (demographic factors), participation in guidance programs, apprenticeships and internships, school support, and parental support (Blokker et al.  ).
Potential adverse life outcomes are associated with unsuccessful SWT (Ngai et al.  ). However, Helms Jørgensen et al. ( ) caution that conceptualizing EA as vulnerable individualizes social problems, rather than focusing on redressing policy to support transitions beyond school.
Boundary‐crosser work is a productive concept for rethinking the role of partnerships in redistributing the responsibility attributed to EA in the SWT. The three case studies illustrate how partnerships in pathways can demystify what might be beyond formal schooling by bridging young people with pathways. The diversity of the case study examples reflects the complexity of navigating the SWT as experienced by EA in the postpandemic world. They also show the value to EA of exploring various possibilities and interests rather than being funnelled into one pathway. The initiatives, UniHub, ASbAs, RLPs, are contextually responsive initiatives that meet the needs of the regions they are serving, highlighting how partnerships in postschool pathways need to be dynamic, contextual, and embedded (see Blokker et al.  ).

5

## Conclusion
The concept of boundary‐crossers is an opportunity to rethink what supports EA need in their SWT. Boundary objects—like places, adjustments to barriers to equity, and communication approaches—are some practical ways boundary‐crossing work is enabled. Effective boundary‐crosser work is supported by institutional backing but is enabled by the boundary‐crosser offering consistent support and guidance as led by the EA's interests and aspirations.
The benefit of boundary‐crosser partnerships is demystifying the SWT, which, in turn, reduces the onus of responsibility of mapping and navigating the SWT on the EA. While preparing young people for meaningful contributions beyond school is everyone's business, the risk and responsibility are not evenly distributed (Bell et al.  ). Pursuing postschool pathways can be a transformational learning experience for young people, with boundary‐crosser partnerships offering one fruitful approach.
There is, however, an inherent fragility to initiatives that support EA facing disadvantage, as they are commonly reliant on insecure funding and/or the enthusiasm of champions (Strategic Partners  ). An implication for policy, therefore, is to create systemic change, embedding such programs into continuing provision. For research, an implication is to draw on the lens of boundary‐crossing to examine—and provide evidence about—initiatives intended to support EA's SWT.

## Glossary
DECYP (Department for Education, Children and Young People)—The Tasmanian Government department responsible for delivering services and support across education, child safety, youth justice, and family wellbeing. DECYP oversees early childhood education through to Year 12, and works to ensure the safety and development of children and young people through integrated programs and strategic partnerships. Its mission is to improve outcomes for children and young people across Tasmania by fostering inclusive, safe, and supportive environments.
TasTAFE—Tasmania's leading provider of VET, formerly known as TAFE Tasmania. As a government‐owned institution, TasTAFE offers nationally accredited courses across a wide range of industries, including trades, health, business, hospitality, and creative arts. With campuses across the state, TasTAFE plays a vital role in workforce development, lifelong learning, and supporting individuals and communities through practical, career‐focused education.

5.2TAFE (Technical and Further Education)—A network of Australian government‐owned institutions offering VET. TAFE provides nationally accredited courses from Certificate I to Advanced Diploma, and in some cases, bachelor's and postgraduate qualifications. It emphasizes practical, career‐focused training across fields like trades, health, business, IT, and creative industries.
VET (Vocational Education and Training)—A nationally recognized system in Australia that provides practical, skills‐based education designed to prepare individuals for employment across a wide range of industries. VET is delivered by TAFE institutes, RTOs, and some universities, offering qualifications from Certificate I to Advanced Diploma. The system emphasizes hands‐on learning, industry relevance, and flexible pathways into the workforce or further study.
