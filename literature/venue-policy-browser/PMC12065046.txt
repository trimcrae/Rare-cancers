## Abstract
Iowa Learning Farms, based at Iowa State University since 2004, is a nationally recognized conservation outreach program that has successfully engaged with farmers and landowners to deliver information and provide tools and guidance encouraging the implementation of agricultural practices that enhance water quality, improve soil health and productivity, and contribute to building a Culture of Conservation in Iowa and beyond. Iowa Learning Farms programs all focus directly on reaching and engaging with farmers. The program's success comes from the processes employed to create, test, and deliver programs that serve demographic groups including traditional row crop, new or next‐generation, female, nontraditional crop, and livestock farmers, as well as tenants, landowners, and conservation professionals. Our programs include field days, webinars, Conservation Station trailers, rapid needs assessment and response workshops, Leadership Circle listening sessions, a youth education program, infographic‐style factsheets, and a Whole Farm Conservation Best Practices Manual, as well as a newsletter, blog, and social media presence. Our programs foster a collaborative relationship with farmer partners, agencies, the university, researchers, and the public. The development, feedback, and iterative process of creating and refining programmatic elements will be highlighted.

## Core Ideas

Evaluation is at the heart of successful outreach.
Bidirectional engagement and conversation are key to influencing conservation adoption.
Focus on science, innovation, and creativity drives effective delivery of conservation messages.

 In addition to these ongoing activities, ILF has developed documentation and resources to empower constituents to learn independently and prepare for discussions with landlords or tenants, and to help guide conservation decision‐making on the farm. We hope that other extension and outreach practitioners can draw on the programs, methodologies, and resources described to build new programs or strengthen existing programs on the foundation of our efforts in the field.
In pursuit of its mission to create a Culture of Conservation, ILF relies on some fundamental organizational tenets upon which its various programs have been built. These tenets include the incorporation of feedback and evaluation into every program, consistent and reliable delivery of current and relevant science‐ and research‐based information, and bidirectionally partnering with farmers—as well as local, regional, and national organizations, government agencies, experts, and influencers—to assure program effectiveness.
ILF comprises three ISU faculty, six professional and scientific staff, five advisers from ISU and various funding partners, and temporary staff including summer interns. This close working relationship with the university helps to ensure alignment with current research and can create opportunities for both researchers and farmers and landowners to participate in studies from pilot scale through field‐scale, multi‐year research projects.

## COMMUNICATIONS AND PROMOTION OF EVENTS
ILF has developed a standardized event promotion process that uses multiple channels and methods to cast a wide net that informs and reminds potential participants of events. The process is modified for some event types, but the standardization helps to streamline efforts as well as to prevent gaps in notifications. Alterations to this process are noted in the descriptions of specific programs, as the paper progresses.

Press release

(b)Distribution varies based on the type and location of the event, but press releases are primarily sent via direct email to a curated list of statewide media, local newspapers, radio and television outlets, county Farm Bureau offices, county ISU Extension and Outreach offices, and county Natural Resources Conservation Service (NRCS) offices. The ISU Extension and Outreach communications team often assists with distribution to extension offices and their list of media contacts.

4.Direct invitation

When appropriate, ILF team members invite participation through personal email or telephone outreach.

ILF maintains active and timely communication with its audience of followers, program supporters, students, educators, conservation professionals, farmers, and landowners. ## FEEDBACK AND EVALUATION
ILF places a high priority on gathering feedback from staff and participants for all outreach activities. Some evaluations are formal and include multiple stages, while others are conducted on an ad hoc basis. In addition, ILF has created specialized in‐person feedback sessions such as its Leadership Circle meetings and practice‐centric rapid needs assessment and response (RNR) workshops.
3.1

## Specialized feedback
3.1.1

## Leadership Circle
Established in 2016, the ILF Leadership Circle is a biennial event at which 20–25 farmer partners gather on the ISU campus to discuss conservation actions on their farms, learn about new research, and provide input on where to place emphases in future outreach activities and efforts. It also serves as a forum to raise and discuss crucial topics such as impediments to conservation adoption by peers and concerns with various agency programs. Participants raise name placards to indicate when they want to speak, and an active emcee moderates the meeting, helping to ensure that all voices have a chance to be heard and that the agenda progresses. Individually completed handouts, online polling, and group discussion help to facilitate participation.
An important aspect of these sessions is the inclusion of representatives from major agriculture and conservation agencies in the meeting as listeners, not presenters. Leadership Circle participants regularly provide unvarnished opinions and feedback directly to these listeners and to ILF regarding what is working and what could be done differently or better to encourage and accelerate conservation adoption.
For instance, in 2024, the Leadership Circle covered topics of discussion around sustainable intensification via a continuum of practices ranging from cover crops to perennial ground cover. While the ILF team proposed the term sustainable intensification, participants made it clear that this did not make sense or resonate with them. Participants discussed how sustainable intensification sounded like an academic term, did not sound grower friendly, and that intensification spoke to the notion of “How do we grow more corn and soybeans?” rather than emphasizing the clear need for conservation and diversification. The group also explored terms including conservation agriculture, sustainable agriculture, and regenerative agriculture, which all resonated much more readily with the participants. The term sustainable intensification was immediately retired following this event, serving as a key example of how ILF continually adapts its education and outreach approaches—in this case, language preferences—based on participant feedback and evaluation. Additional Leadership Circle discussion included tips for telling a better story that were based on recently published research (Witzling, Shaw, et al., ). The event concluded with a provided meal and networking among the farmer partners and agency partners.

3.1.2

## Rapid needs assessment and response workshops
The RNR technique is a useful tool for extension professionals seeking to improve workshop structure and obtain immediate feedback. Facilitators using the RNR technique organize workshop participants into small groups, then rotate the groups through a series of stations where participants discuss questions central to the workshop topic. The technique helps adult learners actively engage with one another, improving their ability to absorb the subject matter. Use of the RNR technique also allows facilitators to understand participants' existing knowledge of a workshop topic and tailor the session to specific questions and needs (Comito et al., ).
For example, ILF implemented the RNR technique at six workshops in 2024 to gain insights into farmer perspectives about relay intercropping, an emerging farming practice in which small grains often used as cover crops become a third crop in the more common 2‐year soybean and corn rotation. Hosting nearly 100 farmers and landowners in total, the workshops were designed to gather data including goals, perceived barriers to adoption, availability of local resources, soil health effects, and water quality benefits of relay intercropping. Researchers noted common concerns across all workshop sessions that would inform the creation of ILF educational and outreach programming and materials. Employing the RNR process also reinforced the ILF commitment to listening to all stakeholders when evaluating and discussing new ideas and approaches to conservation farming, soil health, and water quality.
The RNR process has been used successfully to gather input and coalesce key concerns from farmers in other areas as well. In seeking to better understand slow cover‐crop adoption, the RNR process revealed a significant difference in perspectives between agronomists and farmers regarding barriers to adoption—crucial for ILF in developing both language and approach for outreach materials and resources.

3.2

## Iowa learning farms approach to evaluation
Evaluation is at the heart of successful outreach. A well‐planned and ‐executed evaluation strategy allows ILF to understand not only who is attending outreach events, but also how they are thinking about, responding to, and basing conservation‐related decisions on the information exchange and dialogue that happens both during and after the event. Placing an emphasis on evaluation provides the ILF team with regular and correlated feedback on content, presentation, format, and impact of events and resources—as well as participant demographics—allowing for continual improvement in the effectiveness of outreach efforts.
The ILF evaluation process for formal outreach events grows from a standardized five‐stage approach. This baseline approach is used for most in‐person farmer outreach programs, such as field days, but is adjusted in timing, format, and content to specifically suit other outreach events, such as virtual field days and webinars. This standardization provides opportunities for cross‐correlation of gathered data within the current year as well as longitudinally.
For community engagements at fairs and other less formal events, attending staff discuss and provide feedback to gauge visit impact and engagement, noting what went well, what could be improved, and questions asked during the event.

3.3

## Multistage evaluation strategy
Alterations to the standardized stages below are highlighted in various specific program descriptions throughout the paper.
Event evaluations

ILF team members complete these forms at every event to help the organization understand the audience's level of engagement, document the questions that were asked by participants, and help to improve future outreach activities.
2.Comment cards

These cards are distributed to all participants at each in‐person event. The completed cards request mailing contact information, how they heard about the event, and their interest in conservation practices, for future follow‐up by ILF or agency partners.
3.Demographic cards

All attendees are asked to complete these cards to provide ILF with a demographic snapshot of the group—ages, genders, roles in agriculture, and information about their farming operations. The cards also ask about preferences on event timing and topics of interest for future outreach events.
4.Follow‐up evaluations

These evaluations are mailed or emailed to participants within 3 weeks after the event. The questions focus on the clarity and accessibility of the information presented and whether participants plan to make any land‐management changes as a result of the event.
5.January evaluations

This annual evaluation is mailed or emailed only to farmers and landowners. The survey covers a broad range of topics regarding practice implementation and other data points used by ILF to evaluate impacts and enhance programming.

## BIDIRECTIONAL PARTNERING WITH FARMERS
4.1

## Focus on farmer engagement
Committed to farmer focus in its activities, ILF places a high priority on regular and impactful engagement with farmers and landowners. Understanding that not all farmers own their land, and that many landowners may be absent and often removed from everyday decision‐making regarding farm management practices, ILF crafts messages, materials, and outreach programs to address the full gamut of participants in the farming enterprise. Bidirectional engagement and conversations are at the foundation of successfully influencing the adoption of conservation practices. These conversations most often take place in structured events such as field days, RNR workshops, and biennial Leadership Circle listening sessions.
Contributing hand in hand to the effectiveness and reach of these conversations is the influence of personal networking by constituents. Farmers participating in ILF programs are encouraged to share what they have learned with peers and family members, and networking exerts a strong influence on participation in activities such as field days. In the period of 2019–2023, excluding 2020′s virtual‐only events, participants in ILF field days largely ranked word‐of‐mouth as the source of information for learning about the event (Figure  ). Such organic networking and promotion have been shown to extend the reach of ILF by as much as 68%.
 
4.2

## Farmer focused
The fundamental goal of ILF from its beginnings has been to build a Culture of Conservation in Iowa and beyond by encouraging the adoption of conservation practices. In pursuit of this goal, ILF has regularly enlisted input and feedback from farmers, landowners, researchers, and members of its multidisciplinary team to identify and support the implementation of best management practices that improve water quality and soil health while maintaining profitability for farming operations. Furthering its goal of promoting farmer‐to‐farmer communication, ILF places a strong emphasis on events that support active listening and participation from all participants.
ILF has built a statewide network of farmer partners who serve as local change agents, leading by example and advocating with other area farmers and landowners. Through field days and workshops held across the state, as well as virtual offerings of field days and weekly webinars, ILF strives to offer access to current conservation, water quality, and soil health information.

## DELIVERY OF SCIENCE‐ AND RESEARCH‐BASED INFORMATION
5.1

## Research and science
Through its relationship with ISU, ILF not only has access to ongoing research efforts by faculty and students of the university, but also often participates in and facilitates research with farmer partners who have committed to helping the organization pursue its mission. This close‐knit relationship is further strengthened by the crossover of extension and teaching faculty who also participate in ILF outreach.

5.2

## In‐person field days
ILF's in‐person field days are 2‐h events including a meal and a diversity of speakers, integrating local farmers who are using the featured conservation practices. These in‐person field days provide localized opportunities for education, networking, and idea‐sharing among neighbors. In keeping with the ILF goal of encouraging farmer‐to‐farmer dialogue, the host farmer or an invited farmer is a primary presenter in the formal field day program—building a collegial atmosphere and avoiding simply being another group of university‐based experts talking “at” the farming community.
Since its inception, ILF has produced 368 field days, drawing 16,910 participants (Figure  ). These events have been highly rated for quality of presentation and content, with 95% of attendees rating the field days as excellent or good. Attendees who reported attending multiple field days also were more likely to implement conservation practices such as planting cover crops.
 The ILF Conservation Station trailers are an important support tool for field days. The trailers are equipped with demonstration platforms, sound amplification equipment, and furniture that can be used during a field day program.
All ILF field days also include a meal before or after the formal program. This time is often used by participants for personal networking, questions, and interactions with peers and presenters about the field day topics, and also helps extension professionals build rapport with the attendees.
5.2.1

## The field day success loop
Through examination of evaluation data gathered at in‐person field days over some 20 years, ILF has developed and annually tracks the influence of field day participation on personal networking by farmers, and on their propensities to expand current or adopt new practices on their farms.
ILF has formulated a successful method for conducting field days that has led to a set of progressive impacts we refer to as the “field day success loop.” By drawing on the multifaceted approach to evaluation that contributes to the success loop, extension professionals can strengthen their own field days and track the long‐term impact of farmers' influence on their peers regarding implementing conservation practices (Comito et al., ). Tracking such data aids in fine‐tuning field days to increase influence on adoption as well as to ensure participant needs are being met.
The field day success loop depicting 2023 event outcomes shows that a large majority of attendees highly rated the program and reinforced the findings that attendance at multiple field days has a strong correlation with follow‐through in adopting conservation practices such as planting cover crops (Figure  ).
 
5.2.2

## The Field Day Toolkit
To facilitate the consistent design, promotion, execution, and evaluation of highly effective field days with limited stress and reinvention, ILF has developed the Field Day Toolkit, which provides detailed, step‐by‐step guidance and a complete set of sample materials. Extension practitioners can freely adapt the toolkit materials and methods for local relevance based on their experiences. The Field Day Toolkit is available for download at https://www.iowalearningfarms.org/resources‐1.
Since its publication in 2022, the Field Day Toolkit has been viewed on the ILF website more than 300 times. It has also been shared with extension professionals across the country via service and professional development events, at the 2023 National Nonpoint Source Training Workshop, and with the Soil and Water Conservation Society's 2023–2024 Emerging Leaders Program.
The Field Day Toolkit is a product of ILF's many years of planning and hosting field day events. While ILF uses many different outreach approaches, the importance of farmer‐to‐farmer interactions cannot be overstated. Field days do matter. Field days and workshops need to be well planned and executed to be successful. One key to success is keeping each field day focused on no more than two conservation practices. We encourage the use of the toolkit checklists, sample promotional materials, and evaluation materials (Figure  ) as guidelines for planning successful conservation outreach events.
 A particular strength of ILF is responding to feedback from event participants and staff and then making changes to improve future program delivery. Every best practice recommendation included in the Field Day Toolkit has been refined over years of listening to farmers and other attendees. For example, staff observation and listening to farmer grumblings made it clear that we needed to offer comfortable seating, tables, portable restrooms, and shade at field days. Engagement and learning is better facilitated when participants’ physical comfort is addressed. Both visual and audio considerations are important to ensure information is accessible to all. With this in mind, we discourage presenters from the use of posters in the field (text is often too small for participants to read, and posters blow away in the wind), limit the number and type of handouts, and require speakers to use a portable public address system. Finally, we collected 3 years of evaluation data to assess desired length of events and preferred days of the week. This led to field days being no longer than 2 h, featuring a free meal, and targeting Tuesdays, Wednesdays, and Thursdays as the preferred days.

5.3

## Virtual field days
In 2020, ILF launched its Virtual Field Day program in response to restrictions on travel and in‐person gatherings during the COVID‐19 pandemic (Comito et al., ). The demand for and frequency of virtual field days have declined over time as in‐person events have ramped back up. However, there are still topics of interest that draw audiences from across Iowa and beyond, making these online educational offerings a valuable component to the ILF toolset, particularly during the winter months when outdoor field experiences are not possible.
Among the ongoing benefits of the Virtual Field Day program is that it enables participants to attend multiple events without the cost or disruption caused by travel. In 2023, for example, average attendance at virtual events was 147, compared to an average of 42 at in‐person field days.
Virtual field days also draw consistently different attendee demographics from in‐person field days. The virtual events have drawn younger audiences, with the average age approaching 50—compared to an average age of 54 at in‐person field days in 2023, for example. In addition, the percentage of women attending has been consistently higher, ranging from 51% to as high as 66%, versus a range of 25%–32% at in‐person field days over the same period. The virtual programs have helped ILF move toward two of its key objectives—to reach and influence greater numbers of female farmers and younger farmers.
The Virtual Field Day program elicited excellent marks from participants over 4 years, with good or excellent ratings for both content and presenters exceeding 90% every year.
Past work (Comito et al., ) has supported the idea that attending multiple field days increases the likelihood of adoption of conservation practices. Based on a separate survey of previous in‐person and virtual field day participants (Witzling, Williams, et al., ), we explored additional differences and preferences between the groups. Participants who attended both in‐person and virtual events were likely to serve as opinion leaders and to be actively engaged in seeking and sharing information with others. Both virtual and in‐person field days will continue every year as we work to expand access to conservation information across Iowa to more farmers and landowners.
5.3.1

## Virtual field day format
Virtual field days are 1‐h events hosted on Zoom that feature a video from a field site and a live question and answer session with the presenters. Participants are asked to either unmute to ask their question or type their question directly to the host. An ILF staff member acts as the host of the event, providing background information on the program and the topic of the day and relaying questions from the chat to the presenters. Virtual field days are recorded and uploaded to YouTube following the event, which allows for tracking of archival views. Captions for the videos are generated within the YouTube platform to improve accessibility.

5.3.2

## Virtual field day promotion and evaluation methodology
Adjustments to the ILF standardized promotion plan include not sending promotional flyers and postcards as we would for in‐person field days, but instead promotion is done through ILF E‐news, blog, and social media, similar to webinar promotion (see below). A multistep approach to evaluation follows the standardized evaluation process, but rather than using comment cards, the same information is gathered through the registration portal, and demographic information is gathered through the follow‐up evaluation process. Follow‐up evaluations are emailed to the Zoom‐generated list 2–3 h after the event, and a reminder is sent out 2–3 days later. If the response rate is low, additional reminders are sent out. The evaluation email also contains a link to the virtual field day archive.

5.4

## Conservation Webinar Series
Initiated in 2011, the ILF Conservation Webinar Series is designed to provide attendees with a tightly focused, 45‐min exposure to topics including ongoing research, published research results, emerging practices, historical perspectives, and myriad conservation and water quality subjects. More than 30 different conservation‐related topics have been covered in the series, ranging from cover crops to edge‐of‐field practices and social science to wildlife. In March 2020, the webinar series transitioned from monthly to weekly frequency. Primarily related to pandemic impacts, the new weekly series has proven to be very successful—average attendance at the webinars has exceeded 100 per week in recent years (Figure  ). Since it began, the Conservation Webinar Series has aired 328 episodes, garnering more than 82,366 combined live and archived views, with 97% of attendees rating the webinars as excellent or good.
 5.4.1

## Webinar planning and process
Webinars are hosted each Wednesday at 12 p.m. Central Time using Zoom Webinar. A set of first and last slides is provided to the speaker the Monday before their scheduled presentation to provide a uniform look for the series, as well as to provide participants with information for submitting certified crop adviser continuing education unit requests, and to promote the upcoming webinar. Following a brief introduction by an ILF moderator, the speaker shares a 25‐ to 30‐min presentation. Participants are encouraged to submit questions via the chat box to the moderator, who reviews the questions and asks them aloud of the presenter at the conclusion of the presentation. This method provides archive viewers an opportunity to hear the questions, as they do not have access to the chat feature to view submitted questions. Captions are added to each recorded webinar, and then it is archived on Vimeo. Responding to feedback from farmer partners, in 2023 ILF began posting webinar recordings on YouTube, where they are available in playlists by both year and topic. Recorded webinars are also readily available on the ILF website.
Presenters on ILF webinars have included research and teaching faculty from ISU and many other institutions, graduates from ISU programs who have carried on with careers elsewhere, graduate students and postdoc researchers, local and state agency specialists, farmers, and other conservation professionals. The key to speaker success is providing a precise description of what is expected, regular follow‐up prior to the webinar, and support during the webinar. In addition, obtaining commitments about 1 month before the requested presentation date provides sufficient preparation time yet keeps the commitment current in the mind of the guest presenter.
Prospective speakers are asked to answer a set of simple questions that provide information for promotional materials—their name and title as they would like it to be published, a brief bio, the session title and brief synopsis of the content, and—crucially—why the topic is important and what they would like the audience to take away from the session.

5.4.2

## Webinar promotion and evaluation methodology
Because webinars are virtual, the promotion approach deviates somewhat from the standardized promotion plan. Webinar press releases are distributed 1 week before the event because of the weekly webinar production cadence. The press releases are provided to the presenters, enabling them to magnify reach by sharing the announcement with their colleagues, students, and other contacts. Press releases also tease upcoming sessions when the information is available. Rather than promotional flyers and postcards, the more immediate ILF website, blog, and social media are used for webinar promotion. A blog about each webinar is posted to the ILF website each Tuesday, and an email notice is distributed on the morning of the webinar. A recap blog is posted the Monday following each webinar, providing another opportunity to promote archival views of the webinar. In addition, upcoming webinars are promoted through ILF social media accounts each Friday afternoon prior to the webinar.
Since 2021, ILF has distributed an annual online evaluation questionnaire to all webinar participants to gain an understanding of the webinar audience and the effectiveness of the weekly programs. This questionnaire uses Qualtrics, which automatically sends up to six reminders to recipients who have not responded. Basic email contact information is collected when a party joins a webinar. This pre‐registration information provides the solicitation list for the evaluation.

5.5

## Conservation On Tap
Seeking to engage with a broader audience, including young and prospective farmers, female farmers, and other participants in the farm economy, ILF launched its Conservation On Tap program in 2022. Conservation On Tap offers a lightly structured program and opportunities for large‐ and small‐group conversations between ILF team members and attendees on topics of personal or local interest. The format grew from three sessions hosting 60 participants in 2022 to seven events and 226 participants in 2023.
Conservation On Tap programs are 1‐h events held at local breweries and distilleries. Each session features a member of ILF and a local representative to discuss the importance of water quality in the brewing process. These in‐person events provide localized opportunities for rural and urban residents to discuss, network, and share ideas among neighbors with the common goal of improved water quality.
This is another program (along with virtual field days) that has helped ILF progress toward its long‐term goals to engage female farmers and younger farmers. Women represented 42% of participants at Conservation On Tap events in 2023—12% more than at in‐person field days. Of female attendees, 28% are landowners, 9% are farmers, 2% indicated they are new to farming, and 7% noted they would like to farm. Participants aged 50 or younger made up 61% of Conservation On Tap events in 2023, with an average age of 48‐years‐old (versus the 54 average at in‐person field days). Conservation On Tap was highly rated by participants both in overall quality and likelihood to participate in another similar event (Figure  ).
 5.5.1

## Conservation On Tap promotion and evaluation methodologies
The multiple‐channel approach in ILF's standardized promotion plan has been fully utilized for this program, everything from postcards to blogs, and evaluation data are gathered through application of the standardized evaluation process used for all in‐person farmer events including formal mailings and staff evaluation.

5.6

## Conservation Station trailers
The ILF Conservation Station trailers are a fleet of mobile learning centers that offer a platform for delivering conservation education to audiences of all ages and backgrounds (Figure ). The first Conservation Station trailer, rolled out in 2007, was equipped with the program's original rainfall simulator. This early version of the trailer was used primarily at field days, but also made appearances at farmers markets and county fairs.
 Based on demand and feedback from farmers and agency partners looking to meet the need for increasing outreach on additional conservation practices, the fleet has grown and evolved to include four trailers: two featuring rainfall simulators, one with bioreactor and saturated buffer models, and one focused on wetlands.
The highly adaptive Conservation Stations provide a venue for innovative learning activities incorporating emerging research developments and educational opportunities relevant to Iowa. Each trailer houses a unique combination of highly visual and interactive Iowa‐centric demonstrations that delve into the impacts of land management choices, both urban and agricultural, on water quality and the connections between our state's water, soil, and wildlife.
Available at no cost for field days, community celebrations and festivals, fairs, and farmers markets, Conservation Station appearances include audience‐focused programming ranging from games and activities to demonstrations showing rainfall on in‐field land management scenarios, wetlands’ impact on water quality and wildlife, and edge‐of‐field practices. Since 2007, the Conservation Station trailer fleet has traveled to 1132 events and has hosted 140,197 visitors (Figure  ).
 5.6.1

## Conservation station evaluation methodology
Visitors to the Conservation Stations are encouraged to explore the various informational displays and ask questions of the staff. Consistent with all ILF events, staff completes an event evaluation, noting attendance, what went well, what could be improved, questions asked, and engagement with the activities.

5.6.2

## Conservation station features and application
Through engineering, art, and science, the Conservation Stations create an immersive experience inspiring visitors to care about natural resources—specifically water and soil—and share ways that these resources can and should be protected. Each Conservation Station includes a unique combination of the following features:
Learning lab: It includes walk‐through interior trailer display featuring audio/visual elements, creating an immersive sensory experience, and floor‐to‐ceiling original artwork to engage visitors in dialogue about the beauty and functionality of conservation.
Field day in a box: It includes everything needed to host on‐site field days with farmers and landowners—infographics, fact sheets, tables, chairs, sound amplification equipment, and food service supplies.
Rainfall simulator: It includes visual demonstration of how land management practices impact erosion, water quality, and hydrology using simulated rainfall on five different agricultural and urban land surfaces.
Wetland models: It include three‐dimensional (3‐D) museum‐quality models demonstrating the unique landscape placement and hydrology of three different Iowa wetland types.
Wetlands PLINKO: It includes an interactive game that engages learning about the benefits of wetlands.
Bioreactor and saturated buffer models: It includes 3‐D models demonstrating the function and capabilities of woodchip bioreactor and saturated buffer edge‐of‐field conservation practices.
Watershed game: It includes hands‐on, interactive tabletop game visually illustrating a watershed and how common pollutants move across the land, impacting nearby water bodies.
Poo toss game: It includes hands‐on interactive game about pet waste cleanup for all ages.

5.7

## Water rocks!
Emerging out of the farmer‐ and community‐focused outreach of ILF and the growing demand for building awareness and instilling enthusiasm for nature, water, and conservation among Iowa's young people, the youth conservation‐education program Water Rocks! was launched in 2012. Combining sound science with music and the arts, Water Rocks! has reached more than 200,000 Iowa youth through its signature school visits. Each of these in‐demand visits features a high‐energy, science‐based lesson involving interactive activities, accessible action items, and on‐the‐ground student evaluation. Along with other educational outreach offerings including Water Rocks! music videos, ILF Conservation Station trailers, and the Water Rocks! Summit training workshops, the program is helping the next generation of Iowans understand and advocate for clean water, better conservation practices, and the long‐term resiliency of the land.

5.8

## Literature and resource materials
ILF produces a wide range of accessible resource materials to reinforce learning at outreach events and support conservation‐based decision‐making, including popular infographic‐style fact sheets for various conservation practices. In addition, ILF has collaborated with researchers and other organizations to develop handbooks, pamphlets, and research reports that can provide essential guidance and information to farmers, landowners, and other stakeholders with an interest in conservation, agriculture, and water quality.
5.8.1

## Whole Farm Conservation Best Practices Manual—second edition
Working in collaboration with NRCS and other conservation‐focused organizations, ILF published the second edition of the Whole Farm Conservation Best Practices Manual in 2022. To date, there have been 14,129 copies of the manual distributed and more than 5600 downloads. The manual includes detailed information about 20 individual conservation practices and step‐by‐step decision worksheets to aid farmers and landowners in determining the best practices to meet their goals. Designed primarily for farmers just starting to adopt conservation practices through the first 3 years of the process, the manual provides a broad range of information that could be beneficial to any farm producer or landowner. The manual is available for free download or in hard copy from the ISU Extension Store at https://store.extension.iastate.edu/Product/15823.

5.8.2

## Guides for tenants and landlords
ILF has created two sets of infographic guides to facilitate conversations between tenants and landlords about implementing conservation practices on leased lands. Since the long‐term benefits of conservation practices can be seen as primarily to the landowner, it is important to offer recommendations from both sides of the conversation. For landlords seeking to encourage tenants to implement cover crops, reduced tillage, or modified land use approaches, the Talking With Your Tenant (https://www.iowalearningfarms.org/resources/talking‐with‐your‐landlord‐series?rq=talking%20with%20your%20landlord) series provides a series of pamphlets that can help guide conversations about practice adoption. For tenants seeking to improve soil health, reduce nutrient losses, or contribute to improvements in water quality, the Talking With Your Landlord (https://www.iowalearningfarms.org/resources/talking‐with‐your‐landlord‐series?rq=talking%20with%20your%20landlord) series provides documents that can help facilitate effective conversations. In addition, the series includes advice on communicating with landlords, as well as guides for creating and discussing the financial benefits of practice implementation.

5.8.3

## Sustainable business templates
Created to assist young and emerging farmers with developing a business plan for their operation, the ILF Sustainable Business Templates (https://www.iowalearningfarms.org/resources/sustainable‐business‐templates) series includes business planning documents and templates as well as a model for creating a profitable farming business. The model provides descriptions and guidance on how to capture, create, and deliver value for a customer, and ultimately how to generate revenues and profits.

5.8.4

## Water quality infographic series
ILF continues to publish Water Quality Infographics (https://www.iowalearningfarms.org/water‐quality‐infographic‐series) related to water quality activities, policies, and progress. These downloadable guides include detailed definitions of key processes and topics in the “A Closer Look” series, as well as fact sheets about strategic initiatives that can facilitate education, self‐study, and research.

## PARTNERING IS A HIDDEN STRENGTH OF IOWA LEARNING FARMS
ILF identifies three types of partners that contribute to its success. The essential partners in ILF are farmers—those who participate in events and consume ILF information and publications, as well as those who have a formal relationship with the program, known as farmer partners. Farmer partners agree to implement specific conservation practices on their farms. They also frequently participate in research projects and other activities in support of ILF objectives. Some farmer partners have been active with ILF from its inception. The current roster of farmer partners includes 103 farmers in 61 counties.
In addition to partnering with farmers, ILF is a grant‐funded program that has secured operational funding through multiple public and private organizations. Grant opportunities allow ILF to leverage its wealth of data, strong relationships with researchers and farming communities, and expertise in extension activities.
ILF's primary funding partners include state and federal agencies, nongovernmental organizations, industry, and competitive grants. ILF also partners with commodity organizations, water quality and nutrient loss reduction organizations, and farmer organizations.

## IMPACT ON CONSERVATION
The impact of the ILF field days, farmer partner network, and webinars is well documented in our annual evaluation report. Through 20 years of evaluation, we have learned that field days are essential to conservation adoption, as participants are attending them at different points in their decision‐making process, seeking to gain the knowledge and support necessary to implement conservation practices—illustrated in the Field Day Success Loop.
Through networking during and after field days, participants are furthering their understanding of the practices and sharing their insight with neighbors, enlarging the reach of field days beyond those who attended.
While growth of no‐till, strip‐till, and cover crops among field day participants is difficult to track, we do know that attendees are cumulatively reporting an average of 4500 new acres of no‐till and strip‐till per year and 3500 new acres of cover crops per year.
The impact of our farmer partner network is also widely recognized as we work to enhance the group's leadership skills both within their communities and beyond. Many of our farmer partners are called up as speakers for non‐ILF field days as well as for conferences. At least one‐third of our farmer partners serve or have served as elected commissioners with their Soil and Water Conservation Districts.
Finally, our weekly Conservation Webinar Series has had a significant impact on expanding knowledge and understanding of conservation topics among farmers, landowners, and government agency staff, aiding in conservation adoption, with 98% of participants reporting that they gained new information from the webinar. It is the cumulative impact of all our events and activities that has led to the overall success of this long‐running Cooperative Extension program.

## CONCLUSIONS AND ADVICE FOR EXTENSION AND OUTREACH PROFESSIONALS
When adopting a new practice or routine, it is often advised to start small to set the stage for long‐term success. That advice has helped ILF continue to be a leading voice in conservation, water quality, and soil health education and outreach in Iowa. In 2004, a big idea led by a small team at ISU and 31 farmer partners utilizing on‐farm conservation demonstrations launched—adapting, evolving, and growing over the years into the award‐winning program that exists today. Building a team with a wide variety of talents and backgrounds, as well as building trust amongst farmers, landowners, conservation professionals and agency partners can take time, much like measuring improvements in soil health and water quality.
Through consistent evaluation, as well as accepting and incorporating feedback from farmers, landowners, and agency partners, ILF has grown to more than 100 farmer partners, expanded the Conservation Station from a single trailer to a fleet of four unique and highly interactive trailers, and developed a system for hosting well‐attended weekly webinars and more than 30 field days a year. The strength of ILF is to not only refine existing programs, but to look ahead and identify future needs and meet them with innovative and well‐developed education and outreach opportunities.
