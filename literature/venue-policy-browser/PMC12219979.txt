## Abstract

## Background
Most research on the healthy migrant effect (HME) of migrants and the reduction in such health advantages due to long-term residence has been conducted in countries with a high proportion of migrants. However, in Japan, where there is a low migrant population of 2.0% and a low Migrant Integration Policy Index (MIPEX) rating, only limited health data on migrants is available from official statistics, and no residence length data is available. Therefore, health indicators in studies on the HME in Japan are limited to the cause of death or mortality, and residence length data are estimated from migration historical background or surrogates from age. The study aimed to compare the health status of Thai women residing in Japan with that of Japanese women and clarify the health transition of Thai women due to long-term residence in multiple health indicators.

## Methods
We conducted a cross-sectional study using self-administered questionnaires among Thai residents in Japan aged 20 years or older, assessing their health outcomes, such as subjective symptoms, and health behaviors, such as smoking. Chi-square or binomial tests were performed to compare the health outcomes and behaviors of the Thai migrants during the early stages of migration and the subsequent long-term residence with those of the Japanese population.

## Results
HME was observed only among Thai women aged 20–39 years who had resided in Japan for less than 10 years, with a lower prevalence of subjective symptoms compared to Japanese women in the same age group. HME was not observed in middle-aged women with less than 10 years of residence. Long-term residents exhibited poorer health outcomes and behaviors than Japanese women across most age-groups, despite undergoing more frequent health examinations. Statistically significant differences were particularly noted among middle-aged women.

## Conclusions
These results indicate that the duration of residence is associated with migrant health, with longer stays in Japan linked to a decline in health among migrants, thereby suggesting the need to collect migrant health data in official statistics and position migrants as targets for primary and secondary prevention in public health.

## Introduction
The healthy migrant effect is a phenomenon in which international migrants have better health status than nationals of the destination country, especially during the early migration period [ – ]. Some researchers have argued that this health advantage for migrants does not exist [ ], while others argue that it varies by age, gender, place of birth, and country of destination [ – ]. However, one large meta-analysis conducted in recent years demonstrated that mortality rates among international migrants in high-income countries tended to be lower than those of the general population of those countries [ ]. Other studies have demonstrated that the healthy migrant effect decreases or disappears as the period of residence increases and acculturation occurs, then the health of migrants is assimilated to the same level as the nationals of the destination country or worse [ , – ]. Trends in these health transformations also vary by gender, age, health indicators, migrants’ nativity, and destination country [ , , ].
However, most studies on the health effects of international migrants and their health transformation have been conducted in North America, Europe, and Oceania, where migrants account for more than 10% of the population. Migrants in Japan account for only 2.0% of the population [ , ], and the OECD/EU classifies Japan as an “emerging country with a small migrant population” with little available information on migrants [ ]. Health data on migrants is not available from official Japanese statistics, except for deaths, stillbirths, and infectious diseases such as tuberculosis and AIDS [ ]. The same is true for data on length of residence. Consequently, the health indicators used in the few studies of the HME in Japan to date are limited to causes of death or mortality and the duration of migration is estimated based on the historical background of migration to Japan [ , ] or age is used as a proxy indicator [ , ]. Research on the HME and health transformation in Japan is still in its infancy, and evidence is insufficient.
The Japanese government has not officially approved the acceptance of migrants, and measures to encourage migrants to settle there are insufficient [ ]. In the comprehensive migration policy index MIPEX, countries with large migrant populations in North America, Europe, and Oceania are rated as “Favorable” or “Slightly favorable,” while Japan is rated as “Immigration without integration (Halfway unfavorable)” [ ]. Research has demonstrated that the health risks of migrants decrease as the proportion of migrants in the local population increases [ ], elucidating the health and health transformation of migrants living in a country with a low proportion of migrants, such as Japan, is an important public health issue.
The purpose of this study was to preliminarily clarify the health status of migrants living in Japan at the early stages of their migration and the trends in health changes that occur with subsequent long-term residence by comparing the health status of Thai migrant women with that of Japanese women.
Although Thai nationals constitute only 1.87% of the total foreign population in Japan (approximately 54,000 people), they have a history of residing in Japan for the past 40 years beginning in the 1980s, when the foreign population in Japan began to increase [ ]. This long-term residency qualifies them as an appropriate group for examining health changes associated with prolonged stay. Previous studies have revealed that the rate of chronic diseases among Thai people living in Japan is 41.8% (n = 28) [ ], and that long-term residents are more likely to suffer from chronic diseases, while short-term residents are more likely to suffer from trauma [ ]. While these studies provide preliminary insight into the health status of Thai immigrants in Japan—a subject that remains relatively underexplored—the small sample sizes (n = 74 and n = 67) limit the reliability of the findings. Furthermore, the latter study's data were derived from patient records requiring emergency medical assistance from the Royal Thai Embassy in Tokyo between 2004 and 2020, which may not accurately represent the current Thai migrant population in Japan.
We excluded male respondents whose responses were limited to 118, which is insufficient for the analysis.

## Methods

## Study design, participants, survey location, and investigation period
A cross-sectional study was conducted using a self-administered questionnaire. The subjects were Thai men and women aged 20 years or older residing in Japan who visited the on-site venues of the mobile consular service organized by the Royal Thai Embassy. The mobile consular service is conducted once a year in each location in approximately ten prefectures throughout Japan, where many Thai people live in Japan. The surveys were conducted over four years from 2015 to 2019 (excluding 2017), and annual surveys were carried out in four prefectures with particularly large Thai populations: Tochigi, Ibaraki, Nagano, and Chiba. Although a total of 16 surveys were initially planned, no survey was conducted in the Ibaraki Prefecture in 2016, resulting in 15 rounds of surveys. Furthermore, five additional surveys were administered in four other prefectures, including a suburb of Tokyo. Recruitment and data collection began on May 31, 2015, and ended on November 3, 2019.

## Data collection
Survey assistants who were either Thais who had lived in Japan for over 20 years and had been volunteering in Japan for many years or Thai students attending graduate schools in Japan collected the data. A preliminary meeting was held with the survey assistants to confirm the purpose of the study, the criteria for selecting participants, and the procedures for obtaining informed consent and data collection using a research plan and procedure written in Thai. A procedure hard-copy manual written in Thai was used in the preliminary meeting. Three to five survey assistants at each survey venue provided informed consent to the Thai visitors. They presented the research plan written in Thai and explained the purpose and content of the study in Thai. They also explained that they did not have to answer questions they did not want to answer, that they could stop answering at any time, that it was entirely up to the individual whether or not to agree to the survey and answer the questionnaire, that there would be no disadvantage if they did not answer, and that answering the questionnaire would be considered as consent to the survey.
It was also explained that the information and data obtained would not be given to anyone other than those involved in the survey. However, they added that the data may be entrusted to a third party to enter it into a computer, but even in that case, any information that could identify individuals would not be given to the third party. Finally, they added that the data will be presented as numerical values ​​at conferences and in papers, so individuals will not be identified. After the research assistant confirmed the participants’ verbal consent, they handed out self-administered questionnaires written in Thai only to those who provided informed consent. The research assistant further asked participants to choose whether to fill out the questionnaire themselves or have the survey assistants fill it out. As the survey was anonymous, consent was recorded by collecting the completed anonymous questionnaires. The number of people who did not consent was also recorded. The number of refusals was also recorded. Data collection was concluded after finding that the mobile consular service had closed and there were no more Thai visitors to the venue.
The respondents’ basic attributes, including age, residential status, marital status, nationality, country of birth, period of residence, final education, country of final education, confidence in daily conversation in Japanese, and family economic status, were collected through the surveys. For health outcome measures, the respondents were asked about whether they had subjective symptoms due to diseases or injury in the past few days, their self-rated health at present (5-stage rating), a feeling of rest through sleep in the past one month (4-stage rating), and their height and weight to obtain BMI (body mass index). Health behavior indicators included frequency of smoking and alcohol consumption, number of exercise days per week, whether they had breakfast this morning, whether they had any diseases or injuries that they were undergoing treatment for, whether they were covered by public medical insurance, and whether they had had a periodic health examination in the past year.
To presume the approximate number of visitors on the day of the survey, we asked Thai visitors whether the purpose of their visit was to complete their own consular procedures, come as a companion, or for other purposes, and we also confirmed with the Thai Embassy how many people completed consular procedures on the day. Furthermore, to exclude duplicate respondents over multiple years, we asked the second and subsequent surveys whether they had previously responded to the survey.

## Data analysis, definition, and interpretation of health indicators
A univariate analysis was performed on the basic attributes of the respondents, health outcome indicators, and health behavior indicators. The data items targeted for the analysis were collected annually for the entire four-year survey period. Country of birth data were collected for one year (2019) but were included in the analysis as they were relevant to data interpretation. To adjust for the confounding effect of age, age was divided into three groups: 20–39 years, 40–49 years, and 50–69 years. For each age group, the period of residing in Japan was divided into four groups: less than 10 years, 10 to less than 20 years, 20 to less than 30 years, and 30 years or more.
We then calculated the number and proportion of people who fit the health outcome and health behavior indicators for each category and compared them with the proportion of Japanese women in the same age group. Data for Japanese women regarding subjective symptoms, self-rated health, frequency of smoking and alcohol consumption, feeling of rest through sleep, and health examinations were obtained from the Comprehensive Survey of Living Conditions (2016). Body mass index (BMI) was obtained from the National Health and Nutrition Survey (2017). A chi-square test was performed for comparison, and the significance level was set at less than 5%. However, if one cell (50.0%) had an expected frequency of 5 or less, a binomial test was performed. In addition, considering the accuracy of the test, it was not performed if the number of respondents for the analysis was less than ten. We did not compare the participation rate in public insurance because the participation rate of the Japanese people was 99.5% as of 2019 [ ].
The definitions of health outcome and health behavior indicators are as follows. Subjective symptoms were having subjective symptoms due to diseases or injury in the past few days; poor self-rated health indicated that they felt their current health was “not good” or “not very good”; insufficient feeling of rest through sleep means having rest through sleep was “not enough” or “poorly enough” in the past month; obesity indicated a BMI of 25 or more; smoking means smoking “every day” or “sometimes”; alcohol drinking means consuming alcohol “every day”; and no health examination means not having a periodic health examination in the past year. The analysis was conducted assuming that the health conditions of those living in the country for less than ten years were equivalent to those in the early migrant period. According to the WHO BMI classification, obesity in adult Asians is considered to be 25 or higher [ ].

## Results

## Number of respondents at each stage of the survey
Figure  illustrates the flow of numbers at each stage of the survey. In this study, 1,146 Thai visitors were asked to respond, and 1,019 (88.9%) responses were obtained. Of these, 123 participants were excluded, including 65 with incomplete data on age, sex, or length of residence in one or more strata; 10 aged 70 or older or residing for 40 years or longer in which the number of responses was insufficient for analysis as a single stratum; and 48 participants answered that they had previously responded to this survey. The final valid response rate was 78.2% (n = 896).
 

## Estimated number of visitors on the day of the survey
Of the 896 valid respondents, 852 were asked about the purpose of their visit, and 70.5% (601 respondents) answered that they visited to carry out their own consular procedures. According to Thai embassy records, 1,932 visitors conducted consular procedures. Assuming that 70.5% of the total number of visitors on the day of the survey underwent consular procedures, approximately 2,740 people visited the survey site. The number of valid responses to this survey was estimated to be approximately one-third (32.7%) of the total number of visitors.

## Analyzed participants and their characteristics
As there were only 118 male respondents, 778 female respondents were analyzed. 88.3% of the responses (687) were obtained from the four provinces with a high concentration of Thai people, and the number of respondents in the four provinces ranged from 116 to 199, with no significant difference (Table  ). The number of respondents in each year from 2015 to 2019 (excluding 2017) ranged from 154 to 219, roughly the same among the years. Of the 738 respondents asked how they filled out the questionnaire, 30.4% answered that they filled it out themselves, 44.6% responded that they asked the survey assistant to fill it out on their behalf, and the rest were invalid or no answers.

 

## Basic characteristics
Table illustrates the basic characteristics of the Thai women. Middle-aged and older (40 or more) participants accounted for 81.6% of the total. Thai nationals and those born in Thailand accounted for 98.3% and 97.6% of the respondents, respectively, indicating them first-generation migrants. In total, 85.2% had completed their final education in Thailand, with the highest percentage having completed elementary school in Thailand at 29.3%. Participants with the residence status of “permanent resident,” “spouse of Japanese national, etc.,” “long-term resident,” and “spouse of permanent resident, etc.” made up 83.5%, having resided in Japan for more than 10 years made up 81.5%, and having been married to Japanese made up 68.1%, so were expected to live in Japan for a long time.

 

## Health outcomes and health behaviors
Table presents the characteristics of the respondents’ health outcomes and health behaviors. The percentages of those with subjective symptoms, poor self-rated health, insufficient feeling of rest through sleep, and obesity were 29.9%, 22.6%, 29.7%, and 34.6%, respectively; the percentage of those with poor health ranged from about 20% to 30%. The percentages of participants who smoked, consumed alcohol, and had not undergone health examinations were 15.8%, 14.8%, and 25.2%, respectively; the percentages of those with undesirable health behaviors ranged from about 15% to 25%. Of the respondents, 87.4% were enrolled in Japan’s public medical insurance system.

 

## Existence of the HME in short-term residents but non-existence in long-term young residents
Tables and illustrate health outcomes and health behaviors for Thai women by three age groups and four residence period groups and compare them with Japanese women. The proportion of participants with subjective symptoms among the participants aged 20–39 years who had lived in Japan for less than 10 years was significantly lower than that of Japanese women, indicating their better state of health and the existence of the HME. However, participants in the same age group who had lived in Japan for 10 to less than 20 years did not differ significantly from Japanese women, indicating the non-existence of the HME. On the other hand, the HME was not observed, and the health transformations were unclear among participants aged 40–69.

 

## Poor health and undesirable health behaviors of long-term middle-aged and older residents
The proportions of people with poor self-rated health, an insufficient feeling of rest through sleep, obesity, and smoking were not significantly different from Japanese women in the short-term 40–69-year-old group and no HME was observed. However, the long-term group was significantly higher than Japanese women and had poor health and undesirable health behaviors. The classification of whether the residence period was short-term or long-term was based on a comparison within the same age group, and the actual residence period differed depending on the age group and health indicator.
Poor health status in middle-aged participants was observed in those with a shorter length of residence than those with a longer length of residence. Significant differences with Japanese women were observed for those who had been residents for 10 years or more in the 40–49-year-old group but for those who had been residents for 20 years or more in the 50–69-year-old group for the proportion of people with poor self-rated health. Such significant differences were observed for those who had been residents for 20 years or more in the 40–49-year-old group but for those who had been residents for 30 years or more in the 50–69-year-old group for the proportion of people who smoked.
The age at which poor health status develops in middle-aged and older people differed depending on the health indicator. Significant differences with Japanese women were observed in those aged 40 and over for the proportion of people with poor self-rated health and obesity and who smoked and in those aged 50 and over for the proportion of people with insufficient feeling of rest through sleep. Poor health status was also observed for the proportion of people who drank alcohol in middle-aged and older people, but the relationship with length of residence or age was unclear.

## Desirable health behaviors among long-term resident
The proportion of people who had not undergone health examination was, regardless of age, significantly higher or the same as Japanese women for those who had been residents for less than 10 years but was significantly lower or the same as Japanese women for those who had been residents for 10 years or more. Long-term residents showed more desirable health behaviors than Japanese women.
Desirable health behavior was observed for residents of 10 to less than 20 years among 20–49-year-olds but for residents for 20 years or more among 50–69-year-olds. Desirable health behavior was observed in those residents for a shorter period in the young or middle-aged people than in the older group.

## Discussion
This study aimed to clarify the health status of migrants living in Japan at the early stages of their migration and the subsequent health changes due to long-term residence compared to Japanese women. The results revealed that in Japan, young migrants have the HME in the early stages of their migration in one health indicator but lose it in long-term residence and converge to a health level equivalent to that of Japanese women. In contrast, middle-aged and older migrants do not have the HME in the early stages of their migration. However, their health status that was equivalent to that of Japanese women falls below that of Japanese women in long-term residence resulted in lower health status than that of Japanese women, and further, although long-term residents gain desirable health behaviors in all age groups, they experience health loss, except for some health indicators. It was also suggested that the age at which health loss occurs differs depending on the health indicator and that health loss and gain in long-term residents occur earlier in the middle-aged than the older after migration.

## Limitations and strengths
This study was a cross-sectional study, and by comparing the health status of different groups with different lengths of residence, a relationship between length of residence and health change was found. In addition, because the sample size was limited in this study, the age and length of residence range was set broadly from 10 to 20 years for the analysis. Therefore, there is a possibility that the adjustment for age composition ratio and the accuracy of the effect of length of residence on health are insufficient. As a result, it is possible that HME and health loss that existed may have been overlooked. For example, in the case of young people residing in Japan for eight years, they may have had low health status during the second half of eight years, due to long-term residence, offsetting the HME that may have actually existed in the first half of that period, resulted in the underestimation of HME. In addition, in the case of middle-aged and elderly people residing in Japan for 18 years, they may have had better health status during the first half of 10 to 18 years of residence, offsetting the low health that may have actually existed in the second half of that period, and the health loss may have been underestimated. However, even if this is the case, this study showed the presence of the HME in one health indicator in younger people and the possibility of a risk of health loss due to long-term residence in middle-aged and older people in health indicators with some exceptions. Although the data was collected through self-reporting, raising questions about its validity, the results for several different health indicators nonetheless showed fairly consistent trends.
Furthermore, the proportion of young people aged 20–39 among the respondents in this study (18.4%) was lower than that of the total Thai women living in Japan (41.0%), with the respondents skewed toward middle-aged and older people [ ]. However, the proportion of people with residence statuses of those holders are expected to live in Japan for the long term in the future, such as “permanent resident,” “spouse or child of a Japanese national,” “long-term resident,” and “spouse or child of a permanent resident,” was the majority among both the respondents (83.5%) and the total Thai women living in Japan (73.0%) [ ]. Finally, Thai people in Japan account for 1.87% (about 54,000 people) of all foreigners living in Japan, making them the 10th largest population of all foreigners in Japan [ ]. However, Thai people are one of the groups with a history of continuous immigration from the beginning of the rapid increase in foreign residents in Japan over the past 40 years to the present [ ], making them an essential group for this study to clarify the relationship between length of residence and health. The number of Thai men living in Japan is small, about one-fifth of the number of Thai women [ ], and research on Thai men is a future challenge.
The results of this study support previous research, showing that even in Japan, where the migrant population is relatively small, the HME is observed among young people at the early stages of migration and that the tendency for declining health in long-term residence is observed regardless of age, with middle-aged and older people in particular becoming less healthy than Japanese women. In recent years, the number of migrants in Japan has been increasing. Previous research has shown that as the migrant population rate in a region increases, the reduction in health risks for some migrants decreases [ ]. If we assume that this also applies to Japan, it may contribute to a reduction in the health risks of migrants in Japan to some extent in the future, but we cannot expect this to be the case. The new migration policy that the Japanese government will implement in 2027 aims to develop and secure foreign human resources in light of the serious labor shortage and intensifying international competition for talent [ , ]. The direct benefits to long-term residents, including first-generation migrants like the participants of this study, however, are unclear.
As far as we know, there have been no studies on factors that promote or hinder the utilization of health examination results of migrants in Japan. However, previous studies on access to healthcare have reported that factors related to hindrance include language and lack of knowledge of the Japanese medical system [ ], not enrolling in public insurance [ ], and not paying the health insurance premium [ ]. The results of this study suggest that migrants’ health examination results are not being effectively utilized to maintain and manage their health, and it is necessary to clarify why the acquisition of healthy behaviors as an increased rate of health examination due to long-term residence, does not lead to improved health among migrants.
Furthermore, the health and health risks of migrants are not captured in official government statistics [ ], and migrants remain unable to be included as targets of Health Japan 21, which is positioned as the basic health policy for the nation [ ]. There is a strong need to understand the health status of migrants through government statistics and position migrants as targets for primary prevention aimed at maintaining and improving their health, as well as secondary prevention aimed at utilizing the results of health examination.
Although this study has limitations, it is the first to quantitatively demonstrate the relationship between migrants’ health and length of residence in Japan, where health-related data for migrants are rarely available in official statistics. It provides preliminary but important evidence for considering migrant health measures in Japan. Migrants may lose their health even as they acquire health behaviors in Japan, which may lead to the health of migrants remaining vulnerable in Japan. ​​​​To further clarify the results of this study, it is necessary to conduct studies on a broader migrant population.

## Conclusion
This study was conducted as a cross-sectional study comparing multiple health indicators of Thai migrant women residing in Japan with those of Japanese women to clarify the presence or absence of an HME and the health changes associated with long-term residence. The results showed that the HME exists among migrants but is limited to some health indicators among young people and that middle-aged and older long-term residents are generally in poor health despite more people than Japanese women had undergone health examinations. This result indicates that duration of residence is related to health of migrants, with longer residence period, corresponding to a decline in health status. It is suggested that health data on migrants should be collected in official statistics and targeted as a primary and secondary prevention measure for public health. In the future, cohort studies targeting a broader migrant population are required to further clarify the state of health loss among migrants in Japan.
