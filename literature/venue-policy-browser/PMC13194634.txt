## Abstract

## Objective
The improper disposal of unused and expired medications poses a significant threat of environmental contamination, public health risks, and economic burdens. Therefore, interventions to address this issue are necessary. This review aims to identify the effectiveness of interventions in improving the proper disposal of unused and expired household medications.

## Study design
Systematic review of randomized controlled trials.

## Methods
The review was conducted in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. A comprehensive literature search was conducted using the PubMed and Scopus databases until July 2024 for randomized controlled studies evaluating interventions to improve household medication disposal practices. No publication year restrictions were applied. Two reviewers independently screened the studies, extracted the data, and assessed the outcomes. A qualitative data synthesis was employed.

## Results
Of 112 retrieved articles, nine met the inclusion criteria and were eligible for analysis. Four studies utilized home medication disposal kits, one study employed a medication take-back program, and two studies used educational materials such as leaflets or videos. The remaining two studies employed a combination of interventions: one integrated a pamphlet with incentives, whereas the other provided medication disposal bags alongside an informative web-based resource. Both the medication take-back program intervention and the combination of medication disposal bags with web resources demonstrated consistent effectiveness.

## Conclusions
The interventions implemented had variable effects in terms of both direction and extent on improving the proper disposal of unused and expired household medications. To achieve consistent and positive outcomes, developing interventions that include personalized feedback is essential for enhancing the proper disposal practices of these medications.

Keywords: Medication disposal, Interventions, Unused household medications, Expired household medications

## Introduction
Medication waste ranks second globally among potentially hazardous waste, surpassed only by radioactive materials, with consumption reaching 3.8 trillion defined daily doses [ ]. Nevertheless, the increased use of pharmaceuticals can lead to the accumulation of unused and expired medication, and the increased release of pharmaceutical waste into the environment poses serious threats to both ecosystems and human health [ ]. The presence of these active pharmaceutical ingredients in soil and waterways can significantly impact the environment, as these drugs can enter the food chain, reaccumulate, and inadvertently be ingested by humans, who initially discarded them [ ]. Moreover, improper disposal of medication waste leads to public health risks such as antimicrobial resistance [ ]. This practice also results in economic burdens, with billions of dollars worth unused medications being wasted annually [ , ].
In most developing countries, improper medication disposal remains prevalent, with many individuals disposing of unused medication directly into garbage or flushing it down the toilet [ ]. A study conducted in Indonesia revealed that 82.1% of individuals still improperly dispose of unused and expired medications [ ]. Over half (53.1%) were unaware that improper medication disposal could harm the environment and health, and the majority (79.5%) had never received information on proper disposal practices [ ].
Pharmacists should play a crucial role in educating the public on proper disposal methods. A previous study revealed that nearly all pharmacists recognized the environmental risks associated with improper disposal of expired and unused household medications and acknowledged their responsibility to ensure proper disposal and minimize environmental and health risks [ ]. However, their involvement is often inconsistent [ ], and many do not proactively counsel patients on proper household medication disposal [ ]. Although pharmacists are frequently highlighted because of their direct role in medication management and patient counseling, improving household medication disposal practices may also involve other health care providers and researchers who design, implement, and evaluate behavioral and educational interventions related to medication disposal. Moreover, the interventions implemented by pharmacists remain quite limited in providing at-home medication disposal kits and informational handouts, which have shown no significant effect on improving household medication disposal practices [ ]. Unlike the previous review that predominantly focused on pharmacist-led inteventions and included cross-sectional or mixed study designs, this review adopts a broader perspective by including interventions delivered by pharmacists, other health care providers, and researchers. In addition, this review focuses exclusively on randomized controlled trials (RCT), thereby strengthening causal inference regarding the effectiveness of interventions. Therefore, by synthesizing RCT evidence, this systematic review exclusively identifies which disposal interventions demonstrate consistent effectiveness rather than mere associations. This review focuses exclusively on randomized controlled trials (RCTs) assessing interventions for proper household medication disposal, providing higher-quality evidence and addressing a gap not covered by previous reviews that relied on observational or mixed study designs.

2.

## Methods
This study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) [ ] ( ).
2.1.

## Information sources and search strategy
A comprehensive literature search comprising publications up to July 2024 was conducted in the PubMed and Scopus databases, with no time restrictions. The PICO metric, namely, participants (individuals with unused and expired medications), intervention (all interventions), comparison (standard care or no intervention), and outcome (improvement in unused and expired household medication disposal practices), was applied to develop the search terms. The main keywords utilized in the search strategy for this systematic review included “unused and expired medications," “disposal practices," and “interventions," as detailed in .

2.2.

## Eligibility criteria
Studies, either paid (subscription-based) or free (open access), were deemed eligible if they fulfilled the following inclusion criteria: (1) utilized a randomized controlled trial study design; (2) addressed interventions in tackling improper, unused, and expired household medication disposal practices; and (3) were published in English. Unused and expired household medications are defined as medications that are kept by individuals but are no longer needed owing to changes in health conditions, completion of a treatment course, or expiration of their recommended usage period [ ]. The exclusion criteria included (1) no peer-reviewed studies (gray literature) and (2) reviews, case reports, conference proceedings, opinions, letters to the editor, or commentaries.

2.3.

## Selection process
On the basis of the titles and abstracts, two independent reviewers (CS and EK) determined if the studies were eligible. During the second round of the screening procedure, the reviewers (CS and EK) obtained and screened the full texts of studies that may qualify. Next, QAK and SDA conducted further verification of the abstracts and full-text screening. Any disagreements were resolved by consensus.

2.4.

## Data extraction process
Data extraction was carried out by CS and EK, who also evaluated and validated any extraction process uncertainties. A standardized form with predetermined and tested data extraction criteria was employed. The data were manually extracted from Google Sheets and backed up on Google Drive.

2.5.

## Data items
We extracted information on the country of study, study duration, number of participants, type of intervention, intervention, outcome, effectiveness of intervention, and funding sources.

2.6.

## Synthesis methods
To characterize the included studies, we employed descriptive statistics to summarize key characteristics. Given the heterogeneity in study populations, interventions, comparators, methodologies, and reported outcomes, we opted for a narrative approach to analyze and synthesize the results.

2.7.

## Assessment of the quality of the included studies
To ensure methodological rigor, CS and EK independently assessed the eligibility and quality of the included studies. We utilized the Jadad scale to assess study quality in three key domains, namely, randomization, blinding, and participant withdrawal, with scores ranging from 1 (low quality) to 5 (high quality), with 0–2 indicating low quality and 3–5 indicating good quality [ ].

3.

## Results
3.1.

## Identified studies
A total of 112 studies were retrieved from the literature search across two databases: PubMed and Scopus. Following the removal of duplicates, 109 studies were screened on the basis of their titles and abstracts, and full-text screening was subsequently performed. The final results of the full-text screening identified 14 studies that were assessed for eligibility, resulting in 9 studies that met the inclusion criteria, as detailed in .
 
3.2.

## General characteristics of the included studies
Most of the included studies (88.9%) were conducted in the United States of America (USA) [ , , , , , , , ]. Only one such study was conducted in Europe as a multicountry study in Denmark, Greece, Czechia, and the United Kingdom [ ], suggesting a potential gap in the global representation of findings ( ). The majority of the included studies (55.6%) had a duration ranging from 1 to 6 months [ , , , , ]. One-third of the included studies (33.3%) had a total number of participants ranging from 1 to 100 14,16,19, with participant counts varying from a minimum of 40 14 to a maximum of 3754 individuals [ ]. The majority of the studies (66.7%) received funding [ , , , , , ]. The primary drug class in the included studies was predominantly opioids (88.9%) in oral dosage forms [ , , , , , , , ], suggesting growing concerns over the misuse, abuse potential, and environmental risks associated with improper disposal of opioids. Another study did not specify the drug class or dosage form [ ].
 
3.3.

## Interventions to address unused and expired household medications
Four studies (44.4%) used drug disposal kits, such as special bags containing activated charcoal for unused and expired medication [ , , , ]. Two studies utilized educational media, such as leaflets or videos, as intervention tools [ , ], whereas one study employed medication take-back programs [ ]. The remaining two studies utilized a combination of interventions: one combined pamphlets and incentives [ ], while the other paired medication disposal bags with an informative web-based resource [ ]. This diversity in intervention types highlights a lack of standardization but also shows flexibility in adapting to different patient contexts. Across studies, the definition of “effectiveness” was operationalized using heterogeneous but consistently self-reported outcome measures. These included self-reported disposal behavior, self-reported use of disposal kits, self-reported return of medications to take-back programs, and behavioral intention to dispose of medications properly. No study employed objectively verified return or disposal measures. Therefore, effectiveness reflects reported behavioral change or intention rather than independently verified disposal outcomes.
The overall effectiveness of the included studies was comparable, with 44.4% demonstrating effectiveness [ , , , ], whereas the other 55.6% showed no effectiveness [ , , , , ] ( ). The medication take-back program intervention [ ] and combination of medication disposal bags with an informative web-based resource [ ] demonstrated consistent effectiveness across all included studies. In contrast, the combination of educational leaflets and financial incentives proved ineffective [ ]. Intervention via drug disposal kits appeared less effective, with only 25% of studies showing positive results [ ], whereas 75% showed no effectiveness [ , , ]. Additionally, interventions involving educational information media yielded mixed outcomes, with 50% of the studies indicating effectiveness [ ] and 50% showing no effect [ ] ( ). These findings suggest that multicomponent interventions combining practical access with contextual education may be more effective than single-mode strategies.
 
3.4.

## Methodological quality properties
All included studies met the threshold for high quality on the basis of Jadad scoring (score: 3) [ , , , , , , , , ], indicating a high level of methodological rigor. This suggests that the evidence presented is generally reliable. The consistent Jadad score across studies strengthens the validity of the findings and reduces the risk of bias in the overall review. However, none of the studies met the double-blinding criteria, as it was not feasible to blind the intervention provider for this specific intervention. Consequently, they received a score of 0 for the questions concerning the implementation and appropriateness of double-blinding. presents the methodological quality of the included studies.

4.

## Discussion
This systematic review identified several intervention strategies aimed at improving the proper disposal of unused and expired household medications. Across the included studies, two approaches consistently demonstrated effectiveness, medication take-back program [ ] and the combination of disposal bags with an informative web-based resource [ ]. Both interventions reduce practical barriers and provide a clear, accessible pathway for safe disposal, which likely explains their advantage over single-component or purely informational strategies. Take-back programs offer a convenient [ ], trusted physical venue for returning medications [ , ], while combined disposal bag–web interventions simplify at-home disposal and reinforce motivation through accessible instructions and credible online information [ ].
In contrast, interventions relying solely on educational information [ , ], disposal kits [ , , , ], and the combination of educational materials with financial incentives [ ] appeared to be less effective. These unguided active interventions, such as providing disposal kits without accompanying instructions or digital engagement, are generally ineffective. Effectiveness appears to require a cognitive ‘nudge’ through clear guidance or interactive educational components. These approaches may increase awareness but depend heavily on individuals to remember instructions, self-initiate disposal, and overcome behavioral inertia [ ]. Without reinforcement or a convenient disposal mechanism, information alone appears insufficient to generate sustained behavior change [ ]. This pattern aligns with broader public health evidence showing that interventions combining knowledge with environmental facilitation are more likely to lead to action [ ].
Across studies, interventions that reduced practical effort at the moment of disposal decision-making were consistently more effective than those relying primarily on patient-initiated action. Strategies such as take-back infrastructure or readily available disposal bags function as behavioral “cues” that lower cognitive load, enabling immediate action. Conversely, passive or motivational-only strategies, including financial incentives or educational leaflets, did not consistently prompt behavior change, underscoring the need for interventions that modify both informational and environmental components ( ).
 The predominance of opioid-focused studies reflects heightened concerns regarding unsafe storage, diversion, and community misuse. However, this concentration may overestimate the observed effectiveness of disposal interventions, as heightened risk perceptions associated with addiction and overdose can strongly motivate disposal behaviors. Such effects may not be directly transferable to medications perceived as lower risk, such as antibiotics or antihypertensive agents, where environmental harm, rather than immediate personal danger, is the primary concern. Many individuals retain unused opioids due to habitual behavior or perceived future need [ ], elevating public health risks. Pharmacists play a key role in shifting these behaviors by offering clear disposal guidance and linking patients to practical disposal options [ , ]. In this context, pharmacists play a critical role not merely as information providers but as behavioral gatekeepers by clear counseling, explicit disposal instructions, and offering practical disposal options can alter patient risk perceptions and reduce default tendencies to keep unused opioids at home. Evidence shows that when pharmacists actively guide patients through tailored explanations, disposal demonstrations, or directing them to take-back programs, patients are more likely to recognize opioids as hazardous waste rather than household items, prompting safer disposal decisions [ ]. This underscores the value of integrating disposal counseling into pharmacy practice, particularly for high-risk medications.
The limited geographic representation of the included RCTs reflects a broader scarcity of experimental research on medication disposal interventions globally. Although improper disposal is more prevalent in low- and middle-income countries, evidence from these settings is dominated by cross-sectional surveys rather than trials that test causal effects. As a result, the findings of this review should be interpreted as an initial synthesis of the strongest available evidence, with further studies required in diverse cultural and regulatory contexts. Importantly, the scarcity of randomized controlled trials from low- and middle-income countries represents a key finding of this review rather than a limitation alone. Despite higher prevalence of improper disposal in these settings, experimental evidence remains limited, highlighting an urgent priority for future research.
Overall, the findings suggest that effective interventions must combine information with actionable, low-effort mechanisms that enable immediate disposal. Approaches that integrate educational, behavioral, and environmental components appear most promising for addressing persistent household disposal practices.
4.1.

## Implications and future directions
The promising effectiveness of medication take-back programs and the combination of medication disposal kits with an informative web-based platform suggests that these interventions could be scaled up and widely adopted to address the issue of improper medication disposal. Continued collaboration between health care providers, community pharmacists, stakeholders, and the community will be essential in promoting these solutions and ensuring their long-term success. For pharmacists, understanding the effectiveness of different interventions can guide the implementation of best practices in patient education and community outreach [ ]. Pharmacists can play a crucial role in raising awareness about these disposal options and encouraging patients to utilize them, ultimately contributing to safer medication disposal habits and reduced environmental harm [ ].
Tailored strategies should consider that the level of knowledge, awareness, and practice among pharmacists regarding the proper disposal of unused and expired medications remains inadequate in low and middle-income countries [ , , , ]. Therefore, providing comprehensive training for healthcare workers and organizing workshops to enhance their knowledge to effectively manage proper medication disposal are necessary [ ]. Additionally, pharmacy students need to be exposed to comprehensive educational material on improper disposal of unused and expired medications from an early stage in their training, given that current practices and knowledge regarding the disposal of household medications are inadequate [ , , ]. This curriculum should thoroughly explain safe medication disposal methods, emphasize the environmental and public health risks associated with improper medication disposal, and focus on strategies such as community outreach, patient education, and collaboration with disposal programs [ ]. The incorporation of this knowledge and these practices by future pharmacists could increase their ability to mitigate the risks of medication misuse and environmental contamination due to improper medication disposal practices [ ].
To support proper household medication disposal practices, several policy recommendations can be considered. First, implementing mandatory take-back programs at pharmacies and healthcare facilities can ensure that individuals have convenient and safe options for disposing of unused and expired medications [ ]. Additionally, policymakers should provide funding and resources to support these programs, including public access to safe disposal options [ ] and making them accessible to all communities [ ]. Furthermore, integrating medication disposal education into healthcare provider training and public health campaigns can enhance community knowledge and compliance 45. These findings hold significant implications for global environmental and public health policies, underscoring the need for governments and health authorities to integrate pharmaceutical waste management into broader strategies aimed at achieving sustainable development and safeguarding population health.
From a policy perspective, interventions should be conceptualized and implemented at the population level, including the national adoption of medication take-back programs and the systematic distribution of disposal bags through community pharmacies. Embedding these initiatives within public health and environmental governance frameworks would advance the Sustainable Development Goals, particularly those on good health and well-being and clean water and sanitation. Linking disposal interventions to demographic patterns, urbanization, and social inequalities can also guide future research on the two-way connections between population dynamics and environmental sustainability.
From a pharmacy practice perspective, these findings highlight several practical actions pharmacists can implement to improve medication disposal. Brief, targeted disposal counseling at the point of dispensing can clarify when and how unused medicines should be discarded. Pharmacists can also proactively refer patients to medication take-back programs or, where unavailable, demonstrate the use of disposal pouches at discharge. Directing patients to credible digital resources that accompany disposal tools may further reinforce counseling and reduce barriers to proper disposal.

4.2.

## Strengths and limitations
The strengths of this systematic review lie in its comprehensive search strategy and rigorous inclusion criteria, which ensured a thorough evaluation of relevant studies on interventions for improper household medication disposal. This systematic review focuses on RCT designs that emphasize effectiveness rather than association and help to eliminate confounding factors, ensuring that the observed outcomes are directly attributable to the interventions being studied. However, it is important to acknowledge certain limitations. The heterogeneity of the studies, with variability in study populations, interventions, and outcomes, presents challenges in generalizing the findings. Furthermore, the lack of long-term follow-up data in the included studies limits the understanding of the sustained impact of the interventions. In addition, all included trials relied on self-reported disposal-related outcomes rather than objectively verified return data, which may introduce social desirability and recall bias and potentially overestimate intervention effectiveness.
Moreover, focusing exclusively on published studies in English may introduce publication bias, potentially resulting in an overestimation of the effectiveness of the interventions, as studies with negative or inconclusive results are less likely to be published. The exclusion of databases such as Embase, CINAHL, and Cochrane Central may have limited the comprehensiveness of this review, as the literature search was restricted to PubMed and Scopus. However, the use of multiple major databases and hand-searching references helped mitigate this limitation. Finally, the findings are heavily representative of the U.S. healthcare system, where established take-back infrastructure is available. As a result, the geographic concentration of included studies may not adequately reflect disposal practices in other regions, thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings across diverse cultural, regulatory, and health system contexts. In contexts where formal take-back infrastructure is limited, active but low-infrastructure interventions, such as disposal kits distributed at dispensing or discharge, may offer a more feasible alternative. These approaches can be integrated into existing clinical workflows and, when supported by clear guidance, may reduce behavioral and logistical barriers to proper disposal without reliance on centralized systems.

4.3.

## Conclusion
This systematic review identified a range of interventions designed to improve the disposal practices of unused and expired household medications. While some interventions, such as medication take-back programs and combination medication disposal bags with an informative web-based program, have positive effects on proper medication disposal practices, the evidence remains limited in scope and quality, with most studies focused on opioid disposal in the U.S. setting. Conversely, interventions relying solely on educational pamphlets, videos, or the provision of home disposal kits appeared to be less effective, indicating that more engaging or interactive strategies may be necessary to achieve optimal outcomes. Furthermore, developing interventions that include personalized feedback is essential for achieving consistent outcomes. Future studies in diverse contexts are also needed to confirm the effectiveness and generalizability of the findings.

## What this study adds

•This review synthesizes the strongest available evidence from randomized controlled trials evaluating medication disposal interventions.
•Active, facilitative interventions such as take-back programs consistently improve disposal practices, whereas passive educational materials show limited effectiveness.
•The findings identify key behavioral and structural mechanisms that can guide the design and implementation of more effective disposal programs.

## Implication for policy and practice

•Strengthening public awareness and communication strategies is essential for improving access and uptake of safe medication disposal options.
•Policymakers should prioritize integrating medication take-back infrastructure into community health systems and pharmacy services.
•Effective behavioral interventions can reduce environmental contamination risks and support safer household medication management.
