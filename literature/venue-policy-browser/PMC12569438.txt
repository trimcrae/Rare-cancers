## Abstract

## Introduction
Applying research findings to health care practice has typically been a prolonged process, resulting in frustration to patients, providers, and other health system constituents. The PaTH Clinical Research Network developed the Discovery to Implementation (D2I) Initiative, rooted in Learning Health Systems principles, to engage constituents across seven academic sites/affiliated health systems in a process of identifying and prioritizing topics for future health improvement activities.

## Aims
To develop a consensus process to prioritize topics derived from the network's own research for future implementation efforts conducted by network‐based Learning Communities. To apply this process to identify priority topics based on the network's recently published research.

## Methods
We developed a multi‐institutional process for engaging a broad variety of perspectives to systematically review and evaluate PaTH‐affiliated, patient‐focused health research published between 2020 and 2022. Each site invited local constituents including patients, caregivers, clinicians, researchers, funders, health plan representatives, and health system leaders to help prioritize research topics and identify potential Learning Community areas of focus. D2I activities included brief surveys, review of data from the PCORnet® Common Data Model, and a multi‐step deliberative process during two virtual events.

## Results
The D2I Initiative included the participation of nearly 90 constituents in its iterative process to prioritize research topics. From 21 publications of patient‐focused PaTH‐affiliated research, three topics emerged as the highest priority for potential quality improvement work: back pain, hypertension, and frailty. Furthermore, the D2I Initiative mobilized interest in three potential Learning Communities to work toward implementing health care innovations.

## Conclusions
The D2I format is an innovative, engaging, and scalable model for involving constituents throughout our health systems to prioritize topics for future improvement activities. The process aims, ultimately, to reduce the time from research discovery to health systems improvements while maintaining a focus on the needs and priorities of those involved at multiple system levels.

## Asynchronous article prioritization
We used an asynchronous process to identify PaTH constituents' highest‐priority topics prior to the deliberative event, thus narrowing the scope of topics for deliberation in a time‐limited session (Figure  ). This process involved two brief online surveys. The first listed a simplified title for each patient‐focused article, hyperlinked to the full published article in case participants wanted to learn more. Accompanying text emphasized that the workgroup was interested in learning participants' first impressions of what was compelling to them rather than require a great deal of reading before offering their views. Each participant was asked to select up to 10 topics that they found to be important, potentially adoptable to improve medical practice, and have potential to positively impact the health of underserved populations. We examined the survey results to see if any of the studies were more popular with certain participant categories than others. For example, we did not want to exclude a study that was particularly important to patients even if it did not garner as many overall votes as another study. Survey findings were used to identify 10 “higher priority topics.”
Our second pre‐event survey focused on the 10 higher priority topics. We provided a plain‐language summary for each of these articles, along with the hyperlink to the full article, and asked participants to rate each study using five‐point Likert scales (ranging from 1 “disagree” to 5 “agree”), based on whether they believed that study's findings would: (a) make a substantial improvement in health or health care, (b) be adoptable by health systems to improve their patterns of care, and (c) improve health equity across diverse populations. Although a LHS approach often focuses attention on implementation, the D2I workgroup also included the potential magnitude of impact on health and equity as critical when prioritizing topics in the context of limited resources. Respondents were also asked which article had the best potential to motivate a multi‐perspective “Learning Community” to mobilize and work toward next steps.
For each article, the average Likert score was calculated for reported potential for improving health/healthcare, suggesting adoptable interventions, and promoting equity. The articles were then ranked according to average Likert score. We also tallied which articles were named as strong candidates for mobilizing a LC. For each metric, rankings were examined overall, as well as for the subset of responders who represented patient/caregiver perspectives. We considered highly ranked articles to be those ranked in the top four articles in at least two of the four categories.

## Development of supporting materials and live event logistics
To help participants engage in our live sessions, we prepared a one‐page brief on each of the top five prioritized studies that included: the plain‐language summary and title, background information to contextualize the study, and Altmetrics to illustrate the study article's circulation and engagement within and beyond research communities. These briefs were sent electronically to all invitees several days before the first live event. Additionally, the workgroup hosted an optional, informal patient orientation to answer questions about the activities planned for the events and to reassure the patient participants that they did not need to know all the details about the studies to participate fully. Patients were compensated for their time completing pre‐event surveys and attending synchronous D2I activities.
To provide experiential context for the prioritized health topics, pre‐selected patient stories were identified from the MyPaTH Story Booth repository , that correlated with each of the five prioritized topics discussed in the first session.
The D2I workgroup identified several roles that members would fill: moderator and slide advancer, timekeeper, note‐takers, chat monitor, technology troubleshooter, online poll manager, and presenters. Timing and screen toggling were rehearsed to ensure all planned elements would be feasible in 2 hours and a “run of show” document helped all facilitators stay coordinated.

## RESULTS
3.1

## Asynchronous article prioritization
Of the 56 publications initially identified, 21 articles were included as patient‐focused health research using PaTH data (Figure  and Data  ). The first survey received 59 responses, 50% of 118 potential participants (Table  ). The 10 most‐selected studies encompassed topic areas of wide interest, and patients' preferences were well represented in that list. Since the goal of D2I was not to replicate the specific features of the studies, we referred to the key topic or problem area addressed by each publication.
 Fifty participants (42% of 118 invited) completed the second survey to assess the top 10 topics identified with the first survey (Table  and Data  ). Likert scores revealed participants' overall five prioritized topics as: blood pressure control, avoiding unsafe opioid prescriptions in primary care, aspirin dosing in cardiovascular disease, frailty screening and surgical outcomes in men and women, and implementing stratified care for low back pain. Review of the ranked average Likert scores revealed that four were highest ranked for improving health care (opioid prescribing, blood pressure control, frailty and surgical outcomes, low back pain management ); three were highest ranked for adoptability (opioid prescribing, blood pressure control, low back pain management ); three were highest ranked for their potential to promote equity (opioid prescribing, blood pressure control, frailty and surgical outcomes ), and three were highest ranked for potential for mobilizing a LC (opioid prescribing, blood pressure control, frailty and surgical outcomes ). Only three of the overall top 5 articles met the highly ranked criteria based on responses from patient/caregiver perspectives; two of those articles were also highly ranked by the larger group (opioid prescribing, blood pressure control ) and the third (aspirin dosing ) was included as the fifth prioritized article discussed at the deliberative event.

## Implementation of the deliberative event: Session 1
Attendance at the first session consisted of 79 participants: clinicians (n = 10), patients (n = 10), researchers (n = 24), health system leaders (n = 13), payers (n = 2), and other participants (n = 20) including research staff, engagement experts, and implementation specialists. Each participant was asked to represent the perspective of their role (e.g., patient, clinician, or payer) in the deliberative activities. To facilitate discussion in a video conferencing venue, we encouraged participants to keep their cameras on, mute their microphones when not speaking, display their name on the screen, and avoid full conversations in the chat (but comments in the chat were encouraged).
Topical discussion opened with an introduction to PCORnet and PaTH, as well as the goals and processes of the D2I Initiative. Next, a primer on LHS and LC was presented before reviewing each of the five studies prioritized through the surveys. We also tested the polling technology with attendees, using mobile devices and an online app.
The interactive deliberation activities (Figure  ) incorporated human‐centered design features to invite engagement by all participants and across all perspectives. In the first stage, the moderator introduced “positive vetting,” an identification of the features of each study's process or outcomes that participants found important, useful, or generally appreciated. The goal of this stage was to ensure that the group shared a robust understanding of the importance of each article's topic, such as (a) the benefits to patients, families, health systems, and clinicians; (b) how a LC motivated by the article might contribute to improvements in health or healthcare, lead to an adoptable intervention that improves patterns of care, or improve health equity; (c) how the potential benefits align with their own organization's priorities and strategy; (d) the potential burdens of different approaches to local constituents; and (e) access to relevant data.
 For each of the articles, participants considered the vetting prompts independently and entered one positive aspect into the chat text box. The moderator gave a cue for all participants to submit their comments simultaneously (Figure  ). This “waterfall chat” method allows time and space for every participant to contribute while preventing early responders from swaying the trajectory of the conversation, inadvertently or otherwise. Participants could scroll through the responses of others; the moderator chose several to read aloud and occasionally invited a commenter to expand on the comment, being careful to allow space for discussion across multiple perspectives and roles so that no role hierarchy dominated the conversation. This process was repeated for each of the five articles.
 In the second phase, “comparative deliberation,” participants considered the same implications from the positive vetting phase but were invited to compare any two of the five topics to each other, weighing their relative merits and offering observations both in the chat box as well as in the moderated oral discussion. Following discussion, participants voted in a rapid pairwise poll using their mobile devices and an online app. Participants identified their favored topic for each possible pairwise combination of the five topics.
In the final deliberative phase, participants were asked—after seeing the results of the pair‐wise polling—whether further exploration was needed before finalizing the top three topics. The moderator offered an opportunity for anyone to advocate for particular topics to be included in or excluded from the top three. A final vote via online app was held and participants ultimately shifted their votes from the prior comparison vote, indicating that the arguments offered in the final discussion were persuasive to many.
Ultimately, D2I participants prioritized the topics of blood pressure control (hypertension), low back pain, and frailty. We ended the initial D2I session with a patient‐centered focus on these three topics by sharing a summary, QR code, and hyperlink for the respective pre‐selected patient narratives. Participants were asked to listen to each story before the second session and reflect on how we might use insights from the stories to mobilize and catalyze further action through a LC.

3.3

## Implementation of the deliberative event: Session 2
The second session of the D2I Initiative, also 2 h long, took place 1 week after the first but at a different time of day to ensure the broadest possible attendance across both meetings. Session 2 focused on the potential for developing LCs related to the three topics prioritized during Session 1. As some participants were not able to attend the first week, we offered a brief review of the first session proceedings and held another practice round with the online polling process.
Session 2 began where Session 1 left off: with a focus on patient perspectives related to frailty, hypertension, and back pain. As a research network focused on patient‐centered research, we emphasize the patient perspective as critical to developing relevant and beneficial implementation strategies. The patient stories we distributed during Session 1 were not intended to represent a generalizable perspective nor limit our understanding of the breadth of patient experiences. Instead, we provided anecdotal perspectives as examples of how patients may articulate their experiences in the hopes that D2I participants across the spectrum of health care could broaden their awareness beyond their own specific concerns. Participants shared in the chat box and in oral discussion what they learned from listening to the patient narratives.
Next, a presenter shared a population perspective that leveraged the PaTH sites' access to curated, quality‐checked EHR data. Prior to the gathering, the PCORnet® Common Data Model from each site was queried to generate a summary table of relevance to each topic. The tables displayed aggregate data across the seven participating health systems for the overall patient population of interest and for two subsets based on the topics in the prioritized article. For example, the table related to back pain displayed data on (a) adults with acute low back pain in the past 5 years; (b) the subset of those patients who had been referred to physical therapy; and (c) the subset who had not been referred to physical therapy. Frequencies and percentages were shown overall and by age, sex, race, and ethnicity categories. These data were intended to show participants how many patients in our network would potentially be affected by a change in health care practices and what demographics they represented. Prior to the second session, tailored versions of these data tables were shared with the health system leader participants, restricted to the patients from their own health system, in addition to the aggregate data from across seven health systems. Discussion prompts asked participants to consider how many patients are impacted by each health topic, who those patients are, and whether the data suggested possible next steps for improvement. Those participants working in health systems were asked to consider possible implications for their own organizations.
Armed with insights from patient perspectives and population data, participants each selected a virtual breakout session, specific to one of the three priority topics (hypertension, frailty, or back pain), to engage directly with each other in a forum lightly moderated by D2I workgroup members. Breakout sessions focused on quality improvement challenges; discussion prompts included how to frame the topic of interest while considering aspects of passion/interest and practicality. Participants were asked to identify 1–2 health problems within the domain they would like to improve and to consider if any of these ideas could be acted upon in the short term. They brainstormed on whether the topic aligned with ongoing institutional initiatives and/or priorities. Finally, they discussed possible pilot funding or whether some aspect of the topic might merit grant proposal development. The prompts fostered lively conversation and more robust interpersonal engagement among participants. Although 40 min were allotted for the breakout discussions, participants and moderators alike felt that it passed quickly without exhausting the range of topics prepared in the prompts. A designee from each group offered a brief report‐out to the reconvened large group following the breakout session.
At the conclusion of Session 2, participants used the online polling app to indicate their potential interest in participating in a LC to work iteratively toward implementation of health research in one or more of the prioritized topic areas. They could also identify colleagues or others at their site who might be interested in participation in D2I activities. Many participants, representing all present constituent groups, expressed interest in further pursuing the idea of a LC related to each topic (Hypertension N = 27; Frailty N = 17; Back Pain N = 20).

## DISCUSSION
Our goal was to prioritize topics of importance and relevance to all our constituents, representing multiple health systems and perspectives. This goal aligns with recent scholarship on the critical role of engagement as a catalyst for LHS action, including priority‐setting. We believe our iterative, deliberative process achieved this goal because the topics ultimately prioritized by the collective included those ranked highest in various categories by patients, clinicians, and researchers. D2I can serve as a model for intentionally creating and fostering engagement across the health system spectrum and across multiple sites, thus improving the odds of reaching “critical mass” for sustaining a LHS. Additionally, D2I demonstrates how the PCORnet infrastructure can contribute to LHS goals: the patient‐centered focus, established links with community and health system constituents, and experience with fostering useful conversation among people with varied backgrounds facilitated broad engagement among the partnering health systems. In particular, D2I conversations highlighted the perspectives of patients and caregivers whose lived experience with persistent health conditions adds valuable insight into the opportunities for improvement in care. Likewise, the PCORnet® CDM facilitated access to standardized EHR data relevant to each prioritized topic across multiple health systems, helping to ground the conversation in the real‐world health status of the actual patients that the group aimed to improve.
This exercise demonstrates how LHS concepts can be scaled across health systems: it utilized multi‐institutional data, leveraged the viewpoints of constituents from seven health systems, and launched a LC with members from seven PaTH sites that is still active 2 years after the initial event. A multi‐institutional approach brings unique strengths—for example, topics that resonate for community members across different health systems and geographic regions are likely to be of particular importance for promoting health. Likewise, insights from one health system may help affiliates from another system address a challenging topic more quickly, and co‐designed interventions may hold promise for being more generalizable than efforts designed within a single health system. Conversely, the multi‐institutional approach adds a level of complexity that any resultant LC will need to navigate. For example, some potential interventions will be more appropriate at some sites than others and institutional priorities will differ.
The D2I process has limitations. Because the process examined research data from PCORnet® Network Partners, the prioritization process considered only those topics. We did not address any additional relevant topics for improving health and healthcare in the participating communities. Each site focused on engaging diverse constituents but we were unable to document the group's demographic composition as a sizeable proportion exited the second session directly after the breakout sessions, before reconvening for a brief wrap‐up session and exit survey. Additionally, not all perspectives were represented in equal or equitable measure based on attendance, which limited the opportunity to hear from those voices (e.g., only two payers attended). While we successfully engaged individuals with a range of perspectives across seven health systems, including numerous patients making salient points during the deliberative events, the optional patient orientation session had minimal attendance, raising concerns that we could have provided better support to community participants. Future events will put increased emphasis on this area and may consider additional facets of “successful engagement” to enrich the experience for all. Finally, the process for topic prioritization is not well‐suited for bringing attention to rare diseases and other less common concerns that may not garner majority votes.
All three prioritized topic areas drew sufficient interest from diverse constituents to suggest that a LC for each could be feasible. Although we continued conversations with all three topic‐based groups, participation over the next few months indicated that only the hypertension topic had sufficient engagement and interest to support the launch of a LC. This Hypertension Learning Community will use the findings from the prioritized study as a springboard for additional research and implementation activities focused on improving blood pressure control.
4.1

## Future work and associated challenges
Engaging constituents throughout multiple health systems is an ongoing challenge due to busy schedules, differing time zones, and varying institutional priorities. We also observed that it is difficult to motivate health system leaders and other goal‐focused constituents to prepare for and launch LCs when the goal is not yet clearly defined.
Looking ahead, we anticipate the D2I Initiative to continue on a two‐year cycle: Topic prioritization in Year 1 and a focus on launching at least one LC during Year 2. As of Fall 2024, the Hypertension Learning Community that initiated with our D2I event in January 2023 has been meeting regularly and continues to develop strategy and focus with the participation of invested patients, researchers, and clinicians.
PaTH sites have committed to providing a specified level of data support for the nascent LC as well as administrative support for organizing and documenting LC meetings, offsetting the cost of launch. However, the need to secure additional funds to support LC activities is a challenge the group faces.

5

## CONCLUSIONS
The PaTH D2I Initiative format is an innovative, engaged, and scalable model for involving constituents throughout our health systems' strata in prioritizing topics for future improvements. Throughout the planning and execution, the workgroup focused on the purpose in the process. We maintained a patient‐centered focus in our curation of research articles, development of community partner packets, engagement during the interactive teleconference, and foundation‐laying for future LCs. At each stage, the workgroup sought to increase the contributions of those directly affected by the health issues being discussed. Additionally, the workgroup both practiced and preached embracing uncertainty in the outcomes of each of the D2I stages and maintaining an openness to the discovery of unfamiliar perspectives and ideas that may instrumentally shape future actions.
