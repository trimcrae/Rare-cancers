## Background
Coagulation dysfunction significantly impacts sepsis prognosis. Standardized methods for evaluating anticoagulant efficacy and safety in this population remain lacking. This study aimed to assess the efficacy and safety of anticoagulant therapy in sepsis patients.

## Methods
We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing anticoagulants against placebo/no treatment in sepsis patients. The reduction in 28/30-day all-cause mortality or the regression of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) was regarded as efficacy endpoints, while bleeding complications constituted the most prevalent adverse events.

## Results
Eighteen RCTs involving 8053 patients were included. Anticoagulant therapy demonstrated an 8% mortality risk reduction versus placebo (relative risk [RR] 0.92, 95% CI 0.86–0.98; P = 0.02). In six studies of baseline DIC patients, anticoagulants showed non-significant mortality reduction (RR 0.87, 95% CI 0.62–1.22; P = 0.42) but significantly enhanced DIC regression (RR 1.62, 95% CI 1.32-2.00; P < 0.00001). Anticoagulants increased bleeding risk (RR 1.32, 95% CI 1.16–1.49; P < 0.0001).

## Conclusion
Anticoagulant therapy confers survival benefit in the overall sepsis population despite increased bleeding risk. While improving DIC regression in sepsis-associated DIC, mortality reduction in this subgroup lacked statistical significance. Further research should clarify anticoagulants’ role in DIC-specific sepsis management.

## Introduction
Sepsis is a life-threatening clinical syndrome arising from dysregulated host responses to infection [ , ]. The WHO’s 2017 global sepsis assessment documented approximately 48.9 million cases and 11 million deaths annually, representing 19.7% of global mortality [ ]. Sepsis remains a leading cause of global health loss. Despite the fact that adherence to the Surviving Sepsis Campaign has led to a decline in sepsis mortality in recent times, the diagnosis and treatment of sepsis continue to constitute a central focus within global health research endeavors [ , ].
In 2017, the Scientific and Standardization Committee (SSC) of the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis (ISTH) proposed the concept and diagnostic criteria of sepsis-induced coagulopathy (SIC) [ ]. It is employed for the early detection of coagulation dysfunction in patients with sepsis and for the determination of target groups for treatment.
SIC involves complex pathophysiology beyond platelet/coagulation factor depletion, characterized by relative insufficiency from increased consumption and impaired synthesis. The mechanism is thought to involve excessive activation of the coagulation system, relative insufficiency of the physiological anticoagulation system, inhibition of the fibrinolysis system, activation of platelets, formation of neutrophil extracellular traps, release of microsomes, and alteration of the vascular endothelial glycocalyx structure [ , ]. This imbalance between procoagulant and anticoagulant mechanisms predisposes to microvascular thrombosis and bleeding complications, potentially progressing to DIC with multiorgan dysfunction [ ].
Coagulation abnormalities correlate with escalating organ dysfunction and mortality risks in sepsis [ ], occurring in 50–70% of patients, with DIC incidence approximating 35% [ ]. Therefore, it is plausible to hypothesize that the application of anticoagulants to restrain the overactivated coagulation cascade might be conducive to the alleviation of DIC and the diminution of mortality among patients with sepsis.
In recent decades, several anticoagulants have been used as adjuvant therapy for patients with sepsis and have been evaluated [ – ]. These encompass recombinant human thrombomodulin (rhTM), recombinant human activated protein C (rhAPC), antithrombin (AT), tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI), unfractionulated heparin (UFH), and low-molecular weight heparin (LMWH). However, they have not led to a substantial reduction in mortality, and the potential risk of bleeding cannot be overlooked. Nevertheless, several observational studies and subgroup analyses of RCTs suggest [ – ] that anticoagulant therapy may reduce mortality in patients with disseminated intravascular coagulation caused by sepsis. Studies have demonstrated [ – ] that administering rhTM or AT to patients with sepsis-induced DIC can decrease mortality. The possible reasons for discrepant outcomes include differences in the timing of anticoagulant therapy and significant patient heterogeneity. Overall, there is a lack of a unified understanding regarding the efficacy and safety of anticoagulant therapy in sepsis patients.
The objective of this study is to carry out a meta-analysis of the currently available RCTs, with the intention of comparing the efficacy and safety of anticoagulant therapy in patients afflicted with sepsis and thereby providing theoretical underpinning for the clinical management of this condition.

## Methods

## Endpoints
Primary endpoints were sepsis patient survival and DIC regression. Survival was defined as the proportion of patients alive during the study period, while DIC regression was defined as significant improvement of symptoms during treatment. Since there is a close relationship between sepsis and DIC, we thus used survival and DIC regression as endpoints for pooled analysis. The available literature suggests that successful control of DIC may significantly improve survival outcomes in patients with sepsis [ , ]. Therefore, using survival and DIC regression as endpoints for pooled analysis aims to provide a more comprehensive understanding, reflecting the combined impact of anticoagulation therapy on the overall prognosis of patients with sepsis.

## Search strategy
This study followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines, with the checklist provided in Supplementary File S2. A methodical search was carried out on PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library to identify RCTs published prior to November 2023. The initial records retrieved from each database were as follows: PubMed (n = 4695), Embase (n = 5335), and Cochrane Library (n = 560). Duplicates were removed using EndNote X9 followed by manual verification. The following search keywords were utilized: ((sepsis) OR (bloodstream infection) OR (pyemia) OR (pyemias) OR (septicemia)) AND ((anticoagulants) OR (blood coagulation factor inhibitors) OR (heparin) OR (LMWH) OR (rTM) OR (ART- 123) OR (TFPI) OR (antithrombins) OR (APC) OR (dabigatran) OR (rivaroxaban) OR (warfarin) OR (fondaparinux) OR (gabexate)).

## Inclusion and exclusion criteria
Inclusion criteria were: (1) RCTs; (2) Patients with sepsis; (3) Evaluation of the impact of anticoagulant therapy on mortality, DIC regression rate and bleeding complications in patients with sepsis; (4) Age ≥ 18 years; (5) Access to the full text.
Exclusion criteria were: (1) Repetition of previous studies; (2) Review articles, meta-analyses, case reports, and observational studies; (3) Animal experiments; (4) Inconsistent research content; (5) Combined use of multiple anticoagulants; (6) Age < 18 years; (7) Full text not available.
Language policy: No publication language restrictions; however, to ensure screening quality, non-English records underwent professional translation of titles and abstracts during screening, and eligible full texts were translated.
To isolate the therapeutic effects of specific anticoagulants, we excluded combination regimens for the following reasons: First, to control for confounding factors, as combination regimens may introduce drug interactions that lead to variations in efficacy and safety, potentially obscuring the true treatment effect of individual agents. Second, to maintain scientific rigor, by excluding combination therapies, we were able to more clearly identify and quantify the effect of a single anticoagulant in sepsis patients, thereby providing more direct evidence for clinical decision-making. Third, for clinical applicability; although combination anticoagulant therapy is sometimes used in practice, evaluating single agents offers clearer guidance for clinicians, particularly during initial treatment and therapy optimization. This approach was informed by multiple studies suggesting that combination anticoagulant therapy may lead to inconsistent efficacy and increased bleeding risks [ , ], underscoring the importance of isolated drug assessment and providing a scientific basis for our exclusion criteria.

## Literature screening and data extraction
Two investigators (QW, JYW) independently screened studies and performed data extraction; meanwhile, duplicate risk-of-bias assessments were conducted by QW and WHT. Any conflicts were resolved by a third author (HZ). Reviewers conducted comprehensive literature searches and assessed full texts. Data screening and extraction followed predefined inclusion/exclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. Persisting discrepancies underwent arbitration by HZ.
During data extraction, we collected information including the author’s name, publication year, research location, number of centers, patient count, type of anticoagulant used, and outcome measurements. The definition of DIC must meet the diagnostic criteria. Various definitions of DIC have been proposed by individual study authors, such as the Japanese Ministry of Health and Welfare (JMHW) DIC standard, the International Society of Thrombosis and Hemostasis (ISTH) dominant DIC standard, the ISTH non-dominant DIC standard, and the Japan Acute Medical Association (JAAM) DIC standard or the author’s original standard. The study’s primary outcome was the all-cause mortality rate at 28 days. If this value was not available, the mortality rate at 30 or 90 days or another time point was used for the meta-analysis. Secondary outcomes included the rate of DIC regression and bleeding complications. DIC diagnosis was based on various criteria. DIC regression was defined as the failure to meet these criteria following anticoagulant therapy.

## Literature quality assessment
The quality of the literature was evaluated using the Cochrane Systematic Evaluation manual’s recommended quality evaluation method. The evaluation method included seven aspects: (1) Random sequence generation; (2) Allocation concealment; (3) Blinding of participants and personnel; (4) Blinding of outcome assessment; (5) Incomplete outcome data; (6) Selective reporting; (7) Other bias. Each aspect was evaluated independently and strictly. According to the criteria recommended by the Cochrane Collaboration, we applied a uniform standard for assessing the risk of bias associated with a single randomized controlled trial, and determined ‘low risk of bias’, ‘unclear’ and ‘high risk of bias’ for each item.

## Statistical analysis
Data analysis was performed using Review Manager 5.4.1. The Mantel-Haenszel method was used to analyze the dichotomous variables. The results for relative risk (RR) were presented with a 95% confidence interval (CI). Statistical significance was considered at a P value of less than 0.05. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 index and P value [ , ]. If I2 was less than 50% and the P value was greater than 0.1, the results for each group were considered to have no statistical heterogeneity or only small heterogeneity, and the fixed effect model was used for analysis. On the contrary, it means that there is at least moderate heterogeneity, and the reliability of the results can be ensured by subgroup analysis or sensitivity analysis, changing the model, and removing the maximum weight or minimum weight method. The existence of publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of the funnel plot. In the statistical analysis, we employed appropriate multivariate analysis methods to ensure the rationality of pooled endpoint analysis. We used weighted regression analysis to reduce possible imbalance effects, and the fitness of the model was verified by examining the residuals of the model.

## Protocol and registration
Registration status: This systematic review was not prospectively registered on PROSPERO or other registries. The primary analysis phase commenced before registration opportunity was secured. We acknowledge that lack of prospective registration may introduce reporting bias risk, as protocol modifications during review progression lack advance documentation.
Protocol adherence: We rigorously followed the predefined protocol. Any deviations occurring during implementation with justifications are documented in the Methods section.

## Results

## Literature search
The PRISMA flow diagram, as shown in Fig.  , was selected for the meta-analysis. A total of 10,590 studies were retrieved. After excluding reviews, meta-analyses, case reports, and retrospective studies, 919 studies remained. After title and abstract screening, 748 studies were excluded. The remaining 171 studies were read in full, and 153 of these were excluded because they were duplicates, full text was not available, they were ongoing, or they did not meet our inclusion criteria. Ultimately, we included 18 studies, which involved a total of 8053 patients.

Table displays the fundamental features of the studies included, with 8 international multicenter RCTs [ , – ]. One multicenter randomized controlled trial was conducted in Japan [ ]. The remaining 9 RCTs were single-center studies conducted in various countries [ , , , , – ]. All studies focused on sepsis patients.

 

## Results of bias risk assessment
Figures  and present the results of the bias risk assessment in detail. Overall, only three studies were found to have a high risk of bias due to non-double-blind or random allocation based on hospitalization numbers. Eight studies had a low risk of bias, and the remaining eight studies had an unknown bias risk. Of these, six studies were randomly assigned, but the authors did not specify, and two studies did not describe whether they were double-blind. The primary source of bias in the trials included was the insufficient description of allocation concealment.

 

 

## Mortality rate
All 18 RCTs reported all-cause mortality either at 28/30 days or at the end of the study. The RR of the included RCTs ranged from 0.57 to 1.76. Only one trial found a protective effect of anticoagulant therapy on mortality, while the other 17 studies found no effect. Figure  displays the pooled RR of all eligible patients with death, indicating that anticoagulant therapy is associated with lower mortality (1142/4108 [27.8%] vs. 1187/3945 [30.1%]; RR 0.92, 95% CI 0.86 to 0.98, P = 0.02). The anticoagulant group showed a 8% reduction in the risk of death. The difference between the two groups was statistically significant, and no heterogeneity as observed (I2 = 3%, P = 0.42). The pooled RR of anticoagulants in each subgroup ranged from 0.67 to 0.99, and the heterogeneity among different anticoagulants was not statistically significant (I2 = 0%, P = 0.69). The mortality rate of each anticoagulant intervention group was lower than that of the control group, and the RR value of the gabexate group was the lowest (0.67). The RR value of the TFPI group was the highest (0.99). However, only the AT group was statistically significant (P = 0.04), and no heterogeneity was observed within the group (I2 = 0%, P = 0.85). Six studies analyzed the mortality of patients with DIC at baseline, with a total of 356 patients. Figure  shows that anticoagulant therapy could reduce the mortality of patients with sepsis and DIC, but the results were not statistically significant. The pooled RR was 0.87 (95% CI 0.62 ~ 1.22, P = 0.42), and no heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 0%, P = 0.55). AT can significantly reduce the mortality of sepsis patients with DIC, but the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.06).

 

 

## DIC regression
Six RCTs reported on the regression of DIC during treatment in patients with DIC at baseline. The included RCTs had an RR ranging from 1.18 to 15, and four trials found a positive effect of anticoagulant therapy on DIC regression. Figure  displays the pooled RR of DIC regression in all eligible patients. The incidence of DIC regression in the anticoagulant group increased by 62%, and the difference between the two groups was statistically significant (P < 0.00001; RR 1.62, 95% CI 1.32-2). No significant heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 45%, P = 0.11). The pooled RR of anticoagulants in each subgroup ranged from 0.67 to 0.97, and there was significant heterogeneity among different anticoagulants (I2 = 85.9%, P = 0.008). Both the AT and rhTM groups showed positive results with a P value of less than 0.05.

 

## Bleeding complications
Bleeding complications were reported in 12 trials involving 7947 patients. The risk ratio (RR) of the included RCTs ranged from 0.47 to 7.43, with three trials showing a significant increase in the risk of bleeding complications associated with anticoagulant therapy. Figure  displays the incidence of bleeding complications in all eligible patients, as pooled with RR. The pooled RR indicates that anticoagulant therapy is associated with bleeding (RR 1.32, 95% CI 1.16–1.49; P < 0.0001), with no observed heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, P = 0.57). The pooled RR of anticoagulants in each subgroup ranged from 1.01 to 7.43, with no heterogeneity observed between different anticoagulants (I2 = 0%, P = 0.52). The incidence of bleeding complications in each subgroup of anticoagulant therapy was higher than that in the control group, and the difference between the AT group and the APC group was statistically significant (P = 0.01 and 0.008).

## Discussions

 

This meta-analysis of 18 RCTs (n = 8053) demonstrates that anticoagulant therapy is associated with a significant 8% reduction in 28-day all-cause mortality (RR 0.92, 95% CI 0.86–0.98) in the overall sepsis population. However, this survival benefit is counterbalanced by a 32% increased risk of bleeding complications (RR 1.32, 95% CI 1.16–1.49). In the subgroup of patients with baseline DIC, anticoagulants significantly enhanced coagulopathy resolution by 62% (RR 1.62, 95% CI 1.32-2.00; P < 0.00001) but did not significantly reduce mortality (RR 0.87, 95% CI 0.62–1.22; P = 0.42), likely due to limited statistical power (n = 356) and substantial clinical heterogeneity.
Our finding of a modest overall mortality benefit appears to contrast with a recent large meta-analysis by Umemura et al. [ ] (24 RCTs), which concluded that anticoagulant therapy did not significantly reduce short-term mortality (pooled RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.92–1.02, P = 0.25). This discrepancy can be primarily attributed to key methodological distinctions. Unlike Umemura et al., our analysis explicitly excluded studies investigating combined anticoagulant regimens and focused solely on populations receiving single-agent anticoagulant therapy (monotherapy). This difference in inclusion criteria likely explains the divergent conclusions. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. First, the severity of illness among included patients was not consistently reported. Neither of the two incorporated studies provided detailed information on disease severity scores such as SOFA or APACHE II, which may have contributed to substantial heterogeneity in patient prognosis. Second, variability in DIC diagnostic criteria could have introduced additional clinical heterogeneity. Different studies applied diverse definitions (e.g., JAAM, JMHW, ISTH), each with relatively small sample sizes, limiting comparability across studies. Third, there was considerable diversity in anticoagulant regimens. Both studies included multiple agents with different mechanisms of action, dosing regimens, and timing of administration, which might have diluted any specific drug-related benefit. Taken together, the lack of uniform disease severity assessment, heterogeneous diagnostic standards, and variations in drug types and dosing likely reduced the statistical power of our analysis and masked possible therapeutic benefits. These findings underscore the need for future studies to adopt standardized DIC diagnostic criteria, perform rigorous stratification by illness severity, and focus on specific anticoagulant mechanisms to more accurately evaluate the true clinical efficacy of anticoagulant therapy in sepsis-associated DIC. Notably, both meta-analyses consistently demonstrated a significant and comparable increase in bleeding risk, underscoring a critical class-wide safety concern. Furthermore, Umemura et al. reported a significant mortality reduction in sepsis-associated DIC patients (RR 0.72, 95% CI 0.62–0.85; P < 0.01), a benefit we observed as a strong, non-significant trend (RR 0.87) in our smaller DIC cohort. The lack of statistical significance in our analysis is almost certainly due to insufficient power and greater clinical heterogeneity within our included studies.
In the present meta-analysis, we found that anticoagulant therapy significantly improved the resolution rate of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) in patients with sepsis, whereas its impact on overall mortality remained statistically insignificant. This divergence suggests that the recovery of coagulation abnormalities does not necessarily translate into improved survival outcomes, reflecting the multifactorial nature of sepsis-related death.

Pathophysiological Discrepancy Between Coagulopathy Correction and Survival

The resolution of DIC represents the normalization of hemostatic parameters and partial restoration of coagulation balance. However, sepsis-induced mortality is influenced by a broader range of systemic processes—including dysregulated inflammation, endothelial injury, immune paralysis, and multiple organ dysfunction—that extend beyond the coagulation cascade. Therefore, while anticoagulant therapy may effectively reverse microthrombosis and improve laboratory indices, it may not adequately modulate the downstream inflammatory and metabolic cascades that ultimately determine survival [ ]. 

(2)Mechanistic Differences Between Antithrombin and Thrombomodulin

The two main anticoagulants examined—AT and TM—exert distinct pharmacological effects. AT primarily inhibits thrombin and factor Xa, thereby attenuating hypercoagulability but offering limited anti-inflammatory or endothelial-protective benefits. In contrast, TM not only regulates thrombin activity but also modulates the protein C system and suppresses high-mobility group box-1 (HMGB1)-mediated inflammation, suggesting broader protective potential [ ]. Nonetheless, the survival advantage of TM may have been attenuated by patient heterogeneity, suboptimal dosing, or delayed initiation of therapy in the included studies. Thus, both agents may contribute to DIC resolution, but their impact on mortality depends on timing, patient selection, and concomitant organ dysfunction.

(3)Clinical and Methodological Considerations

Several methodological and clinical factors may also account for the dissociation between these two outcomes. Mortality is a late and complex endpoint that reflects cumulative pathophysiological insults, whereas DIC resolution is an earlier and more specific indicator of coagulation recovery. In the included trials, variations in disease severity, infection source, timing of anticoagulant administration, and concurrent standard therapies (e.g., antibiotics, fluid resuscitation, vasopressors) likely introduced heterogeneity, potentially masking a survival benefit [ ]. Additionally, the sample sizes of DIC subgroups were relatively small, reducing the statistical power to detect modest differences in mortality.

(4)Implications and Future Directions

The findings suggest that anticoagulant therapy can effectively correct coagulation abnormalities but may not independently alter the ultimate outcome of sepsis unless combined with interventions targeting inflammation and organ protection. Future studies should aim to identify phenotypically distinct subgroups of sepsis-associated DIC that may derive greater survival benefit from anticoagulation, and evaluate early, individualized combination strategies integrating anticoagulant, anti-inflammatory, and endothelial-stabilizing approaches [ ]. 
The coagulation phenotype in sepsis is fundamentally distinct from that in non-septic states. Evidence indicates [ , ] that the coagulation process in sepsis is intimately linked to the body’s innate immune system. Immune cells and complement components interact with platelets, leading to localized thrombosis within the microvasculature. This physiological process, termed ‘immunothrombosis,’ serves to identify, contain, and eliminate pathogens, thereby reducing their survival and dissemination. The localized nature of immunothrombosis typically preserves global organ perfusion. While this innate immune response is initially beneficial, persistent and excessive inflammation can trigger uncontrolled thrombotic activation, representing the initial phase in the development of DIC [ , ]. This may culminate in extensive thrombosis and thrombotic disease, deviating from its original host defense role. Our analysis demonstrates that anticoagulant therapy can enhance DIC resolution in patients with established coagulopathy at baseline.
The divergent efficacy among anticoagulants may stem from their distinct pharmacological mechanisms. Heparin—despite its widespread clinical use—demonstrated no mortality benefit in recent sepsis-specific meta-analyses [ ]. This suggests its primary value remains thromboprophylaxis rather than targeted sepsis treatment, contrasting with rhTM and AT, which showed significant coagulopathy improvement. The limited efficacy of heparin likely arises from its inadequate modulation of the Protein C and thrombomodulin systems, insufficient suppression of NETosis-mediated immunothrombosis, and failure to restore endothelial barrier function.
The timing of anticoagulant intervention emerges as another crucial factor influencing outcomes. In an observational study [ ] involving 1787 patients with sepsis-related DIC, the 28-day survival rates were 66.4%, 48.5%, and 31.1% when anticoagulant therapy was commenced on the day of diagnosis, 4 days after, and more than 7 days after diagnosis, respectively. These findings imply that earlier initiation of therapy following the diagnosis of coagulopathy might confer more pronounced survival advantages. The decision to use anticoagulant therapy must therefore hinge on a careful balance between its potential efficacy (e.g., promoting DIC resolution and mortality reduction) and its inherent safety risks (e.g., increased bleeding).
Our study exhibits several notable strengths, including the exclusive inclusion of RCTs and a focused analysis on monotherapy regimens, which enhances the internal validity of our conclusions regarding the effects of single-agent anticoagulation. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The most significant concern is the absence of prospective registration on PROSPERO or equivalent platforms, which introduces a risk of selective reporting bias despite our adherence to an internal protocol.
Furthermore, substantial clinical heterogeneity across the included RCTs poses considerable interpretative challenges. These studies span decades during which sepsis definitions evolved (e.g., from SEP-1 to SEP-3), and no universally standardized diagnostic criteria for DIC existed. We accepted diverse diagnostic standards, including: the JMHW criteria, the ISTH overt/non-overt criteria, the JAAM criteria, and author-defined study-specific thresholds. Marked variations in the sensitivity, specificity, and pathological staging thresholds of these criteria generated heterogeneous patient cohorts. This fundamentally compromises the direct comparability of clinical outcomes (e.g., DIC resolution rates, mortality), anticoagulation efficacy assessments, and prognostic interpretations across studies. Consequently, this diagnostic inconsistency may impair the accuracy of our pooled estimates and challenge the external validity of our conclusions across different DIC subtypes, necessitating cautious interpretation.
Additional sources of heterogeneity include: (1) variability in anticoagulant types (e.g., heparin vs. thrombomodulin), formulations, dosing regimens, treatment durations, and agents with distinct pharmacological profiles; In this study, we aggregated various anticoagulant therapies, including rTM, AT, APC, and heparin, under the general category of “anticoagulant treatment” for meta-analysis. While this approach offers a broad overview of the potential effects of anticoagulation on sepsis outcomes, it inherently carries certain limitations. First, these drugs have significantly different mechanisms of action, not only in terms of anticoagulant potency but also in their additional anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, or endothelial protective effects [ ]. For example, rTM works by binding to thrombin, forming a complex that activates the protein C pathway, which not only inhibits coagulation but also downregulates inflammatory cytokines, reduces endothelial damage, and improves microcirculatory function. In contrast, heparin’s primary mechanism is to enhance antithrombin’s inhibitory effect on thrombin and factor Xa, with purely anticoagulant effects and limited anti-inflammatory or immunomodulatory actions [ ]. Although antithrombin also inhibits thrombin, its anti-inflammatory effects depend on its binding to endothelial heparan sulfate, which may be less effective in patients with severe endothelial injury [ ]. APC, on the other hand, has dual functions as an anticoagulant and anti-inflammatory agent, but its clinical efficacy varies depending on the patient population and timing of administration [ ]. Secondly, the different mechanisms of action of these drugs likely result in differential impacts on the complex pathophysiology of sepsis, including inflammation, microcirculatory disturbance, and multi-organ dysfunction. Combining these therapies under a single “anticoagulant treatment” category may obscure the real heterogeneity between drugs, leading to potential bias or dilution of the overall effect, thereby limiting the interpretability and external applicability of the findings [ ]. Additionally, the limited number of studies or variability in study quality for some therapies may further amplify uncertainties introduced by pooling the data. It is noteworthy that a significant reduction in mortality was observed only in the AT subgroup in this study, suggesting that AT may have a unique biological effect in the treatment of sepsis. AT not only reduces microthrombus formation by inhibiting thrombin activity but also regulates the inflammatory response, improves endothelial function, and inhibits the release of inflammatory mediators through interaction with glycosaminoglycans [ ]. Antithrombin supplementation in sepsis-associated disseminated intravascular coagulation. This composite mechanism may make AT more advantageous in controlling the imbalance between coagulation and inflammation associated with sepsis. In contrast, other therapies, which primarily target coagulation, such as heparin, may have limited impact on systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) and organ dysfunction, potentially explaining the absence of significant clinical improvement in these groups. In conclusion, the diversity of mechanisms of action among anticoagulant therapies and their differential impacts on the pathophysiological processes of sepsisant drugs based on their mechanisms, ideally through biomarker stratification or immunophenotyping, and randomized controlled trials designed with these considerations will help better define which patient populations may benefit most from specific anticoagulant or anti-inflammatory treatments. (2) inconsistent definitions and reporting of bleeding events, where some studies documented only major bleeding events using variable definitions, while our inclusive analytic approach to bleeding complications (incorporating all reported events) risks outcome measurement bias; and (3) the limited sample size of the baseline DIC cohort (n = 356), which restricted the statistical power for this subgroup analysis.
While the exclusion criteria employed in this study were methodologically necessary to isolate the effect of monotherapy, they also introduce a limitation regarding the generalizability of our findings. By excluding patients on combination anticoagulant therapy, our conclusions may not directly extend to real-world clinical settings where combination regimens are sometimes used, particularly for complex or high-risk patients. Therefore, future research specifically designed to investigate combination therapy regimens is needed to validate the applicability of these findings in broader and more complex patient populations.
Finally, due to a lack of comprehensive reporting of baseline characteristics in the included studies (e.g., differences between ICU and non-ICU settings, disease etiology, demographic information, comorbidities, and clinical severity scores), we were unable to perform stratified analyses to fully understand treatment effects across different patient populations. Although the existing studies provide valuable clinical data, differences in individual characteristics may lead to heterogeneous treatment responses, influencing the development of tailored treatment strategies. Future studies should aim to provide more detailed baseline information and conduct stratified analyses to better assess the efficacy and safety of anticoagulant therapy in various patient subgroups.

## Conclusion
Anticoagulant therapy demonstrates a modest but significant survival benefit in the general sepsis population (8% relative risk reduction), offset by a 31% increase in bleeding risk. For patients with sepsis-associated disseminated intravascular coagulation, anticoagulants significantly improve coagulopathy resolution (62% relative increase), without conferring statistically significant mortality reduction. Risk-benefit assessment is paramount, particularly in high-bleeding-risk patients.
