## Abstract

## Introduction
Index case testing (ICT) is an evidence-based approach that efficiently identifies persons in need of HIV treatment and prevention services. In Malawi, delivery of ICT has faced challenges due to limited technical capacity of healthcare workers (HCWs) and clinical coordination. Digitisation of training and quality improvement processes presents an opportunity to address these challenges. We developed an implementation package that combines digital and face-to-face modalities (blended learning) to strengthen HCWs ICT skills and enhance quality improvement mechanisms. This cluster randomised controlled trial will assess the impact of the blended learning implementation package compared with the standard of care (SOC) on implementation, effectiveness and cost-effectiveness outcomes.

## Methods and analysis
The study was conducted in 33 clusters in Machinga and Balaka districts, in Southern Malawi from November 2021 to November 2023. Clusters are randomised in a 2:1 ratio to the SOC versus blended learning implementation package. The SOC is composed of: brief face-to-face HCW ICT training and routine face-to-face facility mentorship for HCWs. The blended learning implementation package consists of blended teaching, role-modelling, practising, and providing feedback, and blended quality improvement processes. The primary implementation outcome is HCW fidelity to ICT over 1 year of follow-up. Primary service uptake outcomes include (a) index clients who participate in ICT, (b) contacts elicited, (c) HIV self-test kits provided for secondary distribution, (d) contacts tested and (e) contacts identified as HIV-positive. Service uptake analyses will use a negative binomial mixed-effects model to account for repeated measures within each cluster. Cost-effectiveness will be assessed through incremental cost-effectiveness ratios examining the incremental cost of each person tested.

## Ethics and dissemination
The Malawi National Health Science Research Committee, the University of North Carolina and the Baylor College of Medicine Institutional Review Boards approved the trial. Study findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals and conference presentations.

## STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY
This study will evaluate the impact of an innovative blended learning approach on healthcare workers capacity-building to implement index case testing services.
This cluster randomised controlled trial is an appropriate design for addressing a facility-level intervention and provides a balanced design across calendar time. A range of meaningful implementation, effectiveness and cost-effectiveness outcomes will be evaluated, with opportunities for informing local policy.
It will be difficult to attribute any changes to specific elements of the multifaceted capacity-building approach, a limitation.
Results will likely be generalisable to a range of Malawian healthcare settings, but adaptation may be required in countries with different contexts.

## Introduction
Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) has the highest HIV prevalence globally. Despite the scale-up of HIV testing services, most countries in SSA have not achieved the first of the UNAIDS 95-95-95 goals. Achieving the first 95, which entails that 95% of the people living with HIV (PLHIV) aware of their HIV status will require targeted innovative HIV testing approaches. Index case testing (ICT), an evidence-based intervention, involves diagnosed PLHIV (index clients) recruiting their contacts (sexual partners and children) for HIV testing. With optimal implementation, ICT can efficiently identify PLHIV in need of HIV treatment and high-risk HIV-negative individuals requiring prevention services. However, suboptimal ICT implementation has led to missed opportunities in diagnosing PLHIV in need of life-saving treatment. Strategies are needed to improve ICT implementation in SSA. We conducted a pilot test and found promising early implementation and effectiveness results over a short period, but it is necessary to evaluate these strategies over a longer period and in more facilities. 
Blended learning is a promising approach, which combines digital and face-to-face learning and can be used to support healthcare workers (HCWs) in building technical skills in ICT implementation. Blended learning can synergise the best aspects of both modalities to improve HCW capacity. WHO has recognised blended learning as a promising implementation strategy for HCW capacity building in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs). Despite its promise, blended learning has not been rigorously evaluated for impact on HCW skills and a full trajectory of clinical and cost outcomes in LMIC.
To address this gap, the primary objective of this study is to assess the impact of a blended learning implementation package compared with the standard of care (SOC) on HCW behaviour, its downstream effect on ICT patient outcomes and cost-effectiveness. We hypothesise that this blended learning implementation package will improve HCW fidelity to ICT implementation, lead to improved ICT effectiveness in testing contacts and identifying persons living with HIV who need treatment and high-risk HIV-negative individuals who need HIV prevention interventions and be cost-effective within the Malawian context.

## Methods and analysis

## Study setting and rationale
This study is conducted in the Balaka and Machinga districts of Malawi, in South-eastern Africa from November 2021 to November 2023. Malawi has an estimated population of 20 million, an adult HIV prevalence of 8.9% and an annual adult HIV incidence of 0.21%. Globally, Malawi has one of the lowest rates of qualified clinical HCWs (<2 clinicians per 100 000 people). To help address its formidable HIV burden with limited human resources, Malawi has a strong track record of innovative task shifting to lay HCW cadres, especially for HIV-related counselling tasks. As a result, more than 50% of the healthcare work force at health facilities throughout Malawi are lay HCWs. The use of lay HCWs has allowed for rapid and efficient scale up of HIV testing services. 
Centralised and synchronous face-to-face training is the most common implementation strategy to equip HCWs with skills to perform their duties in this setting. However, this strategy comes with considerable challenges, including high costs of travel, lodging and per diems for hotel-based trainings; breaks in care when multiple HCWs travel and train simultaneously; limited time for practice or feedback and variability in quality due to different trainers’ teaching ability. Additionally, central staff circulate to all facilities to support in-person continuous quality improvement (CQI) activities. This too presents challenges, such as insufficient central staff to support all facilities, high travel costs and difficulty tracking progress.
We recognised that integrating digital training and CQI elements could address some of these challenges. However, digital approaches have not been evaluated due to intermittent internet connections and low lay HCWs’ digital literacy. We sought to overcome some of these feasibility challenges through a carefully designed blended learning ICT implementation package, the impact of which we will evaluate in the Malawian setting.
Our study is being conducted at 14 health facilities in Balaka and 20 health facilities in Machinga that implement Malawi’s national HIV programme. The facilities are hospitals, health centres and dispensaries operated by Malawi Ministry Of Health (MOH) or Christian Association of Malawi (CHAM), a faith-based organisation. Both MOH and CHAM implement the MOH HIV programme which is free of charge. These 34 health facilities are supported by the Tingathe Programme, a Baylor College of Medicine Children’s Foundation Malawi USAID supported health service support programme that works in partnership with Malawi MOH. Tingathe has over 15 years of experience providing technical assistance for HIV testing, care and treatment programmes in Malawi, including ICT. 

## Study design and aims
We are conducting a cluster randomised controlled trial (RCT) with 34 facilities randomly assigned in a 2:1 ratio to the SOC or blended learning implementation package, respectively. The 2:1 randomisation ratio was used for feasibility. It was more feasible to initially roll out our enhanced package to one-third of the clusters than it was to roll it out to half. A 1:1 ratio provided only minor improvements in power according to our simulation studies, and thus we opted for 2:1. Because of the 2:1 randomisation, the total clusters needed to be divisible by 3; 2 neighbouring health facilities were combined to form 1 cluster, providing 33 facility clusters in total. The clusters were classified into six strata based on cluster-level characteristics—district (Balaka and Machinga), facility type (hospitals, health centres and dispensaries), and baseline number of index clients identified. There are two strata of health centres in Balaka, two strata of health centres in Machinga, one strata of dispensaries in Machinga and one strata of hospitals/very large health centres across both districts. Randomisation occurred within each stratum to help ensure balance of cluster-level characteristics between the randomisation arms. Within each stratum, the clusters were randomly assigned 2:1 (SOC : Enhanced) by an independent statistician using an algorithm coded in SAS V.9.4. Randomisation assignments were revealed prior to start of quarter one study activities. Due to the nature of the implementation strategies, study staff and investigators are not blinded to the study arm.
The study has three specific aims. We aim to compare (1) implementation outcomes, (2) service uptake outcomes and (3) incremental cost-effectiveness of the blended learning implementation package compared with the SOC ( ).
 Our work is guided by the RE-AIM framework. The five RE-AIM constructs—Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation and Maintenance—align with our research questions. Reach will be measured by evaluating the proportion of potential index clients who are offered ICT. Effectiveness will be evaluated by assessing the service uptake outcomes (described below). Adoption is proportion of the implementation package completed per cluster. Implementation will be assessed through ICT fidelity assessments. Maintenance will be examined through the consistency in effectiveness over time. 

## Malawi’s ICT programme
Implementation science examines the strategies used to bring evidence-based interventions, such as ICT, to scale. In Malawi, ICT involves HCWs supporting PLHIV (index clients) with recruitment and HIV testing of sexual partners and family members (contact clients). Since 2019, ICT has been conducted through the following four methods, voluntarily selected by the index client: (1) passive referral, in which the index client independently encourages contacts to receive HIV testing services or provides them with a self-test kit; (2) provider referral, in which an HCW independently trace contacts of index cases while maintaining index client anonymity; (3) contract referral, in which an HCW directly recruits a contact for testing only if the index client does not refer the contact themselves within an agreed amount of time and (4) dual referral, in which an HCW coordinates with index clients on HIV status disclosure and partner testing. ICT implementation requires counselling skills, including skills in discussing HIV status disclosure, sexual histories and other sensitive topics. In addition, implementation poses logistical and coordination challenges in tracing and finding contact clients in the community.

## Standard implementation
Tingathe programme oversees the standard ICT training and quality improvement activities in all its supported districts. In September 2021, at the time of our trial start, a brief 2-hour ICT training of key aspects of the MOH ICT programme was provided to every HCW at our 33 clusters as part of the Tingathe Programme core orientation for a new 5-year grant. The brief training was held in a central location, synchronous and conducted in-person (face to face). It occurred one time, was facilitator-delivered, was primarily didactic and provided an overview of ICT concepts, procedures and counselling approaches. Additionally, the Tingathe programme provides routine in-person monthly supervision to all health facilities for all its supported activities, including ICT. The study will not change anything in the standard implementation arm across all 33 clusters.

## Blended learning implementation package
In addition to standard implementation described above, the 11 clusters randomly assigned to the implementation package arm will receive both (1) the blended learning implementation package and (2) a series of monthly CQI sessions administered through a blended learning platform. Development of this package was based on formative work and is described elsewhere. Here, we describe what is included in the blended learning implementation package. The digital learning portion takes approximately 8 hours over 2–3 weeks, the face-to-face learning portion takes approximately 14 hours over a 2-day weekend, phone feedback takes about 1 hour and each CQI session takes 2 hours once a month ( ).
 The aims of the blended learning training are to describe the principles of ICT, model counselling skills, offer opportunities to practice interacting with index and contact clients, and provide individualised feedback to each HCW on their ICT counselling skills. The training is decentralised and composed of 14 sessions (5 sessions with digitised individual asynchronous learning sessions, 8 sessions with tablet-guided synchronous interactive small group sessions and 1 phone-based feedback session). Its development was informed by social cognitive theory and theory of expertise and generated from the standard implementation strategies and in consultation with key Tingathe Programme stakeholders and the Malawi MOH to ensure content is acceptable, informative and engaging. 
The five digital individual sessions contain an introduction to ICT, descriptions of ICT skills, video vignettes modelling these skills and embedded comprehension questions. Video content was supported by a professional videographer and created around a fictional Malawian woman newly diagnosed with HIV (index client) and her contact network. The total duration of the five sessions is 8 hours. These sessions are designed to be watched individually at one’s facility on a tablet. Tablets were provided to each facility with the content already downloaded on a SurveyCTO platform for staff to use.
The eight small-group face-to-face learning sessions are tablet-guided and contain practice role-plays, individual feedback and facilitated group discussions. These sessions are designed for a group of HCWs at a single facility in a decentralised location and were developed to be conducted over 1 weekend. The final portion is a decentralised phone session in which all HCWs receive personalised feedback via phone after demonstrating their ICT skills using a simulated encounter between an index client and contact client.
Quality improvement sessions are also delivered through a blended modality. HCWs participate in monthly sessions at their site to examine their facility’s ICT indicators, identify gaps and work towards actionable solutions. This CQIs portion contains six structured sessions, followed by monthly follow-up sessions. Sessions 1 and 2 consist of scenarios to reinforce HCW counselling skills. Sessions 3–6 consist of examining facility-specific indicators from the ICT cascade, including: identifying index clients, eliciting contact clients, supporting index clients with referral method selection, and tracing and testing contact clients. After session 6, follow-up sessions are conducted to revisit action plans and ongoing challenges. These quality improvement activities are guided by and documented in a tablet.

## Patient and public involvement
The implementation package was developed in consultation with key Tingathe Programme stakeholders and the Malawi MOH to ensure content is acceptable, informative and engaging.

## Participant recruitment and study activities
HCWs, index clients and contact clients will be recruited at each of the 33 clusters. Research personnel based in the two districts will orient health facility staff to the study procedures and worked with facility supervisors to identify eligible participants.
Prior to study enrolment, research personnel will collect data on cluster characteristics through conducting facility surveys. The data collected will include staffing, availability of space to deliver health services including ICT, modes of communication and transportation for tracing activities.
Recruitment of HCWs from the 33 clusters will occur at the beginning of the study (quarter 0) and then remain open to HCWs who become eligible at any point in the study period. HCWs must meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) ≥18 years of age, (2) working full time at one of the study health facilities and (3) staff in Malawi’s ICT programme. HCWs who cannot provide informed consent or undergo study procedures safely will be excluded. We will enrol a minimum of 2 HCWs per cluster (n=66) and up to 400 across all clusters. After enrolment, HCW participants will be followed for 1 year for primary study outcomes data collection. HCWs will fill out three surveys at three different time points, in the weeks before training, in the days after training, and 1 year after training, and have two audiorecorded simulated ICT sessions—one with an index client and one with a contact client in the quarter after the training. Simulated ICT sessions will be standardised counselling encounters with study staff simulating the role of an index or contact client. They will fill out time-and-motion assessments when tracing contacts. In addition, with support from facility supervisors, we purposively selected 26 HCWs involved in ICT to undergo in-depth interviews (IDIs) at three time points. We selected HCWs from both study arms, districts and strata to be able to compare HCW perceptions between arms over time. We wish to compare between study arms and explore potential differences by type of facility and district. Within these facilities, we purposively selected HCWs who were actively engaged in the ICT programme, as some participants were trained in these activities but more focused on other aspects of the HIV programme.
Index clients will be PLHIV who are newly or previously diagnosed with HIV at the participating health facilities. Contact clients will be their sexual partners, biological children, other household members or social contacts. These definitions are consistent with Malawi’s ICT programme. To be eligible for study participation, index and contact clients must be: (1) ≥15 years of age and (2) a potential index client, contact client or the parent/guardian of a paediatric index client or contact client. Index and contact clients who do not meet these criteria will be excluded. Index clients or contact clients who cannot provide informed consent or undergo study procedures safely will be excluded. Index and contact client participants will have all their study activities conducted at a single visit. We will enrol a minimum of two index clients and two contact clients per cluster. Each index and contact client will have their session with the counsellor audiorecorded (authentic ICT session), complete an exit interview and have their ICT data abstracted from paper-based MOH clinic registers. Authentic fidelity assessments will be conducted with a real (authentic) index or contact client. All adverse events will be recorded and reported to the principal investigator and appropriate regulatory authorities. The data personnel will review trial data weekly.

## Aim 1: implementation outcomes, analyses and power calculation
The primary set of outcomes in aim 1 is HCW fidelity to ICT counselling procedures ( ), defined as delivering ICT counselling as prescribed. Fidelity will be assessed using a 15-item tool assessing quality, completeness and adherence to ICT procedures with scores ranging from 0 to 100 percentage points. Items on the index tool include exploration of the client’s situation, selection of appropriate referral methods, support with client action plans and assessment of consensual and non-judgemental approaches. Coders blinded to the study arm will score each audiorecording for each item. Fidelity scores will be compared between arms using a general linear model fit using generalised estimating equations to account for potential correlation between observations at the cluster level. Fidelity scores for index and contact clients will be analysed separately. With 132 fidelity assessments from 33 clusters and assuming an SD of 20.5 percentage points and a working correlation estimate of 0.3 or smaller, we will have approximately 99% statistical power to detect a 30 percentage point difference in mean scores (90 vs 60) between the two arms. We will have >81% statistical power to detect a smaller difference of 15 percentage points (75 vs 60). Statistical power was calculated by inverting the closed-form sample size equation from section 4.1 of Liu and Liang, with the average cluster size used in place of n.
 Additional implementation outcomes include HCW knowledge, attitudes, acceptability and feasibility; index and contact client acceptability; and implementation package adoption. HCW knowledge and attitudes will be assessed using the adapted subscale from the ‘Measuring HIV Stigma and Discrimination among Health Facility Staff’ tool. HCW acceptability and feasibility will be examined through IDIs at three time points (indicated in ) with 26 HCWs from 14 clusters. Malawian study staff with extensive experience conducting qualitative research will interview HCWs to explore HCWs’ experiences with the standard and blended learning packages and ICT programme barriers and facilitators. IDI data will be transcribed, translated into English, thematically coded and analysed using the qualitative content analysis. Index and contact client acceptability will be examined using adapted validated Perception of Respectful Care Tool. Implementation package adoption will be evaluated through monitoring the proportion of the package taken up by the facility.
We will have data to examine HCW participation at each facility during formal training and CQI activities. This will allow us to examine the extent of interaction between HCWs during formal study activities. Additionally, we will have qualitative data examining reactions to the blended learning training and this includes their perceptions of interactivity during the training.

## Aim 2: service uptake outcomes, analyses and power calculations
As indicated in , we will measure five service uptake outcomes on a quarterly basis: (1) the number of index clients who participate in ICT, (2) the number of contact clients elicited from these index clients, (3) the number of HIV self-test kits distributed, (4) the number of contact clients with recorded HIV testing and (5) the number of contact clients newly diagnosed as living with HIV.
For the five ICT outcomes, facility-level data will be abstracted from index and contact registers on a quarterly basis. Each of these outcomes will be measured as a count per cluster per calendar quarter (3-month period). Facility-level data will be used because the facility (ie, cluster) is the primary unit of randomisation. The cluster-level dataset for the primary analyses will contain 132 follow-up observations (33 clusters ×4 quarters), 44 observations in the blended learning implementation package arm and 88 observations in the standard arm.
Each of the five ICT service uptake outcomes will be analysed using a negative binomial mixed-effects model accounting for quarterly repeated measures within each cluster. Each model will use a log link, contain a parameter for the randomisation arm and the natural-log transformed number of potential indexes will be incorporated as an offset term. The number of potential index clients is the total number of persons eligible for ICT at that facility. Rate ratios and 95% CIs will be used to estimate the effect sizes and quantify the precision of effect estimates. Our main analyses will compare the blended learning implementation package versus standard arms for rates of each outcome per the total number of potential indexes.
Sample size calculations were guided by data from the 33 clusters from 2019 to 2020 in the 2 districts. Across these 33 clusters, 4600 persons were diagnosed with HIV annually and another 49 000 were already taking ART. From this, we estimated approximately 407 total potential index clients per facility per quarter, 86 index clients (21%), 97 contacts elicited (24%), 12 self-test kits distributed (3%), 25 contacts tested (6%) and 3 contacts diagnosed with HIV (0.7%). These values informed our assumptions for the standard arm.
The following parameters were applied to a simulation study of the health facilities in SAS V.9.4 for power calculations. To compute power, the simulated study data were analysed using a negative binomial mixed-effects model accounting for repeated measures over time within a cluster. Statistical power was computed using simulation studies of 10 000 cluster RCT datasets. Using these assumptions and a type I error rate α=0.05, we have 80%–85% statistical power to detect a difference between the arms for each of the outcomes of interest ( ).
 

## Aim 3: cost-effectiveness outcomes and analyses
For aim 3, the primary outcome is the budget impact and cost-effectiveness of the blended learning implementation package, compared with the standard ICT implementation. An empirical costing analysis will be embedded in our trial. Costs will be collected in two ways: microcosting and time-and-motion assessments (observations of HCW time devoted to ICT tasks). Microcosting will include costs of blended learning implementation package development, training expenses, time engaged in face-to-face and individually directed (ie, tablet based) learning, tablets, and other supplies, and oversights. Retrospectively, cost data will be extracted from inventory, programme accounts and human resource records. In each cluster, data for time and motion assessment will be collected in two ways: (1) HCW self-report and surveys and (2) through observation by research staff.
We will first conduct a budget impact analysis, evaluating the total incremental costs associated with the development and implementation of the blended learning implementation package. Since this enhanced package is deployed alongside standard ICT training and programming, all dedicated resources to this package are assumed to be incurred in addition to any costs associated with the standard ICT package. We will evaluate mean programmatic costs at a system and per facility basis, including modelling the potential budget impact of expanding the enhanced strategy to additional districts.
We will conduct a cost-effectiveness analysis, using a decision tree approach to model incremental cost-effectiveness ratios per contact tested and per PLHIV diagnosed. This model will first be limited to the estimated total potential index patients in the 33 clusters over 1 year, and then expanded to the national ICT programme estimates over 1, 3 and 5 years. ICT uptake outcomes will inform base-case estimates (aim 2). One-way sensitivity will be conducted with all model parameters individually. For the evaluation of incremental cost-effectiveness per PLHV diagnosed, we will evaluate two scenarios: (1) including any positive test among referred contacts and (2) limiting to persons with new HIV diagnoses. Probabilistic uncertainty will also be conducted, varying all parameters simultaneously. Cost-effectiveness analyses will be performed using Excel or comparable software.

## Ethics and dissemination
The trial protocol version 3.0 dated 10 January 2023 has been approved by the Malawi National Health Science Research Committee (#20/06/2566), University of North Carolina Institutional Review Board (20-1810) and the Baylor College of Medicine Institutional Review Board (H-48800). Protocol amendments will be submitted to all three regulatory bodies.
Written informed consent is obtained from all study participants prior to enrolment in an appropriate language (English or Chichewa) using forms approved by the Malawi NHSRC, UNC IRB and Baylor IRB. Research staff fluent in the consenting language will be responsible for reviewing the consent form in detail and addressing any questions before the participant signs while maintaining confidentiality.
The results of the study will be reported to the Malawi MOH and local stakeholders, presented at local and international scientific meetings and published in scientific journals. The results will also be submitted to the Malawi NHSRC. Datasets generated and/or analysed during the current study will be available on request.

## Discussion
In SSA, many countries, including Malawi, have adopted ICT as an evidence-based intervention to increase efficient identification of PLHIV, however, limited availability of skilled HCWs and resources may limit implementation of this intervention. To achieve optimal ICT implementation, it is critical to evaluate implementation strategies that could enhance ICT delivery at scale in resource-limited healthcare settings. Specifically, this trial aims to determine whether a blended learning implementation package enhances ICT implementation, increases HIV service uptake and is cost-effective at scale.
This will be the first implementation study of a blended learning implementation package targeting HCW behaviour and patient outcomes in resource-limited healthcare settings. Notably, a small pilot study conducted in Mulanje, Malawi evaluated the blended learning implementation package outlined in this paper and demonstrated that the package was acceptable, improved ICT fidelity and increased service uptake over a brief period (4 months) at a limited number of facilities. While these strategies demonstrated preliminary feasibility, fidelity and effectiveness, they were only examined among 12 HCWs in 6 facilities. Our trial findings will provide critical information on how enhancing HCW skills may impact patient clinic outcomes, a novel contribution to research. This cluster RCT will systematically and rigorously evaluate our blended learning implementation package for scale-up and sustainability, and results will be disseminated nationally, regionally and internationally. The national dissemination will include conducting local stakeholder meetings, presenting the results at the MOH technical working group and at the annual HIV conferences in Malawi.
This study will also impact implementation science within the context of ICT more generally as it assesses the impact of implementing a novel approach of administering HCW training as an implementation strategy, and provide critical insight into how to improve quality of service delivery, impact and scale-up costs. Scale-up of effective ICT interventions could lead to substantial public health benefits. The trial will also add to the literature on CQI as implementation strategy for potentially addressing facility-level implementation challenges and evaluate cost-effectiveness of the approach which was encouraged in a recent study. This has implications on implementing ICT both in Malawi and for other countries in SSA.
This study has some limitations. First, given that the implementation package consists of several elements (self-directed digital training, face-to-face practice, feedback and quality improvements), attributing any improvements of ICT delivery to each element will be difficult. Next, there is a potential for contamination to occur within the study because there may be a small amount of HCW overlap between the standard and blended learning implementation package facilities. Based on historical programmatic records, we expect the degree of cross-over to be minimal (<5% of HCWs) and thus unlikely to undermine results. There could also be a possibility of HCWs in SOC arm being exposed to colleagues who received the blended learning implementation package. This will be assessed by examining and describing improvements in the SOC arm, which may result from HCWs sharing new knowledge and skills across facilities. Lastly, given that the counselling sessions will be audiorecorded for authentic fidelity assessments, there is a possibility that some HCWs or clients may behave differently, given that they are being observed.
ICT, an evidence-based intervention to efficiently diagnose PLHIV in need of HIV treatment, has high potential for impact at scale. This cluster RCT will fill an important gap, evaluating a blended learning implementation package designed to optimise ICT. This represents an important contribution towards pursuing strategies to achieve the first 95% of the UNAIDS 95-95-95 treatment targets and eventual elimination of HIV by 2030 in LMIC.

## Trial registration
The trial was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov in April 2022. Twenty-six HCW participants were enrolled in November 2021, 5 months earlier and were only engaged in a single activity: IDIs for formative qualitative research. Enrolment of the remaining 280 health workers in our 306-person cohort began in July 2022, after our trial had been approved by ClinicalTrials.gov. Introduction of our enhanced implementation package was conducted in August 2022 (4 months after trial registration) and enrolment of individual index and contact client participants started in November 2022. The qualitative interviews conducted months earlier will not bias our cluster randomised trial.
