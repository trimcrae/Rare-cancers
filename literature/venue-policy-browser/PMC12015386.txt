## ABSTRACT

## Background
Behaviour supports plans (BSP), if accurately implemented, have been found to increase skills and decrease behaviours that challenge for individuals with intellectual disabilities. However, skills acquired by staff during trainings on BSPs often fail to transfer into effective implementation. The current study aimed to examine barriers and facilitators to BSP implementation.

## Method
Focus groups were conducted with four staff teams (n = 21) and semi‐structured interviews were carried out with each team's respective team leader (n = 4). Thematic analysis, involving inductive and deductive approaches, was used to analyse the data.

## Results
Environmental context and resources (e.g., compatibility of environment) and social influences (e.g., level of team communication) were the predominant domains impacting on implementation.

## Conclusion
Findings emphasise the importance of service providers aligning with the core values of positive behaviour support, which acts as a facilitator for effective BSP implementation. These include integral social processes such as collaboration across key stakeholders and person‐centred planning.

## Summary

The views of frontline staff and team leaders reflect that the environmental context and resources, and social influences are key domains impacting BSP implementation, both as barriers and facilitators.
The study provides a roadmap of strategies at an organisational and individual level, as well as process factors that can be adopted to enhance behaviour support plan implementation.
The overarching factors impacting implementation.
This research aligns with the advancement of services for people with intellectual disabilities, contributing to the field's understanding of barriers and facilitators to the implementation of BSPs.

## Theoretical Domains Framework
As Braun and Clarke's ( ) method is a flexible inductive analysis, (i.e., not tied to a pre‐existing theoretical framework) it also enabled deductive analysis of data (i.e., using a pre‐existing theoretical framework) into the relevant TDF domains.
Firstly, clear definitions were developed of each domain within the TDF which is outlined in Data  . Secondly, the first author deductively developed beliefs for both interview and focus group transcripts separately by placing open codes into relevant domains. For example, any open code referring to the compatibility of the setting for the individuals supported was placed within the Environmental context and Resources Domain. Frequently mentioned responses were only outlined as beliefs if they were impacting on the implementation of BSPs (see Figure  ). From this, the predominant domains (a domain was identified as a predominant domain if it had a high instance of data coded (beliefs) to this domain), and key barriers and facilitators were identified. The second author independently mapped all identified beliefs into TDF domains to facilitate discussion on the depth of beliefs (Francis et al.  ).
Agreement and differences across interview and focus group predominant TDF domains and determinants (i.e., facilitators and barriers) were narratively summarised. The first and second authors discussed predominant domains (the most data coded to these domains) and key barriers and facilitators across domains and their relation to identified themes. This enabled clarity on the key factors impacting on BSP implementation.

## Results
2.1

## Participants
Participants were from four staff teams, including 21 front‐line staff, and the respective team leaders for each of the staff teams (n = 4). A front‐line staff member fulfilled the duties of a social care worker/care assistant, which involved supporting individuals with intellectual disabilities and implementing elements of BSPs. Each front‐line staff member reported to their respective team leader, who may or may not have been directly implementing BSPs. Team leaders' primary responsibility was to oversee the work of all front‐line staff within a team, ensuring that duties and responsibilities to the individuals supported were fulfilled. For information on participant demographics, see Table  . Team A (n = 6) supported one individual in an apartment. Team B (n = 5) supported three individuals across one house and one apartment, Team C (n = 6) supported six individuals across one house and two apartments. Team D (n = 4) supported 6 individuals across one house and one apartment. Teams A and D were identified as ‘successful teams’ and Team B and C were identified as ‘struggling’ with BSP implementation in their setting.
 
2.2

## Reflexive Thematic Analysis Explanatory Themes
The three over‐arching themes were organisational level, process factors and individual level. The organisational level theme was broken down into the subthemes of clarity of BSP, sharing the details of BSP, informal training, environment, resources and client factors. Process factors were broken down into the subthemes of the creation process, staffs' relationship with the individual they support and team communication (including team dynamics, collaboration with MDT, and level of communication). Individual level included understanding, experience and buy‐in. Many of the subthemes exist on a continuum, acting as either barriers or facilitators depending on their level of presence or absence within the context. A further distinction was made on how these subthemes were identified as both barriers and facilitators across three time points: acquiring knowledge and skills, implementation on the ground and consistency of implementation (see Figure  ). Firstly, acquiring the knowledge and skills includes how staff are first introduced to the BSP. Secondly, implementation on the ground relates to when staff are first trying to implement the BSP. Finally, consistency of implementation encompasses both implementation across the staff team and level of implementation by individual staff. Consistency is distinct from implementation as two staff teams highlighted that they were ‘ticking a box’ to indicate they had tried to implement or wrote lifestyle notes. A thematic map across these three time points indicating whether identified subthemes were at an organisational level, individual level or were related to process factors was developed and represented in Figure  .
 
2.3

## Organisational Level
2.3.1

## Clarity of BSP
The clarity of a BSP was reported to impact on skill acquisition. Structured (e.g., a step‐by‐step day routine), simplified (e.g., working on one target behaviour at a time), and individualised BSPs were outlined as facilitators to implementation. In contrast, large documents with multiple components which can be interpreted differently by each staff member were seen as a barrier to implementation.

2.3.2

## Sharing the Details of the BSP
MDT providing a presentation and incorporating collaboration with the staff team on which elements to prioritise first was identified as a facilitator for acquiring knowledge and skills (Teams A and D). Team C highlighted the need for this input. However, solely reading a plan where there is a lack of clarity on what elements to prioritise without collaboration with MDT (Teams B and C) is a barrier to implementation.And when it comes down on the floor, for the, psychologists to come and give us a presentation. Just to clarify what is in the PBS. So everybody knows the part, that element of oh ____, that element of subjectivity is the key now. So everybody understands what it's about. Gemma—Team C

2.3.3

## Informal Training
For staff members who joined a team after a BSP is put in place (i.e., new staff), informal training, observation, and discussions with other staff members, as well as feedback from other staff members, were identified as facilitators to acquiring knowledge and skills. A barrier to implementation for new staff is not having access to informal training due to staffing levels and solely reading the plan (Team B).I think when I say vague, it's how you interpret it, I might look at it and say, Oh, this is what it means and someone else might look at it and go maybe we should, do you know, maybe we should try it another way. Kim—Team C

2.3.4

## Environment
Other factors impacting implementation on the ground were the compatibility or incompatibility of the environment. A compatible environment meeting the individuals needs was identified as a facilitator for implementation. Whereas staff teams supporting more than one individual (Teams B, C and D) discussed this heavily as a barrier.

2.3.5

## Client Factors
For three staff teams' the level of CB (high) was outlined as making BSP implementation a priority and therefore a facilitator for implementation on the ground. In contrast, client's motivation, a long history of CB and if individuals are in crisis or engaging in elevated levels of CB were reported as barriers to implementation. This was particularly evident for settings supporting more than one individual, with workload, and prioritising the individual in crisis. Team B discussed that it was not possible to implement the BSP when safeguarding for the individual, managing the individual's behaviour, and ensuring they are kept safe during an incident. Descriptions indicated that ‘managing behaviour’ was implementation of the reactive elements of the plan, however a direct link was not made to BSP implementation.They take turns. Like one person could be [good] for a good few days and then someone else takes over having a bad week, at this stage the BSP takes the back seat to just managing behavior, keeping at an even keel and just getting through the day, rather ticking boxes, trying to get through without any incidents. James—Team B

2.3.6

## Resources
Access to adequate resources (e.g., staffing levels for activities in the community) was also discussed comprehensively. Having the availability of adequate staffing (two to one staffing in this instance) was a facilitator to implementation on the ground and consistency of implementation. Staffing levels were only emphasised as a barrier if supporting more than three individuals, with daily responsibilities and finding time to implement elements such as skills building elements reported as the greatest challenge. This was also linked to new staff putting regular staff under pressure or not enough staff on duty to implement.And then also, you have the fact that people come in there, and we have new staff quite a lot. So we have quite a high, I suppose, rotation of newer staff, which can impact on certain people's behaviors, and can escalate behaviors, so that doesn't help and that, that makes it difficult, doesn't it? Jill—Team C

Consistent input from MDT, including consistent review meetings with feedback, on the ground support and having access to support (i.e., available on phone: Teams A and D) facilitated consistency of implementation. The need to have access to support to discuss qualms was emphasised as integral. One specific member within Team C mentioned ethical qualms with an intervention that had a long‐term focus, where short‐term requests were in certain instances denied. While other members of the team did not share this perspective, they indicated that they felt that the restrictive intervention was in the best interest of the individual.
Not having the availability of support from MDT, lack of feedback, and cancelled or inconsistent periods between meetings (Teams B and C) were identified as barriers. One of the ‘successful teams’: Team D, indicated that they also had difficulty with resources, supporting more than one individual, but had strong communication across the team, day service and with family members.This is where the more consistent reviews need to come in where you're not annoying someone, making them do something that they've made it clear they're not that interested in but that you still feel like that because you haven't been told let's just drop it. It's not working, we're still doing it until the next meeting, and we can say it's been tried. Lily—Team C

2.4

## Process Factors
2.4.1

## Creation Process
Staff Collaboration and on‐site observations to inform the plan were a facilitator to buy in and subsequent implementation. On the other hand, Teams B and C felt that components of plans were random and highlighted a need for more individualised plans. They also emphasised that the lack of MDT observations during the creation process was a barrier to buy‐in.so we can tweak it as a team like you know, it will have to be tweaked, because I think with the behavior, the psychologists, like they don't do it case specific, you know. our ladies are complex, if we were part of the whole process, you know, what the final end here's the draft, yeah. And then we all sit down as a team or maybe that wouldn't work. And then it's tweaked, so we're all in. Elizabeth—Team C

2.4.2

## Relationship With Individual
Teams A, C and D and two team leaders emphasised the importance of staff members relationship with the individual they support for implementation on the ground. A strong relationship with the person supported was identified as a key facilitator for implementation. In opposition to this, the time it takes to build rapport at the beginning is a barrier for implementation, during which time it is challenging to implement the plan.From my own experience, it took six months, when I came here first, to even engage that person…so to implement a BSP would be impossible for me at that time. Jill‐Team C

2.4.3

## Team Communication
An integral factor that impacts the consistency of implementation is team communication and whether staff check in with each other, which shifts may not always allow. Consistent communication across the team, checking in with each other, was identified as a facilitator.

Team dynamics in turn impacts on level of implementation, through consistency of implementation across the staff team. One team leader specifically labelled this ‘staff attitudes’. Lack of collaboration with MDT both during the BSP creation process and not receiving feedback and praise from MDT was indicated as impacting on buy‐in.
Resources were identified as a barrier to team communication for Teams B and C. Level of communication was heavily linked to implementation monitoring. Team C discussed how communication at team meetings can be subjective, as can individual notes. Similarly, staff may forget to write something down which is a barrier linked to lack of MDT input. In contrast, Team D, that primarily used lifestyle notes, never mentioned subjectivity, however this team had high levels MDT involvement. While Team B did not mention communication at all, they monitored implementation through a tick chart to show they ‘tried’. Team A reported, with MDT being involved in all team meetings, they didn't feel they had as much of an opportunity to check in with each other. Further highlighting the importance of communication within a staff team.And I think it can be, there's good days and bad days and I suppose like that, the 5 min that it takes to go okay, this happened that happened, are we all okay? I think that's really good to be able to get on with the shift and be able to go no I'm not happy with something or I'm a bit concerned or maybe we need to look into this. Molly‐Team A

2.5

## Individual Level
2.5.1

## Understanding
Three team leaders believed understanding or a lack of understanding had the potential to be a facilitator or a barrier for acquiring skills. A specific facilitator would be formal PBS training in terms of understanding and knowledge for all team members. Staff who had received this training indicated that it had not provided them with skills for implementation. Across staff teams, 57% (n = 12) of staff had received a 2‐day Introduction to PBS Training provided by the organisation; six (26%) staff members (one staff team) had received additional theory‐based bespoke training on a specific individual's BSP. Forty‐three percent (n = 9) of staff members had not received any PBS‐specific training.

2.5.2

## Experience
Two team leaders highlighted experience without knowledge of PBS or intellectual disability as a barrier to acquiring knowledge and skills, with one team leader describing this as ‘the wrong experience’, linking this to a generational gap in terms of education.If you can't help that staff to understand why we're doing this change (PBS) or then you're always going to have a barrier, because you're not going to have the consistency. Cara—Team Leader

the wrong type of experience around supporting people with behaviors that challenge. Joanne—Team Leader

However repeat, refresher training in PBS was outlined as a strategy by team leaders for how ‘the wrong experience’ could be overcome (a facilitator).Yeah, the training will definitely be a facilitator, just to refresh, refresh staff's memory on, you know, just to keep it alive. We keep so many other pieces of training alive. And I think, you know, this is definitely one that we should be. Cara—Team Leader

2.5.3

## Buy In
Whether staff teams were directly seeing the effects of the plans for the individual they were supporting also impacted buy‐in (reinforcement). Staff teams believed that buy‐in did not impact on their level of implementation, which was at odds with three team leaders that indicated both the level and whether elements were implemented at all were impacted.So if it's something that they know will work with, ____, if it's something they're not sure that ____ will pass any heed of or have no immediate response again, and they're less inclined to want to implement it. Joanne—TL

Three of the four team leaders highlighted that understanding or lack of understanding can negatively impact on individual staff members' buy‐in and level of implementation, which impacts on team dynamic.
The reflexive thematic analysis enabled a rich understanding of how the barriers and facilitators to implementing BSPs were experienced differently by the teams that were indicated as successful and unsuccessful in implementation. The TDF analysis enabled clarification on the predominant domains and specific beliefs which staff and team leaders believed to be impacting BSPs on implementation.

## Predominant Domains Impacting on BSP Implementation
The predominant domain identified as impacting (both as a barrier and facilitator) on implementation of BSPs by front‐line staff was Environmental context and resources (e.g., staffing levels and compatibility of living environment), followed by Social influences (e.g., communication across the staff team and support from members of the multi‐disciplinary team). The domains of Beliefs about consequences, Behavioural regulation, Reinforcement, Emotional factors, Goals, and Social professional role and identity were also believed to be impacting on implementation. According to staff, the domains of Knowledge, Skills, Beliefs about capabilities, and memory, attention, and decision processes were not impacting on BSP implementation.
Similar to front‐line staff, the predominant domains identified by Team Leaders were Social influences and Environmental context and resources. Furthermore, Team Leaders identified three additional domains: Knowledge, Skills, and Intentions, which they felt were impacting implementation, see Table  . All other domains were in line with focus group findings, with additional beliefs identified within the domains of Social influence, Reinforcement domain, and Beliefs about consequences.

## Discussion
The current research study provides an in‐depth insight into the perspectives of front‐line staff teams that are both struggling and successfully implementing BSPs in an organisation supporting individuals with intellectual disabilities. Respective team leaders' insights enabled richer understandings of factors facilitating or hindering implementation.
Key facilitators identified for ‘successful teams’ were compatible environments, clarity of BSPs, access to on‐going Multi‐Disciplinary Team support, low turnover of staff (organisational level) and consistent communication across the staff team (process factors). Teams ‘struggling’ with implementation did not have access to these facilitators and identified this lack of access as key barriers: incompatible environments, lack of clarity in BSPs, the lack of access to resources, high staff turnover, inconsistent Multi‐Disciplinary Team input (organisational level), and structures not supporting communication across the team (process factors). The continuum across these factors further supports them as key determinants (barriers and facilitators) to BSP implementation. The current findings align with broader residential setting literature, where common issues are resources and environmental factors (i.e., issues of time), lack of supervision and team meetings (Broadhurst and Mansell  ), the need for staff to have a voice, and using their ‘practice wisdom’ to collaborate on practices within their organisation (Quilliam et al.  ; Bigby and Beadle‐Brown  ).
The TDF analysis enabled clarification that staff teams believed Environmental context and resources, and Social influences were the two predominant domains impacting implementation, which were primarily external factors. This was at odds with their respective team leaders, who identified the additional domains of Knowledge, Skills, and Intentions as also impacting. Three team leaders believed that buy‐in and group dynamics can impact both the level of implementation and whether the intervention is implemented at all.
Three psychological conditions originally outlined by Kahn ( ) which impact on staff buy‐in are meaningfulness, safety, and availability. Meaningfulness relates to increased buy‐in when staff find purpose in their role and feel they are contributing. Safety refers to staff feeling that they can show up as themselves at work and express themselves without negative consequences to status and career trajectory within the organisation. Availability refers to staff feeling that they have the emotional, physical, and psychological resources necessary to fulfil their role (Asiwe et al.  ; Kahn  ; May et al.  ).
Meaningfulness and availability of necessary resources were not always indicated for three staff teams where buy‐in was not always achieved. For these teams it could directly be inferred that this may have affected their level and quality of implementation, where a ‘ticking a box’ approach to implementation was discussed. This turn of phrase was used in four different contexts; in terms of individuals supported not engaging with an intervention, frontline staff not believing something is going to work or not seeing it work, and ethical qualms. For one staff team, they were able to bring their ethical qualms to the attention of the MDT after a trial period. The findings mirror those of Fisher et al. ( ) who emphasised that collaboration throughout the creation process, of developing the BSP, was essential to facilitate implementation. Fisher and colleagues described a ‘set and forget’ approach to BSPs, to highlight cases where there was a lack of support and follow up, which they outlined as not aligned with the values of PBS.
Unfortunately, resources for the other two teams did not facilitate this collaborative support (key barrier) and as a result psychological meaningfulness and availability were impacted. These findings emphasise the importance of having a space where staff members can voice their concerns about ethical qualms or disagreements. In addition, staff should be empowered through training on procedural integrity, restrictive practices, assisted decision making, in conjunction with the human rights policy of care providers to air their concerns about BSPs. In the current study, staff indicated that the lack of MDT support was a direct result of lack of resources.
Important context for the current research is that the delivery of PBS was within a caseload model of support where MDT receive referrals and provide support on a priority needs basis. The caseload model faces limitations as support needs (prevalence of behaviours that challenge) are much higher than the available resources of support (practitioners) (McClean et al.  ). Determining how organisations can align their practices with the key values of PBS is integral. Within the current context the comparative of ‘successful’ teams and teams ‘struggling’ with implementation emphasises that what facilities implementation is alignment with the core values of PBS, collaboration throughout the PBS process with key stakeholders, person‐centered plans, and creating environments that offer choice and predictability, referred to as ‘capable environments’ (McGill et al.  ). One solution may be to ensure all clinicians within an MDT (i.e., Behaviour Specialist/Psychologist, Occupational Therapist, Clinical Psychologist, Speech and Language Therapist. Paediatrician, Nurse; NCPPD  ) are collaboratively working from a PBS framework to ensure the most effective delivery of services and alignment with the core values of PBS.
In the current research the key TDF domains (Environmental context and resources, Knowledge, Skills, Behavioural regulation, Reinforcement and Beliefs about capabilities) identified as impacting on implementation of BSPs mirror closely the subscales of the Performance Diagnostic Checklist‐ Human Services (PDC‐HS). The PDC‐HS is a tool developed and preliminarily validated as an indirect method of assessing performance analysis. Performance analysis is similar to a functional assessment but ‘focuses on the assessment of individual performance in organizations,’ (Wilder et al.  , 1170). However, one key factor which emerged within the current research which is not captured by the PDC‐HS is social processes (i.e., communication across team, staff team dynamic, relationship with clients). It is recommended that if using the PDC‐HS for a staff member working as part of a team that social processes should also be assessed.
3.1

## Implications for Practitioner Working Within a Caseload Model of Service Delivery
The current findings on the barriers and facilitators to BSP implementation link to elements outlined by the PBS Coalition Competency Framework (2015). Taken together, these findings could be used as a “road map” for practitioners within a similar context to enhance implementation at each stage (e.g., BSP creation process, acquiring knowledge and skills, implementation on the ground and consistency of implementation). The findings provide insights both at an organisation and individual level and offer considerations for relevant process factors.
At an organisational level, it is recommended that systems are in place to support PBS: (i) sufficient resources for MDT availability, (ii) release staff for training on: PBS, skills‐based PBS training, self‐reflection, and restrictive practices, (iii) incorporate a person‐focused training approach; (iv) incorporate train‐thethe‐trainer model or practice leadership, enabling on‐thethe‐ground support and key personnel championing PBS could facilitate embedding the model of PBS, (v) if implementation issues arise, conduct the Performance Diagnostic Checklist—Human Services (PDC‐HS) as a source of information related to environmental and resource factors.
When considering Process Factors it is recommended that organisations: (i) ensure MDT collaboration during: BSP creation process, initial introduction of BSP (i.e., what to focus on) and throughout implementation; (ii) assess the contextual fit of a BSP (iii) ensure regular MDT ‘check ins’, so the staff feel heard; (iv) ensure staff are accessing reinforcement: where possible develop achievable short‐term goals, providing direct feedback/praise, a train the trainer model would facilitate this; (v) promote staff‐client relationships through emphasising how staff can develop their own personal relationship with individuals during induction training. Inform staff about the time it may take to build trust and the elements of the plan that will be challenging to implement during that period. If necessary support staff by providing informal rapport training; (vi) plan for strong communication across staff teams through consistent team meetings, a ‘check in’ system throughout the day and at the end of shift; (vii) Collaborate with staff on feasible ways of monitoring implementation within the context of their current workload, (e.g., a checklist incorporated within individual notes); (viii) if staff dynamics are impacting implementation, speak directly to staff, complete direct observation, gain insights from the team leader and use the Team Climate Inventory (Anderson and West  ).
At an Individual Level it is recommended that: (i) staff are upskilled on their knowledge of PBS, intellectual disability, behaviours that challenge, specific behavioural intervention and skills of implementation and self‐reflection, (ii) if ‘buy‐in’ is impacting implementation, review individual process factors (i.e., access to reinforcement, level of collaboration throughout), (iii) incorporate motivational interviewing as an information gathering tool (Miller and Rollnick  ).

3.2

## Limitations and Future Research
While there were many strengths to the current study, there were also limitations. Member checking with participants would have further enriched findings and was not completed due to the timeline and the COVID‐19 global pandemic (i.e., national restrictions at the time prohibited visits to intellectual disability services). As the research was conducted within one regional area of a charity organisation, the findings are to be interpreted within this context. The findings of this research both expand and further support identified barriers and facilitators across different service contexts that were successfully implementing PBS, such as specialist units in the UK (Woolls et al.  ) and community settings in the United States (Bambara et al.  ). Future research could use the key barriers and facilitators identified to inform a survey to assess on a larger scale the needs of organisations in the intellectual disability sector across Ireland on PBS implementation, evaluating if practices are aligned with the core values of PBS and, if not, how this can be achieved.
To date, research has focused on staff teams that support individuals with complex needs and behaviours that challenge (e.g., Bambara et al.  ). Future research could examine the barriers and facilitators which impact on staff supporting individuals with less complex needs, possibly focusing on Tier 1 and 2 levels of the PBS framework. It is suggested that future research on implementation processes could use a measure such as the PDC‐HS to further supplement findings.
In conclusion, the current research builds on and enriches the knowledge that key barriers and facilitators to implementation of BSPs by frontline staff supporting individuals with intellectual disabilities in a charity organisation were primarily within the domains of Environmental contexts and resources and Social influences. It also enables clarity on how organisations can plan for the success of PBS at both individual and organisational levels and highlights the process factors which require consideration.
