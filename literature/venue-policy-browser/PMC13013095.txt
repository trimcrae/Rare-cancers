## ABSTRACT

## Purpose
Our purpose was to measure the research output and impact of the Canadian Network for Observational Drug Effect Studies (CNODES).

## Methods
We used OpenAlex to collect metadata on citation counts for all 115 CNODES articles published between January 2011 and March 2022. We obtained the altmetric scores from Altmetric.com. We examined mean citation counts and altmetric scores overall and by publication venue, study type, and author characteristics. We used general linear and mixed effects modeling to examine the association of citation counts/altmetric scores with the following characteristics: author's gender, trainee status and institution, study design, journal venue, article type, and jurisdiction(s) of data.

## Results
In total, 232 authors co‐authored at least one publication. While about half of authors were women, men accounted for two‐thirds of authorships. Trainees accounted for 28% of first authorships. The median citation count was 19.0 (interquartile range: 9.0–52.0), and the median altmetrics score was 4.0 (interquartile range: 1.0–22.0). Compared with original, observational research, methodologic research was associated with a lower altmetric score (adjusted difference: −57.4, 95% CI: −87.7; −27.1) but not citation counts (adjusted difference: 40.5, 95% CI: −26.2; 107.1). Publication in a “top 10” medical journal was associated with higher citation counts (adjusted difference: 89.8, 95% CI: 42.1, 137.5) and altmetric scores (adjusted difference: 168.3, 95% CI: 91.1, 245.5) scores.

## Conclusions
The patterns identified using bibliometrics and altmetrics demonstrated CNODES's author and institutional networks and their publications' mobilization and use by the academic community and broader publics.

Keywords: alternative metrics, authorship, bibliometrics, knowledge translation, pharmacoepidemiology, research impact

## Key Points

CNODES is a pharmacoepidemiology research network that studies drug utilization, safety, and effectiveness and has developed methods to describe researcher characteristics, quantify outputs, and assess impact for its development and communication with funders and stakeholders.
Bibliometric indicators (e.g., citation counts and scores, author networks) can examine pharmacoepidemiology scholarship in terms of research themes, collaborations, influential researchers, and impact in academia, while alternative metrics (e.g., mentions in media) describe the reach and level of engagement beyond academia.
OpenAlex, a tool for bibliometrics, provided a transparent and cost‐effective approach to document the research outputs and impact of CNODES.
About half of all authors were female, and many CNODES trainees published in top journals. Article type and publication in top medical journals were associated with citation counts and altmetric scores.
Bibliometric and altmetric indicators can assist collaborative research networks to examine their research output, author characteristics, and impact for both learning and accountability.

## Plain Language Summary
The purpose of this work was to demonstrate how bibliometrics and altmetrics can be used to evaluate the output and impact of research networks, using the Canadian Network for Observational Drug Effect Studies (CNODES) as a case study. We used the OpenAlex tool to collect data on 115 CNODES articles published between January 2011 and March 2022 and determined each publication's characteristics such as author's gender, trainee status, and institution, study design, and journal venue. We used altmetric scores from Altmetric.com, based on Twitter (now X), news, and policy document mentions. A total of 232 authors co‐authored at least one CNODES publication. While about half of CNODES authors were women, men accounted for two‐thirds of authorships. Trainees accounted for 5.6% of total authorships, but 28.1% of CNODES publications had a trainee as first author. Article characteristics such as type of article and whether it was published in a “top 10” medical journal were associated with citation counts and altmetric scores.

## Introduction
Research impact assessment, employing bibliometrics and alternative metrics (altmetrics), is used by university administrators for recruitment, tenure and promotion, resource allocation, and reporting; funders for funding decisions and assessment [ , , ]; researchers for career progression; and research teams for identifying research gaps, directions, and collaborators. In Canada, various types of research impact frameworks are used [ , , , , , , ]. Bibliometrics provides insight into research uptake by academics, industry, regulators, clinicians, and policy makers, using indicators such as citation counts [ ]. As many people use social media (79.4% of Canadians) [ ], altmetrics studies the reach of research outside of academia, in policy, traditional and social media, and beyond [ ]. However, there is limited information on the use of bibliometrics and altmetrics for research impact assessment in pharmacoepidemiology [ , , , ].
Since 2011, the Canadian Network for Observational Drug Effect Studies (CNODES) has built a pan‐Canadian network to conduct post‐marketing studies of real‐world drug use, safety, and effectiveness [ , , , ]. CNODES applies sophisticated methods to population‐based health databases to answer queries posed by Health Canada and other government organizations [ ]. CNODES uses both traditional pharmacoepidemiologic methods adapted to multi‐jurisdictional settings and rapid‐response studies using semi‐automated tools and a common data model for standardized analyses, following the standard of the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)'s Sentinel Initiative [ , ]. Since its inception, CNODES has responded to over 40 queries [ ], yielding 115 publications between 2011 and March 2022. CNODES goals include exploring drug safety signals; comparing the safety and effectiveness of drugs across and within drug classes [ ]; examining patterns of drug utilization in response to such issues as off‐label drug use [ ], drug formulary policy changes [ , ], drug formulation changes, and regulatory advisory announcements [ , , ]; and determining data quality [ , ]. Study results are communicated in peer‐reviewed journals, meetings, briefings and reports for query submitters, educational webinars [ ] and podcasts [ , , ]. CNODES collaborates with international networks, such as the FDA's Sentinel System and the European Medicines Agency's DARWIN EU network [ ] to further understand the characteristics of drug safety issues including queries from the International Coalition of Medicines Regulatory Authorities [ ]. Further information on CNODES is found in the .
Despite calls for integrating real‐world evidence into postmarketing processes for regulators, health technology assessment agencies, and the pharmaceutical industry [ ], and determining the use of and impact of this evidence, limited systematic and comprehensive research impact assessment studies have been conducted by pharmacoepidemiologic networks [ ]. Unlike other domains, including medicine (which ranked third after social sciences and computer science), few articles [ , , , ] have examined the use of bibliometrics and altmetrics in pharmacoepidemiology [ ].
The goal of this research was to illustrate how bibliometrics and altmetrics were used to measure the research output and impact of CNODES. Our specific objectives were to (1) describe the characteristics of the CNODES publications and authors, (2) describe stakeholder engagement with CNODES research using bibliometric and altimetric scores, while determining if these vary with publication and author characteristics, and (3) highlight the network's contribution to gender parity, training, and open access publication.

## Methods
2.1.

## Data Collection
2.1.1.

## 
CNODES Publications
This study analyzed all 115 articles attributable to CNODES, published from January 2011 to March 2022 and archived in McGill University's digital repository. Two authors (A.M.Y. and I.S.S.) reviewed the records with members of the CNODES Steering Committee to validate the publication set. We used OpenAlex [ ], an open index of research publications, and Altmetric.com to gather the complete metadata records on March 28, 2023. Using the Altmetric.com free Bookmarklet browser extension, we captured and verified a convenience sample of policy documents referencing CNODES publications. Altmetrics determines the information from open access policy documents, not the way in which the policy audience downloads, reads, or applies the document [ ].

2.1.2.

## Open Access Status
An article can have multiple open access (OA) statuses in OpenAlex because each available open access version is recorded and categorized as gold OA (published in an Open Access journal included in the Directory of Open Access Journals [ ]), hybrid OA (published in a closed journal but with an open license), green OA (found in a repository), or bronze OA (published and accessible for free on the journal website but without an open license) [ ]. Because all CNODES publications must be made open access [ ], we manually documented an OA version when not available in OpenAlex.

2.1.3.

## Relationship to a Drug Safety and Effectiveness Network Query
We determined whether each article was associated with a Drug Safety and Effectiveness Network (DSEN) Query, a research question addressing the safety and effectiveness of a marketed drug [ ].

2.1.4.

## Author Gender and Trainee Status
Gender was estimated using an online service [ ]. We did not have data on authors' self‐declared gender and adopted a binary concept of gender as described previously [ ]. We manually identified publications co‐authored by an early career researcher or “trainee” (undergraduate or graduate student, postdoctoral fellow, medical resident or fellow, or researcher new to pharmacoepidemiology).
We determined gender and trainee distribution by number (percentage) of distinct authors and of total authorships; distinct authors were recounted if they co‐authored more than one CNODES publication. We further analyzed these distributions by authorship order (first, middle, and last).

2.1.5.

## Article Category and Scale
Two authors (A.M.Y./I.S.S.) reviewed all 115 articles to assign the following categories (subsequently collapsed by K.B.F./P.R.): original research (observational), original research (methods), other (letter, editorial, commentary, knowledge translation), and review (expert/non‐systematic, systematic, meta‐analysis).
The geographic scale of each query‐related publication was captured by noting the number of provinces and countries contributing data (international/multiple Canadian provinces, single Canadian province, UK only, or US only).

2.1.6.

## Medical Journal Ranking
Journal Citation Reports (JCR), assessed from 2019–2020 [ ], and Scimago Journal Rankings on Medicine, assessed from 2018–2020 [ ], were used to identify those publications within the “top 10” of either of these ranking systems.

2.2.

## Data Analysis
2.2.1.

## Descriptive Analysis
We stratified publications by time, journals, and OA statuses. We analyzed the gender and trainee status composition of teams, considering the authors' byline position. We evaluated CNODES collaboration activities by examining the number of authors and institutions on each publication, and by visualizing the institutional and co‐authorship networks. The network nodes represent CNODES authors' various institutional affiliations as recorded in OpenAlex, manually cleaned to remove duplicates. The node color represents the institution type. The size of the nodes is determined by the number of edges the node has in the network. The edges are based on collaboration, and the weight (thickness) of the edge is determined by the number of co‐authored publications between the institutions. For the author network, edges are formed between nodes when two individuals are authors on the same publication. For the layout of the author network, we used ForceAtlas2, an algorithm that makes strongly interconnected nodes gravitate toward one another [ ]; thus, authors who collaborated on publications more frequently appear closer to one another. We generated a word cloud that shows the top 100 most frequent words in the titles after lemmatization and removal of stop words.

2.2.2.

## Statistical Analysis
We described the publications' citation and altmetric scores, Twitter (now X) mentions, online news mentions, and Wikipedia citations, as median (interquartile range) overall and by publication, study, and author characteristics. We estimated crude and adjusted mean differences and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) using generalized linear models (GLM) for unadjusted estimates and linear mixed models (LMM) for adjusted ones. Similarly, we described policy citations as count (percent) and estimated mean differences. We investigated publication, study, and author characteristics by query status, article type, first and last author gender, trainee inclusion as author, data source jurisdiction(s), publication open access status, and publication in a “top 10” medical journal.

## Results
3.1.

## Research Output
CNODES published 115 articles from January 2011 through March 2022 (Appendix ), relatively evenly distributed over the study period, except for outlier years (2016 and 2020) with more than 20 publications each (Figure  ). The two most frequent venues for CNODES work were Pharmacoepidemiology and Drug Safety and the BMJ (Figure  ). All articles were open access, about half directly through the journal website and the other half through online repositories (Figure  ).
 
3.2.

## Collaboration
CNODES collaboration was reflected in the number of author bylines, with most articles having between three and six authors, and 23 publications produced by teams of 10 or more co‐authors (Figure  ). Similarly, all but 31 publications involved multiple institutions (Figure  ). The top 20 institutions and the number of publications are presented in Figure  . The top educational institution was McGill University, and one of its research institutes, the Lady Davis Institute of the Jewish General Hospital. CNODES collaboration is presented as both institutional (Figure  ) and co‐authorship (Figure  ) networks, in which the most prolific and central authors/institutions are highlighted. The topics of study are presented as a word cloud (Figure  ).
 
3.3.

## Citation Count and Altmetric Score
We documented the articles with the top citation (Table  ) and altmetric scores (Table  ) and selected policy mentions (Table  ). The top 10 most cited articles were three methods and seven original research and/or meta‐analysis papers, with five of them in the BMJ. This differed from the altmetric score, where the top 10 were all original research publications, with seven in the BMJ, demonstrating the value of multiple metrics. The overall mean citation count was 56.9 (standard deviation: 105.8), and the overall median was 19.0 (interquartile range: 9.0–52.0) (Table  ). The overall mean altmetric score was 40.9 (standard deviation: 96.3), and the overall median was 4.0 (interquartile range: 1.0–22.0).
 
3.4.

## Association Between Publication and Author Characteristics and Citation and Altmetric Score
There were some differences in both citations and altmetric scores by type of article (Table  ). Methods articles had similar citations as original research articles (crude mean difference 2.0, 95% CI −63.8, 67.7), although they had a wide range of scores (1 to 166), and some key methods papers were highly cited. However, methods papers had lower altmetric indicators than original, observational research (mean difference −65.5; 95% CI −98.6, −32.4). There were only 12 review articles, but with strong citation counts. After adjusting for other article and author characteristics, trends remained similar. Compared with original, observational research, original methods research (adjusted difference: −57.4, 95% CI: −87.7, −27.1) and other publication types (adjusted mean difference −57.5, 95% CI −88.4, −26.5) were associated with lower altmetric scores. For articles appearing in the top 10 medical journals, citation counts and altmetric scores were both higher than for articles published in other journals (mean crude differences 89.9; 95% CI 43.1, 136.8; and 170.6; 95% CI 93.4, 247.8, respectively). These differences held after adjusting for other article and author characteristics (citation count: 89.8, 95% CI: 42.1, 137.5; altmetrics: 168.3, 95% CI: 91.1, 245.5) (Table  ). Having data from multiple jurisdictions was associated with higher scores. For example, the unadjusted mean difference between database studies that used international data (two or more countries) and data from a single Canadian province was 60.2 (95% CI 23.7, 96.7) for citation counts and 85.0 (95% CI 45.5, 124.6) for altmetric scores (Table  ). These differences were attenuated after adjusting for year of publication and article characteristics (citation counts: 28.1, 95% CI −7.7, 63.9; altimetric score: 3.9, 95% CI −26.7, 34.6) (Table  ).
The research teams reflected CNODES' emphasis on training, mentoring and striving for gender equity. For first authorships, men tended to have higher citation counts than women (mean difference 37.9; 95% CI −1.9, 77.7), although these differences were attenuated after adjusting for publication characteristics (28.4; 95% CI −4.9, 61.7). For last author, citation counts were similar across genders (mean difference 13.4; 95% CI −38.3, 65.2). Females accounted for 48.3% of distinct authors and 38.3% of total authorships (Figure  ). Trainees accounted for 5.6% of total authorships, but 28.1% of CNODES publications had a trainee as first author (Figure  ) with citation counts highly skewed, ranging from 1 to 341 for articles with trainee first author.

4.

## Discussion
Our study demonstrates the use of bibliometrics and altmetrics for assessing the research impact of CNODES. We documented the impact of a specific collection of pharmacoepidemiology articles, as has been done for other disciplines, to track progress and compare with other research networks and fields [ , , ]. These assessments complement peer review [ , ]. While peer review uses qualitative expert opinion, bibliometrics examine citations by academics and altmetrics assess uptake in social media. They capture different dimensions of research quality, audience, and impact. Timelines differ, with social media references to articles appearing much sooner than new citations. By drawing on all three approaches, information on the quality, mobilization, attention to, readership, and use of articles by both academics and the broader public can be obtained [ , ]. The quantification of article characteristics allowed CNODES to identify factors contributing to successes and opportunities for growth.
While bibliometrics has been conducted for journals, departments, and institutions [ , ], we assessed a multi‐institutional research network. Moreover, our study used an open bibliometric database (OpenAlex) allowing for increased transparency and reproducibility of our results, while most previous studies have employed proprietary data and tools.
By using multiple metrics, CNODES documented its contribution to the understanding of drug utilization, appropriateness, risks, and benefits, as well as innovating methods in pharmacoepidemiology. CNODES engaged pan‐Canadian and international researchers, with 232 researchers co‐authoring these studies. The many publications with trainees (28.1% with trainee as first author) demonstrated its commitment to capacity building. CNODES' reach beyond academia was demonstrated with several publications that were highly cited, tweeted, mentioned in the news, or referenced in policy documents.
Our study contributes to the pharmacoepidemiology literature by identifying factors associated with high citation counts and altmetric scores, which are important for both organizations and individual researchers. Studies with international databases had higher citation scores (crude difference: 60.2, 95% CI: 23.7, 96.7) than those with a single Canadian database, suggesting that international collaborative investigations are more impactful. Studies published in top medical journals also had higher citation counts (crude difference: 89.9, 95% CI: 43.1, 136.8), suggesting that publishing in a well‐known journal trusted by clinicians and harnessing the journal's outreach activities helped increase the publication's reach. CNODES benefited from influential researchers who mentored trainees, strengthened methods, and accessed their international networks (e.g., Suissa, Platt, Filion, LeLorier, Sketris, and Hemmelgarn, who are Fellows of the International Society of Pharmacoepidemiology and/or the Canadian Academy of Health Sciences). Other studies have noted increased citation counts and altmetric scores with “author power” and star researchers [ , ].
Citation counts for methods papers were highly skewed, with high citations for several papers reporting new methods or explaining methods well. A 2013 methods paper had the highest number of citations (863) but also 9 years to accumulate these citations. In this paper, Austin used Monte Carlo simulations to examine the performance of different propensity score methods to estimate marginal hazard ratios [ ]. In another highly cited methods paper (166 citations), Suissa et al. described the prevalent new‐user cohort design for comparative drug effect studies [ ]. However, methods articles in general had low altmetric scores, an issue to consider when evaluating individual researchers.
The top review article was a systematic review and meta‐analysis of the cardiotoxicity of drugs used to treat breast cancer (165 citations) [ ], conducted to inform a subsequent observational study. One of the top two original research articles (94 citations) determined whether the use of incretin‐based drugs, compared with the use of two or more other oral antidiabetic drugs, was associated with an increased risk of acute pancreatitis, using health records from seven Canadian sites, the United States, and the United Kingdom [ ].
In addition to CNODES, other pharmacoepidemiology networks (e.g., Sentinel in the US; Data Analysis and Real World Interrogation Network in Europe (DARWIN), Vaccine monitoring Collaboration for Europe (VAC4EU), and European Network of Centres for Pharmacoepidemiology and Pharmacovigilance (ENCePP) in the EU; Asian Pharmacoepidemiology Network (AsPen) in Asia; and Observational Health Data Sciences and Informatics (OHDSI) internationally) generate research evidence to inform regulatory, reimbursement, clinical, and other decision makers [ , ]. However, these networks have not often systematically and comprehensively measured their research output and impact [ ].
CNODES has previously examined the bibliometrics and altmetrics of its publications in smaller sample sizes, and Australian researchers examined parity in female authorship using bibliometrics. Recently, Zheng et al. conducted a systematic analysis and bibliometric review of common data model usage in pharmacoepidemiology [ ]. They included 308 articles published between 1997 and 2024 by 1580 authors from 32 countries in 140 different journals. Among the top 10 institutions, eight were based in the US and two in South Korea. The most cited articles were multicentre. The top three journals reported by Zheng et al. were Vaccine, Pediatrics, and Pharmacoepidemiology and Drug Safety, whereas for CNODES they were Pharmacoepidemiology and Drug Safety, the BMJ, and then Statistics in Medicine, Epidemiology, and Diabetes Care tied for third.
Some pharmacoepidemiology units have described publication outputs and others their contributions to regulatory policy [ , , , ]. For example, Carroll et al. analyzed the characteristics of post‐authorisation studies on the ENCePP EU PAS register [ ]. They noted type 2 diabetes drugs were commonly studied using databases (80% of studies), whereas databases were only used for 11% of oncology drugs. Twenty‐three CNODES publications studied drugs for diabetes and two for oncology.
Maillard et al. [ ] conducted a systematic review of research articles using the French National Health Data System from January 2012 until August 2018. Study objectives were drug utilization (65%), safety (22%), and effectiveness (9%). Methodologic issues were the primary objective for 7% of publications. CNODES used Canadian and international databases, added associated review articles and editorials, examined both bibliometrics and altmetrics, and used Open Alex as well as proprietary tools. Among CNODES publications, 26 of 115 (22.6%) focused on methodology.
Sultana et al. identified 1426 post‐authorization studies of which 86% were observational studies [ ]. For claims database studies (N = 171), 86.5% included adults and 24.6% children. In CNODES, only one study included children [ ]. Of Sultana et al.'s studies, 37% of studies using secondary data alone were multidatabase studies compared to 50% of CNODES's. Of secondary data studies, 77.5% had pharmaceutical company funding whereas CNODES has federal government funding. Drugs of interest were non‐biologic (67.8%) and biologic (19.3%) compared to CNODES having only two studies which included biologicals [ , ]. Of observational trials, 58% had a protocol deposited whereas CNODES had the protocols of 52.9% of query‐related publications registered (18/34). The most common reason for not registering CNODES protocol was that the study was a drug utilization study (6/34), review article (4/34), or methods study (2/34). The rate of protocol registration is not just an issue in pharmacoepidemiology. A scoping review of the registration rate of protocols for observational studies in the top 5 general medicine journals noted that only 20 of 200 studies were publicly available (14 of these prospectively) [ ].
Almas et al. assessed the regulatory outcomes of post‐authorization safety studies (PASS) with a selection of studies required by a European regulator and conducted in one or more European countries including the UK (2012–2020) [ ]. Of the 84 PASS included, 46% had information on regulatory outcomes identified such as Pharmacovigilance Risk Assessment Committee meeting minutes. CNODES has not yet systematically assessed regulatory outcomes.
Future studies could determine training program effectiveness and follow career trajectories. CNODES could also work with other research units, using similar methodology, to provide a more global perspective on the impact of pharmacoepidemiology studies.

5.

## Limitations
Our study has several potential limitations. First, we only examined English‐language peer‐reviewed journal publications and excluded abstracts, grey reports, policy documents, etc. Second, non‐query related publications were self‐reported, and some may have been missed. Because Health Canada does not always add citations to its safety warnings, we could not determine its use of our studies as others have done in examining FDA or EMA communications [ , , ]. In addition, citations were not qualitatively assessed [ ]. Third, trainees' contribution was limited to authorship position, without taking into account factors such as degree, training stage, relationship to thesis, professional licensure, or career trajectory [ ]. Fourth, we lacked author characteristics such as age, academic degrees, or type and length of employment, and a clear denominator for the pool of researchers from which CNODES draws. Comparisons of author and publication characteristics may thus be affected by residual confounding. Fifth, we did not analyze the COVID‐19 pandemic impact (2020–2023).

6.

## Conclusions
CNODES used bibliometrics and altmetrics, emerging methodologies, to examine the characteristics and impact of its pharmacoepidemiology publications using mainly open sources. This approach can guide pharmacoepidemiology researchers wishing to assess the reach and engagement with their research within and beyond academia.
