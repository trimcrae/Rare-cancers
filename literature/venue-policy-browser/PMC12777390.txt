## Abstract

## Objectives
This study developed system dynamic modeling (SDM) based on the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) framework to simulate and identify key leverage points for enhancing social participation among older adults with noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), aiming to offer a robust theoretical foundation for designing effective interventions.

## Methods
Utilizing a system dynamics modeling, we simulated intervention strategies to augment social participation among older NCDs patients. Initial insights from in-depth interviews with 31 older individuals informed the construction of a causal loop diagram, elucidating the multifactorial relationships and feedback mechanisms among pertinent variables. Data from 500 patients were analyzed using correlation analysis, an independent weighting approach, and multiple regression to identify key variables, determine their equation coefficients, and construct a stock flow diagram for the model. The model then simulated social participation trends over 16 months under varying intervention intensities.

## Results
The results showed significant improvements in mobility function, functional ability, pain management, and social support, resulting in increases of 54.9%, 32.4%, 28.7%, and 25.0%, respectively, at a 10% intervention level. When intervention intensity was escalated to 20%, the impact became even more pronounced, with mobility function leading the way at a 127.5% increase, followed by pain management (63.6%) and functional ability (62.8%), and social support (48.7%). Self-care and emotional functions showed minimal improvement in social participation, with increases of less than 3% and 5%, respectively.

## Conclusions
Improving social participation in older NCDs adults requires understanding complex system dynamics. This study highlights mobility function, functional ability, pain management, and social support as key leverage points, stressing the need for targeted interventions based on individual assessments.

## Introduction
Noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), stemming from a complex interplay of genetic, physiological, environmental, and behavioral factors, pose a significant burden on global health, accounting for an overwhelming 74% of global mortality, as reported by the World Health Organization in 2019 [ ]. With the aging of the worldwide population and the increase in life expectancy, the prevalence of NCDs among older adults has surged, particularly in China, where a substantial portion of the older population grapples with one or more NCDs [ , ]. This demographic, often constrained by functional limitations and psychological distress, tends to withdraw from social engagements, resulting in diminished social participation—a cornerstone of the “Active Aging” strategy. Social participation, a vital aspect of aging gracefully, not only fosters self-worth and mental well-being but also promotes social cohesion, alleviates healthcare burdens, and contributes to an age-friendly society [ ]. Conversely, social isolation exacerbates functional decline and elevates mortality risks among older adults with NCDs [ ].
Extensive research in geriatrics, disability studies, and rehabilitation underscores the multifaceted nature of social participation, encompassing life fulfillment, emotional gratification, community engagement, and societal contributions [ – ]. Several factors have been identified as crucial determinants of social participation among older adults with NCDs. Mental health conditions, such as depression and anxiety, along with self-efficacy, significantly influence patients’ confidence and willingness to engage socially [ , ]. Physical impairments, characterized by slow gait speed, muscle weakness, fatigue, and reduced activity levels, pose formidable barriers to social participation [ ]. These physical limitations hinder daily activities and travel, further isolating older adults [ ].
Moreover, a robust social support network, both traditional and digital, plays a critical role in fostering social participation. It provides essential knowledge and skills, emotional solace, and confidence to engage in and willingness in social activities. Economic stability (such as retirement pensions, social security benefits, and low-income subsidies) and a conducive living environment also facilitate social participation by reducing financial constraints and enhancing accessibility to social opportunities. Global social participation policies, driven by UN and WHO initiatives like the Universal Health Coverage Declaration [ ], have significantly bolstered engagement, including among older adults with NCDs, fostering their active involvement [ ].
Given the complex interplay of individual, societal, environmental, and policy factors that dynamically influence social participation, a holistic and nuanced approach is paramount. This study embraced the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) framework, a globally esteemed standard for health and disability assessment established by the World Health Organization (WHO) [ ]. By integrating physical, individual, environmental, and social dimensions, the ICF offers a comprehensive lens to evaluate patients’ functional status and pinpoint the critical factors that either facilitate or hinder social participation [ , ]. To analyze deeper into the dynamic nature of social participation and the complex feedback mechanisms among its determinants, this research leveraged system dynamics modeling (SDM), a powerful tool for simulating complex systems over time. SDM not only simulates the complex interactions among various determinants but also identifies leverage points—those factors that, when targeted, can elicit significant and sustainable change in the system [ , ]. By simulating various intervention scenarios, SDM offers predictive insights into long-term outcomes, thereby strengthening evidence-based policymaking through a deeper understanding of how interventions can shape social participation trajectories over time [ ].
This study aims to address the challenges faced by decision-makers in devising efficient and sustainable interventions to enhance social participation among older adults with NCDs. By introducing a system dynamics model, we provide a dynamic tool to navigate the complexities and uncertainties inherent in intervention design. This approach enables policymakers to comprehend the short- and long-term implications of various interventions, guiding the optimal allocation of resources and refinement of policy implementation. Ultimately, our goal is to facilitate the integration of medical and community resources, optimize policy interventions, and contribute to the global vision of healthy aging.

## Methods

## Study design
Forrester pioneered System Dynamics Modeling (SDM), a robust methodology for comprehending, visualizing, and analyzing multifactorial dynamic feedback systems [ ]. This methodology amalgamates qualitative analysis with quantitative simulation, facilitating the representation of interactions among variables within a system via a causal loop diagram (CLD). Furthermore, it facilitates dynamic simulation and prediction by employing a stock flow diagram (SFD) and difference equations within a quantitative modeling framework [ ]. This study presents Sterman’s system dynamics modeling process [ ]. The SDM process, as illustrated in Fig. , encompasses six critical steps: problem definition, CLD development, stock flow diagram creation, data entry and formulation, model verification, and scenario simulation and analysis [ , ]. SDM transcends qualitative constraints by quantifying subjective aspects while simulating temporal dynamics through computational models [ ]. Its applicability in the realm of population health, particularly in the context of chronic diseases like diabetes and cardiovascular disorders, has been well established [ , ].
 The construction of the causal loop diagram was based on qualitative interviews. Semi-structured in-depth interviews, guided by the ICF framework, were used to gather the authentic experiences and perceptions of patients during their social participation, allowing for the identification of key variables and their causal relationships. The initial draft of the causal loop diagram was created and revised according to the principles of system dynamics, clarifying both positive and negative feedback loops. The stock flow diagram was constructed using cross-sectional data analysis, based on questionnaire surveys collecting multidimensional data. Statistical analysis was performed to identify key variables and determine the quantitative relationships between them. Combining the causal loop diagram with Vensim PLE software, an inventory process diagram was drawn to display the dynamic relationship between stocks and flows within the system, system dynamics equations were established, and the simulation of the System Dynamics Model (SDM) was completed.

## Participants
Inclusion criteria encompassed NCDs diagnosis (diabetes, COPD, asthma, coronary artery disease, stroke, cancer), age ≥ 60 years, stable condition, and ability to independently complete questionnaires with informed consent. Exclusion criteria were participation in other studies and severe sensory impairments (visual acuity < 0.3 or hearing loss > 40 dB).

## Qualitative interview
The participants of the qualitative interview were recruited between August and October 2021. Within 48 h of obtaining consent from the ward head, the researcher entered the ward with a negative COVID-19 nucleic acid test result and began data collection. The interview protocol was grounded in the ICF framework, incorporating open-ended queries tailored to uncover diverse influences on social engagement (see supplementary table ). Data were collected through one-on-one, face-to-face semi-structured interviews. The study received ethics approval from the research ethics committee of University (NO. QYFY WZLL 25890). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. For participants with limited understanding, the informed consent process involved their legal guardians. Data collection continued until data saturation was achieved. Saturation was determined when no new themes or information emerged from subsequent interviews; specifically, after the 31 st interview, no further insights were obtained.
The research team comprised senior nursing clinical experts and experienced clinical coordinators from tertiary hospitals, all of whom held at least a bachelor’s degree. Before data collection, all members received systematic training prior to data collection to ensure interview consistency and sensitivity to the needs of older adults. Given the advanced age of the patients and the relatively low education levels of some participants, researchers underwent systematic training before the interviews. They ensured that patients that patients had sufficient time to comprehend the questions. When delivering crucial information, the researchers slowed their speaking pace and incorporated deliberate pauses at key points. Due to the patients’ limited energy, each interview was generally limited to approximately 30 min, and interviews were scheduled at times when patients were physically and mentally most comfortable.

## Cross-sectional study
This study employed a cross-sectional design with convenience sampling. Participants in the cross-sectional data were recruited between October and December 2021 from community health centers and various wards (cardiology, endocrinology, oncology, respiratory, rehabilitation) of a tertiary hospital. Data acquisition encompasses hospital and community settings, with a preference for hospitals due to their centralized infrastructure, facilitating the compilation of extensive sample sizes and precise, retrospective analyses of patients’ social participation behaviors spanning the preceding three to six months. This approach aimed to capture the diversity of medical and social contexts influencing social participation. This research received ethics approval from the research ethics committee of Qingdao University, and participants provided informed consent after being informed of the study’s objectives, risks, and benefits.
Sample size was calculated using the formula for cross-sectional studies , based on an NCDs prevalence of 74.2% in Chinese older adults [ ] and an anticipated attrition rate of 10–20%. This yielded a target sample size of 327–368. We collected 515 questionnaires and, after excluding 10 questionnaires due to inconsistent responses (e.g., obvious patterned answers) and 5 due to substantial missing data (>20%), 500 complete questionnaires were included in the final analysis, yielding an effective response rate of 97.1%.

## SDM development
SDM has been instrumental in designing effective interventions for chronic diseases [ ]. This study follows Sterman’s SDM process, blending qualitative modeling (causal loop diagram) with quantitative modeling (stock and flow diagram) to address system complexity and interdependence [ ].

## Causal loop diagram (CLD)
A CLD is a sophisticated tool for dissecting system dynamics and elucidating interactions among variables through causal chains with positive or negative signs. The polarity of these chains defines feedback loops that either amplify (positive) or stabilize (negative) system behavior. Leveraging semi-structured, in-depth interviews, we collaborated with participants to define system boundaries and analyze factors that modulate social participation.
The interview data were transcribed verbatim and analyzed using NVivo 12 software (QSR International, Melbourne, Australia) following a content analysis approach. To ensure reliability and consistency of the findings, two researchers independently coded the data. Any discrepancies in coding were resolved through discussion, and if necessary, adjudicated by a third researcher. To safeguard respondent privacy, all data were anonymized.
The interview data were transcribed and analyzed using NVivo 12 software (QSR International, Melbourne, Australia) through content analysis. Two independent coders ensured the reliability and consistency of findings through rigorous data coding. To safeguard respondent privacy, all data were anonymized. Researchers, well-versed in the ICF categories, strictly adhered to the content analysis rules, linking the extracted statements to the appropriate categories [ ]. If any ambiguity arose, a third researcher reviewed and made a joint decision.
Ultimately, a pool of social participation ICF categories for elderly patients with NCDs was developed. The rules for extraction and linkage are as follows: In general, linking to the NCDs secondary category suffices; however, if the interview provides more detailed information, linkage to lower categories is necessary. For instance, if a participant reports suffering from lumbar disc herniation and frequent severe back pain, with the key statement being ' back pain ‘, it should be linked to the ICF category b280 sensation of pain. Additionally, if clear and meaningful statements are made but do not fit precisely into an ICF category, such as those related to quality of life, they are marked as undefinable. Specific linking content has been added to supplementary table . Guided by the ICF framework, we linked themes to categories and used Vensim PLE software (Ventana Systems Inc., Harvard, MA., USA) to create a structured diagram illustrating the interactions and polarities of factors, thereby refining the CLD’s logic and coherence.

## Data entry and formulation
For the definition of variables, we strictly follow the operating manuals of relevant scales and the definition standards of previous studies. The questionnaire used in this study is a pre-existing, validated instrument developed and published. We sourced data from validated questionnaires rooted in the ICF framework, encompassing demographics, functional assessment (ICF-RS), environmental factors (CHIEF), and social participation (IPA). This study conducted a comprehensive questionnaire survey on elderly patients with NCDs to systematically quantify multidimensional data, including demographic information, physical function assessment, environmental factors, and social participation. We have adopted a rigorous statistical analysis workflow, including correlation analysis, independent weight analysis, and multiple regression analysis. This method not only accurately identifies key variables that affect social participation, but also quantifies their relationships. These findings lay the foundation for incorporating relevant variables into the SDM and provide a solid basis for modeling complex interactions within the framework of social participation. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire comprising four sections: demographic characteristics, the ICF-RS, the CHIEF scale, and the IPA questionnaire.

## The international classification of functioning, disability, and health-rehabilitation set (ICF-RS)
The ICF-RS [ ] instrument selectively assesses 30 key function categories from the broader 1,400 ICF framework, offering a comprehensive evaluation tailored for patients with NCDs, older adults, and individuals with disabilities. Employing a 0–4 scoring system (ranging from no dysfunction to total dysfunction, with ‘not specified’ for insufficient information and ‘not applicable’ for irrelevant entries), higher aggregate scores indicate impaired physical function and reduced participation capability.

## Craig hospital inventory of environmental factors (CHIEF)
Introduced by Whiteneck in 2004 [ ], the CHIEF instrument quantifies a broad spectrum of environmental barriers (physical, attitudinal, service-related, productivity, and policy factors) encountered within home and community settings. Given the study’s focus on retired older adults with NCDs, work-related items were excluded. The CHIEF scores range from 0 to 80, with increasing values reflecting heightened perceived environmental obstacles.

## Impact on participation and autonomy questionnaire (IPA)
The IPA questionnaire is a specialized instrument crafted to assess the extent of social participation among research subjects. This instrument, originally developed by Cardol et al. [ ], has been adapted and refined for a Chinese context by Li et al. [ ]. The instrument examines participation restrictions and autonomy across four dimensions: autonomy indoors, social relations, family role, and autonomy outdoors. Each of the 25 items is rated on a 0–4 scale, with 0 indicating no participation dysfunction and 1–4 reflecting varying levels of impairment.

## Statistical analysis
Data entry was facilitated utilizing Microsoft Office Excel 2020, whereas the statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and the SPSSAU 21.0 online platform (QingSi Technology Ltd., Beijing, China). To evaluate the relationship between the total scores of ICF-RS, CHIEF, and IPA and social participation. Multiple linear regression analysis was used to compute the regression coefficients of various scales on social participation. These coefficients were subsequently integrated into system dynamics equations, enabling the quantification of the impact of various variables on state variables, such as social participation, thereby formulating dynamic change equations. In addition, due to the multidimensional nature of the ICF-RS and CHIEF scales, we employed an independent weighting method to allocate weights to each dimension, thereby determining their relative weight coefficients. This enabled each dimension within the model to be adjusted according to its actual impact on social participation.

## Model verification
The social participation system dynamics model for older adults with NCDs underwent rigorous validation. Initial checks in Vensim verified the model’s structural integrity and equation accuracy. Stability assessments involved adjusting the simulation step size to ensure consistent simulation curves across varying increments, thereby confirming the model’s high degree of stability.

## Scenarios simulation and analysis
Utilizing Vensim PLE software, simulations were conducted with a meticulous step size of 0.25 to assess the dynamics of social participation among older NCDs patients. Given that Longitudinal studies and intervention trials for NCDs typically span six months to a year or longer to observe changes in health outcomes, a 16-month simulation period was chosen. This timeframe allowed for a comprehensive assessment of the long-term effects on patients’ health status and social participation, facilitating a detailed understanding of the trajectories of social participation under different intervention strategies.
In line with system dynamics and policy simulation methodologies, we strategically varied intervention levels by 10% and 20%, reflecting a practical range for small-to-medium scale adjustments in real-world settings. This approach aimed to clarify the potential impact of different intervention intensities on social participation among older NCD patients, thereby informing the design of feasible and effective interventions.

## Results

## Causal mechanism analysis
Through semi-structured in-depth interviews with 31 older patients with NCDs (see Supplementary Table for demographics), we constructed a systematic CLD. The analysis of interview transcripts identified critical themes (e.g., leg pain limiting outdoor activities, fear of falling) which were systematically linked to ICF categories (e.g., b280 Sensation of pain, b455 Mobility function) following established linking rules [ ]. This process directly informed the identification of key variables in the CLD (e.g., pain severity, mobility capacity). The detailed linking of themes to ICF categories is provided in Supplementary Table .
As depicted in Fig. , the CLD illustrates the complex web of variables and their causal relationships, revealing nine reinforcing feedback loops (R1 to R9) and three balancing feedback loops (B1 to B3). For example, reinforcing loop R3 emerged from the interplay between ICF d450: walking (activity) and ICF e570: social security services, systems, and policies (environmental enablers). Balancing loop B1 was grounded in themes mapped to ICF b280: sensation of pain and b455: mobility function. This framework offers a holistic understanding of factors influencing social participation enhancement among older adults with NCDs.
 

## Stock flow analysis of dynamic systems
The transition from the qualitative CLD to the quantitative Stock and Flow Diagram (SFD) necessitated both a structural mapping and a rigorous empirical grounding of the hypothesized causal relationships. Whereas the CLD, derived from patient interviews, identified relevant variables and feedback loops, a targeted literature review was conducted to validate the generalizability of these relationships across broader populations and to inform the strength of these relationships for parameter estimation in the model equations [ ].
The development of the Stock Flow Diagram was fundamentally grounded in the qualitative structure of the Causal Loop Diagram (CLD, Fig. ), which was itself derived from patient interviews and structured using the ICF framework. To affect this transition from a qualitative CLD to a quantitative and simulatable model, we employed a rigorous, multi-tiered process for variable selection and empirical justification. First, the core auxiliary variables in the SFD, representing the key leverage points within the system, were subjected to a targeted literature review to establish their empirical validity and generalizability. To this end, team members independently searched major electronic databases (PubMed, Web of Science, and CNKI) using a structured set of keywords including, but not limited to, “social participation” “noncommunicable diseases (NCDs)” “older adults” and “International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF)”. A variable was retained as a key driver only if it fulfilled two dual criteria: (1) being empirically supported by findings from three or more independent studies, and (2) demonstrating direct alignment with a specific ICF category. For instance, the critical role of Mobility function (ICF b455) was confirmed by studies linking physical capacity to social activity levels [ , , ], while Pain management (ICF b280) was supported by literature on its profound impact on social withdrawal behaviors [ – ]. Similarly, Social support (ICF e3) and Functional ability (ICF d2-d4) were included based on extensive evidence of their facilitative role [ – ]. Variables such as Emotional functions (ICF b152) and Self-care ability (ICF d5) were also retained due to their well-established theoretical and empirical links to participation in the literature [ , , – ].In order to improve transparency, Supplementary Figure provides a process for screening each of our key variables into the criteria.
Complementing the empirically validated variables, a distinct set of constructs was incorporated based on the intrinsic requirements of system dynamics modeling and the comprehensive ICF framework. Fundamental mathematical components, such as Stocks (e.g., Body function status, Activity levels) and Flows (e.g., good health status, poor health status), constitute the essential architecture of any system dynamics model. Their inclusion is a methodological imperative for simulating the system’s states and rates of change, rather than a hypothesis subject to empirical validation. Furthermore, auxiliary variables like Urination functions (b620), Sleep functions (b134), and Energy drive (b130) were included because they emerged prominently from our patient interviews and are integral components of the ICF framework. Their validity stems from their status as patient-reported outcomes within this internationally recognized model. Additional intermediary auxiliary variables, such as Health status and Daily routine, were used to operationalize the causal pathways from the CLD into computable equations, serving as composite constructs that decompose complex relationships without requiring separate external validation.
To ensure model parsimony and enhance analytical focus, variables that lacked consistent empirical support or posed significant operationalization challenges were deliberately excluded. For instance, Genetic Predisposition was omitted due to the absence of consistent evidence for a direct and modifiable link to social participation behaviors within the model’s scope. Similarly, broad constructs like Cultural Context were excluded as they could not be reliably operationalized or measured with the available data, which would have compromised model precision and testability. This structured and transparent approach ensured that our final SFD is both empirically grounded in the relevant clinical and epidemiological literature and structurally sound as a system dynamics model for simulation, providing a comprehensive representation of the dynamics of social participation.

## Model validity testing
Rigorous validation of the model structure and equations using Vensim’s Check Model function confirmed error-free and logical consistency. Stability tests (Figure ) demonstrated consistent simulation curves across varying step sizes, attesting to the model’s robustness. This validated social participation system dynamics model accurately captures the dynamics of NCDs in older adults, facilitating further analysis and prediction.

## Simulation runs
After thorough deliberations within the research team, a comprehensive review of pertinent literature, and the construction of a causal chain for our system dynamics models, we conducted simulations to assess the impact of modulating six important variables: mobility function, pain management, functional ability, social support, emotional functions, and self-care ability, on social participation. These simulations were carried out under two intervention scenarios, corresponding to 10% and 20% modifications. The ultimate outcomes were obtained at the termination of the 16-month simulation period, through a comparative analysis of the adjusted final state under each intervention scenario with the baseline results. This comparison enabled the calculation of percentage changes, thereby allowing for the quantification of the precise impact each variable exerted on social participation.

## Scenario one: 10% change in each variable
Figure depicts that a 10% adjustment in each of the six variables—mobility function, emotional functions, functional ability, self-care ability, social support (increased), and pain (decreased)—led to a deceleration in the decline of social participation among older adults with NCDs. The simulation results indicated that while self-care ability and emotional functions exerted only a modest impact on social participation, with cumulative improvements staying below 3% throughout the simulation period, modifications in mobility function, functional ability, pain management, and social support yielded more pronounced effects. Specifically, these four variables exhibited maximum enhancements of 54.9%, 32.4%, 28.7%, and 25.0%, respectively, within the simulation timeframe.
 

## Scenario two: 20% change in each variable
Figure presents the simulation outcomes following a 20% adjustment in each of the six variables. Improvements in mobility function, pain management (pain level reduced by 20%), functional ability and social support by 20% resulted in increases in social participation rates among elderly patients with NCDs by 127.5%, 63.6%, 62.8%, and 48.7%, respectively, when compared to older patients with NCDs who did not receive any intervention measures after the simulation. Conversely, emotional functions and self-care ability have minimal effects, contributing less than 5% to overall social participation.
 

## Discussion
The application of System Dynamics Modeling (SDM) in the assessment of intervention scenarios for NCDs has received considerable attention in recent years [ , , ]. In this study, we employed the ICF framework to dynamically and systematically evaluate the impact of intervention programs on the social participation of older adults with NCDs. The dynamic nature of SDM empowers policymakers and healthcare practitioners to scrutinize outcome trends from a range of hypothetical scenarios, predict the potential consequences of various decisions, and navigate the complex layers inherent in healthcare systems [ ]. This methodology lays a theoretical groundwork for optimizing social participation among older adults with NCDs and informs the development of relevant and effective policies. While the model prioritizes identifying dynamic leverage points rather than quantifying absolute clinical thresholds, the relative improvements (e.g., 127.5% vs. 5%) provide actionable insights for intervention prioritization. It is essential to clarify how the percentage changes reported in our simulations should be interpreted. These values reflect the relative improvement in the model’s internal metric for social participation, measured against a simulated baseline scenario (no intervention) over a 16-month period. Rather than serving as direct predictors of absolute changes in standardized instruments such as the IPA questionnaire, these percentages are principally valuable for identifying relative leverage points within the system—for example, revealing that mobility function exerts a substantially stronger influence on social participation than self-care ability. To anchor these model-based outcomes in clinical reality, improvement in social participation as simulated in our study corresponds to tangible, meaningful shifts in patients’ daily lives. For instance, such an improvement might manifest as a transition from being predominantly home-bound to engaging in a weekly community activity; from dependence on family for transportation to independent use of public transport for social visits; or from avoiding social contact due to low self-confidence to actively maintaining meaningful relationships. These qualitative transitions embody the functional gains that real-world interventions targeting the identified leverage points ultimately aim to achieve.
This simulation study analyzed the influence of multiple factors on the social participation of older patients with NCDs, assessing their effects across intervention intensity scenarios set at 10% and 20%. These scenarios were designed to dynamically assess the impact of varying intervention levels on social participation among elderly NCDs patients using a system dynamics model, identify key leverage points, provide evidence-based support for policy formulation, and validate the model’s predictive capabilities. The findings underscored mobility function, pain management, functional ability, and social support as critical leverage points for enhancing social participation in this demographic. As Meadows describes, leverage points are strategic variables within a system where minor adjustments can lead to substantial improvements in overall system performance [ ]. The identification of these leverage points was primarily derived from simulation outcomes of accumulation (stock) variables, illustrating their enduring impact on the system through gradual accumulation over time. Our SDM analysis further unveiled complex feedback mechanisms between these leverage points, collectively contributing to an upsurge in social participation. By refining each leverage point, system efficiency can be optimized, leading to a significant augmentation in social participation among older adults with NCDs. Furthermore, CLD illustrates complex feedback mechanisms among these leverage points, and the subsequent discussion will integrate the leverage points identified by the SDF with the feedback loops in the CLD, aiming to enhance overall system efficiency and substantially improve social participation among older individuals with NCDs.
Simulation outcomes highlighted the profound impact of enhancing mobility function on physical rehabilitation and social engagement. Mobility function concentrates on the capacity to perform specific actions, including upper and lower limb motility, motor coordination, flexibility, balance, and neuromuscular control. The model illustrated that enhanced mobility function positively affected patients’ clinical health, leading to better respiratory and cardiac function, increased walking capacity, and greater exercise tolerance (B3 feedback loop). Furthermore, this enhancement facilitates social engagements with family and friends (R7 feedback loop), improves social support, and boosts overall health and social engagement. Group exercises (e.g., tai chi) were found to increase social participation among older adults through regular peer interactions [ ]. Additionally, enhanced functional ability aids in managing daily routines (R5 feedback loop), heightens health awareness, encourages group involvement, bolsters confidence, mitigates negative emotions (R2 feedback loop), and encourages engagement in family and community roles. A multimodal rehabilitation program combining physical training with social activities has been shown to reduce loneliness in elderly stroke survivors [ ]. This aligns with research by Blake et al. [ ] and the Yun team [ ], which indicated that older adults engaging in sports often establish new social connections, strengthening social support and benefiting their physical and mental well-being. Such engagement not only enhances quality of life but also expands opportunities for social engagement and fosters a more robust social network. Through various feedback loops, mobility function dynamically influences other variables within the social participation system. These insights underscore the necessity for healthcare professionals to prioritize interventions to enhance mobility function, thereby facilitating social participation among older adults with NCDs. In this context, Azizan and Fadzil [ ] emphasized that while motivational factors such as social engagement and self-efficacy drive older adults to stay active, perceived risks, health limitations, and lack of support remain persistent barriers, highlighting the need for both personalized and systemic strategies in intervention design. We recommend structured physiotherapy programs targeting these domains, such as twice-weekly group Tai Chi sessions or individualized resistance training regimens. Comprehensive exercise programs that combine strength training, balance training, and aerobic exercises, such as the Otago Exercise Program (OEP), should be implemented to improve physical function and reduce frailty in older adults [ ]. These programs should be tailored to individual capabilities and regularly adjusted to ensure continuous improvement.
Chronic pain, a widespread challenge among older adults with NCDs, permeates multiple bodily domains, exerting a substantial impact on their social participation and overall quality of life. Our system dynamics model identified pain as a critical leverage point within complex balance feedback mechanisms. The model shows how pain management affects social participation in older adults with NCDs through various pathways. For example, pain directly constrains physical functional ability and reduces mobility by impairing joint function (R3 feedback loop), which in turn diminishes the individual’s perception of their social participation. Specifically, pain restricts autonomy both indoors and outdoors, limiting movement at home and in the community. Moreover, pain indirectly affects social willingness and self-care ability (R5 feedback loop) through its effects on emotional and sleep functions (R2 feedback loop), leading to reduced interaction with family and friends in the dimensions of social relations and family role, thereby weakening the individual’s role within the family. Our model demonstrates that effective pain management can alleviate discomfort, enhance emotional functions, and encourage social participation, aligning with the findings of Ashton-James et al. [ ], who revealed that self-management of chronic pain enhances emotional functions and facilitates social participation. Conversely, chronic pain adversely affects emotional and social functioning, often leading to social withdrawal, as observed by Timm et al. [ ]. This complex feedback mechanism suggests that strengthening pain management not only addresses a crucial leverage point but also amplifies its effects through feedback loops that permeate the entire system. Based on these insights, we propose a multimodal approach that integrates pain neuroscience education (PNE) and behavioral strategies. Initially, intensive group-based PNE (6 sessions/10 hours) should be delivered to address pain catastrophizing and fear, while utilizing group dynamics for social support. This has been shown to enhance pain literacy and reduce fear-avoidant behaviors [ ]. Furthermore, we recommend incorporating mindfulness training to improve emotional regulation. This comprehensive strategy exploits systemic feedback mechanisms by simultaneously targeting biological, psychological, and social factors, achieving synergistic effects.
In our system dynamics simulations, we define functional ability as an individual’s overall ability to perform tasks across various real-life scenarios, encompassing physiological, psychological, social, and economic facets. A decline in functional ability often poses significant challenges to personal life accomplishment and restricts social engagements. Enhancing functional ability leads to substantial clinical health benefits, reduces disease-related medical expenses, and positively impacts financial situation (R4 feedback loop). It also increases mobility, granting greater independence in outdoor activities, fostering social engagement, and reducing reliance on others, which, in turn, strengthens psychological resilience. Concurrently, the improvements in functional ability alleviate symptoms of anxiety and depression, promoting emotional well-being (R2 feedback loop) and facilitating greater engagement in social activities.
The R6 feedback loop highlights that enhanced functional ability enables participation in various leisure pursuits, such as fitness and community events, which enrich daily experiences and create further opportunities for social engagement, particularly regarding indoor and outdoor autonomy. Wang et al. [ ] further described that functional decline, encompassing diminished functional ability, significantly curtailed social participation among older adults, accentuating the imperative to sustain and augment their functional ability. The various dimensions of functional ability reinforce each other within the feedback loop. This loop not only reflects the role of an individual’s abilities but also demonstrates how these abilities influence one another, contributing to overall functional performance. To optimize this leverage point, intervention strategies must be multidimensional, incorporating support from various aspects to comprehensively enhance activity capabilities. Enhanced functional ability also aids in managing daily routines (R5 feedback loop). To operationalize this leverage point, community-based “Active Living” programs could be designed, combining functional task training (e.g., sit-to-stand exercises) with real-world skill practice (e.g., grocery shopping simulations). Prescribing task-oriented training using real-life scenarios, such as stair climbing or using public transport, could further support functional ability improvement.
Enhanced social support significantly strengthens social participation by providing both emotional encouragement and tangible assistance, empowering patients to immerse themselves more vigorously in social endeavors. The R7 feedback loop shows that patients frequently receive support from multiple sources, including family, friends, society, upon their engagement in healthcare activities. This multifaceted support not only enhanced patients’ mental health, but also strengthened their energy and motivation. The R4 and R5 feedback loops indicate that social support indirectly promotes the participation of older adults with NCDs in daily life and community activities by improving their functional ability and self-care ability, thereby affecting their social participation. These feedback loops underscore the crucial role of social support in enhancing social participation among the older adults through various feedback mechanisms. Choi et al. [ ] revealed that emotional support serves to elevate mental health, boosting patients’ energy and motivation. This form of support fosters psychological resilience and facilitates greater interaction with the social environment. Harasemiw [ ] highlighted the significance of cultivating extensive social connections that transcend traditional ties among older Canadians. Additional studies have indicated that broadening social networks fulfilled emotional requirements, enhanced a sense of belonging, and thereby enriched social participation [ , ]. To optimize the advantages of social support, it is imperative to devise individualized plans tailored to patients’ needs and to augment community- and policy-level support measures, ensuring sustained integration into social activities over time. Enhanced social support significantly strengthens social participation by furnishing both emotional encouragement and tangible assistance. Clinically, this could involve establishing peer-support networks facilitated by physiotherapists, where patients with similar NCDs can share experiences and actively engage in rehabilitation activities together.
This study unexpectedly uncovered that emotional functions and self-care ability exerted only a modest short-term impact on social participation within the 16-month simulation timeframe. This contrasts with existing literature that underscores their substantial importance for well-being [ ]. Several factors may account for this discrepancy. First, significant time-lag effects are likely at play. The improvement in emotional health and the consolidation of self-care skills often require extended periods to manifest as an observable increase in social engagement. For instance, the benefits of depression intervention on motivation and self-confidence may need time to consolidate before robustly affecting social behavior, a process exceeding our simulation horizon. Second, our model’s structure prioritizes more direct, physically-mediated pathways. While emotional functions interact with other variables (e.g., sleep, energy in the R2 loop), and self-care ability supports daily routine (R5 loop), their short-term impact on social participation may be indirect and overshadowed by more immediate facilitators such as mobility and social support. Patients, especially those grappling with significant NCDs, may prioritize physical capacity for social engagement, while improvements in psychological and self-management domains tend to exhibit delayed effects [ ]. Future modeling efforts employing longer time horizons will be essential to elucidate these delayed psychological mechanisms.
The simulation scenarios involving 10% and 20% improvement in key variables are grounded in empirically achievable effect sizes reported in existing intervention studies. For instance, in the domain of mobility function, a structured, group-based exercise program such as the Otago Exercise Programme has been demonstrated in meta-analyses to improve balance and strength while reducing fall rates by approximately 35% among community-dwelling older adults, an effect magnitude that corresponds closely with our modeled intervention intensity [ ]. In the context of pain management, intensive group-based Pain Neuroscience Education (PNE) supplemented with graded activity has been shown in clinical trials to achieve a 20% reduction in pain-related disability and fear-avoidant behaviors, thereby validating the feasibility of this target [ ]. Furthermore, to attain a 20% improvement in social support, facilitated peer-support networks, where patients with similar NCDs engage in shared activities and collaborative goal-setting, have demonstrated significant reductions in perceived isolation and an increase in social engagement, with psychosocial effect sizes frequently surpassing this threshold [ ].
The insights delineate clear and actionable pathways for translating key leverage points into practical strategies across both community and clinical settings, particularly in discharge planning processes.
In the context of community-based interventions, the model underscores the efficacy of structured and cost-effective exercise programs, such as twice-weekly Tai Chi or chair yoga, which directly target mobility and balance to enhance social participation, as conceptualized in the R7 feedback loop (Fig. ). The implementation of established programs such as the Otago Exercise Program is highly recommended, given their documented efficacy and scalability. A critical facilitator for sustainable implementation is the integration of these programs into existing community infrastructures. Nevertheless, a potential barrier lies in the recruitment and retention of qualified instructors, a challenge that could be mitigated through train-the-trainer capacity-building models. Concurrently, pain management—another critical leverage point—can be addressed through group-based Pain Neuroscience Education (PNE) workshops, facilitated by trained nurses or physiotherapists. Although logistical constraints such as venue availability and participant adherence may arise, these can be navigated through carefully structured scheduling and the integration of telehealth support. To counteract social isolation, low-cost yet high-impact initiatives such as facilitated “Walk-and-Talk” groups or peer-led social clubs are strongly advocated. As supported by empirical evidence [ ], the success of these initiatives hinges on vigorous initial promotion and the cultivation of a dedicated core group of participants.
In the context of hospital discharge planning, the model highlights the necessity of a seamless transition from acute care to sustained community support. This process should be initiated with comprehensive assessments of physical function, pain, and social support systems at discharge to inform individualized care protocols. An explored strategy involves the systematic embedding of referrals to evidence-based community programs (e.g., exercise classes, pain management workshops) directly into the discharge process. This effectively operationalizes the R5 feedback loop, bridging the critical gap between functional recovery and social participation. The prevalent fragmentation between hospital care and community resources presents a significant systemic barrier. To address this, stronger hospital-community partnerships must be forged, supported by the strategic deployment of case managers to coordinate care transitions and ensure continuity. Finally, empowering patients and caregivers through education on condition management, medication adherence, and the vital role of social engagement is essential. This education transforms them from passive recipients into active advocates for their own health, thereby converting a potential barrier into a robust facilitator for long-term social participation.
The leverage points identified by our system dynamics model necessitate a coordinated, multi-stakeholder response that transcends individual clinical disciplines and sectors. Our simulation framework provides a strategic “map” for orchestrating this collaboration by aligning specific professional roles with the key system dynamics. For instance, physical therapists are central to enhancing mobility through evidence-based exercise programs like the Otago Exercise Programme [ ]. Occupational therapists improve functional ability by conducting task-oriented training for daily and community activities. The management of pain and the bolstering of social support require the integrated efforts of nurses, social workers, and community health staff; this can involve nurses co-delivering Pain Neuroscience Education, social workers linking patients to community resources and facilitating peer-support networks, and community health workers providing ongoing follow-up [ , ]. Ultimately, the role of policy-makers and health system administrators is critical for creating sustainable, enabling environments, which includes developing supportive reimbursement models, funding accessible infrastructure, and fostering formal partnership agreements between hospitals and community organizations to ensure service continuity. The successful implementation of these strategies hinges on a feasible multisectoral coordination framework that involves (1) Hospital-based initiation, where standardized discharge protocols trigger referrals to community programs; (2) Community-based execution, where local centers deliver interventions and share outcomes data; and (3) Policy-level enablement, where resources are allocated, and cross-sector training initiatives (such as train-the-trainer models for community instructors) are supported to break down silos and champion integrated care models.
By integrating these context-specific strategies, healthcare stakeholders can effectively transform the model’s theoretical leverage points into sustainable, real-world interventions that tangibly improve social participation among older adults with NCDs.

## Conclusion
This study innovatively employed a system dynamics model to systematically and dynamically explore the issue of social participation among older adults with NCDs. By integrating qualitative analysis (causal loop diagram) with quantitative analysis (stock flow diagram), a comprehensive and holistic model was established. This model describes the complex interplay and interactions among a myriad of variables. Through rigorous simulation of feedback loops within diverse contextual frameworks, the study offers insights into the potential effects and optimization strategies of intervention measures, transcending the constraints of static analyses. This dynamic approach facilitates the prediction of how changes in health status impact social participation among older adults with NCDs. By providing a forward-looking perspective for intervention decision-making, it enhances the efficiency of health management for this population. Furthermore, the model allows for an in-depth analysis of the various factors influencing social participation and establishes a solid theoretical foundation for precision and personalized nursing. Ultimately, applying this method optimizes nursing intervention strategies, effectively addressing the individual needs of patients.

## Limitations
This study is subject to several limitations inherent in its modeling approach. First, as a simplification of reality, the model does not incorporate broader socio-political factors such as specific policy interventions, which may influence social participation at a systemic level. Second, the analysis did not disaggregate the four dimensions of social participation (autonomy indoors, social relations, family role, and autonomy outdoors), thereby limiting a nuanced understanding of how each dimension contributes to the overall construct. Third, the model may not fully capture the complex, indirect pathways through which psychological and self-care variables operate—such as those mediated by self-efficacy or psychological resilience—potentially leading to an underestimation of their long-term role. Finally, the model is designed primarily to identify feedback mechanisms and relative effects among variables, rather than to establish clinical thresholds (e.g., the Minimal Clinically Important Difference of the IPA). Translating simulated trends into clinically meaningful metrics will require future empirical validation.
To address these limitations, future research could incorporate explicit policy variables, distinguish sub-dimensions of social participation, and integrate mediating psychological constructs such as self-efficacy to better capture the long-term role of self-care and emotional well-being. Coupling system dynamics modeling with clinical trial data may also help bridge the gap between simulated trends and patient-centered outcomes. Additionally, the integration of monitoring technologies such as wearable sensors could offer novel data streams for simulating real-time feedback on mobility and social engagement in future model iterations [ ].
