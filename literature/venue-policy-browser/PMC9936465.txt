## Abstract
The Visegrad Group format — coordinated policy forum for Czechia, Slovakia, Poland and Hungary — has been dubbed as a significant policy tool that advocates the interests and builds synergies among the four partners. The “Visegrad Four + ” format, which coordinates foreign relations of these four countries, has been narrated as the key foreign policy venue of the V4, and the V4 + Japan is often understood as the key partnership within this format. Due to the recently growing Chinese influence in Central and Eastern Europe, and the impacts of the war in Ukraine in 2022, many have come to expect the coordination to strengthen and ramify. This article argues, however, that the V4 + Japan platform represents only a marginal policy forum and is unlikely to gain any significant political momentum in the foreseeable future. Basing the analysis on a set of interviews with the V4 and Japanese policymakers, the paper posits three reasons that have prevented the deepening of the V4 + Japan coordination: (i) there are significant limits to socialization in the group, (ii) there are diverse threat perceptions among V4 members, and (iii) there is little interest in deepening economic coordination vis-a-vis third parties. These findings question the viability of foreign policy coordination among the Visegrad Group members, and highlight the impediments for the expansion of V4 + Japan cooperation.
Keywords: Visegrad Group, V4, Social norms, Coordination, Small states

## Introduction
On March 17, 2022, just a few weeks into the Russian invasion of Ukraine, the representatives of the Visegrad Group countries (Poland, Hungary, Czechia and Slovakia, also known as Visegrad Group, Visegrad Four, or simply V4) met with the Japanese Foreign Minister Yoshimasa Hayashi to discuss support for Ukraine and enhanced development of economic and political relations. This meeting was considered to be a further sign of continuing strategic congruence between Japan and the V4, and broadly of Japan and Europe. Following the end of the Cold war, the EU has worked to enhance its partnership with Japan, which has led to a series of trade and political deals being signed; most recently, the 2019 Economic Partnership Agreement (EPA) and the Strategic Partnership Agreement (SPA). While working on making closer ties with the EU, Japan has also looked to many of the newly democratic countries. The V4 countries, it seemed, were a new venue of Japanese interest since as far as 2003, when Japanese Prime Minister Junnichiro Koizumi visited Central Europe. Their newly opened economies, skilled, but cheap labour force, and political push towards integration with the EU was attractive to Tokyo, and Japan’s long serving Prime Minister Shinzo Abe made it clear in 2007 that Japan values and intends to develop the V4 + Japan format.
The Russian invasion of Ukraine in March 2022 was seen as a clear sign for the need to bolster existing allied security and economic ties. Japan’s rather strong reaction to the invasion, which included asset freezes, condemnation of Russian actions, several waves of sanctions and unprecedented military aid to Ukraine was perhaps a surprise to many observers, especially given the restrained reaction of Tokyo to Russian annexation of Crimea in 2014. Together with the growth of Chinese influence in Central and Eastern Europe (CEE) following the establishment of its 17 + 1 (now 16 + 1) partnership in 2012, one could assume that Japan would become ever more interested in bolstering its partnerships with both the EU and the V4, and most of the existing literature on V4-Japan concludes so. Higashino ( ), for instance, argues that despite initial Japanese reluctance to engage the V4 politically, the recent development of the 16 + 1 strategy has positively influenced Japanese interest in the CEE region. Cautious about the growth of Chinese influence, Japan could pursue the V4-Japan framework as a means of countering the Belt and Road initiative within CEE countries. Tsuruoka ( ) argues that Brexit could play into V4 + Japan hands by making the region more interesting for Japanese foreign direct investment (see also Bertalanič , for a broader overview of V4 + Japan, see Laš ). The V4, itself, has on many occasions voiced its optimism about the V4 + Japan framework, stating that it “improves the stability and importance of the four nation group” (V4 ) and that the “dynamics of the V4 + Japan meetings have been constantly strengthening” (V4 ).
Although there might be some relevance to the claim that CEE has become somewhat more interesting for Japan in the recent years, I argue that despite this optimistic discursive framing, the cooperation lacks solid grounding in both political and economic spheres, and is hardly likely to function as a basis for further political or economic integration among these countries. There are at least three reasons why this is so. First, there are significant limits to V4’s socialisation. Despite the fact that these countries are usually considered to be close in cultural, political and economic terms, the fall of communism has exacerbated several social and political cleavages that have become stronger over time, diminishing the potential of sharing social norms and prohibiting the constitution of a collective identity. Second, there are severe differences in terms of strategic cultures, threat perceptions and desired mode of engagement of these four countries in security matters, which were laid bare by the Russian invasion of Ukraine. Since Japan considers the V4 as a complementary tool to the coordination with the EU, it is wary of these differences. Third, there are significant impediments to any form of a V4-Japan economic integration due to the internal EU rules on investment incentives, and competitive, rather than collaborative, nature of their economic relations. These three factors significantly limit the possibilities of deepening the V4-Japan framework, despite the (somewhat artificial) will on both sides to continue in it, as was shown by the March 2022 meeting. Illustrating these dynamics sheds a new light on the limits of the V4 + Japan framework, but also the limits of the V4 as a policy tool on its own.
My aims are primarily empirical. There is a very limited supply of literature on various subregional formats within the EU and their foreign policy coordination. The literature on V4, and especially, the “V4 + ” format is so scarce that there are virtually no sources of data besides direct consultations with the foreign ministries. In order to obtain the data, I have conducted in 2022, a series of semi-structured interviews at the ministries of foreign affairs of the V4 countries (altogether four interviews with officers from the CEE bureaus of the four countries), and an in-depth qualitative interview with a high political representative of Japanese embassy in one of the V4 countries. In 2022, I have also carried out several interviews with V4 experts from CEE and Japan from both governmental and non-governmental organisations. These have helped me to illustrate the hidden functioning of this format and the various perceptions of this format among the participating countries. These insights can be found throughout the text, including in the historical overview, but mostly in the analytical part. Despite this primary empirical focus of the paper, I also evaluate the findings with the help of several theoretical concepts (socialisation, strategic culture and interdependence), and inductively test their relevance vis-a-vis the sub regional group’s development.

## The evolution of the V4 and the V4 + format
The V4 was created in 1991 following the dissolution of the Soviet Union. Promoted by the late Czech President Vaclav Havel, the framework’s principal targets were democratization and integration with the Western Europe and the USA through membership in the EU and NATO. Sharing similar historical experience, central European culture and relatively similar economic systems, the V4 countries believed that the organisation would help them promote shared interests, attract foreign direct investment (FDI) and consolidate their new democratic systems. From the onset of this coordination, however, there were visible limits of what it can achieve. The means of cooperation were restricted to consultations and meetings on various levels as it was laid out in the 1991 Declaration on Cooperation between the Czech and Slovak Federal Republic, the Republic of Poland and the Republic of Hungary in Striving for European Integration (Kolmašová ). Though the EU played the key part in the V4’s political and economic transition (mainly through the PHARE program), Japan was quick to invest into the newly emerged democracies. Tokyo was one of the founding partners of the European Bank for Reconstruction and Development (EBRD), and made significant strides to build preferable relations with CEE countries prior to their accession to the EU in 2004. Japan’s investment rose gradually, and the trade between Japan and CEE tripled between 1995 and 2005 (Bertalanič ).
After the accession of the V4 into the EU, the group’s initial goals were achieved. Confident of the viability of the framework for the post-2004 years, the member states re-oriented the V4 framework to coordinate their interests within the EU in cultural, political, and security spheres. While aiming at coordination of V4 positions within the EU institutions, the V4 made it clear that it was “not created as an alternative to the all-European integration efforts, nor does it try to compete with the existing functional Central European structures” (V4 webpage). The V4’s integration has never been a linear process. Quite to the contrary, since the organisation is a purely non institutionalised one, built on common ties, specific interests, inter-ministerial and other means of cooperation, it had ups and downs depending on the at-the-time political representations in the V4 countries (compare Samson ; Střítecký ; Kořan ; Lorenz ; Madej ; Tarö et al. ; Kolmašová ). Although the cooperation flourished in the 1990s and early 2000s (calling itself “a recognised symbol of successful political and economic transformation and, in many areas, also a model for regional cooperation” (V4 )), after the accession to the EU in 2004, it stumbled somehow, only to be reinvigorated in the 2010s with the newly emergent security coordination within NATO (in the form of a Poland-led joint battle groups). The group had also coordinated its interests during the 2015 refugee crisis, in which the V4 countries adopted common anti-migration rhetoric in the EU. After the refugee crisis subsided, the organisation seemed to split into 2 + 2 format depending on the level of engagement with the EU (Czechia and Slovakia were more inclined to follow EU norms, whereas Hungary and Poland were in conflict with EU norms, see Bayer and Cienski ). In the last few years (2020–2022), the organisation was barely kept alive due to the internal discord among the V4 countries, especially with the change in governments in Czechia and Slovakia in 2021 to a relatively pro-Western, moderate ones led by PMs Petr Fiala and Eduard Heger (leading to the cancellation of some regular events such as the PM-level meeting in June 2022).
The fact that the organisation was kept purely flexible has always been understood as both the strength and weakness of the pact. “It allows us the freedom to talk on issues where we see there is a common position,” one diplomat explained. “But not only on ministerial level, but also on inter-ministerial, or even non-ministerial levels”. Specific teams from each administration would team on particular issues (such as heads of energy bureaus at the ministries of industries and trade on diversification), share good practice and discuss the challenges. But this flexibility works better on bureaucratic issues, where no political capital is required. In terms of foreign policy, there is often a discord among the four. Especially Hungary has become the thorn in the pact, with its visible support for China and Russia and harsh anti-migration rhetoric. “We do not want to be seen as going against the main EU currents,” a Czech MOFA officer explained to me. “But with Hungary this is often the case”. This has been laid bare by the war in Ukraine. Despite initial condemnation of Russian actions, Hungary has been restrained in its actions and discourse on Russia, bombarding common EU positions on sanctions (Barigazzi ). Poland and the Czech Republic, on the other hand, have followed a tough position on Russia, mobilising support for Ukraine and calling for tougher sanctions against Moscow.
Meaningful cooperation of V4 countries in foreign relations (the V4 + format) was thus mainly possible in circumstances, which were seen as non-problematic (such as the bilateral relations with the Republic of Korea and Japan), or cases where the V4 felt they have special existing relations from which the whole EU could benefit (Western Balkans and the Eastern partnership). Other venues of the V4 + format that emerged in the 2010s, such as V4 + Latin America and the Caribbean, V4 + Egypt and V4 + African Union never reached any meaningful formats outside of ceremonial acknowledgment of their political relations. The success, but also the target of the V4 + framework heavily depends on which country is holding the presidency, because the presiding country draws the outline of V4’s meetings with third countries, and sends it to the other members for comments and suggestions. According to several V4 ministerial officers, the possibility of commenting on the draft programme varies with the presiding state — for instance, the Hungarian presidency hardly ever acknowledges comments from other members. This means that if a particular country has better ties and connections to specific countries in the “ + ” format, the (inter) ministerial (or PM-level) meetings between them are more likely to take place. Another perhaps less expected benefit of the V4 + format is that it allows the presiding country to multilateralise a meeting with a particular world leader, which would otherwise be seen as arguable were it in a bilateral format (Hungary has often been criticised for doing this, see Zachová ). Though the origins of the V4 + format were mostly on the expert level, in the last 5 years (2017–2022), the meetings have more often become political and were carried out on the levels of foreign ministers or PMs, which many of the ministry officers do not see as positive, because “if there is no expert coordination prior to the meetings, they can easily lack substance” (Interview 1).
The V4 + Japan framework is often understood as one of the most important of all of the V4 + frameworks, due to its significant substance and long history (Higashino : 113). It came to life in 2003, but was basically started only after V4’s joined the EU in 2004. Initial contacts leading to its formalisation were made when the Japanese emperor Akihito with the empress Michiko visited CEE in 2002 followed by a visit by the Prime Minister Junnichiro Koizumi in 2003. Japan bolstered the relations under the first mandate of PM Shinzo Abe in 2006 through 2007. Although it had somewhat slowed down after government change in Japan in 2009, under Abe’s second government since 2012, Tokyo has returned to the region following Abe’s Arc of Freedom and Prosperity strategy, to bolster its relationship with both the EU and various regional groupings (besides the V4, it has also cooperated with Nordic and Baltic countries through NB8 + Japan framework, see Bertalanič ; Laš ). For the 10th anniversary of the V4 + Japan framework, Abe visited Warsaw in 2013, and launched a new “Partnership Based on Common Values for the 21st Century”. The Warsaw summit marked a new stage in the partnership and further expanded the cooperation into areas such as security, science, technology and environmental policy. The leaders of both V4 and Japan seemed determined to pursue high-level dialogue on a regular basis, and also on the level of ambassadors and foreign ministers (which was the case with the March 2022 meeting in Tokyo). Both sides expressed their intention to establish the V4 plus Japan Policy Planning Dialogue to consult and coordinate on major international issues (V4 , see also Bertalanič ).
Since then, the cooperation has diversified and expanded. Abe’s second cabinet has stabilised Japanese domestic politics, and his push for a proactive foreign policy has reinvigorated the Japan-EU relations leading to the adoption of the Economic and Strategic Partnership Agreements in 2019. The discussions with the V4 became regular and showed first signs of institutionalisation, dealing with topics such as coordination prior to UN climate conferences (COPs), the fallout of Brexit and with the issues connected to migration and COVID-19. In 2021, the already 7th meeting of foreign ministers of the V4 + Japan format took place in Poland, where the Japanese Foreign Minister Toshimitsu Motegi praised “major achievements of cooperation,” such as ““V4 plus Japan” seminars on cybersecurity and connectivity, assistance for the Western Balkans, joint research on science and technology and coordination among think tanks, and shared the view to continue and enhance such cooperation” (MOFA ). Japan has also been very active in supporting the V4’s and the EU’s push for an Indo-Pacific strategy and was happy to see the strategy launched by the Von der Leyen Commission in September 2021.
These developments have led many to believe that the coordination is alive and well, or, in fact, that it is prospering. Spilling from democracy promotion to security cooperation and diplomatic dialogues, many have come to believe it will continue to spillover and expand. The war in Ukraine, which started in February 2022, was another key moment with a great potential to propel these two together due to the increased levels of shared threats. But, as this paper will now illustrate, there are significant limits of where the V4 + Japan framework can develop, and its current stage may be the most these two partners can achieve.

## Lack of a common identity
Varieties of social constructivism have advocated various logics of social integration, based on collective identities (Acharya ), shared social norms (Katzenstein ; Finnemore and Sikkink ), discursive framing of identities based on inclusion/exclusion (Hansen ), or the sheer amount of contacts among the members of a bloc (Deutsch ; Adler and Barnett ). Although there were fierce debates among various strands of social constructivism (ranging from the most quantitative security complex theories to the most interpretative and discourse-driven critical constructivist ones, for a broader discussion see Kolmaš ), the concept of socialisation, or a more broadly norm transfer and internalisation has served as the main driving force of institutionalisation and cooperation in general.
The V4 countries seem like the perfect location for norm transfer. Sharing similar political backgrounds, joint Central European history, relatively similar economic models, cultural closeness, and political preferences (mainly within the EU), one could assume that these countries would develop a shared normative understanding similarly to a security community. Many authors have argued this is in fact the case (Fawn , ). But despite three decades of post-revolution development, internalisation of norms within the community has yet to materialise. The reasons can be traced to both internal and external dimensions of the V4 societies, discourses and policies. Internally, the last three decades of post-communist development have exacerbated several cleavages (religious, political, and social) that have been suppressed by the communist regimes.
In terms of religion, Poland has become dominated by conservative political forces advocating for a strong role of religion in the society, leading to a regression on some societal issues such as the rights to abortion (which was basically banned by the Constitutional Tribunal in 2021, see Amnesty International ). Though the Hungarian Prime Minister Viktor Orbán was once an atheist, he has also used the Christianity argument in order to shield Hungary from the “decadent” West as well as from Muslim refugees. Orbán has also resurrected anti-semitic rhetoric by (inter alia) making the Jewish Hungary-born billionaire Goerge Soros his main political enemy. Contrary to that, Czechia (and to a lesser extent Slovakia), with a high percentage of atheists, has become relatively progressive, for instance hosting large rainbow parades in Prague every year and advocating for same-sex marriages (though these are yet to be passed).
Furthermore, the Hungarian and Polish governments have had a lasting struggle with the EU because of their backsliding in terms of liberal democracy. The rise of “ethnopopulism” (Vachudova ) in Warsaw and Budapest combined with economic crises and the pressure of migration waves have resulted in the consolidation of power of the two major conservative forces (Fidesz and PiS) leading to the rise of authoritarianism (Ciannetti et al020, Bernhard ). Becoming isolated in the process, they have sought the V4 for assistance and support, which was not always forthcoming (leading some to speak of the 2 + 2 format rather than V4, see Bayer and Cienski ). Especially after the elections in 2021, which brought back pro-EU moderate coalitions in Czechia and Slovakia, these have been worried about their own image within the EU and have backpaddled on the V4 cooperation in order not to be seen as supportive of their struggle.
Hungary was often noted as the most problematic of the four countries (even the Hungarians sometimes talk about the V4 becoming a V3 + 1, interview 2). Czechia has in 2021 enacted a new strategy of rapprochement with Poland, after it managed to settle some long-standing issues in the bilateral relations, such as the hurting environmental impacts of the Turów coalmine. Following the Russian invasion of Ukraine, the PMs Fiala and Morawiecki have struck a common tone and even the respective two ministries speak of a new wave of interest in coordination of their policies and strategic interests bilaterally as well as within the EU. These changes are momentary and there is no guarantee that the rapprochement will continue in the future. It, however, continues to exacerbate the tension between the Czech Republic, Poland and Slovakia, and Hungary. The divide between the “V3” and Hungary is, furthermore, clearly visible in their perception of the V4 + format as a foreign policy tool.
For Poland, Czechia, and Slovakia, the V4 + format represents a complementary policy field to the V4’s main goal, which is building synergies and cohesion within CEE. They do not consider the V4 as a means of foreign policy making outside of some specific regions such as the Western Balkans and the Eastern partnership (interviews 1, 2, and 3). Mostly, the V4 countries would use the partnership in order to bolster relationships and attract investment from several like-minded countries that share the V4’s values (such as Japan, ROK, or India). Besides that, the V4 uses this format in areas where they have historically good connections from which others (EU, NATO) could benefit — such as in the Western Balkans and the Eastern partnership. Its role is thus, according to the ministries of Czech Republic, Poland, and Slovakia, largely ceremonial. It also offers the “ + ” countries several practical benefits, since their high-level politicians can meet the four countries together and not travel to each capital separately. Although this sometimes has negative consequences (after V4 + meetings, the third parties would often not see the point in meeting bilaterally, the officers said), Poland, Czechia, and Slovakia generally understand these limitations in exchange of the political value these meetings bring.
Hungary, on the other hand, sees the V4 + format as a means of promoting its own foreign policy agenda, sometimes hand-picked by Orban himself (rather than by the foreign ministry). This agenda is twofold. First, Budapest aims to pragmatically attract interest in countries, which sometimes might not have a good image with the rest of the EU. This was visible for instance by the Hungary-initiated meeting between the V4 and Egypt in 2017 (Hungary has a long history of trade with Egypt, especially in the rail transportation sector. Orban himself, rather than the Hungarian MOFA, initiated this meeting, interview 4). Second, Budapest uses the V4 format to bolster its own image as a non-isolated coalition partner (this has been referred to repeatedly by officers of other V4 MOFA’s and corroborated by the Hungarian experts). Orban, for instance, initiated meetings with Morocco and Egypt and wanted another one with Turkey. This was not well received by the other three V4 members, who did not want to be associated with leaders of these countries and would have a hard time explaining why it is necessary to meet with them in the V4 + format (interviews 1 and 2). Though the other ministries were surprised to see these events, they eventually joined them to show a fundamental level of unity. It is apparent, however, that Hungary’s pragmatic, but also instrumental use of the V4, marks a different motivation from the other group members.
The lack of common identity has limited the possibilities of V4 + Japan framework, and has forced Tokyo to navigate among these cleavages within the V4 (and between the V4 and the EU). Japan does not want to engage in the domestic politics of the V4 countries, and it has largely refrained from commenting on the democratic development there (the internal issues of these countries such as democratic backsliding are an internal matter of the EU and we have no right to criticise them, Japanese MOFA officers explained). Tokyo’s intentions, however, were from the onset not only to support a newly established group of democratising and liberalising states, but also to bolster its coordination with the EU through the support of its sub regional grouping (in line with Abe's proactive “global Japan” strategy), and feels uneasy about the division between some of the V4 members and Brussels. As long as the V4 + functions as a flexible means of coordination of V4 and Japan’s economic and other sectors without ostensibly damaging its relationship with Brussels, Japan is generally happy dealing with it. But the visible and strengthened cleavages among the V4 members prohibit any possibility of a common identity and limit what could be achieved from the network as well as Japanese willingness to deepen it.

## Diverse strategic preferences
There are several realist-derived concepts that could be usable for understanding the integration through the V4. Strategic preference, threat perception, power balancing or alliances, among others, are concepts that are often used to define reasons for integration or cooperation (i.e., Nexon ). There has been a strategic convergence among the V4 and Japan for some time now. The rise of China, which is generally feared by Japan as well as by largely anti-Chinese (with the exception of Hungary) societies in the V4 countries converges their interests in countering it. The rise of China’s 17 + 1 (now 16 + 1) format since the 2012 then instils fears in Tokyo about the possible rapprochement of Beijing towards its partners in CEE. These divisions have only deepened with the Russian invasion of Ukraine in 2022, and as a result, it seemed only obvious that the security integration both within the EU/V4 and NATO, and between these and Japan would strengthen due to the rise of the common threat (be it after or even before the invasion, see, i.e. Usiak , Nad et al. ). Japan positioned itself firmly in the pro-Western camp, and many saw Japan’s participation at the EU security meeting in Brussels in Spring 2022, and in the NATO Military Chiefs of Defense meeting in May 2022 together with the NATO summit in June 2022 in Madrid, as signs of this dynamic (Barlett ).
As several authors have illustrated in the past (i.e., Bukh ), Russia has since pre-modern times occupied the role of the threatening other contributing to the evolution of modern Japanese identity. Although Abe has tried to strike a cooperative tone with Vladimir Putin prior to the 2022 invasion (believing that these two countries can achieve the resolution of the South Kurile Island Dispute), in the wake of the invasion Japan did much to bolster its alliance with NATO countries, adopting tough sanctions, freezing Russian assets and condemning its actions. The invasion has also alienated China, because Beijing was unwilling to follow the West in their condemnation of Russian behaviour, and continued to support Moscow in their struggle (discursively and economically, China is yet to assist Russia through transfer of military equipment as of October 2022).
Most of the EU countries, and the EU itself, reacted much in the same way as Japan did. The Council of Europe condemned the Russian invasion of Ukraine right after it took place in February 2022. The EU followed with several waves of sanctions, trade liberalisation, economic assistance and military support to Ukraine struggling with the gruesome Russian war. The Visegrad Four countries initially supported the retaliation measures against Moscow, with Czechia and Poland taking on the lead role in initiating strong actions against Russia. The Prime Ministers Fiala and Morawiecki even travelled to Kiev to meet with the Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky several weeks after the invasion started, and promised support to hundreds of thousands of Ukrainian refugees. With the time passing since the invasion, however, Hungary became more cautious about the retaliatory measures. Worried about straining the good relationship between Orbán and Vladimir Putin, and afraid of the rising costs of oil and gas, Hungary found itself protesting against strengthening sanctions. Budapest has effectively barraged the common EU position, and has also severely damaged any further means of cooperation on Ukraine within the V4 and the V4 + Japan format. For the representatives of Poland, Czechia and Slovakia, it was apparent from the onset that there would be no coordination on the Ukrainian war (interviews 2 and 3). Although they considered the V4 as an ad hoc coalition working where there is a possibility of a common position, it was obvious that radically different positions as emerged after the invasion would have lasting effect on other fields of cooperation (interview 4).

Although all four of the countries are NATO members, and have coordinated their security efforts on, for instance, building a joint V4 battlegroup (led and initiated by Poland), their perception of the cooperation varies. Despite all of them sharing some inclination to Atlanticism in their defence orientation, Czechia and Slovakia have kept basically a low profile stance in security coordination, engaging only weakly, and were motivated mainly by the necessity not to be understood as a free-rider, or in order to gain credibility within NATO and the EU. Hungary has focused on protection of borders against migrants and understood NATO as an actor in this struggle, but has been unwilling to engage Russia in Eastern Europe, leading to its soft position vis-a-vis the Ukraine war. Contrary to that, Poland has been the only country among the V4, which has managed to influence NATO policies. Warsaw has repeatedly drawn NATO’s attention to Russian expansionism, and has favoured stronger measures in tackling it (for a broader discussion, see Kolmašová ). Although all four countries would benefit from a tighter security coordination, the lack of strategic unity, and the differences in modes of engagement and motivation has prevented this coordination from happening. The war in Ukraine has only exacerbated these differences, and — as noted above — has deepened the security cleavages among the V4 (see Table ).
 Japanese strategic interest in the V4 visibly reflected the rise of Chinese engagement of these countries through the 17 + 1 framework and bilaterally. Japan became worried about the EU’s “soft” stance on China, demonstrated by the willingness of many European countries to participate in Beijing’s Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB), together with the rise of mutual trade followed by high-level visits both within Europe and in China. V4 was understood similarly — under the previous governments in Czechia and Slovakia (PMs Andrej Babiš and Igor Matovič), but also in Poland and most notably Hungary, there was a visible push towards friendlier ties with China, in order to attract investment into the region. In the late 2010s going to the 2020s, however, this push has partly disappeared. New governments in Prague and Bratislava have shied away from China. The lack of desired investments and China’s unwillingness to join the allied countries in their condemnation of Russian actions in Ukraine has significantly cooled their relations with Beijing (leading to both Czechia and Slovakia to send envoys to Taiwan, to a great Chinese ire). This was visible also on the EU level, with the freeze of the ratification process of the EU-China Comprehensive Agreement on Investment (CAI) in May 2021 widely understood as a proof that the honeymoon period is over (Higashino : 114), and was supported by both Czechia and Slovakia. 

Poland and Hungary, however, have been much less inclined to drop the pleasantries with Beijing. Hungary has had a positive pro-Chinese policy for at least a decade, and it has been one of Orban’s primary foreign policy preferences. But Poland has also reinvigorated its relations with Beijing since 2021, when the Polish and Chinese ministers exchanged phone calls and the Polish President Andrzej Duda virtually joined the largely neglected 17 + 1 summit calling it an “important pillar of cooperation between the EU and China” (Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Poland ). The Polish were also among the most vocal critics of the way in which the EU froze the CAI agreement in May 2021. In February 2022, Chinese Prime Minister Xi Jinping met Duda and declared Poland a “key point in China-EU relations” (Lo ). Although this rapprochement may very well be just a temporary phase that could fade with any future government, it still marks a difference inside the V4 that prevents internal coordination on foreign policy and strains the V4 + Japan framework. Thus, Japan tackles two issues when dealing with the V4 strategically. First, it tries to reconcile its support for the V4 with its policy for the EU as a whole. Second, it needs to overcome the strategic inclination of two V4 members towards Beijing. Both of these, however, undermine the viability of the V4 framework and its future trajectory.

## Competitive, rather than cooperative, economies
International Political Economy and economic liberalism literatures would assume that increasing interdependence brought about by the signed S/EPAs would only propel these together politically (i.e., Reuveny and Kang ; Press-Barnathan ). To some extent, this has been the case with the V4 + Japan framework. Japan became initially interested in the V4 + framework in the 1990s, due to the fall of communism and the expectations regarding economic growth of the CEE countries. With a significant amount of skilled population, industrial basis and infrastructure, but a significantly cheap labour force, the CEE countries seemed like good venues for Japanese FDI. Czech Republic and Hungary, in particular, were seen in the 1990s as safe targets for investment. Japanese companies operating in the Western Europe saw this as an opportunity, and started moving their operations Eastwards, and followed with strengthening investment in the V4. Because of the CEE's experience with heavy industry, and relatively high-class level of industrial/technology education, Japanese automotive companies, and later electronics companies, moved in and expanded their operations. Companies like Sony and Matsushita Electric announced plans to build full-scale factories in some of the V4 member states. By the time the European Commission launched the Agenda 2000 and fast-tracked several CEEC–including all V4 members–for EU membership, the Visegrad Group had already successfully redefined its position in Central Europe (Bertalanič : 6).
The accession of the V4 into the European Union only speeded up this process. The growing compliance with EU regulations (market access, standardisation of investment rules and procedures, etc.) made the investment in CEE even more interesting for Japanese companies, and by the time, they joined the union in 2004, there was already a significant FDI inflow to V4. Toyota and PSA established their production plant close to Kolin (CZ) in 2002, swiftly followed by similar investments in other V4 countries (including companies such as Asahi Glass, Ibiden, Bridgestone, Sony and Matsushita Electrics), with perhaps only Slovakia somewhat lagging behind (which is visible in smaller numbers of employees of Japanese companies, see Table ). Despite a somewhat significant growth of Chinese — and especially South Korean — investment in the V4, Japan has retained its position as the most important Asian investor to Czechia and Poland, and second largest to Hungary and Slovakia. Japanese multinationals, operational in the automotive, electronics, and other industries (such as Asahi Beer’s purchase of Pilsner Urquell), as well as in certain services, play an important role in CEE economies (Szunomár ). Japan also cooperates with the V4 through the International Visegrad Fund (IVF), which is often understood by the V4 ministerial officials as one of the most important means of “practical” identity/cultural cohesion building mechanisms. Japan together with V4 offers grants on advanced material projects, and supports inter-university cooperation.
 The V4 + Japan framework was often understood as a vehicle for Japanese investment in CEE, and also a tool for the V4 countries to cooperate in attracting it. This is, however, rather a significant exaggeration of the influence the V4 + Japan had on FDI inflow. First, there are significant limits in the possibilities of cooperation among the V4 in attracting Japanese investment. All of the four countries have mostly similar industrial economies with good education and relatively high productivity, but still cheap labour costs compared to Western Europe (of course, there are also some differences there. Poland has the largest market and thus has a special position among them. Hungary has a lower work productivity, whereas Czechia struggles with availability of workforce, see Natsuda et al ). All four are also tied by the European common market legislation, which coordinates investment rules and procedures. The V4 countries have often found themselves competing for investment. The most visible example of this competition was in 2018, when Slovakia offered a €125 million investment incentive package to attract Jaguar/Land Rover’s €1.4 billion package to build a car manufacturing facility in the region of Nitra. Other V4 countries were also interested in this investment, and Slovakia eventually found itself investigated by the European Commission for breaching the EU investment rules. Although the investigation eventually cleared Slovakia arguing that “the positive effects of the project on regional development clearly outweigh any distortion of competition brought about by the State aid” (European Commission ), it proves that there is a competition, rather than cooperation, on attracting foreign aid.
This is all the more visible in Japanese perception of the cooperation. Although Japan sees the V4 as a viable mode of political coordination, the manufacturers and industries have only a very limited knowledge of what Visegrad Four or even CEE mean as a unit. This is perhaps understandable fact that companies operate on a market basis and are not guided by political manoeuvring. But the same can be said about the public sector. Maybe for some exceptions at the Japanese Ministry of Foreign Affairs and the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, most ministry officers are oblivious about the V4 (interviews 6 and 7). Bilateral coordination is usually understood as a sufficient mode of economic cooperation and the V4 format does not provide many opportunities outside of it, because of the competitive nature of investment in the V4 countries. Possibilities for joint ventures are in terms of cultural exchange and tourism, but not in private-sector trade and investment (interview 8).
To conclude this section then, although Japan is among the most important Asian economic partners for all of the V4 countries, and although the mutual trade has grown significantly in the last three decades, this has been largely done through bilateral means, rather than through the V4. Despite the growth in terms of political agenda during the V4 + Japan meetings, the most palpable economic outcome of the framework has been Japanese investment into the International Visegrad Fund. There might be a possibility in the future to coordinate the approach of the EU (incl. V4) and Japan on rebuilding the war-torn Ukraine, and Tokyo is already preparing for this. But it is unlikely that the V4 + Japan framework will strengthen as a result of that.

## Conclusion
In 2021, the Visegrad Four celebrated thirty years since its inception. During this time, it has evolved significantly. Despite initially coordinating democratisation and liberalisation of the newly created post-communist states, the cooperation now spreads from highest-level PM meetings, through inter-ministerial expert cooperation, to cultural and scientific exchange through the International Visegrad Fund. The V4 seems to be stronger than ever. But while it may seem this way, in reality, despite the discursive fanfare, there remains significant impediments that continue to plague the minilateral grouping and its possibility of transcending the “ad-hoc” status of its functioning.
In terms of shared social norms, there is a growing discrepancy between the societies and political cultures of the V4. The disappearance of the communist regime’s control over CEE societies has exacerbated several cultural and societal cleavages among the group, what have prevented larger cohesion and shared identity. This has been further undermined with the rise of authoritarianism and conservatism in Poland and Hungary, leading to several commentators speaking about 2 + 2 instead of 4. Differences among the group persist also in terms of their strategic targets. Although generally, all of the group share a certain level of Atlanticism (reliance and support for NATO) in their security doctrines, they widely differ in their threat perceptions regarding the main “Eastern” superpowers Russia and China. Economically, the four countries have acted more like competitors in attracting Japanese investment. Although Japan has been visibly interested in the framework when it was founded in 2003, there is no real push towards further economic integration outside of the existing bilateral and multilateral (EPA/SPA) frameworks.
Thus, the prowess of the V4, and its V4 + Japan framework, grows and falls with the “at the moment” shared interests among their governments. This bears some promise (the cooperation can flourish if the leaders are aligned), but also some perils — which is much more often the case. On the highest political level, the framework has remained largely ceremonial and void of practical political substance. The same can be said about the economic level. Perhaps the most that the V4 (and V4 + Japan too) has achieved, however, is the coordination on the expert level. Be it among the offices at various ministries, or through research and cultural cooperation via the IVF, the V4 (inclusive of V4 + Japan), V4 has helped to coordinate and exchange expert knowledge, share good practices, and tackle challenges.
It is unlikely that the group will dissolve (as that would require radical political action). It is almost certain, however, that the events in Ukraine will diminish the interest of several V4 parties in the framework, especially at its most visible political level. The coordination on the expert level, and through the IVF are then perhaps the most likely venues (and perhaps the most important pillars of the format in terms of substance) of the V4 future.
