## Abstract

## Background
This study aimed to quantify ethnic differences in preferences for maternal health services in rural Yunnan province and provide insights for patient-centered service delivery.

## Methods
A total of 248 women who experienced childbirth between 2017 and 2021 were selected using stratified cluster sampling from 12 villages across three impoverished counties in three prefectures. A Discrete Choice Experiment was conducted, presenting hypothetical scenarios described by several attributes and levels to elicit preferences for maternal health services, including prenatal examination, hospital-based childbirth, and postnatal visits. Conditional Logit regression models were employed to evaluate the relative importance of attributes within the choice sets. Subgroup analyses were performed among the Han majority, Zhiguo ethnic groups, and other ethnic minority populations.

## Results
All participants received prenatal examination and opted for hospital-based childbirth. Approximately 62% of them utilized county-level hospitals, leading to relatively higher medical expenditures. However, only 86% of women received postnatal visits, with the majority accessing these services at hospitals. Compared to others, the Zhiguo ethnic group faced longer travel times to reach hospitals and demonstrated lower awareness of national basic public health services. Overall, women preferred services provided by female doctors at hospitals of county level or above, located within a 30-minute drive. Specifically, the Zhiguo ethnic group preferred free prenatal examination at prefecture/municipal level-hospitals (OR: 5.266, 95% CI: 1.473–18.832), but favored hospital-based maternity waiting room services before childbirth (OR: 2.917, 95% CI: 1.066–7.983). Other ethnic minorities preferred less expensive hospital-based childbirth (OR: 0.999, 95% CI: 0.998–0.999), but accepted hospital-based postnatal visits despite higher costs (OR: 1.902, 95% CI: 1.395–2.592).

## Conclusions
Diverse preferences may contribute to disparities in maternal health service utilization. If primary-level maternal health services fail to meet women’s needs, they may seek care at higher-level facilities, potentially resulting in inadequate or delayed treatment. Consequently, for specific groups such as the Zhiguo ethnic minority groups, enhancing the quality and cultural appropriateness of primary healthcare is a prerequisite for achieving equitable, patient-centered maternal healthcare. In light of these findings, future public health policies must strategically adjust resource allocation.

## Background
Maternal health remains a global priority, with reducing maternal mortality recognized as a critical objective in public health initiatives [ ]. High-quality maternal health services stands out as one of the most critical strategies to achieve this goal [ ]. The United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) have set a target to reduce the global maternal mortality ratio to 70 deaths per 100 000 live births by 2030 [ ]. However, as of 2020, the global maternal mortality ratio remained high at 223 deaths per 100 000 live births [ ], and UN data from 2023 indicate that, between 2016 and 2020, maternal mortality rates in 133 countries either stagnated or declined only marginally [ ]. These findings highlight the urgent need to accelerate progress in maternal health improvement [ ].
In alignment with SDGs’ goal, China has made substantial progress in improving maternal health outcomes over the past three decades. The national maternal mortality ratio declined from 88.8 deaths per 100 000 live births in 1990 to 15.1 deaths per 100 000 live births by 2023 [ ]. Nonetheless, substantial disparities in maternal health services utilization persist across populations and regions, influenced by socioeconomic, cultural, and structural factors, including low literacy, traditional beliefs, inadequate transportation infrastructure, and limited access to medical services in less developed areas [ ]. Maternal mortality remains disproportionately higher in rural areas than urban regions, largely due to differences in socioeconomic development, education, health service quality, and public health awareness [ ]. As a multi-ethnic country, China faces significant discrepancies in maternal and child health outcomes, with ethnic minority populations experiencing lower levels of health service utilization compared to the Han majority [ ].
These disparities highlight the need for targeted and equity-oriented strategies to enhance maternal health services utilization in ethnic minority areas, such as Yunnan Province which located in the underdeveloped southwestern region of China with a wide range of ethnic minority groups. Historically, maternal mortality ratio and the incidence of pregnancy-related complications among ethnic minorities in Yunnan have exceeded the national average [ ]. While recent years have witnessed notable improvements, substantial gaps persist in maternal and child health outcomes in rural regions, as well as the accessibility and utilization of maternal health services [ ]. Previously published research suggest that existing maternal health service might not adequately align with the specific preferences and healthcare-seeking behaviors of local ethnic communities [ ]. Specifically, evidence for service misalignment included the conflict between facility-based childbirth practices and local ethnic traditions in western China, women’s strong preference for female providers despite a scarcity of female professionals, and prevalent concerns over discrimination related to socio-economic and linguistic barriers [ ].
Understanding patient preferences can provide valuable insights into factors influencing service utilization. Preference, an economic concept, involves individuals ranking utility levels and choosing the optimal option among available choices [ ]. Studies on patient preference have gained increasing recognition in evidence-based medicine and health economics, as they help tailor health services to better meet patients’ needs, address utilization barriers, and improve service quality [ ]. By understanding patients’ preferences, it becomes possible to enhance demand-side responsiveness, identify which service attributes deliver the greatest value to patients, and enable the enhancement of economic efficiency by directing resources towards the aspects of care that they value most highly. A robust method for quantifying patient preferences is the Discrete Choice Experiment (DCE), which is grounded in random utility theory. From a health economics perspective, DCE is particularly well-suited to investigating gaps in health service provision and utilisation, as it offers a means of understanding patients’ preferences from the demand-side perspective. Unlike conventional surveys that collect stated importance ratings, or qualitative approaches that explore perceptions without quantifying trade-offs, DCE requires respondents to make choices between hypothetical service alternatives. This design allows researchers to quantify the trade-offs patients make between key service attributes, determine their relative importance, and estimate their marginal willingness to pay. By leveraging these demand-side insights to optimize supply models, the DCE as a patient-centered approach subsequently generates the necessary evidence for efficient and user-centered resource allocation. As a result, DCE has been widely applied as a quantitative approach in health-related research, including maternal health, to assess preferences for service attributes and specific interventions [ – ].
To address these gaps, this study investigated women who experienced pregnancy or childbirth experience between 2017 and 2021 in ethnic minority–concentrated areas of Yunnan Province, China. A DCE was utilized to assess and compare maternal health service preferences across different ethnic groups and identify unmet needs related to accessibility, affordability, provider characteristics, and cultural barriers, and determine adverse factors affecting maternal and child health in local communities. Notably, this study is the first in China to use a DCE to compare maternal health care preferences among Han, Zhiguo, and other ethnic minority women. By quantifying demand-side preferences and the trade-offs, the findings are expected to inform supply-side reforms and support for the design of more responsive, acceptable, and cost-effective maternal health services, ultimately contributing to more equitable and sustainable maternal health outcomes.

## Methods

## Study design
This study employed a cross-sectional design to assess preferences for maternal health services among women who had experienced childbirth. A total of 248 participants were selected using a stratified cluster sampling method from 12 villages across three economically disadvantaged counties in three prefectures of Yunnan Province. A DCE was conducted to elicit preferences for maternal health services, including prenatal examination, hospital-based childbirth, and postnatal visits. Participants were presented with hypothetical service scenarios described by multiple attributes and corresponding levels. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee (2022MEC027).

## Study participants
The target population comprised women who had experienced pregnancy or childbirth between 2017 and 2021. In cases where a participant had multiple pregnancies or births within the five-year period, information was collected for the most recent event. When direct information from a selected woman was unavailable, a family member provided responses on her behalf. Participants were excluded if they had severe hearing impairments, were unconscious, were unable to independently complete the questionnaire, or declined participation. Prior to the survey, the purpose of this research, the general procedures for completing the questionnaire, and the use of anonymous responses were explained. As participants had sufficient educational attainment to complete the questionnaire in writing, written informed consent was obtained from all participants.
This study focused on the Han majority and several ethnic minority groups. Among the ethnic minorities, the Lisu, Lahu, Blang, and Wa, identified as part of the Zhiguo ethnic group, were included. The concept of the Zhiguo ethnic groups, also referred to as the “directly-entering-socialism ethnic” ethnic groups, was introduced by the Chinese Government in the 1950s to describe a subset of ethnic minorities that followed a unique trajectory of social development. At the founding of the People’s Republic of China, these particular ethnic groups were still at an early stage of societal development [ ]. They have historically resided in remote mountainous regions and experienced prolonged social and geographical isolation, leading to distinct cultural and developmental trajectories [ ]. These groups were compared with other ethnic minorities included in the study, such as the Zhuang, Bai, Dai, Miao, Yi, and Yao.

## Sample and sampling procedure
The DCE was conducted using a cross-sectional study design. The minimum sample size required was determined based on the number of choice sets (t), the number of options (a), and the maximum number of levels (c). The formula for calculating the sample size was as follows:
 In this study, the DCE examined three maternal health services, each comprising five choice sets. Each set was formed by two scenarios, with a maximum of three levels defined per attribute. Accordingly, a minimum sample size was determined to be 150 participants (N = 500 × 3 / (5 × 2) = 150).
We employed stratified cluster sampling approach to select the study population. The sampling criteria included: gross domestic product (GDP), per capita GDP, the proportion of ethnic minority populations, county-level poverty status in 2019, and maternal and child health indicators. The selection process proceeded as follows: (1) One prefecture was selected from each of the southeastern, northwestern, and southwestern regions of Yunnan, totaling three prefectures; (2) Within each prefecture, one nationally recognized impoverished county with an ethnic minority population exceeding 40% was chosen, resulting in three counties; (3) Within each county, one township with a high maternal mortality rate and one with a low mortality rate were identified, totaling six townships; (4) Within each township, two villages were randomly selected, resulting in a total of 12 villages; (5) All eligible individuals in each village who met the inclusion criteria were surveyed.

## Questionnaire
The questionnaire comprised two parts: a basic information questionnaire and a DCE questionnaire.

## Basic information questionnaire
The first section collected demographic characteristics and socioeconomic information of participants, as well as their utilization of maternal health services. Variables related to maternal health services utilization included the level of medical institution utilized, travel distance, mode of transportation, out-of-pocket medical expenses, and availability of financial compensation.

## DCE questionnaire
The development of the DCE questionnaire followed a two-stage process: (1) attribute identification and (2) scenario development.
(1) Attribute identification A comprehensive review of existing literature [ – ], previous research conducted by project team [ , ], and two rounds of consultations with experts in health economics, health policy, and maternal and child health were undertaken to inform attributes and levers selection. Based on these sources, six key attributes were identified: level of medical institution, out-of-pocket costs, travel distance, medical staff, mode of service provision, and reimbursement type. The selection of attributes and levels was guided by the “Three Delays” framework [ ] which provided a comprehensive consideration of multiple factors including sociocultural status, perceived benefits/risks of care-seeking, financial affordability, geographic accessibility, institutional capacity, patient satisfaction. With a particular attention to current maternal health policies in China, all attributes and levels were further refined through expert consultation to ensure contextual relevance, policy plausibility, and clarity for survey respondents.
(2) Scenario development An orthogonal design was generated using SPSS 25.0 software to filter an initial pool of 672 potential scenarios and identify a subset that preserved statistical independence among attribute levels and minimized multicollinearity. From this pool, 16 scenarios were selected for each maternal health service, resulting in a total of 48 scenarios. The selection criteria for service scenarios are based on their consistency with existing policies and practical realities. Scenarios were excluded if they featured logically inconsistent attributes, such as those assigning zero out-of-pocket costs to the service provided by prefecture/municipal level-hospitals, or requiring a travel time of over 30-minute driving to the township health center. As a result, the number of scenarios was reduced to 12 for each maternal health service. The final 12 scenarios were divided into two blocks of 6 scenarios each (version A and version B), with all levels of each attribute were represented to maintain statistical efficiency across versions. Each questionnaire included three services, with six scenarios per service (Table  ).
 

## Survey methods
Given that the majority of respondents were ethnic minority women with lower education levels, this study presented the service scenario attributes and levels in pictorial form. A Java-based electronic questionnaire was developed, employing a bubble sort algorithm to first randomize pictorial service scenarios and then conduct iterative pairwise comparisons. This algorithm dynamically presented ranked pairs by adjusting the sequence based on users’ prior selections. For each of the three maternal health services, six distinct scenarios were constructed and systematically paired via the same algorithm to form five forced binary choice sets per service. Each respondent made five binary choices per service, thereby producing ten binary-coded data points (1 = selected, 0 = not selected) per service in the dataset. The DCE attributes and levels were developed with the “Three Delays” framework [ ] and refined through expert consultations to ensure they were context-appropriate and comprehensive. Prior to the formal data collection, a preliminary survey was conducted among ten pregnant women or new mothers from local hospitals to assess the cultural relevance and cognitive feasibility of the questionnaire. No major adjustments were required, as the preliminary participants demonstrated high acceptance and comprehension. During formal data collection, visual aids were also used to present each scenario, allowing respondents distinguish between alternatives and make choices more intuitively. An example of a DCE choice card is illustrated in Fig. and a complete example of the presented DCE choice card is provided in the Supplementary Materials.
 

## Data analysis
The statistical analysis of DCE data was based on the random utility model. Prior to conducting the Conditional Logit analysis, the Hausman test was performed to assess the Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA) assumption for all three services (prenatal examination, hospital-based childbirth, and postnatal visits) using Stata software. The results for all tests indicated that the null hypothesis (H0) was not rejected, confirming the validity of the IIA assumption. Then a Conditional Logit Model was employed to estimate the main effects of service attributes while each respondent completed multiple choice tasks. The Conditional Logit Model was selected due to its computational efficiency and its ability to provide easily interpretable average preference estimates across the entire sample. To address the expected preference heterogeneity across different ethnic groups, subgroup analyses were subsequently conducted. The formula for the Conditional Logit Model is as follows:
 Pi(j) represents the probability of individual i choosing scenario j, and z’ij denotes the attributes of the scenarios that vary with both the individual and the scenario. Attributes and levels were designated as independent variables, while the binary choice (selected = 1, not selected = 0) served as the dependent variable. Data processing and analysis were performed using Excel and Stata 15.0 software. Difference was considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. Conditional Logit regression models were applied to assess the relative importance of attributes within hypothetical choice sets, indicated by Odds Ratio (OR) and 95% Confidence Interval (CI). Internal validity was assessed by examining advantage selection patterns. Model fit was evaluated using log-likelihood and pseudo R². Subgroup analyses were conducted for the Han majority, Zhiguo ethnic group, and other ethnic minorities. The willingness to pay (WTP) was derived from the ratio of the coefficient of a non-monetary attribute to the coefficient of the monetary attribute. The out-of-pocket cost attribute was specified as a continuous variable to facilitate direct WTP estimation. All non-monetary attributes were dummy-coded to allow for meaningful interpretation of level effects.

## Results
A total of 250 questionnaires were distributed. One respondent requested to discontinue participation during the survey, and one respondent, the mother of a seven-year-old child, did not meet the inclusion criteria and was therefore excluded. Ultimately, 248 valid questionnaires were recovered, yielding a response rate of 99.2%. Survey respondents were randomly assigned to complete one of two DCE questionnaire versions (A or B). Key demographic characteristics, such as age and ethnicity, did not differ significantly between the groups, ensuring baseline comparability for the main analyses. Preliminary checks of choice patterns for key attributes, such as level of medical institution and out-of-pocket costs, indicated a high degree of internal consistency among respondents.

## Demographic characteristics of participants
Among the 248 respondents, 188 were women (75.81%), 44 were husbands (17.74%), and 16 were other family members (6.45%). In terms of ethnicity, 45 respondents were Han (18.15%), 89 were from the Zhiguo ethnic group (35.89%), and 114 were from other ethnic minorities (45.97%).
The majority of the sample population were aged 26–34 years (52.02%), predominantly with rural residency status (95.16%), had completed junior high school education (37.09%), and reported a monthly household income exceeding 4,000 CNY (38.71%). Most families had two children (54.84%), and vaginal delivery was the most common mode of childbirth (63.17%). Among the Han respondents, employment was primarily as farmers (26.67%) or unemployed (26.67%). For the Zhiguo ethnic group, primary education was most common (33.71%), with a monthly household income ranging between 1,000 and 1,999 CNY (29.21%). A significant number of other minority groups had three or more children (16.77%).

## Utilization of maternal healthcare service

## Prenatal examination
All respondents reported that women had utilized prenatal examination, with county-level hospitals being the primary venue (62.10%). Most visits involved travel times of 31-60 minutes (32.26%), and half of the women drove themselves (50.81%). The cost for a single check-up was predominantly 100-299 CNY (45.16%), and 50.00% of respondents did not access free services. Compared to the overall sample, the Han majority had a greater proportion of travel times within 30 minutes (55.55%) and a higher compensation utilization (64.44%). The Zhiguo ethnic group had a higher proportion of respondents with travel times exceeding 30 minutes (56.18%), with a greater tendency to use buses for transportation (33.71%) and visit township health centers (24.72%). Additionally, a larger proportion of Zhiguo ethnic group utilized free prenatal examination (24.72%), while 60.67% of them did not access free services, primarily due to limited awareness of the national basic public health services.

## Hospital deliveries
Among the 248 surveyed individuals, seven women were still pregnant, one woman gave birth en route to a hospital. The remaining 240 women delivered in medical institutions. Most deliveries occurred in county-level hospitals (62.08%), with travel times ≥ 60 min reported by 35.00% of respondents, most of whom drove themselves (55.93%). Delivery costs typically exceeded 3,000 CNY (34.58%). The Han majority had a higher proportion of travel times within 30 min (50.00%) compared to the overall sample. In the Zhiguo ethnic group, only 8.24% had travel times within 15 min. Additionally, 30.59% of Zhiguo respondents relied on public transportation. Among other ethnic minorities, most respondents had travel times exceeding 30 min (65.77%).

## Postnatal care
A total of 207 individuals (85.89%) reported the utilization of postnatal visits. The majority of these visits were conducted at hospitals (48.31%), with county-level hospitals being the preferred facility (43.48%). Over half of respondents utilized free services (51.69%), and a similar proportion did not consider receiving compensation (50.72%).
The Han majority primarily received telephone visits (40.00%) and had higher compensation utilization (74.29%). The Zhiguo ethnic group was more likely not to seek compensation for postnatal visits (57.53%). In contrast, other ethnic minorities had a higher proportion of hospital visits (58.59%).

## DCE results
Regarding prenatal examination, the most influential factors were the level of healthcare institution (prefecture- or municipal-level hospitals, OR: 2.927, 95% CI: 1.383–6.196) and the gender of medical staff (female, OR: 2.700, 95% CI: 1.312–5.554). The Zhiguo ethnic group exhibited a stronger preference for free services at designated hospitals. Based on WTP estimation, switching prenatal examination from county-level hospital to township health center would require additional compensation of 422.54 CNY would for the total sample, 1,066.89 CNY for Han majority, 204.66 CNY for Zhiguo ethnic group, and 519.05 CNY for other ethnic minorities (Table  ).
 For hospital delivery, the most influential factors were the level of medical institution and travel distance. Women preferred hospitals within a 30-minute drive (OR: 3.938, 95% CI: 2.132–7.272 ) but were less inclined to choose township health center (OR: 0.068, 95% CI: 0.028–0.166). WTP results indicate that switching childbirth service from county-level hospitals to township health center would require additional compensations of 2,730.10 CNY for Han majority and 3,244.79 CNY for other ethnic minorities. To access delivery facilities within a 30-minute travel distance, the Han majority were willing to pay an extra 1,363.19 CNY, while other ethnic minorities were willing to pay an additional 2,136.78 CNY. The Zhiguo ethnic group exhibited a stronger preference for self-management and hospital-based maternity waiting room services before childbirth (Table  ).
 For postnatal visits, the most influential attributes were gender of medical staff (female, OR: 2.300, 95% CI: 1.933–2.735), service mode (hospital mode, OR: 1.712, 95% CI: 1.392–2.106), and management mode (self-management mode, OR: 1.651, 95% CI: 1.304–2.089). Women generally preferred self-management models, while seeking female medical staff for postnatal visits at hospitals. Compared to the overall sample, the Han majority showed a stronger preference for home visits, while the Zhiguo ethnic group and other ethnic minorities exhibited a slightly higher preference for out-of-pocket costs.WTP estimates revealed a premium for female medical staff: 88.79 CNY for the total sample, with 53.35 CNY for the Zhiguo ethnic group and 66.94 CNY for other ethnic minorities. When switching from home visits to hospital visits, the total sample indicated a willingness to pay an additional 57.34 CNY, with the Zhiguo ethnic group willing to pay an additional 41.76 CNY and other ethnic minorities willing to pay an additional 51.18 CNY (Table  ).
 

## Discussion
This study provides significant insights into maternal health service preferences among various ethnic groups in Yunnan Province, China, with a focus on key determinants such as service costs, accessibility, and healthcare provider characteristics. Pseudo R² and log-likelihood values for all three DCE modules indicated adequate model fit, and the low proportion of dominant choices further corroborates analytical robustness of the analyses.
The respondents’ preference for low-cost health services in this study is consistent with previous studies conducted by Beam NK in Ethiopia and Pitchforth E in the UK [ , ]. This inclination may be attributed to the relatively underdeveloped social infrastructure and limited economic status among the Zhiguo ethnic group in Yunnan Province. Respondents also demonstrated a preference for health facilities within a 30-minute travel distance, a pattern aligning with findings by Pitchforth E and Lambooij M Son on facility-based delivery in the UK and the Netherlands [ – ]. In Yunnan Province, the rugged terrain and challenging travel conditions likely reinforce this preference.
Participants exhibited a strong preference for female doctors during prenatal examination and postnatal visits but were less concerned about the gender of doctors during childbirth. This contrasts with Mahumud R A’s research in Bangladesh, which reported a preference for female doctors during hospital-based childbirth [ ]. These findings suggest that during the highly acute and urgent phase of childbirth, participants prioritize service quality and technical proficiency over the gender of the healthcare provider.
Most respondents favored hospitals at the prefecture- or municipal- level hospitals, consistent with preferences observed among populations in other poor and ethnic minority regions of Western China [ , ].This likely reflects greater trust in the medical technology and expertise available at higher-level facilities [ ].
The study also identifies discrepancies between the preferred healthcare services and actual utilization patterns, pointing to potential barriers that could affect the sustainability of maternal and child health initiatives.

## Prenatal examination
Respondents preferred higher-level hospitals for prenatal examination and were willing to pay an additional 349.56 CNY to move from a county-level to a prefecture-level hospital. Despite this, most opted for county-level hospitals. Although township health centers provide free prenatal care, women perceived county-level hospitals as offering higher-quality services. This preference increases both medical and transportation costs, which can limit service utilization. Previous studies indicate that women in remote mountainous areas of Yunnan spend an average of one hour travelling to reach high-level hospitals [ ], highlighting how travel time acts as a critical barrier to accessing preferred care [ ]. Consequently, it is evident that, despite preferences for higher-level care, physical accessibility remains a critical barrier to achieving optimal maternal health outcomes. Strengthening the quality of care at primary healthcare facilities could ensure the adequate and timely prenatal examinations and reduce the additional monetary and time costs.
Han respondents, who were relatively characterized by higher socioeconomic status, showed a preference for female doctors, possibly due to greater demand for quality of care and concerns about privacy. They were willing to pay an additional 1,108.86 CNY to choose female doctor. In contrast, the Zhiguo ethnic group, characterized by lower levels of education and reliance on public transportation [ ], expressed a strong preference for free medical services. This suggests that socioeconomic factors, including education and transportation access, significantly influence health service preferences.

## Hospital delivery
Women generally preferred hospitals within a 30-minute travel time for childbirth, willing to pay an additional 2,519.26 CNY for this convenience. However, actual travel times often exceeded this threshold, consistent with findings from other rural and ethnic minority populations [ , , ]. Providing maternity waiting room service for expectant mothers in extremely remote areas before childbirth might accommodate their preference. Moreover, most Zhiguo respondents incurred costs exceeding 3,000 CNY for childbirth, reflecting their preference for higher-level hospitals. This is exacerbated by the decreasing reimbursement rates from health insurance as the level of healthcare facilities increases, leading to higher out-of-pocket expenses for families. When delivering at township health centers rather than county-level hospitals, ethnic minority groups required greater additional economic compensation than the Han population. This reflects the aspiration for high-quality healthcare and highlights the limited diagnostic and treatment capabilities of primary care facilities in their regions.

## Postnatal visits
For postnatal visits, Zhiguo respondents preferred hospital visits over home visits. This contrasts with other domestic studies that reported a higher demand for home visits in postpartum care [ , ]. This preference could be attributed to logistical challenges faced by primary care providers, including lack of trust or limited access [ , ], and from cultural taboos against postpartum visits from outsiders in some ethnic minority communities [ ]. Willingness to pay extra for hospital visits was relatively consistent across ethnic groups, suggesting that cost is not a major barrier and that adaptations to postnatal service models are broadly acceptable.
Discrepancies were noted between participants’ preferences and current policies in China. Chinese policies encourage hospital-based deliveries at the prefecture/municipal level [ ], which aligns with the respondents’ preferences. However, preferences for prenatal care differ from policy recommendations that promote free services at primary healthcare facilities. Participants expressed concerns about the limited equipment and treatment capability at these facilities [ ]. In light of these challenges, the study suggests that strengthening grassroots service capacity through medical consortia, physician rotations, and multi-site practices could improve service delivery and increase community acceptance. Enhancing health literacy is also critical to increase understanding of the value of primary healthcare services.

## Strengths and limitations
This study provides valuable insights into maternal health service preferences in Yunnan’s ethnic minority populations but has several limitations. First, only 11 ethnic minority groups were included, which may not fully capture the diversity within the province. Two women of Myanmar nationality were included but not analyzed separately due to the small sample size, despite their potentially unique challenges. Future research should broaden the study population. Furthermore, the survey focused primarily on institutional attributes, while respondents emphasized on the attitudes of medical staff. Incorporating this factor in future DCE studies could better reflect patient priorities. Third, as a cross-sectional study, recall bias may have affected participants’ reporting of past prenatal, delivery, or postnatal experiences. Finally, inherent assumption bias of DCE may limit the extent to which stated preferences reflect actual behavior. To address this, the survey first collected data on actual service utilization, allowing comparisons between stated and revealed preferences, which were incorporated into the discussion.

## Conclusions
Although the overall utilization rates of prenatal care and hospital-based delivery are relatively high, this does not imply that current services fully align with maternal preferences. Significant disparities remain between stated preferences and actual utilization. If local primary healthcare facilities fail to adequately address the specific needs of ethnic minority women, such as providing culturally competent staff and accessible transportation, these women may bypass local primary healthcare facilities. This often leads to care-seeking at higher-level, more distant, and costlier facilities, potentially resulting in delayed care, increased financial burdens, and suboptimal service utilization.
Consequently, for groups such as the Zhiguo ethnic minority, enhancing the quality and cultural appropriateness of primary healthcare is a prerequisite for achieving equitable, patient-centered maternal healthcare. In light of these findings, future public health policies must strategically adjust resource allocation. Priority should be given to service attributes with high economic valuation, including improved accessibility and culturally specific services, to maximize social welfare and resource efficiency. Specifically, policy efforts should focus on optimizing supply-side efficiency by strengthening the tiered diagnosis and treatment system, improving incentives for grassroots staff, and promoting culturally and linguistically appropriate service models. Ultimately, we call for future health economics research to quantitatively evaluate the welfare gains associated with bridging the gap between preference and supply, thereby ensuring the sustainable and equitable development of maternal health services.
