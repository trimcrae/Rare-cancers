## Background
Female sex workers (FSWs) in Nigeria carry a disproportionate Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) burden and remain a key target group for pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP). Understanding PrEP acceptability and willingness to use is critical to scaling prevention in this population. This study examined factors associated with PrEP acceptability and willingness to use among PrEP-naïve FSWs in Lagos State, Nigeria.

## Methods
A cross-sectional study was conducted between June and August 2022 among 310 FSWs recruited from 24 hotspots across four local government areas in Lagos State using a multistage sampling design. Data were collected with a semi-structured quantitative questionnaire. Analyses were restricted to the 223 participants who had never used PrEP. Bivariate analyses using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and Kruskal–Wallis test were conducted to identify factors associated with PrEP acceptability and willingness scores. Non-standardised coefficients (b) are reported.

## Results
Participants had a mean age of 26.19±4.11, and most (90%) had completed secondary education. In the final multivariable model for PrEP acceptability (adjusted R² = 0.264, p < 0.001), higher PrEP knowledge (b = 0.102, p = 0.006), Islamic religion (b = 1.937, p = 0.002), non-majority ethnic group (Others: b = 1.872, p = 0.003), and higher education (b = 2.455, p = 0.009) were independently associated with greater acceptability. For willingness to use PrEP (adjusted R² = 0.228, p < 0.001), higher PrEP knowledge (b = 0.12, p = 0.019) was associated with increased willingness, while later entry into sex work (b = -0.45, p < 0.001) was associated with reduced willingness. There was a marginal trend toward reduced willingness with increasing age (b = −0.15, p = 0.055).

## Conclusion
Among PrEP-naïve FSWs in Lagos, PrEP knowledge emerged as a key correlate of both PrEP acceptability and willingness, while education, religion, and ethnicity were primarily associated with cognitive acceptability, and age-related factors influenced willingness. Interventions that improve PrEP knowledge and incorporating age-, literacy-, and sex-work–entry considerations may strengthen both PrEP acceptability and willingness among FSWs.

## Introduction
Nigeria bears the world’s fourth-largest Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) epidemic, with 1.9 million people living with HIV and an adult prevalence of 1.4%, disproportionately affecting women (1.9% vs. 0.9% in men) [ , ]. Although national prevalence has declined from 5.8% in 2001, a concentrated epidemic persists among key populations—particularly female sex workers (FSWs), their clients, and partners—who account for approximately 20% of new infections [ ]. The 2020–2021 Integrated Biological and Behavioural Surveillance Survey (IBBSS) reported an HIV prevalence of 15.5% among FSWs across 12 Nigerian states and 13% among FSWs in Lagos [ ].
Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is a cornerstone biomedical intervention in HIV prevention, offering user-controlled protection independent of partner cooperation [ ]. Female sex workers are prioritized for PrEP due to their heightened vulnerability arising from frequent unprotected sexual encounters, stigma, and discrimination [ , ]. Reflecting this risk, Nigeria’s 2017–2021 National HIV Strategic Framework identifies PrEP delivery to FSWs as a key prevention strategy [ ].
Despite this policy prioritization, evidence among FSWs in Nigeria suggests important gaps between awareness of PrEP and the readiness to use it. Existing literature highlights significant variability in PrEP awareness among female sex workers (FSWs). For instance, the IBBSS reported a PrEP awareness level of 24.6% and a lifetime use of only 5% among FSWs in Nigeria [ ]. While some studies report high awareness and generally favourable attitudes, substantial proportions of FSWs have limited understanding of eligibility, alongside persistent barriers such as stigma, cost, and misconceptions [ – ]. Notably, a study in South-West Nigeria (including Lagos) reported high PrEP uptake (88.6) among FSWs [ ]. These contrasting findings underscore the need to examine the attitudinal and motivational precursors to PrEP initiation—specifically acceptability and willingness—rather than focusing on uptake alone.
Understanding PrEP acceptability and willingness among FSWs is essential for designing effective and equitable HIV prevention interventions. Acceptability encompasses the perceived appropriateness and desirability of PrEP, primarily influenced by individual beliefs, cultural norms, and perceived clinical benefits [ ]. In contrast, willingness reflects a situational readiness or affective receptivity to use PrEP when available, often shaped by structural and practical factors such as accessibility, affordability, and perceived personal risk [ ]. These constructs, while related, are conceptually distinct: an individual may find PrEP acceptable in principle (cognitive approval) yet remain unwilling to use it due to immediate barriers such as stigma or cost; conversely, others may express willingness only if such barriers are systematically addressed.
This study examines factors influencing the acceptability and willingness to use PrEP among FSWs in Lagos State, Nigeria, to address the evidence gap and inform locally and regionally relevant PrEP scale-up strategies.

## Methods

## Study setting
Lagos State, located in southwestern Nigeria, comprises 20 Local Government Areas (LGAs) [ ]. It is a major commercial and transit hub, creating a dynamic environment for FSW operations [ ]. Hotspots—venues such as hotels, bars, clubs, and streets where FSWs meet clients—are fluid due to police raids and mobility patterns [ ]. Most FSWs in Lagos work in hotels and bars, with only about 6% operating in public spaces [ , ]. Previous mapping surveys estimate approximately 2,974 FSW hotspots in Lagos, with a large percentage of FSWs (over 90%) working in and around hotels and bars [ ].

## Study design
This cross-sectional study was conducted among FSWs in Lagos State, Nigeria, between June and August 2022. Data were collected using a semi-structured questionnaire to assess PrEP acceptability and willingness to use.

## Study population
The study population comprised FSWs in Lagos State aged 18 years and above who engaged in consensual transactional sex for monetary or other material benefits and were willing to participate. Participants were excluded if they were unable to provide informed consent due to acute illness or intoxication.

## Sample size determination
The sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula, assuming a population greater than 10,000 and an estimated PrEP use proportion of 0.1754 [ ]. At a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error, the minimum sample size was 222. Adjusting for a 10% non-response rate, this increased the required sample to 246. The final sample size was increased to 310 to enhance statistical power and generalizability.

## Sampling technique
A multistage sampling method was employed:
First, four LGAs—Ojo, Alimosho, Agege, and Ikorodu—were selected from a sampling frame of 19 LGAs in Lagos State using simple random sampling (balloting technique) [ ].
Second, field visits were conducted in the selected LGAs to identify FSW venues, noting congregation days and times, and estimating FSW numbers. A venue-daytime (VDT) sampling frame was then created, excluding venues with low FSW populations [ ]. A subsequent feasibility survey assessed venue owner permission, interviewer safety (especially during evening operations), and venue layout to ensure interview confidentiality. Only venues meeting these criteria were included in the final sampling frame.
Third, six hotspots were randomly selected from each LGA using the balloting technique, resulting in a total of 24 hotspots.
Finally, at selected venues, research assistants approached potential respondents, explaining the study objectives, confidentiality, and voluntary participation. Most respondents provided written informed consent. Surveys were conducted in private areas provided by venue owners; some participants scheduled appointments for the following morning. Recruitment continued through both on-site engagement and appointments until the target sample size of 310 was achieved.

## Data collection procedure
The questionnaire used in this study was developed specifically for this research, drawing upon validated tools and theoretical constructs from previously published studies on PrEP acceptability, and willingness among key populations, particularly FSWs and other high-risk groups [ – ]. Relevant items were adapted and contextualized to reflect the sociocultural setting of Lagos, Nigeria. The final instrument was pretested to assess clarity, appropriateness, and internal consistency. An English-language version of the questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 1.

## Research assistants and pretesting
Three female research assistants, each holding a Bachelor of Science degree and possessing prior experience with research among FSWs through a non-governmental organization, were recruited and trained. Training emphasized eligibility screening, informed consent administration, and adherence to ethical standards including confidentiality and non-coercive recruitment and right to withdraw from the study.
The questionnaire was pretested in Kosofe Local Government Area, which was purposively excluded from the main study sample. Thirty respondents (representing 10% of the final sample size) completed the pretest to identify issues related to question clarity, comprehension, and administration flow.

## Quantitative data collection and scoring

## Variable description and measurement

## Outcome variables
Two related continuous variables were used as primary outcomes in separate multivariable regression models.

## PrEP acceptability
Acceptability reflected respondents’ overall attitude toward PrEP. Six positively worded items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), including perceptions of PrEP safety, effectiveness, dosing convenience, and formulation. Total scores ranged from 6 to 30, with higher scores indicating greater acceptability. The scale demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.84) [ ].

## Willingness to use PrEP
Willingness represented participants’ stated readiness to initiate PrEP if available. Eight items assessed enabling and barrier factors, rated from 1 (definitely will not) to 5 (definitely will). Negatively worded items were reverse scored. Total scores ranged from 8 to 40, with higher scores indicating stronger willingness. Internal consistency was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.65), consistent with exploratory behavioural scales [ , ].

## Independent variables

## Sociodemographic and Knowledge Variables
Sociodemographic information was collected to contextualize PrEP acceptability and willingness and to identify potential sources of variation. Age in years was retained as a continuous variable, reflecting the gradual, linear change in PrEP attitudes suggested by existing behavioural health literature [ ]. Ethnicity was categorised as Yoruba, Hausa, Igbo, or Other to reflect cultural diversity in health-seeking behaviour [ , ]. The Other category comprised participants from minority ethnic groups outside the three major Nigerian ethnicities [ ]. Lagos is a highly cosmopolitan urban centre and a major destination for internal migration from across Nigeria, resulting in substantial representation of diverse minority ethnic groups within this category. Religion was grouped into Christianity/Other and Islam to ensure sufficient sample sizes. Education was dichotomized into Primary/None versus Secondary/Higher, reflecting literacy differences relevant to PrEP comprehension and uptake. Regular Paying Job (Yes/No) captured financial stability beyond sex work, hypothesised to influence agency and preventive health decisions. Number of Clients per Month was retained as a continuous variable, representing sexual risk exposure and potential motivation for PrEP use. Offered PrEP (Yes/No) indicated whether participants had previously been offered PrEP, reflecting exposure to PrEP messaging and services.

## Statistical analysis
All analyses used R (version 4.4.0) with packages tidyverse, broom, car, and flextable for data handling, modeling, and output. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed) for final multivariable models; however, bivariate predictors with p < 0.20 were retained to avoid excluding potentially relevant variables.

## Descriptive statistics
Participant characteristics and variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages (%) for categorical data (e.g., religion, ethnicity, education) and means ± Standard Deviation (SD) for normally distributed continuous variables (e.g., age) or medians with Interquartile Range (IQR) for skewed ones (e.g., age started sex work).

## Analytic sample and exclusions
From 310 recruited FSWs, we excluded 87 ever-users of PrEP to focus on non-users for the primary outcomes (acceptability and willingness), yielding a final analytic sample of 223. This ensured outcomes reflected potential uptake intent rather than prior behavior, avoiding conflation in modeling.

## Data preparation and transformations
Data distribution for all continuous variables was assessed using histograms, Q–Q plots, and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Several key predictors—notably age at sex-work initiation and PrEP knowledge scores—demonstrated significant departures from normality (W < 0.95, p < 0.001). While logarithmic and square-root transformations were attempted, they did not meaningfully improve residual normality or enhance the interpretability of the results. Consequently, these variables were retained in their raw continuous form within the multivariable linear regression models to preserve statistical power and avoid the loss of information inherent in categorisation. Although statistical tests identified some deviations from ideal Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) assumptions, residual diagnostics were considered acceptable for reliable inference given the moderate sample size (n = 223) and the robustness of the linear model under the Central Limit Theorem [ ]. For descriptive statistics and preliminary bivariate analyses only, continuous predictors were grouped into quartiles (age at start of sex work) or tertiles (knowledge) to facilitate visual interpretation.

## Bivariate analyses
Associations were tested using Spearman’s correlations for continuous predictors and Wilcoxon rank-sum or Kruskal-Wallis tests for categorical ones; candidates with p < 0.20 advanced to multivariable modeling to avoid excluding potentially relevant variables.

## Multivariable modeling rationale and approach
PrEP acceptability and willingness scores, derived from composite Likert scales, were modeled as continuous outcomes, consistent with common psychometric practice given their approximate interval properties and the moderate-to-large sample size [ ]. The analysis proceeded in two stages. First, unadjusted associations between each predictor and the outcome were estimated using simple linear regression to illustrate the crude magnitude and direction of each effect. Second, adjusted multivariable models were constructed. Prior to fitting these models, potential multicollinearity was evaluated using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF), and categorical predictors with sparse counts (< 5 observations per category) were excluded to preserve model stability and precision. Predictors demonstrating insufficient bivariate association (p ≥ 0.20) were excluded to reduce overfitting and improve model parsimony, while retaining variables with potential confounding relevance.
Residual diagnostics indicated that while the assumption of constant variance (homoscedasticity) was met in one model (acceptability scores, Breusch–Pagan test p = 0.61), Willingness score models exhibited statistically significant heteroscedasticity (willingness scores, Breusch–Pagan test p < 0.001). Furthermore, all models showed statistically significant deviations from normality (Shapiro–Wilk test, p < 0.05); therefore, all final models were estimated using Heteroscedasticity-Consistent Standard Errors (Type 3) (HC3) to ensure reliable inference by providing robust standard error estimates that account for both heteroscedasticity and potential non-normality [ ]. Beta coefficients, 95% confidence intervals, and p-values are reported for all predictors, and full diagnostic results are provided in the appendix.

## Ethical considerations
Approval was granted by the Lagos University Teaching Hospital Health Research Ethics Committee (HREC No: ADM/DCST/HREC/APP/5093), adhering to the Declaration of Helsinki. Trained female research assistants obtained written informed consent after briefing participants on objectives, voluntary withdrawal, and benefits for HIV prevention evidence. Confidentiality used serial IDs; data were securely stored with restricted access and destroyed post-study.

## Discussion
The primary objective of this study was to identify the independent factors associated with PrEP acceptability and willingness among FSWs in Lagos, Nigeria. Analyses were restricted to 223 PrEP non-users to focus on the precursors to uptake. Two separate multivariable linear regression models examined independent correlates of acceptability and willingness.
The independent association between PrEP knowledge and both acceptability and willingness represents a central finding of this study. After adjusting for sociodemographic and behavioural covariates, higher PrEP knowledge scores were significantly associated with both greater acceptability and stronger willingness. This association supports the Health Belief Model (HBM), in which health literacy acts as a foundational antecedent to perceived benefits and self-efficacy—both critical drivers of preventive health behaviour [ , ]. However, because willingness reflects an affective, situational openness rather than a planned intention, this relationship is also well-situated within the Prototype Willingness Model (PWM) [ ]. From this perspective, greater knowledge may expand an individual’s situational openness to PrEP, potentially bridging the gap between cognitive acceptability and the reactive readiness to embrace PrEP use in high-risk contexts.
The data suggest that targeted interventions aimed at building comprehensive PrEP understanding—beyond superficial awareness—will likely yield substantial returns in moving FSWs from passive awareness to situational receptivity. This finding is consistent with evidence from southeastern Nigeria, where a greater desire to learn about HIV was associated with higher PrEP willingness, and Ghana, where FSWs who had heard of PrEP had more than twice the odds of being willing to use it [ , ]. However, research in Uyo, Nigeria, documented a critical gap: although 76.5% of FSWs were aware of PrEP, only 8.1% expressed willingness to use it [ ]. This underscores that awareness alone is insufficient to foster affective openness. The conceptual distinction between cognitive attitude (acceptability) and reactive receptivity (willingness) suggests that intervention strategies should be tailored to address these distinct drivers of behaviour.
Secondary or higher education emerged as a strong independent correlate of greater PrEP acceptability. This pattern echoes findings from other Nigerian studies, where formal education consistently facilitates engagement with biomedical HIV prevention. For instance, Olugbenga-Bello et al. found that educated FSWs in southwestern Nigeria were more likely to report positive engagement with PrEP services [ ]. Education may foster health literacy, sharpen the ability to evaluate medical evidence, and build confidence in interacting with health services—each of which helps women perceive PrEP as a credible and personally relevant tool [ , ]. Both religion and education may influence key HBM constructs by reducing perceived barriers—such as moral objections or mistrust of biomedical interventions—and amplifying cues to action through community networks and educational campaigns. Within this framework, education acts as a cognitive stabilizer that moves PrEP from a foreign concept to a socially and personally acceptable option.
Notably, about 90% of participants in our study had completed secondary schooling, signalling a population structurally primed for PrEP scale-up provided supply-side constraints are addressed. Yet, the fact that education predicted acceptability but not willingness highlights a critical distinction: while formal education may facilitate the cognitive endorsement of PrEP in theory, it does not necessarily translate to situational receptivity. This suggests that even informed women may perceive PrEP as a credible tool (acceptability) while confronting practical or social hurdles—such as cost, stigma, or partner dynamics—that dampen their immediate openness to adopt the intervention [ , ]. This divergence reinforces the necessity of distinguishing between cognitive attitudes and affective readiness in HIV prevention research.
Islamic affiliation was independently associated with higher PrEP acceptability but not with willingness, suggesting that religious identity may influence the cognitive endorsement of PrEP without necessarily increasing affective receptivity. This finding contrasts with a study in Ghana, where Muslim FSWs reported higher willingness than their Christian counterparts, highlighting that local sociopolitical contexts likely modify the relationship between religion and PrEP outcomes [ ]. These results indicate that faith-based considerations could be influential in shaping the theoretical credibility of PrEP. However, further research is required to understand how religious affiliation interacts with structural and social barriers to influence the transition from cognitive approval to situational readiness.
Non-majority ethnic FSWs—those identifying outside the Yoruba, Igbo, or Hausa groups—reported significantly higher PrEP acceptability, though ethnicity was not associated with willingness. In the Yoruba-dominant context of Lagos, these women may experience a ‘double exclusion’ as both sex workers and ethnic outsiders. This marginalization may plausibly increase their cognitive appraisal of PrEP as a necessary tool, potentially due to a heightened perception of personal risk or reduced social surveillance from traditional family structures. However, this increased acceptability did not translate into greater willingness. This divergence likely reflects the practical barriers—such as cost, geographic distance to friendly services, or navigating unfamiliar health systems—that dampen affective readiness despite high theoretical endorsement [ ]. Consequently, outreach programs must move beyond building acceptability to addressing the specific structural hurdles that prevent these marginalized subgroups from reaching a state of readiness to use PrEP.
Willingness to use PrEP was significantly associated with age at entry into sex work, while current age showed a marginal trend toward lower willingness (p = 0.055). Neither variable influenced acceptability. These associations align with regional evidence: Olugbenga-Bello et al. reported that Nigerian FSWs who began sex work after age 18 were significantly less likely to engage with PrEP, and Guure et al. observed that Ghanaian FSWs entering between ages 25 and 34 were 16% less willing than those under 25 [ , ]. Younger entrants and those with earlier initiation appear to demonstrate greater situational receptivity toward biomedical innovation. From an HBM perspective, age and sex work experience likely modify perceived susceptibility to HIV; younger or more recent entrants may perceive themselves as more vulnerable, heightening their openness to new preventive tools. Conversely, older FSWs or late entrants may rely on established risk-management routines, which may diminish their perceived need for PrEP, particularly when weighed against structural barriers like clinic wait times. These findings suggest that while older and later entrants find PrEP as acceptable as their younger counterparts, their readiness to adopt the tool is markedly lower, necessitating a cautious interpretation of age-based trends in this cohort.

## Limitations
This study’s cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the direction of associations between the examined covariates and PrEP acceptability and willingness. Recruitment at hotspots likely excluded brothel-based or more hidden FSWs, who may exhibit different openness to PrEP due to isolation or distinct risk profiles. Additionally, self-reported data on sensitive topics are susceptible to social desirability bias. While we utilized robust estimation (HC3) to account for data characteristics such as non-normality and heteroscedasticity, certain unmeasured factors—including HIV status, client pressure for condomless sex, or police harassment—could act as confounders. Finally, findings from urban, Yoruba-dominant Lagos may not generalize to rural or northern Nigeria, where different socio-cultural norms and health infrastructures prevail.

## Conclusion
Among PrEP-naïve FSWs in Lagos, higher knowledge, education, Islamic affiliation, and non-majority ethnicity emerged as significant independent correlates of greater PrEP acceptability, while younger age and earlier sex work entry were associated with higher willingness. These findings highlight that while many sociodemographic factors relate to the cognitive foundation for PrEP approval, age-related experience and comprehensive knowledge are the primary factors linked to situational readiness. Interventions should move beyond superficial awareness to foster deep health literacy and accessible services tailored to the distinct needs of FSWs across different age cohorts, thereby bridging the gap between theoretical acceptability and the affective willingness to use PrEP.
