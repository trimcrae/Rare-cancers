## Background
Saskatchewan, Canada, has one of the highest prevalence rates for multiple sclerosis (MS) in the world, and as treatment options evolve, individuals living with MS (ILMS) are living longer. Medical assistance in dying (MAID) has been a legal end-of-life (EOL) care option in Canada since 2016, and some individuals living with MS are choosing this option as they age and/or their disease progresses. Given the complexity of MS, including the risk of fluctuating capacity in end-of-life decision-making, ongoing communication about EOL options is essential. However, sparse information is available regarding individuals’ perspectives on communication when discussing end-of-life care, particularly when MAID is a legally available option. This project addressed this gap.

## Methods
A qualitative research project using the Interpretive Description methodology was completed. The data set included twenty-seven interviews with individuals living with MS and healthcare professionals, participant contextual and demographic data (including the Multiple Sclerosis Impact Scale-29 and the Patient-Determined Disease Steps), interviewer field notes, and reflective content. Descriptive statistical analysis and inductive reflexive thematic analysis were completed.

## Results
Participants identified the importance of (1) Forging trust, (2) Framing information, (3) Facilitating conversation, (4) Balancing timing, and (5) Navigating misinformation when discussing end-of-life care when MAID is a legally available option. Participants emphasized the need for a trusted relationship with their healthcare professionals to discuss EOL options without hesitation and fear. They also highlighted that end-of-life care information should be inclusive of all options and made available in various formats. They acknowledged that these conversations are complex and sometimes avoided, and how, after overcoming their fear and hesitancy, they felt gratitude or relief in being able to talk about end-of-life issues. Participants also shared that the most effective decision-making time occurs during periods of illness stability. Participants identified a need for reliable resources to address misinformation and facilitate informed decision-making.

## Conclusions
The findings support end-of-life care planning throughout the MS care trajectory. Through relationships, access to accurate and inclusive information on EOL and conversations during periods of relative illness stability, an informed and person-centred decision-making process can be facilitated.

## Introduction
Medical Assistance in Dying (MAID) has been legally available in Canada since 2016, following the passage of Bill C-14, and was further expanded in 2021 through Bill C-7 [ ]. The revised legislation introduced two eligibility streams: Track 1 applies to individuals whose natural death is reasonably foreseeable, while Track 2 applies to those with serious and incurable conditions whose death is not reasonably foreseeable [ ]. Track 2 requires additional safeguards, including a 90-day assessment period and consultation with a clinician with expertise in the condition causing the suffering [ ]. The intersection of multiple sclerosis (MS) and MAID remains under-researched, and there is limited literature on how individuals living with MS (ILMS) understand, access, and/or engage in end-of-life decision-making with MAID as a legally available end-of-life care option [ , ]. Due to the complex and varied clinical presentations and care needs of individuals living with MS, addressing this gap is particularly important to support informed and person-centred care.
MS is a chronic, progressive neurological disease with a high prevalence in Canada, particularly in the province of Saskatchewan, which reports some of the highest prevalence rates in the world [ ]. While 60% of Saskatchewan’s population resides in urban centers, approximately 40% live in rural areas, where access to specialized MS care is often limited, contributing to inequities in disease management and support [ ]. As treatment options evolve, more individuals are living longer with MS, often into older adulthood [ , ]. This demographic shift presents new challenges in care, especially for older adults living with MS who may experience increased disability, comorbidities, and unique psychosocial needs. Geographic barriers contribute to significant disparities in care, particularly for older adults with MS who require coordinated, multidisciplinary support outside of specialized urban-based care centers [ ]. In addition to geographic barriers, systematic barriers include waiting times to access MS specialists, rehabilitation care, and mental health services, as well as limited access to mobility aids [ , ]. 
Given the complexity of MS, including unpredictable disease progression and risk of fluctuating capacity in decision-making, nuanced and ongoing communication about end-of-life options is essential [ ]. Yet, little is known about the communication preferences, interactional dynamics, and decision-making experiences of ILMS in this context. Understanding these perspectives is crucial for aligning policy and practice with patient needs [ ]. This paper focuses on the second objective of a broader research project examining the intersection of MAID and end-of-life (EOL) care among ILMS. Specifically, this paper will report on the communication perspectives of ILMS when discussing end-of-life care, particularly when MAID is a legally available option.

## Methodology
We used interpretive description (ID) methodology in this qualitative exploratory study. ID is a constructivist methodology for exploring complex clinical experiences through inductive analysis [ , ]. ID seeks to uncover meaning through the collaborative generation of knowledge between researchers and participants, focusing on the lived experiences of those directly affected [ , ]. The methodology is especially well-suited to addressing multifaceted research questions and generating findings that are descriptive, practical, and applicable to real-life healthcare settings [ ]. It moves beyond surface-level reporting by interpreting data in ways that can inform and enhance clinical decision-making and patient care [ , ].
Our team included an individual living with MS and their spouse (research patient-partners). They actively contributed to grant writing, recruitment, data analysis, and knowledge sharing. The individual living with MS was diagnosed in 2005, and they live in rural Saskatchewan. Other team members included a physiatrist, a nurse researcher, an aging studies student, a MAID provider, and two MS specialists. Ethical approval was granted by the University of Regina’s Behavioural Research Ethics Board (REB #029).

## Ethical considerations
The research assistant (RA) participated in a standardized program (safeTALK) to train individuals with the skills to identify signs of psychological distress and potential suicidal ideation. The study’s informed consent process permitted the RA to contact the Saskatchewan HealthLine if they identified concerns of acute suicidality during the interview. The HealthLine is a 24-hour, province-wide mental health support service that ensures participants receive immediate and appropriate care, including coordinating in-person assistance if necessary. The study protocol includes discontinuing the interview and excluding any data collected from analysis if an individual would have been referred to care through the HealthLine. Additionally, all participants were provided written information via email on mental health resources following their interview, and the RA conducted a follow-up check-in with each participant within one week of their interview session.

## Sample recruitment
The inclusion criteria for ILMS were Saskatchewan residents aged 18 or older who had been diagnosed with MS by a neurologist using the McDonald criteria [ ] at least one year before potential participation. The exclusion criteria for ILMS were individuals with communication barriers that could not be resolved through technology or a communication support person. The inclusion criteria for key informants included Saskatchewan professionals involved in the care of individuals living with ILMS and the support partners of those receiving MAID-related care.
Participants were selected through purposive sampling in collaboration with the Saskatchewan Multiple Sclerosis Clinical Research Chair and their research associate, who facilitated access to a research database of individuals who had consented to be contacted for future research. We randomly selected potential participants from the research database within the following parameters: (1) around 30% of the potential participants had an Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) score of 8 or higher, (2) at least 30% of potential participants had been diagnosed within the last five years, (3) no more than 75% of potential participants identified as female, and (4) around 33% of potential participants lived outside the two major urban centers. Additionally, potential participants were invited through clinic posters, via MS Canada, which shared project information online, and through social media outreach. Upon connecting with the research team, interested individuals were provided with complete project details. Key informants were recruited through direct referrals and existing partnerships with the Saskatchewan Health Authority and patient-partners.

## Data production
If potential participants remained interested after reviewing the project information and consent form, the research team scheduled an interview at a time and in a modality that were comfortable for the participant. The participants could choose either an in-person, phone or Zoom interview. Flexibility in modality often promotes individual comfort, especially when discussing sensitive topics [ ], such as MAID and end-of-life care. To enhance the trustworthiness of the findings and support triangulation, the interviewer recorded field notes and reflexive memos throughout the data collection process. NVivo 12 software was employed for data management, coding, and thematic analysis. All coding was conducted manually by the research team, without the use of automated coding or features, ensuring full researcher engagement in developing codes and themes.
The interview guide was prepared and refined through the active involvement of patient-partners, ensuring its relevance and clarity. Before commencing each interview, the interviewer reviewed the consent form with the participant, obtained and documented verbal informed consent on the audio recording, and provided access to a brief Qualtrics survey. This survey, which again confirmed consent, collected demographic and contextual data, including standardized assessment tools such as the Multiple Sclerosis Impact Scale-29 (MSIS-29) and the Patient-Determined Disease Steps (PDDS).
MSIS-29 is a validated self-report measure designed to evaluate the impact of MS on an individual’s physical and psychological well-being [ ]. It comprises 29 items; 20 of which address physical well-being domains and nine address psychological well-being domains. Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale. Raw sub-scores for the physical and psychological sections range from 20 to 100 and 9 to 45, respectively, with higher scores indicating a greater symptom burden [ ]. For analytical consistency, raw scores were converted into percentages in the analysis. MSIS-29 has demonstrated high internal consistency and strong convergent validity in prior studies assessing the impact of MS-related symptoms [ ]. The PDDS is a validated, self-reported ordinal scale that assesses mobility-related disability on a scale from 0 (normal functioning) to 8 (bedridden) [ ]. It is widely used as an accessible and reliable proxy for clinician-administered disability measures, particularly among individuals with mild to moderate MS-related disability [ ]. 
Semi-structured interviews were conducted with each session lasting approximately 60 min. While interviewing, flexibility was maintained to allow for participant-led discussion and elaboration, as well as for the interviewer to clarify and follow up. This approach is consistent with the principles of Interpretive Description, which prioritize understanding participants’ lived experiences and fostering collaborative knowledge generation [ ]. All interviews were audio recorded and subsequently transcribed for analysis.

## Data processing and interpretation
Demographic and contextual data were summarized using frequencies and percentages. The interview recordings were transcribed, retaining participants’ filler words while removing any identifying information and omitting the interviewer’s filler words. Transcripts were reviewed for accuracy and anonymization by a research assistant and a member of the research team. Data were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s (2013) six-phase approach to reflexive thematic analysis [ – ]. Two research team members independently read the transcripts multiple times to develop familiarity with the data and conducted inductive coding using NVivo 12 to generate initial themes. Additional team members, including patient partners, reviewed these preliminary themes and triangulated with data from healthcare providers, field notes, and the research assistant’s reflective journal. Themes were defined narratively and supported with illustrative quotes. The thematic structure of the results was collaboratively developed and finalized through consensus with the broader research team and patient-partner group. We followed the COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative) reporting standards.

## Results

## Demographic data
We interviewed 22 ILMS and 5 with key informants (supplementary file one). One ILMS did not complete the survey. Among the 21 individuals living with MS who completed the demographic and contextual survey, most were female (66.67%), aged 18–44 (57.14%), married (71.43%), and employed (45%). The majority of participants hold some post-secondary education. Most participants (15 of 21) had lived with MS for 4–14 years, showing a mid-range disease duration. Few had recent diagnoses or lived with MS for over 20 years (Table  ).

 

The PDDS scores of the ILMS indicated varied levels of mobility, with one-third reporting no functional limitations and the remainder experiencing mild to severe disability, including the use of a cane, bilateral support, or a wheelchair. MSIS-29 scores reflected a moderate physical and psychological symptom burden, with physical impact averaging 36.2% and psychological impact averaging 35.2%. Most participants lived with another individual in the home. The level of independence in activities of daily living varies from complete independence to requiring assistance from a support person. One person reported using regular home care services. Spirituality held varying importance, and 71.43% accessed primary care within 10 km of their primary dwelling. ## Themes
The communication perspectives of ILMS when discussing end-of-life care, when MAID is a legally available end-of-life care option, were themed as: (1) Forging trust, (2) Framing information, (3) Facilitating conversation, (4) Balancing timing, and (5) Navigating misinformation (Fig.  ).

## Forging trust
The theme of building forging trust included the sub-themes of being askable and attending to patient and family cues. The majority of participants stated they would first discuss end-of-life plans with their partner, followed by their children. However, participants stated that discussing end-of-life care options with their healthcare professionals was also important, as it provided a secondary venue to discuss their feelings and evolving disease processes with their care team. Participants discussed the vital importance of having a trusted, honest relationship with their healthcare provider and identified the need for the healthcare provider to be “askable.” Being askable was characterized as being relational, holistically patient-focused, and actively listening and seeking to understand the patient’s experience. Being askable would facilitate information exchange between the healthcare provider and the individual with MS and their family, as well as help the individual living with MS and their family supporters understand and recognize the signs and symptoms of an evolving illness. Participants further emphasized that being askable encompassed an honest and open relationship that enables transparent information sharing regarding end-of-life and care options. They emphasized that the primary outcome of this trusted healthcare provider relationship was to provide unbiased information, including all relevant details, rather than to obtain or grant approval or endorsement of choices.
Participants discussed that forging trust can be accomplished by attending to conversation cues as they present in appointment visits. When healthcare practitioners (who are askable, trusted, and have a meaningful relationship with their patients) learn of life events such as an illness in an extended family member or a change in the patient’s health status, these moments can serve as a meaningful entry point for initiating discussions regarding the patient’s goals of care. Participants suggested that acknowledging such cues creates opportunities to sensitively invite patients and their families to reflect on and ask questions about their health trajectories and end-of-life preferences.

## Framing information
The theme of framing information included the sub-themes of objective conversations, information that was inclusive of all options, and the need for non-intrusive information alternatives to conversation. Participants were clear that when information is shared regarding their care, including EOL care, the information must be shared neutrally and without indication (overt or subtle) of healthcare provider preferences. Furthermore, they identified that, much like in all other MS care areas (i.e., medication options), not one option should be framed more prominently than another. They viewed their healthcare team members as trusted sources of information and expected healthcare practitioners to share accurate and relevant information in a confidential, unbiased, and professional manner.
Participants also discussed non-intrusive information alternatives to conversation. It is preferred that non-intrusive information alternatives be available to those not yet ready to discuss EOL with others. Participants emphasized that such resources should be neutral, balanced, and professionally curated to ensure credibility and avoid bias. While these alternatives were not viewed as replacements for interpersonal communication, they were considered essential support for individuals needing more time to process complex topics before engaging in deeper conversations.

## Facilitating conversation
The theme of facilitating conversations included the sub-themes of death avoidance, overcoming fear and hesitancy, and emotional gratitude. Participants acknowledged that discussing changing health status and moving towards EOL discussions can be uncomfortable and potentially emotional, resulting in complete avoidance of end-of-life care conversations. Some participants reported that when they attempted to initiate end-of-life conversations with their care team, the conversations were dodged, deflected, or closed. As a result, important issues related to end-of-life care may remain unaddressed, leaving individuals unprepared and unsupported during critical moments of the illness trajectory.
Others acknowledge that it may not be complete avoidance but rather hesitancy or fear holding the conversations back. They elaborated that fear and hesitation may stem from a fear of judgment by others that they are giving up, or from a fear that their healthcare provider does not wish to delve into these topics, as it may scare their patients or signal that they are giving up on them. This fear and hesitancy may result in a missed conversation opportunity, which may be overcome if the healthcare provider/individual living with MS relationship is forged in trust.
When able to work through avoidance, fear and hesitancy, participants reflected on the emotional impact of having open and safe discussions about end-of-life topics. They emphasized the importance of having a space where they can express themselves freely, without judgment or discouragement. For many, this opportunity led to a sense of emotional release, often accompanied by relief and gratitude. Numerous participants expressed appreciation for the research being conducted in this sensitive area, noting that it provided an opportunity to share their experiences and brought long-avoided or feared conversations into the open. The ability to voice inner thoughts in a supportive environment was cathartic and empowering. This highlights the therapeutic value of creating safe spaces for dialogue around end-of-life issues, particularly for those who have previously felt silent or isolated.

## Balancing timing
As participants moved through the themes of forging trust, framing information, and facilitating conversations, they discussed the balance of timing. They reflected on the nature of the MS disease trajectory and the accompanying “ups and downs” as they navigated chronic illness, including exacerbations in physical symptoms, medication changes, and the time immediately post-diagnosis. They emphasized the importance of the timing of conversations and decisions. They underscored the need to avoid making crucial decisions on their difficult days, when their ability to engage in decision-making objectively may be compromised. Participants identified that planning and discussions would optimally occur during periods of illness stability, allowing for clearer thinking, better communication, and more balanced participation in shaping their care and future.

## Navigating misinformation
Lastly, as participants move through the themes of foundational trust, framing information, facilitating conversations, and balancing timing relative to their disease progression, they risk obtaining misinformation regarding EOL and MAID. Participants highlighted that they access a wide range of information sources (e.g., Reddit, social media groups, news articles), which vary in their risk levels. During interviews, participants shared their understanding of MAID eligibility and safeguards, which was often factually incorrect, thereby risking choices being made based on a flawed understanding. Some participants expressed concern that misinformation can create confusion or false expectations, particularly when information cannot be verified with a trusted health professional. Ensuring access to complete, accurate, and evidence-based information about MAID and other end-of-life care options is critical to support informed decision-making and protect individuals from the potential harm associated with misunderstanding such a complex and sensitive issue.

## Discussion
To our knowledge, this is the first qualitative study to explore the communication perspectives of individuals living with MS when discussing end-of-life care, particularly when MAID is a legally available option. Our study identified the foundational importance of a trusted relationship with healthcare professionals when discussing EOL care in the context of MAID. The hallmarks of a trusted relationship are when patients can ask their care provider anything without fear of disdain or judgment, and when the healthcare provider attends to patient cues that signal openness to the conversation. This aligns with previous research that highlights individuals living with MS are more likely to engage in advance care planning when they feel respected and comforted by healthcare providers [ ] and that rapport and attending to patients’ emotional cues can reduce anxiety and support shared decision-making [ , ]. When patients trust their healthcare practitioner, sensitive topics, such as prognosis and future care options, can be discussed [ ]. These trusted and open EOL conversations can help align care with patient values [ ] and may reassure patients, facilitating informed decision-making [ ]. 
Most participants in our study indicated they preferred to first discuss EOL with close family members, such as a partner and/or parents. However, they emphasized the critical role of their healthcare professional in providing a secondary place to discuss their evolving illness, feelings, and care options. This finding is supported by previous research highlighting the crucial role that family plays in EOL decision-making [ ] and by patients’ view of their healthcare practitioners as reliable sources of evidence-based treatment recommendations and support [ ]. 
Participants in our study emphasized the importance of having complete and accurate information. They further identified that this EOL information must be objective and include a non-intrusive alternative to verbal conversation. Sharing resources, such as written materials or videos, can improve content understanding and contribute to informed decision-making [ , ]. Furthermore, evidence suggests that conversations at multiple time points can foster trust-building and open dialogue, ensure content clarity, and allow patients to explore their values, thereby supporting informed decision-making [ , ]. 
Our study highlights that, within the theme of facilitating conversation, a sub-theme of death avoidance emerged. We know that healthcare practitioners may be hesitant to discuss EOL options (including MAID) due to a fear of causing distress to the patient, as well as their own uncertainty about the legal frameworks [ , ]. This hesitation may delay or truncate conversations, leaving the patients with unanswered questions regarding EOL options. The non-participation of healthcare practitioners regarding end-of-life care may result in significant barriers for patients and family members. It is crucial to approach this issue with understanding and empathy, as non-participation, whether due to personal, ethical, or institutional causes, can leave patients and families feeling unsupported, disempowered, and forced to navigate a complex system on their own [ ]. Capacity-building training for healthcare practitioners to develop effective communication skills to navigate EOL planning when MAID is a legally available option is crucial for enhancing the quality of care and ensuring patient-centered decision-making.
The project highlighted the importance of timing balance in EOL care planning for ILMS. Navigating the unpredictable and fluctuating nature of the MS disease trajectory, including accompanying highs and lows of navigating chronic illness (symptom exacerbation, medication changes, and the time immediately post-diagnosis), largely influenced this balance of timing. Additionally, timing and decision-making for ILMS can be particularly challenging due to cognitive difficulties, such as reduced processing speed, memory issues, and problems with visuospatial abilities [ ]. As these cognitive impairments may worsen over time, engaging in conversations about end-of-life care and making informed decisions may become increasingly complex and challenging [ ]. Individuals involved in advance care planning report higher satisfaction with the care they receive [ ]. Initiating EOL options in advance of cognitive decline and during relatively stable periods of the disease trajectory ensures that ILMS can participate in meaningful conversations and make informed decisions.
Our study highlighted the wide range of information sources patients are accessing and demonstrated the misinterpretation of the MAID eligibility and safeguards. Misinformation about eligibility and EOL processes may lead to fear, confusion and delayed planning [ , ]. Inaccurate information can increase emotional distress and hinder informed consent [ ]. Thus, providing clear, evidence-based resources is necessary to avoid misconceptions and support valued choices [ , ]. Navigating misinformation requires ongoing dialogue, accessible resources, and culturally sensitive communication to empower patients and families in making confident, informed EOL decisions.

## Strengths and limitations
One of this study’s main strengths is its use of Interpretive Description methodology, which enabled the collection of diverse participant perspectives and was further strengthened by triangulation with key informant data from demographically and contextually varied ILMS. However, there remains a possibility of response bias, as participants who felt more comfortable discussing EOL options may have been more likely to participate. Importantly, the study did not seek to specifically capture the perspectives of immigrant, minority, or Indigenous communities, groups whose cultural backgrounds may shape distinct views on EOL care. As the research was conducted in a single Canadian province, the results may not be generalizable to other regions. However, we hope that the provision of ample demographic and contextual data will support the transferability of the results where appropriate. To address these gaps, future studies should involve not only family members but also policymakers, as their perspectives are essential for informing EOL care practice, particularly in the context of evolving MAID legislation around advance consent and directives.

## Conclusion
This study aimed to understand better the communication perspectives of individuals living with MS when discussing end-of-life care in the context of MAID as an end-of-life care option. It highlights the importance of trusted patient-provider relationships, providing complete and objective information, as well as compassionate conversations that are sensitive to their MS care trajectory, supported by factual information. As the number of individuals living with MS increases and end-of-life care options evolve in Canada, incorporating patient population-specific perspectives into EOL planning will be necessary to ensure equitable, responsive, and ethically grounded end-of-life care.
