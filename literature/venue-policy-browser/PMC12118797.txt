In the past 15 years or so, poster presentations have become an integral part of conferences of professional organisations and continuing medical education programs. The number of postgraduate (PG) poster presentations at medical conferences has soared of late. This trend is partly driven by guidelines from Indian medical universities and the National Medical Commission (NMC), which require PGs to actively participate in academic forums, often through presenting a research paper or poster, before being eligible for their final (exit) examinations.[ ] Many institutions interpret these guidelines as effectively mandating at least one conference presentation at the national, zonal, or state level, thereby contributing to the surge in poster presentations. Many PGs prefer posters over oral presentations, largely due to the relative ease of preparation and acceptance. With the increased use of e-posters, a wide variation exists in how posters are presented. Several organisers judge the posters for awards, using diverse criteria.
The guidelines for presenters and the judging criteria for awards often ignore the unique demands of posters as a communication medium (e.g., design, conciseness, use of visual means). For more than 15 years, we have attended, presented in, organised, and judged numerous poster competitions across state, zonal, national, and international psychiatry conferences and experienced the design dilemmas, logistical bottlenecks, and scoring ambiguities that can undermine them. This editorial distils those lessons, blended with information from websites of major organisations, into a concise set of doable recommendations aimed at helping conference organisers deliver poster sessions that are visually engaging, logistically smooth, and scientifically rigorous. We also propose some criteria for judging the posters competing for an award and some minimum standards for the judging process. These will help avoid common errors and make the process smoother for the presenters, organisers, and judges. Furthermore, the unique aspects of posters as a presentation medium will be respected. Though most of our suggestions apply to conferences, organisers of exhibitions or research day events may also find them useful.

## DOS AND DON’TS FOR GUIDELINES

## General suggestions
Organisers should share the guidelines on their websites sufficiently early to avoid confusion among the participants, mostly PGs who are fresh to the process. Brochures and the website often contain conflicting information due to the website getting updated after the brochure release. Hence, it is better to include in the brochure, only a suggestion to visit the website for the latest guidelines, preferably with a QR code link to the page.
Provide well in advance detailed instructions about the format (digital or print), size, orientation (portrait or landscape), who can present (PG students, early career researchers or faculty under 40, members of the organisation only or open to all), and preferably the name and location of the venue. If feasible, offer both print and e-poster options to cater to diverse preferences. However, standardize judging criteria across formats. Will there be an opportunity for an oral presentation of the poster? (Ideally, it should be there.) If yes, especially for posters not in the competition category, if a presenter cannot travel to the venue, can they include a QR code in the poster that links to an audio (voice-over) or video recording of the presentation? Mention how much time is allowed for the presentation and the questions from the judges. (Provide this information to the judges, too.)
Though organisations such as The International Association for Child and Adolescent Psychiatry and Allied Professions (IACAPAP) impose templates to ensure visual consistency,[ ] we feel that templates, especially those with headings and boxes with fixed area, are better not enforced. They restrict freedom of design, limit creativity, and fail to accommodate diverse submissions with different foci. If necessary, a template can be provided for the first-timers, with encouragement to explore other designs.
Consider not dictating specific fonts. (If compatibility during e-poster presentations is a concern, asking to submit the posters in PDF or a picture format would work.) The American Psychological Association recommends a font size of ≥72 for titles. The ideal and minimum font sizes they suggest are 56 and 46 for section headings and 36 and 24 for body text.[ ] However, for the same size, different fonts can have different heights. Hence, another option is to suggest the expected height of the text. For example, the American Psychiatric Association (APA) recommends that, as posters would be viewed from a distance of three feet or more, the title section should be at least 1” high while all lettering should be at least 3/8” high and preferably in bold type.[ ]
If the organisation’s or program’s logo should be included, it is better to let the presenters choose where and how to place it. The upper left corner is where we start reading and one of the crucial spots in the poster where vital information can be placed—using that space for logos, especially large ones, removes that opportunity.
Organisers could share with the participants a sample poster that incorporates design best practices (e.g., high-contrast colours, legible fonts, concise captions and bullet points, standout and concise visuals, ensuring a logical flow with numbering or arrows, uncluttered look). To set the standards high, consider showcasing the winning posters from past events in a pre-conference webinar or posting them as a rolling video on the conference website.
Once announced, it is desirable not to change the deadlines for submission of abstracts or final posters without valid reasons (such as a dearth of submissions), particularly if an award is involved. Such postponements diminish the time those who submit before the first deadline get to prepare and polish their work.

## Tips from around the world
The IACAPAP World Congress requires e-posters as single-page, portrait PDF (<4 MB) files named in a prescribed style. Enforced through a submission portal that rejects non-compliant files, this simplifies vetting and enables a searchable e-library for post-event access.
The World Association of Social Psychiatry (WASP) Regional Congress accepts only completed studies and requires the presenting author to be fully registered before acceptance is confirmed; this upfront filter prevents late dropouts and keeps the discussion focused on finished data.[ ] The American Medical Association (AMA) mandates that the primary author stand by the poster during judging.[ ] Such insistences for completed works and physical presence help maintain scientific rigour, keep floor layouts tidy, and ensure every poster is actively defended.
The Royal College of Psychiatrists (RCPsych) International Congress displays e-posters on large touchscreens for four days and allows the top submissions (chosen based on the scores the abstracts got) to be presented as five-minute offline “rapid fire” talks.[ ] Mirroring this model lets organisers cut printing hassles and create lively micro-presentation slots. Likewise, the World Psychiatric Association (WPA) World Congress specifies a single-page PDF with 16×9 landscape format, font size ≥11, and ≥200 dpi images and allows QR codes but not animations—clear layout rules that can be baked into an auto validation portal and help keep the screens legible even in bright exhibit lighting.[ ]
Complementary insights come from the APA and the Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Psychiatrists (RANZCP) Congress.[ ] APA mandates one investigator-presenter, complete data (no “results pending”), strict conflict-of-interest (COI) disclosure, a ban on brand-name promotion, and a font size of 72 for funding statement of industry-sponsored studies; organisers can embed the same compliance checklist in their upload portal, auto-flagging abstracts that use commercial logos or omit generic drug names. RANZCP, like RCPsych, keeps posters on display for the entire meeting and strengthens engagement through three additional measures: (i) A rapid-fire tier in which the highest-scoring posters are upgraded to 10-minute, ≤10-slide talks, bridging poster and oral formats; (ii) all poster presenters must register two months in advance or risk removal, reducing late withdrawals and empty boards; and (iii) an exclusive trainee/early-career category linked to its Psychiatry Interest Forum, which boosts participation of students and junior doctors through a separate judging stream and prize. The Japanese Society of Psychiatry and Neurology (JSPN) adds a further quality safeguard by allowing authors to revise their abstracts as many times as they like during the submission window; adopting a similar model can help minimise post-acceptance corrections.[ ] Taken together, combining APA’s style integrity checks, RANZCP’s rapid fire enhancements and early career track, and JSPN’s flexible revision policy ensures rigorous governance and sustained audience engagement.
These examples offer a flexible menu of proven options from global contexts, not a rigid checklist. Organisers can select elements like pre-acceptance editing, integrity-check portals, or rapid-fire sessions that suit their focus, venue, and budget.

## Specific suggestions for print posters

Mention whether printing and transporting are the responsibility of the presenters, the organisers would print (for a fee) the posters submitted in advance, and if the conference provides complimentary printing/travel/shipping expenses.
Mention whether the conference would have a printing service on-site.
Advise to avoid printing posters on heavy materials that may tear while applying or may be difficult to mount with pushpins.
Mention the space available for mounting the poster in terms of ‘height x width’ and the maximum size allowed for posters. Consider adding an illustration of the dimensions to avoid confusion. To ensure visibility without overwhelming space, one can follow the “4 ft × 8 ft board size” recommendation from APA, the “90 cm × 120 cm single sheet” one from RCPsych[ ] or the “≤45 × 45 inches” one from AMA, which even levies a penalty for size violation.
Mention that the presenters are responsible for mounting and demounting the posters.
Allocate setup and teardown times, like APA’s 30-minute windows before and after sessions, to keep things running smoothly.
Mention what materials will be available (e.g., push pins, tapes).

## Specific suggestions for e-posters

Mention the screen size in terms of ‘height × width’.
Specify the allowed file type(s) (e.g., jpeg., pptx., pdf).
Consider using the American Psychological Association’s recommendations: a one-page PDF in landscape format with a minimum font size of 24 for body text. This ensures readability across devices like tablets, laptops, and large screens.[ ]
Mention the maximum number of slides allowed per presentation (preferably one). Mention whether the slides should be static or whether rolling additional content on the same slide through animations is allowed.
Mention if videos can be embedded in the poster.
Include instructions about uploading the poster file. Allow for at least a 48-hour window between the deadline and presentation time.
Ensure submission portals are user-friendly and compatible with standard formats (e.g. PDF without multimedia, per WPA).
Mention what resources will be available (e.g., pen drives, email sharing).
On the presentation day, allow a time window to check if the presentation files are displayed properly and to correct any errors detected.

## SUGGESTIONS FOR AWARD PRESENTATIONS

As posters on research (primary or secondary) and those on case reports and series have different structures, foci, and importance, it is better to have separate competitions for these two categories.
If feasible, posters not selected for the competition section can be allowed to be presented in a free-poster category. However, ‘best free-poster awards,’ not uncommon in our programs, are meaningless and undermine the importance of the actual award competition.
Ensure that at least two judges assess each competing abstract and, later, the posters.
Identify judges early on. This helps choose those without a COI and allows time to collect COI statements. Besides, picking the judges from among the program audience at the last minute can demotivate presenters.
Providing the competing posters to floor judges in advance helps them to evaluate design and content beforehand. Then, on the presentation day, they can concentrate on the oral presentation and ensuing discussion.

## Proposed judging criteria for awards

To be eligible for the best poster award, a submission must fulfil each of the following criteria:
1.1. It must be based on work done in India (if the conducting organisation wants to stipulate so) or a systematic review/meta-analysis. (Narrative or scoping reviews may also be included if relevant for the program and considering the expertise level of the potential presenters).
1.2. This or a substantially similar paper must not have been published in or currently submitted to a scientific journal, nor presented at any other conference.
1.3. Ethics clearance (where necessary) has been obtained from an appropriate ethics committee/review board and explicitly mentioned in the poster. Details of trial or PROSPERO registrations should be mentioned, too, when relevant. Case reports/series should specify that consent was obtained from the patient or nominated representative for the presentation.
1.4. No judges or the Awards Committee members should be first authors or co-authors of a poster submitted for the award.
While submitting the abstract, the authors should furnish a Declaration containing the following items:
2.1. A statement that the submission adheres to clauses 1.1 to 1.3.
2.2. Consent from all authors about submission of the abstract.
2.3. A statement that the poster’s content shall be free from copyright infringement.
2.4. A disclosure statement on whether generative AI was used to prepare the poster’s content and, if yes, the names of the tools and to what extent.
At least two judges should assess all the received abstracts. They shall select for the competition section a convenient number of submissions (e.g., 10–20) that obtained a prespecified minimum percentage score (e.g., 50%).
Abstracts of research papers can be assessed using the criteria in , and those of case reports and series, using those in .
If accepted for presentation, the authors should retain in the poster the same title, objectives, sample size, and findings as mentioned in the submitted abstract. If deviations are detected, the poster can be disqualified from presentation.
On the day of the presentation, at least two judges (different from those who screened the abstracts) should assess each of the posters in the competition section. They can use the criteria in . Do not assign different sets of judges to different screens or groups of print posters, as inter-rater reliability cannot be ensured.
Compute the total marks by combining those obtained for the abstract and the poster presentation. The poster with the highest total marks (can specify the minimum percentage of marks required, if needed) shall win. In case of a tie, both the posters shall be declared joint winners.
All authors of the winning poster shall be considered to have won the award and given individual certificates (at least a soft copy), if possible.

 

## BEHIND-THE-SCENES STRATEGIES
Running a poster session involves more than guidelines and technology; practical details can significantly influence its success. Below are some key operational considerations:

## Feedback summaries for presenters
Constructive feedback helps participants refine their work for future conferences. Whenever possible, share anonymised judge comments or scoring sheets. Organisers can place small whiteboards or sticky-note stations near each poster for attendees to leave comments or suggestions. This would help quieter attendees share their thoughts without the pressure of direct conversation. Presenters can later collect these ideas for collaboration or to improve future research. This advantage could motivate young researchers to present posters.

## Plan for physical space constraints
Allocate sufficient room between poster boards (e.g., 1-2 meters) to prevent overcrowding, especially during peak viewing times. Poor spacing frustrates attendees and obscures visibility, undermining presenters’ efforts. Map the venue layout in advance and cap poster numbers if space is limited. Posters can be grouped by theme or discipline. The exhibition should be in a well-lit area with adequate space for clear viewing (not in corridors). Provide adequate signage leading to the poster area.

## Manage presenter no-shows
Account for last-minute withdrawals by maintaining a reserve list of posters or, in hybrid setups, allowing flexible scheduling for virtual presenters. No-shows disrupt judging and waste space, so proactive planning maintains program integrity. Confirm participation 48 hours prior via email or call.

## Last-minute logistic issues
Designate a local print shop or have an on-site large-format printer as a backup for poster-printing catastrophes. Provide basic supplies (e.g., tape, tacks, Velcro, extension cords). Presenters may forget or break their poster stands; prearranging some backup stands or boards can save the day. Also, a retrieval area may be designated, with staff overseeing unclaimed items. Encourage presenters to label poster tubes with their names and contact info; anything left behind can be easily returned. This also protects valuable (and often expensive to print) posters. Allotting all posters a specific number that indicates the day of presentation and the board number, and affixing the same on the poster board and tube would be useful, too (e.g., D1P34 would mean day 1, poster board 34).
For e-posters, test the displays in advance to prevent technical glitches on the big day. If you combine many PowerPoint slide submissions into one file, ensure the formatting has not been wrecked.

## Ensure committee presence throughout
For print posters, assign an organising committee member to be present from poster setup through session conclusion and removal of the posters. This ensures prompt resolution of disputes (e.g., space conflicts), support for logistical challenges (e.g., damaged boards), and efficient supervision of cleanup to avoid confusion and minimise loss of materials or participant belongings. For e-posters, posting one member at the venue throughout the session helps prevent the theft of laptops, which we have experienced once.

## Use visual timing aids
A simple signboard or timer visible to presenters (and judges) can help them keep track of the presentation duration. It also subtly manages crowd flow.

## Assign shadow judges
Some judges cancel at the last minute. Have a backup set of judges briefed on scoring criteria who can step in promptly. Have a quick plan if a judge realises they have a COI with a particular poster.

## Schedule judging thoughtfully
Avoid scheduling award judging during lunch breaks, as this risks low attendance, rushed evaluations, and presenter fatigue, undermining fairness and engagement. An ideal time is mid-morning (e.g., 10:00-11:30 AM) or early afternoon (e.g., 1:30-3:00 PM), when participants would be alert and the evaluation would not be competing with meals or keynotes.
Consider open-floor judging[ ] where other delegates can watch the presentations made to the judges and the Q and A. This converts the judging process into a live teaching opportunity. To improve attendance, avoid parallel academic sessions. Ensure proper etiquette: spectators should stand a metre back and hold questions until marks are submitted so that presenters and judges can interact without distraction.
If open-floor judging is not adopted, a ‘poster walk’ time can be specified. Parallel sessions may be avoided in that time slot. The posters may still be made available for viewing for half or a full day (or maybe for an extended period on the conference website).

## Ensure prompt prize distribution
Announce awards on the competition day whenever possible. Publish the winner’s details on the organisation’s website and social media pages. If logistical constraints (e.g., budget approvals) delay prize money or certificates, commit to delivery within a week. Long delays, sometimes stretching to months, erode trust and diminish the recognition’s impact. Prearrange funding and communicate timelines in award announcements.

## SUGGESTIONS FOR HYBRID SESSIONS
Hybrid conferences blending in-person and virtual elements are becoming common, and poster sessions must adapt accordingly. Organisers should prioritise seamless e-poster compatibility across platforms and test the posters on devices like tablets, laptops, and large screens to avoid formatting glitches. To reduce technical issues during the program, organisers can offer advanced short video tutorials on formatting and uploading. Provide dedicated tech support, such as a helpdesk or live chat, to troubleshoot issues for presenters and attendees alike. Attendees with disabilities can be provided large-print alternative versions, audio descriptions, or short podcast summaries.
Embrace tools like virtual poster galleries (e.g., platforms like vFairs or Gather.town) to showcase e-posters with clickable navigation. Integrate interactive Q and A apps (e.g. Slido, Mentimeter) to foster real-time engagement between presenters and global audiences.[ , , , , ] Leaderboards and polls would be beneficial, too. Participation can be incentivised using virtual poster walk challenges, where attendees are rewarded for visiting lots of posters or asking questions. One increasingly popular method is the ‘Poster Passport’ or scavenger hunt concept, where attendees collect stamps or digital checkmarks at each poster. This gamification approach is effective in boosting attendee interaction.[ ]

## CONCLUSION
At Psychiatry conferences, poster competitions serve as a critical mechanism to disseminate novel research, validate methodologies, and cultivate the next generation of scholars. The strategies outlined here, such as standardised guidelines, rigorous and comprehensive evaluation, and hybrid technologies, will enable organisers to optimise this important scientific discourse. These approaches mitigate common organisational challenges, such as inconsistent judging or logistical oversights, ensuring a streamlined process. Collectively, these efforts could elevate poster sessions into a cornerstone of psychiatric scholarship, advancing the field’s scientific integrity and impact.
