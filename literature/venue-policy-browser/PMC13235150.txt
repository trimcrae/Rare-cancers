## Abstract

## Background
Mass gatherings entail unique crowd behavioral, health and safety risks for participants; however, risk perception can vary widely, and factors that influence perception of risk are not completely understood. Mass gathering organizers must take into consideration recognition, mitigation, monitoring, and communication of risk. This scoping review aims to (1) examine key factors affecting public perceptions of risk in mass gatherings and; (2) identify contemporary risk communication strategies specific to these events.

## Methods
We conducted a scoping review of articles indexed in MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, CINAHL, and PsycINFO published between 1990 and 2024. Eligible articles included peer-reviewed original research (qualitative and quantitative), case studies, commentaries, reviews, and book chapters that discussed either factors influencing public risk perception of mass gatherings or strategies for mass gathering risk communication. Title and abstract screening, full article review, and data extraction were performed independently by team members. Final article inclusion was determined by team consensus, and findings were analyzed using thematic content analysis.

## Results
A total of 29 articles were included. Of these, four articles addressed factors shaping public risk perception of mass gatherings, 14 articles addressed risk communication strategies, and 11 articles addressed both. Factors influencing risk perception include mass gathering risk education, nature of the crowd, overcrowding, as well as event and venue specific characteristics. Risk communication strategies for mass gatherings thematically highlighted- communications flow, timing and content of messaging and differing modalities such as digital media, physical media and in-person communication.

## Conclusions
Understanding factors affecting risk perception of mass gatherings can inform risk communication strategies tailored for different target participants. While a variety of risk communication approaches have been employed in differing contexts, gaps remain in determining effectiveness of the wide range of risk communication strategies used for mass gatherings and suggest future directions for risk communication research.

## Introduction
Mass gatherings (MGs) are characterized by planned or spontaneous crowds reaching thresholds that strain the planning and response resources of the community, jurisdiction or country where they are held [ ]. Often associated with sports, religious, music, political, or sociocultural events, MGs present unique crowd behavioral, health, and safety risks. When major crowds convene, MGs may amplify emergencies and health crises arising from communicable and noncommunicable diseases, injuries, natural disasters or security threats with resultant casualties [ , ]. A detailed review of 154 MGs from 1990 to 2025 found a total of 169,732 recorded injuries and 18,585 recorded deaths [ ]. 
Prior studies have reported on health and safety risks during MGs [ – ], preparedness and response for MG events (e.g., the Hajj) [ , ], and specific MG disasters (e.g. stampedes) [ , ]. However, one important domain that has received comparatively less attention is risk communication in MGs [ ]. Despite its vital importance in MG medicine and public health, risk communication is often embedded within broader domains of preparedness, emergency response, and surveillance, rather than explicitly conceptualized as a distinct area of study.
Broadly, risk communication is defined as “the exchange of information about the nature, severity, and management of a risk” [ ]. Risk communication and community engagement are closely interconnected across all phases of MGs, including mitigation, preparedness, response, and recovery. Risk communication entails the exchange of information that enables those at risk (e.g., attendees) to make informed decisions for taking preventative and protective measures, while community engagement emphasizes the involvement of host communities in the response process, including identification of risks and solutions that affect them [ , ]. Public health risk communication seeks to improve health and safety outcomes and effect harm reduction by informing at-risk populations and promoting engagement in health-protective behaviors [ ]. Risk communication is an interdisciplinary field, grounded in communication, sociology, psychology, and decision sciences, as well as practical experience.
With respect to MGs, risk communication is complex and multifaceted, requiring integrated coordination among organizers, first responders, public health and safety agencies, attendees, and the general public [ ]. Factors, such as social context, crowd characteristics, and organizational dynamics, have been explored as to how they may influence an individual’s perception of risk [ , ]. Additionally, understanding recipients’ motivational, psychological, and behavioral factors in risk judgement has long been recognized as essential pillars in the design of effective risk communication campaigns that encourage behavior change [ , ]. However, the ways they can inform effective risk communication strategies remain less understood [ ]. To better characterize these issues, our scoping review aimed to examine key factors affecting public perceptions of risk in MGs and identify contemporary risk communication strategies for these events.

## Methods

## Search strategy
A scoping review was undertaken to categorize factors influencing risk perception, understand risk communication strategies, and identify knowledge gaps in risk communication for MGs. Search strategies were developed by the study team in collaboration with a university informationist. Given the distinct characteristics, and management and response strategies of more structured mass gathering events (e.g., religious gatherings, sporting events) compared to spontaneous and unplanned mass gatherings (e.g., protests or civil unrest), the search strategy focused on established types of organized mass gathering events [ , ]. Search terms included keywords related to the stated aims and adapted to database-specific index terms (Additional File , Supplemental Table ). The following databases were included: MEDLINE (Ovid; National Library of Medicine, National Institutes of Health; Bethesda, Maryland, US), Embase (Embase.com; Elsevier; Amsterdam, Netherlands), Scopus (Scopus.com; Elsevier; Amsterdam, Netherlands), CINAHL (EBSCO; Ipswich, Massachusetts, US), and PsycINFO (EBSCO; American Psychological Association; Washington DC, US).

## Eligibility criteria
Inclusion criteria required that the article: (1) must address one or more of the stated aims; (2) was published between 1990 and the date of the search (December 18, 2024); and (3) was published in the English language. Articles were excluded if they did not meet all the above criteria and/or were (1) not peer-reviewed or; (2) based on simulations. To obtain a broad overview of the evidence pertaining to MG risk communication, we included peer-reviewed studies of all types, including original research (qualitative and quantitative), case studies, commentaries, reviews, and book chapters.

## Data management
Following the initial search, records were imported and managed in Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation; Melbourne, Australia) and duplicates were removed. First, record titles and abstracts that met the inclusion criteria were independently screened by two members of the study team and with a third, when necessary to resolve any conflicts. Second, full articles were extracted and independently reviewed by two members of the study team, and with a third, when necessary, until consensus was reached. In both steps, titles and abstracts, and full text articles were assessed for eligibility using the criteria outlined above.
Data were extracted from selected studies using an adapted Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome, Timing/Type of study, and Setting (PICOTS) framework by three members of the study team (Additional File , Supplemental Table ). The PICOTS framework, originally designed for summarizing clinical trials, was tailored to meet the topic and study objectives. For instance, Population broadly summarized information on participants (for original research studies), media (for case studies), or mass gathering events (for review or commentary article types, or book chapters). Risk communication strategies were defined as any intervention, tool, or approach designed to convey MG health and safety risk-related information to interested parties, such as stakeholders, authorities or organizers, MG attendees, and the public. Timing included the timing of data collection and study scope (publication years included in review search strategies). We tabulated general information on article author(s), year of publication, article type, setting, aims, methodology and key findings relevant to each study’s aim. A second member of the team reviewed the extracted information for accuracy.
Next, relevant text excerpts and article results related to the aims of this scoping review were compiled. Here, data were extracted independently by members of the study team separately by aim, with a second member reviewing extracted information for accuracy and completeness. All data were managed in Excel Version 16 (Microsoft Corporation, 2023).

## Data analysis
Given the heterogeneity of included studies, extracted data were analyzed using an inductive thematic content analysis, with findings organized into conceptual categories defined post-hoc. First, one member of the study team independently reviewed the extracted information to develop preliminary codes aligned with the study objectives: (1) factors affecting perceptions of risk and; (2) risk communication strategies. Thematic areas were then refined collaboratively before team members independently reviewed the selected articles and synthesized extracted data. Results were validated and discussed among all authors. In line with the comprehensive purpose of scoping reviews, the research question criteria were iteratively adapted to capture the breadth of the available literature on this topic. This scoping review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses, extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) statement and guidelines (Additional File , Supplemental Table ) [ ]. 

## Results

## Article characteristics
After title and abstract screening followed by full article review, 29 articles were included (Fig. ). Among selected articles, four articles addressed public perceptions of risk surrounding MG events [ – ], 14 articles addressed mass gathering risk communication strategies [ , – ], and 11 articles addressed both [ , , – ]. 
 The included articles comprised nine cross-sectional surveys [ , , , , , – ], six qualitative studies [ – , , , ], five reviews [ , , , , ], four case studies [ , – ], three commentaries [ , , ], one framework paper [ ], and one tool development study [ ]. The most popular study settings were Saudi Arabia, Australia or collective nations worldwide. Article summaries are presented in Supplemental Table (Additional File ).

## Analysis of selected articles
Key themes and subthemes around MG risk communication fell into two broad categories: (1) individual, crowd, and event factors shaping public perceptions of risks associated with MGs, and (2) attributes of MG risk communication strategies, including communication flow, timing, content, and modality.

## Factors influencing public perception of risk
A total of 15 articles discussed factors shaping MG risk perception were defined as those impacting an individual’s assessment of susceptibility, severity, and personal relevance of a health and safety concern (Table  ). These included factors at the individual-, crowd-, and event-level.
 

## Individual factors
The most prominent factor described that affected an individual’s risk perception was having received education regarding MG events and possible risks [ , , – ]. Hajj pilgrims from Australia in 2014 had increased risk perception if they had received pre-travel health advice [ ]. Additionally, those who were employed or had higher education also had higher risk perception. Event-based education can potentially reduce risk for individuals attending MG events, whereas a lack of MG event education can affect participant well-being [ ]. 
Perception of risk is also culturally and behaviorally dependent [ , ]. Sociocultural values such as prior experienced traumas (e.g., war, famine, natural disasters) and religious or belief systems affect perception of risk by shaping how individuals interpret personal susceptibility and vulnerability to risks [ ]. Alcohol and drug use shaped the risks attendees recognized, though individuals engaging in these behaviors did not always perceive the associated risks [ , , , , , ]. 
Demographic characteristics, including age [ – ], education [ , ], and gender [ ], were associated with differences in risk perception. Among adolescents and young adults attending youth festivals, primary health and safety concerns focused on immediate needs such as access to water and food [ ]. Gender differences were also observed, with women more likely than men to recognize high-risk behaviors [ ]. 

## Crowd factors
Risk perception can shift in response on crowd psychology [ , , , , , , ], and crowd dynamics [ , , , , ]. Shared social identity among attendees may increase social support, collective trust, and cooperation while lowering individual perceived level of risk [ , ]. On the contrary, crowd dynamics, particularly crowd density and movement, heighten risk perception. Perceived overcrowding associated with restricted freedom of movement increased perceptions of possible health and safety threats among attendees [ , ]. 

## Event factors
Public perception of risk is also be influenced by the type of MG, such as religious, sporting, holiday, or festivals [ , , , ]. Risk awareness differs by event type, with sporting event attendees more aware of alcohol-related risks and altercations, and pilgrims at religious gatherings more aware of infectious disease and stampede risks [ ]. 
Three articles reported that environmental factors, including climate and features of the physical space, influence risk perception [ , , ]. In a study of outdoor music festivals, high temperatures were identified as a key factor shaping perceptions of health risk, with heat stress and dehydration cited as foremost concerns [ ]. The built environment – including inadequate space and venue design – was also reported to influence perceived safety risks [ , , ]. In a survey of religious pilgrims, high crowd density compounded by inadequate space or suboptimal venue design intensified perceptions of health and safety concerns [ ]. 

## Mass gathering risk communication strategies
A total of 25 articles addressed MG risk communication strategies, categorized into four domains(Table  ; Fig.  ). The risk communication strategy domains include: (1) communication flow (2) timing (3) content and (4) modality. Communication flow relates to the direction of communications. Horizontal communications pertain to exchange of information between agencies, departments, or organizations at the same hierarchical level. Vertical communications refer to the information transfer along the chain of command between different levels of government. Timing relates to the temporal nature of risk communications taking place either before, during or after an event. Content of risk communications may be subdivided by purpose and syntax. The purpose may be strictly informational or intended to be educational while syntax encompasses how messages are crafted to most readily be taken up by its intended audience. Modality is how risk communication is conveyed, via digital means (e.g. social media, email, text messaging), physical (e.g. signs, pamphlets) or in-person channels (e.g. PA systems).
 A carefully considered risk communication strategy addresses each of these interrelated domains through a tailored campaign that leverages multiple risk communication approaches for the intended audience. Accounting for the interrelationships among these key domains is essential to optimizing successful risk communication. For example, prior to an event, educational content promoting healthy behaviors may be delivered through digital or mass media modalities (e.g., event website, social media) to support greater awareness of potential health and safety risks and increased preparedness among attendees. During an event, timely and verified informational messaging about emerging risks (e.g., excessive heat, crowding) may be communicated vertically to attendees via text messages, public address systems, or signage to support informed decision making and protective behaviors. After an event, communication may shift toward debriefing, evaluation, and future planning with horizontal communication among event organizers, emergency responders, community public health officials and healthcare personnel facilitated through meetings and formal reports.

## Communication flow
Communication flow, encompassing both horizontal and vertical communication, was identified in 17 studies as a key component of comprehensive MG risk communication strategies. Horizontal communication – the exchange of information among organizations or personnel involved in event planning and response, including organizing entities (e.g., planners, promoters, venue managers), public safety and security (e.g., law enforcement, private security), medical services and emergency response teams (e.g., first responders, prehospital personnel, emergency personnel, public health officials), and government and regulatory agencies – facilitates timely information sharing and coordinated service delivery [ , , , , – , , ]. Types of information shared among stakeholder organizations included emergency response protocols, contingency planning, responsibility and resource allocation, MG risk identification and assessment, and recommendations for development of educational campaigns, communication with attendees, and crowd control measures. Additional information exchanged included real-time situational updates and crowd surveillance, operational capacity (e.g., surges in medical demand), and public health guidance (e.g., infection prevention measures, hydration and heat safety messaging). In contrast, suboptimal horizontal communication can lead to delays and increase the potential for harm [ ]. 
Vertical communication or the transmission of risk information across hierarchical levels (e.g., event organizers, management, and attendees), was also highlighted as critical [ , , – , – , , , , , , ]. Inadequate two-way communication between organizers, emergency responders, and attendees has been cited as a contributing factor in crowd disasters, including stampedes, where delays in information flow hindered timely response and crowd notification.

## Timing
Twenty-two articles describing risk communication strategies reported on the timing of communication, including prior [ , , – , – , , , , – ], during [ , , , – , – , – , , , , , , ], and after a MG [ , , ]. 

## Prior the event
Risk communication should begin well in advance of an event, as early engagement with attendees may help reduce health risks at MGs [ , ]. Providing information on potential hazards before the event can increase awareness of risk, while pre-travel advice from physicians or family members may further shape risk perception [ ]. Mass media campaigns have also been used prior to events to warn of potential dangers [ , ]. 
Pre-event awareness campaigns can take many forms depending on the event context. For example, campaigns addressing climate-related risks may promote hydration and sun protection, while others aimed at curbing risks associated with excessive alcohol use, focus on reducing alcohol-related health and safety risks in large MG contexts [ , , ]. 

## During the event
The effectiveness of during-event risk communication may be enhanced by integrating real-time data from crowd surveillance and adapting messaging to crowd mood and density [ , , , , , ]. To be most effective, risk communication should be timely, structured, and coordinated [ ]. Frameworks, such as the WHO’s “Jeddah Tool,” place particular emphasis on emergency messaging systems to alert attendees of crises while an event is ongoing [ ]. Conversely, withholding real-time information from attendees out of concerns about provoking panic has been linked to serious adverse consequences and crowd disasters [ ]. 

## After the event
Post-event communication also plays a role in shaping risk understanding. Media reporting following disasters may use sensationalist language, which can unintentionally stigmatize MG attendees and distort public perceptions of risk associated with these events [ ]. Post-event debrief meetings among event organizers and emergency responders can also help identify operational challenges and lessons learned [ , ]. 

## Content
Message content, comprising message purpose and syntax, represents a key domain of MG risk communication strategies. Across studies, message purpose was typically categorized as informational or educational. Informational content provides attendees with practical event details, such as weather conditions, terrain, and access to facilities [ , , ]. Educational content focuses on raising awareness of safety concerns, including vaccination campaigns, alcohol-related risks, and general strategies for staying safe at events [ , – , , , , ]. 
Deliberate syntax, or the choice of words and structure used to convey risk communication messages, is an important element of effective risk communication [ , ]. Clear and carefully constructed language can improve how messages are understood and received. Campaign slogans may reinforce risk messaging; for example, the Australian youth campaign slogan “Don’t Drink and Drown” clearly highlighted the dangers of alcohol use while swimming [ ]. Additionally, effective message syntax should be empathetic and tailored to the characteristics of intended recipients, incorporating their cultural and social values [ ]. 

## Modality
The modality of risk communication was addressed by 15 articles, ranging from digital media, physical media, and in-person communication [ – , , , , , – , , ]. Often, use of a combination of these modalities can be effective, e.g. distributing handbooks in addition to posting information online to ensure those with limited access to technology can still receive necessary information [ ]. 

## Digital media
Technology is a cornerstone of effective communication [ ]. Mass media can be used not only to draw attention but to highlight important safety information and risks. Organizers can leverage online presence such as social media, websites, and videos to communicate with specific audiences [ , , , , , ]. Dissemination of health and safety promotional messaging in partnership with television, radio, and film can expand message reach to broader audiences [ – , , ]. In the context of an MG sporting event, text messages were found to be the preferred method for communicating with attendees [ ]. 

## Physical media
Physical media, such as public signage, can successfully convey risks and important safety information [ , , , ]. The provision of printed handouts such as brochures or pamphlets are useful for disseminating risk information, particularly prior or during MG events [ , ]. Branded merchandise that reinforces public safety campaigns also has been successfully used to communicate risks [ ]. Between event organizers, procedural handbooks may clarify risks, designation of roles, and protocols during event planning [ , ]. 

## In-person communication
In-person communication strategies were referenced in four articles [ , , , ]. This includes direct interpersonal counseling or consultation with health care providers, family, or friends to describe risks of attending large MGs such as the Hajj pilgrimage [ ]. Broad dissemination of information, particularly during emergencies, can be supported through public address systems [ , , ]. 

## Discussion
Understanding the factors that shape public perceptions of MG risks is central to public health preparedness, as risk perception is a well-established precursor in decision making and behaviors [ , ]. Risk perception is the subjective judgment of the severity of a risk, formed through how individuals recognize, interpret, and process information to draw conclusions about potential harm. Within disaster and crisis research, perceived risk is a key predictor of preparedness [ , – ]. Collectively, these frameworks conceptualize risk perception as arising from interconnected cognitive and affective processes, including appraisals of susceptibility and severity, perceived benefits and barriers, and confidence in one’s ability to enact protective behaviors. These appraisals are further shaped by contextual influences such as environmental cues, sociocultural factors, and the credibility of the information source [ – ]. Consistent with this broader literature, factors influencing public risk perceptions in MG settings can be organized into three domains: individual-, crowd-, and event-level determinants.
Among these factors, most studies emphasized event-level characteristics, including event type, physical venue, environmental conditions, and visible safety infrastructure, underscoring how contextual cues shape perceived likelihood and severity of harm. Crowd-level factors — such as crowd dynamics, crowd density, and shared social identity — were also frequently discussed. In the context of MG disasters, a shared social identity may foster collective action and mutual support and strengthen cooperation between emergency responders and the public [ ]. At the same time, shared social identity may attenuate perceived susceptibility to health and safety risks, as greater collective trust can diffuse individual caution and lower personal risk appraisal [ , , ]. Taken together, these findings reinforce broader disaster and crisis research that social and environmental cues interact with cognitive and emotional evaluations in shaping risk perception.
Far fewer studies examined individual-level determinants of risk perception, despite their centrality in behavioral theory [ , ]. Education — both event-specific messaging and general health literacy — was the most consistently reported individual factor. However, individual characteristics widely supported in the broader disaster and crisis risk perception literature as predictors, mediators, and amplifiers of perceived risk were largely underexplored in MG contexts [ , ]. These include demographic factors (e.g., age, income, gender), psychological attributes (e.g., hazard-related anxiety, trust in authorities, confidence in information sources), and prior direct or indirect hazard experiences [ , , , ]. Notably, prior experiences, often cited as one of the strongest predictors of perception and preparedness behaviors, was absent from MG studies [ , ]. Given that many MGs are recurring events (e.g., pilgrimages, concerts, parades), understanding how accumulated life experiences shape receptivity to risk communication messaging may be critical for improving tailored, context-sensitive communication strategies.
In the context of MGs, risk communication strategies aim to provide credible, clear, and cross-cutting messaging that are accessible and appropriate for at-risk populations, often from diverse communities [ ]. Each component of a well-crafted risk communication strategy, namely, communication flow, timing, content and modalities, must be considered to maximize message receptivity and informed decision-making [ , ]. With respect to communication flow, establishing efficient communication channels far in advance often requires significant time and effort, but lack of proper communication between event organizers, public health and safety agencies and first responders or poor communication with attendees at MGs can have catastrophic consequences [ ]. Clear and consistent content that is responsive to audience characteristics (e.g., language, culture, and age-appropriate messaging) and attuned to event-specific features (e.g., physical environment, anticipated crowd size, and crowd dynamics) enhances receptivity and increase the likelihood of protective behavior adoption [ , , ]. Timing of message delivery is an equally important component of risk communication, whether before, during or after an event. Risk communication efforts should leverage the expanding array of communication platforms to broaden message dissemination and engagement. Innovations in technology-facilitated communication have increasingly enhanced the accessibility, availability, and speed at which the public seeks health and safety information [ ]. 
Nevertheless, not all modes of communication are accessible to everyone, making it advisable to incorporate multiple modalities to ensure the greatest reach. For example, while newspapers or television may represent mainstays of risk communication for older populations, text messaging or social media may be much more suitable for younger generation attendees. Social media has assumed an increasingly prominent role in enhancing rapid information circulation, direct communication, and multi-directional communication between authorities and the public [ , ]. However, ensuring accurate information from only trustworthy sources is conveyed remains a challenge. Event organizers may consider strengthening their presence across multiple social media platforms prior to MG events to build source credibility and audience trust. Partnerships with traditional and digital media outlets may further enhance reach and message consistency [ ]. Such proactive strategies can strengthen coordination and real-time information flow among event organizers, public health and safety agencies, first responders, general public and attendees.

## Research gaps
Overall, there is limited robust evidence addressing the effectiveness of MG risk communication strategies. Most literature were descriptive case studies focused on event preparedness or crisis-response systems of a specific event, rather than formal evaluations of communications effectiveness. To advance evidence-based practice, future research should assess both proximal (e.g., message reach, comprehension, knowledge) and distal outcomes (e.g., behavior change, health and safety outcomes).
In this review, only one study examined differences in risk perception between individuals who did and did not seek pre-MG health education from their physician; however, the lack of comparability among groups and potential variations in physician counseling limit the strength of its conclusions [ ]. More rigorous evaluation designs are needed. Ideally, studies should assess outcomes both before and after an intervention to determine whether observed changes can be attributed to the intervention itself. For example, educational initiatives could be evaluated by measuring changes in participants’ knowledge of risks, preparedness behaviors, and mitigation strategies over time. Even still, gains in knowledge alone may not translate into behavioral change, adoption of health protective behaviors or improved health outcomes.
Furthermore, to assess various risk communication strategies, comparative evaluations of different communication systems and formats in MGs are needed [ , ]. Surveys assessing message reach, comprehension, and influence on subsequent behaviors could aid in determining the relative utility of the growing number of communication platforms, particularly those enabled by widespread digital technology. The development and use of standard evaluation frameworks and outcome measures would facilitate comparison across studies, event types, and audiences. Additionally, mixed-methods and qualitative research with attendees, organizers, public health practitioners, and first responders/medical personnel can provide a more nuanced understanding of how communication strategies are received and acted upon in real-world MG settings.
From a policy and practice perspective, this review underscores the need to refine evidence-based guidance for risk communication in MGs that account for variation in attendee characteristics, event features, and environmental considerations. Sustained investment in surveillance and real-time communication infrastructure that extends beyond individual MG events may offer more sustainable and cost-effective solutions, reducing the need to re-establish communication systems for each event. Further, strengthening coordination among host community public health authorities, event organizers, emergency response systems, and hospitals or medical resources is also critical. Establishing clear protocols, defined roles, and appropriate allocation of resources – supported by sufficient training, simulations, and risk assessment – is essential for preparedness. These measures support the delivery of consistent and credible messaging between hosts and attendees, and timely, coordinated responses during MGs.

## Strengths and limitations
This review has several limitations. First, given the interdisciplinary nature of risk communication and MG research, multiple high-quality databases across diverse fields were searched. Some relevant studies, including non-English studies or those in the grey literature, may not have been captured. Second, MG disaster prevention, preparedness, and response involve a wide range of stakeholders across sectors and disciplines; however, standardized terminology is not well established. Although the search strategy incorporated a comprehensive set of terms describing various MG-related outcomes (e.g., stampede, crowd crush), some events, including protests and other spontaneous and unplanned MGs, may not have been captured if described using only event-specific terminology or broader public health emergency terms not explicitly linked to MGs. Last, as with other scoping reviews, thematic synthesis is subject to interpretive variation. In emerging research areas characterized by heterogeneous study designs and conceptual approaches, this remains salient. Nevertheless, a transparent and systematic search and analytic process enhance the rigor and reproducibility of the findings, while allowing necessary flexibility given the relatively limited number of studies, particularly those involving primary data collection.

## Conclusions
Although numerous reviews have examined risk communication in the context of public health emergencies, disasters, and crises, this is the first, to our knowledge, to focus specifically on risk communication within MG contexts. To this end, the included studies span diverse global MG events and highlight emerging commonalities in disaster preparedness and risk communication strategies relevant to event organizers, researchers, practitioners, policymakers, emergency responders, and health systems. Together, the findings underscore that, as with other public health communication, risk perception is not uniform but is rather developed through both individual experiences and the social context, shared identities, and event environments. The extent to which these factors are contextualized within risk communication strategies for MGs impacts their effectiveness. While a variety of risk communication approaches have been employed in differing events, gaps remain in determining effectiveness of the wide range of risk communication strategies used for MGs. Synthesis of relevant evidence highlights the need for more targeted research to identify determinants of risk perception and communication effectiveness applicable to mass gatherings.
