## Abstract

## Background
Firearm thefts from motor vehicles are currently the leading source of stolen firearms in the United States. Yet little is known about the temporal, spatial, and contextual characteristics of these thefts. This study aims to advance understanding of firearm theft from vehicles by analyzing all reported incidents that occurred in San Antonio, Texas, in 2024.

## Methods
Data are extracted from police reports from all reported incidents of firearm theft from vehicles reported to the San Antonio Police Department between January 1 and December 31, 2024. Information on the number and type of firearms stolen, vehicle make and model, date, and location of the incident was analyzed.

## Results
In 2024, 2,465 firearms were reported stolen across 2,210 separate incidents. Most incidents (91.7%) involved the theft of a single firearm. Handguns represented 92.1% of all stolen firearms. Firearm thefts were most common on weekends, particularly Saturdays, and were more prevalent in April, July, and October. Pickup trucks were the most frequent type of vehicle with a firearm theft reported (68.9%). The most common locations of thefts included vehicles parked at hotels (24.6%), shopping centers (19.8%), and private residences (16.0%).

## Conclusions
The results reveal distinct patterns in the timing, location, and types of vehicles associated with firearm thefts. The findings hold potential implications for strategies to reduce firearm thefts from vehicles, such as targeted public awareness campaigns, promoting vehicle secure storage, and place-based policing strategies.

## Introduction
As of 2023, there were nearly 400 million firearms in circulation in the United States (US), [ ] with approximately 40% of households reporting firearm ownership [ ]. Alongside the high levels of firearm ownership, the US also experiences significant firearm-related harms. For instance, firearms are used in nearly 20,000 homicides annually [ ]—accounting for approximately 80% of all homicides [ ]—and tens of thousands more incidents of nonfatal firearm-related injury occur each year [ , ]. 
One theorized contributor to firearm-related harm is gun thefts [ ]. For instance, firearm theft may contribute to violence by channeling weapons into illegal markets, where they become accessible to individuals who might otherwise face barriers to purchase. Stolen firearms can circulate through social networks or informal street transactions, where they may be acquired by persons prohibited from legal ownership and ultimately used in assaults, robberies, or other violent crimes [ , ]. Even so, due to data limitations, quantifying the contribution of firearm thefts to violent crime is challenging. For instance, as Phillip Cook has previously explained, “an obvious question is what percentage of guns used in violent crime were stolen at some point in their history, or, relatedly, what percentage of such guns were diverted from legitimate use by theft. Unfortunately, the data to answer these questions either do not exist or are not available to researchers [ ].”
Each year, hundreds of thousands of firearms are estimated to be stolen nationwide. The Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, and Firearms (ATF) estimates that between 2017 and 2021 alone, there were over 1 million firearm thefts reported, with over 96% of thefts occurring from private citizens [ ]. Among the various settings where firearm theft occurs, motor vehicles have emerged as the leading source for stolen firearms in the US. A report by Everytown for Gun Safety analyzing data using the FBI’s National Incident-Based Reporting System (NIBRS) from 337 law enforcement agencies across 44 states estimated that nearly half of all firearm thefts in the US occurred from vehicles [ ]. Similarly, firearm thefts from vehicles are a growing issue over time. A recent report by the Council on Criminal Justice, drawing on data from 2,098 law enforcement agencies that report to the NIBRS, representing roughly 12% of agencies nationwide, found that gun thefts from vehicles rose by 31% between 2018 and 2022, with most thefts occurring from vehicles parked at residences, and thefts from vehicles in parking lots or garages having increased by 76% over the study period [ ]. 
Although, these findings offer valuable insights into the overall scale of firearm thefts from vehicles, there is a paucity of research that provides insights into the granular detail of firearm theft from vehicle events, such as the temporal and spatial characteristics of firearm theft incidents within a city, the types of firearms most commonly stolen and types of vehicles subject to firearm thefts, and the features of locations where theft incidents occur. To address this gap, the present study analyzes data on all incidents of firearm theft from vehicles in San Antonio, Texas, reported to the San Antonio Police Department (SAPD) in 2024. The aims of the current study are to: (a) to document temporal (monthly and day of the week) trends in reported firearm thefts from vehicles; (b) to describe key characteristics of theft events, including type of firearms most commonly stolen, and the vehicle types most commonly subject to thefts, and (c) to examine the spatial distribution of firearm thefts across the city and the types of locations where firearm thefts from vehicles most commonly occur.

## Data
This study uses data on all reported firearm thefts from vehicles to the SAPD from January 1, 2024, to December 31, 2024. With a population of 1,526,656 as of the 2024 American Community Survey, [ ] San Antonio is the seventh-largest city in the US and the second-largest city in Texas [ ]. San Antonio is a city within Bexar County, Texas, the 16th -largest county nationally, with a population of 2,127,737 according to the 2024 American Community Survey [ ]. While city-level firearm ownership rates are not systematically collected, approximately 37% of households in Texas are estimated to own at least one firearm, [ ] and the state is characterized as having relatively permissive firearm laws [ ]. Notably, an analysis by Everytown for Gun Safety found that San Antonio had the fifth-highest rate of firearm thefts from vehicles among a study of 337 cities [ ]. 
Data used in this study are from SAPD police reports in cases where a firearm was reported stolen from a vehicle and includes: (a) the number of firearms reported stolen in a given incident, (b) the date the firearm theft incident occurred, (c) law enforcement categorizations of the firearm type (i.e., handgun, rifle, shotgun) based on the victim report of the firearm type, make and model, which are entered into police reports using the Mark43 system, [ ] (d) the year, make, and model of the vehicle which the firearm was stolen from, and (e) the address as well as longitude and latitude coordinates (produced via Google Geocoding API) of where the firearm theft incident occurred. The current study received approval from the University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston Committee on Protection of Human Subjects (HSC-SPH-24–0345).

## Methods
We first present the distribution of the number of firearms stolen from vehicles in 2024. Next, using the date of the incident, we examine patterns of firearm theft incidents by month and day of the week. Firearms were categorized in police reports as (a) handgun (including handguns, pistols, and revolvers), (b) rifle, (c) shotgun, or (d) missing (i.e., cases where insufficient detail was available within police reports to categorize the type of stolen firearm). Next, the research team used information on the vehicle make and model to categorize vehicles into distinct categories, as well as examine the makes and models most commonly involved in firearm thefts from vehicles. The research team conducted online searches based on the vehicle make and model, and using information and descriptions on the manufacturer’s website, categorized vehicles into the following categories: Car, Pickup Truck, sport utility vehicle (SUV), Van, Motorcycle, Trailer, and Semi-Trailor. The vehicle make and model in each category are provided in Appendix A. In some circumstances in the data, more than one vehicle was listed within a specific incident case number, suggesting that firearms were stolen from multiple vehicles within a particular incident. When this happened, a vehicle was considered unique if, within a specific incident case number, there was more than one vehicle listed with a firearm theft and those vehicles differed in the (a) year, (b) make, and/or (c) model.
Next, we used the longitude and latitude coordinates of the theft location provided by the SAPD to generate a heat map, which visually depicts the concentrations of thefts across the city. Each specific incident included a unique address matched to a set of longitude and latitude coordinates. Finally, to identify the types of locations where firearm thefts were most likely to occur, we used the addresses associated with each theft, searched them on Google/Google Maps, and manually coded the corresponding locations. Through an inductive coding process, these locations were then grouped into categories described in Appendix B.

## Results
Table  presents the patterns and characteristics of firearm thefts from vehicles. In 2024, there were 2,210 reported incidents of vehicle firearm theft and 2,465 total firearms reported stolen from vehicles. Overall, 2,027 (91.7%) firearm theft incidents involved the theft of a single firearm, 139 incidents (6.3%) involved the theft of two firearms, and 32 incidents (1.4%) involved the theft of three firearms; 12 (0.5%) incidents involved the theft of four or more firearms. Firearm theft incidents fluctuated slightly on a month-to-month basis, with the lowest incident months being May (n = 129), November (n = 147), and December (n = 154), and the highest months being April (n = 250), July (n = 217), and October (n = 212). By day of the week, firearm thefts were most commonly reported on Fridays through Sundays (54.3% of all incidents occurred on weekends), with Saturdays (n = 437) being the most common day for theft incidents. Across weekdays, thefts were slightly higher on Mondays (n = 292), compared to Tuesdays (n = 244), Wednesdays (n = 222), or Thursdays (n = 251).
 Among the 2,465 firearms stolen from vehicles in 2024, handguns – including pistols and revolvers – made up the vast majority of firearms stolen from vehicles (n = 2,270; 92.1%), with stolen rifles (n = 116; 4.7%) and shotguns (n = 70; 2.8%) being comparatively rare. There were 2,215 vehicles from which at least one firearm was reported stolen. Thefts from pickup trucks were most common (n = 1,527; 68.9%), followed by cars (n = 375; 16.8%), and SUVs (n = 267; 12.1%). Table  lists the make and model of vehicles most subject to firearm theft. Among pickup trucks, the Ford F Series (F-150, F-250, F-350, or F-450) accounted for 35.6% of all thefts from pick-up trucks, followed by Chevrolet Silverado (21.1%), Dodge Ram (20.6%), and GMC Sierra (12.5%). Among cars, the most common thefts occurred from the Dodge Charger (15.0%) and the Toyota Camry (7.0%). Among SUVs, the Chevrolet Suburban had the most thefts (11.2%), followed by the Chevrolet Tahoe (7.1%). Figure  displays the distribution of model years associated with vehicle thefts, showing that most thefts are newer models, with 64.2% of vehicle thefts occurring in model years between 2017 and 2024, and the most common model years of firearm thefts being 2019 (n = 211) and 2021 (n = 200).
 Visually, Fig.  displays a heat map of the distribution of firearm thefts from vehicles throughout San Antonio, which shows several notable patterns. First, most thefts occur near major highway corridors (indicated by dark grey lines), particularly along major interstate highways, including the I-410 and I-35 corridors. The densest concentrations of firearm thefts from vehicles occur in areas in several northern and central commercial corridors, including major city shopping centers such as The Rim, La Cantera, Legacy Shopping Center, Alamo Ranch, Westover Marketplace, Park North, and North Star Mall. These locations include key features such as large parking lots, retail centers, and entertainment venues (i.e., movie theaters, arcades). In addition, the Park North (2 miles; 4 min according to Google Maps) and North Star Mall (1.4 miles; 4 min according to Google Maps) are also near the San Antonio International Airport and are areas with a high density of hotels. Finally, there is also a large amount of firearm thefts in the downtown area, which includes a mixture of locations that draw a high level of thefts, including bars and restaurants, parking lots/garages, hotels, and apartment complexes. Smaller pockets of activity are also visible in mixed-use neighborhoods on the east and west sides of the city.
 Finally, to better understand the type of area where firearms are most stolen, addresses were searched for each location on Google/Google Maps and manually coded. Using an inductive coding process, the location types were categorized into groups (see Appendix B). The results in Table  showed that the that the most common area for thefts include: hotels (n = 543), shopping centers (n = 438), private residences (n = 353), restaurants/bars (n = 265), apartment complexes (n = 251), private businesses (n = 199), and parking lots/garages (n = 66). Table  presents the percentage of firearm thefts across locations by day of the week. The results show that most locations experienced elevated levels of theft on weekends, including hotels, shopping/retail centers, private residences, restaurant/bars, and parking lot/garages. Apartment complexes experienced higher levels of theft on Mondays.
 

## Supplemental analyses
As a supplemental analysis, we obtained vehicle registration data for the City of San Antonio from the Texas Department of Transportation, which included information on 2,323,771 unique vehicles registered to addresses within the City of San Antonio in 2024. In Appendix C, we present firearm theft rates per 10,000 registered vehicles. The analysis is limited to the five most common vehicle makes and models involved in firearm thefts, all of which are pickup trucks, as smaller sample sizes could produce biased results. After adjusting for the number of registered vehicles, the GMC Sierra had the highest rate of firearm thefts (89.6 per 10,000), followed by the Dodge Ram (65.6 per 10,000), Ford F-Series (56.1 per 10,000), Chevrolet Silverado (46.6 per 10,000), and Toyota Tundra (23.6 per 10,000). However, we consider this analysis supplemental, as firearm thefts may also involve vehicles registered outside the city. Nonetheless, it is important to present these results to account for the prevalence of vehicles registered within San Antonio.

## Discussion
The current study expands prior research on firearm thefts from vehicles [ , ] by analyzing patterns of reported firearm thefts from vehicles in San Antonio, Texas, in 2024. To our knowledge, these findings represent the first study that has provided granular insights on key features of firearm thefts from vehicles, including within-year temporal trends (by month and day of the week), details on the types of firearms stolen, types of vehicles subject to theft, and detailed geospatial analysis of patterns of thefts within a city.
In 2024, 2,465 firearms were reported stolen from vehicles to the SAPD. This translates to approximately 6.7 firearms stolen from vehicles per day on average. Results revealed that over 90% of incidents involved the theft of a single firearm and that handguns were the most commonly stolen firearm type. Although comprehensive data on firearm ownership in San Antonio are lacking, handguns may be more likely to be left unattended in vehicles parked in public places because they are easier to store in vehicles and are more common to publicly carry than long guns. They may also be more susceptible to theft because they are easier to conceal following a theft than long guns. Future research should examine how firearm owners store different types of firearms in vehicles and whether perpetrators of vehicle theft intentionally target specific weapons or rely on cues (i.e., firearm decal on vehicle) suggesting that handguns are present.
Firearm thefts also varied temporally. The findings reveal that firearm thefts from vehicles were most concentrated on Fridays through Sunday. This temporal clustering is consistent with prior research that documents elevated risks of general theft [ , ] and motor vehicle theft [ ] during weekends. One likely explanation is opportunity, considering that weekends are characterized by increased social gatherings outside the home, and vehicle traffic at locations such as hotels, shopping centers, restaurants, bars, parking garages, and public street parking – settings that our results also identified as common sites for theft. Notably, among the weekdays, Monday also emerged as a higher risk day for firearm thefts, and thefts on Mondays were higher at apartment complexes than other locations. One explanation may be that offenders exploit predictable routines, such as residents leaving or returning for work after the weekend, and leaving vehicles unoccupied for extended periods at home. Alternatively, this pattern may reflect thefts that occurred over the weekend but were not discovered or reported until the beginning of the work week.
The findings also showed that firearm thefts were highest in April, July, and October. The reasons for the emergence of greater firearm thefts in these months are unclear. However, it may be that these months correspond with major holidays and festivals that draw large public celebrations, including Fiesta® San Antonio in April, which is a two-week festival that brings approximately 2.5 million individuals to the city and generates over $340 million in local activity, [ ] the 4th of July, and Día de los Muertos/Halloween celebrations in October. Future research examining how trends of firearm theft fluctuate alongside the dates of holidays and festivals with large public gatherings would be valuable for examining whether these periods of heightened social activity create increased opportunities for theft due to higher vehicle density, longer periods of unattended parking, and greater movement of people across public spaces. Examining whether periods with spikes in firearm theft also correspond with increases in violent crime and shootings is an important direction for future research.
Theft incidents were concentrated in pickup trucks – especially Ford F-Series, Chevrolet Silverado, GMC Sierra, and Dodge Ram models. While the precise reasons for thefts from these vehicles are unclear, several possibilities exist for why pickup trucks are more frequently targeted. Pickup trucks are popular in Texas and much of the southern United States. Therefore, they represent a large share of the overall vehicle fleet and, by extension, potential theft opportunities. Pickup trucks often have larger cabins, center consoles, and glove compartments that may enable easier storage of firearms, which might be more challenging for other vehicle types. It is also possible that firearm owners (or firearm owners who store in vehicles) are more likely to drive pickup trucks than other vehicle types. Although, to our knowledge, no research has established an association between firearm ownership and the type of vehicle owned. In addition, it is possible that offenders may perceive pickup trucks as more likely to contain firearms.
Across places, firearm thefts were concentrated in areas with large concentrations of hotels, shopping/retail areas, and restaurants/bars, as well as near private residences. Notably, areas that are leading spots for firearm theft, including hotels, shopping centers, and apartment complexes, share characteristics such as large surface-level parking lots and/or parking garages where people leave their vehicles unattended for extended periods. These locations are often situated in higher socioeconomic areas of the city, including many that feature major restaurants and higher-end retail chains (e.g., The Rim, La Cantera, Westover Marketplace, North Star Mall, Park North). These findings suggest that areas that are subject to firearm theft from vehicles may differ from crime hotspots associated with firearm violence.
The geospatial patterning of these findings can be understood within the context of theories about crime location choice [ ]. For instance, routine activities theory [ ] suggests that for a crime to occur, three factors must converge in time and space: a motivated offender, a suitable target, and the absence of a capable guardian. Leading locations of theft include places (e.g., shopping centers, hotels) that create environments prime for motivated offenders targeting vehicles for theft, particularly in areas with high volumes of vehicles left unattended for extended periods, coupled with limited supervision relative to the large number of vehicles parked. The findings can also be interpreted within the context of crime pattern theory [ ]. Central to this perspective is the “geometry of crime,” which suggests that offenders are not randomly searching across a city but instead focus on areas within their awareness space where targets align with their learned templates of what constitutes a favorable crime opportunity. Awareness spaces are formed through people’s routine activities, such as where they live, work, shop, or socialize, and the routes that connect these nodes. Within these spaces, cues in the built environment can signal opportunities that match an offender’s expectations of a suitable target. Applied to firearm thefts from vehicles, this framework suggests that hotels, shopping centers, residential areas, and apartment complexes become high-risk places because they function as both activity nodes and environmental cues. They generate large volumes of traffic and parked vehicles (crime generators) and simultaneously present recurring opportunities for theft, particularly when firearms are stored in vehicles (crime attractors). Additionally, crime pattern theory suggests that these selected locations are close to the offenders’ routine activity nodes. Thoroughly testing these theories requires future research with information on offender characteristics and how offenders perceive and navigate locations that shape the selection of targets for firearm thefts from vehicles [ , ]. 
Overall, the findings of this study also have implications for policy and practice aimed at reducing vehicle firearm thefts. One promising approach is to conduct public awareness campaigns that highlight the risks associated with storing firearms in vehicles. These messages could be tailored to high-risk groups, such as owners of pickup trucks, and target specific locations where thefts are especially common, including hotels and large retail shopping centers [ ]. While the current study does not include information on how firearms were stored at the time of theft, it is possible that using storage devices, such as lockboxes or cable locks, in vehicles could help reduce thefts by making firearms more difficult to steal. Understanding firearm owners’ preferences for secure storage devices in vehicles and identifying ways to encourage their use is a critical direction of future research [ , ]. Moreover, the observed concentration of thefts in certain locations and on specific days of the week suggests opportunities for place-based prevention strategies. These may include the installation and deployment of mobile watch towers in public parking spaces, [ ] or focused law enforcement approaches such as hotspot policing in areas with high rates of theft to increase capable guardianship [ ]. However, to optimize strategies aimed at reducing firearm thefts from vehicles, future research is needed to understand why individuals store firearms in vehicles (e.g., for safety; self-protection; lack of awareness about risks) and the circumstances under which they choose to do so, which can inform the design of targeted interventions that address both behavioral motivations and environmental risk factors.

## Limitations
This study has limitations that should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. First, the analysis is based on data from a single city. Relatedly, the SAPD jurisdiction covers the City of San Antonio; thus, firearm theft data for all of Bexar County was unavailable. Future research should replicate this approach in other jurisdictions and prioritize developing multi-jurisdiction datasets with detailed information on the location and the vehicle involved in each firearm theft. Second, the data includes only firearm thefts reported to law enforcement. Because not all crimes are reported to the police, this may result in an underestimate of the number of incidents. Relatedly, potential demographic and geographic biases in reporting behavior (i.e., some populations may be more or less likely to report firearm thefts) could affect the spatial distribution of observed incidents. Third, the dataset lacks information on where firearm theft victims live relative to where the theft occurred. This spatial relationship is important for guiding targeted public awareness campaigns and assessing the impact of policies, such as local ordinances that regulate firearm storage in vehicles, or determining whether a disproportionate number of firearm theft victims reside outside the city limits. This is also particularly important given that hotels were a leading location of firearm thefts, suggesting that many of those who experienced a theft from a vehicle may not live within the city. Fourth, the study examined thefts occurring over a single year. Accordingly, future research should investigate multi-year temporal trends in vehicle firearm thefts and evaluate whether the characteristics and geographic patterns of these incidents vary over time. Fifth, although the overwhelming majority of firearms stolen from vehicles were handguns, the reasons underlying this pattern remain unclear. Because data on firearm ownership by type in San Antonio are unavailable, it is not possible to determine whether this reflects higher rates of handgun ownership in the area. Alternatively, the pattern may stem from handguns being more frequently stored in unoccupied vehicles or from handguns being disproportionately targeted for theft compared to long guns. Finally, the precise time of day when a firearm was stolen was unavailable. Such information would be important for determining whether firearm thefts are concentrated during daytime versus nighttime hours, and how those patterns vary by day of the week and location of the firearm theft.

## Conclusion
This study presents a novel examination of the patterns and characteristics of firearm thefts from vehicles within a large urban setting with high rates of such thefts. Moving forward, future research is needed to understand why and how firearm owners store firearms in vehicles, their awareness of the risks, and what indicators signal opportunities for firearm theft. Such information can be valuable for crafting potential solutions that advance public health and safety goals.
