## Abstract

## Rationale
Help-seeking can convert an individual's bonding social capital into social support, which has been shown to buffer the impact of psychological distress. The younger generation (individuals aged 15–35 years) have been the least likely to actively seek help despite facing a rising burden of mental health problems. COVID-19 pandemic restrictions may have altered their help-seeking behaviors, but the extent of such shift remains little understood, particularly in Asian contexts.

## Objective
To understand how the younger generation's patterns of help-seeking (activation of different combinations of support sources) have shifted in pandemic times, who have experienced the shift, and what explanatory factors are involved.

## Methods
Data were obtained from two waves (2019, 2020) of online survey responses by 438 community-dwelling younger generation people in Hong Kong, recruited through the authors' affiliated institutions and territory-wide community outreach organizations. Latent class analysis was conducted on participants' self-reported help-seeking behaviors in each survey wave. Constituents' characteristics in each latent class were examined, and between-wave changes in individuals’ class membership were identified. Logistic regressions identified explanatory factors that significantly explained the changes.

## Results
Three consistent patterns of help-seeking were identified in both survey waves. A major shift was observed for individuals with poorer mental health histories who faced moderate distress. They relied on their family, friends, and partner pre-pandemic, but no longer activated these supports during the pandemic. Posting status updates on social media, along with various communication habits and sociodemographic factors that differed by age group, were associated with this shift.

## Conclusions
Shifts in the younger generation's patterns of help-seeking may be an early warning signal to invest additional resources in facilitating help-seeking among the younger generation. Findings also serve as a reminder that public health restrictions may have inadvertent mental health implications that should be considered in future scenarios.

## Introduction
Evidence accumulated over the years has indicated that strong social relationships are linked to better individual mental health outcomes ( ; ; ). One way to understand this link is through the lens of bonding social capital ( ). Individuals invest time engaging in shared activities together, through which they strengthen their social bonds over time, and build a sense of relatedness and trust with one another. In turn, when the individual faces psychological distress (e.g., from stressful life events), they can seek help from these social connections. Here, the help-seeking process can be conceived as a social mechanism whereby one's bonding social capital is converted into various forms of social support – emotional support, informational assistance (e.g., referral to trusted professionals), and instrumental support ( ) – which altogether buffer the psychological impact of the episode of distress ( ) before it intensifies and develops into mental disorders ( ; ; ). The term help-seeking in this article encompasses support-seeking from close/strong ties, typically family members, friends, and partners, as well as weak ties, who in this context are formal, more distant sources of support such as social workers, mental health professionals, and social service personnel.
This help-seeking process, however, may need to be actively initiated by the distressed individual themselves ( ). In this regard, youth and young adults have consistently been the least likely demographic groups to seek help for mental health problems ( ; Picco et al., 2016; ) despite clear evidence that mental disorders in early life implicate profound socioeconomic and health consequences in later adult life ( ; ; ) and that most mental disorders occur by young adulthood ( ; ), with first and second onset peaks at ages 14.5 and 30.5 years respectively ( ). In this paper, youth are defined as individuals aged 15–24 years ( ), whereas young adults refer to individuals aged 25–35 years, following Arnett's rationale that young adulthood follows the period of emerging adulthood which itself ends by the age of 25 ( ). Hereafter, due to lack of proper terminology in the literature, the term younger generation (YG) is adopted to collectively refer to both groups combined.
Extant literature indicates that the YG exhibited specific patterns of help-seeking; that is, they activated different combinations of support sources, depending on the intensity of psychological distress. The YG typically started seeking help once confronted with moderate distress, but mostly sought informal support from their close ties such as family members, friends, and partners. Formal support from sources such as hotline supports, social workers, and mental health professionals was generally sought only when their mental health problems became severe. Such help was typically sought in addition to informal help, suggesting that the YG regarded formal channels as complementary sources of support ( ; ). Thus, lowering barriers for help-seeking such as mental health-related stigma, mental health illiteracy, and lack of trust in mental health professionals ( ; ; ; ), has been a major focus in the effort to tackle the rising burden of mental health problems facing the YG in recent years ( ), a public health challenge that has likely been exacerbated during the COVID-19 pandemic ( ; ).
Studies have suggested that having access to social support during the pandemic can moderate the impact of distress on an individual ( ; ; ; ). However, pandemic-related responses such as venue closures and limiting of social contacts and social activities have potentially altered how individuals experiencing psychological distress access social support through both formal and informal help-seeking. Despite this, the extent to which the YG's help-seeking behaviors have been affected over the pandemic has received little focus in scientific discourse.
A clearer understanding of shifts in the YG's patterns of help-seeking, along with their associated factors, is thus urgently needed in order to inform strategies to preserve the YG's mental well-being, and ultimately prevent the current pandemic from leading to a mental health pandemic. While related evidence for young populations in Western contexts are emerging ( ; ; ), the corresponding evidence for Asian contexts is critically lacking.
1.1.

## The present study
This study took place in Hong Kong, where local transmission of SARS-CoV-2 was confirmed on January 22, 2020. Subsequently the government introduced “social distancing” measures from January 29, 2020 in adherence to a zero-infection policy. The second half of the study period (2020) coincided with the height of the third wave of the pandemic in Hong Kong, which at that time was the most severe yet and had the tightest-ever distancing measures enforced. Authorities strongly urged people to stay home and avoid unnecessary social contact and activities. Schools, recreational spaces, sports premises, places of religious worship, and other common socializing venues were closed; dine-in hours for eateries were restricted; public gatherings were limited to two people; and wearing masks was mandatory in all public spaces ( ). There were, however, few disruptions to mental healthcare services such as hotline supports and psychiatric clinics.
Constant advocacy of “social distancing” potentially caused many people to internalize the avoidance of social contact. Venue restrictions, especially sports premises, cafes, and schools, could particularly impact the YG. For many, these venues were the safe spaces where they built and maintained bonding social capital and converted it, as needed, into social support through help-seeking ( ; ). Based on this premise, we hypothesized that individuals who only activated and sought help from their close ties would experience the largest decline in help-seeking.
Understanding the shifts in patterns of help-seeking among Hong Kong's youth and young adults during the COVID-19 pandemic, along with the factors explaining such shifts, might provide a lens to better understand behavioral changes among people of the same age group who face similar situations in other Asian societies. Findings from this study may thus provide broader insights into strategies for preserving mental well-beingamong youth and young adults in other Asian environments.

2.

## Methods
2.1.

## Study participants, procedures, and settings
As part of a broader cross-institutional initiative in early suicide prevention for Hong Kong's YG, longitudinal online surveys targeting community-dwelling individuals had been conducted annually from 2018 to 2020 to understand their well-being and help-seeking behaviors. This initiative was led by the Hong Kong Jockey Club Centre for Suicide Research and Prevention (CSRP) at the University of Hong Kong (HKU). There was an emphasis on identifying the needs and behaviors of at-risk individuals in the community who required more support, such as those who experienced bullying, family problems, previous suicidal behaviors, or psychiatric disorders. This study utilized data from survey waves in 2019 and 2020.
For each survey wave, links to the online survey were disseminated through posters and other promotional material, emails to members, newsletters, and Facebook and web pages of the authors' affiliated institutions. For maximum outreach to targeted participants, links to the survey were also disseminated as (i) poster promotions at branches of three major community outreach organizations, and (ii) notices to members of these organizations ( , Hong Kong Federation of Youth Groups, and The Boys' and Girls’ Clubs Association of Hong Kong). These organizations have service centres covering the whole Hong Kong territory that provide counselling and social work services to young people up to 35 years of age ( ; Caritas, n.d.; ).
Clicking the survey link would direct participants to a secure webpage containing the survey. Both Chinese and English versions were available. Participants were informed of the survey's purpose (gaining an in-depth understanding of the YG's general well-being), approximate survey duration (10 min), strict confidentiality of their data, and of their freedom to discontinue at any time. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant before they started the survey. Careful consideration was taken to ensure that survey questions would incur no risk and pose the least distress to participants. Contact information of emotional support hotlines and services were made available in the survey to encourage any distressed participant to seek support immediately.
All procedures and protocols adopted in this study were approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee for Non-Clinical Faculties of HKU under the reference number EA1709039. Consent from parents or legal guardians for under-aged participants was not required by the ethics committee as participation in this study was deemed to pose minimal risk.
The 2019 and 2020 survey waves were conducted from 5 June to July 8, 2019 and from 29 June to September 29, 2020 respectively. The 2020 survey wave coincided with the height of the third wave of the pandemic in Hong Kong during which the tightest yet distancing measures were enforced. Thus, the 2019 and 2020 survey waves provided measurements before and during the COVID-19 pandemic respectively, or alternatively, under the absence and presence of distancing measures. Only participants who completed both waves and were 15–35 years of age by the 2020 wave were included in the analyses.

2.2.

## Measures
Demographics. Questions on demographics included gender, age, education level, occupation, family structure, and living arrangement. Questions other than gender and age were categorical variables.
Daily communication habits. Participants were prompted with the question, “Consider your recent communication habits, how often do you use the following methods to communicate with others?” Eight different means of communication were provided: (i) “face-to-face communication”; (ii) “phone calls”; (iii) “messaging others through SMS”; (iv) “messaging others through instant chat applications, e.g., WhatsApp, Facebook Messenger, Line, WeChat”; (v) “status updates on social media platforms (Facebook, Instagram, Twitter, Weibo, WeChat moment)” (vi) “sharing on personal channel or website (e.g., original videos on YouTube, posts on personal blog);”; (vii) “commenting, posting new threads, or replying on online forums”; and (viii) “online live broadcasts (e.g., Facebook Live, YouTube Live, TikTok)”. Each response was scored on a five-point Likert scale from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“always”).
Psychological distress. There were two measures of distress in the survey. The first measure of distress was self-rated. Participants were asked to rate how much distress they experienced in eight aspects of their lives within the past four weeks: (i) academic; (ii) job; (iii) financial; (iv) social life (pertaining to colleagues, friends, classmates); (v) physical well-being; (vi) emotional well-being; (vii) relations with family; and (viii) relations with partner or spouse – from 1 (“not at all”) to 5 (“very serious”), or “not applicable”. For each participant, items with “not applicable” responses were excluded and the remaining values were averaged to obtain their overall self-rated distress.
The second measure of distress was the 12-Item Chinese Health Questionnaire (CHQ-12) ( ) which measured participants' psychological distress in the past two weeks. The scale has been widely used in assessing overall psychological health and screening for psychological distress among Chinese community-dwelling populations. Validations of CHQ-12 in multiple settings have demonstrated good psychometric properties, with specificity and sensitivity values between 0.70 and 0.95 ( ; ) and Cronbach's α of 0.83 ( ). Each item was scored on a four-point scale – from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“much more than usual”) – and summed up. Higher total score indicated greater psychological distress.
Mental health risks. Suicidal behaviors and diagnoses of psychiatric disorders were the main risks of interest in the survey. Participants' current suicide risk was measured with the five-item Suicidal Ideation Attributes Scale (SIDAS) (Van Spijker et al., 2014), which assessed severity of suicidal ideation over the past month based on the frequency and uncontrollability of suicidal thoughts, closeness to suicide attempt, and distress and interference brought about by suicidal thoughts. The five items were scored on an 11-point Likert format from 0 to 10 and summed. Higher total score indicates greater suicidal ideation. Validation of the scale within the Chinese context has shown a Cronbach's α of 0.84, indicating good internal consistency ( ). Participants' histories of suicidal behaviors were retrieved from the baseline 2018 survey, in which they were asked whether they had ever considered suicide, self-harmed, and attempted suicide in their lifetime. These questions had binary responses (“yes”/“no”).
A single binary-response question asked participants whether they had been diagnosed with any of major depressive disorder (MDD), schizophrenia, social phobia, or avoidant personality disorder in the past year. Similarly, participants’ histories of these disorders was also retrieved from the baseline 2018 survey, in which a single binary question asked about their lifetime diagnosis.
Self-reported help-seeking behaviors. Participants were asked on ten sources of support that they had sought to deal with distress within the past four weeks: (i) family members; (ii) friends, classmates, or colleagues; (iii) spouse or partner; (iv) teachers or tutors; (v) free hotline support; (vi) healthcare professionals; (vii) social workers or counsellors; (viii) religious services; (ix) online friends whom they had never met physically; and (x) online social services. All questions had binary responses.

2.3.

## Statistical analyses
Descriptive statistics of the study population were first calculated. Paired t-tests and Chi-squared tests examined trends in study variables between the 2019 and 2020 survey waves. Effect sizes of between-wave differences were quantified by Cohen's d and odds ratios. All analyses were conducted on R, and throughout the analyses, a p-value less than 0.05 indicated statistical significance.
We took a person-centred approach to characterize individuals in the study population based on their self-reported help-seeking behaviors. We conducted a latent class analysis (LCA) on study participants' activation of the ten help channels to identify these behavioral patterns, using the poLCA package ( ) in R version 4.0.3. A separate LCA was conducted for each survey wave in order to identify each individual's help-seeking pattern before and during the COVID-19 pandemic.
Each LCA started with a one-class model that was sequentially increased up to five classes to find the optimum number. At each subsequent step, the model was evaluated based on four model-fit parameters that had been established as the most reliable for LCA with categorical outcomes – the bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLR), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), sample size-adjusted BIC (SABIC), and the Lo–Mendell–Rubin (LMR) likelihood ratio test – as well as the interpretability of each class ( ; ; ). Each model iteration had to converge before being evaluated. The model yielding approximately the lowest BIC and SABIC, significant p-values for BLR and LMR tests, and an intuitive interpretation of help-seeking behaviors for each of its latent classes was chosen. The number of latent classes in this model would correspond to the number of unique patterns of help-seeking within the study population, and each individual would belong to exactly one class.
We then identified changes in participants' class membership, which denoted shifts in participants' help-seeking patterns between the two waves. Major shifts in the study population were identified, followed by logistic regression analyses to identify factors that could explain such shifts. Youth and young adults were analyzed in separate regressions. Records containing either an empty or “refuse to answer” response (n = 3, less than 1% of study population) were excluded. For each regression, a dummy variable denoted class change and was designated as the outcome variable, and covariates included sociodemographics, daily communication habits, overall self-rated distress, and mental health risks. Overall self-rated distress was used in the main analyses instead of CHQ-12 score as it had the same four-weeks’ timeframe as help-seeking behaviors.

## Descriptive statistics

In both waves, the most frequently used mode of communication was instant chat applications, followed by face-to-face communication and status updates on social media platforms. Frequency of face-to-face communication and online forum activities showed a statistically significant decrease in 2020. A decrease in average SIDAS scores was also observed. Importantly, even though the study population's overall self-rated distress and CHQ-12 score remained largely unchanged throughout both waves, the number of individuals who sought help in response to distress fell considerably – from 289 before the pandemic (66.0%) to 160 during the pandemic (36.5%). There was less activation of all help channels except online social services in 2020. In particular, help-seeking from family, friends, partner, and religious services channels significantly decreased between the two waves.

Of note is that the same observations for these communication habits remained when regression models included CHQ-12 scores instead of overall self-rated distress (see in Online Appendix).

4.

## Discussion
4.1.

## Summary of findings
This study adds new and important knowledge on changes in help-seeking behaviors of the younger generation (YG); that is, individuals 15–35 years of age, during the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic. While there have been related studies covering the same demographic groups in similar environments such as Chinese contexts ( ; ), these studies were nevertheless unable to fully unpack this phenomenon as their cross-sectional nature did not allow comparisons with pre-pandemic times and they only investigated help-seeking from formal sources.
The sample in this study was largely recruited through community outreach organizations, suggesting that it adequately represented community-dwelling youth (15-24 year-olds) and young adults (25-35 year-olds) in Hong Kong who were concerned about their mental health and well-being. High representation of females and prevalence of suicidal behavior histories in the study population concurred with established understanding that at-risk females are more inclined to seek such organizations for help ( ; ). 2019 and 2020 survey waves in this study corresponded to pre-pandemic times and the third wave of the pandemic, the most severe yet at the time, respectively. Corresponding with pandemic restrictions on social activities, the study population engaged in face-to-face communication less in 2020. However, there was no real increase in other modes of non-face-to-face communication, suggesting that the enforced reduction in direct social contact might not have been offset by alternative modes of communication.
Latent class analysis clearly categorized study samples into three patterns of help-seeking: 1) Non-seekers (NS), who sought minimal help and had relatively the lowest distress levels; 2) Inner circle seekers (IS), who activated their family, partner, and friendship ties, faced moderate distress, and had a worse mental health profile than NS; and 3) Diverse seekers (DS), who sought help from formal channels in addition to their close ties, and had far heavier distress and poorer mental health than IS. This gradient of help-seeking behaviors echoes findings from previous studies ( ; ; ) where individuals expanded the activation of their social convoy as they faced increasingly heavier distress, especially when they sought help from formal sources in addition to informal help from their close ties.
Individuals dealing with moderate psychological distress who used to seek support from their close ties before the pandemic were the ones whose help-seeking patterns were altered most during the pandemic. Approximately 57% of them no longer sought help, therefore lending support to this study's hypothesis that individuals who only relied on their close ties would experience the largest decline in help-seeking. In particular, the observed decrease in 2020 for general help-seeking as well as help-seeking from family members, friends, and partners were not evenly contributed by the whole study population but instead largely driven by these individuals. Studies covering young adults have similarly observed reduced help-seeking in the US ( ) and UK ( ), where findings in the latter were attributed to lack of access to both peer support and formal mental healthcare. Taken altogether, these findings add to an accumulating body of evidence that the YG irrespective of geographical and cultural contexts might have been rendered less capable of alleviating psychological distress during the pandemic. This study nevertheless adds new knowledge by highlighting that people's informal help-seeking might have been more adversely affected than their formal help-seeking during the pandemic.
Critically, underlying otherwise unchanged measures of distress among the aggregate study population were elevated measures of distress of IS during the pandemic compared to their counterparts in 2019. This might indicate an increased threshold/barrier for informal help-seeking during pandemic times, wherein a greater level of distress than before was required to spur youth and young adults in Hong Kong to start seeking help from their close ties. This phenomenon might have been a consequence of venue restrictions and public health messaging. Restrictions imposed on venues important for cultivating bonding social capital and serving as safe spaces for the YG to open up (e.g., sports premises, cafes, and schools) would limit their opportunities to seek help, whereas policymakers’ constant advocacy for “social distancing” might have been internalized by the YG, leading them to limit social contacts with others ( ; ).
Finally, conditional logistic regressions revealed that given equal levels of distress, and after controlling for other variables, factors explaining the dominant shift differed for different age groups. Male youths were much more likely than female youths to no longer seek help from their close ties, signifying that longstanding higher barriers to help-seeking among males ( ; ; ) persisted into the pandemic. Higher usage of SMS for youths, and content creation on personal online channels for young adults, were respectively associated with them no longer seeking help from close ties during the pandemic. Preference for SMS in the Internet age may suggest low extraversion ( ), a trait that could be tied to stronger reluctance to disclose one's distress to and seek help from others. Meanwhile, content creation and sharing on personal channels might be driven by motivations contrary to help-seeking, such as sharing information, promoting social contacts, and providing encouragement to one's followers ( ).
In contrast, increased frequency of posting status updates on social media platforms – Instagram, WeChat (Moments), Facebook, or Twitter – was associated with those YG classified as IS pre-pandemic still seeking help from their close ties during the pandemic. This observation echoes reports of the YG's prevailing habits of using social media to share their distress and seek social support in pandemic times ( ; ), which may point to a unique potential of status updates and Instagram stories in catalyzing help-seeking among today's YG. particularly documented the ubiquity with which the YG's friends would simply respond with a “PM me” to signal to the distressed individual that they were available to talk privately. This simple gesture could conveniently convert private chat inboxes into virtual safe spaces for the YG to comfortably open up ( ), thus facilitating distressed YG to seek help from their friends despite physical accessibility hurdles imposed by pandemic restrictions. This phenomenon warrants future investigation.

4.2.

## Study implications
Behavioral factors can act as protective buffers against adverse mental health effects of the COVID-19 pandemic and pandemic-era restrictions. While recent studies have shed light on the effects of behaviors such as physical activity, screen time, and snacking on mental health during the pandemic ( ), this study adds another dimension of behaviors to scholarly discourse, namely the activation of social support in response to distress. This mechanism could indirectly influence mental health outcomes by buffering the impact of psychological distress during the pandemic. Specifically, this study draws attention to individuals aged 15–35 years with moderate distress who had previously sought help from their close ties but stopped seeking help during pandemic times.
Even before the pandemic, the rising burden of mental health problems on the YG ( ) had already been compounded by their low tendency to seek help to deal with stressors (Picco et al., 2016; ). The observed regress by individuals facing moderate distress, from seeking close ties’ support in pre-pandemic times to not seeking help at all during the pandemic, would mean unbuffered psychological distress that could translate into a steep increase in the incidence of mental disorders among the YG. Therefore, this regress should act as an early warning signal for parents, teachers, clinicians, and policymakers to make timely investments in additional efforts to facilitate help-seeking among youth and young adults in this peri-pandemic world. Such efforts could be in the form of digital interventions incorporating personal involvement and narrative sharing ( ) and public health messaging encouraging the community to actively check in on their family and friends. Groups endorsing higher help-seeking stigma such as shier, introverted young men, should also be proactively targeted.
Findings reported in this article also indicate that policymakers should not overlook mental health implications when drafting pandemic restriction measures. Whilst socializing venues may be seen as high-risk venues for physical transmission of airborne viruses, they may also be safe spaces to bond and seek social support. Therefore, policymakers should consider promoting alternative contexts like online peer communities ( ) and Internet-delivered counselling ( ) to offset the loss of these safe spaces, such that people are not left even more vulnerable to psychological distress exerted by the pandemic and distancing measures. It may also be prudent for public health messaging to emphasize on “physical distancing” instead of “social distancing” since the latter, as scientists argued, could instil a sense of a break in social connections, invoke feelings of social isolation, and ultimately leave even more people vulnerable to adverse mental health effects of the pandemic ( ; ). Practising pandemic-related distancing should not mean losing social bonds; in fact, the opposite should occur during challenging times like the COVID-19 pandemic.

4.3.

## Limitations
There are three main limitations in this study. First, due to participant recruitment procedures, at-risk individuals with histories of mental health problems were more likely to be represented in the sample, and thus caution should be taken when generalizing the findings to the mentally healthy general young population. No question on ethnicity was also asked in the survey, and thus the proportion of ethnic minorities in the study population could not be inferred.
Second, Hong Kong was rocked by social unrest that lasted from mid-2019 to early 2020, the biggest demographic constituent of which were youth and young adults. The unrest might have changed relationships between individuals and their close ties. Since the data collection period for the 2019 survey wave only coincided with the first half of the social unrest, the possibility of the second half of the unrest confounding study participants’ help-seeking preferences in 2020 could not be ruled out.
The study's third limitation lies in its survey design. Questions on daily communication habits did not specify a definite timeframe. Additionally, survey questions did not ask the distresses for which participants specifically sought help from particular channels, thus precluding analyses on finer classifications between distress category and help channels sought, or the profiles of individuals with specific patterns of help-seeking. This should be further explored.

5.

## Conclusion
Availability of, and access to, social support allows a distressed individual to buffer the psychological impact of an episode of distress and prevent it from developing into mental disorders. This study describes the significant shifts in youth and young adults’ patterns of help-seeking under the COVID-19 pandemic. Findings here suggest that these shifts may be an early warning signal for stakeholders to invest additional resources to maintain and even improve opportunities for youth and young adults to seek help in challenging times such as a pandemic. At-risk groups could be proactively targeted using digital interventions and public health messaging to encourage more help-seeking.
Policymakers faced with a similar pandemic situation in the future should carefully monitor and appropriately attend to potential collateral damage of restriction measures on mental wellness, especially that of vulnerable members in the community. Such prudence would be integral in preventing a future physical illness pandemic from becoming a mental health pandemic.
