## Abstract

## Introduction
Addressing cigarette and e-cigarette use in China is key to reducing the global tobacco epidemic. Marketing exposure is one causal factor for adolescent smoking and e-cigarette use. Currently, China restricts cigarette and e-cigarette ads in public places and online; however, there may not be full policy compliance. We collected real-time data in the natural environment to estimate how much and where Chinese adolescents—a group susceptible to smoking and e-cigarette use—are exposed to cigarette and e-cigarette marketing to inform policy responses.

## Aims and Methods
In June 2022, we conducted a 7-day ecological momentary assessment (EMA) study with 15–16-year-olds (n = 96) across eight Chinese cities. Participants completed up to 42 EMA surveys (six per day), sent at random intervals outside of school hours. In each survey, participants reported whether they saw (1) displays and (2) ads in the past hour (none, cigarette, e-cigarette, both) in the past hour. We also captured the source of cigarette/e-cigarette ad exposure.

## Results
Most participants were exposed to cigarette and/or e-cigarette displays (89.6%) or ads (79.2%) more than 7 days. On average, participants reported past-hour exposure to displays 12.7 times and past-hour exposure to ads 10.8 times over the week. The most common sources of cigarette ads were public places (eg, kiosks, supermarkets); the most common sources of e-cigarette ad exposure were social media/internet or e-cigarette stores.

## Conclusions
Findings highlight the need to enhance enforcement of restrictions on cigarette and e-cigarette ads in public places and online in China and extend restrictions to ban displays.

## Implications
Marketing exposure is a causal factor in youth smoking and e-cigarette use. We used EMA to estimate cigarette and e-cigarette display and ad exposure among Chinese adolescents. On average, participants reported past-hour exposure to cigarette and/or e-cigarette displays 13 times and past-hour exposure to cigarette and/or e-cigarette ads 11 times more than 1 week. Most saw ads in public places and online. Results suggest strengthening implementation of China’s ban on cigarette and e-cigarette ads in public places and online and banning product displays. These are policy responses that can contribute to reducing adolescent cigarette and e-cigarette uptake in China.

## Introduction
Approximately 308 million Chinese adults smoke, representing roughly one-third of all people who smoke around the world. , Reducing cigarette use in China through evidence-based tobacco control policies is an important part of addressing the global tobacco epidemic. Cigarette sales are restricted to individuals under 18 years old ; however, most people who smoke in China start smoking daily before age 20. The latest data from the National Youth Tobacco Survey indicates that 4.2% of senior high school students and 12.1% of vocational high school students smoked cigarettes in 2021. In addition, roughly 10 million Chinese adults currently used e-cigarettes in 2019, with the highest rate of use among 15–24-year-olds. 
Marketing exposure—in the form of ads and product displays—is one driver of adolescent smoking, and the link between youth marketing exposure and future cigarette and e-cigarette use is well-documented, particularly in Western countries like the United States. , Young people who live in areas with a higher density of retail advertising for cigarettes and e-cigarettes have higher odds of cigarette and e-cigarette use. , Exposure to retail ads and point-of-sale displays for both cigarettes and e-cigarettes can also increase susceptibility to use among youth. Comprehensive policies that restrict ads and displays have the potential to reduce youth uptake of cigarettes and e-cigarettes. , , 
In 2015, Article 22 of China’s Advertising Law prohibited commercial cigarette ads in mass media (including online platforms) and in public places, like retail stores. Article 22 was extended on May 1, 2022 to prohibit commercial e-cigarette ads in public places, in mass media, and online. No laws are in place to ban cigarette and e-cigarette displays in retail environments in China, and a 2021 point-of-sale assessment across 10 Chinese cities suggests that cigarette ads and displays remain commonplace in retail environments near schools. 
Only a handful of studies to date have examined exposure to cigarette or e-cigarette advertising among youth in China, and suggest notable levels of past 30-day exposure. In 2019, approximately half of senior and vocational high school students reported seeing “tobacco” ads in retail outlets in the past 30 days. Around 20% of senior and 28% of vocational high school students also reported seeing “tobacco” ads on the internet within the last month in 2019. 
These data suggest that Chinese youth may be exposed to cigarette ads in public places and online despite the 2015 legislation prohibiting cigarette advertisements. However, these estimates are limited as they are based on past 30-day assessment, which cannot estimate the frequency of exposure, and the survey question was not specific to the type of product featured in the ad (ie, cigarette vs ‘tobacco’) or the specific ad location (ie, grocery store vs ‘retail outlet’). In addition, no studies to date have assessed e-cigarette ad exposure since the 2022 ban on e-cigarette ads in public places and online. There is a gap in understanding the frequency and source (ie, convenience store) of cigarette and e-cigarette ad exposure, which are relevant for informing targeted enforcement of the current laws. Estimates are also limited to investigating ad exposure only. Evidence on the frequency of cigarette and e-cigarette display exposure could provide a more comprehensive assessment of marketing exposure among Chinese youth.
In the present study, we focus on the two most popular products in China—cigarettes and e-cigarettes —to address these gaps and utilize ecological momentary assessment (EMA) to enumerate the amount and location of cigarette and e-cigarette (a) ads and (b) displays that a sample of young people in China are exposed to in their daily routine. EMA captures real-time data on the amount of participant exposure to marketing in their natural environment, reducing the recall bias and recency effects associated with reporting past 30-day exposure. EMA has been used successfully to enumerate the magnitude of cigarette and e-cigarette marketing exposure among adolescents and young adults in the United States. Findings from this study can inform regulatory efforts to reduce youth exposure to cigarette and e-cigarette marketing in China.

## Methods
This study was part of a larger, two-phased mixed-methods study to understand youth perceptions of cigarette and e-cigarette marketing in China. In Phase 1 (December 2021–January 2022), we conducted 20 online focus groups on sources of and appeal of cigarette and e-cigarette marketing with 119 adolescent aged 15–16 and in Grade 10, equally recruited by gender and across 10 Chinese cities: Shanghai, Beijing, Guangzhou, Shenyang, Chongqing, Jinan, Kunming, Guilin, Kaifeng, Baiyin. In Phase 2, we reached out to the same 119 participants to recruit them to participate in the follow-up EMA study. Because of COVID-19 restrictions in Shanghai and Beijing, we were unable to conduct the EMA study in these two cities. For the remaining eight cities, EMA data were collected from 96 participants (n = 12 per city) in June 2022 using a mobile phone EMA survey application developed by MetricWire (http://metricwire.com). All data were collected in Chinese, and data collection took place over a period of 7 days from Monday, June 20th (Day 1) to Sunday, June 26th (Day 7). Results from the Phase 1 focus group discussions informed the final prompts used in the Phase 2 EMA survey.
The Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 00017173) and Zhejiang University School of Public Health (IRB No. 0571-88208219) approved this research. We obtained informed consent from participants and parental permission. Participants received up to US $55 for completing the study based on the number of EMA surveys completed. Parents of participants received between US $75 and US $130 based on the number of EMA surveys completed by their child. It is the prevailing social norm in China that all young people are financially dependent on their parents, and it is considered improper to pay children a large amount of money that could alter their shopping routine. In line with presiding norms, we provided parents with a higher incentive to distribute to their child at their discretion, while providing the youth participant a smaller incentive to increase their response rate.

## Sampling Approach
A China-based organization, Rising Sun, carried out all recruitment activities. Rising Sun re-contacted the parents of all Phase 1 participants to determine if they were interested in their child’s continued participation in Phase 2. In Phase 1, participants were recruited through contact with past adult (≥18) research participants in an existing, proprietary database who lived in a preidentified neighborhood and were a parent of at least one child enrolled in Grade 10, referrals to parents in the pre-identified neighborhood with at least one child in Grade 10 through existing neighborhood committees, and snowball sampling (in Baiyin and Guilin only). If granted permission, they conducted a study eligibility screener. Youth were eligible if they were aged 15–16 years old; currently enrolled in Grade 10 of a senior or vocational high school; able to speak and read Chinese; owned/had access to their own personal cell phone; and resided in a preidentified neighborhood in one the eight study cities where recruitment was possible (Baiyin, Chongqing, Guangzhou, Guilin, Jinan, Kaifeng, Shenyang, Kunming). Cities were chosen because of their different population size, economic development, and geographic location. We purposively selected neighborhoods in each city with the highest cigarette and e-cigarette retailer outlet density based on results from a prior point-of-sale study. These neighborhoods were purposively selected as sites to recruit information-rich participants for Phase 1 focus group discussions and subsequently prime participants in Phase 2 to be more aware of cigarette and e-cigarette advertising and display exposure, enhancing the ecological validity of our findings. We included Grade 10 students as this group is susceptible to initiating smoking between the ages of 15 and 18. In addition, the academic testing requirements were less intensive in Grade 10 versus older grades, which optimized our recruitment success.

## EMA Data Collection
Participants attended an online training session in June 2022 hosted by Rising Sun. In the session, they were asked to install MetricWire’s EMA app, Catalyst, on their personal cell phones. Rising Sun staff explained the procedures for completing the EMA surveys and instructed participants to complete a short questionnaire that collected data on gender, age, household financial security, ever and current cigarette and/or e-cigarette use, and susceptibility to future cigarette/e-cigarette use among those who did not currently smoke/use e-cigarettes. Susceptibility was based on two questions, “At any time in the next year, do you think you will [smoke a cigarette]/[use an electronic cigarette]?” and “If one of your best friends were to offer you [a cigarette, would you smoke it]/[an electronic cigarette, would you use it]?” with response options definitely yes, probably yes, probably not, definitely not. Participants who answered definitely not to both questions were ‘committed to never smoking/using e-cigarettes’. Participants who did not answer definitely not to both questions were “susceptible” to cigarette/e-cigarette use. Those who currently smoked/used e-cigarettes were coded as “susceptible.”
Participants also provided information on their school and sleep schedule, which was used to set up their EMA survey notifications based on school arrival time, school departure time, and weekend wakeup time.
Data were collected from Monday (Day 1) to Sunday (Day 7). The Catalyst app was programmed to push notifications for 6 EMA surveys per day (total of 42 surveys per participant). EMA surveys asked about exposure to displays and ads in the past hour with response options: none, cigarettes only, e-cigarettes only, both cigarettes and e-cigarettes (either together in the same ad/display or separate ads/displays). For displays, participants were asked if any products in the displays they saw within the last hour were flavored. For ads, participants were asked the location of all ads that they saw in the last hour. outlines the EMA survey prompts and response options. Participants were shown examples of cigarette and e-cigarette displays and ads that were embedded with the survey (see ). In this study, we defined displays as any visible, physical presentation of cigarettes/e-cigarettes for sale in a retail environment on a shelf, rack, table, or other surface. We defined ads as any commercial physical, digital, online, or audio advertisements or signs promoting cigarettes/e-cigarettes.
Morning survey notifications were sent 5 min before school arrival time and the first evening survey notification was triggered 15 min after school departure time on weekdays. The remaining surveys were sent at 1-h intervals following the first evening notification after school departure. On the weekends, the first survey notification was prompted 1 h after weekend wakeup time and the remaining survey notifications were sent randomly over the next 8 h. Participants in Baiyin and Kunming were required to attend school on Saturday and followed the school-day sequence on Saturday. Participants were instructed to complete the surveys as soon as possible after receiving the prompt. All surveys closed 15 min after the notification was sent.

## Data Analysis
Analyses were conducted using Stata 15 SE. We report results at the EMA response level (n = 3438) and the participant level (n = 96). Our study was powered (β=0.80) to detect a medium effect size (d = 0.5) under the assumption participants would submit six surveys per day more than 7 days, with an assumed completion rate of 50% (https://kleimanlab.org/resources/power-curves/).
At the EMA response level, we present descriptive statistics of the proportion of all EMA responses for each outcome (past-hour display exposure, flavored product display exposure, past-hour ad exposure, source of ad exposure) by the type of product exposure: none, cigarettes only, e-cigarettes only, both cigarettes and e-cigarettes.
At the participant level, we conducted bivariate analyses of exposure to (a) any cigarette or e-cigarette display and (b) any cigarette or e-cigarette ads by the number of days a participant reported at least one past-hour exposure to a display or ad, respectively (0 days, 1–2 days, 3–4 days, 5–6 days, and 7 days). We also estimated negative binomial regressions because of the overdispersion of our count data and modeled the adjusted incidence rate ratio (aIRR) of number of past-hour exposures to (a) any cigarette or e-cigarette displays, (b) any flavored cigarette or e-cigarette products in a display (for those reporting any display exposure only), and (c) any cigarette or e-cigarette ads during the week-long EMA period. All models were adjusted for demographic variables, current cigarette/e-cigarette use, and susceptibility to cigarette/e-cigarette use, which have been associated with past 30-day cigarette ad exposure. 

## Results

 presents demographic characteristics of participants (n = 96). Most (79.2%) participants were 16 years old, and half were female (50.0%). Approximately two-thirds reported a household financial situation where they lived comfortably (32.3%) or were able to meet needs with little left (38.5%). Most participants were in senior high school (62.5%) versus a vocational high school (37.5%).
 Overall, 81.3% of participants ever smoked a cigarette and 5.2% currently smoked. In addition, 17.7% ever used an e-cigarette and 2.1% currently used e-cigarettes. With respect to susceptibility to smoking, 53.1% of participants were committed to never smoking and 46.9% were susceptible to future smoking. Approximately, 50.0% were committed to never using an e-cigarette and 50.0% were susceptible to future e-cigarette use.

## Summary of EMA Exposure Outcomes
The mean number of EMA responses submitted across respondents was 36.8 (range: 19–42 responses, see ), for a total completion rate of 87.6%.

 summarizes the proportion of EMA responses submitted for each outcome. Of the 3438 total EMA responses submitted, most reported “None” or no exposure to a display (64.4%) or an ad (69.8%) in the past hour. For displays, around one-fifth (22%) of responses reported exposure to a cigarette display, 9% reported exposure to an e-cigarette display, and 4.6% reported exposure to both cigarette and e-cigarette display in the past hour. Among those responses reporting exposure to a display for cigarettes, e-cigarettes, or both, the majority indicated flavored products were present (64.8%, 88.4%, 83.4%, respectively). For ads, 16.8% of responses reported exposure to cigarette only ads, 10% reported exposure to e-cigarette only ads, and 3.4% reported exposure to both cigarette and e-cigarette ads in the past hour. The most common sources of cigarette only ad exposure were kiosks (41.2%), supermarkets (36.2%), and tobacco/e-cigarette stores (33.1%). The most common sources of e-cigarette only ad exposure were social media/internet (34.7%), tobacco/e-cigarette stores (22.2%), and shopping malls (20.1%).
 
 presents the proportion of youth exposed to cigarette and/or e-cigarette displays and ads by number of days exposed. Most participants were exposed to displays (89.6%) or ads (79.2%) on at least 1 day over the week-long data collection period and approximately 15% of participants were exposed to displays (14.6%) or ads (14.6%) on all 7 days. provides estimates of the mean number and range of past-hour exposures reported by participants during the week-long EMA data collection. On average, participants reported past-hour exposure to cigarette and/or e-cigarette displays 12.7 times and past-hour exposure to cigarette and/or e-cigarette ads 10.8 times over the week.
 

## Binomial Regression of Exposure Outcomes

 presents results from binomial regression models. Overall, there were no significant differences in past-hour exposure to cigarette and/or e-cigarette displays, flavored products in displays, or cigarette and/or e-cigarette ads, by participant age, gender, financial situation, ever use or current use of cigarettes or e-cigarettes, or susceptibility to future smoking or e-cigarette use. There was a significant difference in past-hour exposure by school type, where vocational (vs. senior) high school students were more likely to be exposed to cigarette and/or e-cigarette displays (aIRR = 1.70[95% CI: 1.04 to 2.78]) and ads (aIRR = 1.91[95% CI: 1.01 to 3.63]).
 

## Discussion
Reducing smoking in China is essential to addressing the global burden of tobacco-caused death and disease. Effective implementation of comprehensive marketing restrictions is one policy option to curb the influence of appealing ads and displays on youth uptake of cigarettes and e-cigarettes. , , This is one of the first EMA studies conducted among Chinese adolescents to examine the frequency of cigarette and e-cigarette marketing that young people may be exposed to under China’s current commercial cigarette and e-cigarette advertising restrictions. Despite these restrictions, we found that high school students in our study were regularly exposed to cigarette or e-cigarette ads during the week-long EMA data collection period. These findings are in line with and augment prior studies, which show that past 30-day exposure to cigarette and e-cigarette advertising among Chinese youth was substantial. Most study participants saw ads (79.2%) on at least one of the 7 days of the study and participants reported an average of 10.8 past-hour exposures to cigarette and/or e-cigarette ads over the week. Further, most young people were also exposed to cigarette and/or e-cigarette displays (89.6%) on one of the seven days, which is a form of marketing that can contribute to youth initiation, , and they reported an average of 12.7 past-hour exposures to displays over the week.
Of note, we did not observe significant differences in marketing exposure by demographic or cigarette/e-cigarette use, highlighting the ubiquity of cigarette and e-cigarette marketing exposure across youth in this study regardless of gender, income, and history with or susceptibility to smoking or e-cigarettes. However, we did find that vocational high school students were significantly more likely to be exposed to cigarette or e-cigarette ads and displays compared to senior high school students, which aligns with past research. , Evidence suggests that a greater proportion of retail stores located near vocational high schools (vs. senior high schools) have cigarette ads inside the store, and vocational high school students generally spend more time online compared to senior high school students. These studies highlight unique factors that could increase exposure among vocational high school students and indicate a need for research to confirm levels of exposure to and influence of in-store and online cigarette/e-cigarette marketing among this group.
More broadly, our findings suggest that average exposure to cigarette and e-cigarette marketing among youth in China may be comparatively higher than in other countries like the United States. For example, Ohio youth (ages 11–16) reported an average of 1.9 exposures to cigarette, e-cigarette, smokeless tobacco, or cigar ads over a 10-day EMA reporting period and young adult college students (ages 1–-24) reported an average of 8.2 exposures to cigarette marketing and promotions over a 21-day reporting period. Another EMA study conducted with young adults (ages 18–24) in Washington D.C. found that around one-third of participants (38.1%) reported seeing “advertisements or signs promoting tobacco products” ads on at least 1 day over the 2-week study. In our study, Chinese youth reported a much higher level of exposure. Although direct comparison is difficult given differences in study methodology and country-specific tobacco control context, these data offer initial insight into the potential magnitude of marketing exposure among Chinese youth.
Within China, our results have important implications for tobacco control policy development, implementation, and enforcement. Currently, China does not restrict displays. We observed that youth participants in this study were routinely exposed to cigarette and e-cigarette displays, which is concerning given that displays can serve as a source of advertising and influence uptake of cigarettes or e-cigarettes among youth. Our data provide evidence to support restrictions on product displays, which could require that products be kept out of sight in retail environments. Other countries, like Canada and Australia, have implemented such measures and observed declines in marketing exposure and impulse cigarette purchasing. 
In addition, most displays that participants were exposed to contained flavored cigarettes and e-cigarettes, which may be particularly appealing to youth. , , Flavored cigarettes are still legal to sell in China; however, in March 2022, China prohibited the sale of flavored e-cigarettes, except for flavored e-liquids that are sold separately from a device. Although we are unable to discern whether participants who reported flavored e-cigarette display exposure saw flavored e-cigarette devices or flavored e-liquids, our results suggest that young people in the study saw a substantial number of flavored e-cigarette products on display after the ban was implemented. These findings indicate a need for continued enforcement of China’s flavored e-cigarette device ban and highlight opportunity to extend the ban to other products, including flavored cigarettes and flavored e-liquids sold separately from a device.
Commercial ads for cigarettes and e-cigarettes are currently prohibited in public places, on mass media, and online in China. , Data from our study provide insight into opportunities for enhanced, targeted enforcement of the advertising law based on source of exposure. Specifically, youth participants reported that the most common sources for cigarette ads were everyday public places, like supermarkets, convenience stores, and kiosks, and the most common sources for e-cigarette ads were social media/internet and shopping malls. Both cigarette and e-cigarette ads were frequently seen at tobacco/e-cigarette stores. Enforcement measures should be prioritized and tailored toward ensuring these specific retail locations—supermarkets, convenience stores, kiosks, shopping malls, and tobacco/e-cigarette stores—are aware of and following the law by removing all ads from inside and outside of the venue. Other measures may be needed to restrict online and social media commercial cigarette and e-cigarette ads. This could include mandated cooperation between social media platforms and the government to monitor and report violations of the advertising law.

## Limitations
Our recruitment approach focused on enrolling students who were in Grade 10 and from pre-specified neighborhoods in eight cities. As such, results may not be reflective of the experience of all Chinese students. We did not gather information on school transportation or other activities (eg, chores) that may change participants’ level of exposure to cigarette and e-cigarette marketing, which limits our ability to characterize how representative the daily activities of our study population is compared to other Chinese students. We also recruited youth who lived in areas with high cigarette and e-cigarette retailer outlet density. Because of this approach, it is likely that our findings overestimate marketing exposure for the average Grade 10 student in China. However, the purposive sampling approach provides insight into the maximum range of cigarette and e-cigarette marketing that youth in China may be exposed to and can be used to guide policy making to protect all youth, including those who live in cigarette/e-cigarette outlet dense neighborhoods. Youth enrolled in this study also participated in a focus group study on the topic of cigarette and e-cigarette marketing months prior to the EMA study, and they were shown examples of cigarette and e-cigarette displays and ads each time an EMA survey was launched. It is possible that participants were primed to notice and report more marketing exposure, which could contribute to the high number of exposures reported in this study.
We did not differentiate between e-cigarette device and e-liquid exposure in our EMA survey and are limited in our ability to estimate marketing exposure for each product type. We also did not ask participants to differentiate between commercial and user-generated social media ads/content. While participants were trained to report on commercial ad content, it is possible that some reported past-hour exposure to user-generated social media ads, which could have inflated our ad exposure estimates. Additionally, our EMA survey only asked participants to report if they were exposed to ads and displays in the past hour (yes/no) versus the number of ad/display exposures that occurred. We were also unable to determine the sequence of past-hour exposure, including if coexposure to ads and displays from one source (eg, e-cigarette display with an ad for e-cigarettes) occurred. However, our ability to report ad and display exposure separately demonstrates the volume of marketing exposure and generates evidence to support unique policy responses for ads and displays. Future research could assess the number and sequence of exposures to each type of marketing over the study duration to provide a more refined estimate of marketing exposure among Chinese adolescents.

## Conclusion
Results from this study indicate that adolescents in China, particularly those living in retailer outlet dense neighborhoods, are frequently exposed to cigarette and e-cigarette marketing during their weekly routine. Most young people in this study reported exposure to cigarette or e-cigarette displays and ads on at least one of the 7 days of data collection, and they reported cigarette or e-cigarette ad exposure in public places where they are prohibited. These findings provide insight into where additional resources may be needed to enhance enforcement of the existing ban on cigarette and e-cigarette ads and to develop restrictions on display exposure. Such measures have the potential to reduce youth exposure to marketing , and contribute to reducing smoking and e-cigarette use uptake among young people in China and lowering the health burden of the global tobacco epidemic.
