## INTRODUCTION
Nobel Prize winner and president of the National Institutes of Health (NIH), Harold Varmus, envisioned a transformative approach to scientific publishing in the late 1990s. He and others proposed E-biomed, a pioneering concept for an open-access electronic publishing platform that combines a preprint server with peer-reviewed articles ( ). This vision ultimately led to the creation of PubMed Central ( ) and embodied the ideals of freely accessible science. However, contrary to these noble beginnings, today’s open-access model faces a perverse challenge. The proliferation of questionable open-access journals, which prioritize profit over scientific integrity, poses a substantial threat to the quality and credibility of the open publishing model ( ). These venues publish low-quality articles that hinder scientific progress and dilute the value of open dissemination.
Our study defines “questionable open-access journals” as journals violating the best practices outlined by the Directory of Open Access Journals (DOAJ) and showing indicators of low editorial standards ( ). Alongside paper mills [e.g., see Richardson et al. ( )], questionable journals have become an existential issue in academia since the inception of open-access publishing. Unscrupulous actors adopt the open access model and charge article processing fees by promising speedy publications with only a nominal review ( ). We must note that subscription-based and hybrid questionable journals also exist ( ). Many organizations (e.g., the DOAJ) and companies (e.g., Cabell’s) have been trying to vet journals by examining their websites, publication practices, and turnaround times. While the criteria for labeling a journal as questionable might sometimes conflict across these directories—some journals might appear in one index but not others ( )—these indexes have helped researchers quantify the impact of these venues ( ). However, once caught, the companies behind these venues adapt, quickly spawning journals with slight name variations or altered website designs ( , ). Given the number of open-access journals, it can be overwhelming for scientists, publishers, and funders to oversee them. Thus, with the recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), it is reasonable to wonder whether this technology can help to flag questionable open-access journals. We can use these advances to scale methods and make them more interpretable than what has been done before [e.g., see ( , )].
In this work, we are guided by the best practices in DOAJ ( ) to build an AI-based method for distinguishing legitimate from questionable journals. Our method effectively and efficiently detects questionable journals at scale and agrees with expert human evaluators. An analysis of tens of thousands of journals reveals the presence of more than 1000 previously unknown questionable venues. In addition, we found that bibliometric factors can be sufficient to distinguish these journals. In our analysis, we emphasize that these detections are provisional, as balancing false positives and false negatives still yields a false-positive rate of around 24%. Our error analysis reveals that some journals may appear problematic due to atypical publication patterns or limited indexing history. To take action, subject-area experts should be engaged before labeling a venue as questionable.

## The emergence of questionable journals
Historically, journals have been “closed” and available only to readers with subscriptions. This model may create global inequality in accessibility, exacerbated by rising subscription fees. For example, universities’ expenditures on this item increased by 223% from 1986 to 2011 ( ), much faster than inflation. The open access movement was set out to fix this lack of accessibility by changing the payment model. Open-access venues ask authors to pay directly rather than ask universities or libraries to subscribe, allowing scientists to retain their copyrights. In this manner, articles become openly available. The open-access model was initially a fringe movement, but it has gradually become legitimate and desirable in science. For example, a relatively recent policy by the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy mandated that all publications generated by federally funded research be open by 2025 ( ).
Questionable journals took advantage of the above publishing trends ( ). The lack of oversight by professionals, such as librarians vetting the subscriptions to journals, in addition to the pressures to publish and the direct financial contributions of authors in the form of author processing charges (APCs), created the perfect storm for questionable venues to appear ( – ). Let us note that legitimate venues might not necessarily guarantee quality ( ), and good-quality articles can also be present in questionable journals. Jeffrey Beall, a librarian at the University of Colorado, first coined the term “predatory publishing” in 2009 to describe questionable journals that exploit author fees without providing proper editorial services like peer review ( , – ). Beall contends that the APC model can be particularly prone to abuse, leading to the proliferation of questionable journals. These journals use deceptive practices to attract submissions and profit ( ). Several studies have examined the characteristics and prevalence of questionable journals ( , ). They mainly originate from Asia and Africa and target inexperienced researchers ( ), although the phenomenon can happen anywhere. Researchers must, therefore, carefully evaluate journals based on criteria like editorial board members, publisher information, and quality of editorial processes. Education efforts aim to increase awareness of questionable practices and strategies for identifying reputable journals.
From the authoring side, studies indicate that most authors publishing in questionable journals come from developing countries in Africa and Asia, often due to publication pressure and limited access to research training opportunities ( ). As a result, publishing in questionable journals is often considered an economic loss, with estimates suggesting financial damages of tens of thousands of dollars per article ( ). To mitigate these issues, researchers have developed methods to identify and avoid questionable journals in academic publishing and education.

## Authoritative guidelines for legitimate, open-access journals
Identifying questionable journals is labor-intensive. One possible solution is to use guidelines from institutions that monitor the publication landscape. The DOAJ is a community-based organization that produces one of the most comprehensive guidelines. DOAJ is a body of research volunteers who work on examining journals submitted for vetting and checking any complaints about the journals in their index. DOAJ started in 2003 and has over 17,500 open-access journals in its index ( ).
In contrast to the public and volunteer nature of DOAJ, Cabell’s International is a private and closed institution that started curating lists of whitelisted and excluded journals that some universities make available to their researchers ( ). Individual librarians have also created such lists. For example, Walt Crawford created the Gray Open Access (Gray OA) directory, which he frequently updates ( ), and Beall created the first list of “predatory journals,” which publishers colloquially know as “Beall’s list” ( ).
DOAJ, Gray OA, and Beall’s list attempt to maintain journal classification guidelines. These guidelines usually include checking the editorial board, journal name (to identify potentially deceiving name similarities with reputable journals), publication procedures, copyright information, and review timeline. There are overlaps and differences between these guidelines. DOAJ, Cabell’s, and Gray OA consider journals with a very short peer review as a worrying signal; DOAJ considers journals without an open access (OA) statement as potentially problematic, but Gray OA does not. In contrast, Cabell’s has criteria based on journals’ behavioral indicators, such as misleading metrics ( ), but little is known about how Cabell’s reviews the journals in its database.
Human screening is demanding, given the enormous volume of open-access publications [e.g., millions of open-access publications in life science ( )]. In addition, screening is mostly reactive (e.g., someone complains about a journal) or based on application submissions by journals (e.g., journals submit their details to be indexed by DOAJ). Crawford ( ) manually reviewed journals from the publishers in Beall’s lists ( ) to label journals with questionable practices, such as hidden article processing fees.
DOAJ makes its criteria openly available ( , left column). In the DOAJ list of journals, “unwhitelisted” journals might differ from “blacklisted” or “predatory.” This difference is because some unwhitelisted journals do not have questionable practices but cease to publish or request to be removed. However, some unwhitelisted journals do have questionable practices in their operations. For example, if a journal does not have an ethics policy, editorial board information, or a peer review policy, it is likely to be unwhitelisted. DOAJ mentions this detail as an unwhitelist action with a “reason” field suggesting questionable practices.
 

## The promise of automating the detection of low-quality publishing with AI
Recent studies have shown the potential of machine learning (ML) and AI in detecting questionable journals. Zhuang ( ) showed the potential of AI for detecting graphical research integrity issues, which could be preliminary factors in questionable journals. Chen et al. ( ) classified journals using web data and metadata, while Ateeq and Al-Khalifa ( ) applied convolutional neural networks to detect fraudulent websites. Dadkhah et al. ( ) examined spam solicitations.
Our approach expands upon this work by integrating multiple feature sets—website design, website content, and bibliometric indicators—to improve classification robustness and connect the features to factors that organizations such as DOAJ already use. By leveraging diverse features, our model is less susceptible to superficial changes that questionable journals may implement. In addition, unlike deep learning models that operate as “black boxes,” our method provides interpretable results, enabling greater transparency if used for AI-driven journal assessment.
Despite these advances, challenges remain. Existing AI models must be continuously updated to track evolving questionable publishing tactics. In addition, there is a lack of standardized datasets for training and evaluating these classifiers, as many existing journal lists (e.g., Cabell’s) are proprietary. Future work should explore ways to incorporate real-time web crawling and community feedback into AI-driven screening tools to create a dynamic and adaptable system for monitoring research integrity.

## RESULTS
In our work, we develop an AI-based method for operationalizing the DOAJ guidelines and estimate its predictive power for detecting questionable open-access journals. Traditionally, vetting questionable journals has been labor-intensive and manual. An automated method can complement these efforts by continuously monitoring the entire publishing landscape. Automated methods can also adapt and keep track of these venues’ changing tactics.

## Definition of questionable journals
We define questionable journals as those that were once part of DOAJ but were removed from it due to violations of guidelines or “questionable practices.” This is sometimes called unwhitelisted. Legitimate journals are all those that remain in DOAJ, which we call “whitelisted.” details how we operationalize each DOAJ criterion. We know that the dataset is imbalanced because all journals are first added as whitelisted and are only removed if they do not comply with further DOAJ review. To understand how journal features are distributed among legitimate and questionable journals, we compiled a set of 12,869 whitelisted journals (considered legitimate) and 2536 unwhitelisted journals (considered questionable) from DOAJ (see fig. S1) ( ).
Our classifier is trained to use a diverse set of journal features. These features are numerical representations of the underlying DOAJ criteria, enabling an ML method to learn how to use them to predict whether a new journal is questionable. The features include the website’s content, design, and journal bibliometrics ( ). Some features are relatively difficult to interpret, such as a visual embedding of the web page’s screenshot. In contrast, others are straightforward, like the number of publications and citation counts.
 The distributions of the number of publications, citation count, years of inception, and average number of publications by year overlap between legitimate and questionable journals (see fig. S2). However, a Kolmogorov test revealed that these distributions are significantly different except for the number of publications (see the “Statistical analysis” section in Materials and Methods). Still, the overlap suggests that bibliometrics might be a good indicator for distinguishing questionable versus legitimate journals.

## Unpaywall’s dataset
Once we have trained our ML model based on legitimate and questionable lists, we applied such a model to the Unpaywall’s dataset of open-access journals. Unpaywall’s dataset consists of 196,567 journal entries labeled as open access. After we removed journals with duplicated journal names and International Standard Serial Numbers (ISSNs), 93,804 open-access journals were left. For Unpaywall’s dataset, which does not provide the uniform resource locator (URL) of journal websites, we restrict our scope of analysis to Unpaywall journals present in the Microsoft Academic Graph (MAG) (see Materials and Methods and fig. S1). There is a large variety of journals inside Unpaywall’s dataset. A few features, such as the number of published papers, citations, year since inception, and year of publication, show this diversity (fig. S3). We found that the Unpaywall journals founded in 2009 have the highest number of journals across years, similar to those indexed by DOAJ (fig. S3C). The publications per year remain almost constant (fig. S3D).

## Model evaluation
We evaluate our AI-based method’s predictive power in two ways: (i) machine evaluation based on cross-validation and (ii) agreement with human experts.

## Cross-validation performance
We have a subset of journals for which we know whether they are legitimate or questionable according to DOAJ’s best practices (see Materials and Methods for more details). To predict questionable journals, we collected three sets of features about a journal: website content features (e.g., information from the aims and scope and editorial board sections), journal bibliometric features (e.g., average h-index of authors), and website design features [e.g., homepage code and a deep learning embedding of its front page’s screenshot using ( )]. We first built separate classifiers for each set of features ( ). Our classifiers were based on random forest (see the Supplementary Materials). We use the precision-recall area under the curve (PRC AUC) metric to compare their performances. This metric goes from 0 (no predictive power) to 1 (perfect predictive power). This metric can account for unbalanced datasets ( ). We use the feature importance calculated directly from the random forest to understand how strongly each feature contributes to the prediction. We use a logistic regression classifier to understand in which direction each feature contributes (i.e., positively or negatively associated). We use fivefold cross-validation for all experiments.
 

## 
Website content

Our questionable journal classifier can be built on website content features. We operationalized the DOAJ guidance on website content as features for our classifier (see for the feature engineering method and for the methods to compute readability scores). Our model has a performance of PRC AUC of 0.34 (±0.01). The importance of the feature reveals that the institution rank [measured by MAG as the importance of the institution in the publication network ( )] of the editorial board affiliation and the number of members are the most critical features in the prediction (fig. S4).
 

## 
Website design

Anecdotes about questionable journals suspect their websites will likely reuse designs [e.g., ( )]. Thus, we collected the journal website code and screenshots. For website code, we computed the term-frequency inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) of the HyperText Markup Language (HTML) tags as website structure features (see Materials and Methods for more details). We applied a convolutional neural network classifier to screenshots to create a representation of a journal’s front page. The website design classifier had moderate performance ( ). It predicted questionable journals through complex relationships in HTML tags on the journal homepage, which is hard to interpret.

## 
Journal bibliometrics

Journal bibliometrics reflect journals’ relationships between papers, authors, author affiliation, and other journals. Bibliometrics has shown effectiveness in mapping and characterizing scientific literature ( ). For example, researchers showed an association between bibliometrics and the emergence of research topics in technology research ( , ). Because questionable journals often relate to low visibility and young researchers, we hypothesize that bibliometric features can also indicate questionable journals. The performance of this classifier confirms that bibliometric features have a strong predictive power: PRC AUC of 0.64 (±0.04) in detecting questionable journals (see for feature importance and for feature explanations). The top features of each group are the year of first publication, the h-index of middle authors, and the number of affiliations per publication.
 

## Interpretation of models’ features and predictions
To further understand how our model distinguishes questionable journals, we analyzed feature importance and the direction of effects on predictions ( ). Feature importance was derived from the random forest classifier, while the association direction was determined using logistic regression.

## 
Institution bibliometrics

Features such as the number of institutions referenced and the number of institutions citing a publication are negatively associated with questionable journals. This suggests that legitimate journals tend to have more institutional diversity and citations from established sources. Conversely, the number of author affiliations per publication is positively associated with questionable journals, possibly indicating that these journals publish papers with inflated or loosely verified affiliations ( ).

## 
Journal bibliometrics

Self-citation metrics are important in identifying questionable journals. A higher self-citation count within individual publications and across journals is a strong positive predictor of questionable journals. In contrast, features like the number of journals cited and citation count per publication are negatively associated, meaning that reputable journals are more likely to cite a broader range of sources ( ).

## 
Author bibliometrics

Author-level indicators, such as lower average h-index values of first, middle, and last authors, are positively associated with questionable journals, reinforcing that these journals attract authors with weaker citation histories. In addition, high levels of author self-citation correlate with questionable journals, further suggesting a lack of engagement with the broader academic community ( ). These findings show that bibliometric patterns serve as reliable indicators of journal credibility.

## 
Combined models (website content, design, and bibliometrics)

Training a classifier with more features in ML might improve its performance. Specifically, we present the evaluation of our classifiers trained on website content features, journal bibliometrics, and website structure features. Then, the stacked model concatenates these three feature sets as one final feature set. We note that some websites do not have all features (e.g., URLs were unavailable or down at the time of scraping); therefore, we estimate these features statistically by imputation. In fivefold cross-validation, our classifier with data imputation has a PRC AUC of 0.61 (±0.01), and without data imputation, it has a PRC AUC of 0.79 (±0.03). Thus, combining multiple features improved performance compared to the singleton models above ( ).

## Validation against human reviewers
To further understand the predictability of questionable open-access journals, we examine whether our method agrees with the DOAJ criteria (see ). We sampled 360 journals from the Unpaywall dataset that are not in the DOAJ index and, therefore, not in the training data. Then, we asked human reviewers to review these journals according to DOAJ’s best practice criteria. We use Kendall’s τ coefficient to estimate whether the AI model’s scores and human reviewers’ scores are statistically dependent. measures the correlation between the AI model’s scores and human reviewers’ scores ( ; see the “Statistical analysis” section in Materials and Methods for more details). Our AI technique has a statistically significant agreement with all three reviewers [Kendall’s W (320) = 0.44, P < 0.001]. In addition, our AI model (website content–based) agrees with at least two human reviewers on the more subjective governing body (editor affiliation information), publication ethics, and copyright and licensing.
 

## Prediction analysis on journals from Unpaywall
Our bibliometrics-based classifier can be applied to the widest range of journals because it is the set of features most likely available. We apply this classifier to Unpaywall journals with more than 10 publications in our dataset (not indexed by DOAJ and present in MAG; see Materials and Methods). This threshold allowed the removal of data with few examples, which might include noisy metrics. This dataset contains 15,191 never-before analyzed journals.

## 
Prediction error analysis: Precision, recall, and false positives

Unless otherwise stated, we adopt a 50% decision threshold (i.e., a journal is labeled as questionable if the ratio of questionable to nonquestionable problematic predictions is greater than 1). This setting achieved a precision of 0.757 and a recall of 0.376. Applied to the 15,191 journals in Unpaywall corpus, the model flags 1437 titles: About 1092 are expected to be genuinely questionable, ~345 are false positives (24% of the flagged set), and ~1782 problematic journals would remain undetected (false negatives).
Our model can work with multiple thresholds (e.g., stringency levels). We report two levels: a high-recall setting at a 10% threshold (precision = 0.31, recall = 0.96) and a high-precision setting at an 80% threshold (precision = 0.98, recall = 0.08). At a 10% threshold, the model flags ~8800 titles, of which a high number of 6100 are expected to be false alarms while only missing fewer than 150 problematic venues. At an 80% threshold, only 240 journals are flagged and five are false alarms, but 2600 problematic venues escape detection. The 50% setting, therefore, offers a workable compromise for an initial triage queue of 1437 journals of the total 15,191 journals, where only 345 (or 2.2%) are expected to be false positives.

## 
Manual review of sample of predictions

To ground the cross-validated figures in human judgment, we drew a random sample of 200 journals that the model had flagged at the 50% threshold and had the authors review them independently. The evaluation gave precisions of 0.74, 0.73, and 0.80 (mean = 0.76), producing a false-discovery rate of 0.24, essentially the same as the 0.243 obtained from cross-validation.
Most of the false positives shared a few patterns. Many sites were unreachable or had been formally discontinued; some records referred to book series or conference proceedings whose titles mimic journals; and several were small, legitimate society outlets with plain web pages and modest citation counts. These patterns point to tractable fixes such as checking live ISSN status, filtering ISBNs, and toning down web-design and impact-factor signals.

## 
Estimation of publications, citations, funding, and countries

We now turn our attention to 1437 likely questionable journals. On the basis of the fivefold cross-validation, we estimate that the 95% conference of precision is 0.696 to 0.818, which translates into 1000 to 1175 journals being truly problematic. Using these intervals, we examined the publishing trend of these journals and found that the number of publications has been increasing with a slight drop in 2019 and 2020, but their citation count increased ( ). Similarly, we analyzed their funder acknowledgments ( ) and geographical distribution ( ). Similar to previous work, we find that publications in these venues acknowledge funding support ( ). We estimate that developing countries have more publications in questionable journals than developed countries. To discard potential language biases learned by our classifier, we compare performance for English, Portuguese, and Hindi websites and determine that they are comparable in precision: 0.48, 0.56, and 0.54, respectively. We found that the NIH has more grants acknowledging questionable journals than other US research funders, such as the National Science Foundation or the US Department of Agriculture (as a percentage of all their grants). Last, we estimated the chances of a grant (from agencies indexed by Dimensions) acknowledging a questionable journal. We found that the US, China, and Japan grants are most likely acknowledged.
 

## DISCUSSION
With advances in AI and computational methods, researchers have conducted computational studies to predict journal impact ( ), estimate journal characteristics ( ), and study unusual publication patterns in science ( ). Questionable journals have become an active research topic in academia since the release of Beall’s list ( ). The present study evaluates the extent to which AI techniques can replicate the expertise of human reviewers in identifying questionable journals and determining key predictive factors.
We show that AI-based classifiers can achieve strong cross-validated performance with high agreement with expert assessments. While the DOAJ criteria consider editorial board expertise, this measure can vary among reviewers. In contrast, our AI approach can provide a standardized quantification of editorial board expertise. Our bibliometric features capture multiple dimensions of journal characteristics, including author and institutional metrics. Notably, bibliometric indicators alone proved highly effective in distinguishing questionable journals, suggesting that problematic publishing practices leave detectable patterns in citation behavior. Specifically, a low h-index for the last authors and a high rate of author self-citation emerged as key predictors in our model. These results could complement existing journal evaluation systems.
Not all predictive features showed an obvious relationship with journal quality. For example, the year of first publication emerged as an important factor, possibly because many questionable journals are relatively recent. Older and established journals are more likely to have rigorous peer review and indexing. Some questionable journals rebrand or relaunch under changed names, making publication age a relevant proxy.
Although questionable journals may attempt to evade detection by modifying website content or editorial claims, certain features, such as the composition of the editorial board, are difficult to manipulate. Future advancements in AI-based detection could leverage additional publication-level signals that are even less susceptible to deliberate alteration, such as citation network effects.
Our analysis observed a marked geographical disparity in the distribution of publications in questionable journals, with a higher prevalence in developing countries ( ). This trend aligns with earlier findings showing that reputable publishing platforms may be limited ( ). Moreover, our findings on the presence of funders suggest a need for increased awareness among funding bodies as well ( ); relying on publishers alone is one side of a multifaceted solution. Similarly, the increase in publications and citation counts is concerning (e.g., ), suggesting that scientists should evaluate the kind of journal they cite. Some of the journals detected may also belong to reputable publishers. However, as recently seen, reputable publishers are not immune to large-scale issues in their journal portfolio [e.g., ( – )]. Questionable journals are becoming more numerous and entrenched in the academic landscape ( , ), and some countries are trying to take policy against this advance ( ).

## Practical suggestions and pitfalls when adopting AI tools in journal evaluation
The good agreement between our AI-based classifier and human reviewers indicates that AI tools can meaningfully contribute to journal evaluation, adapting to threats on a larger scale. However, transparency, accountability, and bias mitigation are essential when implementing AI in journal evaluation ( ). AI-generated assessments must be accessible and explainable, with final decisions resting with human editors and funders. Publishers have expressed caution toward AI, often requesting reviewers to disclose whether tools like ChatGPT have been used ( , ). Similarly, systems for tracking questionable journals should proceed cautiously, ensuring fairness. AI models should be developed to avoid favoritism toward well-known authors or institutions. AI holds great potential to enhance human expertise when appropriately applied, and users of such systems need to be cautious of inaccurate predictions.
Although the classifier streamlines the discovery of suspect venues, its scores must be read as preliminary signals rather than final verdicts. At the adopted 0.50 threshold, the cross-validated precision of 0.76 translates into ~345 of the 1437 flagged titles being expected to be legitimate and into a false-negative rate of about 62% (estimated to produce 1782 missed venues). Depending on resources available, however, research integrity investigators could choose to lower the detection threshold to capture most cases. We demonstrated that at a 10% threshold, we could lower the false-negative rate to 4% at the expense of a significantly higher false-discovery rate of 79%.
In any case, the Principles of Transparency and Best Practice in Scholarly Publishing, issued by DOAJ, Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE), Open Access Scholarly Publishing Association, and World Association of Medical Editors, state that the judgment of journal quality cannot be fully verified automatically ( ). Algorithmic verification can help, however, in adapting to a quickly changing landscape. COPE has warned that static “watch-lists” risk ethical and legal harm if entries are not reexamined by domain experts ( ). Every automated system is prone to errors, and, as others have shown, even top journals could be misclassified by automated systems [e.g., see ( ) in relation to the work of Chen et al. ( )]. We therefore present the 0.50 cutoff as a triage flag: Every flagged title should be reviewed against the DOAJ checklist and subject-area expertise before any public action is taken, and downstream users should publish their own error bars if they recalibrate the threshold.
Future research can refine AI-based methods by integrating more sophisticated bibliometric signals, large language models for content evaluation, and network-based analyses of editorial board relationships. In addition, longitudinal studies tracking journal behavior over time could improve predictive accuracy and reveal evolving strategies bad actors use. By combining AI with expert oversight, the academic community can build resilient systems that enhance trust in scholarly communication. If leveraged responsibly, AI-driven detection tools could help funders, institutions, and researchers make more informed decisions, mitigating the influence of predatory publishing and ensuring that knowledge dissemination stays rigorous.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Data

## 
DOAJ whitelisted journals and unwhitelisted journals

From DOAJ, we collected whitelisted journal records (15,296 journals added to the DOAJ whitelist) and unwhitelisted journal records (4784 journals removed from the DOAJ whitelist). Because some journals were removed from and added to the whitelist multiple times, we used their “added” and “removed” records to identify if a journal was in the whitelist or the unwhitelist by the time of our analysis. We removed unwhitelisted journals unwhitelisted for legitimate reasons (not questionable), such as ceasing publication or requesting to be removed from DOAJ. We also matched our journal lists with the journal metadata from DOAJ to find their homepage link. For journals without homepage links in the DOAJ dataset, we used the Bing search engine API to search for their homepage. In the end, we obtained 12,869 legitimate journals and 2536 questionable journals.

## 
Unpaywall journal dataset

The Unpaywall journal dataset consists of 196,567 open-access journals ( ). After we removed journals with duplicated journal names and ISSN, there were 93,804 open-access journals left. We restrict our scope of analysis to Unpaywall journals present in the MAG (see below). This matching left us with 25,348 journals, and we analyzed 15,191 of them, which have more than 10 publications. We could not match most of the Unpaywall journals to MAG because the Unpaywall journal dataset consists of articles from around 50,000 publishers and repositories. The matched MAG dataset comprises articles from 138 publishers; their overlap is relatively small. Among these journals, only 2847 are indexed by DOAJ.

## 
Microsoft Academic Graph

MAG is a sizable scholarly dataset comprising more than 83 million publications and their associated journals, authors, and affiliations ( ). We matched our Unpaywall journal sample to MAG with the journal’s title and ISSN and found around 12.9% of Unpaywall journal samples in MAG. We also used journal titles and ISSNs to match DOAJ journals with MAG and found 28.6% of DOAJ journal samples in MAG. Although MAG does not cover all the literature, it is a valuable dataset because it has the most documents compared to other datasets, such as Scopus ( ). We developed an AI technique that depends on bibliometric features to learn and generalize what makes a journal questionable.

## 
Data for expert review

In one of our evaluations, we recruited a professional librarian with expertise in open-access publication. For such evaluation, we selected a random set of 360 Unpaywall journals that had never been whitelisted or unwhitelisted by DOAJ before. We also included the two authors’ ratings in the analysis.

## Methods
Open-access publishing organizations like DOAJ usually rely on experts to review journal information and websites. As AI techniques, such as plagiarism checkers, have been adopted by publishers and academia, they have shown great potential to contribute to the open-access journal review process. In this section, we estimate to what extent AI techniques can mimic open-access journal reviewers, extracting information from journal websites to predict questionable journals (see fig. S5). We examine how website design information can be used to predict questionable journals. Last, we estimate to what extent journal bibliometrics can predict questionable journals and strengthen our journal website–based AI technique.

## 
Transforming DOAJ criteria into machine-readable information

To formalize this idea, we designed our AI technique to transform journal websites into machine-readable information, according to DOAJ criteria, such as editorial board expertise and predicting questionable journals. More specifically, our feature engineering extracts aims and scope, peer review policy, copyright policy, editorial board, open-access information, and ethics information from journal websites and quantifies them following DOAJ ( and ).

## 
Transforming journal website source code and screenshots into machine-learning features

Some questionable journals are established by the same publishers. Thus, specific questionable journals are likely to have website designs different from legitimate journals. We collected website source code and screenshots for journals in our sample to examine the journal website design’s effectiveness in predicting questionable journals. First, we transform the journal website source code into a TF-IDF vector (see Supplementary Method) to represent the webpage code design pattern as features for our classifier. Second, we transform journal website screenshots into embeddings by fine-tuning the ResNet neural network and classifying journals into legitimate and questionable journal websites ( ). Even though DOAJ does not have requirements on journals and their websites from these perspectives, some problematic publishers might need more professional web designers and web developers for their websites. Therefore, we include this information in our feature set.

## 
Computing journal bibliometrics for questionable journal classification

Unlike some legitimate journals, questionable journals have a relatively low-visible community, which suggests a high author self-citation count or a low journal self-citation count. Thus, we use MAG to produce journal bibliometric features, which represent their basic characteristics (see ). We use these features to train our classifier and predict questionable open-access journals. Bibliometric data show the descriptions of the author, journal, author affiliations, and their relationships. We apply these data as the training feature for our classifier.

## 
Classifier

We trained a random forest classifier (see Supplementary Method for details) of questionable journals with each feature set, except for the feature set of journal homepage screenshots. Because we have different feature sets, we present three strategies: One is to use a random forest classifier [we use ResNet 50 V2( ) for journal homepage screenshots] with each feature set; one is to use a random classifier with a concatenated feature set of the website content, website structure, and bibliometric features; and the other one is to use a stacked model based on predictions from individual classifiers (website design, website content, and bibliometrics-based classifiers) trained on their corresponding feature set.

## 
Survey for expert evaluation

We developed a survey that asked three expert reviewers (one expert librarian and two authors from this article) to review each of the 360 journals (see the “Data for expert review” section in Materials and Methods). The survey asked the reviewers to assign on a Likert scale how much the journal adhered to the criteria set by DOAJ. A final question asked whether the reviewer considered the journal to be questionable.

## 
Statistical analysis

Figure S2A shows the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test to examine the difference between the paper count of whitelisted journals and unwhitelisted journals from DOAJ. After removing outliers (journals with the top 1% of paper count), we found that there is no statistically significant difference [D(4295) = 0.021, P = 0.865] between the paper count of whitelisted journals and unwhitelisted journals from DOAJ.
Figure S2B shows the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test to examine the difference between the paper count of whitelisted journals and unwhitelisted journals from DOAJ. After removing outliers (journals with the top 1% of citation count), we found that there is a statistically significant difference [D(4295) = 0.064, P = 0.002] between the citation count of whitelisted journals and unwhitelisted journals from DOAJ.
In fig. S2C, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test examines the difference between the year of first publications of whitelisted journals and unwhitelisted journals from DOAJ. We found that there is a statistically significant difference [D(4427) = 0.26, P < 0.001] between the year of the first publications of whitelisted journals and unwhitelisted journals (from DOAJ).
In fig. S2D, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test is used to examine the difference between the average number of publications (by year) of whitelisted journals and unwhitelisted journals from DOAJ. We found that there is a statistically significant difference [D(44) = 0.565, P = 0.001] between the average number of publications (by year) of whitelisted journals and unwhitelisted journals (from DOAJ).
To validate against human reviewers, we first transform the options in our survey into numeric values. More specifically, we transform “strongly agree” into 1.0, “somewhat agree” into 0.75, “neither” into 0.5, “somewhat disagree” into 0.25, and “strongly disagree” into 0. After excluding unanswered questions, we retained 320 journals for overall assessment agreement analysis, 318 journals for aims and scope statement assessment agreement analysis, 314 journals for peer review process assessment agreement analysis, 319 journals for governing body (editor expertise) assessment agreement analysis, 317 journals for governing body (editor affiliation information) assessment agreement analysis, 317 journals for copyright and licensing assessment agreement analysis, and 320 journals for publication ethics agreement assessment analysis. Then, we computed the Kendall’s τ coefficient to examine the agreement between our AI technique and the reviewers.
