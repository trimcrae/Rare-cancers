## Abstract

## Background
Long-acting injectable cabotegravir (CAB-LA) pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) has superior efficacy compared with daily oral PrEP. However, implementation in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is constrained by high costs and limited access. Assessing willingness to pay (WTP) is critical for the sustainable rollout of CAB-LA PrEP. This study evaluated WTP for CAB-LA PrEP among heterosexual male and female sex workers in Uganda.

## Methods
Within a discrete choice experiment (DCE) to evaluate CAB-LA delivery preferences, we surveyed HIV negative heterosexual male and female sex workers (SWs) in two high prevalence cities in Central and Southwestern Uganda (October-December 2024). Willingness to pay (WTP) for injectable CAB-LA, including the maximum amount per injection, was assessed among sex workers (SWs) by trained interviewers. Trained interviewers collected data on WTP for injectable CAB-LA, including the maximum amount in Ugandan shillings (UGX) per injection. Modified Poisson regression was used to identify factors associated with WTP.

## Results
We enrolled 251 sex workers (SWs), including 52 (21%) males. Males had higher education (27% vs. 5%), while females had been in the industry longer (24 months vs. 18 months). Females reported higher median weekly earnings (250,000 Ugandan shillings [UGX] [US $71.43]) than males (145,000 UGX [US $41.42]). Overall, 51% of females and 42% of males expressed WTP for injectable CAB-LA, with a median WTP of 10,000 UGX [$3], compared with the current average cost of 100,000 UGX [$29] in Uganda. WTP was positively associated with residing in Kampala (adjusted prevalence ratio [aPR] 2.41; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.64–3.55) and with older age, including ages 25–30 years (aPR 1.51; 95% CI 1.03–2.22) and 31–35 years (aPR 1.87; 95% CI: 1.25–2.92), compared with ages 18–24 years. Higher WTP was also observed among respondents with previous PrEP use (aPR 1.52; 95% CI: 1.14–2.03), while no significant differences were observed by sex (aPR 0.96; 95% CI: 0.65–1.43).

## Conclusions
This cohort of sex workers preferred injectable PrEP, but their willingness to pay remained modest. Future studies should assess the feasibility and effectiveness of out-of-pocket payment models for injectable PrEP programs.

## Background
Uganda has made substantial progress in its HIV response, achieving a 61% decline in new HIV acquisitions between 2010 and 2024 [ ], exceeding the Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) regional average of 56% [ ]. This progress is partly attributed to the scale-up of evidence-based prevention and treatment programs, including condom promotion and distribution, test-and-treat approaches, voluntary medical male circumcision, and oral pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) [ , ]. Despite these gains, HIV burden remains significant, with an adult prevalence of 4.9% (6.4% among women and 3.4% among men) [ ]. In 2024, an estimated 37,000 HIV acquisitions occurred in Uganda [ ], underscoring the continued need for targeted prevention strategies and sustained investment in HIV prevention and care.
Uganda’s HIV prevention program has included oral pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) for HIV-negative individuals at increased risk, including sex workers (SWs), since July 2017 [ ]. Through support from the U.S President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR) and the Government of Uganda, oral PrEP is provided free of charge at PrEP-accredited facilities nationwide [ , ]. Long-acting injectable cabotegravir (CAB-LA) was introduced in September 2024 [ , ] and is currently available at accredited facilities for 100,000 Ugandan shillings (UGX) per injection (approximately US$29). Lenacapavir (LEN), approved in Uganda in January 2025 [ ], is expected to further expand HIV prevention options, particularly through its six-monthly dosing schedule.
In the HPTN 083 and 084 trials, HIV acquisition was reduced by 66% compared with oral PrEP among LGBTQ+ individuals, and by 89% among cisgender women [ – ]. Studies globally, including in SSA, show that individuals at high risk for HIV infection prefer injectable PrEP options such as CAB-LA [ – ], suggesting that its rollout could increase PrEP acceptability and uptake in Uganda. However, the ongoing national rollout is constrained by limited funding. Uganda’s HIV response relies heavily on international donors, with 82% of funding coming from external sources between 2020 and 2022 [ ]. The United States Agency for International Development (USAID) and (PEPFAR) stop-work orders issued in January 2025 [ ] have reduced available funding for the HIV response in Uganda, increasing the likelihood that users may need to pay out of pocket.
A total market approach (TMA) that engages the public, social marketing, and commercial sectors [ ] could be a viable strategy to ensure access to CAB-LA PrEP. This collaborative effort would leverage each sector’s strengths to reach diverse populations and maximize its impact. Market segmentation, a key step in the TMA process, involves analyzing willingness-to-pay (WTP) across key population segments [ ]. Studies in Nigeria and Kenya showed varying WTP for PrEP services among potential users [ , ]. However, these findings were specific to oral PrEP. In Uganda, sex workers (SWs), who have an HIV prevalence of approximately 31% and contribute 7–11% of new HIV infections [ , , ], are among the key populations prioritized for PrEP programs [ ]. However, little is known about their WTP for CAB-LA PrEP. This study aimed to assess WTP for CAB-LA PrEP among heterosexual male and female sex workers in two Ugandan cities.

## Methods

## Study design and setting
This cross-sectional survey, conducted from October to December 2024, was nested in a discrete choice experiment (DCE) to identify preferred service delivery attributes and models for CAB-LA PrEP among female sex workers (FSWs) and heterosexual male sex workers (MSWs) [ ] in Kampala, the capital of Uganda, and Mbarara, a city in western Uganda (combined population, 2,200,909 in 2024) [ ]. We previously established research cohorts of MSWs and FSWs in Kampala and Mbarara [ , ] —cities with high general population HIV prevalence and large sex work communities [ , ].

## Sampling and recruitment procedures
Study eligibility included HIV-negative, heterosexual men or women aged ≥ 18 years engaged in sex work (defined as exchanging sex for goods or money in the prior three months), and proficient in English or Luganda (local language). In this study, the term heterosexual male sex workers refers to cisgender men engaged in sex work who report having female clients. Using snowball sampling, we enrolled 251 respondents from previously identified sex work hotspots in Mbarara and Kampala [ , ]. Sex work hotspots included streets, lodges, bars, clubs, and brothels for FSWs, while MSWs solicited their clients through pimps, online advertising, social media, and recreational venues. Recruitment in each city began with the purposive selection of four FSWs, each representing a different venue type (streets, lodges, bars/clubs, and brothels), and three MSWs from our established cohort, as previously reported [ , ]. Selected potential respondents were contacted by telephone and invited to a personal meeting with a member of the research team at a location of their choice, where they were informed about study details, eligibility criteria, and peer recruitment procedures. The research team consisted of counsellors with at least a bachelor’s degree and experience in data collection, HIV counselling, and testing in sex work settings. Each respondent received two paper coupons containing an identification number, the research team’s contact information, and the study duration to distribute to potential respondents within their network.
To ensure participant safety, the research team completed ethics training, engaged with sex worker leaders, established safety protocols (e.g., working in pairs in the field and sharing contact information), sought community input, and conducted study procedures at venues chosen by participants. Data were anonymized, with no identifiers or names recorded to maintain confidentiality. FSWs and MSWs who presented a coupon to a research team member at a study site consented to undergo a rapid HIV antibody test; those testing HIV-negative were consented to participate in the survey. SWs testing positive for HIV and not already on antiretroviral therapy (ART) were referred to their preferred HIV-ART clinics for care and treatment. Upon completing the survey, each respondent received two additional paper coupons to distribute within their network, continuing recruitment until the target sample size was reached at each study site.

## Statistical analysis
The primary outcome was willingness to pay for CAB-LA PrEP, defined as the self-reported willingness to obtain CAB-LA at a nominal fee and the maximum amount respondents were willing to pay per injection. Respondents who expressed willingness to use injectable PrEP were subsequently asked whether they would be willing to pay for the two-monthly injection if offered at cost, and, if willing, to specify the maximum amount they would be willing to pay. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic characteristics, PrEP preferences, and WTP. Pearson’s chi-square (χ²) test was applied to assess differences in WTP by gender, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the maximum amount respondents were WTP by gender. Because WTP prevalence exceeded 10%, factors associated with WTP were assessed using a modified Poisson regression model with robust standard errors [ ]. Model fit was evaluated using both deviance and Pearson chi-square goodness-of-fit tests, neither of which was statistically significant. Crude and adjusted prevalence ratios (PRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated. A two-sided p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

## Results

## Population Characteristics
A total of 251 sex workers participated in the study, of whom 52 (21%) were men. Except for age and marital status, demographic characteristics differed significantly between MSWs and FSWs. The median age was 25 years for both groups (interquartile range [IQR] 22–29 for MSWs and 22–31 for FSWs). A higher proportion of MSWs had attained secondary or higher education (> 7 years of schooling) compared to FSWs (66.0% vs. 49.2%). MSWs reported a shorter duration of sex work than FSWs (median 18 months vs. 24 months; p = 0.009) and were less likely to rely exclusively on sex work for income (30.8% vs. 71.9%; p < 0.001). FSWs reported a greater number of weekly clients (median 26 vs. 3; p < 0.001) and higher weekly earnings (median UGX 250,000 [ US$71.43] vs. UGX 145,000 [US$41.42]; p < 0.001) (Table  ).
 

## PrEP awareness and willingness to pay among SWs
Overall, PrEP awareness was high but significantly lower among MSWs compared to FSWs (86.5% vs. 95.5%, p = 0.027; Table  ). Oral PrEP was the most widely known prevention option, with no significant difference by sex (91.1% vs. 95.8%; p = 0.25). However, less than half of the respondents reported current use, with similar uptake among MSWs and FSWs (42.2% vs. 42.1%, respectively). Among non-users, willingness to initiate PrEP was higher among MSWs than FSWs (67.3% vs. 45.7%, p = 0.006). Awareness of injectable PrEP was low prior to the study (11.1% vs. 6.3%, respectively), but after providing information, it became the most preferred option among both MSWs (73.1%) and FSWs (78.4%). Among respondents who preferred injectable PrEP, nearly half (49%) were willing to pay a nominal fee, including 50.6% of FSWs and 42.0% of MSWs (Table  ). The median maximum amount participants were WTP per injection was UGX 10,000 (IQR 5,000–20,000), equivalent to US $2.8 (1.4–5.6).
 In adjusted analyses, WTP was independently associated with residing in Kampala (adjusted prevalence ratio [aPR] 2.25; 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.54–3.30; p = 0.001), past PrEP use (aPR 1.52; 95% CI 1.14–2.03; p = 0.005), and being aged 25–30 years (aPR 1.63; 95% CI 1.14–2.33; p = 0.008) or 31–35 years (aPR 1.85; 95% CI 1.26–2.72; p = 0.002) (Table  ). Prior but not current PrEP use was also associated with higher WTP (aPR 1.52; 95% CI 1.14–2.03).
 

## Discussion
This study provides one of the first insights into willingness to pay (WTP) for long-acting injectable HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (CAB-LA PrEP) among heterosexual male and female sex workers in Uganda. More than two-thirds of both groups preferred injectable CAB-LA over other oral PrEP options, highlighting its potential acceptability in this key population. This preference aligns with previous evidence indicating that long-acting PrEP modalities can improve adherence and overcome challenges associated with daily oral PrEP use, such as pill fatigue, stigma, and inconsistent access [ – ]. The landmark SEARCH study in Kenya and Uganda, which offered person-centred choice of oral PrEP, post-exposure HIV prophylaxis, and CAB-LA, with the option to switch, found that most intervention participants (56%) chose CAB-LA, thereby increasing prevention coverage [ ]. The impending rollout of lenacapavir (LEN), a six-monthly injectable PrEP option, may further enhance PrEP acceptability and improve prevention uptake among sex workers.
Fewer than half of respondents reported WTP even at a nominal fee, with a median WTP of US $3 per injection—substantially lower than US $29 per injection at implementing health facilities in Uganda. Although median weekly earnings among FSWs (US$71.44; US$571.52 over two months) and MSWs (US$41.42; US$331.36 over two months) suggest some income-generating capacity, an out-of-pocket payment of US$29 represents a potential threat to financial protection, accounting for approximately 5.1% of FSWs’ and 8.8% of MSWs’ bi-monthly income. Nevertheless, the USAID and PEPFAR stop-work orders have necessitated out-of-pocket payments [ ], particularly in donor-dependent health systems such as Uganda’s, where HIV prevention services have historically been publicly financed and heavily subsidized [ ]. This observation underscores the need for increased community sensitization on shared responsibility for health care and the potential benefits of modest out-of-pocket contributions. Comparable studies among FSWs in Kenya and Nigeria reported higher WTP levels (77%–83%) [ , , ], likely due to higher incomes than those observed in Uganda. Notably, while these studies focused on oral PrEP or the vaginal ring, only 14% of respondents in Nigeria were willing to pay US $9.20 or less per month [ ], and in Kenya, about half of the FSWs were willing to pay only US $2 per month [ ]. For long-acting injectable PrEP to be sustainably scaled up in Uganda, it must be affordable to the population at risk. The current facility-level cost of approximately US$29 per injection poses a major barrier to access, underscoring the need for rapid expansion of generic formulation production and market entry to reduce unit costs, enhance affordability, and support equitable access within a resource-constrained health system. The impending rollout of LEN has the potential to substantially improve the affordability of long-acting HIV prevention. Recent global health agreements indicate that generic LEN — the twice-yearly injectable for HIV prevention — is expected to be available in low- and middle-income countries at a cost of approximately $40 per person per year (equivalent to roughly $20 per six-month dose) or US$3.33 per month starting in 2027 [ , ]. This projected pricing represents a substantial reduction from current branded costs. Notably, the anticipated cost of generic LEN (US$3.33/month) closely aligns with our observed WTP among SWs—approximately US$3.00 every two months for CAB-LA—suggesting that LEN rollout could substantially reduce affordability barriers.
Our study found that WTP was positively associated with residence in Kampala (the capital city), prior oral PrEP use, and age 25–35 years, likely reflecting greater financial capacity, higher risk perception, and familiarity with prevention services. City residents, in addition to earning more, may have easier access to services [ – ] and perceive greater value in injectable PrEP, while prior PrEP users are more motivated to maintain protection. The higher WTP observed among SWs aged 25–35 years may reflect their peak earning potential, supported by established client networks and stronger negotiation skills that attract higher-paying clients [ , ], and with longer exposure and higher client volumes, which are associated with a greater perceived risk of acquiring HIV. Many in this age group juggle family and secondary income-generating responsibilities [ , ], making the convenience and discretion of injectable PrEP especially appealing. Their greater exposure to HIV prevention programs and peer networks may further enhance awareness, acceptance, and willingness to pay for biomedical prevention. Contrary to expectations, sex was not significantly associated with WTP. Despite having fewer clients and lower median weekly earnings, MSWs demonstrated a comparable WTP to FSWs (42% vs. 51%), suggesting that perceived need and motivation for HIV prevention may offset economic differences. These findings underscore the need for subsidized or government- or donor-supported delivery models if CAB-LA is to be effectively scaled up among SWs in similar low-resource settings. Combining subsidized public-sector CAB-PrEP delivery for high-risk, low-income groups with private-sector options for those able to pay could increase access. Sustainable financing will require domestic resource mobilization through health insurance schemes, external funding, and private-sector involvement through public-private partnerships.
The strength of this study is that, to our knowledge, it is the first to examine willingness to pay for PrEP among MSWs and FSWs in Uganda. Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes assessment of changes in WTP over time and limits causal inference. Second, participants were recruited from only two urban settings using snowball sampling, which may reduce generalizability due to potential selection and reporting biases and the homogeneity of respondents; findings may not reflect the experiences of sex workers in other regions, particularly rural areas with different socioeconomic contexts. Finally, we did not assess factors such as service delivery preferences or potential side effects that could influence WTP, nor did we account for clustering during analysis.
Despite these limitations, our findings underscore several policy implications for introducing and scaling injectable PrEP among SWs in Uganda. First, the strong preference for CAB-LA suggests that injectable options, including LEN PrEP, could substantially improve PrEP acceptability and uptake in this population. Second, the finding that nearly half of respondents were willing to pay a modest fee supports a cost-sharing model across public, private, and donor-funded programs to promote sustainability and equitable access. The anticipated rollout of LEN, with potential generic pricing as low as US$3.33 per month, could further improve the affordability and feasibility of injectable PrEP delivery. Third, integrating injectable options into existing HIV prevention and sexual health services—particularly those targeting urban centers—could enhance uptake by leveraging established delivery platforms and peer networks. Tailored communication strategies and community engagement should emphasize the importance of individual contributions to funding HIV prevention programs, including injectable PrEP. Finally, implementation strategies should address geographic disparities by extending access beyond major cities to rural areas, where health service availability and income levels are lower.

## Conclusion
This cohort of sex workers preferred injectable PrEP, but their willingness to pay remained modest. Future studies should assess the feasibility and effectiveness of out-of-pocket payment models for injectable PrEP programs.
