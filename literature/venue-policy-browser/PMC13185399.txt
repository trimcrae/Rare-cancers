Embedding research integrity (RI) education within institutions is a cornerstone of responsible conduct of research (RCR), however practical and scalable strategies remain under documented in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). The Kenya Medical Research Institute (KEMRI) established a Research Integrity Committee (RIC) in 2023 to institutionalize RI training. This paper describes the training strategies adopted by the KEMRI RIC to enhance institution-wide awareness and practices of RCR. Five complementary approaches were implemented: (1) a train-the-trainer (TTT) programme for RIC members; (2) the integration of RI symposia into national scientific and bioethics conferences; (3) geographically clustered, centre-specific training tailored to local needs; (4) hybrid in-person/virtual training delivery platforms; and (5) digital dissemination through bulletins, social media, and the institutional website. Over 1200 of over 4000 of the participants were reached across all 15 KEMRI research centers. Presentation of the committee work at the WCRI (Athens 2024) raised the profile of the RI awareness efforts locally and globally. This KEMRI experience describes opportunities for institutions seeking to foster a culture of responsible research.

## Introduction
Globally concerns over fabrication, falsification, plagiarism, and other questionable research practices continue to erode public trust and compromise/undermine the reproducibility of science [ , ]. In response, funding agencies and scientific publishers increasingly mandate clarity in documenting reporting training in Responsible Conduct of Research (RCR) [ ].
While many high-income countries have well-established programmes, LMICs often lack sustained, institution-wide approaches owing to limited awareness, resources, competing health priorities, and insufficient policy frameworks [ , ].
The Kenya Medical Research Institute (KEMRI), one of sub-Saharan Africa’s largest biomedical research organizations with 15 research centres nationwide, recognized the need to embed research integrity as a core institutional value. In July 2023, the KEMRI inaugurated a Research Integrity Committee (RIC) hosted at the Scientific and Ethics Review Unit (SERU) to create awareness, develop institutional policies, and lay the groundwork for an Office of Research Integrity (ORI). This manuscript is a descriptive, non-empirical case study documenting the first two years of integrating research integrity into KEMRI’s operations. It describes specific strategies employed, the lessons learned during implementation, and the initial results observed within the institution. The goal is to provide a practical case study that can guide the development of policies and practices for other low- and middle-income country (LMIC) settings.

## Methods

## Design
This was a descriptive institutional implementation case study documenting planning, delivery, and uptake of Research Integrity (RI) training activities. Quantitative data (attendance logs, webinar participation metrics, website analytics) and qualitative feedback (post-session reflections and trainer debrief notes) were used to assess reach and engagement, although formal impact and competency evaluations were not undertaken at this stage.

## Setting and participants
KEMRI employs ~ 4000 staff and hosts postgraduate students and collaborators across 15 research centers located in Nairobi, Kilifi, Kisumu, Kericho, Busia, Uasin Gishu, Kirinyaga and other research sites and counties in Kenya.

## Intervention development

Needs assessment: RIC members conducted an exploratory needs assessment using informal but structured approaches, including: (i) guided conversations with centre leads and training coordinators, (ii) email and telephone exchanges with potential participants, and (iii) review of prior institutional audit reports. Notes from these interactions were synthesized to identify recurring gaps in RI knowledge, preferred training modalities, and logistical constraints, which informed the design of subsequent training activities.
Training content development: Information obtained through communications with the target groups was used to inform the design of the training content, articulation of session objectives, and the selection of facilitators best suited to address the identified needs and knowledge gaps.
Strategy selection: Approaches were chosen on the basis of feasibility, scalability, and ability to leverage existing institutional platforms.
Monitoring: Attendance sheets, webinar logs, website analytics (Google Analytics), and structured feedback forms documented reach and engagement.
Expected outcome: To create an institution-wide awareness on research integrity and foster a culture of responsible conduct of research.

## Ethical considerations
This descriptive programme did not meet the threshold for human subject research. The RIC received a letter of non-research determination from the Kenyatta National Hospital-University of Nairobi Ethics Review Committee referenced as KNH-ERC/01/PUB/7 dated 10th November 2025. No identifiable personal data or sensitive information about individuals was collected or reported. All data and information in this paper were obtained from credible sources and have been cited appropriately.

Across all activities, more than 1,200 staff, students, and collaborators were reached directly through structured RI training sessions, with additional institutional visibility achieved through national scientific conferences and international presentations.
To further cement RI training programs into the institutional calendar of events, we developed RI policy that guides on the processes of creating awareness across the organization. Through this policy, the institution places a high priority on cultivating a culture of research integrity through adoption of comprehensive training programs. The element of RI training and mentorship in the RI policy will be implemented through the guidelines and standard operating procedures (SOPs).

## Training-the-trainers (TTT) Programme
The train-the-trainer (TTT) model has been widely used to expand knowledge and skills and attain educational sustainability [ ]. In November 2023, 18 RIC members completed a three-day TTT workshop led by international experts on Research Integrity (RI). The workshop covered key RI topics including principles of RI, mechanisms for detecting and reporting misconduct, and operationalizing a Research Integrity Office. A formal competency-based assessment was not conducted following the TTT programme to verify trainer readiness. However, upon completion of the TTT, the Training Sub-Committee identified and engaged suitable internal trainers to lead subsequent research integrity awareness sessions. The selection of the trainers was based on their educational background, professional experience, and prior involvement or training in the training’s focus areas. This approach ensured that trainers deployed were appropriately prepared to support ongoing institutional research integrity strengthening efforts. While no formal competency assessment followed the TTT workshop, future cycles will incorporate structured pre- and post-training evaluations to assess knowledge acquisition and instructional readiness.

## Leveraging on the annual scientific and health conferences
During the 15th KEMRI Annual Scientific and Health (KASH) conference held in Nairobi on 11th–14th February 2025 [ ], the topic of research integrity was enhanced in a plenary session entitled “Inculcating a Culture of Research Integrity”. Furthermore, the RIC hosted a symposium titled “Elevating Research Standards for Better Science”. The interactive session generated significant engagement, with numerous questions being raised concerning the establishment and functionality of an institutional RIO. The symposium featured a keynote address followed by panel discussions. The keynote together with the plenary session attracted over 750 delegates drawn from various institutions.
During the 9th Bioethics Society of Kenya (BSK) conference held on 11th and 12th September 2025 in Nairobi, Kenya [ ], the RIC hosted a panel discussion on research integrity. This session provides in-depth discussions on both global and local contexts of RI, the development and implementation of institutional RI frameworks, and RI sustainability mechanisms across different institutions in Kenya.

## Cluster-based training at institutional research centers
The KEMRI currently has a total of fifteen (15) research centers and various research stations/sites spread across Kenya. Partners and collaborators are domiciled in different research centers. The KEMRI RIC, through the training subcommittee, clustered the 15 research centers in terms of geographical location. Institutional research centers were grouped into Western, Coastal, and Central clusters for tailored sessions. The Western cluster consists of KEMRI research centers located in Busia, Kisumu, Kericho, and Uasin-Gishu counties of Kenya. The coastal cluster includes the KEMRI research centers located in Kilifi, Taita Taveta, and Kwale Counties. The central cluster includes the research centers in Nairobi and Kirinyaga Counties. Participation was open to all research and technical staff within each cluster, ensuring equitable access across professional levels.

## Procedure for selecting training participants in different clusters
Once the research center clusters were determined, we circulated the call for RI training through the institutional email. The research center administrative heads circulated the emails to all target audience for the training. Additionally, the training program and the webinar link were circulated to all the KEMRI scientific staff, affiliated partners and collaborators.
The training for the Central cluster was conducted on 14th March 2024, and it focused on mechanisms for reporting, investigating, and resolving research misconduct. In June 2024, the RI training for the Western cluster covered key topics including: principles and frameworks of RI, research misconduct, data-management ethics, mentor-mentee responsibilities and conflicts of interest in research. Coastal cluster training was held on 11th June 2025, addressing topics such as research ethics versus RI to data management ethics [ ].

## Hybrid delivery
Hybrid delivery increased participation by an estimated 35%, determined by comparing mean attendance for hybrid sessions to in-person-only sessions conducted in the prior training year.

## Digital dissemination
Regular bulletin articles and social media updates enhanced awareness of research integrity and RIC activities. All planned RI trainings were featured in the official institution’s social media platforms and highlighted/captured in the weekly bulletin.

## International visibility
Two staff members presented oral and poster abstracts at the 8th World Conference on Research Integrity (WCRI), held in Athens, 2024, facilitating knowledge exchange and institutional benchmarking. Participation in WCRI facilitated benchmarking against global standards and strengthened institutional credibility in the international research integrity discourse.

## Limitations
The KEMRI RIC did not fully utilized all the available strategies for engaging diverse audiences. Future efforts will focus on expanding outreach through additional training opportunities, including quarterly webinars and a self-administered online training course on Research Integrity. Other planned initiatives include the posting of clips and videos related to the different RI topics on the website. A key limitation is that this phase focused on reach and awareness rather than measuring knowledge retention or behavioural change. Future evaluations will incorporate structured assessment tools and long-term tracking indicators such as mentorship quality scores and misconduct reporting trends.

## Discussion
KEMRI’s multifaceted approach demonstrates how LMIC institutions can rapidly institutionalize RI training by:

Leveraging existing platforms—utilizing national conferences and bulletins reduce operational costs and ensure high visibility.
Building internal capacity—implementing a TTT model to develop a sustainable cadre of RI trainers [ ].
Adopting hybrid models—facilitating participation among geographically dispersed staff and collaborators [ ].

Using data to guide adaptation—employing web analytics and attendance logs to refine information and training content. Our experience aligns with global recommendations emphasizing context-specific, multimodal training as a means of fostering a culture of integrity [ , ]. Formal impact evaluations such as pre/post knowledge assessments and longitudinal tracking of misconduct reports, are planned to quantify outcomes.

## Recommendations
We recommend institutionalization of research integrity training and all efforts to inculcate a culture of responsible conduct of research through the development of appropriate policies, guidelines, standard operating procedures, conducting regular sensitizations and refresher trainings, and integrating RCR modules in academic programs. We also recommend localization of training programs to suit the context-specific demands gathered through structured training needs assessments using the existing platforms and resources as well as development of avenues for continuous improvement.

## Outlook
This institutional experience illustrates how coordinated, context-specific strategies can effectively embed research integrity across diverse institutional settings. The approaches described here are adaptable and may inform similar capacity-strengthening efforts in other LMIC research systems.
