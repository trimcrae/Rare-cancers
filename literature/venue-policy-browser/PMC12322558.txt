## Abstract

## Background
Sarcopenia has become a significant health issue, particularly as a common consequence of COVID-19 in older adults.

## Objective
This study aimed to explore the clinical and psychological effects of integrated physical training with a high-protein diet compared with a regular protein diet in community-dwelling older men who had recovered from COVID-19 and exhibited symptoms of sarcopenia.

## Methods
This is a single-blinded, randomised, controlled study conducted from March 2020 to December 2023 at the University hospital. The eligible participants were randomly assigned to two groups using the block randomisation method. The first group underwent integrated physical training with a high-protein diet (group A; n=38), with an average age of 64.1±3.8 years, while the second group underwent integrated physical training with a regular protein diet (group B; n=38), with an average age of 64.5±3.6 years over an 8-week period. Clinical parameters (handgrip strength and muscle mass—cross-sectional area CSA) and psychological measures (kinesiophobia and quality of life) were assessed at baseline, the fourth week, the eighth week and at a 6-month follow-up. The data were analysed using a 4×2 mixed model for repeated measures at different time points.

## Results
Demographic characteristics such as age, height, weight and body mass index did not show any statistically significant differences between the groups (p>0.05). After the 8-week intervention and at the 6-month follow-up, handgrip strength decreased by −5.0 (95% CI −6.21 to −3.78), midthigh CSA decreased by −3.7 (95% CI −6.53 to −0.86), midcalf CSA decreased by −4.4 (95% CI −6.80 to −2.00), kinesiophobia level increased by 8.1 (95% CI 7.16 to 9.03) and quality of life decreased by −6.3 (95% CI −9.0 to −3.5). The findings indicated significantly greater improvement (p<0.001) in group A compared with group B, although there was no significant difference in muscle CSA in the arm region (p>0.05).

## Conclusion
Integrated physical training with a high-protein diet led to improvements in clinical (muscle strength and muscle mass) and psychological (kinesiophobia and quality of life) parameters compared with integrated physical training with a regular protein diet in older men recovering from COVID-19 and displaying symptoms of sarcopenia.

## WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC
Integrated physical training, including resistance and aerobic exercises, is recognised as an effective intervention to improve muscle strength, muscle mass and quality of life in sarcopenic individuals.
High-protein diets (≥1.2 g/kg body weight/day) are more effective than regular protein intake in preserving lean muscle mass and enhancing the effects of resistance training.

## WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
It highlights the synergistic relationship between physical training and high-protein diets, showcasing their combined efficacy in accelerating recovery from sarcopenia symptoms in a post-COVID-19 population.
The findings provide evidence-based guidelines for optimising rehabilitation strategies in older adults recovering from COVID-19, emphasising the importance of dietary adjustments alongside physical training.

## HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY
This study reinforces the need for a multidisciplinary approach to post-COVID-19 rehabilitation, emphasising the interplay of physical activity and diet in restoring health and independence in older adults. By bridging critical knowledge gaps, it sets a precedent for improved care models, fostering better recovery outcomes and quality of life in this vulnerable population.

## Introduction
 The coronavirus disease (COVID-19), classified as a communicable disease by the WHO, has been affecting the world since December 2019. It is a significant health concern for the elderly (those over 60) and imposes financial and socioeconomic burdens on communities. The virus enters the human body through the nasopharyngeal route and progressively affects all body systems. The spikes on the coronavirus contain a protein that attaches to the ACE2 receptor on host cell membranes, facilitating the virus’s entry and deactivation of the host cell. Individuals infected with COVID-19 exhibit symptoms that can become severe or life-threatening, especially when accompanied by comorbidities such as being over 60, respiratory or cardiac issues or diabetes mellitus. However, evidence is increasing that many COVID-19 infections are asymptomatic. These individuals test positive for the virus through reverse transcriptase-PCR (RT-PCR) but do not show typical clinical symptoms or abnormalities on CT scans, yet they can still spread the virus. Furthermore, COVID-19 patients who are inactive or lead sedentary lifestyles may develop muscle weakness, weight loss and general lethargy. Sarcopenia, a common muscle disease among older adults, can be aggravated by COVID-19. It is believed that COVID-19 patients are at a higher risk of developing acute sarcopenia, characterised by low skeletal muscle mass and reduced strength. A recent meta-analysis indicates that 48% of COVID-19 patients are at high risk for sarcopenia, emphasising the importance of diagnosing and treating this condition. 
During the pandemic, people are advised to stay at home, leading to reduced mobility and a more sedentary lifestyle, which hastens muscle weakness and loss of muscle function. Physical inactivity also causes systemic changes, such as altered inflammatory responses, decreased muscle proteins, impaired mitochondrial function, hormonal imbalances, general weakness, weight loss and muscle pain. Sheahan et al observed that immobility and a sedentary lifestyle are the primary causes of muscle mass loss and muscle pain in rat models. Recent studies on the impact of COVID-19 lockdowns and home confinement reveal negative changes in cardiorespiratory function, heightened insomnia severity, disrupted sleep patterns and significant predictors of decreased mental well-being. Additionally, older adults with COVID-19 who have systemic illnesses may be more susceptible to developing secondary sarcopenia. Therefore, these patients are advised to engage in regular physical exercise and maintain a proper diet to mitigate these negative effects. 
During the COVID-19 pandemic, there is a heightened risk of physical disabilities and increased mortality due to the negative impacts of sarcopenia. The 2014 recommendations from the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism expert group emphasise that daily physical activity or exercise and a protein-rich diet are essential for preserving muscle health and preventing weakness in adults over 60. Similarly, for older COVID-19 patients who have developed sarcopenia, maintaining regular physical activity and a proper diet is crucial for supporting clinical functions and psychological well-being.
Recognising sarcopenia as a serious health concern among older men who are recovering from COVID-19 and living in the community, clinicians and allied health professionals have expressed the need to develop effective physical training protocols and optimal dietary plans to address these consequences. While there is a significant body of research devoted to understanding and managing sarcopenia in older adults, it is important to note that there is still a lack of well-defined exercise protocols and specific dietary recommendations tailored for this particular population. Therefore, further research is required to develop more targeted interventions for older men with sarcopenia symptoms post-COVID-19. This study aims to investigate and compare the clinical and psychological effects of integrated physical training with a high-protein diet versus a regular protein diet in community-dwelling older men recovering from COVID-19 and experiencing sarcopenia symptoms. The findings from this trial are expected to provide updated evidence and guidelines for prescribing physical training and optimal dietary plans for older men with sarcopenia following COVID-19. This study highlights the potential of integrated physical training combined with a high-protein diet as a targeted intervention to enhance muscle recovery and overall functional health in post-COVID-19 older men with sarcopenia, offering valuable insights for researchers, clinicians and patients in addressing age-related muscle loss and postviral rehabilitation.

## Methods

## Trial design
The study was conducted from March 2020 to December 2023 at the Physical therapy department (OPD), Prince Sattam bin Abdulaziz University, Al Kharj, Saudi Arabia as a single-blinded, randomised, experimental trial. Selected participants were required to complete a written consent form and underwent measurements for personal and anthropometric data before the intervention. The participants were randomly assigned to two treatment groups using a two-block, simple random sampling method. Group A (n=38) received integrated physical training along with a high-protein diet, while group B (n=38) received the same integrated physical training with a regular protein diet over an 8-week period.

## Ethical approval
The trial received approval from the ethical committee (DEC), Department Ethical Committee (DEC), Prince Sattam bin Abdulaziz University, Al Kharj, Saudi Arabia with approval number RHPT/020/51. The DEC reviewed and approved the subject consent form, treatment protocols and outcome parameters measured in the trial. The trial followed the ethical guidelines of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and was registered in the clinical trial registry under registration number NCT05224453 on 4 February 2022.

## Participants
Subjects for the study were recruited from government hospitals in the Al-Kharj and Riyadh regions of Saudi Arabia by distributing study brochures and sending emails to referring doctors. The study focused on male patients aged 60–80 who had been diagnosed with post-COVID-19. Sarcopenia was diagnosed according to the guidelines of the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia, and the International Classification of Diseases−10 code for the disease Sarcopenia is M62.84. They were diagnosed by orthopaedic surgeons with 10 years of experience in diagnosing and treating sarcopenic patients. Those meeting all criteria—low muscle mass (Appendicular Skeletal Muscle Mass Index (ASMI) <7.0 kg/m²), low handgrip strength (<28 kg) and low physical performance (≤6 points on the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) test)—were included in the study. 
ASMI, calculated using the formula ASMI=0.193×body weight+0.107×height−4.157×gender−0.037×age−2.631 (weight in kg; height in m; gender: 1 for men), was used to identify low muscle mass. Handgrip strength was measured using a handheld dynamometer (Baseline, W50174, RMS UK, Somerset, UK). Participants were instructed to squeeze the handpiece as strongly as possible, and the recorded values were used to measure muscle strength. The SPPB assesses gait speed, chair stand and balance, providing a score from 0 (worst performance) to 12 (best performance) and is a predictive tool for disability and function in older adults.
Female participants were excluded due to differences in metabolic threshold during exercise, resistance to fatigue and hormonal changes. Subjects with a history of physical exercise, recent surgeries, joint problems, heart or lung issues, neurological conditions, systemic diseases or contraindications for physical training were also excluded. Participants showing COVID-19 symptoms during the study were excluded for safety reasons. The study strictly followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials guidelines, and illustrates the methods and procedures for recruiting study subjects in this trial.
 

## Interventions
Participants in both group A and group B (n=38) underwent integrated physical training for 8 weeks. The training protocols were administered by trained physical therapists while adhering to strict COVID-19 prevention measures. Details of each session were recorded in a treatment logbook, which was reviewed by the study supervisor after each session. Before each exercise session, baseline tests including body temperature, blood pressure, oxygen saturation rate, heart rate and physical fitness were conducted. If any of these measurements exceeded normal values (eg, body temperature >38.0°C, blood pressure >150/100 mm Hg, heart rate >90 beats per minute or <50 beats per minute), participants were instructed to refrain from training for that session. To prevent cross-infection, both the therapists and participants strictly followed precautionary measures mandated by local health authorities. These measures included maintaining a safe distance, sanitising hands and all contact surfaces before and after every intervention and wearing masks covering the nose and mouth.
Exercise intensity was determined using the maximum heart rate (MHR), calculated by subtracting the subject’s age from 220. Aerobic training was conducted at 40%–50% of the MHR for all participants, which was classified as low-intensity aerobic training (LAT). Each session started with 10 min of upper and lower limb muscle stretching. After stretching, participants performed 30 min of LAT, consisting of 20 min on a treadmill (Axos, Kettler Runner, China) and 10 min on a cycle ergometer (Axos, Kettler Runner, China), followed by a 10 min cool-down period. 
The exercise interventions were administered individually by four trained physical therapists at different times throughout the study. All participants underwent personalised strength training exercises based on their individual muscle assessments. The resistance for each muscle group was determined using the 10-repetition maximum (10-RM) principle, which identifies the maximum weight a person can lift for 10 repetitions. In our study, each session included three sets of exercises, progressively increasing in intensity: set 1 at 50% of 10-RM for 10 repetitions, set 2 at 75% of 10-RM for 10 repetitions and set 3 at 100% of 10-RM for 10 repetitions, with 1–2 min of rest between sets. The 10-RM was reassessed periodically to ensure adherence to the overload principle, with weight adjustments made by the treating therapist. These exercises were conducted 4 days a week for 8 weeks, targeting key muscle groups in the spine, upper limbs and lower limbs. 
In addition to the integrated physical training exercises, group A received a high-protein diet providing 1.0–1.3 g/kg of protein per ideal body weight per day (>1 g/kg aBW/day), while group B received a regular protein diet providing 0.8–1.0 g/kg of protein per ideal body weight per day (<1 g/kg aBW/day) for 8 weeks. These diets, including both animal and vegetable proteins, were prescribed by a qualified nutritionist based on individual preferences. Daily dietary intake was recorded in a diet logbook (7-day dietary record), and protein intake was calculated at each session by the nutritionist. To prevent bias, participants were instructed not to discuss their treatment protocol or diet information with fellow participants or assessing therapists. They were also advised against engaging in other forms of physical training or taking additional medications during the study period. After completing 8 weeks of training, participants were allowed to resume their regular physical activities and dietary habits according to their preference.

## Outcomes
The study’s outcomes were recorded by a blinded therapist at various time points: baseline (T0), 4 weeks (T1) to assess intermediate effects, 8 weeks (T2) to measure immediate effects and 6 months (T3) to evaluate short-term effects of the intervention. All expenses related to the intervention and measurements of the study participants were covered by the research grant.

## Hand grip strength
Hand grip strength was measured using a handheld dynamometer (Baseline, W50174, RMS UK, Somerset, UK), which is a simple and cost-effective test to assess upper extremity strength. Participants were instructed to squeeze the dynamometer to their maximum effort, and three values were recorded for each participant. The mean value was used for analysis, ensuring reliability and validity. 

## Muscle cross-sectional area (CSA)
Muscle CSA was evaluated using MRI scans (Philips, Achieva 1.5T, USA), a more costly measurement method. CSA of three major muscles—halfway at arm biceps, thigh quadriceps and calf muscles—were measured and included in the analysis. 

## Kinesiophobia
The Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia Questionnaire (TSK-11) was administered to assess the fear of movement among participants. This questionnaire comprises 11 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1—strongly agree to 4—strongly disagree). Scores range from 11 to 44 points, with higher scores indicating greater fear of movement. Participants were categorised into no fear (11), mild fear (12–22), moderate fear (23–33) and severe fear (34–44) based on their scores. 

## Quality of life
Quality of life was measured using the Sarcopenia Quality of Life (SarQoL) questionnaire, which participants completed themselves. This tool consists of 22 questions rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with a maximum score of 100 indicating higher quality of life. The SarQoL questionnaire is considered a robust measure of participants’ well-being. 

## Randomisation and blinding
Participants in this study were randomly assigned to one of two groups—integrated physical training with a high-protein diet or integrated physical training with a regular protein diet—using the two-block, simple random sampling method to ensure allocation concealment. Due to the nature of the study, it is difficult to blind the treating and assessing therapist. Blinding was maintained by masking participants to the dietary intervention, with meals provided in standardised, indistinguishable formats to eliminate bias in dietary perception.

## Sample size
The sample size for this study was calculated based on data from a previous study conducted by Yoo et al. It was determined assuming a 40% standard mean difference in the primary outcome of hand grip strength, with an SD of 2. Initially, each group was planned to include 35 subjects. To accommodate a 10% dropout rate expected at the 6- month follow-up, an additional three subjects were added to each group. Consequently, a total of n=76 subjects were selected and evenly divided into two groups, with n=38 participants in each group. The sample size calculation aimed to achieve 80% statistical power with a significance level (α) of 0.05.

## Statistical methods
The baseline demographic and physiological measurements of the participants were analysed for study homogeneity, using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Measurements were taken at the baseline, during the treatment, 4 weeks and 8 weeks after the treatment, and at the 6-month follow-up. Data analysis was done using an intention-to-treat principle method, where missing data were replaced with the last recorded values for each participant. The results were shown as mean and SD, along with a 95% CI, with the upper and lower limits. The primary and secondary outcomes were analysed using a 4×2 mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM) to compare group A and group B at different time points. Differences between the two groups were checked using Student’s t-test, and changes within each group were analysed using repeated measures analysis of variance (rANOVA). The confounding variables are controlled through statistical adjustments in the statistical tests. All statistical tests were done using IBM SPSS Statistics (V.20), with a significance level of α=0.05.

## Results
At the beginning of the study, one of the research team members screened 129 participants based on the selection criteria for inclusion in the study. Out of these, 18 participants had a history of cardiorespiratory, neurological or metabolic diseases; 16 had arthritis in their joints and 9 had recently undergone minor abdominal or general surgeries. In addition, 10 participants refused to participate in the study due to some personal reasons. Finally, 76 (n=76) participants were recruited through the random allocation method and assigned to groups A and B. The study followed the intention-to-treat principle method, in which all participants’ data were included in the analysis. At the end of the 8-week period, one participant in group A and two participants in group B did not complete the follow-up measurements. Similarly, at the 6-month measurement, one participant from each group did not provide measurements, which is shown in .
Demographic characteristics such as age, height, weight and body mass index (BMI), as well as physiological parameters like VO2 max (maximum oxygen volume) and heart rate, did not show any statistically significant differences between the groups (p>0.05). Also, the gait speed (p=0.085) and protein intake (p=0.118) of the study participants before the study intervention did not show any statistical difference between the groups.  The data for all outcome measures were presented as mean and SD with a 95% CI, including upper and lower limits. The 4×2 MMRM of the primary variable (handgrip strength measured by handheld dynamometer) showed a statistically significant difference (p=0.001) between group A and group B at baseline, 4 weeks, 8 weeks and at 6 months follow-up. The baseline measurement of handgrip strength did not show a significant change (p>0.05). However, at 4 weeks, group A showed an improvement of −1.7 (95% CI −2.82 to −0.57) compared with group B. Similar effects were observed after 8 weeks of intervention. At the 6-month follow-up, a greater effect of −5.0 (95% CI −6.21 to −3.78) was observed (p=0.001) in group A compared with group B, as shown in . The rANOVA for the primary outcome, handgrip strength, within group A, showed a significant difference (p=0.001), while no significant difference was observed in group B (p>0.05).  The 4×2 MMRM of the secondary variables (muscle CSA—MRI (thigh and calf), kinesiophobia— TSK 11 and quality of life—SarQol) showed a statistically significant difference (p=0.001) between group A and group B at baseline, 4 weeks, 8 weeks and at 6 months. However, there was no statistical difference in the muscle CSA of the arm region (p>0.05). The baseline measures of muscle CSA (arm, thigh & calf), kinesiophobia and quality of life did not show any statistical change (p>0.05). At 4 weeks’ measurement, improvements were observed in group A compared with group B: midthigh CSA showed −2.8 (95% CI −5.54 to −0.05), midcalf CSA showed −2.5 (95% CI −4.78 to −0.21), kinesiophobia level showed 1.9 (95% CI 0.62 to 3.17) and quality of life showed −4.3 (95% CI −7.0 to −1.5) (p=0.001), while midarm CSA showed −0.5 (95% CI −2.51 to 1.51) with no significant improvement. Similar improvements were observed after 8 weeks of intervention. ## Discussion
This study aimed to investigate and compare the clinical and psychological effects of combining integrated physical training with a high-protein diet versus a regular protein diet in community-dwelling older men with sarcopenia symptoms post-COVID-19. Medical and allied health professionals typically adhere to the Sarcopenia and Physical Frailty in Older Adults: Treatment guidelines for managing sarcopenia, which were also followed in this study amid the COVID-19 pandemic. Recently, Welch et al suggested a multidisciplinary approach, including early enhanced physiotherapy and a protein intake of up to 2 g/kg/day, to improve outcomes in older adults with COVID-19 and sarcopenia symptoms. Similarly, our study’s findings indicated that integrated physical training with a high-protein diet had superior effects compared with a regular protein diet on clinical and psychological outcomes in older men with COVID-19 and sarcopenia. In contrast, Robinson et al reviewed current evidence on nutrition and muscle strength as central components of sarcopenia, highlighting uncertainties regarding the effects of dietary pattern changes and their interactions with exercise training. 
Integrated physical training activities were safe procedures designed to engage the entire body and preserve the physiological properties of muscles. These exercises activated the body’s energy systems, promoting physical well-being and healthy ageing in older adults. LAT was found to be effective in maintaining muscle physiological properties, thereby preventing the adverse health consequences of ageing. These changes were associated with alterations in inflammatory response, mitochondrial activity and insulin sensitivity. LAT also improved peripheral circulation, increased local blood flow to cells and enhanced overall muscle endurance. It improved muscle oxidative capacity and enhanced mitochondrial function within cells. Similarly, our findings were consistent with Beaudart et al, indicating that physical exercise had a positive impact on muscle mass and function in healthy individuals aged 60 years and older. However, the combined effect of dietary supplementation on muscle function appeared to be limited. A well-balanced diet with optimal protein supplements is necessary to maintain and manage muscle properties in older adults experiencing symptoms of sarcopenia. Tieland et al conducted a survey across Europe and found that approximately 35% of healthy older adults living in communities did not meet the estimated average requirement of protein per day (0.7 g/kg body weight/day). The disparity between protein intake and demand could lead to a decrease in muscle size, strength and overall performance, ultimately impacting quality of life. 
Our research observed significant clinical improvements in hand muscle strength between two experimental groups. Generally, older adults with sarcopenia develop anabolic resistance in muscles, which inhibits protein synthesis activity and diminishes muscle physiological properties. Thus, there was an increased need for protein intake of approximately 1.0–1.2 g/kg body weight/day when combined with physical training, as recommended by Dideriksen et al and Nistor-Cseppento et al. According to Koopman et al, adopting a high-protein diet (group A) boosted amino acid secretion from the gut, reduced the postmeal requirement of amino acids and facilitated their introduction into muscle postmeal, thereby decreasing anabolic resistance and alleviating strain on the digestive system. Within-group analysis using rANOVA revealed substantial improvements in hand muscle strength in both groups regardless of their protein consumption levels. Additionally, the minimum clinically important difference (3.00) score for hand grip strength indicated a greater effect in group A compared with group B.
Our study also observed that the CSA of thigh and calf muscles was larger in group A compared with group B, which is consistent with the findings of Kim et al Cadore et al found that the sequence of exercise, such as starting with strength training followed by aerobic training, is an important factor in changing muscle tone and size. In our study, we implemented LAT in the first phase, followed by strengthening exercises in the second phase. This sequence may have contributed to the minimal or negligible changes observed in the CSA of the thigh and calf muscles. However, the precise physiological and biochemical changes behind the sequence of these exercises were not examined. Moreover, the minor changes in muscle CSA of the thigh and calf regions could be attributed to either the parameters of the exercise training or the type of dietary supplements. 
Additionally, our study found that combining physical training with a high-protein diet positively affected the psychological well-being of individuals with COVID-19 and symptoms of sarcopenia. Improved muscle strength enhanced daily performance in older men, which reduced their fear of falling or movement (kinesiophobia). Variations in protein intake between groups accounted for differences in outcome variables. It was observed that older adults had a more pronounced physiological response in muscles to a high-protein diet compared with young adults. These clinical and psychological changes improved overall well-being and quality of life (QoL), potentially preventing sarcopenia development and supporting community-based prevention efforts, as supported by Wang et al and Piotrowicz et al. Regardless of COVID-19 symptoms, integrated physical training with a high-protein diet was positively associated with functional activities and QoL in sarcopenia patients. However, controversies and potential adverse effects related to protein consumption and synthesis in older adults with sarcopenia symptoms were noted by Paddon-Jones and Leidy. In summary, our study indicates that both physical training and a high protein intake are crucial for maintaining muscle mass and function in older men with sarcopenia during the COVID-19 pandemic. These physical training regimens and dietary patterns should be continued throughout their lifetimes to prevent further consequences of sarcopenia and could also be prescribed for individuals without COVID-19 symptoms. Potential confounding factors such as medication use, age and adherence are addressed by implementing strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, balancing these factors across groups through randomisation, monitoring adherence and statistical adjustments as needed.
The authors encountered several limitations and difficulties during the execution of the study. First, the study included only male participants, which might have limited the generalisability of the results due to potential physiological differences and variations in disease symptom presentation in female participants. Second, subjects in both groups underwent physical training at the same venue and at the same time, which could have compromised participant masking by allowing them to share study protocols, potentially influencing the study outcomes. Thirdly, due to the large number of outcome measures analysed, the study did not include many basic outcome measures such as muscle endurance, muscle power, range of motion, balance, coordination, risk of falls and gait components, which could have provided more detailed information on this topic. Fourth, the study did not find an association between clinical and psychological traits after completion, which could have provided insights into the relationship between these variables. Fifth, the study did not include a control group, which could have shown the true effects of integrated physical training with a high-protein diet compared with a regular protein diet. Therefore, future studies should focus on masking participants and including a control group. Future research is also recommended to explore the association between clinical and psychological traits and to investigate the long-term effects of this training regimen in depth.

## Conclusion
Overall, this study examined how integrated physical training with a high-protein diet increased muscle strength and mass in the thigh and calf regions, reduced kinesiophobia and improved quality of life compared with integrated physical training with a regular protein diet in older men with sarcopenia living in the community with post-COVID-19. However, neither the high-protein nor regular protein diet had any effect on muscle mass in the arm region. The findings of this study provide significant insights and clinical evidence for health professionals and patients in the field of geriatric rehabilitation, particularly in managing sarcopenia symptoms in older men during or after COVID-19.
