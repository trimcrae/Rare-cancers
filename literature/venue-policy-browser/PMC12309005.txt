## Abstract

## Background
An increasing number of older adults receive home healthcare in their homes, some of whom lose their capacity and ability to take care of their own health, including oral health. Poor oral health may have serious consequences that can lead to poor nutrition, diseases, isolation, and even death. There is an urgent need to involve users in developing targeted interventions to ensure that eligible older adults exercise their right to receive appropriate dental care. The purpose of the study was to explore what user groups emphasised in co-creating a targeted dental care intervention for older adults receiving home healthcare.

## Methods
In this study, an exploratory design with an inductive and interpretive approach was employed. In two six-hour workshops, eleven participants representing three older adults, one next of kin, two nurses, and five dental care providers exchanged and discussed their perspectives on dental care as part of a co-creation process. Data were collected from group and plenary discussions during the workshops and were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.

## Results
The different user groups conveyed confusing and contradictory understandings and interpretations of the dental healthcare landscape. This uncertainty resulted in missed information regarding dental care, leading to situations where they do not always seek dental healthcare services until an acute issue arises. A blurred perception of older adults’ needs for support, combined with shortcomings in collaboration, communication, and the exchange of digital information systems, has led to a lack of knowledge about one another and inadequate dental healthcare for this vulnerable group. The various user groups suggested different solutions; however, several key issues emerged as urgent to address. There is a need to build and integrate systems into home healthcare’s digital administrative and health record systems to support the clarification and identification of eligible patients, as well as to develop a digital coordination and communication system to connect the two levels of service.

## Conclusions
Unpacking the perspectives of the different user groups uncovered that an intervention must take into account the views of all groups. Understanding and incorporating the perceptions of all the different user groups were crucial for developing an intervention that enables a preventive and collaborative dental care approach.

## Background
The ageing population worldwide entails an increasing number of older adults who are facing various health challenges and who live in their own home, where they receive healthcare services [ , ]. The complexity of healthcare services and the comprehensiveness of healthcare needs pose challenges for the provision of care [ ]. Older adults who receive home healthcare (HHC) often have poor oral health [ , ]. Older adults who lose their capacity and ability to take care of their own health, including oral health, may face serious consequences in the form of bad nutrition, diseases, isolation and even death [ , ].
In Norway, where the present study was conducted, older adults who receive HHC for more than three months are entitled to free dental care in the public dental health service (PDS) [ ]; however, only 18.2% of these older adults make use of this right [ ]. One reason for this may be that HHC is organised at the municipality level whilst the PDS is organised at the county level. HHC is part of Norway’s long-term care that is provided and coordinated by the municipalities. All inhabitants can apply for general healthcare in the municipalities, but only those who are assessed as having necessary healthcare needs are granted it. Despite much effort to connect the different levels of healthcare services, most of the research, both internationally and nationally, has addressed the hospital–home dyad, where dental care is not included. The providers involved in the PDS experience several challenges related to organisation and communication when providing dental care for older adults [ , ]. Compared to other healthcare services, the two levels of services in Norway are not equipped with digital solutions for exchanging information and for collaborating with each other. Also, the increased specialisation and fragmentation of healthcare obstructs the providers’ efforts to ensure continuity and coherence in care provision [ , ].
There is therefore an urgent need to develop more targeted interventions to connect the levels of services to ensure that eligible older adults exercise their right to receive appropriate dental care. User involvement is recognised as an important strategy for expanding the understanding of the needs of the end users, which in turn may enable better research outcomes [ , , ]. Co-creation has gradually emerged as a method to involve different user groups for the purpose of developing interventions and the improvement of healthcare. It has evolved into a process of joint knowledge generation, where academic researchers collaborate with stakeholders and/or end-users [ , , ]. However, researchers need to consider the potential for power imbalance in research [ ], which has been revealed in clinical commissioning groups that are responsible for the planning and commissioning of healthcare services [ ]. Henderson [ ] found that healthcare providers in clinical practice maintained the power imbalance by being negative towards the involvement of older adults in clinical decision-making processes due to their belief that ‘they know best’. Researchers’ attitudes as well as organisational and practical aspects must be taken seriously to avoid non-tokenistic participation in co-creation processes [ ].
Different user groups attribute different values to different aspects, which enables the fostering of mutual respect even if they prioritise different aspects [ ]. We have not identified previous studies that report on involving user groups – i.e., older adults who receive HHC and providers – in the generation of targeted dental care interventions for older adults receiving home healthcare.

## Aim
The aim of the study was to explore what the user groups emphasised in the co-creation of a targeted dental care intervention to older adults receiving home healthcare.

## Design and methods
This study derives from data that was collected in two workshops where the purpose was to co-create a targeted intervention to enable the connection of oral health and home healthcare services (CORAL). We applied an exploratory design with an inductive, experiential and interpretive approach, followed by a reflexive thematic analysis [ ]. We followed the UK Medical Research Council’s framework for developing and evaluating complex interventions [ , , ], and the recommendation of involving stakeholders and end-users in the co-creation process [ ]. The framework was combined with the integrated care model, which reflects the need for the individual, professional, organisational, and policy levels to be united in order to ensure continuity and coherence at the micro, meso, and macro levels of care [ ]. Principles from experience-based co-design, as described by Bate et al. [ ] guided the development process to avoid tokenism [ ]. This study complies with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ).

## Participants and organisation of workshops
Three user groups were invited to participate: one group representing older adults and next of kin as one dyad, one representing HHC and one representing the PDS. Several measures were conducted to recruit individuals representing all three user groups. Initially, the recruitment of users was based on the CORAL researchers’ professional networks of HHC and the PDS who were involved at the proposal writing stage. In addition, the Norwegian Pensioners’ Association joined the project. A leaflet about the project was distributed to older adults by staff in the HHC in the area in which workshops were planned to be organised. The recruitment efforts resulted in consent being obtained from four individuals from both urban and rural areas, comprising older adults (one of whom was a relative), two dentists and three dental hygienists representing the PDS, and two nurses representing HHC. However, the ongoing COVID-19 pandemic posed some challenges, such as cold symptoms, fear of contamination, recent contact with infected individuals, and additional duties in HHC, leading to one older adult and one HHC representative withdrawing from participation the day before and on the same day as the first workshop. After the first workshop, the researchers discussed whether further recruitment of participants should take place; however, the input from the existing participants was comprehensive, and introducing new individuals might disrupt the fruitful discussions. The uncertainty surrounding COVID-19 was also a consideration. The costs for participating were covered by the project.
The two workshops were carefully planned in line with recommendations for how to create a safe, friendly and open-minded environment in order to foster free discussions in the co-creation processes [ ]. Each workshop was facilitated by one researcher from the research team. The workshops were held in conference rooms at a hotel to create a quiet and comfortable space for co-creation. Considering the various health issues of older adult representatives, we chose a venue that was easy to reach by public transport, that was accessible for people with disabilities, and that was guaranteed to have reliable routines to ensure compliance with the prevailing COVID-19 measures. To enable familiarity with each other, all participants and facilitators were invited to have dinner together the evening before each workshop. To minimise power imbalance between users and researchers [ , ], everybody participated in various ice-breaking exercises, which entailed drawing activities and other creative interactions with other workshop participants. Both workshops began with the co-creation of a set of rules that the group considered important. This set of rules is often referred to as a ‘group contract’ or a ‘group agreement’ and is used to foster participation and inclusivity during workshops, as well as to create safe spaces for collective learning [ ]. The participants agreed on values such as ‘respect each other’, ‘make space for everybody’, ‘share experiences’ and ‘one person speaks at a time’. To facilitate fruitful, engaged and targeted discussions in the groups and in plenary, two patient cases were prepared. The cases were presented in the form of two short videos depicting the trajectory of an older adult transitioning from independent living to increasing dependence on homecare nursing. The decline of the case’s cognitive status also impacted on the case’s capacity to care for their oral health. The cases served as boundary objects [ ] and provided a point of departure for elaborating on the older adults’ needs and how to address these needs throughout the different phases of the illness trajectory, with a particular focus on dental care.
The procedure for each workshop was to combine group discussion with subsequent plenary discussions. In the first workshop, the groups consisted of the same user groups to ensure that the older adults felt secure to provide their perspectives without interruption from the healthcare personnel. In the second workshop, the different user groups were mixed. The topics and ideas identified from the first workshop formed the basis for the discussions in the second workshop. Each workshop lasted for six hours. The material used in this paper is derived from audio recordings and transcribed data from the two workshops, plenary in meetings, user group conversations, and the researchers’ notes. A total of 10 h and 1 min of audio recordings are included. The workshops took place in December 2021 and March 2022.

## Data analysis
We conducted a reflexive thematic analysis applying an interpretive and iterative process of understanding the patterned meaning of participants’ experiences [ ]. Braun & Clarkes [ ] six-step approach enables a balancing of the variation of experiences and meaning for developing and conceptualising reflective themes.
In the initial familiarisation, we identified that the participants were concerned about some overall similar ideas of meaning. During the coding process, we clustered codes that formed preliminary sub-themes. One example was related to patient rights and legal regulations for free dental healthcare (e.g., codes reflecting ‘lack of overview of older adults eligible for free dental healthcare’, ‘don’t know who to contact’, ‘lacking procedures’, ‘different practices in municipalities’, ‘some municipalities have systems’, ‘there is no system’, ‘several older adults do not know their rights’, ‘leaders’ responsibility to make systems’, etc.). Another example in this phase that was addressed by all user groups was older adults’ needs for dental healthcare (e.g., ‘help for self-care is important’, ‘several older adults do not want others to look into their mouth and refuse’, ‘being well-groomed is important’, ‘it is the individual’s choice’, ‘they need supervision for their oral healthcare’, ‘equipment to brush teeth’, ‘lack knowledge about the importance of oral healthcare’, ‘oral problems among frail older adults’, ‘practical issues’, ‘economy’, etc.).
We identified that the different user groups emphasised various facets and experiences related to these codes. To gain an in-depth understanding of what each user group was concerned about, we decided to first re-read the codes and generate preliminary sub-themes of the expressions by members of the separate user groups. We went back and forth between reading the transcripts, listening to the audio recordings, and discussing our analysis.
The initial sub-themes from within the separate user groups provided a point of departure for obtaining new sub-themes across the user groups. To generate themes beyond the different workshops and the groups’ experiences, we searched for both manifest and latent meaning in the material [ ]. The analysis provided insight that enhanced our previous understanding of the challenges involved with the current provision of dental care services for older adults. We used thematic maps as a dynamic working document to figure out the interconnection between the codes, sub-themes and final themes.
The refinement of the themes reflected multifaceted perspectives and several blurred areas that need to be accounted for in developing a targeted dental healthcare intervention for older adults. The organising concepts resulted in four main themes.

## Ethical considerations
The study has been reported to SIKT – Norwegian Agency for Shared Services in Education and Research, reference number: 301792. Participation in the study was voluntary and based on the provision of written informed consent. Data has been presented in such a way that makes it challenging to identify individuals in the study. The data material has been stored on a secure server for sensitive data, in line with University of Oslo’s guidelines for storage and management of empirical data. The dataset used in the current study is available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

## Findings
The results reflect that the user groups conveyed confusing and contradictory understandings and interpretations of the current practice within the dental healthcare landscape for older adults, which influences the content of the intervention.

## Confusion about the regulations regarding eligibility of older adults
In general, the participants discussed and emphasised that the legislation is old and has not been updated regarding general changes in the healthcare services and the implications of having an ageing population. The PDS as well as HHC providers were concerned because they found the regulations for whether a patient is eligible for free dental care to be confusing. They discussed and emphasised how the national regulations should be interpreted, and expressed different understandings related to the patient rights to free public dental care as enshrined in Norwegian legislation.
We uncovered a lack of consensus among representatives of the PDS working in the counties and of HHC in the municipalities, which leads to uncertainty about which patients are really eligible and who is responsible for informing them of their entitlement to free dental care:

This is part of the problem, that the counties define it differently […] I suppose it is part of the main problem here, because we interpret it differently [the paragraph describing who is entitled to free dental care]. It depends on which municipality you live in, which county you live in, what kind of services you get. (PDS, Workshop (WS)1)

The older adults themselves were unaware of their entitlement to free dental care. They called for general information about their rights to free dental care, and expressed concern about the consequences for several older adults who were not aware of their rights. There was also a lack of clarity among all the user groups regarding how the patients in general should be informed about their legal right. One older adult suggested a solution for how they could be informed:
I think it was a very good suggestion that you [PDS] can show up at pensioner gatherings […]. (Older adult, WS2)
During the co-creation process, all user groups developed an understanding that the varying interpretation of the regulations impacted on the patients’ rights and accessibility to the PDS.

## Blurred perceptions of older adults’ need for support
The user groups expressed contradictory perceptions of the older adults’ need for dental care support regarding when and how to intervene, and for whom, along a continuum from being an independent older adult towards becoming dependent.
The older adults and the HHC representatives described how most older adults preferred as little help as possible, and that they believed it was important that healthcare providers help older adults to achieve self-management:

Help for self-help is important […] When you are older, there are often temporary needs […] you may not want as much help then if you are used to taking care of yourself, unless they have to have a little help […] if you manage yourself, it will be done when you feel like it and you will not have to wait for help. (Older adult, WS1)

An HHC representative expressed that they:

[…] must follow up and think that she [the patient] needs help. They must ask her if she needs help with brushing her teeth, if it has been a long time since she has been to the dentist […]. (HHC, WS1)

Representatives of the PDS described how they had experienced a disparity between what the older adults and they themselves considered to be necessary dental health treatment, meaning that the perception of what is considered bad dental health status may differ between the older adults and the PDS:

In the PDS, we will often impose our needs on everyone, but they are not their needs. (PDS, WS1)

Representatives of HHC and the PDS were concerned that the older adults were not aware of the consequences of inadequate dental care, and stated that the patients sometimes “need instruction or guidance in how to do it [maintain oral hygiene]” (HHC, WS1). The HHCs expressed that it was sometimes difficult to get the older adults to accept help with dental care because they were reluctant to accept help. Representatives of the PDS suggested that increased knowledge could lead to better self-care of their dental health.
Overall, the older adults felt that it was important for them to be well-groomed, with good breath, a beautiful smile and clean clothes. As one of them put it: “There’s no use wearing a pretty dress […] if we don’t have pretty teeth” (Older adult, WS1).

## Diffusion of responsibility for ensuring continuity of dental care
Experiences of discontinuity of care were highlighted by all user groups. They discussed and elaborated on why discontinuity occurred and the diffusion of responsibility for ensuring dental care for older adults. The analysis revealed diffusion that led to discontinuity at organisational, professional and practical levels.
One organisational issue was related to a concern that several healthcare providers are often involved in the daily care of the older adults, which represented a risk for a reduced sense of individual responsibility as they know that there may be others who can also intervene. Such a risk was expressed as, for example: “tomorrow perhaps another nurse will come who doesn’t know her [the patient]” (HHC, WS1). The analysis uncovered inadequate routines and confusion of responsibilities for ensuring that the patient received access to qualified dental care. For example, all groups discussed whose responsibility it was to accompany the older adult to visit the dental clinic if they were not able to do it themselves or had no relatives who could assist them.
A concern about professional diffusion was expressed by the older adults. They experienced that some representatives of HHC appeared to have a fragmented and limited scope in their practice:

I don’t think the HHC look after older adults’ teeth and they don’t ask them whether they go to the dentist [Older adult: “No”]. It’s the medicines they’re interested in. (Older adult, WS1)

Some practical challenges were expressed by the older adults that hampered the continuity of care:

She said the dentist had retired so she needed someone to organise contact with the PDS or a dentist. She needs help with that […] and help to travel there. (HHC, WS1)

To compensate for such fuzziness and diffusion of responsibility, all user groups reflected on whether having a coordinator might be helpful; however, it became evident during their discussions that they had different expectations of the role of a coordinator. Representatives of HHC stated that a coordinator would be helpful in order to have a dedicated person who had overall responsibility for clarifying the older adults’ needs, thereby contributing to continuity. The PDS, however, expressed that having a patient coordinator would primarily clarify whom they should contact in the municipalities. This was compared to the current practices, where they are frustrated that they do not always know who is responsible for the patient.
During the discussions, the participants agreed upon and suggested a division of work, whereby HHC should conduct a thorough mapping of everything that could affect oral health, including oral hygiene, whilst the PDS should conduct a mapping of oral health status and schedule follow-up checks and treatment when needed based on the outcome of the oral health status mapping.

## Disconnecting communication, information and collaboration structures
A general feeling of being disconnected led to discussions across the groups during both workshops. In the deepest sense, the issues they raised generally boiled down to a lack of awareness and knowledge of each other, and a lack of an appropriate information and communication system for ensuring easy access to each other. It emerged that the PDS workers were almost invisible to the HHC workers, and in fact became ‘strangers’ in the Norwegian healthcare system:
We are, in a way, on the outside of a joint healthcare service […], all the other healthcare services have a platform [electronic communication platform] but we do not have access to that platform […]. (PDS, WS2)
We as a PDS must be more visible both to the HHC and among users. We should show better what we can contribute with– for example, attending pensioner meetings/associations and talking about who are entitled to the rights [to free dental healthcare] […], but also once a year we should share a professional day with the HHC […]. (PDS WS2)
Compared to other healthcare services, such as the collaboration between hospitals and HHC using digital communication through an e-messaging system, the participants emphasised that the PDS and HHC still only have old-fashioned paper-based exchanges of information, and telephone was the only communication channel that was available for contacting each other. The following quote illustrates one of the consequences of the disconnected organisations:
[…] if someone has a wound in the mouth that we want to see heal over the course of a few weeks, then we send pictures, send feedback, but we never hear anything about that and then we have to call the patient in and they have to take a taxi maybe an hour to get there so that we can look over a tiny wound that may have disappeared, which someone else [the HHC] could have done much more easily […]. (PDS, WS2)
Both HHC and the PDS not only expressed frustration with the lack of digital connecting systems but they also highlighted that the current organisation lacked meeting points to facilitate making acquaintance with each other, as was described in the first workshop: “[…] this thing with meeting points is a challenge and an opportunity, if we can make it, because you have to sort of talk together, that’s the first commandment” (PDS, WS1).
This part of the conversation indicates that the PDS must be more visible and available for both the older adults and the HHC, because “the key is that we need to understand the [different] services better to know who to contact” (PDS, WS2). The older adults sometimes acted as a connector between the different groups of providers, and compensated for the “logistical problem” (PDS, WS2) and the “information problem” (HHC, WS2). As one older adult suggested, “then perhaps you should ask the patient who is your dentist and who is your general practitioner on an annual basis” (Older adult, WS2).

## Discussion
The aim of the study was to explore what the user groups emphasised in the co-creation of a targeted dental care intervention for older adults receiving home healthcare. The different groups of users brought diverse experiences, views, perceptions and perspectives into the co-creation process [ ]. Achieving insight beyond the perspectives of the single user groups resulted in a comprehensive understanding of the complexity of the dental care services terrain for older adults.
We identified confusion, uncertainty and lack of clarity at the individual, professional, organisational and policy levels that hamper the provision of oral healthcare. This is in line with Valentijn et al.’s [ ] evidence-based conceptual framework of levels of care for augmenting integrated care. Balancing and connecting the levels appears to be complex for all user groups. At the individual and professional level, the tension between balancing patient autonomy and professional standards is challenging. All groups were concerned about the older adults’ autonomy and their rights to make decisions about their own lives and what help they needed and when. On the other hand, HHC services sometimes find it challenging to identify the need when the older adults’ deteriorating health and cognitive status affects their capacity to care for their own oral health [ ]. On the other hand, HHC services sometimes find it challenging to identify the need for support when older adults’ deteriorating health and cognitive status affect their capacity to care for their own oral health [ ]. At times, it is difficult for HHC providers to know how to approach patients who resist or refuse care [ ].
One proposed strategy to overcome this tension is to build ethical knowledge and trust over time. The possibilities to build trust are challenged when several providers visit the patient in their home– thus, building relational continuity of care is demanding [ ].
At the organisational level, the specialisation and fragmentation of services has led to a PDS that appears largely invisible for HHC, and thus they admit that there is less focus on the prioritisation of oral health. According to the Norwegian legislation, the PDS is responsible for dental care, but this requires the provision of information about eligible patients before they can be approached. The services’ disconnection and lack of knowledge about each other make collaboration and coordination blurred– the division of tasks becomes fragmented and their working processes inefficient.
The participants emphasised the need to be digitally connected, which has been shown to be essential in other settings [ ]. Digitalisation connects providers across organisational levels of care, and also changes their working processes and relationships with each other. However, even if digital communication is available, having additional appropriate meeting points to facilitate acquaintance and build collaborative relationships and trust is essential in order to foster quality care [ ].
The older adults presented challenges regarding the availability of the PDS. Such organisational obstructions have been emphasised from the perspectives of older adults and the PDS in previous studies [ , , , ]. In our findings, these challenges are also evident for HHC. Another challenge was that some older adults had experienced a lack of accessibility for people with disabilities that erected physical barriers for them, which we have not identified in previous studies. However, Uhlen-Strand et al.[ ] identified that the PDS has experienced challenges related to practical organisation that have made treatment difficult– for example, that the equipment at the dental clinic is not adapted for older adults with impaired functioning.
The user groups expressed frustration with regard to why it should be so difficult to be digitally connected, as is the case in other services. However, one concern regarding the implementation of new solutions is the difficulties involved in getting a new system integrated beyond the project period. Benefitting from the lessons learned in previous studies, we decided that building on and expanding the existing digital systems would be crucial. Andreassen et al. [ ] found that many digital projects have failed because they are not integrated into existing digital systems, which leads to a risk that they will not be prolonged when the project is completed. Another crucial consideration is the fact that different municipalities organise the work in different ways [ ].
Discussions in our workshops at the system level uncovered variation in the interpretation of the legislation, which impacted the PDS’ work with older adults. Our findings show that the provision of dental care may fail at the very beginning of a patient trajectory because older adults lack adequate information about their rights and are thus not followed up until an emergency arises. The services are characterised by a regime of ad-hoc solutions, where older adults mainly receive dental care only when acute oral problems arise [ , ]. Prioritising dealing with acute situations precludes a preventive approach being adopted in today’s healthcare. This is also reflected at the policy level– for example, only 4% of Norway’s GPD is spent on preventive and public health [ ].
Given the professional, technological, organisational and legislative challenges that are associated with ensuring that older adults’ rights are safeguarded, we decided to develop a generic intervention that may be contextualised in local organisation [ ]. Several factors may have impacted the outcome of the co-creation workshops. We identified that HHC and the PDS were more active in advocating their perspectives, whereas the voices of older adults were less prominent. One reason may be that HHC and the PDS had more knowledge about the organisation. Other reasons could be that they were less active or that there was a feeling of power imbalance, as highlighted by Stuhlfauth et al. [ ]. Nevertheless, our analysis showed that, at the second workshop, the participants listened more to each other, supported new perspectives, and discussed in more detail what was emphasised by other user groups, compared to the first workshop. In a scoping review, Knight-Davidson et al. [ ] found that openness is important in user involvement. They claim that one may consider that older adults are not always able to be active because the situations are not always comparable. We organised two workshops, which may have provided the different user groups with time for reflection between the sessions. Our effort to facilitate safe and constructive discussions may also have fostered an environment that opened for different perspectives and fruitful discussions. In addition, we strived to follow Terkelsen et al.’s [ ] recommendation of having an agreed aim and a realistic scope, and acknowledging the participants’ time, competencies and resources.
The insight from the outcome of the co-creation process guided us in developing a targeted dental healthcare intervention for older adults. In line with the CReDECI’s framework which provide a 13-items recommendation for development and evaluation of complex intervention, our study represents the development phase [ , ] structure for developing complex interventions provided a logic model of public dental care. To be able to provide in-time dental care for older adults, the findings suggest a need for taking a proactive approach compared to current practice and build a preventive management systemic intervention [ ] with two main components. One component is to build and integrate into the two levels of services a system that support HHC for clarifying and identifying patients who are eligible for free dental care. Furthermore, routines to ensure that these patients are informed must be integrated into their administrative and health record system. The second component is to develop a digital coordination and communication system to connect the two levels of services. The intervention is currently being pilot tested, involving one larger municipality and one affiliated county, in collaboration with two vendors who provide digital administrative and patient records. The process is governed by the CORAL research group.

## Methodological strengths and limitations
It was difficult to recruit participants due to the ongoing COVID­19 pandemic, and this was particularly true for older adults. A possible explanation is that dental care may have been a low priority for them during this period. As this group of users were also more reluctant to share their perspectives in the plenary meetings, it might have strengthened the perspectives from older adults if we had been able to recruit more recipients of home healthcare services. Whether different levels of frailty among older adults receiving homecare should be taken into account when designing or tailoring interventions needs to be clarified in further studies. All participants were fluent in Norwegian. It might have strengthened the study if we were able to include immigrants’ perspective.
We have used a reflective approach in the analysis, which provided us with organising concepts regarding the main themes. This approach does not provide in-depth understanding of the power balance between the different user groups. We are aware that we might have generated knowledge about possible power imbalance across the user groups by applying other analytical approaches, such as critical discourse analysis [ ].
On the other hand, bringing the main user groups from the services together with the older adults offered a comprehensive understanding of the complexity of developing an integrated dental care intervention.

## Conclusion
The co-creation process contributed with in-depth knowledge about different user groups’ perspectives on dental healthcare for older adults receiving home healthcare. The dental health- and home healthcare service is a complex landscape that influences the development of a targeted dental care intervention. Firstly, older adults with a legal right to receive free dental care are not informed of their rights and thus do not always attend dental healthcare services. Secondly, shortcomings in collaboration, communication and exchange of digital information across the levels of care have resulted in today’s practice, which is characterised by solving problems when acute situations arise for the patient. Developing and testing a digital systemic and generic preventive intervention is suggested. Understanding and building on all the different user groups’ perceptions is crucial to the development of a targeted dental care intervention.

The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Norwegian Personal Data Regulations. The study has been reported to SIKT– Norwegian Agency for Shared Services in Education and Research, reference number: 301792. SIKT assessed that the processing of personal data was regulated in the Personal Data Regulations and did not raise any ethical issues that required further ethical approval from another national regulatory authority. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. All participants in the study received written and verbal information about the study, and participation was voluntary and based on the provision of written informed consent. Data has been presented in such a way that makes it challenging to identify individuals in the study.
