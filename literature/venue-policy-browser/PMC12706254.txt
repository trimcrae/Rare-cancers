## Abstract

## Abstract

## Objective
This proof-of-concept study explored the feasibility and acceptability of research cafés as a community-based model to engage racially minoritised communities in health research, with a focus on mental health.

## Design
Adopting a community peer research approach, a research team led by researchers from racially minoritised backgrounds, partnered with four voluntary organisations to conduct four research cafés. A mixed-methods feasibility design combined descriptive quantitative questionnaire data with thematic analysis of discussion notes to evaluate the impact of these cafés in South West London.

## Setting
The study took place in Wandsworth, Kingston, Croydon and Sutton, in community venues provided by the voluntary organisations.

## Participants
A total of 75 participants from racially minoritised backgrounds attended the sessions. Participants were intentionally selected based on age, ethnicity, location, mental well-being experiences and willingness to engage in mental health research.

## Main outcome measures
Quantitative data were collected on participant attendance, demographics and feedback. Qualitative data captured participant perceptions and experiences related to healthcare and research.

## Results
The study took place between October 2023 and March 2024. Out of the 112 individuals who registered, 75 people attended with 74 contributing to the analysis (excluding one on the day sign-up). Participants were predominantly Asian/Asian British (62%) and Black/Black British (31%). All participants reported feeling comfortable and respected. Understanding of research improved for 96% and 95% expressed interest in further research involvement, most commonly the idea of joining a peer research network (64%). Participants also highlighted a need for training in research methods and communication skills for ongoing involvement. Five main themes emerged from the café discussions: (1) systemic barriers to accessing safe healthcare and mistrust of UK healthcare systems; (2) the need for cultural competence and sensitivity in healthcare and research; (3) research as a positive step for change; (4) barriers to research participation and (5) the importance of incentives and feedback for research participation.

## Conclusions
Preliminary findings suggest that diverse and inclusive community-based partnerships are the basis for developing research cafés as a feasible and acceptable model for engaging racially minoritised communities in health research. They complement existing participatory approaches by creating inclusive, peer-facilitated spaces that build trust, improve awareness and generate high intention for future involvement and participation. Future studies are needed to test the model’s scalability across different geographic and demographic contexts and evaluate its long-term impact on research literacy, participation and equity.

Keywords: Community-Based Participatory Research, Health Equity, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH, MENTAL HEALTH

## STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY
The study employed a mixed-methods feasibility design, integrating both quantitative and qualitative data to capture a comprehensive view of participant experiences and engagement.
University researchers from racially minoritised groups brought key skills, knowledge and cultural competence in shaping and executing a community-based design to deliver the project. Community and peer researchers in the voluntary organisations helped to shape the research design, conduct and dissemination, enhancing the relevance of findings to racially minoritised groups.
The use of specific local voluntary organisations for recruitment facilitated access to racially minoritised groups, but reliance on these networks and recruitment via Eventbrite may have introduced some bias, limiting the generalisability of findings.
Tight timeframes limited community and peer researchers’ involvement in the data analysis phase, which may have reduced opportunities for iterative refinement of the model and affected the depth of qualitative insights.

## Introduction
 Improving research accessibility and inclusiveness for diverse communities in the United Kingdom is fundamental to understanding and effectively addressing health and healthcare inequalities. Healthcare research can better meet the needs of racially minoritised groups and promote equitable access to services if it prioritises diverse perspectives and coproduction. For example, the underrepresentation of racially minoritised groups in mental health research restricts the generalisability and applicability of research findings. Current patient and public involvement (PPI) approaches in mental health services have been criticised for not effectively engaging racially minoritised groups, limiting the potential for these services to adequately address the unique needs of these communities. These disparities are concerning given the inequities experienced by Black, Asian and racially minoritised groups in relation to mental health in the United Kingdom. 
Addressing inclusivity in research requires distinguishing between research participation, which involves actively taking part in studies and research involvement, which focuses on engagement in the design, conduct and dissemination of research. Both are essential for the coproduction of knowledge and shared decision-making yet racially minoritised groups remain underrepresented in both areas. Traditional PPI initiatives often lack the cultural competence and targeted strategies needed to effectively reach racially minoritised groups, signalling the need for innovative approaches. One such approach is community-based participatory research (CBPR), which aims to address systemic barriers and structural inequalities by involving communities as active partners with researchers and institutions throughout the research process. For promoting inclusive public engagement, participatory approaches should involve racially minoritised groups in decision-making processes, breaking down cultural barriers and building trust. However, achieving this requires a cultural shift that values inclusivity in research teams, provides comprehensive training for researchers and collects rigorous data on engagement experiences.
In South West London Integrated Care System (SWL ICS), we piloted ‘research cafés’ in partnership with King’s College London and four voluntary organisations as a mechanism to increase research literacy and diversity of participation in research, focusing on mental health. There is an under-representation of marginalised and racially minoritised participants in research studies. These cafés aimed to encourage local communities to develop skills and confidence to become involved in research and build trust, improving the relevance and uptake of research findings. Research cafés are structured, informal settings, specifically designed to create an environment where public members can engage in two-way conversations with researchers and each other over a cup of tea or coffee, by leveraging the ethos of Café Scientifique. This approach differs from traditional focus groups and World Café approaches. Like the World Café approach, research cafés harness the energy of small group discussions to develop insights and shared learning regarding a topic of interest. However, in research cafés, participants do not rotate between tables with moderators, and these stable groups are facilitated by peer or community researchers, using plain language. The cafés are designed to enhance research awareness and gather insights into lived experiences in underserved communities where they can help improve health literacy. These cafés emphasise accessibility, trust and feedback, offering promising potential as safe spaces for dialogue and learning among racially minoritised groups, encouraging more inclusive research participation and involvement. However, applying Café Scientifique principles in health research is an emerging area of study and the application of research cafés is under-studied. This proof-of-concept study aims to explore the feasibility and acceptability of ‘research cafés’ as a methodology for engaging racially minoritised groups in health research, combining community engagement and community peer research methods to reach under-represented groups.

## Methods
Funded by NHS England’s Research Engagement Network, this proof-of-concept feasibility study was conducted between October 2023 and March 2024 in South West London (a collective of the six boroughs of Richmond, Wandsworth, Kingston, Merton, Sutton and Croydon).

## Study design and setting
We adopted a mixed-methods design combining descriptive quantitative data with thematic qualitative analysis. We drew on evidence on mental health inequalities among Black, Asian and racially minoritised groups in South West London Integrated Care Board (ICB) showing there is an over-representation of Black, African and Caribbean groups using mental health services. We then worked with four voluntary organisations identified through the South West London Voluntary, Community and Social Enterprise (SWL VCSE) Alliance, to codesign the research cafés, drawing on CBPR principles. The four voluntary organisations that work with Black, Asian and racially minoritised groups on mental health were Tooting Community Kitchen in Wandsworth, Mind in Kingston’s Magpie Project, Centre of Change in Croydon and People Arise Now in Sutton. All organisations ran coffee mornings which were conducive to setting up research cafés. Each organisation hosted one research café in their community venues.

## Development of the research café model
Research cafes were designed as structured, informal sessions adapted from Café Scientifique and World Café principles but tailored for research engagement. Distinctive features included:
Coconstructing the design of the cafe sessions with university researchers and speakers who were themselves from Black and Asian backgrounds and with community leads from the voluntary organisations hosting the cafes; JO had particular expertise on the use of participatory methods and knowledge in research which she was able to use to guide the design of the work.
Identifying the importance of using peer and community researchers who could cofacilitate the cafe sessions with the support of the university researchers.
Use of accessible community venues identified by voluntary sector partners.
Use of plain-language and paper-based evaluation tools.
Identifying the need for interpreters and translated materials in the cafe session with Korean participants who did not speak English.

## Community and peer researcher roles
Four leads from the partnering voluntary organisations worked on this pilot as community researchers, collaborating with the university researchers (JO and SSNK) to design the research cafés. This included the ethics application, development of participant-oriented documents, such as recruitment flyers, information sheets, consent forms, evaluation and diversity monitoring forms, and mental health resource cards. The community researchers also helped organise the research café sessions, participant recruitment, leading discussion groups and gathering data. Training and support in research methodologies, use of digital recruitment platforms (eg, Eventbrite), and conducting research café sessions were provided by SSNK.
Eleven peer researchers (ie, people with lived experience of mental well-being challenges) supported the research café sessions. They were nominated and recruited through the partnering voluntary organisations and facilitated table discussions and collected data. On the day of the café sessions, SSNK provided brief training on discussion facilitation, data collection and addressing any participant concern or distress. Peer researchers were compensated for their time and travel.

## Participant recruitment
Each voluntary organisation aimed to recruit 30 participants with the eligibility criteria as follows: 18 years and older, self-identified ethnicity as Black, Asian and racially minoritised groups, self-reported experience with mental well-being challenges or interest in mental health research, residence or work location within the boroughs under study and capacity to provide consent. As a feasibility study, no a priori sample size calculation was undertaken; numbers were determined pragmatically by partner capacity and timelines.
A template flyer was developed and promoted on Eventbrite, with pages live for an average of 5 days aligning with the study’s rapid timeline and ethical requirements. Ineligible registrants were contacted by telephone to inform them why they were not selected. All registered participants received an information sheet and consent form via email before the sessions. Based on the inputs from two of our community researchers at the time of codesigning the sessions, the following additional support was provided. For the Wandsworth session, face-to-face recruitment was used for those who did not have a smartphone or internet access. Language barriers were addressed for the Korean community at Mind in Kingston’s Magpie Project by including a Korean translator and translating participant materials.
Participants initially self-assessed their eligibility, which was then confirmed by SSNK through email or phone calls. SSNK explained inclusion criteria to ensure that ineligible participants did not feel excluded. Participants had twenty-four hours to consider their involvement. Their participation was then confirmed at least 3 days before the session to meet ethical requirements. Informed consent was formally recorded via email or in person by SSNK before the session. Participants included those affiliated with the voluntary organisations and individuals from the wider community. Following National Institute of Health Research (NIHR) guidelines for involvement, participants were paid £25 per hour.

## Research café sessions
The research café sessions were held in community venues provided by the four SWL VCSE organisations. These settings were chosen by the voluntary organisations for their relevance, accessibility and ability to create a safe, inclusive environment for diverse groups. The research cafés were organised to last for a maximum of 4 hours, with a break for refreshments.
Each café session aimed to understand participants’ interest in participation and involvement in research and discuss ways to enhance healthcare experiences through their involvement in research. The events were structured into three distinct parts:
An introduction by JO at each session who as an ‘insider-outsider’ researcher and community activist, was able to talk about why she came into healthcare research from a personal perspective as well as from a research perspective about why diverse participation and involvement in research, was important.
Group discussions where participants were divided into small groups of up to six per table. This included initial introductions, an icebreaker, and focused discussions on: (a) reactions to the initial introductory talk and importance of diverse participation and involvement in research; (b) experiences with health services and relevance of research, including barriers and facilitators to participation and involvement; (c) strategies to strengthen effective participation and involvement and collaborative peer research approaches (see for table discussion topics). The research team (community/peer and university researchers) observed the discussions and took handwritten notes ensuring participant comfort and confidentiality. At Mind in Kingston’s Magpie Project, a dedicated table was set up for Korean participants who did not speak English or speak it fluently, with a translator to assist with translation and interpretation.
A concluding session on the development of peer research approaches and the setting up of a South West London Research Support Network. This initiative, in partnership with the South West London VCSE Alliance and voluntary organisations, aims to empower diverse local communities to be more research-active, enhance evidence-based decision-making and address health inequalities with a focus on equity, diversity and inclusion. (See for presentation content used for the sessions.)
Professional workers and trained therapists from the voluntary organisations involved, provided support for any participant experiencing emotional distress during the sessions. All participants received a Mental Health Resource Card, containing essential contact information and online resources.

## Measures
As this methodology of a research café is new, we adapted the evaluative approach from a similar proof-of-concept study focused on community-engaged research and inclusive participation in health research. We used a questionnaire (Evaluation Form) to evaluate the structural setup of the cafés (venue suitability, resource availability and staff involvement), the processes involved (participant comfort, engagement and collaborative discussion), the feasibility outputs (number of research cafés, their attendance, demographics and research ideas generated) and the proximal outcomes (self-reported understanding of research and interest in future involvement). This also measured the inclusiveness and fairness of the cafés, such as perceptions of equitable treatment, respect and cultural sensitivity. To complement this, discussions within the research cafés provided rich qualitative insights into participants’ perspectives of healthcare and on research engagement. These data were captured through contemporaneous notes and thematically analysed to inform further theory building on use of research cafés for participation and involvement in research.
Participants completed hard copy versions of the Evaluation Form (see ) and NIHR Applied Research Collaboration SL’s Diversity Monitoring Forms at the end of each café session. Translated versions were used for the Korean participants from Mind in Kingston’s Magpie Project. Research team members, including the community and peer researchers, aided in form completion when requested by participants, ensuring inclusivity. This approach captured valuable quantitative data on participant experiences and demographics, reflecting the diverse intersectional characteristics of the participant cohort.

## Data analysis
Quantitative data were collected using Participant Evaluation and Diversity Monitoring Forms from the 74 participants. SSNK converted the hard copy data into an electronic format using Excel, and analysed descriptively (frequencies and percentages). This provided a description and baseline of demographics, participation and involvement and proximal outcomes of the cafés on research awareness and intention for future involvement.
Qualitative data comprised table discussion notes capturing 74 participants’ and 11 peer researchers’ reactions to the introductory talk, experiences with health services, strategies for effective participation and involvement, and developing peer research approaches. Notes were transcribed by SSNK, uploaded to the qualitative software package NVivo 14 and analysed using thematic analysis to understand the perceptions and needs around diverse participation and involvement in research. Thematic analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s six stages: (1) data familiarisation, (2) initial codes generation, (3) themes development, (4) themes review, (5) themes definition and naming (6) and report production. JO and SSNK independently analysed 25% of the qualitative dataset to develop a coding framework. SSNK then applied this framework to code the remaining data, using both—inductive and deductive approaches, with periodic peer debriefs to enhance rigour. Thematic summaries were then produced and distributed to the community and peer researchers of the four voluntary organisations for comment.

## Reflexivity
The research team included academic researchers (SSNK, JO) with prior collaborations with the SWL ICS and the VCSE Alliance, both of whom identify as members of racially minoritised communities. Four community researchers and eleven peer researchers, most also from racially minoritised groups, codesigned and delivered the cafés. We recognise potential power imbalances between academic, ICS and community partners. To mitigate these, cafes were held in community venues, discussions were peer-facilitated and VCSE partners shaped session design and recruitment. Time constraints limited peer and community researcher involvement in formal analysis.

## Patient and public involvement (PPI) statement
Patients and members of the public were involved in multiple stages of the study. Four community researchers (VCSE leads) and 11 peer researchers (with lived experience of mental well-being challenges), most of whom were also from racially minoritised communities, codesigned materials, supported recruitment and cofacilitated cafés. Their involvement ensured cultural relevance and inclusivity in both delivery and participant engagement. They also contributed to planning for dissemination, ensuring findings would be fed back through community organisations. Their involvement in formal data analysis was limited by project timelines. A plain-language summary was shared with VCSE partners.

## Results
Results are presented in three parts: (1) feasibility indicators, (2) proximal outcomes and (3) summary of qualitative themes.

## Feasibility indicators

## Registrations and attendance
As shown in , there were a total of 112 registrations with 75 participants attending on the day. One additional participant signed up on the day of an event; however, as they had not received the study information and consent materials in advance, they were excluded from the analysis to ensure ethical and procedural consistency across participants. The analytic sample was therefore 74 (adjusted attendance=74).
 

## Participant demographics
 presents a summary of participant demographics. The majority of participants were female (73%), 62% were Asian/Asian British and Black or Black British made up 31.8%. Most participants were aged 40–59 years (43%). A notable 24% identified as having a disability, and 76% did not have caring responsibilities.
 

## Proximal outcomes

## Views on the structure and environment
Participants completed feedback questionnaires on the structure and environment of the research café (see : Participant Evaluation Form). All participants (100%) felt comfortable and respected. One participant expressed the importance of such an atmosphere, stating that the sessions ‘helped break down personal barriers around how to get myself heard’, while another appreciated the clarity of the presentations, noting the ‘use of simple, comprehensible language’ that ‘opened my eyes about the importance of being involved by highlighting inequalities in research’.
Ninety-three per cent of participants felt that venues were well-suited, with one participant noting the environment was ‘well-ventilated and spacious’, contributing to a ‘welcoming feel’. Accessibility was also emphasised, with venues being ‘local to residing place, convenient’, enhancing inclusivity of the sessions. For detailed participant feedback on experience from research café sessions, please see .

## Value of diverse participation
Almost all participants (93%) highlighted the value of diverse community participation in research and recognised its benefits. Participants described JO’s personal story during the introductory session as ‘powerful, encouraging and inspirational’. This narrative moved many participants and helped them to feel ‘safe to express opinions’ and that ‘every voice matters and can make a difference’.

## Future research involvement
The enthusiasm for continued involvement in research was high (95%), with the most common interest being joining a peer research network (64%), indicating eagerness to engage in collaborative research approaches.
Participants also identified key areas for skill development to support their active involvement in research. Understanding the impact of diversity in research (58%) and effective communication and presentation skills (58%) were considered essential. ## Summary of qualitative themes
Five main themes emerged from the research café discussions: (1) systemic barriers to healthcare and mistrust of UK healthcare system; (2) the need for cultural competence and sensitivity in healthcare and research; (3) research that could be a positive step for change; (4) barriers to research participation and (5) importance of incentives and feedback for research participation.

## Systemic barriers to healthcare
Participants shared their experiences of persistent systemic barriers undermining the quality and safety of healthcare they received. These included medical negligence, racial discrimination, lack of transparency in clinical decisions and inconsistencies in prescribing practices. One participant stated, ‘the system is geared away from us and even further from minority ethnic groups’. Another added, ‘Doctors prescribe cheaper medicines to people of colour but we pay higher prices—doctors are making off profits!’
Language barriers were also highlighted, with one participant describing a medical incident caused by a lack of interpreter support: ‘I was there (at the hospital) all alone. Now I use my phone’s translator app, but at that time, I did not know what to do, who to talk to, resulting in a medical accident’. Participants expressed that these experiences contributed to a deep mistrust of the UK healthcare system, which extended to research participation and involvement. As one participant stated, ‘What can I do? It won’t matter even if I participate in research’.

## Cultural competence and sensitivity in healthcare and research
A significant concern expressed by participants was the lack of cultural competence and sensitivity in healthcare and research practices. They spoke of the need for improved knowledge of diseases endemic to certain countries. This lack of knowledge led to medical harm. Participants highlighted that current medical education and research practices often fail to account for ethnic differences, resulting in biased outcomes, such as ‘all standard/reference values are based on the white genetic make-up such as Body Mass Index (BMI), they need to consider differences based on ethnicities’.
There was a strong call for educational reforms to ensure healthcare professionals and researchers are better equipped to serve diverse populations, to be ‘more culture sensitive’. Participants also emphasised that cultural competence in healthcare and research requires diverse representation among healthcare professionals and researchers. One participant noted, ‘There must be representation for encouragement to participate’. They stressed that representation helps build understanding and trust, ensuring that research practices are responsive to the needs of diverse communities.

## Research as a step to make positive change
Participants saw the research café sessions as a positive first step towards improving trust and encouraging future participation in both healthcare and research: ‘This kind of event (research café) is very important and has great potential. I think it is an early step to improve and do something positive’. Participants believed that research ‘should be done with people from racialised backgrounds so the solutions are not catering to just the majority white population’. They also felt that there was a ‘need for diverse research teams that foster comfort and trust’. They suggested that there ‘should be shadowing opportunities with experienced researchers’.

## Barriers to participation in research
Participants identified numerous obstacles that hinder their participation in research, such as logistical challenges and technological barriers. Inefficient administrative processes in health services (‘I have been waiting over a year to get registered with a GP’) were also associated with research. One participant added that ‘research does not come to fruition’, reflecting perspectives that they do not see the benefits of research in the healthcare they receive. This discouraged them from engaging with research activities. Participants also emphasised the need for more community outreach to raise awareness about research opportunities and ensure that their voices are included in developing healthcare solutions. There was also concern about the neglect of mental health research, particularly for children and young people, highlighting that ‘mental health research should be equally prioritised as medical research’.

## Importance of incentives and feedback for research participation
Participants emphasised the importance of providing meaningful incentives and ongoing support to encourage participation in research. They stressed that their contributions should be valued and that they should see tangible outcomes from their involvement. As one participant said, ‘incentivisation can be done in different ways—prize draws, free health checks, information exchange’. Many expressed frustrations over the lack of effective feedback mechanisms in healthcare, which left them feeling that their voices were not being heard. ‘You are pushing and pushing and pushing, yet nothing is being done about it. So what is the point?’ Working with researchers was seen similarly as participants spoke of never hearing back once they had been interviewed or surveyed.
Creating comfortable environments with informal settings was also highlighted as a key factor in encouraging participation and reducing the fear of harsh judgement.

## Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings
Quantitative indicators of feasibility and acceptability (such as comfort, respect, understanding and intention for future research involvement) were consistent with the emerging qualitative themes. For example, the finding that all participants (100%) reported feeling comfortable and respected aligned with the qualitative accounts emphasising the importance of safe, culturally sensitive spaces and peer-facilitated discussions. Similarly, the high proportion expressing interest in future research involvement (95%), through joining a peer research network (64%), reflected participants’ calls for ongoing training, support and feedback mechanisms. These patterns suggest that the research café model was feasible to deliver and acceptable to participants, while qualitative insights provided depth on the underlying reasons for engagement and the barriers that remain.

## Discussion

## Principal findings
To our knowledge, this proof-of-concept study is the first study to look at the applicability of research cafés to healthcare research in the United Kingdom. Research cafés are structured, peer-facilitated small-group dialogues held in trusted community venues, designed to make research accessible and inclusive. This makes them relevant for racially minoritised groups and others underrepresented in health research. The study extends the evidence base by showing how research cafés embedded in established community initiatives can enhance research awareness and intention to engage. Nearly all participants (95%) expressed interest in future research involvement, with 65% showing enthusiasm for joining peer research networks. Our findings suggest that the research café model is both feasible and acceptable, as indicated by high comfort ratings (100%) and strong interest in future participation, while thematic analysis provided insight into the underlying drivers and barriers. Participants saw research as a positive step for change but identified obstacles such as logistical and technological challenges, previous negative experiences of healthcare and mistrust in institutions. A particularly strong theme was the need for cultural competence and sensitivity in healthcare and research.
 shows the main barriers and proposed enablers that emerged from the cafés. The identified barriers, along with associated enablers, align closely with known ones such as language constraints, lack of cultural sensitivity, logistical or socioeconomic barriers and racism in research. Our study therefore supports and extends previous work by demonstrating how a community-based, peer-led model can provide a structured environment for discussing and addressing these issues.
 Participants highlighted the importance of receiving follow-ups from researchers. They expressed frustration about contributing their time and insights, only to feel abandoned when researchers failed to maintain contact or provide updates. Building peer research networks and ongoing ‘safe spaces’ align with calls for deeper coproduction. Instead of one-off engagement events, forming sustained peer research networks is likely to enable ongoing and transparent dialogue, trust-building and shared leadership, shifting to long-term community partnerships. While many of these concerns might be echoed by non-minority research participants, the disproportionate inequalities faced by racially minoritised groups, make inclusive methodologies particularly urgent.

## Positioning with existing approaches
Research cafés share similarities with other dialogic engagement approaches such as World Café and Café Scientifique, as well as broader PPI and CBPR frameworks. Like the World Café, research cafés use small group dialogue to generate insights. However, a key distinction is that research cafés retain participants in stable groups rather than rotating tables, and discussions are facilitated by peer or community researchers using plain language. This structure was valued by participants in our study, who described feeling safe, respected and more able to share experiences of systemic barriers and mistrust.
In contrast to Café Scientifique, which is primarily designed to promote science communication in informal public settings, research cafés explicitly focus on inclusivity, coproduction and follow-up. Participants in our study repeatedly stressed the importance of feedback, training and sustained involvement – concerns less central to traditional Café Scientifique models.
Compared with conventional PPI approaches, research cafés appear particularly well-suited to engaging racially minoritised groups who may not access mainstream routes into research. The combination of trusted VCSE partners, peer facilitation and culturally safe venues helped to reduce barriers to participation, consistent with principles of CBPR. Our findings suggest that research cafés offer a complementary methodology within the broader ecosystem of community-based research engagement. They are not intended to deliver system change in isolation, but can realistically achieve proximal outcomes such as improved trust, awareness and intention to participate.

## Strengths and weaknesses of the study
One major strength is that a community peer research approach aligns with calls for inclusive, coproduced methodologies. This approach was enhanced by ensuring our university team was reflective of groups they were working with. University researchers from racially minoritised backgrounds brought key skills, knowledge and cultural competence in shaping and executing a community-based design to deliver the project. By training and collaborating with community and peer researchers from voluntary organisations, we also ensured the study remained contextually relevant and responsive to participants’ lived experiences. Another strength is the integration of quantitative and qualitative data, which demonstrates consistency between measured outcomes (eg, comfort, intention to participate) and thematic insights (eg safe spaces, incentives, feedback).
However, our study has limitations. Research cafés are an emerging methodology and to date, lack standardised guidelines for implementation and evaluation. We adapted the model to embed it within established community engagement initiatives but as the timeline for the study was restricted, we could only do four cafés in one geographical location. This limited the ability to implement iterative feedback loops that might have refined the café approach in real time.
In terms of execution, reliance on community organisations may have introduced selection bias, since individuals already engaged with mental health services may differ from those more socially or economically marginalised. We had an over-representation of Asian or Asian British participants, reflecting a common limitation in recruitment via voluntary organisations. Recruitment through Eventbrite also has limitations, as it tends to attract individuals with higher literacy levels and greater digital access, potentially excluding those with lower literacy or less familiarity with online tools.
Language needs were only partially addressed. For example, a Korean interpreter and translated forms were provided at one café, reflecting the specific needs identified by the host VCSE organisation. However, equivalent support was not provided for other linguistic groups, which may have limited the ability of some participants to fully engage. Future iterations of the model should consider more systematic assessment of language needs and offer broader translation and interpretation support. Finally, the small scale of the study means findings are not intended to be generalisable. Instead, they provide proof-of-concept insights into feasibility and acceptability, laying the groundwork for larger studies. Despite these limitations, our findings suggest that the research café methodology holds promise for improving research literacy and increasing participation among diverse communities. Future work should test its scalability and long-term impact across different conditions and populations.

## Conclusion
Research cafés can serve as a practical and culturally responsive vehicle for equitable participation and involvement, creating spaces for colearning and dialogue among community members as potential partners in health research. This proof-of-concept study demonstrates the feasibility and acceptability of the model, with high levels of participant comfort and strong interest in future involvement, particularly through peer research networks. It is grounded in diverse and inclusive community-based partnerships—bringing together university researchers, voluntary and community sector partners, peer researchers and advisory groups—the cafés can help address existing barriers, such as linguistic constraints, mistrust and a lack of cultural competence, by bringing research opportunities in trusted, community-friendly spaces and using peer facilitation. They complement existing participatory approaches by prioritising inclusivity, feedback and sustained involvement rather than one-off engagement. Their proximal contribution is in building trust, awareness and intention to engage, which are important foundations for longer-term change. Further research is needed to test the scalability of research cafes across different communities, conditions and formats (including virtual delivery), and to evaluate their long-term impact on research literacy, participation and equity. As health systems seek to embed inclusive research in everyday care, research cafes offer a promising blueprint for engaging racially minoritised groups and strengthening coproduction in health research.
