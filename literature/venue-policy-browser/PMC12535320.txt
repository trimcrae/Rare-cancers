## Abstract

## Aim
The aim of the study was to investigate the impact of the use of baby‐friendly community initiative (BFCI) model on various stakeholders in the community.

## Design
Quasi‐experimental research design.

## Method
The study was conducted in public premises and online workshops from April 2019 to September 2022. Participants were followed up for a period of 1 month, except for those employed at public premises. The program involved training based on an accredited BFCI framework to cultivate a breastfeeding‐friendly attitude and knowledge. A paired sample t‐test was used to examine breastfeeding attitude and knowledge scores before and after BFCI training among staff employed from public premises. An analysis of variance was conducted to examine the breastfeeding self‐efficacy and attitude scores, measured repeatedly at different timepoints over 1‐month timepoint (T0, T1 and T2) among pregnant and postpartum women.

## Results
A total of 2340 perinatal women and 1339 staff from public premises were recruited. For staff, there was an increase in the mean score of breastfeeding knowledge and attitude by 5.8 and 6.1, respectively, at T1. Similarly, for perinatal women, there was an increase in the mean score of breastfeeding self‐efficacy and attitude by 6.6 and 3.3, respectively, at T1.

## Conclusion
In summary, a BFCI model, with active community participation, accreditation and an award system, has been effective in promoting breastfeeding. Adapting the baby‐friendly hospital initiative to local contexts and employing a social theory model can enhance breastfeeding promotion and improve infant health outcomes. Prioritizing culturally sensitive breastfeeding education is crucial for successful BFCI implementation.

## Implications for the profession and/or patient care
Healthcare professionals should consider clients' culture and socio‐economic backgrounds when providing breastfeeding education to maximize effectiveness. The target audience for breastfeeding education should be expanded to include various community stakeholders beyond families.

## Impact

What problem did the study address? This study addressed the problem of knowledge gaps among stakeholders in building a breastfeeding‐friendly community, particularly in implementing a baby‐friendly community initiative (BFCI) as part of a baby‐friendly hospital initiative (BFHI). The research filled a service gap by providing effective interventions targeting community stakeholders and assessing the impact of a BFCI program on their knowledge and attitudes towards breastfeeding.

What were the main findings? The findings highlighted the effectiveness of a BFCI program in enhancing breastfeeding knowledge and attitudes among frontline staff and increasing breastfeeding confidence among mothers. These findings contribute to the understanding of the program's impact on different stakeholders in the community.

Where and on whom will the research have an impact? It impacts on global policymakers by providing insights for developing comprehensive guidelines for future BFCI implementations. It also contributes to the creation of a more baby‐friendly community, benefiting breastfeeding families and their infants by promoting and supporting breastfeeding families.

## Reporting Method
This study has adhered to relevant EQUATOR guidelines using the TREND reporting guideline.

## Patient or Public Contribution
No patient or public contribution.

## What Does This Paper Contribute to the Wider Global Clinical Community?
This study provides an overview of the establishment of a localized BFCI program. It also opens up a new direction for the community to investigate BFCI strategies for community stakeholders. It also provides evidence to support other countries in following a similar process, as each country approaches becoming breastfeeding‐friendly in its own unique way.

HKU‐BF The SCT model comprises three main forms: bonding, bridging and linking social capital. Bonding social capital refers to close relationships among individuals within a specific group (Hawe & Shiell,  ). Bridging social capital refers to close connections and interactions between different groups or communities. Linking social capital refers to connections between individuals or groups who possess different levels of power or influence, enabling access to resources and opportunities (Hawe & Shiell,  ). Implementation science in the health aims to identify the factors, processes and methods that effectively integrate evidence‐based interventions into policies and practices to improve population health (Lobb & Colditz,  ). To achieve this, governments need to go beyond simply promoting breastfeeding as a positive practice. They need to create supportive policies and programs to enable the environments that parents need such as maternity benefits, breastfeeding facilities in public spaces, destigmatizing breastfeeding and addressing the challenges faced by women who struggle with breastfeeding (Baker et al.,  ; Hastings et al.,  ; Pérez‐Escamilla, ; Tomori et al.,  ). The primary goal of a BFCI is to provide training, supported by the SCT model and an accredited framework (BFCI), to effectively educate families, communities and societies. This model aims to foster positive breastfeeding‐friendly attitudes among women in public places within the community.

## THE STUDY
3.1

## Aims
This study aimed to assess the impact of a BFCI model on various stakeholders within the community.

3.2

## Theoretical framework
Hawe and Shiell's ( ) SCT model provided the theoretical guidance for this study. The model provides a theoretical framework for understanding and analysing the social relationships, networks, trust and norms that exist within a community or society. By utilizing the SCT model, researchers and practitioners can better understand how social capital can be harnessed to promote positive changes and interventions within communities. We structured a BFCI program supported by the SCT model (see Figure  ). The SCT posits that social networks, relationships and resources within a community play a crucial role in achieving common goals and facilitating collective actions. In the case of a BFCI program, the SCT provides a theoretical framework for understanding how the collaboration and engagement of various stakeholders, including the public, non‐governmental organizations, government agencies and commercial organizations, can contribute to the establishment of a positive breastfeeding culture. A BFCI program sought to leverage the social capital within the community by fostering cross and multidisciplinary collaboration. Through the engagement of diverse stakeholders, such as healthcare providers, policymakers, business owners and community members, the program aimed to create a supportive and conducive environment for breastfeeding mothers. By applying the SCT, the research team recognized the significance of building social networks and relationships, as well as mobilizing available resources, to promote breastfeeding‐friendly practices. The program's success relied on the collective efforts and contributions of different stakeholders, who played a vital role in establishing breastfeeding‐friendly premises, disseminating information and providing support to breastfeeding mothers.
 Overall, the integration of the SCT in the research design and implementation of a BFCI program helped elucidate the importance of community collaboration, relationships and resources in creating a breastfeeding‐friendly environment. It provided a theoretical lens to understand the mechanisms through which social capital can positively impact breastfeeding practices and outcomes.

## METHODS
4.1

## Design
Quasi‐experimental research design was adopted, with three data collection points (baseline, post‐workshop and 1 month after the workshop) for pregnant and breastfeeding women. Additionally, two data collection points (pre‐ and post‐workshop) were utilized by staff to evaluate the effectiveness of a BFCI model. In this study, we aimed to assess the effectiveness of a BFCI in two distinct arms: enhancing knowledge of staff in shopping malls and improving breastfeeding self‐efficacy in pregnant women regarding breastfeeding. However, it is important to note that the 1‐month follow‐up period did not allow us to measure the impact of the first arm on the results related to staff.

4.2

## Data sources
4.2.1

## Adopting and implementing BFCI model
To inform and evaluate implementation and knowledge translation efforts, we chose the SCT theoretical framework for implementation because of the core element of increasing social cohesion among families, communities and society to strengthen trust, norms and networks within the community, which can significantly influence various aspects of individual health, education and overall well‐being. The evaluation of knowledge translation efforts is based on validated breastfeeding self‐efficacy, breastfeeding knowledge and attitudes.
We reflected on the implementation phase of transferring a BFCI model using a range of specified criteria as shown in Figure  . The consensus among the research team demonstrates how a BFCI was conducted in four phases and how we cooperated in a train‐the‐trainer program for sustainability.

4.2.2

## Breastfeeding knowledge and attitudes of staff and management
Staff and management from public venues in shopping malls and restaurants were invited to attend a 2‐h training course based on WHO breastfeeding counselling course. The objective was to enhance their knowledge of breastfeeding practices and increase their self‐efficacy in assisting mothers with common breastfeeding challenges [19]. Guidelines were provided to them for implementing breastfeeding measures based on the Department of Health Breastfeeding‐Friendly Premises scheme. Pre‐training and post‐training questionnaires were administered.
To assess attitudes and knowledge related to breastfeeding, a validated breastfeeding questionnaire developed by Ho and McGrath ( ) was utilized. The questionnaire consisted of 29 questions measuring attitudes to and knowledge towards breastfeeding. Responses were recorded using a Likert scale, ranging from strongly agree (rated 5) to strongly disagree (rated 1). The breastfeeding attitude was evaluated using the modified Iowa Infant Feeding Attitude Scale (Ho & McGrath,  ), which comprised 18 questions with a maximum score of 90. The knowledge score was based on 11 questions, with a maximum score of 55. A higher score indicated better knowledge or more positive attitudes towards breastfeeding.

4.2.3

## Breastfeeding self‐efficacy and attitudes of breastfeeding women
Maternal breastfeeding self‐efficacy is defined as a mother's perceived confidence in her ability to breastfeed her child, which influences her breastfeeding decisions and is significantly associated with a longer duration of breastfeeding (Loke & Chan,  ). Higher BSES scores indicate higher levels of breastfeeding self‐efficacy and confidence (Ho & McGrath,  ). All participating women were surveyed before and after the breastfeeding workshop using the breastfeeding self‐efficacy scale—short form (Dennis,  ) to assess confidence in breastfeeding. Additionally, a record of their breastfeeding experiences was collected to evaluate the advice and support they received regarding any problems encountered. Breastfeeding attitude was assessed using 17 questions, with a higher total score indicating greater knowledge (Ho & McGrath,  ). The questions utilized a Likert scale with responses ranging from strongly agree (rated 5) to strongly disagree (rated 1). Questionnaires were administered immediately before, after and 1‐month post‐training.

4.3

## Sample and setting
From April 2019 to September 2022, a convenience sample of shopping malls and premises was identified throughout the entire geographical area of Hong Kong using the internet. A total of 464 shopping malls and premises were outreached by telephone and email. Letter of invitations was sent by the study sites to senior managers and customer service of respective shopping malls and premises. The emails and letter of invitation described the study, which included exposure to a breastfeeding mobile app. The mobile app is a territory‐wide information application on breastfeeding facilities, with the goal of fostering a breastfeeding‐friendly community. Additionally, in acknowledgement of public premises support, an award ceremony will be held to present the Best Breastfeeding‐Friendly Premises award. Participants received HK$50 cash coupons as incentives upon completion of the evaluation to participate in a BFCI program. Criteria to participate in the study included age 18 years or older and employees from a public venue, who attended the training session. A pre‐workshop questionnaire was collected for basic demographic information from staff participants, while breastfeeding knowledge and attitude were assessed in both the pre‐ and post‐workshop questionnaires.
A convenience sample of pregnant women and postpartum women were recruited through social media posters, leaflets distributed in public hospitals and the network of two NGO collaborators including their websites and social media platforms. Data collected included demographic information, obstetric and birth characteristics, breastfeeding attitude and breastfeeding self‐efficacy. Inclusion criteria for study participation consisted of being 18 years of age or older, being pregnant or breastfeeding women, and having the intention to breastfeed and without any significant medical or obstetrical complications. Participants were invited to attend a 1–2 h breastfeeding workshop aimed at enhancing their knowledge of breastfeeding practices and self‐efficacy in addressing common breastfeeding challenges. The intervention included providing information for perinatal women and the public about the BFHI, the benefits of breastfeeding, and showing videos that highlight the challenges breastfeeding mothers face in public. It also covered breastfeeding workplace policies and how employees can support breastfeeding women in public, such as assisting them in a comfortable seating area or a designated private space for breastfeeding or expressing milk. The cultural environment encompasses traditional beliefs about breastfeeding, family dynamics and the role of healthcare providers in supporting new mothers. Socio‐economic aspects include the availability of resources for breastfeeding support, work policies related to maternity leave and public attitudes towards breastfeeding in communal settings. By considering these elements, the study can tailor a BFCI model to fit the local context, potentially leading to more effective strategies for promoting breastfeeding and supporting mothers in the community. Data collection took place at baseline (pre‐workshop) (T0), immediately after the workshop (T1) and 1 month later (T2) to assess breastfeeding attitude and self‐efficacy.

4.4

## Ethical considerations
The present study was approved by the University of Hong Kong and Hospital Authority Hong Kong West Cluster (UW 18‐489). Those who agreed to participate read the informed consent form and clicked “I am willing to participate on the webpage” before they formally entered the online survey.

4.5

## Data analysis
Descriptive statistics, such as mean (standard deviation [SD]) and frequency (percentage), were used to summarize and present the characteristics and outcome data of the participants. For the primary outcome, breastfeeding knowledge and attitude mean scores were analysed using the paired sample t‐test to examine if there was a significant increase in breastfeeding attitude and knowledge scores pre‐ and post‐BFCI training program. Analysis of variance was conducted to examine the breastfeeding knowledge and attitude scores measured repeatedly at different timepoints over 1‐month timepoint (T0, T1 and T2) among the pregnant or breastfeeding women. Such an average was anticipated to quantify the overall sustainability of breastfeeding knowledge and attitude as well as baseline/post‐workshop. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 16.1 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA) with the level of significance set at 0.05.

## RESULTS
5.1

## Participants' characteristics
From April 2019 to September 2022, a total of 1448 eligible staff participated in a BFCI workshop. 100 declined to fill in the questionnaire after the project staff explained the study details. 1348 staff participants consented to join the study and completed baseline data collection (Figure  ). The majority of the participants were women with a mean age of 37.3 years (SD 10.9), married or living with a partner (55.4%), had a monthly household income above HK$40,000 (USD (39.5%)) and are working as a full‐time employee (97.6%). Nearly one‐half (45.7%) were educated at university level or above. Most staff participants were from Hong Kong and have lived here since birth (77.7%) (see Table  ). A total of 1339 staff participants completed the post‐workshop questionnaire. The remaining 1339 staff participants were contacted at T1 to complete the follow‐up surveys online. A total of 290 responded at T1. The vast majority (92.4%) responded pre‐ and post‐workshop which was the main primary outcome (Table  ).
 From July 2020 to September 2022, a total of 2340 eligible pregnant women and postpartum women participated in a BFCI workshop but 800 declined to fill in the questionnaire after the project coordinator explained the study details. 1540 participants consented to join the study and completed baseline data collection and the post‐workshop questionnaire (Figure  ). The majority were women with a mean age of 32.2 years (SD 6.8), married (96.0%) and had a monthly household income above $40,000 (60.9%). Majority of them (73.2%) were educated at university level or above. Most participants were from Hong Kong and have lived here since birth (80.1%), were primiparous (88.7%) and planned to exclusively breastfeed (41.5%) with some breastfeeding experience (10.0%) (see Table  ). A total of 1224 women completed the post‐workshop questionnaire for breastfeeding self‐efficacy and 1178 women completed the breastfeeding attitude (Table  ). The remaining 1540 participants were contacted at T2 to complete the follow‐up surveys online. A total of 696 responded at T2 with completed breastfeeding self‐efficacy questionnaire and 672 responded at T2 with completed breastfeeding attitude questionnaire (Table  ).
 
5.2

## Breastfeeding knowledge and attitude
5.2.1

## Community support for frontline staff to implement breastfeeding policy
At baseline (i.e. before workshop), the mean breastfeeding knowledge score was 33.1 (SD 3.8) and the mean breastfeeding attitude score was 56.8 (SD 6.4). There was statistical improvement in both breastfeeding knowledge (p < .001) and staff attitude towards breastfeeding (p < .001) after the training workshops. In the program evaluation, 65.6% of staff considered that the program was effective in raising awareness of breastfeeding benefits, with 88.4% reported that they feel more confident supporting mothers in breastfeeding after the training, and 63.5% reported that they had enough training to help assist breastfeeding.

5.2.2

## Community support for pregnant and breastfeeding women to initiate and sustain breastfeeding
At baseline (i.e. before the workshop), the mean breastfeeding self‐efficacy score was 37.6 (SD 9.8) and the mean breastfeeding attitude score was 59.5 (SD 6.3). There was statistical improvement in both breastfeeding self‐efficacy (p < .001) and attitude towards breastfeeding (p < .001) after the training workshops. At 1‐month follow‐up, the mean breastfeeding knowledge score was 45.7 (SD 10.4) and breastfeeding attitude score was 62.3 (SD 7.0). Breastfeeding knowledge and attitude score slightly increased 1 month after the training. Among the participants who completed the 1‐month survey, 496 were still pregnant. 93.9% of the participants were breastfeeding at the 1‐month post‐training follow‐up, among which half were exclusively breastfeeding (48.3%). In terms of program satisfaction, participants have given an average of 4.00 (SD 0.63) satisfaction out of the maximum of 5. Additionally, 94.3% of respondents considered that the program will benefit all breastfeeding mothers and that it will increase breastfeeding rate in Hong Kong.

5.2.3

## Community Maternal and Health Centres
A BFCI model has been adopted by Family Health Service, Department of Health in Breastfeeding‐Friendly Community, and contributed to how to establish breastfeeding‐friendly premises as a video resource for the public on the Department of Health website. As of September 2022, we distributed 7228 promotional materials (including 6875 A5 leaflets, 47 A3 posters, 183 stickers and 123 decals) about a BFCI program and breastfeeding app to 24 Maternal and Child Health Services and eight public and three private hospitals with obstetric units.

## DISCUSSION
The purpose of implementation science is to identify evidence‐based implementable policies and actions that can effectively improve public health. The process of implementation is complex as is the evaluation of its outcomes. However, each country faced unique challenges in the policy arena that could hinder the process of enacting real change for the health and well‐being of infants, families and the efficient use of government funds for public health (Kendall et al.,  ). This study focuses on the implementation of a BFCI model in Hong Kong, taking into account the cultural environment. By utilizing the SCT framework, this study provides an approach for countries to develop a set of feasible recommendations that can be evaluated. A BFCI model sought to leverage the social capital within the community by fostering cross‐ and multidisciplinary collaboration. Through the engagement of diverse stakeholders, such as healthcare providers, policymakers, business owners and community members, the program aimed to create a supportive and conducive environment for breastfeeding mothers. By applying the SCT, the research team recognized the significance of building social networks and relationships, as well as mobilizing available resources, to promote breastfeeding‐friendly practices. The program's success relied on the collective efforts and contributions of different stakeholders, who played a vital role in establishing breastfeeding‐friendly premises, disseminating information and providing support to breastfeeding mothers.
Overall, the integration of the SCT in the research design and implementation of a BFCI program helped elucidate the importance of community collaboration, relationships and resources in creating a breastfeeding‐friendly environment. It provided a theoretical lens to understand the mechanisms through which social capital can positively impact breastfeeding practices and outcomes.
6.1

## Effectiveness on frontline staffs
Our study shows a preliminary positive change in breastfeeding knowledge and attitude change after the program, with a significant increase in both breastfeeding knowledge and attitude by 60% before and after the workshop. After 1‐month follow‐up, the mean knowledge and attitude scores were retained and maintained after 1 month of the workshop, which demonstrates the effectiveness of the program.
There is limited research on BFCI and its impact (Fallon et al.,  ). Current research on BFCI has limited data on its effect on community stakeholders. Despite the lack of comparable studies, this research can act as a starting point for future studies to investigate various stakeholders. Based on the SCT model, different groups across the population can serve as social capital and contribute to change (Lin,  ). This indicates that not only parents but community members shall also be looked upon in the matters of promoting breastfeeding. The interactive dynamic between the community and mothers' breastfeeding behaviour can be further explored in future studies.
There can be a few ways in which the community can positively influence the mothers' breastfeeding attitude and behaviour. Frontline staff can make a change by having a breastfeeding‐friendly attitude and learning more about local breastfeeding facilities (Figure  ). Management can contribute by building a more baby‐friendly premise. The welcoming surrounding can also make a positive impact on maternal motivation to breastfeed through fulfilling maternal psychological needs (Lau et al.,  ).

6.2

## Effectiveness on mothers
BFCI workshops have helped 1540 mothers significantly increase breastfeeding self‐efficacy (BSES). There was a significant 10% increase in mothers' breastfeeding self‐efficacy after the workshop. Higher breastfeeding self‐efficacy is associated with a longer duration of breastfeeding and EBF that for every point increase in self‐efficacy score, there will be 4%–7% increase in odds of breastfeeding maintenance (Fan et al.,  ). Consequently, the longer duration of breastfeeding would provide a better health outcome for the infant. As aforementioned, 48.3% of mothers exclusively breastfeed at 1‐month post‐training. This is much higher than the Hong Kong general population, in which the 1‐month postnatal EBF rate only reaches at 28.8% (Department of Health,  ). Maternal attitude was also improved and maintained at 1‐month post‐intervention.
Our findings are consistent with the few studies that focus on the impact of BFCI. A study carried out in Migori reported a 3.5% and 17.1% increase in the early initiation and EBF rate (Kavle et al.,  ). Cambodia also noticed a 49‐point increase in 6 months of EBF rates after 5 years of the implementation of BFCI (National Institute of Statistics/Cambodia, Directorate General for Health/Cambodia, & ICF International, 2011 as cited in Kavle et al.,  ).
Another local study also shows evidence of the association between the infant feeding preferences of mothers' immediate social network and early cessation (Bai et al.,  ). This indicates the potential influence of society on mother's breastfeeding behaviour (Bai et al.,  ). BFCI, a strategy targeting on community, can provide a solution in terms of co‐creating a baby‐friendly environment. Governments must go beyond mere promotion of breastfeeding as a positive act and instead focus on creating supportive policies and programs that facilitate the necessary environments for parents. BFCI is one such strategy aimed at informing breastfeeding facilities in public spaces and destigmatizing the act of breastfeeding, and addressing the challenges faced by mothers who find breastfeeding difficult (Baker et al.,  ; Hastings et al.,  ; Pérez‐Escamilla, ; Tomori et al.,  ). Furthermore, this study aligns with the World Alliance for Breastfeeding Action (WABA) and contributes to their goal of improving the health of communities worldwide. A BFCI program adapts the BFHI to local social and health services, aiming to provide comprehensive support throughout the entire continuum of care, from pre‐birth to postpartum. This approach ensures optimal support for breastfeeding mothers and their infants.

6.3

## Limitation
First, the convenience sampling recruitment methods may result in biased data, as these participants are known to be more active and can thus have a higher breastfeeding rate than the general population. Moreover, the majority of recruited samples are primiparous mothers and university graduates with higher family incomes. These are known factors associated with higher breastfeeding rates (Leung et al.,  ). The majority of the participants are from a relatively high social class and with a high level of education. The findings may not be representative of the broader population, as socio‐economic status and education level can influence behaviour, attitudes and access to resources. The loss of participants over the course of the study may have resulted in a smaller sample size, reducing the statistical power and potentially affecting the generalizability of the results. To overcome this limitation in future studies, several strategies can be implemented, such as minimizing participant attrition by maintaining regular communication and engagement throughout the study. Providing incentives or rewards for completing all data collection points can also help incentivize participation. Future studies with larger sample sizes and a randomized controlled design are recommended. Additionally, implementing strategies to minimize attrition and conducting an analysis of participant characteristics and reasons for dropout can provide insights into any potential biases introduced. This can also help identify patterns and factors contributing to participant attrition, allowing for targeted intervention strategies to mitigate dropout.
Second, our follow‐up stops at 1‐month post‐intervention. Longer follow‐up duration, like up to 6 months, can be done in future studies to check on breastfeeding maintenance rate. As mothers in Hong Kong usually practice “doing the month,” or are still on maternity leave within the first 4 weeks postpartum, the breastfeeding rate is higher within the initial period. Therefore, long‐term outcome data can provide more insights into whether BFCI can have an impact after mothers return to the community or their work life. Lastly, due to the 1‐month follow‐up period, we were unable to evaluate the specific impact of the enhanced breastfeeding friendliness in shopping malls on the participating mothers. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the results and generalizing the findings to the broader population of breastfeeding mothers in similar settings. Future research should aim to include assessments of the impact on mothers' breastfeeding practices or outcomes.

6.4

## Recommendation
A BFCI model, which involved cross and multidisciplinary collaboration across public, non‐government, government and commercial organizations, aimed to establish a positive breastfeeding culture and have a broader impact on health in Hong Kong and beyond. To effectively implement a BFCI model in the community, it is crucial to protect, promote and support breastfeeding, using a theoretical framework such as the SCT and providing practical training to different stakeholders in the community to ensure successful uptake.
In Hong Kong, the advocacy of government resources for scaling up a BFCI model, along with an assessment of the scaling environment in the context of BFHI, using the Becoming Baby‐Friendly Index at local levels, played a crucial role in the success of this model. Future efforts should consider linking the implementation of BFCI and BFHI, following the updated WHO ( , ) guidelines, to ensure a continuum of care for breastfeeding counselling and support, from facility to community level (Perez‐Escamilla,  ).
The Hong Kong model, which involved all relevant community providers in achieving BFCI accreditation and implementing an award system, has proven to be an effective public health promotion strategy. Additionally, culturally sensitive breastfeeding education should be prioritized, taking into consideration the socio‐economic status of the community, as demonstrated in this study, to ensure effective dissemination and sustainability of knowledge.
By implementing these recommendations and utilizing the experiences and success of a BFCI model, stakeholders can continue to promote and support breastfeeding, creating a more breastfeeding‐friendly environment and positively impacting the health of communities.

7

## CONCLUSION
A BFCI model was designed to provide comprehensive support and care throughout the perinatal period, aiming to optimize breastfeeding practices. Through our study, we have demonstrated the positive impact of a BFCI model on various stakeholders in the community. Implementation of a BFCI program has led to increased knowledge and positive attitudes among community stakeholders, as well as serving as a motivating factor for mothers to initiate and continue breastfeeding. These findings have significant implications for supporting and facilitating the effective global implementation of a BFCI model. By adapting and tailoring a BFHI to local contexts and employing a social theory model, communities can enhance their efforts to promote breastfeeding, thus improving infant health outcomes.
In conclusion, BFCI has proven to be an effective approach in increasing knowledge, fostering positive attitudes and promoting breastfeeding among community stakeholders. It is imperative that further efforts are made to implement and scale up a BFCI model globally, addressing the barriers to breastfeeding and ensuring the provision of comprehensive support and care to promote optimal infant health.
