## ABSTRACT

## Purpose
An increasing number of studies based on secondary data use, including registry‐based studies, have been initiated to address post‐authorization regulatory commitments. We map differences across tools used in data quality (DQ) assessments, including those embedded in fitness‐for‐purpose (FFP) assessments, and describe their strengths and limitations for use in DQ assessments of registries considered for use in post‐approval safety studies (PASS). We focus on the use case where marketing authorization holders (MAHs) cannot directly analyze patient‐level data. Furthermore, we propose complementary measures to improve DQ assessment, including a set of data quality indicators (DQIs).

## Methods
We analyzed the extent to which the selected tools used in registry assessments address DQ dimensions and metrics defined in the European Medicines Agency‐DQ Framework (EMA‐DQF). We specifically considered the use case where DQ assessment was purely based on registry documentation and qualitative due to data access restrictions.

## Results
None of the tools covered all DQ dimensions and metrics; they had limited utility for evaluating extensiveness (i.e., completeness and coverage), semantic coherence, and reliability (i.e., accuracy and plausibility). Furthermore, some supporting document requirements were more useful than others. For example, the data dictionary is useful for evaluating precision and structural coherence. In contrast, publications have limited utility in DQ assessment.

## Conclusions
We propose a set of consistent definitions of information requirements and quantitative DQIs that complement existing tools that can be used for evaluating DQ throughout the registry‐based PASS lifecycle. If DQIs cannot be evaluated during the preparation of the PASS protocol, uncertainties and their potential impact on the study results need to be acknowledged in the relevant study documents. Additionally, scenario mapping for the removal or replacement of registries with insufficient DQ after PASS initiation is needed.

Keywords: data quality indicators, patient registries, post‐authorization safety studies

## Summary

We assessed the utility of selected tools designed and information requirements for evaluating data quality (DQ) in registries with restricted patient‐level data access for Marketing Authorization Holders.
None of the tools evaluated covered all dimensions and metrics defined in the European Medicines Agency‐DQ Framework.
Some information requirements were more useful than others for DQ evaluation; these would benefit from consistent definitions and better clarifications of minimum content to support DQ evaluation.
Key dimensions and metrics, such as extensiveness, semantic coherence, and reliability, can only be evaluated through quantitative analysis, which is challenging in registries with restricted patient‐level data access.
We propose examples of disease‐area agnostic Data Quality Indicators to supplement qualitative DQ evaluations conducted during feasibility assessments for registry‐based Post‐Authorization Safety Studies.

## Introduction
Patient registries are valuable sources of longitudinally collected, real‐world data (RWD), especially in diseases where long‐term patient follow‐up is crucial for evaluating disease evolution and treatment impact. The key strengths of registries include their wide geographic coverage, long‐term data capture, and collection of disease‐specific data elements that may not be available in other RWD sources [ , ].
In 2015, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) launched the patient registries initiative to expand the use of existing registries for the benefit–risk evaluation of medicines [ , ], leading to several registry‐based post‐approval safety studies (PASS) (Figure  ). Subsequently, the EMA published guidelines on registry‐based studies in 2021 [ ].
 Concurrently, the landscape of data quality (DQ) assessments was evolving. In 2017, a framework for DQ evaluation of RWD from electronic health records was published by Kahn [ ], followed by a 2019 Duke‐Margolis framework for the systematic fitness‐for‐purpose evaluation of RWD, which builds on Kahn by providing operationalization guidance and examples of verification checks [ ].
The Heads of Medicines Agencies (HMA)‐EMA Big Data Steering group published a DQ framework that applies to a broader class of RWD sources, metadata, and supporting information [ ] (referred to as the EMA‐DQF) and a draft Annex dedicated to RWD [ ] (referred to as “EMA‐DQF RWD”). Due to the breadth of the EMA‐DQF, different methods and metrics are needed for the data sources in scope. This manuscript focuses on patient registries as a potential RWD source for PASS.
DQ evaluation of registries being considered for conducting registry‐based PASS may present specific challenges. For example, local privacy laws and registry governance may restrict access to patient‐level data by marketing authorization holders (MAHs). Under this access model, a MAH evaluates DQ by proxy based on available registry documentation, qualitative assessment of registry‐reported information, and publications. The MAH subsequently receives aggregated results in the PASS.
In 2019, the European Network for Health Technology Assessment (EUnetHTA) published the Registry Evaluation and Quality Standards Tool (REQueST) (referred to as “EUnetHTA REQueST”) to support consistent registry DQ assessment [ , ]. The EUnetHTA REQueST was adapted by the EMA in their guideline on registry‐based studies (referred to as “EMA REQueST”) [ ] for evaluating registries being considered for use in PASS and/or post‐authorization efficacy studies.
From 2021 to 2022, Gatto et al. published the Structured Process to Identify Fit‐for‐Purpose (FFP) Data (SPIFD, SPIFD2), emphasizing the need for framing DQ assessments according to the research question while highlighting the importance of key operational considerations [ , ]. Gatto et al. refer to various guidance, frameworks, and tools that allow for compliance with regulatory standards, including an active framework from a European Health Technology Assessment (HTA) body, the 2022 National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) Real‐World Evidence (RWE) framework, which comes with the Data Suitability and Assessment Tool (DataSAT) [ ]. Finally, in November 2024, the draft EMA‐DQF RWD outlined specifics applicable to RWD, provided guidance on evaluating the dimensions and metrics of RWD to a research question and offered actionable recommendations to enhance the utility of RWE for regulatory purposes. Within the EMA‐DQF RWD, a data source characterization checklist was included to support DQ evaluation (referred to as “EMA‐DQF RWD checklist”).
For two registry‐based PASS pilots (EUPAS24484 and EUPAS19800) started between 2016 and 2017 that the authors participated in, qualitative DQ assessments were conducted as part of feasibility assessment prior to PASS protocol design, submission and approval. The assessments were based on information from peer‐reviewed and gray literature, questionnaires, requests for documentation (e.g., variable lists or data dictionaries, if available), and interviews with the registry leads. Notably, the questionnaires attempted to explore the availability and level of completeness of key variables such as demographic information, medical history, and safety data collection (Figure  ). Operationally, the level of detail that can be requested at this stage is often minimal given the absence of contracts between the registry and MAH. From this step, registries most likely to deliver data needed for the registry‐based PASS are selected, contracted, and included in the study protocol.
 Following PASS protocol approval, all analyses are subsequently conducted by the registry in accordance with the protocol and a statistical analysis plan agreed upon with the MAH. Thus, MAHs have first visibility on aggregate data from the registries during the first regulatory reporting cycle. A key observation noted for both studies is that reported levels of completeness during feasibility were often lower than those observed in the study (Figure  ). Furthermore, other quality issues related to reliability, coherence, and timeliness were noted on receipt of aggregate data.
With the rapid evolution of tools and frameworks for DQ assessment since 2016, we aimed to assess whether tools released after PASS pilots allow more accurate DQ assessment in registries with restricted patient‐level data access. More broadly, we aimed to understand the differences across the tools and their strengths and limitations when evaluating DQ in registries with restricted patient‐level data access. This study thus aimed to:

Map areas of convergence and divergence of DQ definitions across tools used for RWD DQ evaluation for regulatory submissions
Compare the utility of tools and information requirements for DQ assessment according to the EMA‐DQF
Propose consistent definitions for key documentation requirements to enhance their utility for DQ evaluation
Develop quantitative DQ indicators (DQIs) that can complement qualitative DQ assessments
In this manuscript, we specifically focus on the utility of EUnetHTA REQueST, EMA REQueST, SPIFD, DataSAT, the EMA‐DQF RWD checklist, and AttractR in assessing the dimensions and metrics of the EMA‐DQF. Our findings can be used to improve DQ evaluations for registry‐based regulatory studies.

## Mapping of DQ‐Related Concepts Across Tools Usable for Registry DQ Evaluation
We sought to assess available tools that can be used to assess DQ in registries with restricted patient‐level data access. We included tools that are recommended for use as part of active EU guidelines related to RWD DQ evaluation (Table  ). Tools that require the generation of quantitative output (e.g., programs or code) based on direct access to patient‐level data were excluded.
 For each tool, the authors independently checked whether equivalent concepts or definitions for EMA‐DQF dimensions and metrics were present (P), absent (A), or indirectly referenced but not defined (I) in a tool (Figure  ). 2.4.

## Definition of Data Quality Indicators to Complement Qualitative DQ Assessments
Data quality indicators (DQIs) were defined to address several aspects of DQ that cannot be fully evaluated using the information or documentation requirements in Section  : (1) cover key EMA dimensions and metrics requiring quantitative assessments; (2) are not research question specific, but involve variables that are generally used to define treatment exposure and contextualize safety events, regardless of therapeutic agent; (3) include checks that assess the impact of variable transformations (e.g., from free text to controlled vocabulary); and (4) focus on the intrinsic determinants of DQ for registries [ ]. The DQI in reliability (accuracy, plausibility), extensiveness (completeness, coverage), and coherence (semantic, uniqueness) dimensions and metrics were defined using as a starting point key DQI presented in the reports of the EMA workshops on MS registries (e.g., completeness of core data field completion, frequency of errors, duplicated/wrong data, % of patients covered) [ ].

Reliability: to assess accuracy of study outcomes against external benchmarks and plausibility of treatments against medical guidelines.
Extensiveness: to assess missing values in registries and coverage of patients treated against the number of patients treated in other data sources.
Coherence: between recorded and derived values and duplicates.
In practice, these DQIs were requested as supplemental analyses from registries during the course of the PASS [ ].

3.

## Results
3.1.

## DQ Concepts Coverage Varied Across Tools, and Are Often Implicitly Defined
With the exception of the EMA‐DQF RWD checklist, which is derived from the EMA‐DQF, none of the other tools have all equivalent DQ concepts defined or implied (Figure  ). For instance, there are no definitions that correspond with EMA‐DQF dimensions or metrics within EUnetHTA REQueST, while only Relevance (1 out of 11) is defined in SPIFD. EMA REQueST and Authentic Transparent Relevant Accurate Track‐Record (ATRAcTR) have the highest number of concepts overlapping with the EMA‐DQF defined explicitly (5 out of 11 EMA‐DQF dimensions and metrics). If one also considers indirect references to similar concepts, both EMA REQueST and DataSAT cover nine out of 11 EMA‐DQF dimensions and metrics.
Among the EMA‐DQF dimensions and metrics, precision and structural coherence are not explicitly defined in any other tool apart from the EMA‐DQF RWD checklist. Definitions for coverage, format coherence, semantic coherence, uniqueness, and timeliness are also provided in only one more tool apart from the EMA‐DQF RWD checklist.
Furthermore, even if definitions are provided for a given DQ dimension or metric, there are differences in how these are defined. For instance, the EMA‐DQF defines format coherence as “whether data are expressed in the same way throughout a dataset (e.g., a dataset mixing dates represented as DD‐MM‐YYYY and MM‐DD‐YYYY will not be suitable for integrated analysis).” This can be mapped to the notion of consistency, which is present in both EMA REQueST and DataSAT (Table  ). However, EMA REQueST has an added focus on the consistency of formats over time, while DataSAT emphasizes the consistency between the format of recorded data elements and data source specifications.
The situation can be more complicated when definitions for a DQ dimension or metric are not explicitly provided. For example, timeliness is defined in the EMA‐DQF as the availability of data at the right time for regulatory decision‐making, which entails that data are collected and made available within an acceptable time. Currency is a specific aspect of timeliness that considers how fresh the data are (e.g., current and immediately useful). EMA REQueST defines timeliness as “timely recording and reporting of data and data updates, based on their intended use in compliance with an agreed procedure.” In contrast, SPIFD is more focused on operational aspects that influence timeliness such as assessing contracting logistics, time to data access, and time to complete the analysis. In this example, if a MAH were using EMA REQueST to assess the timeliness of a registry, the operational components mentioned in SPIFD may be missed.

3.2.

## Some Information or Documentation Requirements Are More Useful Than Others for DQ Evaluation
We evaluated the utility of the selected tools for assessing the dimensions and metrics of DQ in the EMA‐DQF. We identified 67 unique information or documentation requirements across the tools analyzed (Tables  , and ). None of these requirements allowed for the full evaluation of all EMA‐DQF dimensions and metrics. Furthermore, some of these requirements were more informative than others for DQ evaluation (Table  ). Of the 67 information requirements, 8 (12%) were found to be useful for full or partial evaluation of at least 5 out of 10 of the EMA‐DQF dimensions and metrics. Among these, the most important are the data dictionary, the data model and the data management plan (Figure  , Table  ). Moreover, all tools required information on data collection processes, while information related to data manipulation (e.g., data extraction, transformation and loading) was required in all tools except EUnetHTA REQueST (Figure  ).

3.3.

## Providing Consistent Definitions of Information or Documentation Requirements May Enhance DQ Evaluation
In the analysis of information requirements across the tools described in Section  , we noted variations in the level of detail included in each tool to define the requirement or the elements of a requirement that are needed in DQ evaluation. A valuable addition from the EMA‐DQF RWD is a mapping of information requirements against DQ dimensions and metrics that they can be used to assess; furthermore, the EMA‐DQF RWD often provides detailed descriptions of the information requirement (Table  ). For instance, the EMA‐DQF RWD specifies that the data manipulation steps should include documentation on data transformations performed, data cleaning steps, data mapping steps, and any loss of precision that arises from data manipulation. In contrast, EUnetHTA REQueST only requires a description for the data cleaning plan, whereas the EMA REQueST requires capability (and experience) for data cleaning, extraction, transformation, and analysis (Table  ).
There are cases, nonetheless, where there may be an assumption that all users of the tool understand information and documentation requirements in the same way. For instance, all tools require a data dictionary (Figure  ). However, none of the tools define what a data dictionary is. In our experience with registries, it is important to emphasize the difference between a data dictionary and a variable listing or a design specification [ ]. Furthermore, it is important to emphasize that data dictionaries are exported from the database management system. Finally, it would be useful if minimum required data dictionary fields were identified (Figure  ). Except for EUnetHTA REQueST, which indicates that identifying attributes (name, ID), definitional attributes (definition and purpose of the data element); and representational attributes (permissible values, representation class, data type, format) should be included in the data dictionary (Table  ), none of the other tools provide guidance on the minimum fields that it should contain. In addition to these elements, we would suggest the addition of the nullability field (Figure  ). Data dictionaries with these fields enable the full assessment of precision and format coherence while supporting partial review of completeness as well as structural and semantic coherence. For example, if the field for nullability is missing, it would not be possible to determine if a variable designated as part of the minimum dataset is truly mandatory.
We also observed that some of the information requirements across the tools are stated very differently. For example, EUnetHTA REQueST outlines specific queries related to interoperability and readiness for data linkage, including data access and sharing procedures; average time to answer information queries and to perform data linkage; modalities for data sharing and use across geographies; technical standards that allow for harmonization of linked data; and fees for data linkage. In contrast, EMA REQueST, which was adapted by the EMA from EUnetHTA REQueST, asks for the capability and experience for record linkage (Tables  and ). In EUPAS24484 and EUPAS19800, “Capability and experience” were interpreted differently, and only a few registry holders provided relevant information for operationalizing linkage for registry‐based PASS.
Finally, we noted that there are highly related information requirements such as data flow (mentioned in SPIFD, Figure  ) and data collection processes (all tools, Figure  ) that normally feed into a document requirement such as a data management plan (DataSAT and EMA‐DQF checklist). In the case of all these requirements, no definitions are provided for what constitutes data flow or a data management plan (Table  ). Examples of what elements of the data collection processes are provided for the tools but are again variable in scope and specificity (Tables  and ). For the eight information requirements presented in Figure  , we propose definitions and descriptions that can be used to clarify the needed depth of detail required to support better DQ evaluation. Where possible, the definitions are derived from software engineering terminology or existing regulatory guidance or templates (Table  ).

3.4.

## Key Dimensions and Metrics of the EMA‐DQF Can Only Be Evaluated Through Quantitative Analysis
Although qualitative assessments are a useful first step in DQ evaluation, extensiveness (e.g., completeness and coverage), semantic coherence, and reliability (e.g., accuracy and plausibility) can only be fully evaluated via quantitative analysis. This can be seen in the examples of reliability metrics in the EMA‐DQF (Section 6.1.3, Table  ) [ ]. However, there has been no relevant guidance for evaluating reliability in registries with restricted patient‐level data access until the EMA‐DQF RWD was published. The EMA‐DQF RWD acknowledges the limitation in addressing reliability in secondary use of data studies and highlights that the focus is on error detection that could lead to record removal or amendment through proxies. Based on best practices in 2017, MAHs utilized questionnaires and interviews to evaluate the completeness of safety outcomes collected by a registry [ ]. Under this model, registry owners provide completeness estimates for study variables (Figure  ), which are validated against available publications and supporting documents. The level of completeness is of particular importance when reviewing composite outcomes such as safety. A complete safety outcome includes the event name, onset date, end date (or resolution date), and the status of resolution. Unless all these fields are mandatory (i.e., non‐nullable) in the data capture system, there is a likelihood that not all elements are completed, with some components of this outcome such as end date of serious adverse events having a lower level of completeness (Figure  ). If the registry has not systematically collected non‐nullable safety outcome data prior to the onset of the feasibility, then the actual completeness of the safety outcomes can only be determined when a quantitative analysis is performed, which may coincide with the first PASS interim report.
We propose a minimal set of disease‐area agnostic DQIs on reliability, extensiveness and coherence that can be used for registry‐based PASS (Figure  ), together with an extended set of DQIs in Table  .
 In coherence, the incidence of adverse events compared with external background population (accuracy) and treatment exposure conformance with treatment guidance (plausibility) can be assessed. For extensiveness, several completeness of core data field completion (completeness) and coverage of treated patients (extensiveness) are used. For coherence, coherence between recorded and derived values or between information in different registries (semantic) and the number of duplicates (uniqueness) are defined.
For instance, semantic coherence can be partially evaluated using the data dictionary. Here, one can check if:

–The same variable is collected in multiple data entry forms, which could result in semantic incoherence.
–The same variable appears in multiple data entry forms, but is cross‐referenced in the database using foreign keys, which is a better guarantee for semantic coherence.
However, if a variable can both be directly collected in data entry forms (e.g., age of death) AND derived from other independent variables (e.g., date of death and date of birth), a simple analysis of the data dictionary will be inconclusive. In such cases, a DQI that compares the concordance of the values will be the only conclusive evaluation of semantic coherence.

4.

## Discussion
We present an assessment of selected tools that can be used to evaluate DQ in registries with restricted patient‐level data access. This analysis does not constitute endorsement of any specific tool or the EMA‐DQF; rather, it provides an in‐depth examination of available tools for evaluating registry DQ against the EMA‐DQF. Our assessments showed that DQ concepts, definitions, and specifications for information and documentation requirements varied across tools. Moreover, while certain information is always required, such as the data dictionary, the tools do not provide definitions or minimum content criteria to ensure their utility. Thus, a degree of consistency, especially at the level of definitions for information and documentation requirements, will enable a wider range of stakeholders to have a common understanding of what needs to be evaluated [ ].
Ideally, tools should be aligned with a set of DQ dimensions and metrics for ease of interpretation and higher utility. Except for the EMA‐DQF RWD checklist and ATRAcTR, all tools were published before the EMA‐DQF. Thus, the observed gaps and discrepancies across these tools may reflect both the purpose of the tool and the evolution of thinking around DQ assessments. For instance, EUnetHTA REQueST was designed for use by registry owners to improve the quality of their registry and by international organizations (HTA and regulatory) that are evaluating whether to use registry data in evidence development [ , ]. EMA REQueST was specifically generated for evaluating the suitability of conducting a regulatory study as a registry‐based study [ ] (Table  ). In contrast, SPIFD is a framework for FFP rather than a DQ assessment tool. As such, it outlines the principles and focuses on the strategy rather than execution through detailed definitions. Nonetheless, the observed gaps and discrepancies can be used as the basis for recommendations that maximize the utility of currently available tools for DQ assessment.
4.1.

## Recommendation 1
A tighter association between DQ concepts in DQ frameworks and implementation tools is needed.
Most of the analyzed tools do not provide definitions of the DQ concepts. Furthermore, except for the EMA‐DQF RWD checklist and DataSAT, most tools also do not map information requirements to the DQ concept(s) that they can help evaluate. Providing these associations systematically is likely to support completeness of a DQ evaluation. Furthermore, this would support data users in articulating why an information requirement is important for DQ evaluation, especially in registries with restricted patient‐level data access.

4.2.

## Recommendation 2
The selection of the tool(s) for DQ evaluation should be geared toward minimizing uncertainties in the planned study.
None of the tools evaluated allows for the evaluation of all EMA‐DQF dimensions and metrics in registries with restricted patient‐level data access. The implications and risks associated with this gap depend on the study type and the variables impacted by DQ issues. While we do not specifically demonstrate how DQ evaluation minimizes risks and uncertainties related to the selection of a dataset, we encourage readers to consider how DQ evaluation can be tailored to more closely evaluate the risks and regulatory impacts of decisions based on the data [ ]. Some influencing factors include: (1) the impact of the decision and the weight of the data as part of a regulatory submission package (e.g., Is the study intended to be descriptive for contextualization, or a comparative study that significantly influences decision‐making? Is the study meant to detect a safety signal versus establish causal inference?); (2) the acceptable level of uncertainty given the clinical and regulatory circumstances (e.g., the level of unmet medical need, rarity of disease, existing experience with the product), including the urgency of the data need; and (3) the risk of bias and the availability of key data variables required to meet the study objective.

4.3.

## Recommendation 3
Providing consistent definitions of information requirement definitions will simplify and improve DQ evaluation.
We have noted that key information requirements useful for evaluating EMA DQ dimensions and metrics (Figure  ) have widely varied descriptions across tools (Table  ). While the scope of this work does not cover harmonization of DQ terms or information requirements for DQ assessments, we have provided a starting point by using software engineering terminology or by referring to guidance on templates used in regulatory studies (Table  ). Through this exercise, we noted that the eight information requirements could be covered by five documents: data dictionary, database schema, registry protocol, data management plan, and a statistical analysis plan. Only two of these 5 documents (the data dictionary and data management plan) were mentioned in the tools analyzed. It was also noted that other information requirements detailed in Table  (e.g., inclusion and exclusion criteria, follow‐up, confounders, etc.) are already covered by the provision of these 5 documents. Finally, if a registry is missing these documents (e.g., the registry does not have a study protocol or a data management plan), it is already a useful indicator of its likely DQ and maturity. While the EMA‐DQF does not provide a definition for maturity, it provides clear descriptions of different levels of maturity: the minimum level for data to be considered for regulatory decision‐making is defined by the availability of accurate, verifiable, and versioned documentation related to processes that pertain to data generation and manipulation [ ]. A formal harmonization exercise in an appropriate venue will likely improve DQ assessments for secondary data use (e.g., the recently‐initiated ICH E23: Considerations for the Use of RWE to Inform Regulatory Decision‐Making with a Focus on Effectiveness of Medicines).

4.4.

## Recommendation 4
Use DQIs to complement existing tools for a more definitive DQ evaluation.
Qualitative assessments using the evaluated tools can demonstrate the theoretical viability of conducting a regulatory study in a registry. However, our findings show that these are insufficient for evaluating all EMA‐DQF DQ dimensions and metrics in registries with restricted patient‐level data access. Definitive DQ evaluation can only occur with quantitative evaluation [ ]. This can be partially addressed by incorporating analyses of key DQIs as part of the DQ assessment (Figure  , Table  ) given that a feasibility study (as opposed to a feasibility assessment) is not always possible under the typical timelines of PASS protocol approval. Assessing DQIs is crucial for identifying gaps in data. Pre‐defined DQIs support early issue detection and could allow for timely corrections. If used throughout a PASS, DQIs can be used for monitoring study uncertainties and for eventually justifying the exclusion of registries that continuously fail to provide the DQ necessary for a regulatory study.

5.

## Limitations
This study is not a systematic review of all tools that may be used for evaluating DQ in registries with restricted patient‐level data access. Instead, it focuses on tools recommended in current regulatory guidance documents or developed as part of multi‐stakeholder efforts [ ]. Additionally, it does not address the harmonization of these tools across different contexts of use. It also does not cover the generalizability of the recommendations to other RWD sources (e.g., EHRs or claims), study purpose different to safety, or tools that are more appropriate for evaluating their DQ. Finally, even if EMA‐DQF dimensions and metrics are more thoroughly evaluated from a qualitative and quantitative perspective, uncertainties still remain after DQ evaluation. In the draft EMA DQF‐RWD, the EMA implies that evaluation using tools used at study feasibility stage is ultimately focused on error detection and subsequent record removal or amendment [ ]. Furthermore, the EMA acknowledges that certain issues of DQ inherent to a data source initially considered FFP for use in a regulatory study may be challenging to resolve [ ]. For example, the use of DQIs alone will not fill in all unknowns related to registries that need to augment their data collection practices. There are still many cases where the first PASS interim report may be the earliest point where DQ can be definitively confirmed. Thus, the EMA recommends that these uncertainties are transparently acknowledged and their potential impact on study results evaluated in relevant documents (i.e., feasibility analysis, study protocol, and potentially in interim reports) [ ]. The EMA further recommends scenario mapping and defining go/no‐go criteria to remove or replace data sources that do not fulfill DQ requirements—and subsequently FFP—at a later stage [ ]. Finally, it is critical to have a pathway to discuss exit strategies and early termination criteria with both the EMA and registry owners. In line with the EMA‐DQF recommendation, this can be done by continuously evaluating DQ throughout the study lifecycle.

6.

## Conclusion
None of the tools assessed in this study covered all the dimensions of the EMA‐DQF in registries with restricted patient‐level data access. Additionally, some information requirements were more useful than others for evaluating DQ. In this work, we identified five key documents (data dictionary, database schema, protocol, data management plan, and a statistical analysis plan) that are most useful for qualitative DQ evaluation. The utility of documents such as publications is much more limited. We recommend supplementing the qualitative DQ evaluation with quantitative DQIs at the feasibility stage. If analyses are not possible to perform at this stage, it is important to acknowledge uncertainties and their potential impact on PASS results in key study documents. In this context, scenario mapping with criteria for the removal/replacement of registries after study initiation is needed. These scenario maps should also incorporate operational aspects that influence the continued relevance of the study results, such as timeliness. Finally, we recommend continuous DQ assessment throughout the lifecycle of a registry‐based PASS.
