## Introduction
Global neurosurgery (GNS) has emerged as a field dedicated at addressing disparities in neurosurgical access, training, and workforce distribution worldwide. Persistent inequities adversely affect population health outcomes contributing to preventable morbidity and mortality. Addressing these gaps is an essential component of universal health coverage and United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 3 (SDG3). This study aimed to characterize U.S. academic engagement in GNS over the past decade, with emphasis on geographic focus, thematic and subspecialty trends, and institutional leadership.

## Methods
PubMed, Embase, and Scopus were searched for studies published between 2014 and 2025. Eligible publications included original research, case reports or series, reviews, technical or programmatic studies, and commentaries involving neurosurgical populations in World Bank–classified low- and lower-middle–income countries (LIC/LMIC) and at least one U.S.-affiliated author. Extracted data included publication type, country of focus, U.S. academic institution, neurosurgical subspecialty, and thematic domain. Descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and linear regression analyses were performed.

## Results
A total of 340 publications met inclusion criteria. U.S.-affiliated GNS output increased over time, with a mean annual increase of 5.9 publications. Collaborations involved 68 of 81 (81%) LIC/LMICs; however, research activity was unevenly distributed, as 57% of represented countries appeared in fewer than five publications. Sub-Saharan Africa accounted for 61% of country-specific publication instances. Most studies addressed general GNS topics (69%), while 31% focused on subspecialties. Education, training, and workforce development comprised 24% of publications. Among subspecialties, pediatric neurosurgery (36%) and trauma/traumatic brain injury (23%) predominated. Three journals accounted for 53% of publications, and three U.S. institutions contributed 37% of total output.

## Conclusions
Engagement in global neurosurgery has increased over the past decade but remains concentrated within a limited number of countries, subspecialties, journals, and institutions. These findings highlight opportunities to expand partnerships, and promote more equitable and inclusive neurosurgical scholarship.

## Introduction
Global neurosurgery (GNS) has emerged as a distinct and rapidly expanding field focused on addressing profound inequities in neurosurgical care, training, and workforce distribution worldwide. These disparities directly undermine population health outcomes and exemplify broader global challenges to achieving equitable access to safe, affordable, and timely surgical care, a cornerstone of universal health coverage and a central target of SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being) endorsed by the United Nations [ ]. Accordingly, GNS emphasizes capacity building in regions with limited neurosurgical infrastructure and human resources, recognizing that surgical systems are essential to resilient health systems [ , , ].
Over the past decade, neurosurgeons and academic institutions in high-income countries (HIC) have increasingly engaged in efforts to support neurosurgical development in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) and low-income countries (LIC) [ ]. These engagements encompass a wide spectrum of activities, including clinical partnerships, educational exchanges, collaborative research, guideline development, and global advocacy [ , ]. Collectively, such initiatives reflect a broader shift in academic medicine toward global health equity, while also contributing to SDG 9 (Industry, Innovation, and Infrastructure) through the strengthening of research ecosystems, institutional partnerships, and sustainable neurosurgical infrastructure [ ].
Despite this growing momentum, the scope and structure of United States (US).-affiliated contributions to GNS remain incompletely characterized. Mapping scholarly activity provides a practical framework to standardize academic milestones for faculty and residents, identify successful collaborative models, and guide future investment in global neurosurgical initiatives. Understanding patterns of publication, thematic focus, geographic engagement, and institutional representation enables academic neurosurgery to optimize training, strengthen research networks, and support sustainable capacity building worldwide. Additionally, understanding these patterns is critical, as academic publications play a key role in shaping research priorities, guiding investment, fostering innovation, and informing policy—mechanisms that directly link health outcomes (SDG 3) with infrastructure and innovation development (SDG 9).
This study seeks to address this gap by analyzing GNS publications authored by individuals affiliated with U.S. institutions. We evaluate temporal publication trends, geographic and subspecialty focus areas, thematic domains, and institutional representation, providing insight into current contributions, identifying gaps and opportunities, and informing strategies to advance equitable neurosurgical care and sustainable health infrastructure.

## Methods and materials

## Literature review strategy, inclusion and exclusion criteria
A scoping review of the GNS literature was conducted to characterize U.S. academic contributions from January 1, 2014, through December 31, 2025 [ ]. Searches were performed in PubMed, Embase, and Scopus using combinations of the term "global neurosurgery" and related geographic, clinical, and institutional keywords.
Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) they reported original research, case reports, reviews, commentaries, technical notes, or case series involving neurosurgical populations located outside the U.S. and specifically included at least one LIC/LMIC; (2) included at least one author affiliated with a U.S.-based institution at the time of the study or publication, regardless of author position; and (3) were published in English with full-text availability. LIC/LMIC country status was defined according to the World Bank country income classifications in 2025.
Studies were excluded if they: (1) involved cadaveric, animal, or phantom models; (2) were standalone books or chapters from neurosurgical textbooks; or (3) did not clearly identify the LIC/LMIC country or explicitly state that LIC/LMIC populations were included in the study.
IRB approval was not required since all data were derived from publicly available literature.

## Data extraction
All identified records were imported into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) for deduplication, title and abstract screening, and full-text review. Five reviewers independently screened records, with each record reviewed by at least two reviewers; conflicts were resolved by a sixth reviewer. Data were extracted using a standardized template and included study type, year of publication, journal, U.S. institution(s), geographic focus, sample size, neurosurgical condition(s) or procedure(s) addressed, U.S.-affiliated author position, key findings, and thematic category. Only studies addressing GNS relevant to LICs/LMICs, as defined by World Bank income classifications for the year of publication, were included [ ]. Each eligible manuscript was additionally reviewed to determine whether at least one co-author was affiliated with an institution based in the LICs/LMICs under study at the time of publication.
Each study was categorized into one of seven mutually exclusive methodological types: (1) quantitative research, (2) qualitative or mixed-methods research, (3) case-based reports or series, (4) survey-based studies, (5) reviews and evidence syntheses (including narrative and systematic reviews), (6) technical or programmatic studies, and (7) commentaries, opinion pieces, and historical analyses. These categories were developed through iterative review of the included literature to capture the breadth of academic output in GNS and to distinguish between empirical, narrative, and implementation-focused contributions.
Studies were also coded into thematic domains, including healthcare access, education/training and workforce, policy and advocacy, general neurosurgery, research development, and neurosurgical subspecialties. Each publication was assigned a single primary thematic classification to avoid overlapping across domains defined as the theme representing at least 50% of the publication’s content. Publications categorized as neurosurgical subspecialties were further labeled by clinical focus, including trauma, pediatric, spine, neuro-oncology, cerebrovascular surgery, and functional/epilepsy surgery.

## Statistical analysis
Descriptive statistics were used to summarize categorical variables, including study type, thematic domain, neurosurgical subspecialty, geographic focus, and U.S. institution. Temporal trends in publication volume from 2014 to 2024 were assessed using linear regression analysis. Chi-square goodness-of-fit tests evaluated whether study type, thematic domain, and subspecialty distributions deviated from uniformity. All statistical analyses were conducted using Python 3.10 with the SciPy library. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## U.S. Global neurosurgery publications over time
The initial search yielded 7,815 publications. After duplicate removal and title and abstract screening, 703 full-text articles were reviewed. A total of 340 studies met inclusion criteria (Fig.  ). Of the 340 included studies, all featured a U.S.-affiliated first or senior author, and all included at least one co-author affiliated with an LIC/LMIC relevant to the study publication. 
GNS publications involving U.S. institutions increased markedly over the study period. The number of annual publications increased at an average rate of approximately 5.9 publications per year (slope = 5.90, R2 = 0.72, p = 0.0005), demonstrating a strong and statistically significant upward trend in U.S.-affiliated GNS research over time, with particularly pronounced growth in 2024 and 2025 (Fig.  ). 

## U.S. Global neurosurgery geographic focus within LMIC and LIC
U.S.-affiliated GNS studies included collaborations with 68 LICs/LMICs during the study period, totaling 615 country-specific publication instances. The top five most frequent partnerships were with Uganda (57 publications), Tanzania (52), Nigeria (45), Haiti (36), and Ethiopia (32). Sub-Saharan Africa accounted for the majority of collaborations (373 publications, 60.7%), followed by South Asia (11.9%) and Southeast Asia (9.6%). The geographic distribution differed significantly from an even distribution (χ2 = 1124.8, p < 0.001) (Fig.  ). Of the 68 represented LIC/LMIC countries, 39 (57%) were represented in fewer than five publications. 

## Global neurosurgery study type, thematic distribution, and subspecialty topics
The most common publication types were original quantitative studies (36%), followed by reviews or evidence syntheses (23%). Commentaries, opinion, and historical pieces (13%) and survey-based studies (14%) comprised similar proportions, while technical or programmatic studies accounted for 8%. Qualitative and mixed-methods studies (2%) and case-based studies (3%) were relatively uncommon. The distribution of publication types differed significantly from an even distribution (χ2 = 211.35, p < 0.001) (Fig.  ). 
Among included publications, 31% (105/340) focused on neurosurgical subspecialties, while 69% addressed general GNS domains. The most common general categories were education/training and workforce (24%), followed by policy and advocacy (14%) and general neurosurgery (14%), with healthcare access (11%) and research-focused studies (7%) less frequently represented. The thematic distribution differed significantly across categories (χ2 = 79.36, p < 0.001) (Fig.  A). 
Among the 105 subspecialty-focused studies, pediatric neurosurgery was most common (36%), followed by trauma/TBI (23%). Neuro-oncology (14%), spine (11%), and functional/epilepsy surgery (10%) were moderately represented, while cerebrovascular surgery (5%) was least frequent. The subspecialty distribution also differed significantly from an even distribution (χ2 = 39.86, p < 0.001). (Fig.  B).

## Discussion
Our study provides a comprehensive characterization of U.S.-affiliated scholarly engagement in GNS over the past decade, integrating trends in publication growth, geographic reach, thematic focus, and institutional contribution. Together, these findings situate U.S. GNS scholarship at the intersection of health equity and health system development, reflecting both a sustained expansion in academic output and a persistent concentration of research activity within select countries, subspecialties, and institutions. Beyond documenting trends, this analysis illustrates how patterns of knowledge production may shape research priorities, capacity-building strategies, and the focus of academic partnerships across diverse health systems. In this context, U.S. engagement in GNS aligns with broader global efforts to advance equitable access to surgical care, a central objective of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, particularly SDG 3. Importantly, this framework may also serve as a model for other HIC countries to systematically evaluate their own GNS engagement, identify strengths and gaps, and inform more strategic, collaborative, and equitable approaches to future partnerships across the globe.

## Growth of global neurosurgery scholarship and its system-level implications
Over the past decade, U.S.-affiliated academic engagement in GNS has increased steadily, as demonstrated by a significant rise in annual publication volume. This upward trend may reflect growing awareness of global surgical disparities, increased institutional prioritization of global health, and expanded opportunities for international collaboration. A transient decline in publication output during 2022–2023 coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic, which substantially disrupted academic, clinical, and cross-border research activities worldwide [ , ]. This fluctuation highlights both the momentum of GNS scholarship and its vulnerability to external disruptions, underscoring the need for more resilient and adaptable models of international academic collaboration.

## Geographic concentration and equity in GNS scholarly output
Despite the overall growth in U.S.-affiliated GNS publications, engagement remains heavily concentrated in a limited number of LMIC and LIC settings. Uganda, Tanzania, and Haiti accounted for the largest share of U.S.-authored collaborations, whereas 45% of LIC/LMIC were individually represented in fewer than five publications and 19% were not represented at all. This uneven geographic distribution highlights a persistent imbalance in GNS scholarship that parallels broader disparities in surgical capacity and research infrastructure worldwide.
Concentrated engagement may reflect pragmatic considerations, including longstanding institutional partnerships, relatively stable research infrastructure, and established philanthropic or training programs [ , , , , , ]. In countries such as Uganda and Tanzania, sustained collaboration has enabled deeper capacity-building initiatives and longitudinal research efforts. While these focused partnerships can yield meaningful local impact, they may also inadvertently perpetuate inequities by limiting scholarly engagement in other high-need regions.
From an SDG 3 standpoint, this concentration risks reinforcing disparities in access to evidence-informed neurosurgical care. From an SDG 9 perspective, it highlights missed opportunities to extend research infrastructure and innovation capacity to a broader range of settings. Coordinated strategies—such as inter-institutional consortia, shared partnership mapping, and intentional engagement with underrepresented regions—could help diversify geographic participation while preserving the benefits of long-term collaboration.

## Thematic and subspecialty emphasis: Aligning burden, innovation, and systems needs
Our findings reveal a pronounced thematic and subspecialty skew within U.S.-affiliated GNS publications. Pediatric neurosurgery emerged as the most frequently studied subspecialty, followed by TBI. This emphasis is consistent with the substantial global burden of pediatric neurosurgical conditions and neurotrauma, particularly in resource-limited settings, where these conditions contribute disproportionately to morbidity and mortality [ , , , , , ].
The prominence of pediatric neurosurgery may also reflect ethical and philanthropic imperatives associated with childhood disease, as well as the long-term societal benefits of early intervention [ , , ]. These characteristics make pediatric neurosurgery a natural entry point for capacity-building initiatives, training programs, and donor-supported research, thereby reinforcing its visibility within the literature.
Conversely, subspecialties such as cerebrovascular, functional, and epilepsy surgery were comparatively underrepresented. While these areas may require advanced infrastructure and specialized expertise, their relative absence highlights a critical gap in the comprehensive development of neurosurgical systems. Progress toward SDG 3 necessitates not only addressing high-burden conditions but also expanding the full spectrum of neurosurgical services as health systems mature. From an SDG 9 perspective, broader subspecialty engagement reflects growing innovation capacity and institutional readiness within partner settings.

## Knowledge dissemination, journals, and research infrastructure
The concentration of U.S.-affiliated GNS publications within a small number of journals underscores the influential role of editorial policies and publication models in shaping the global research landscape. World Neurosurgery, the most frequent publication venue in this analysis, historically did not require article processing charges, a feature that may have facilitated broader participation by authors from LIC/LMIC settings. Journals that offer fee waivers and reduced-cost access for authors from LIC/LMIC settings may lower barriers to participation and partially explain their central role in disseminating GNS scholarship [ , ].
While open-access publishing can enhance visibility and inclusivity, it also introduces new challenges, including publication fees, editorial gatekeeping, and vulnerability to predatory practices if not supported by transparent and equitable safeguards [ , ]. Broadening the range of journals that actively engage with GNS research could enhance cross-disciplinary visibility, stimulate innovation, and reduce overreliance on a limited publication ecosystem—an important consideration for strengthening research infrastructure under SDG 9.

## Institutional concentration and capacity building in U.S. global neurosurgery
At the institutional level, a small number of U.S. academic centers accounted for a disproportionate share of GNS publications, with three institutions contributing nearly 40% of total output. These institutions often benefit from dedicated global health divisions, established international networks, and stable funding streams that support sustained engagement [ , ]. Their success demonstrates the potential of structured investment, mentorship, and long-term partnerships to generate scalable and sustainable models of global neurosurgical collaboration [ ].
However, the concentration of scholarly output within a limited set of institutions also reflects persistent structural barriers faced by emerging programs, including limited funding, lack of mentorship, regulatory constraints, and insufficient institutional recognition of global health-oriented work [ , ]. Addressing these challenges is essential to broadening participation and diversifying academic engagement.
To address these gaps, strategies such as cross-institutional collaboration, shared mentorship pipelines, targeted funding mechanisms, and virtual networking platforms have shown promise in lowering barriers to entry and increasing collaborative output [ , ]. Strengthening these mechanisms aligns directly with SDG 9 by expanding research capacity and innovation ecosystems, while simultaneously advancing SDG 3 through more equitable knowledge generation and dissemination.
In addition to HIC–LMIC collaborations, the development of organized neurosurgery within LMICs faces significant structural challenges. The limited presence of major pharmaceutical and medical device companies on the continent constrains opportunities for financing, research partnerships, and technology transfer, which could otherwise accelerate capacity building [ , ]. Strengthening both HIC–LMIC partnerships and intra-LMIC collaboration is therefore essential to expand training, foster sustainable research, and fast-track the growth of neurosurgical systems across the continent.

## Rethinking the scope of “global” in global neurosurgery
Our analysis also identified frequent collaborations between U.S. institutions and other HIC. While these partnerships differ in scope and objectives from capacity-development efforts in LIC/LMIC settings, they contribute to GNS through comparative research, multicenter trials, and the generation of globally generalizable evidence [ , , , ]. Such reciprocal collaborations can accelerate innovation, refine best practices, and inform international guidelines, with potential benefits across both high- and low-resource settings [ , ].
Collectively, these findings suggest that GNS encompasses a spectrum of collaborative intent, ranging from equity-driven capacity building to bidirectional scientific exchange. Explicitly distinguishing these collaboration types in future analyses may help clarify their respective contributions to health equity, infrastructure development, and innovation.

## Limitations
Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, our analysis was limited to publications indexed in PubMed and Scopus and to those published in English. Consequently, relevant research appearing in non-indexed journals or other languages may have been excluded, potentially underestimating the full scope of U.S. involvement in GNS. Second, although inclusion criteria required at least one author to be affiliated with a U.S.-based institution, the extent of individual author contributions and engagement likely varied widely across studies. Third, the classification of studies into thematic categories and subspecialties involved subjective judgment, despite standardized criteria and independent review. Given that some studies span multiple domains, decisions regarding mutually inclusive coding may have influenced thematic distribution patterns. Fourth, chapters and full books were omitted to maintain analytic consistency and focus on peer-reviewed empirical work, potentially excluding meaningful contributions, especially in education and advocacy. Fifth, publication data may not accurately reflect real-time clinical activity or sustained international partnerships; therefore, this study represents academic output rather than a comprehensive measure of all global neurosurgical engagement by U.S. institutions. Sixth, all included countries were appropriately classified as LIC/LMIC; however, income status was determined using World Bank designations at the time of data extraction rather than contemporaneous with publication, which may have resulted in the exclusion of countries that advanced economically over the study period. Finally, the cross-sectional nature of the analysis limits our ability to assess the long-term impact or quality of these studies. Future research should evaluate outcomes related to capacity building and the extent to which published work translates into sustainable improvements in global neurosurgical care.
The patterns of U.S.-affiliated scholarly engagement in GNS may offer useful lessons for programs in other high-income countries, including Europe. Structured mentorship, resident engagement programs, and concentrated institutional investment have facilitated robust scholarship, supported less popular subspecialties, and optimized funding utilization. European programs may consider these strategies when designing resident tracks, allocating resources, and expanding comprehensive subspecialty coverage, thereby strengthening both academic impact and global neurosurgical capacity-building efforts.

## Conclusion
GNS literature has grown steadily over the past decade, mirroring increased academic and institutional engagement with international neurosurgical challenges. This growth reflects heightened recognition of neurosurgical care as a critical component of global health systems and underscores the field’s potential to advance health equity (SDG 3) and strengthen research, innovation, and infrastructure (SDG 9). However, scholarly output remains unevenly distributed across countries, institutions, subspecialties, and publication venues, revealing both areas of strength and missed opportunities for broader engagement. Advancing a more inclusive and sustainable global neurosurgical agenda will require deliberate strategies to diversify partnerships, broaden clinical focus areas, and democratize academic participation. By addressing these gaps, the neurosurgical community can play a pivotal role in fostering a more equitable, resilient, and globally impactful future for neurosurgical care. Moreover, this framework may serve as a model for other HIC to systematically evaluate their own GNS engagement, identify strengths and gaps, and guide strategic, collaborative efforts in neurosurgery across the globe.
