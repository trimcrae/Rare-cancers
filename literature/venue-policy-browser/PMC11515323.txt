## Abstract

## Background
Medicine wastage occurs when medicines are damaged, obsolete, or expired and become unsafe for use. In Ethiopia, although there are reports that reveal the presence of wasted medicines in the country, there is limited objective evidence on the type and extent of medicine wastage as well as its contributing factors.

## Objective
The study aimed to assess the medicine wastage rate and identify its contributing factors in the sampled public health facilities (HFs) in Hadiya Zone, Central Ethiopia Regional State.

## Method
A facility-based cross-sectional study complemented by a qualitative study was conducted in selected public HFs in Hadiya Zone, Central Ethiopia Regional State, from November 1 to 31, 2022. All the hospitals were selected intentionally, and health centers were selected using simple random sampling techniques. For the qualitative study, the chief clinical officers and pharmacy department heads of HFs were purposefully selected as key informants because they were supposed to be more information-rich than other health professionals. The quantitative data were entered and analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences version 20.

## Result
The total monetary value of wasted medicines in the assessed HFs in the Ethiopian Fiscal Year (EFY) 2011–2013 was 652,479.45 ETB (5.24%). The three-year trend showed that, in the 2011–2012–2013 EFY, medicine wastage was 7.57%, 6.44%, and 3.1%, respectively. Expiration solely accounted for 97.3% of this total value. The alleged explanations for the waste of such medicines were a lack of accurate data available for medicine quantifications, the presence of overstocked medicines due to improper forecasting, a lack of electronic stock management, an abrupt change in the treatment regimen, and improper use of stock management. Whether the perceived causes had an impact on the medicine wastage was assessed, and the results confirmed that a significant association between medicine wastage and overstock (p-value = 0.006, AOR = 8.57, 95% CI = 1.87–39.26), accurate data (p-value = 0.013, AOR = 5.43, 95% CI = 1.42–20.76), and electronic stock management (p-value = 0.05, AOR = 0.311, 95% CI = 0.094–1.03) was observed.

## Conclusion
The identified medicine wastage rate is significant for a nation that struggles to allocate limited resources fairly. It may negatively affect the initiatives taken by the government to increase public engagement in the healthcare system through insurance contributions. It is a glance for HFs to take immediate corrective action.

## Introduction

## Background
Medicines are substances used to prevent, diagnose, cure, mitigate, or treat disease [ ]. They are indispensable products in the course of health care service delivery and when available and properly managed, they have been demonstrated to increase customer satisfaction along with patient adherence [ , ]. In the process of ensuring the availability of medicines to the population, there is a possibility that these medicines may be damaged—change color, texture, and smell, stick together, become harder or softer than normal, or crack or chip, etc.; or expired—the dates on drug labels medicine companies guarantee their effectiveness being enforced by the Food and Drug Administration to do so and become unsafe for use [ , ]. Besides, medicines left over from patients and those identified to be defective may accumulate over time adding to the stock of unsafe-for-use medicines [ ]. The resulting inventory of unfit-for-use medicines is commonly referred to as medicine waste [ , ].
Medicine wastage is defined as unwanted medications which include expired, damaged, unused, spilled, improperly sealed, labeled or stored, counterfeit, substandard and adulterated, prohibited or unauthorized medicines, and contaminated medicines that are no longer required and need to be disposed of properly [ – ]. Some literature even goes on to include medicine lost through pilferage and theft in the medicine wastage definition [ ]. As expiry is a legal element of medicine's shelf life, complete avoidance of medicines wastage is unlikely. Therefore, countries set a threshold point. Under its Health Sector Development Programme IV, for example, Ethiopia aimed at reducing medicines wastage due to expiration to 2% [ ]. For a nation that heavily depends on the import (80% of medicines); where many stakeholders play a key role in the drug supply chain, transparency and accountability on medicine transactions and services is poor, inventory management is weak; and port clearance and medicines delivery meantime is relatively long, maintaining the promised plan is unlikely [ – ].
Many nations have been finding it difficult to reach their target cutoff point, even though their progress has lagged or fallen short. This permits medicine waste to continue harming the healthcare system because; medicines account for about 25% of total health expenditure [ , ]. According to some findings, about 70% of the money invested in essential medicines is wasted in normal drug supply systems [ ]. This, in turn, reduces the quantity of medicines available to patients and, hence, the quality of health care at large [ ]. Besides creating an unnecessary burden on an already financially restrained health care system by being over-stored in HFs—limiting available space for inventory of usable medicine supplies; and loaded and stored for a long period—posing significant risks both to the environment and humans, it is increasing health care costs and jeopardize human life and health [ , , ].
Lack of good governance, inefficient medicines supply management, and poor inventory control were the most common causes reported by papers [ , , ]. Beyond enacting proclamations and issuing regulations, as well as poor and inconsistent enforcement, for a sector that accounts for 5.06% of the nation's GDP and experiences yearly average growth of 14.3%, government insufficiency allows opportunists to thrive and alter drug prescribing patterns [ , ]. An agent (Medicine Fund and Supply Agency) established to supply health commodities uninterruptedly for both public and private health sectors, fails to secure drug supply chain health [ ]. Furthermore, there is a lack of transparency and accountability in medicine transactions and services, a poor inventory control culture, and an absence of tools that automatically capture and system that obligates HFs to document and report contributions for medicine waste [ , ].
Various studies attest that one of the widespread issues facing the healthcare system is medicine waste. Globally, its prevalence reached 15% [ ]. It essentially has a significant impact on both developed and underdeveloped nations. According to the WHO, out of 25% of the total health expenditure, or 1.4 to 1.63% of the Gross Domestic Product, which has been invested in medicines, for instance, the United States of America (USA), Saudi Arabia and Ethiopia lost 23%, 25.8%, and 3.7% of their medicines as a waste, respectively [ , , ].
The current study was conducted to determine the medicine wastage rate and identify factors contributing to the wastage of medicines in selected HFs of the Hadiya Zone.

## Methods and materials

## Study design, area, and period
A facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted applying standardized data gathering tools in selected public HFs of Hadiya zone, Central Ethiopia Region State, Ethiopia from November 1–31, 2022. The zone has 3 hospitals, 61 health centers, and 311 health posts. Fifty-two pharmacists and 163 druggists were working in these public HFs’ pharmacy units.

## Populations
All the public HFs, health professionals, and documents that were used for medicine wastage tracking, like medicine wastage registration book or chart and/or disposal report and model 19, were sources of population. Health professionals involved in DTC and medicine supply management activities, and documents that tracked medicine wastage between EFYs of 2011 and 2013, i.e., medicine wastage registration chart and/or disposal report and model 19 selected as a source population.

## Inclusion and exclusion criteria
All public HFs having a history of more than 3 years of operation, health professionals who have more than 6 months of work experience, and the value of medicine wasted with their price recorded from 2011 EFY-2013 EFY were included in the present study. However, program and donation medicines that were wasted and recorded as free of charge were excluded from the study.

## Study variables
In this study, we employed medicine wastage rate as the dependent variable and calculated using the following formula [ ]. Socio-demographic (Table  ), professionalism, and health facility factors were independent variables of the study (Supplementary files: Annex 1).

MedicineWastageRate=ValuesofWestedMedicineinaYear∗TotalValueofMedicinesReceivedDuringtheSameYear∗x100% *—The monetary value taken was the selling price, (value received or purchased cost was changed into selling prices).

## Sample size determination and sampling techniques
The sample of HFs is calculated by using the Logistics Indicators Assessment Tool (LIAT) [ ]. Accordingly, 12 HFs were selected through a simple random sampling technique, and 3 hospitals were purposively selected. It resulted in HF coverage of 23.4%, over the 15% minimum threshold suggested in the LIAT guideline [ ].
Volunteered health professionals involved in DTC and medicine supply management were enrolled for self-administered questions. Medicine wastage tracking documents between the EFYs of 2011 and 2013 were assessed to produce quantitative data. Key informants for the qualitative data were chosen to be the chief executive officers (CEOs-15) and the heads of the pharmacy departments (15) of the HFs.

## Data collection tool and procedure

## Data collection
The quantitative data were collected by two trained data collectors using data abstraction formats and self-administered questionnaires adopted from LIAT (Supplementary Files: Annex 3 and Annex 4). The qualitative data were collected through in-depth interviews of HFs’ CEOs and pharmacy department heads/representatives (Supplementary Files: Annex 5 and Annex 6).

## Data collection instrument

## Quantitative study
Records of medicine wastage registration books/charts, disposal reports, and model 19 were reviewed to abstract the value of medicine wastes and the types of wasted medicines. For medicine wastage value, data collection sheets were developed from the Logistics Indicator Assessment Tool (LIAT) based on the EFMHACA medicine waste management and disposal directive (Supplementary Files: Annex 4) [ , ]. Questions concerned with contributing factors of medicine wastage were adapted from previous literature (Supplementary Files: Annex 5) [ ]. Each question was scored as 1 for participants’ response ‘Yes’ and 2 for participants’ response ‘No’.

## Qualitative study
A semi-structured interview guide was prepared to explore the ideas of key informants about the situation of medicine wastage, contributing factors, consequences, and efforts to prevent or minimize the problem. The interview guide was developed after a literature search (Supplementary Files: Annex 5) [ , , ].

## Data quality assurance
The data-gathering instruments were pretested, and data collectors received training. The data was then cleaned, and its completeness was confirmed daily, while it was being gathered. Incomplete data were removed, and data collectors were switched. The digitally recorded voices from the in-depth interview were examined, along with their transcriptions.

## Data entry and analysis
Individual responses to each question were coded, and quantitative data were entered and analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 20. The analysis was done in aggregates and used descriptive statistics. Binary regression was used to investigate the association between independent and dependent variables, taking a P-value of less than 0.05 is considered significant.
The selling price was taken from the medicine wastage registration book and disposal list documentation report for each wasted medicine from assessed HFs to estimate the extent of medicine wastage. To describe the type/classes of medicine wastage, the pharmaco-therapeutic classification system of the Ethiopian national essential medicine list was utilized [ ]. When a medicine was found in multiple dosage forms and strengths, it was considered a variable medicine and assessed separately. The total cost of the medicines that were received or purchased was estimated using the prices listed on Model 19.
For the qualitative analysis, the data was handled manually and analyzed using a thematic analysis approach. An initial category for analyzing data was drawn from the interview guide, and themes and patterns emerged after reviewing the data. A key theme to emerge was: the situation of medicine wastage; contributing factors; consequences; and suggestions for improving medicine wastage.

## Results

## Socio-Demographics of Respondents
Seventy-five self-administered questionnaires were distributed to health professionals at the studied health institutions who were involved in DTC and other medicine management activities. Once completed, all of the questionnaires were returned, yielding a full response rate. Of the participants, 48(64%) were male and 27 (36%) were female, with a mean age of 32.5 years (± SD = 5.65). Most of the participants (69.3%) were degree holders. The three participants who had master's degrees resided in the studied hospital settings studied. In terms of professions, the majority of respondents (48%) were in the pharmacy profession, and the least were environmental health professionals (4%). Regarding work experience, over half (57.3%) had spent five to ten years working in HFs (Table  ).

## Magnitude of medicines wastage
The studied HFs had an average year of operation of 20.06 years (± SD = 10.26). The minimum year since the facilities became operational was 5 years (the maximum was 47 years). They received a total monetary value of 12,460,946.80 Ethiopian Birr (ETB) within the selected study period. Between the 2011 and 2013 Ethiopian Fiscal Year (EFY), these HFs lost 652,479.45 ETB due to medicine wastage resulting in an overall wastage rate of 5.24% (Table  ).

 In 2011, 2012, and 2013 EFY, the total monetary value of medicines received was 3,497,493.40 ETB; 3,331.164.13 ETB, and 5,632,289.27 ETB, respectively. When the overall amount of waste was evaluated annually, the average amount of waste decreased over time as more values were received. The total monetary value of wasted medicines was 264,734.30 ETB, 214,467.10 ETB, and 173,278.05 ETB respectively, for the consecutive studied EFYs, making the annual wastage rates 7.57%, 6.44%, and 3.1% (Fig.  ).
 When the hospitals’ findings were separated from the analysis, the average rate of wastage in the remaining twelve health centers was 48.7%, worth 317,692.94 ETB. In the hospital (n = 3), the average medicine wastage rate was estimated to be 51.3%, with a value of 148,097.62 ETB.

## Wasted medicines category, dosage forms, and cause
This study assessed the wasted medicines’ monetary value among the drugs category, the dosage forms, and their origins as follows.

## Category
In total, 96 different types of wasted medications were identified throughout all HFs evaluated from 2011 to 2013 EBY. Anti-infective (42.4%), gastrointestinal tract medications (14.2%), respiratory medications (11.5%), and fluids (10.2%) were the most frequently used therapeutic classes of wasted medications in terms of monetary value (Table  ). The three most wasted medicines by value were Erythromycin 500 mg tablets (8.6%), followed by Cloxacillin 500 mg capsule (5.7%), and Cotrimoxazole 960 mg tablets (5.4%) (Fig. ).

 

## Dosage forms
In the current study, wastage of all dosage forms was discovered. Two-thirds of the wasted medicines (67%) were in solid dosage forms. Tablets lead the others in this category, making up 31.5% of the overall cost of wasted medications (Table  ).

## Causes, contributing factors, and associations
According to the quantitative data, expiration was by far the major cause of medicine wastage (97.3%), accounting for 634,862.5 ETB loss. Damage and regimen change were the next two contributing factors, accounting for 2.1% and 0.06% of the total costs of waste medicines, or 13,702.07 and 3,914.88 ETB, respectively (Fig. ). Other postulated variables, like pillaging and theft, were found to have insignificant impact. The contributing factors for such amounts of losses have been evaluated by applying "Yes" or "No" questions to the healthcare workers involved in DTC and Medicine supply management operations. As per their responses, lack of accurate data available for medicines quantifications (72%) was the main factor, followed by the presence of overstocked medicines due to improper forecasting (69.3%), lack of electronic stock management that easily captures wastage (66.7%), abrupt change of treatment practice resulting in medicine wastage 49(65.3%) and improper use of stock management using FIFO/FEFO (61.3%) (Table  ).

 Binary logistic regression was computed to investigate the association between independent and medicine wastage rates. All the variables were considered in the bivariate logistic analysis; however, accurate data, treatment practice, stock management (FIFO/FEFO), Electronic stock management, and Overstock were candidates for multivariate logistic analysis at p-value < 0.5 (Table  ).
The candidate variables were computed and finally, a significant association was observed between medicine wastage rate and overstock at (p-value = 0.006, AOR = 8.57, 95% CI = 1.87–39.26), medicine wastage rate and accurate data at (p-value = 0.013, AOR = 5.43, 95% CI = 1.42–20.76) and medicine wastage rate and electronic stock management at (p-value = 0.05, AOR = 0.311, 95% CI = 0.094–1.03) (Table  ).

## Qualitative findings
In-depth interviews were conducted to learn more about the current state of medicine wastage in their HFs, the contributing factors, the impact on service delivery, and suggestions for reducing medicine waste.

## Factors contributing
Several reasons were identified by the key informants as contributing factors to medicine wastage in their facilities. Since the percentages of responses were shown under the quantitative assessment, here, causes that have been proven to have a direct association with medicine wastage are shown.
The majority of the key informants concurred that medicine waste is an issue at their health facility. They also stressed that the reason it sometimes appears to be diminishing isn't because of steps taken to reduce it; rather, it's because of inadequate drug supply and rising demand for it. Some said that, compared to earlier, it was rising. A reply clarified the circumstances as follows:
I believe that the inadequate implementation of IPLS is largely to blame for the rising rate of medicine waste. However, we don't always receive the desired outcome when we start implementing the IPLS with the assumption that inadequate record-keeping and documentation are the primary causes of our drug supply issue. The agent in charge of providing us with the medication we require based on our quantification report rejects the report and responds as it pleases. The management is responsible for carrying out internal demand and does not use the IPLS's offered information. Therefore, it seems pointless to use medicine inventory control systems—to generate accurate and reliable information. Forecasting and procuring medicines based on complaints and individual suggestions would be an option left for the institutions to run their business.
Nearly all of the key informants thought that overstocking was a significant contributing factor. According to their perception, weakness in applying the drug Supply management (DSM) cycle, and poor participation of clinicians in the process were the main causes of overstocking.
A statement made by one key informant demonstrated this fact as:
I believe the DTC is not carrying out its mandate. Not all concerned bodies are allowed to participate in the DSM process (drug selection, quantification, procurement, distribution, and use). Or the DTC failed to set up a venue where all health units could voice their opinions about the process. Ignorance of the mandate allowed others, particularly those working in finance and medicine inventory control and management, to alter forecasts for the quantification and procurement of drugs. Any attempt that leaves out the real players—drug prescribers, clinical pharmacists, and occasionally pharmacy employees who dispense medicines—lets acquired medicines become overstocked and ultimately wasted.
Electronic medicine stock management systems were not used by more than half of healthcare facilities. Some users who installed it confessed that they weren't getting all the advantages they had hoped for from the system. Each gave comparable explanations for their respective issues. One of the respondents mentioned:
…Power outrage and the absence of a standby power supply in our health facility forbade our craving to possess electronic medicine stock management systems. Our management would not be pleased to spend money on buying either a generator or computers and recruiting more staff.
The other respondent, who had an electronic medicines stock management system gave the following opinion.
We are not able to benefit from the system as we had hoped due to staff shortages, lack of motivation, inconsistent data feeding, corruption during training, incomplete data generation, reliability issues, professionals assigned to the system performing poorly, and interruptions in the electric power supply.

## The impact
The consequences of medicine waste for the delivery of services were discussed with key informants. Key informants cited several impacts, including financial scarcity and a lack of storage space.
According to all major informants’ congested storage and following store management are the main obstacles resulting in wasted medicines. A significant amount of the space at the pharmacy is reportedly taken up by unused medicines, according to the key informants. One respondent provided the following example:
As far as I know, expired and damaged medicines are occupying the space within the store, which causes poor stock management. Therefore, they are causing service interruptions and precipitating public trust loss at our health facility, whatever we do to keep our routine normal.

## Suggestions
The respondents were asked to provide suggestions on how to minimize medicine wastage in public HFs. Providing training on medicine forecasting, availing computers at medical stores with job training, and strengthening IPLS implementations were the suggestions delivered by key informants. Beyond what this report could cover, one key informant stated as.
I believe that if pharmacy workers allocated to stores had IPLS training and also engaged in forecasting, the number of wasted medicines would be reduced. Furthermore, as it is impossible to prevent medicine waste, it is preferable to have contextualized waste disposal and management protocols that eliminate bureaucratic processes and excessive responsibilities that nobody wants to take on. It must be straightforward to use. Ethiopia lacks a medicine industry to ensure the timely delivery of fresh products and to return any that are unused. As consumers, we are overburdened and require a simple way to dispose of our leftovers, and damaged and expired medicines. IPLS might not ensure that medicine wastage is kept to a minimum.

## Discussions
The current study revealed that 5.24% of medicines were wasted, which was higher than the target of < 2% set on HSDP IV [ ]. The result was promising compared with findings conducted in other parts of the country: the national report (8%) and public HFs of the South West Shoa zone (7.5%) [ , ]. This discrepancy could be explained by the former's lack of use of Auditable Medicine Transaction and Services and the latter's inclusion of fewer medical facilities. However, it is somewhat higher than the results of the Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (2.86%), public HFs in Dessie (3.68%), Dire Dawa (3.07%), and teaching hospitals in Addis Ababa (4.8%) [ , , , ]. The variation might be attributed to differences in the numbers and levels of HFs studied, the skills and professional readiness of the participants, and the study subjects’ commitment to inventory management.
The results of the current study demonstrated the enormous cost burden caused by medicines wastage (652,479.45 ETB). It exceeds the total monetary value received by all studied HFs throughout the 2013 Ethiopian Fiscal Year (Table  ). This indicated the financial burden to manage them and refilling their gap to ensure availability of essential medicines would be challenging in these facilities [ ]. However, compared to a study conducted at seven public hospitals in Dire Dawa (11,078,910.52 ETB), this 15 HFs incorporated study found the least loss [ ]. This was because different study HFs were used—only teaching and referral hospitals were included in the Dire Dawa study.
In order to achieve the HSDP-IV goal of reducing medicines wastage due to expiration to 2%, the government has been enforcing HFs to apply and consistently follow regular standards, implementing the APTS, and searching for novel methods and best practices [ , , ]. This study found that, though APTS has not yet been implemented in health centers, the average waste rate in the studied HFs has generally decreased. It showed a decrease from 7.57% in 2011 EBY to 6.44% in 2012 EBY to 3.1% in 2013 EBY. This may be the result of recent improvements in IPLS implementation. Upon examining the data about medicines wastage throughout the three hospitals (51.3%), it appeared that all efforts put forward by the concerned bodies to reduce the rate of waste could not be applied. The finding contradicts the notion that the implementation of APTS dramatically reduces medicine wastage [ ].
According to the current study, anti-infective medicines accounted for a larger proportion (42.4%) of the medicine wastage. This finding was in line with the result from Southwest Shoa, South Gondar Zone, and Dessie of Ethiopia and Tanzania, where anti-infectives were the most frequently prescribed class of medicines in these regions since infectious diseases are the major causes of morbidity and mortality in developing countries [ , , , ]. Erythromycin 500 mg tablet (8.6%), Cloxacillin 500 mg capsule (5.7%), and Cotrimoxazole 960 mg tablet (5.4%) are on the top list of wasted anti-infectives from the two formulations tablets (31.5%) and capsules (23.5%) of the solid dosage form (67%). Erythromycin and Cotrimoxazole are usually obtained through a program called ‘push’ without considering the recipient’s needs. Besides, as a result of the availability of convenient alternatives and perceived resistance issues, their use has been declining. This supply–demand imbalance permitted these medicines to be overstocked and wasted.
Ignorance, despising the work, and losing motivation are training healthcare workers to lose documentation in order to thrive. In the HFs' pharmacy units, where documentation determines the fate of quality service of the whole institution, professionals forbade attention to it. When a report is required, one could need to gather data aggregates and look for a means to make the request meaningful [ ]. In this study, 97.3% of the sources of medicine wastage were related to expiration. This finding was in line with the result from the South Gondar Zone, where expiry (99.3%) was the main source of wastage of medicines [ ]. These wastes are specifically thought to be brought on by obtaining medicines close to their expiration date and stocking them in excess [ ]. According to findings from Ethiopia and Tanzania, overstocking of medicines was a major contributing factor to expiry [ , ]. Overstocking of medicines normally produces a high number of expired medicines and a high cost of storing excess stock. Therefore, it urges a transparent system of proper forecasting and quantification plan using reliable consumption data to get medicines through a practical demand-based approach from suppliers.
The key informants who participated in this study thought the lack of accurate data for quantifications, the presence of overstocked medicines due to improper forecasting, the lack of an electronic stock management system for easy tracking stock data, poor storage practices, and poor inventory control were major causes of medicine wastage. Studies conducted in the South Gondar Zone and South West Shoa Zone, Ethiopia, shared the same thought [ , ]. Other literature also suggest that the main causes of medicine wastage were poor stock control systems, a weak medicine supply management system, and an oversupply of medicines [ , ].
The electronic logistic management information system generates information more accurately and with greater reliability than other systems [ ]. It facilitates the time-consuming task of managing the inventory of medications, saves professionals time, and enhances service quality [ ]. Thus, it's important to ensure that stock recording tools and other electronic instruments are properly implemented and well-established.
As 50% of medicines wastage is preventable, all key informants suggested that having an adequate number of pharmacy professionals, strengthening DTC, conducting regular supervision, having regular discussions on medicines supply management activities with key stakeholders, and improving the communication of the health facility with other HFs and supplier as improvement measures.

## Conclusion
This huge money loss was mainly attributed to expiration. None of the assessed HFs received this amount of money during the three EFYs except one. In terms of reducing medication expiration, the practices currently in place in all assessed HFs were not promising. Unless the government is committed to strengthening the pharmacy unit in the HFs or medication expiration rules have been changed, exaggerated wastage will persist as an integral normal component of inventory management. Further investigations and interventions have to be proposed in a way that compels the government to take action and/or directs stakeholders' focus towards pharmaceutical waste reduction.

## Limitations
The study included wasted medicines with price lists; due to time and budgetary constraints, it was unable to demonstrate the extent of medicine wastage that occurs from medical supplies, lab reagents, programs, and donated medicines. Furthermore, the study's findings were limited to the viewpoint of HFs and excluded other relevant parties including EPSA, the Central Ethiopia Regional State Health Bureau, the Zonal and Woreda health offices, and other collaborators. Additionally, the study did not assess medicine wastage resulting from theft or pilferage in public HFs.
