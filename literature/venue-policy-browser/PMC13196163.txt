## Abstract

## Background
The prevalence of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) varies by region and gender, with the most significant impact observed in low-income countries where STI testing is infrequent, and treatment is primarily symptom-based. Partner notification is a vital strategy for STI control; however, its implementation is hindered by sociocultural, structural, and systemic factors.

## Methods
This study forms part of a sequential exploratory mixed-methods investigation that assessed youth perceptions and social dynamics affect the partner notification strategies to control curable STIs in Maseru, Lesotho. The present article reports findings from the qualitative phase of the study. Data were collected through four focus groups, each comprising six to ten purposively sampled participants aged 18–24 years, and were conducted between June and July 2025. The data were collected in two 24-hour facilities in Maseru.

## Results
Thematic analysis was employed to interpret the data. The study identified four major themes: (1) perceptions on partner notification, (2) social and relational dynamics influencing partner notification, (3) perceived barriers to STI partner notification, and (4) preferred partner notification methods before and during the relationship.

## Conclusion
The youth in Maseru demonstrate an understanding of STI transmission and generally maintain positive attitudes toward partner notification services. However, the process of disclosure is influenced by factors such as stigma, fear of relationship dissolution, anticipated violence, and confidentiality concerns. They also expressed preference for specific methods of partner notification, both before and during relationships.
Key recommendations underscore the importance of prioritizing confidentiality and adopting gender-sensitive approaches within youth-responsive partner notification services in Lesotho. Health systems are advised to incorporate digital platforms and integrate risk screening processes. The establishment of sexuality education and community stigma reduction initiatives is essential for enhancing disclosure among young individuals. Further research into alternative notification strategies is warranted.

## Background
Sexually transmitted infections (STIs), particularly among young individuals, continue to pose a significant global health challenge [ ]. Despite the persistent burden of STIs, the global response remains inadequate [ ]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that 376.4 million new cases of curable STIs occur annually, with the sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) region accounting for approximately 40% of global infections between 2016 and 2021 [ ]. Effective control of STI infections necessitates timely diagnosis, treatment, and partner management [ ].
Partner notification involves informing sexual partners of potential exposure to an STI. In addition to being notified, sexual partners are screened for STIs and treated if necessary [ ]. The advantages of partner notification include identifying undiagnosed STI cases, reducing reinfections in index clients, and preventing further transmission within the community [ ]. Various approaches to partner notification exist, including patient referral, provider referral, and contract referral. Patient referral entails the index client informing their sexual partners of exposure and encouraging them to seek treatment. Provider referral involves a healthcare worker notifying partners of potential exposure and advising them to seek care [ , ]. Contract referral combines these approaches: the index client agrees with the healthcare worker on a timeframe to notify partners, and if the client fails to do so within the agreed time, the healthcare worker informs the partners [ , ]. Although partner notification strategies can be effective, their uptake among youth remains limited due to stigma, fear of disclosure, confidentiality concerns, and the potential for relationship disruption once a partner is informed of an STI [ , ].
This work is part of a project whose purpose is to develop youth-friendly partner notification strategies to control curable STIs in Lesotho. The study is divided into three phases, and this paper explores phase 1. The aim of this work is therefore to assess youths’ perceptions and social dynamics that affect the partner notification strategies to control curable STIs in youth in Lesotho.

## Methods

## Study design
This study represents Phase 1 of a larger sequential exploratory mixed-method study conducted among youth using adolescent-friendly health services, including general health, mental health, sexual and reproductive health, and maternal and child health, in the Maseru district. The larger sequential exploratory mixed method has Phase 1, Phase 2 and Phase 3. The research questions at phase 1 are: what are experiences and practices for youth on partner notification to control curable STIs in Lesotho? What are the perceptions of youth on partner notification strategies to control curable STIs in Lesotho? and What are the social dynamics on partner notification strategies to control curable STIs in Lesotho in youth? For Phase 2, the research questions are: What are the types of curable STI occurring among youth in Lesotho? What are the factors associated with disclosure of curable STIs for partner notification by youth in Lesotho? and Phase 3 covers how could youth-friendly partner notification strategies for curable STIs be developed for Lesotho?. The study is conducted using a phased approach and each phase informs the next. The sequential exploratory design is a research strategy that starts with the collection of qualitative data to explore the phenomenon and followed by the quantitative data collection with intention to test the findings from the previous phase in a general context [ ]. The results of the first two phases (phase 1 and phase 2) will be integrated to develop youth-friendly strategies for partner notification to cure STIs in Lesotho [ ].
An exploratory qualitative design was employed, as this approach is suitable when there is limited knowledge about a phenomenon or when a topic has not been sufficiently defined [ ].

## Study setting and context
The study was conducted in two 24-hour healthcare facilities within the Maseru district. Maseru, the capital city of Lesotho, is one of the ten districts in the country, encompassing urban, rural, and peri-urban areas, including the central business district, residential areas, and informal settlements. The district has a population of 519,186 [ ]. Both facilities operate adolescent corners that provide a range of youth-friendly health services.

## Study population, sampling, and recruitment
This paper exclusively presents the qualitative findings from Phase 1, which aimed to assess the perceptions and social dynamics of youth on partner notification strategies to control curable STIs in Lesotho using four focus group discussions (FGDs). Each FGD consisted of 6–10 participants. For this paper, the study population included youth from mixed, males-only, females-only, and key population groups. The FGDs were composed as follows:

Mixed youth group (males and females)
Males-only group
Females-only group
Key population group (males who have sex with males, sex workers, and key informants)

Purposive sampling was employed to recruit youth for this study. The researcher used this method to gain detailed youths perceptions and social dynamics that affect partner notification strategies to control curable STIs in Lesotho.
Recruitment was conducted in two ways. Firstly, the principal investigator introduced the study during a health talk session using a recruitment flyer outlining the study objectives and purpose. Interested participants were invited to a private room for the focus group discussion. Secondly, organizations providing health services to key populations assisted in identifying eligible participants, as this group was not easily accessible through public health facilities. Initially the researcher had a meeting with the organizations providing services for key populations to discuss the study, explain the study objectives, and inclusion criteria. Following the meeting, the key population peer staff assisted in recruitment of participants. The key population members who were willing to participate in the study were invited for focus group discussion. The female sex workers and men who have sex with men were mixed in the focus group to facilitate exploration of perceptions and social dynamics within the marginalized sexual health context rather than identity based. A convenient time and venue were agreed upon for the key population discussion.

## Inclusion criteria
Participants were recruited based on the predetermined eligibility criteria: youth aged 18–24 years residing in the Maseru district, who were sexually active and ever been diagnosed with curable STI, had used services at the selected facilities, and were literate. The literacy of participants enabled the participants to be able read the study related information and consent materials. The age of consenting for healthcare services in Lesotho is 18 years of age [ ]. This age group was selected due to the relationship between youth sexuality and vulnerability [ ].

## Data collection
The data collection team was composed of two members (researcher and the research assistant). The researcher was facilitator of the focus group discussion whilst the research assistant was responsible for logistical support. Prior to data collection, a training session was conducted for the research assistant to ensure a comprehensive understanding of the study objectives and data collection instruments. Data was collected from June to July 2025. A semi-structured focus group discussion guide was employed, incorporating primary and probing questions tailored to participant responses. The focus group discussions were conducted in Sesotho. The core questions addressed the following areas: perceptions on STI partner notification in Lesotho and social dynamics on partner notification in Lesotho. Under each of the core questions, there were probing questions which included: awareness of partner notification services, perceived benefits of partner notification, observations of STI management among couples, and how adolescents handle STI issues in relationships. The probing questions were asked after the participants had discussed the core questions.
Demographic information was gathered using a consent form. Both written and verbal informed consent were obtained before participation and recording of discussion. Each focus group discussion lasted approximately 60–90 min and was audio-recorded. The recordings were professionally transcribed verbatim and translated from Sesotho into English.

## Data analysis
Data were analyzed using thematic content analysis, following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework [ ]. The researcher familiarized themselves with the data by listening to audio recordings while reading the transcripts. Initial codes were independently generated on the basis of the research questions, and a manual codebook was developed based on the focus group discussions, which was refined as the analysis progressed. The combined induction and deductive approach to coding starting with broad codes from focus group guides was used, ensuring allowance of new codes to emerge. The initial coding framework was reviewed by a supervisor, who served as a peer reviewer to enhance analytical rigor. Transcripts were imported into QSR NVivo version 15.3.0 [ ] to facilitate coding and thematic organization. Using the code book, the researcher continued to code on NVivo and shared the NVivo project for supervisor review ensuring that codes are applied on the transcripts. Codes were categorized into broader themes and subthemes through inputs of one of the supervisors which resulted into thematic maps. Demographic data were summarized using Microsoft Excel. Conclusions were drawn directly from participant quotations without altering their meaning.

## Trustworthiness
Trustworthiness was ensured by enhancing credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability, as recommended by Korstjens and Moser [ ]. Credibility was bolstered through the training of the research assistant.
Debriefing sessions were conducted between principal investigator and supervisors following each focus group discussion to refine the discussion guide and deepen inquiry in subsequent sessions.
Dependability was supported through peer review of coding and theme development. Confirmability was ensured through documentation of the analytical process and secure storage of signed consent forms.
Transferability was enhanced by purposive sampling across diverse youth groups within the Maseru district.

## Ethical considerations
Ethical approval was obtained from the Sefako Makgatho Health Sciences University Ethics Committee (Protocol No. SMUREC/H/32/2025) and the Lesotho Ethics Committee (No. ID177-2025). Permission was also secured from the Maseru District Health Management Team, the Christian Health Association of Lesotho, and facility management.
Participants were informed that participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time without consequences for accessing services. Pseudonyms were used during discussions, and real names were not recorded.
In accordance with South Africa’s Protection of Personal Information Act [ , ], demographic data collection was limited, and all data was securely stored in a locked cabinet accessible only to the research team. Data will be stored for five years.

## Results
Four focus group discussions were conducted with 31 youth aged 18–24 years. The discussions were conducted using distinct groups: mixed youth, females only, males only, and key population groups. The thematic analysis generated four major themes:
1) perceptions on partner notification, 2) social and relational dynamics influencing partner notification, 3) perceived barriers to STI partner notification, and 4) preferred partner notification methods before and during the relationship.
Themes and subthemes

## Theme 1: perceptions on partner notification
Participants exhibited diverse understanding of partner notification. Some perceived it as a responsible health practice, while others associated it with fear, stigma, and relationship conflict.

## Sub-theme 1: attitudes toward disclosure
Participants showed diverse attitudes toward partner notification. Some individuals perceived disclosure as a responsible course of action. Conversely, others indicated that their decision to notify would depend on specific circumstances, such as the seriousness of the relationship and the potential reaction of the partner. A minority of participants expressed reluctance to inform partners due to anticipated negative repercussions. Overall, perceptions ranged from supportive and proactive to conditional or avoidant.

I think it just depends on what kind of person your partner is, some people are calm, and others jump to conclusions. If you know that they will understand and listen to you, and they won’t judge you, then yes, I will tell them. (Participant 06, Aged 20, Women’s group)
If it were a marriage, maybe, but in casual relationships, I do not think it is worth it. (Participant 02, Aged 19, Women’s group)

## Theme 2: social and relational dynamics influencing partner notification
Partner notification decisions were strongly influenced by the context of the relationship, emotional considerations, and perceived social obligations.

## Sub-theme 1: moral and social responsibility
Multiple participants regarded partner notification as a moral obligation, one that is important in protecting the partner’s health and preventing further transmission. Disclosure was therefore viewed as being responsible and caring in relationships.

I will have to take responsibility that I am the one who made a mistake here, and, therefore, accept any decision that they make whether it is to stay or to leave. At the end of the day, they will at least see a doctor. (Participant 07, Aged 23, Key population group)

## Sub- theme 2: relationship preservation and emotional considerations
Some participants showed concern regarding notifying partners and what implication that might have on the relationship. Others indicated that notifying their partner could strengthen trust in the relationship if handled appropriately, while others feared that the disclosure could threaten the relationship. Feelings of guilt were also cited as motivators for disclosure, especially where individuals believed they may have exposed their partners to infection. Conversely, mistrust and anticipation of blame were acknowledged as some of the things that complicate disclosure, especially in relationships where there are persisting power dynamics and existing conflict.

I think that if they did tell me like that to me it would mean that this person knows and has gone to the doctor to actually find out. So, because of the fact that they took action, I too must take action. What this will also show me is that my person is responsible. If they did something like that, it also means they are trustworthy or at least are taking accountability for their actions. And because they will have gotten the STI from elsewhere and from me, it will also give me the impression that they are choosing to correct their behavior. (Participant 06, Aged 24, Key population group)
If I suggest using protection, they might say, do you not trust me? And since I want to win them over and have them as mine, I will just go with the flow. Even if it means getting sick. (Participant 08, Aged 22, Key population group)

## Theme 3: perceived barriers to STI partner notification
Although participants recognized the importance of disclosure, they still identified several barriers that could prevent partner notification.

## Sub theme 1: stigma and gossip
The fear of being stigmatised emerged as a notable concern. Some health facilities are church-based and seem to pass judgment on STI infections. Participants described unease about peer judgment or about being the subject of gossip within their communities. Others, especially women, expressed their fear of being blamed by their partners, even when they know for a fact that they were never unfaithful. Some viewed social disclosure as damaging to personal reputation.

One begins to blame the other, and I know that, often, men are the ones who will be blaming the women. (Participant 06, Aged 24, Key population group)
You go to the clinic, and then they start gossiping about you. That kind of fear keeps a lot of people from coming forward, so I think, even in casual sex situations, the best thing we can do is just go to the clinic, but even that is not easy for everyone. There is still stigma on both sides. (Participant 08, Aged 22, Key population group)

## Sub theme 2: fear of conflict and relationship dissolution
Numerous participants anticipated negative reactions from their partners upon notification, including anger, blame, or accusations of infidelity. Disclosure was perceived as a gateway to conflict in the relationships, with some partners resorting to violence, and others ending the relationship.

I don’t think it’s worth it, because it could lead to conflict and not getting along because they know they are clean, and now I am telling them I am infected. It will just cause tension at home. (Participants 03, Aged 20, Women’s group)
They might even beat you and say you gave them the infection even though you know they did not have it. (Participant 03, Aged 20, Women’s group)

## Sub theme 3: fear of public exposure
Some participants showed concern that their partners might expose that they are infected on social media platforms, particularly Facebook. This exposure could intensify stigma and embarrassment. The possibility of public exposure amplified the reluctance to disclose to respective partners.

As young people, on social media, we insult each other a lot. When you walk past a group of guys, they will start pointing fingers at you. Even if a guy tries to date you and reject him, he will say “you are the one who gave that one an STI.” (Participant 06, Aged 20, Women’s group)

## Theme 4: preferred partner notification methods before and during the relationship
Participants exhibited varied preferences regarding the method of partner notification. They indicated that the approach to notification should depend on the stage and nature of the relationship.

## Sub-theme 1: direct disclosure
The majority of participants in stable and long-term relationships expressed a preference for direct communication with their partners. Face-to-face disclosure was favoured as it demonstrated respect and responsibility.

I think it is important to be able to discuss anything and everything with our partners and, therefore, it would be important to tell them ourselves. (Participant 04, Aged 24, Mixed youth group)

## Sub theme 2: healthcare worker-assisted notification
Some participants expressed a preference for having a healthcare professional facilitate the disclosure process, as this approach mitigated confrontation. Assisted notification was deemed optimal for alleviating personal burden and ensuring the accurate communication of information.
It is easier to disclose if there is something wrong. Even if the case is that I am scared to tell them that I have a problem, once we get to the clinic, we will meet nurses, counselors, doctors, who will help to explain and break it to them. (Participant 03 Aged 24, Key population group)

## Sub theme 3: facility-based notification
The notification process within a healthcare facility was also regarded as an optimal support mechanism, particularly for key populations. This approach was viewed as offering a professional environment for notified partners.

I think that it would be important if the health professional who is providing services for me could write in my health booklet that my partner should come to the hospital, because if I told him myself, there is a good chance that he will not come. (Participant 09, Aged 24, Mixed youth group)

## Discussion
This study investigated the perceptions and social dynamics influencing partner notification services for STIs in Maseru, Lesotho. Participants expressed a range of supportive views toward partner notification, with their decisions primarily influenced by factors such as relationship context, emotional considerations, and structural barriers.
As highlighted in Theme 1 (perceptions on partner notification), most participants regarded partner notification as a responsible and ethical approach, particularly within committed relationships. This finding is consistent with a study conducted in Durban, South Africa, where partner notification cards achieved a notification rate of 74% [ ]. Additionally, research involving South African students and taxi drivers indicated that over 70% of participants were willing to disclose their STI status to their partners [ , ]. However, two studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa reported partner notification uptake rates of 5.7% and 35%, with variations in notification rates of 41% and 74%, respectively [ , ].
In accordance with Theme 2 (social and relational dynamics influencing partner notification), the nature of relationships significantly impacted the decision to disclose STI status among the youth. Partner notification was deemed more appropriate within committed or stable relationships, whereas disclosure in casual relationships was deemed unlikely. These findings are consistent with literature indicating that partner notification is more successful within committed relationships, with lower disclosure rates in casual, concurrent, or short-term relationships [ , , , ]. Similarly, a case-control study in Ethiopia found that patients often did not notify their casual partners due to a lack of contact and inability to trace them [ ]. Participants in this study also recognized disclosure as part of their moral and social obligations, particularly when trust and mutual care were present. However, some emotional considerations complicated STI status disclosure, including fear of blame, mistrust, and existing power dynamics, demonstrating that partner notification is embedded within relational dynamics rather than being a purely clinical action.
Despite generally favorable attitudes towards partner notification, Theme 3 (Perceived Barriers to STI Partner Notification) revealed significant challenges to its implementation. Participants consistently identified fear of stigma, conflict, gossip, and relationship dissolution as major impediments. Concerns about violence, particularly against women, and public exposure, especially through social media, further discouraged disclosure. These barriers align with findings reported in the literature [ , , , ], underscoring the reality that social consequences often outweigh health benefits in youth decision-making, particularly in this context. Another distinct factor is the fear of blame, especially directed towards women, which reflects gender-based power dynamics that influence disclosure experiences.
Regarding Theme 4 (Preferred Partner Notification Methods Before and During the Relationship), participants reported varied preferences. The majority favored direct, face-to-face disclosure, particularly in committed relationships, while some preferred healthcare worker-assisted partner notification to reduce conflict and ensure accurate information is communicated. Facility-based notification was regarded as supportive and protective, mainly for key populations. These findings reflect the reliance of patients on patient-initiated and health provider-assisted approaches, which appear to be the principal methods available in Lesotho. Conversely, the literature describes additional strategies such as technology-based partner notification, expedited partner notification services, and referral slips, which are not accessible in the context of this study for curable STI [ , , ]. This limited range of available partner notification strategies may restrict the effectiveness of youth engagement in partner notification.
Overall, this study highlights that the youth of Maseru, Lesotho, recognize the importance of partner notification. However, the success of its implementation depends on various factors, primarily stability within relationships, adequate knowledge, emotional safety, and access to supportive partner notification approaches. By strengthening youth-friendly, context-sensitive partner notification services in Lesotho, the appreciation of these services could contribute to improved STI control outcomes in the region.
Key recommendations underscore the necessity of prioritizing confidentiality, mitigating stigma, and implementing gender-sensitive approaches within youth-responsive partner notification services in Lesotho. Health systems should incorporate digital platforms, integrate risk screening for gender-based violence, and expand youth-specific modalities. The establishment of initiatives focused on sexuality education and community stigma reduction is crucial for enhancing disclosure and improving overall STI control among young people in Lesotho. Therefore, it is warranted to support future research on the feasibility and effectiveness of alternative notification strategies.

## Strengths
The study has several notable strengths. Foremost, it is among the limited number of qualitative investigations examining youth and sexually transmitted infections in sub-Saharan Africa, with a particular focus on Lesotho. The use of purposive sampling facilitated the inclusion of diverse youth perspectives, thereby contributing to the generation of evidence that could inform the development of youth-friendly services. Thematic analysis, conducted in accordance with Braun and Clarke’s framework, alongside peer review by a member of the supervisory team, enhanced the credibility of the findings, thereby contributing to public health programs and policy formulation.

## Limitations
The study was conducted in two health facilities, which limits the generalizability of the results. Additionally, the findings were derived from self-reports by participants, potentially subject to social desirability bias. Literacy formed part of eligibility criteria and this may have introduced selection bias leading to exclusion of the illiterate youth.

## Conclusion
The study indicates that although young individuals express support for partner notification concerning sexually transmitted infections, the process of disclosure remains influenced by various factors. It is, therefore, imperative to thoroughly comprehend the barriers affecting partner notification among youth and to use this understanding to inform the development of tailored services for this demographic. The provision of comprehensive sexuality education, non-judgmental and inclusive health services, and the integration of partner notification for diverse youth populations are essential for delivering youth-friendly services. Further research is necessary to investigate the experiences of youth during the implementation of partner notification strategies beyond patient and provider notification in Lesotho.
