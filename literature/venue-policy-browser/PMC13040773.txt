## Background
As sports socializing is becoming a dominant lifestyle that integrates physical health with social interaction in China, understanding the underlying drivers of participation is crucial. However, traditional research predominantly relies on a “variable-centered” paradigm, which assumes population homogeneity and focuses on linear relationships between single motives and behaviors. This approach often fails to capture the complexity of how multiple motivations are configured within individuals (heterogeneity), and how these internal configurations are associated with external behavioral choices.

## Methods
To address this gap, this study employed a novel hybrid methodological framework combining Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) and Random Forest (RF) modeling. Based on data from 1,104 adults, LPA was first used to identify distinct motivational subgroups. Subsequently, RF algorithms, utilizing feature importance ranking and “One-vs-Rest” strategies, were applied to identify the associative patterns between these motivational profiles and key behavioral indicators, including sports types, media usage, and economic investment.

## Results
The analysis identified four distinct motivational profiles: (1) Psychologically Introverted (3.6%), prioritizing internal psychological rewards over social status; (2) Physiologically Oriented (44.1%), the largest group, driven primarily by physical health needs; (3) Balanced (39.0%), exhibiting moderate levels across all motivational dimensions; and (4) High-Motivation/Comprehensively Oriented (13.3%), showing high intensity in both internal and external rewards. The RF model achieved a training accuracy of 99.9% and identified that Sports Type (specifically large-ball games), Media Channels (particularly Douyin/Rednote), and Annual Spending were the top three salient behavioral markers distinguishing these profiles. Notably, the High-Motivation group was characterized by heavy reliance on visual social media for social display.

## Conclusion
Participation in sports socializing among Chinese residents is not characterized by a singular, homogeneous motivation but features a clear internal stratification structure. The specific pattern of motivational combinations (i.e., the type) systematically maps onto external behavioral choices, where the sociocultural attributes of the sport and the media characteristics of digital social platforms constitute the key predictive markers of behavioral differentiation. The establishment of this “Motivation Type—Behavioral Signal” integrated framework promotes a theoretical shift in the sports socializing research paradigm from “homogeneity” to “heterogeneity” and deepens the understanding of the complex manifestations of Self-Determination Theory and Social Capital Theory in a sports context. It also provides precise user profiles and behavioral insights for sports social platforms, commercial clubs, and public sports service departments. Exploring service customization and policy adjustments based on different motivation-behavior patterns could potentially enhance user engagement and satisfaction, suggesting a possible direction for the development of the sports socializing industry.

## Introduction
Sports socializing is a social activity that uses sports activities as a vehicle to engage in interpersonal communication through participation or spectating, with the aim of obtaining corresponding emotional or instrumental returns within social networks, thereby accumulating, utilizing, and maintaining social resources. The author has previously conducted an in-depth analysis of sports socializing among Chinese residents from two key perspectives. First, a study based on the variable-centered perspective systematically revealed the inherent causal pathway and boundary conditions of “social environment to practice methods to need satisfaction,” clarifying the linear relationships and moderating patterns between various elements. Subsequently, another study, starting from a group comparison perspective, mapped the differential landscape of sports socializing based on external demographic characteristics such as gender and age.
However, both these studies implicitly share a common methodological premise: that the groups being discussed are internally homogeneous and the relationships between variables are essentially linear. This assumption may overlook a core reality: Individuals’ motivations for participating in sports socializing are complex, multidimensional, and manifest in various combinations of patterns. For example, some participants may simultaneously pursue health maintenance and emotional connection, whereas others may focus more on the accumulation of social capital and display of status. Therefore, how this individual-specific, heterogeneous configuration of motives is associated with overt sports socializing behavioral choices constitutes a critical gap that current research has not yet fully explored.
From a broader public health perspective, physical inactivity has become an independent risk factor threatening global population health. In response to this challenge, the Chinese government has strategically implemented the “Healthy China 2030” Planning Outline, aiming to transition from illness treatment to health management and to elevate population-level physical activity through extensive social mobilization [ ]. However, despite the increasing supply of public sports infrastructure, a significant “intention–behavior gap” persists among residents. Public health policymakers are increasingly recognizing that traditional “one-size-fits-all” health promotion strategies are often not adhered to in the long-term, because they overlook the heterogeneity of individual regulatory processes [ ]. Therefore, identifying distinct motivational profiles is not merely a psychological inquiry but a critical step toward shifting public health interventions from “broad-spectrum” to “precision-targeted” approaches, thereby optimizing resource allocation and enhancing the effectiveness of national fitness programs.
Theoretically, this heterogeneity stems from the synergistic effects of sports socializing motivations, encompassing both the intrinsic psychological needs posited by Self-Determination Theory [ ] and the instrumental pursuit of social resources highlighted by Social Capital Theory [ ]. Although these theories identify key dimensions such as autonomy and social bonding, traditional empirical research has predominantly adhered to a “variable-centered” paradigm [ ]. Although effective in isolating linear relationships, this framework implicitly assumes population homogeneity. It fails to capture the “configurational” nature of human psychology, specifically, how high levels of one motive (e.g., health) might combine with low levels of another (e.g., social status) within individuals to form qualitatively distinct subgroups [ ].
To address this limitation, health psychology research has shifted toward a “person-centered” perspective, utilizing Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) to uncover hidden typologies. For instance, studies on student populations have successfully identified distinct profiles such as “intrinsic immersion” versus “extrinsically driven.” However, little is known about the prevailing motivational phenotypes in sports socializing among Chinese residents, which is a unique context that integrates physical exertion with complex social networking. Furthermore, identifying motivational types alone is insufficient for practical interventions; a critical gap remains in decoding the mapping mechanism between internal types and external behavioral signals. Given the complex nonlinear interactions among lifestyle variables, traditional regression is often inadequate. This necessitates the introduction of machine learning approaches, such as Random Forest (RF), to effectively pinpoint which behavioral cues (e.g., specific sports preferences or media channels) serve as critical salient behavioral markers for distinguishing between motivational subgroups.
This study bridges these gaps by proposing a hybrid methodological framework. It employs LPA to answer “who they are” by identifying latent motivational profiles, and utilizes RF to answer “how do they behave” by revealing the nonlinear decision patterns linked to these profiles. Specifically, this study aims to address two core research questions: [ ] What representational latent profiles of sports socializing motivations exist among Chinese residents [ ]? Which behavioral characteristics (e.g., sport preference, media channels, economic investment) serve as the most critical predictors for distinguishing these participation patterns? The exploration of this series of questions, at the theoretical level, can promote a shift in the research paradigm from “variable-centered” to “person-centered,” enriching and deepening the explanatory framework for sports socializing behavior by focusing on the associative role of the overall pattern of motivation rather than a single dimension. At the practical level, the research findings may offer preliminary user motivation classifications for stakeholders, providing possible insights for exploring service optimization from a broad-spectrum to targeted approaches.

## Methods

## Latent profile analysis
The core function of LPA is to identify potential, qualitatively distinct subgroups within a sample. This is based on the idea that a seemingly unified population is composed of multiple subtypes with essential differences. By analyzing individuals’ overall response patterns across multiple observed variables (e.g., questionnaire scores or behavioral indicators), this method can bring forth these hidden categories, each possessing distinct intrinsic traits, thereby shifting the research perspective from “average trends” to “group heterogeneity.”
Ultimately, the analysis generates several distinct and non-overlapping qualitative profiles, with each profile representing a unique type of subgroup. For instance, it might reveal essentially different patterns of burnout-engagement among employees, such as the “Vibrant Dedication Type,” the “Over-Committed Type,” and the “Detached Exhaustion Type.” The successful identification of these latent subgroups provides crucial empirical evidence for understanding the diversity underlying complex phenomena and implementing precise interventions tailored to different types [ , ].

## Random forest model
The RF Model is an ensemble machine-learning algorithm centered on predictive accuracy, stability, and variable importance. Its core idea is similar to the wisdom of collective decision-making: By constructing and aggregating a large number of small decision trees, and allowing them to vote together or take an average, a powerful and robust prediction system is achieved that is far superior to any single model. It is utilized as an exploratory, association-oriented tool rather than a causal inference mechanism.While it is known for predictive stability, its primary value in this study lies in variable importance assessment, which allows for the objective identification of behavioral features that most strongly correlate with specific motivational profiles. This approach prioritizes feature discovery and pattern exploration over high-precision clinical prediction.In this process, it objectively evaluates the contribution of each variable to the prediction result, which is known as variable importance.
RF’s operational process fully reflects its “forest” characteristic. First, it uses the bootstrap sampling method to generate multiple different training subsets from the original data and builds a decision tree for each subset. Crucially, when constructing each node of every tree, the model does not select the best splitting point from all variables but rather from a randomly selected subset of variables. This dual randomness mechanism—random sampling of data rows, and random selection of variables—ensures that every tree in the forest is as unique as possible, thus effectively reducing the overall variance of the model and the risk of overfitting. Finally, for classification problems, the forest produces the final result through “voting” (majority rule); for regression problems, it outputs the final prediction by averaging the predictions of all trees.
The value of this model lies not only in its outstanding predictive performance but also in its powerful interpretive tool: variable importance assessment. It can quantify the contribution of each predictor variable to improve the model’s predictive accuracy, thereby allowing researchers to identify the key factors driving the prediction results. For example, in the medical field, when characterizing disease risk, it can tell doctors which physiological indicators are the strongest predictors of that risk; in financial risk control, it can identify which customer behavioral characteristics are most likely to be associated with fraudulent behavior. Therefore, RF is not just a “black box” prediction tool; it is also a powerful analytical method that helps researchers understand the critical drivers within complex variable relationships [ , ].

## Data source and variable selection

## Data source
To explore motivation stratification and behavioral decision-making in sports socializing among Chinese residents, this study designed a detailed questionnaire covering various aspects of social behavior and sociodemographic characteristics (Supplementary Material-Questionnaire). The questionnaires were distributed using both online and offline methods, with the primary target group being adults across different regions of China. A cross-sectional survey design was adopted. The data were collected between March and May, 2024.
To ensure relatively robust research findings, the sample size was required to be 5–10 times the number of valid items. Apart from the screening questions, this study contained 66 questions. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were whether potential participants regularly participated in sports activities. Through recruiting volunteers online and random distribution in offline shopping malls, 1,380 questionnaires were distributed, of which 1,220 were recovered (recovery rate of 88%), and 1,104 were valid (validity rate of 90%), which meets the requirements.The sample distribution across regions and the specific quantities are illustrated in the below (Table  ).

 

This questionnaire combined general questions and a measurement scale. As the reliability and subsequent validity tests apply only to the scale portion, these tests refer only to questions 9–67 (excluding the 28th screening question), totaling 58 items. This study analyzed reliability using Cronbach’s internal consistency coefficient, which represents the average of the split-half reliability coefficients obtained from all possible ways of dividing the scale items. The resulting coefficient was 0.912, indicating excellent reliability.
The results of a survey are influenced by the participants’ emotions and the external environment at the time of answering. The test-retest method was adopted to reassess the participants’ answers from the first survey, and an analysis of variance was performed to determine the homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test), thereby exploring whether the obtained survey results are representative.
Of the 1,104 valid recovered questionnaires, 110 were selected for the second test. A follow-up survey using the same questionnaire was conducted 30 days after the first test. The resulting homogeneity of variance was 0.399, with a significance probability of p = 0.672 > 0.05. This indicates that the variances were homogeneous, allowing the researchers to conclude that the participants’ answers were stable and the findings of the questionnaire were valid.
Furthermore, construct validity is one of the most important validity indicators and can be examined using factor analysis. Factor analysis was employed to distill the fundamental constructs from the data, which were then used to analyze the construct validity of the measurement. Specifically, KMO and Bartlett’s tests of sphericity were performed on the data. The test results showed that the KMO measure for the questionnaire was R = 0.896, with a significance of < 0.05. This indicates that the data were correlated and suitable for factor analysis, confirming the validity of the questionnaire(Table  ).

 

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuhan Sports University (Approval No. 2024023). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before their inclusion in the study. The participants were fully informed about the study’s purpose and their right to withdraw at any time, and were assured of anonymity and data confidentiality.

## Variable selection
First, the core motivational variables form the basis for the LPA, aiming to capture the intrinsic drivers for an individual’s participation in sports socializing. This study selected eight items (Q52 to Q59), which collectively constitute a multidimensional motivational measurement system. Specifically, these items measure the following needs: Q52 (Physiological Satisfaction) focuses on improving physical health and fitness through sports socializing; Q53 (Psychological Satisfaction) looks at its utility for stress release and mood improvement; and Q54 (Enhancing Well-being) measures its role in boosting overall life satisfaction and positive emotions. Concurrently, the questionnaire also covers the measurement of social needs, where Q55 (Seeking Affiliation) reflects an individual’s need for a sense of belonging, desire to integrate into a group, and avoidance of loneliness. Furthermore, Q56 (Seeking Economic/Political Status Improvement), Q57 (Seeking Prestige Enhancement), and Q58 (Displaying Social Status) collectively point to instrumental motives, that is, the individual’s hope of accumulating social capital, acquiring resources, and enhancing personal prestige through sports socializing. Additionally, Q59 (Other Needs) serves as an open option for capturing personalized motives not covered by the above categories. Together, these eight items delineate the complex motivational landscape of individual participation in sports socializing.Additionally, Q59 (Other Needs) was included as an open-ended Likert item to capture residual or individualized motives that may not be fully represented by the seven predefined categories. By accounting for these idiosyncratic drivers, the measurement system ensures greater content validity and provides a more comprehensive landscape of the diverse motivations underlying sports socializing.
Second, behavioral manifestation variables are used to characterize an individual’s actual sports socializing practices and serve as predictor variables in the decision-tree model. This set of variables can be subdivided into three dimensions:
Participation Intensity: This consists of Q21 (Years of Sports Socializing) and Q22 (Frequency of Sports Socializing per week), used to measure the persistence and frequency of participants’ involvement.
Mode Preference: This is a comprehensive dimension, where Q23 (Offline Physical Activity Participation) and Q24 (Online Physical Activity Participation) differentiate between the physical and virtual settings of participatory socializing, Q25 (Offline Spectating) and Q26 (Online Spectating) distinguish the different forms of spectating-based socializing, and Q27 (Participation in at least two of the above) identifies individuals who favor mixed-mode participation. Regarding preference for sport types, Q29 (Major Ball Games), Q30 (Minor Ball Games), Q31 (Cycling/Running), Q32 (Leisure Sports), and Q33 (Other Sports) reveal the role of different sports as vehicles for socializing.
Platform and Expenditure: This includes Q36 (Communication Software such as WeChat), Q37 (Social Software such as TikTok/Rednote), Q38 (Commercial Software such as Tencent/Migu), and Q39 (Mainstream Software such as CCTV Video), which measure the media channels relied upon for arranging and spectating activities. Finally, Q40 (Total Annual Expenditure) and Q41 (Single Expenditure Amount) quantify the level of resource commitment to participation behavior from an economic investment perspective.

## Results

## Latent profile analysis: identifying motivational types
To identify potential typological differences in individuals’ sports socializing motivation, this study used the eight motivational items from the questionnaire (Q52–Q59) as observed indicators and employed LPA to cluster and identify the motivational structures of the subjects.
The specific content of the items is as follows:
Q52: Your sports socializing with others is for seeking physiological satisfaction.
Q53: Your sports socializing with others is for seeking psychological satisfaction.
Q54: Your sports socializing with others is for seeking enhanced well-being.
Q55: Your sports socializing with others is for seeking affiliation/group integration.
Q56: Your sports socializing with others is for seeking an improvement in economic level or political status.
Q57: Your sports socializing with others is for seeking an enhancement of prestige.
Q58: Your sports socializing with others is for seeking to display a unique social status.
Q59: Your sports socializing with others is for seeking to fulfill other needs not mentioned above.

Model specification and fitting process

Before formal analysis, eight motivational items from the 1,104 valid questionnaires were processed for missing values and tested for normal distribution. The results indicated that all items used a 5-point Likert scale, and the data distribution was suitable for continuous latent variable modeling. The LPA method, based on the Gaussian Mixture Model, was adopted to sequentially fit models with one to five classes, using Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) to solve the parameters.It should be noted that Q59 was treated as a continuous indicator consistent with the other seven motivational items, as its 5-point Likert scale distribution allowed for integration into the Gaussian Mixture Model without disrupting the mathematical assumptions of the LPA. This ensures that any idiosyncratic motivational variance is reflected in the final latent profiles.
The criteria for model selection included (Table  ):
Information Criteria: AIC, BIC, and aBIC (smaller values indicate a better fit).
Classification Accuracy Index: Entropy (a value > 0.8 indicates accurate classification).
Class Proportion Test: The proportion of samples in each class should not be less than 5%.
Data analysis was performed in the R programming environment, supplemented with Python’s Gaussian Mixture for model comparison and verification.

 

(2)Model selection and classification results

The selection of the four-class model was guided by the principle of parsimony, in which statistical evidence converged to indicate K = 4 as the optimal and sufficient solution. The decisive criterion was the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), reaching its global minimum at K = 4, providing the strongest signal for an optimal balance between model fit and complexity. This conclusion was fortified by likelihood ratio tests; although the tests supported adding a fourth class over a three-class model (p < 0.001), they indicated no significant improvement to a five-class model (p > 0.05). Furthermore, the classification accuracy, measured by entropy, was near-perfect (0.980) for the four-class model but showed no gain with additional classes. Together, these metrics provided a clear statistical rationale for stopping at four classes, as further increases risked overfitting without substantive benefits.
Beyond statistics, the four-class solution yielded the most theoretically coherent and behaviorally meaningful typology. Each of the four profiles—Niche/Special Preference, Physiology-Oriented, Balanced, and High Motivation/Comprehensively Oriented—represented a distinct, interpretable configuration of motivational drivers. The validity of this typology was subsequently confirmed, as the RF analysis demonstrated that these four groups exhibited systematically different patterns in key behavioral variables such as sports preference and platform use.
The decision to retain the smallest class, despite its size being slightly below the conventional thresholds, was based on methodological rigor and theoretical value. This group’s unique motivational signature—high in introspective well-being and low in extrinsic status pursuit—constituted a qualitatively distinct subtype that would be obscured if merged with others. The methodological literature supports retaining small but well-differentiated and interpretable classes when they are reliably identified (as indicated by high entropy) and contribute meaningfully to understanding population heterogeneity. Consequently, this study determined the number of latent profiles for sports socializing motivation to be four (K = 4).

## Random forest analysis: identifying salient behavioral markers of motivational profiles
To systematically identify the differentiated markers of distinct motivational groups, this study employed an RF model to rank feature importance. These variables represent associative markers that distinguish how different motivational types manifest in outward behavior, rather than serving as definitive causal predictors.
Integration of LPA and RF: To bridge the gap between internal psychological drivers and external behavioral choices, this study established a strictly coupled analytical framework. Specifically, the distinct latent class membership (Categories 1–4) assigned to each individual by LPA served as the labeled target variable (Dependent Variable) for subsequent supervised learning. Meanwhile, 15 behavioral indicators (e.g., sports type and media usage) served as input features (Independent Variables). The RF algorithm was then tasked with “ exploratory association analysis the classification rules, thereby identifying the behavioral signals that most accurately predicted an individual’s underlying motivational profile(Fig.  ).

 

To systematically identify the differentiated representations of distinct motivational profile groups in specific sports socializing behaviors and identify the key behavioral variables that can effectively distinguish these motivational types, this study further employed the RF model for machine learning analysis. As an ensemble learning method, the RF classifies samples by constructing a large number of decision trees and basing the final classification on a “collective voting” mechanism. Its core advantages are its ability to handle high-dimensional data effectively, avoid overfitting, and provide a robust ranking of feature importance. This ranking quantifies the predictive importance of each behavioral variable for classifying individuals into different motivational types. These variables represent key distinguishing features associated with the motivation–behavior link.

Model specification and variable processing

Variable Definitions: The dependent variable for the predictive model was the latent profile membership identified in the LPA stage, categorized as: 1 (Niche/Special Preference), 2 (Physiology-Oriented), 3 (Balanced), and 4 (High Motivation). The independent variables (predictors) consisted of 15 behavioral indicators spanning three dimensions: Participation Intensity (e.g., Q21, Q22), Mode Preference (e.g., Q23, Q29, Q30), and Platform/Expenditure (e.g., Q37, Q40).
Algorithm Implementation and Parameters The RF classifier was implemented using the scikit-learn library in Python. To ensure model robustness and control for overfitting, the following key hyperparameters were employed:
Number of Trees (n_estimators): Set to 100 to stabilize error rates.
Splitting Criterion: Gini Impurity was used to measure the quality of a split.
Maximum Features (max_features): Set to “sqrt” (square root of total features) to ensure diversity among trees.
Class Weight: Set to “balanced” to automatically adjust weights inversely proportional to class frequencies, addressing the imbalance issue.
Bootstrap Strategy: Standard bootstrapping was enabled to facilitate Out-of-Bag (OOB) validation.
Participation Intensity: Q21 (Years of Sports Socializing) and Q22 (Frequency of Sports Socializing per week) were used to measure the persistence and activity levels of individual participation.
Mode Preference: (1) Participation Modes: Q23 (Offline physical activity participation), Q24 (Online physical activity participation), Q27 (Participation in at least two modes) (2). Spectating Modes: Q25 (Offline spectating), Q26 (Online spectating) (3). Sport Type Preference: Q29 (Major ball games), Q30 (Minor ball games), Q31 (Cycling/running), Q32 (Leisure sports), Q33 (Other sports).
Platform and Expenditure: (1) Appointment and Spectating Platforms: Q36 (Communication software such as WeChat), Q37 (Social software such as TikTok/Rednote), Q38 (Commercial software such as Tencent/Migu), Q39 (Mainstream software such as CCTV Video) (2). Economic Investment: Q40 (Total annual expenditure), Q41 (Single expenditure amount).
Data Preprocessing and Model Settings: Fifteen core behavioral indicators (spanning participation patterns, sports types, media channels, and economic investment) were selected as input features. Categorical variables were encoded numerically, and missing values were handled using zero imputation to preserve data integrity. Addressing Class Imbalance: Given the unequal distribution of the identified latent profiles, this study explicitly addressed class imbalance by setting the class_weight parameter to “balanced.” This method automatically adjusts weights that are inversely proportional to class frequencies in the input data, ensuring that the model is not biased toward the majority class.
Training and Hyperparameters: The analysis was conducted in a Python 3.9 environment using the scikit-learn library. This study constructed a global classification model followed by “One-vs-Rest” models for specific profile identification. The key hyperparameters were configured as follows: The number of trees (n_estimators) was set to 100 to ensure stability, the Gini impurity was selected as the splitting criterion, and the maximum number of features per split was set to the square root of the total features.
Model Evaluation Strategy and Performance Metrics: Instead of traditional k-fold cross-validation, this study utilized OOB validation, a method intrinsic to RF that provides an unbiased estimate of the generalization error using data left out of the bootstrap samples. OOB validation was particularly suitable for this study because it preserves the full training potential of the limited sample size for minority classes. To comprehensively evaluate the model’s classification performance, this study reported a suite of metrics, including Accuracy, Macro-averaged F1-score, Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC), R-squared, and Mean Absolute Error (MAE).
As shown in Table  , the results indicate that the model exhibited a high degree of fit on the training set. Specifically, the training accuracy reached 99.9% with an R-squared value of 0.994 and an MAE of only 0.002. These figures suggest that the model effectively captured and reconstructed the complex behavioral patterns and explained the vast majority of the variation in the sample data. Regarding generalization, the OOB performance was moderate but informative. The Overall OOB Accuracy was 57.1% (corresponding to an OOB Error Rate of 42.9%). Although this reflected some generalization loss, it significantly exceeded both the random guess baseline of 25% and the majority class baseline of 44.1%, thus demonstrating valid predictive power. Furthermore, the model achieved a Macro F1-Score of 0.5617, indicating a balance between precision and recall, and a Macro AUC of 0.6875, reflecting acceptable discriminative ability.

 

It is worth noting the discrepancy between the high training accuracy (99.9%) and OOB accuracy (57.1%). The near-perfect training accuracy reflects the model’s exceptional fit to the specific patterns within the current sample, demonstrating its ability to reconstruct the complex behavioral structures of the surveyed residents. In contrast, the OOB accuracy serves as a more conservative and unbiased estimate of the model’s generalization capability. This gap suggests that while the model effectively captures the associative nuances of this dataset, its predictive power may be more limited when applied to external populations, a common characteristic in high-noise behavioral research.
This characteristic also reflects the inherent tendency of the RF algorithm’s inherent tendency to grow deep trees to capture intricate data patterns, while relying on ensemble voting to mitigate variance. Although the gap suggests a tendency toward overfitting, which is common in behavioral data with high noise and heterogeneity, the application of ensemble voting and feature randomness effectively mitigated this risk. Crucially, as the primary objective of this study was feature selection and importance ranking, rather than developing a high-precision clinical predictive tool, the current performance level provided a robust foundation for identifying the key behavioral markers distinguishing different motivational profiles.Consequently, the resulting feature importance rankings should be interpreted as exploratory behavioral differentiation signals rather than universal decision patterns. The model’s primary utility lies in identifying associative markers within the current dataset, and it is not intended for use as a high-precision predictive tool for external populations.

(2)Overall model performance and feature importance

Primary Salient Behavioral Markers: Sport Type Preference and Social Media Choice.
Q29 (Preference for major ball games) emerged with significant advantage as the most discriminative predictor variable. This suggests that the preference for participating in major ball games, such as basketball and soccer, is the primary behavioral signal for judging an individual’s sports socializing motivational type. Major ball games typically involve strong collaboration, high interaction, and a degree of public display, thus effectively attracting and distinguishing groups with high motivational complexity.
Q37 (Use of social software such as TikTok/Rednote) followed closely, ranking second. The core function of these new social media platforms lies in content creation, sharing, and interaction, which features a distinct display-feedback loop. Its high importance indicates that an individual’s choice to use such platforms for sports socializing profoundly reflects the intensity of their pursuit of extrinsic motivations such as social recognition and prestige accumulation.
Secondary Salient Behavioral Markers: Participation Mode and Economic Investment.
Additionally, variables such as Q40 (Total annual expenditure), Q31 (Preference for cycling/running), and Q22 (Weekly frequency) also demonstrated high discriminatory value. Economic investment (Q40) directly reflected participants’ level of resource commitment, showing a positive correlation with motivational intensity. Cycling/running (Q31), as endurance-based activities dominated by individuals or small groups, are closely associated with motivational types that prioritize intrinsic experience(Fig.  ).
In summary, the feature importance ranking indicates that preferences for specific sports (such as major ball games) and the use of specific social media platforms are the strongest associative markers for differentiating between motivational types in this sample, their importance surpassing intensity indicators such as simple participation frequency. This provides strong evidence for inferring an internal motivational structure from external behavioral observations.

 

(3)Performance and feature importance for specific motivational models

This model aims to capture the group with introverted motivations who prioritize personal psychological experience. The feature importance analysis (Fig.  ) shows that Q30 (Preference for minor ball games, such as table tennis or badminton) is the most critical feature for identifying this group. Minor ball games are generally both social and highly technical, often played in relatively enclosed environments, which aligns with this group’s inherent tendency toward “seclusion” rather than “display.”
Additionally, the importance of Q36 (Communication software, such as WeChat) and Q33 (Other sports) is highlighted. This suggests that this group tends to use private communication tools such as WeChat, which focus on strong social ties, for arrangements rather than public social platforms. Furthermore, they show a higher acceptance of non-mainstream or niche sports (Q33). These behaviors collectively delineate a participant profile that is “self-contained and self-amused,” and less concerned with external social evaluations.

 

The behavioral pattern of the group whose core motivation is health maintenance (Physiology-Oriented Type, Class 2 is highly centered around physical exercise. The analysis results (Fig.  ) indicate that Q29 (Preference for major ball games) is also a primary feature for identifying this group. This may be because major ball games can provide high-intensity aerobic and anaerobic training and are widely regarded as efficient ways to keep fit.
Q31 (Preference for cycling/running), another classic form of individual fitness exercise, follows closely in importance. Furthermore, Q40 (Total annual expenditure) is also a significant indicator, suggesting that individuals in this group are willing to make a stable economic commitment to achieve their health goals (e.g., purchasing equipment or covering venue fees). Their behavioral pattern clearly points toward “efficiency priority,” meaning they select the sports and commitment levels most conducive to achieving physiological health objectives.

 

The Balanced Type group exhibits equilibrium across all motivational dimensions, and their behavioral pattern also reflects a characteristic of “inclusiveness” (Fig.  ). The most critical feature for identifying this group is Q40 (Total annual expenditure). This suggests that moderate, sustainable economic commitment is their most notable behavioral characteristic, being neither extremely low, like the “Niche Type,” nor potentially very high, like the “Comprehensively Oriented Type.”
Simultaneously, their participation in mainstream sports such as Q29 (Major ball games) and their use of Q39 (Mainstream software such as CCTV Video) collectively define their behavioral profile. This reflects a stable, mainstream participant image: They actively engage in popular sports socializing activities, obtain information through official or mainstream channels, and maintain a reasonable level of consumption.

 

The High Motivation/Comprehensively Oriented Type group pursues diverse levels of satisfaction, including social status and prestige, and their behavior exhibits a strong external orientation. The analysis results (Fig.  ) convincingly show that the importance score of Q37 (Social software such as TikTok/Rednote) is overwhelmingly high, far exceeding that of all the other variables. This finding confirms their core motivation for seeking prestige and displaying status, as these platforms are a central arena for self-presentation, personal brand building, and gaining social attention and recognition.
Q40 (Total annual expenditure) and Q31 (Cycling/running) are also important auxiliary identification features. The high expenditure is consistent with their comprehensive resource commitment, whereas the preference for cycling/running (especially projects such as marathons that have social currency attributes) reflects their tendency to combine personal achievement with social display. The behavioral decisions of this group are highly dependent on channels and methods that can maximize their social visibility and capital returns.

 

## Discussion
Centering on the core topic of “Motivation Stratification and behavioral differentiation patterns of Sports Socializing among Chinese Residents,” this study utilized a hybrid approach combining LPA and the RF model. The LPA revealed four latent profiles for sports socializing motivation among Chinese residents. This finding fundamentally questions the theoretical assumption in traditional research that treats sports socializing participants as a homogeneous group. This aligns with recent research trends and reveals critical differences in behavioral expression across different motivational types using the RF model. The results obtained further extend this typological perspective to the broader domain of sports socializing.

## The synergistic and dissociative patterns of motivation in a digital collectivist context
This study employed a hybrid framework to reveal that sports socializing among Chinese residents is characterized by a stratified motivational structure rather than a homogeneous impulse. This typological heterogeneity aligns with the recent findings of Liu et al. [ ], who identified distinct motivational clusters among student populations. However, this study’s results extend beyond mere classification to propose a theoretical integration of Self-Determination Theory and Social Capital Theory. This study argues that intrinsic agency (Self-Determination) and extrinsic resource accumulation (Social Capital) are not dichotomous opposites but operate through complex dissociative or synergistic patterns, deeply rooted in the cultural transition of contemporary China.
Self-Determination Theory suggests that individuals’ behavioral motivation stems from their own will and choices rather than external pressures. The niche/special preference groups exhibit clear intrinsic motivational characteristics, with moderately high scores in happiness and belongingness needs, while scoring lower on external motivations such as social status and prestige. This pattern is consistent with the emphasis on autonomy needs within Self-Determination Theory and suggests that even in social sports settings, some participants may prioritize internal experiences over external social evaluations [ ]. This contrasts with the traditional focus on social capital accumulation theories in sports socializing motivation research, which reveals the complexity and diversity of motivations for sports socializing.It is noteworthy that the Niche/Special Preference group constitutes a small subpopulation within the current sample. While their “Introverted-Satisfaction” pattern offers a unique perspective on individualization in China, these cultural interpretations should be regarded as tentative. Given the limited sample size of this specific group, the findings provide exploratory evidence rather than definitive conclusions regarding this demographic.
Furthermore, the cultural context of Chinese society provides deeper support for the existence of this group. In recent years, amid social transformation and the continuous development of social modernization and individualization trends, an increasing number of individuals have begun to pursue social models that align with their personal needs. This trend is particularly pronounced among younger generations. Intrinsic and personalized needs have gradually become focal points in mainstream society, marking a shift from collectivist values rooted in traditional Chinese culture. Self-fulfillment, emotional satisfaction, and inner experiences have gradually become more prominent concerns. The context of sports socializing is no longer limited to external social interactions and the accumulation of social capital, but is gradually evolving toward personal emotional expression and the maintenance of mental health. Against this backdrop, the presence of a niche/special preference group, though small, aligns with the broader discourse on individualization in contemporary Chinese society. Their motivational pattern can be seen as a manifestation of a growing emphasis on personal needs alongside traditional social values, such as seeking happiness, psychological fulfillment, and emotional belonging. This transformation mirrors profound changes in Chinese society: a gradual shift from traditional collectivism and social evaluation orientation toward a greater emphasis on individual needs and self-realization. For instance, the rise of digital and online social platforms has enabled more individuals to choose social methods that better suit their personal preferences.
The “Synergistic Reinforcement Mechanism” is most evident in the “High Motivation/Comprehensively Oriented” group. Contrary to the traditional view that extrinsic rewards (e.g., status) might crowd out intrinsic motivation, this group demonstrates that in China’s “Digital Collectivism” culture, high social capital accumulation can coexist with and even amplify intrinsic autonomy. Gustafsson et al. [ ] found that elite athletes with high autonomous-high controlled profiles experienced lower burnout, suggesting that motivation quality is paramount. Similarly, for these “High Motivation” individuals, the social recognition gained through sports (a form of social capital) serves as an internalized feedback loop that fulfills their need for competence and relatedness. This reflects a unique cultural phenomenon where “performing health” on public platforms has become a new form of social currency. Consequently, public health interventions for this segment should not shy away from extrinsic incentives. Instead, they should leverage gamification and social comparison—integrating “leaderboards” or “virtual badges” into digital health platforms—to utilize their desire for status display as a catalyst for sustained physical engagement.
Conversely, the “Dissociative Mechanism” observed in the “Niche/Special Preference” group highlights a shift toward “Networked Individualism.” This group scores high on intrinsic rewards, but actively rejects extrinsic validation, effectively decoupling psychological satisfaction from social instrumentality. This mirrors Pedersen’s [ ] observation that intrinsic factors are often more robust drivers for specific subgroups. Culturally, this represents a departure from traditional collectivist obligations toward a more personalized, “tribe-like” social structure. The RF identification of their preference for “private communication tools (WeChat)” over public platforms validates this withdrawal. For public health policy, this implies that “one-size-fits-all” mass mobilization strategies may be counterproductive for this sensitive demographic. Instead, interventions must respect privacy and psychological safety, fostering small-scale, semi-private sports communities (e.g., niche clubs) to protect their autonomy from the pressure of social surveillance.

## Functional pragmatism and the “Healthy China” imperative
Whereas the extremes of the motivational spectrum offer theoretical richness, the “Physiology-Oriented” group (representing 44.1% of the sample) underscores the pragmatic reality of the “Healthy China 2030” initiative. This group exhibits a “Utility-Dominant” mechanism, where participation is strictly driven by physiological health needs rather than complex social exchange. Bang Jong-gyun [ ] noted that different sports attract different motivational types; this study’s findings confirm a “functional matching” effect where this group gravitates toward high-efficiency activities like cycling and running. This preference indicates that, for the silent majority of Chinese residents, sports socializing is less about “socializing” and more about “sports” as a tool for health maintenance.
This widespread pragmatism has profound implications for public sports service provision. The high proportion of this profile suggests that the primary barrier to population-level physical activity is not a lack of social incentives, but likely infrastructural and efficiency barriers. Therefore, policy resources should shift from organizing elaborate social sports events to optimizing basic accessibility. The construction of “15-minute fitness circles,” smart trails, and the provision of “Quantified Self” tools (e.g., simple step-counters or heart-rate monitors) would align perfectly with their utility-driven mindset. Unlike the “High Motivation” group that seeks social validation, this pragmatic majority requires efficient, low-friction feedback on their health progress to maintain adherence.

## Decoding behavioral signals for precision intervention
The integration of RF modeling allows this study to move from a theoretical typology to practical behavioral decoding. The identification of “Sport Type Preference” and “Social Media Channel” as the top salient behavioral markers validates the premise that internal psychological configurations map systematically onto external behavioral choices. The integration of RF modeling facilitates a transition from theoretical typology to the identification of exploratory behavioral markers. The identification of sport preferences and media channels as top indicators validates that internal psychological configurations are reflected in external behavioral choices. These markers provide a directional roadmap for targeted public health strategies, where specific behaviors serve as proxies for underlying motivations.This supports Yang and Hwang’s [ ] Affordance Theory perspective, in which specific media platforms are chosen to mediate specific social motivations.
The stark contrast in media usage—with “High Motivation” individuals flocking to visual-centric platforms like Douyin (TikTok) and “Balanced/Niche” individuals preferring WeChat—provides a roadmap for precision public health algorithms. Instead of broad-spectrum health education, future digital health systems could use these behavioral signals (e.g., app usage patterns or sport choices) to algorithmically predict a user’s motivational profile. For a user identified as “High Motivation” via their preference for competitive ball games and video sharing, the system could push social challenges and community rankings. Conversely, for a user identified as “Balanced” or “Physiology-Oriented” via their preference for solitary running, the system should push health tips, recovery advice, and personal progress reports.
Furthermore, the “Balanced” profile, which mirrors the “homeostatic” patterns observed by Rottensteiner et al. [ ] in sustainable athletes, serves as the ideal model for long-term retention. Their moderate investment and broad-spectrum motivation make them the ““stabilizers” of the sports ecosystem. Public health strategies should aim to guide extreme groups toward this balanced state—encouraging the “Physiology-Oriented” to gradually embrace low-pressure social interaction, and helping the “High Motivation” group find intrinsic joy beyond status display—thereby fostering a more resilient and sustainable national fitness culture.

## Limitations and future research directions
Although this study provides novel insights into the motivational stratification and behavioral differentiation patterns of sports socializing, several limitations warrant caution when interpreting the findings and point to directions for future research.
First, regarding sampling strategy, the study relied on a non-probability convenience sampling method. Although this study covered a wide range of demographics, the sample may have been biased toward younger, urban, and internet-savvy populations, limiting the generalizability of the findings to rural or older demographics in China. Future studies should employ stratified random sampling to enhance representativeness.
Second, concerning data measurement, the study’s reliance on self-reported questionnaires introduces potential common-method variance and social desirability bias. For instance, participants might underreport status-seeking motivations (e.g., Q56), while overreporting health-oriented motivations owing to social norms. While this study employed anonymous data collection to mitigate this issue, future research could integrate objective behavioral data (e.g., actual consumption records or fitness app logs) to triangulate the findings.
Third, regarding research design, the cross-sectional nature of this study precludes causal inferences. This study identified associations between motivational profiles and behavioral choices but could not determine whether specific motivations preceded behavioral changes or evolved dynamically alongside sports participation. Longitudinal designs are required to track the shift in motivational profiles over time.
Fourth, a specific statistical limitation is the relatively small sample size of Class 1 (“Psychologically Introverted,” 3.6%, n = 40). Although Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) is robust in identifying minority classes, the relatively small size of Class 1 may affect the statistical stability and representativeness of this specific subgroup. Consequently, this finding highlights Class 1 as a niche subpopulation that requires targeted oversampling in future longitudinal or quantitative studies to further validate the robustness and distinctiveness of this motivational phenotype.
Finally, regarding methodological constraints, the disparity between the training and OOB performance of the Random Forest model suggests that its generalizability is limited. The identified behavioral signals, such as the preference for major ball games (Q29) and social media choices (Q37), reflect sample-specific associative patterns that may vary across different sociocultural contexts. Furthermore, as this study employs a cross-sectional design, these patterns must be viewed as predictive markers of differentiation rather than evidence of a causal decision-making process. Future research should prioritize longitudinal validation and the use of independent test sets to refine these exploratory findings into more robust, generalizable frameworks.

## Conclusion
This study systematically investigated the motivational heterogeneity and behavioral differentiation patterns of sports socializing among Chinese residents. By employing a hybrid methodological framework combining LPA and RF modeling, this study identified four distinct latent profiles: the Niche/Special Preference Type, Physiology-Oriented Type, Balanced Type, and High Motivation/Comprehensively Oriented Type. Self-Determination Theory and Social Capital Theory, highlighting the potential utility of a multi-theoretical perspective for understanding motivation in sports socializing, complement the traditional “variable-centered” approach and provide an empirical case for considering typological heterogeneity in sports socializing research. Beyond these specific empirical findings, this study offers a broader conceptual contribution: It serves as a proof-of-concept for integrating motivational typology with machine learning. This hybrid approach demonstrates that identifying “who they are” (motivational phenotypes) is a prerequisite for accurately characterizing “how they behave” (behavioral signals), providing a scalable, data-driven template for public health behavioral prediction that moves beyond traditional variable-centered paradigms.
At the behavioral level, the RF model successfully decoded the external signals of the internal profiles. It identified “Sport Type Preference” (specifically, major ball games) and “Social Media Channel” (specifically, visual-centric platforms) as the most critical salient behavioral markers. This confirms that the sociocultural attributes of sports and media characteristics of digital platforms constitute the key predictive markers of behavioral differentiation. Practically, these findings provide precise user profiles for sports social platforms and public service departments. They suggest a transition from “broad-spectrum mobilization” to “precision intervention.” For instance, services can be customized based on these validated motivation-behavior patterns to enhance engagement and well-being effectively, for example, designing gamified challenges for status-seekers or creating privacy-focused environments for niche groups.For stakeholders, creating privacy-focused environments could be a potential pilot direction for engaging niche groups. However, as this group represents only a small fraction of the participants, conclusions regarding service customization for this profile are preliminary and should be validated through further targeted research before large-scale policy implementation.
These findings should be interpreted with caution. As this study employed a cross-sectional design, the relationships identified between motivational profiles and behavioral choices were associative and exploratory rather than causal. The results indicate that specific motivational structures are robustly associated with specific platform choices, but they do not confirm a directional “drive.” Future research should build on this exploratory framework by employing longitudinal designs to track the temporal evolution of these profiles and further validate the stability of this classification system across broader demographic contexts.
