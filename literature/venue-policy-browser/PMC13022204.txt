In densely populated and land-scarce areas, pocket parks provide a practical solution for increasing green space supply. This research establishes an opportunity measurement method based on cultural coefficients and a cultural perception evaluation system combining the Semantic Differential Method (SD) and the integration of Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) with the entropy method. Using 104 pocket parks in Liwan Old Town as case studies, the feasibility of these methods was validated. Lorenz curves, Gini coefficients, location entropy, and spatial autocorrelation analysis were employed to compare the overall spatial equity and spatial distribution patterns of cultural opportunities and cultural perception. Results indicate that the spatial equity of cultural perception is higher than that of cultural opportunities, revealing a mismatch between the spatial supply of cultural resources and their experiential realization. In the old town with distinct local characteristics, pocket parks provide abundant cultural opportunities, but their cultural perception is constrained by park construction and maintenance conditions. This research explores the role and impact of local culture in achieving green equity. The findings can be applied to similar old towns with rich local contexts to identify development potential in the early stages of pocket park planning and design, providing a reference for future decision-making.

## Introduction

## Background
Urbanization is a global trend, with an estimated 68% of the world’s population expected to reside in urban centres by 2050 . This process generates a demand for identifying new green spaces within cities. However, urban densification continues to encroach upon green areas, making it increasingly difficult to provide large green spaces in urban environments . In cities of developing countries in Asia, communities are increasingly affected by social and spatial inequalities ,so the equitable distribution of public parks is now a key indicator of sustainable urbanisation . China’s national Standard for Planning of Urban Green Space (GB/T 51,346–2019) recommends a minimum of 7 m2 of park land per capita for every municipal district. The Guangzhou Green System Master Plan (2020–2035) accordingly sets a target of ≥ 5.5 m2 per capita for Liwan District by 2035. However, according to the current total area of park green space within Liwan old town and the Seventh National Population Census, the historic core of Liwan currently provides only 2.23 m2 of park land per capita—far below both the requirements. Because this area is already a high-density built-up environment, opportunities to insert new city-level parks are extremely limited. Pocket parks, due to their small scale and flexible location and distribution, offer a solution for providing public park spaces in densely populated and land-scarce areas to closing this critical green-gap deficit .

## Pocket parks and green space equity
Pocket parks are a type of urban park smaller than typical community parks . As population density continues to rise and the difficulty of providing large green spaces in urban areas increases, the popularity of pocket parks is steadily growing . They are more economical in construction and maintenance than traditional large urban parks and community parks and are more adaptable to the surrounding environment . In cities with dense populations and scarce land resources, supplementing pocket parks has become an inevitable trend. This approach helps alleviate the severe imbalance between the supply and demand of recreational resources in built-up areas .
The equity of residents’ access to green space resources is affected by the rationality and equitability of the distribution of park green spaces. Historical urban areas with more concentrated resources have a worse green space environment than other areas of the city due to tight roadways and intricate internal operations . These phenomena reflect environmental injustice; In recent years, changes in living conditions and age structure have led to differentiated demands for park green spaces, with green space equity for specific groups, especially the elderly, becoming increasingly prominent – . To achieve environmental justice and maximize the benefits of green space, it is essential to improve social equity and ensure equal access for all groups.
The existing research primarily focuses on the user experience dimensions of pocket parks, emphasizing how green space characteristics influence user experiences and directly affect the functionality and comfort of parks through spatial configuration and environmental design – . Satisfaction is the primary indicator used to assess visitors’ perceptions and the quality of their experience in parks , . The park’s in-ternal facilities and surrounding environment can enhance the flexibility and use in-tensity of pocket parks . The types of recreational activities and level of participation of visitors are directly influenced by green space features, including spatial facility arrangement, historical elements, and the path network . In China, research on pocket parks mainly focuses on planning and design strategies with limited studies on integrating function, form, and cultural elements. The pocket park at Daxin Road in Guangzhou integrates Southern-style ivory carving into its design . However, the outcome merely showcases a patchwork of characteristic elements with low practicality. Pocket parks serve as public activity spaces and emotional attachment areas, and integrating cultural perception with emotional experience can enhance the cultural depth of urban landscapes.

## Cultural equity, cultural opportunity, and cultural perception
Cultural equity is commonly used to describe the distribution of cultural resources and participation opportunities among different groups . Cultural indicators and spatial history are intrinsic factors linking cultural equity and environmental equity . In green space, cultural equity can be regarded as a manifestation of environmental justice . Therefore, understanding cultural perceptions and uses of green spaces is crucial for designing and managing historical landscapes.
Cultural opportunity is the combination of cultural value and spatial opportunity, quantified by assessing a site’s cultural significance and the opportunities it offers. Building on the Recreation Opportunity Spectrum (ROS), this research proposes a cultural opportunity evaluation. Since supply–demand interactions are key to opportunity accessibility, they are widely used to assess urban opportunities . Traditional assessments of park recreational opportunities often focus on residential area or population size within the service area , , overlooking the impact of the green space conditions and cultural context on supply and demand efficiency. To more accurately assess urban green space supply and demand, internal environmental factors should be comprehensively considered.
Cultural perception refers to the process by which people perceive and understand objective elements in various cultural environments through sensory experiences and their own cultural background . When urban green spaces serve as cultural spaces, their physical environment, interactive contexts, and cultural activities are key elements in shaping spatial cultural perception . The quality of the recreational environment is crucial in evaluating green space perception, with vibrant green public spaces often being safer, more attractive, and providing abundant social and cultural exchange opportunities , . Previous studies have explored methods for preserving historical architecture by analysing cultural perception in historic districts , yet they overlook the impact of green space in old towns on cultural identity and transmission. To accurately assess the social effects of urban green spaces and harness their spatial potential, it is essential to incorporate recreational quality and spatial vitality as key factors in the green space cultural perception evaluation system.
In this research, cultural opportunity refers to what spaces provide, defined as the cultural value and spatial conditions provided by urban green spaces. Cultural perception refers to how residents experience, defined as residents’ subjective experiences of cultural elements during park use. The two are conceptually independent and are parallel and complementary perspectives, rather than interdependent variables. They are analytical frameworks specifically constructed for this research to address the practical issues of cultural equity in pocket parks and have an interactive relationship in practice. Abundant cultural resources may enhance residents’ perception, but perception is also influenced by cultural background, maintenance of the environment, and social atmosphere. Moreover, cultural opportunity focuses on the district-wide scale, assessing the number, distribution, and spatial balance of pocket parks across Liwan Old Town, while cultural perception operates at the individual-park scale, evaluating the cultural experience quality of each park through residents’ feedback and field observations. Together, these two dimensions reflect both the fairness of spatial provision and the quality of experiential realization, forming a macro–micro framework that links overall supply patterns with local experiential differences. This research compares cultural opportunity and cultural perception as parallel dimensions and examines potential spatial mismatches between them, offering a new perspective for understanding the equity of urban green space resources.

## Research gap and objects
Pocket parks are outdoor spaces strongly tied to local cultural emotions, serving as venues for activities and social interactions among residents , . As micro-green spaces with greater environmental adaptability , they hold significant value in old urban areas , . Existing studies primarily focus on the impact of internal environmental characteristics of pocket parks on user experiences – , but lack practical applications for incorporating local culture . Current discussions on cultural equity remain focused on singular resource functions and patterns – , without systematically addressing detailed spatial distribution. Cultural equity and environmental equity are complementary ; However, prior research has emphasized urban green space equity under the influence of objective supply–demand relationships – , , without recognizing the critical role of cultural perceptions of green spaces in areas with rich local culture . Urban green spaces have the potential to become cultural venues . By inserting pocket parks, ensuring the equitable distribution of green spaces that carry local culture across urban spaces and different social groups helps promote social equity and environmental justice.
To address the identified gaps, this research conducted a comprehensive survey and database creation of pocket parks in Liwan old town. It evaluates the spatial equity of cultural opportunities and perception across different geographic units, explores the cultural features, and examines the role of local culture in achieving green equity. Specifically, the research makes three key contributions, achieved through the following approaches.

Developing a cultural opportunity measurement method and cultural perception evaluation system.

Building on population size-based models, this study incorporates a cultural coefficient to refine supply–demand measurement for pocket parks. It improves single-perspective evaluations by introducing a multidimensional cultural perception framework. Using the Semantic Differential (SD) method and the integration of Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) with the entropy method, the research quantitatively assesses internal spaces, external environments, and local cultural values, considering recreational quality and spatial vitality.

Evaluating spatial equity differences in cultural opportunities and cultural perception.

This research applies Lorenz Curve, Gini Coefficient, and Location Entropy to assess overall and local spatial equity across governance units.

 Analysing spatial distribution patterns and clustering of cultural opportunities and perception.

By calculating Global Moran’s Index and Local Moran’s Index, the research identifies spatial autocorrelation and significant local clusters.

## Materials and methods

## Study area
Guangzhou, located in southern China which is one of the core cities in the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao Greater Bay Area. It has over 2,200 years of history and culture. Liwan District is regarded as the birthplace and core-bearing area of Lingnan culture . For this case study, the old town area located north of the Pearl River in Liwan District was selected as the research area (Fig.  ), the area covers approximately 11.3 km2. It is the most concentrated and well-preserved area for Guangzhou’s traditional culture and intangible cultural heritage . 
The reasons for selecting Liwan District are as follows: (1) It represents a typical densely populated old town with a rich local cultural atmosphere, ensuring the study’s findings are applicable and generalizable to similar regions in China and internationally. (2) With over a hundred established pocket parks, the abundant sample size guarantees scientifically reliable results. Furthermore, as Guangzhou is actively implementing the policy of constructing at least one pocket park within every 5-min community living circle , the number of pocket parks still continues to grow.
Pocket parks generally range from 400 to 10,000 square meters. According to City pocket park design guideline , areas smaller than 400 square meters can be flexibly adjusted to micro-pocket parks. Given the spatial constraints of densely built old urban areas, this research extends the lower size limit to 50 square meters for green spaces with well-equipped facilities and distinctive features. (Table .) 
As of 2024, 104 pocket parks had been established in Liwan Old Town, encompassing diverse forms of green spaces, including neighbourhood gardens and small parks. These parks exhibit a spatial distribution pattern of "net-like distribution with uneven density" (Fig.  ). 

## Research framework
Distance is a key factor influencing park visits, as users typically walk to nearby parks on weekdays . To improve community green service quality and achieve the goal of 83% park walking coverage, as outlined in the Guangzhou Green Space System Planning accessibility should be incorporated into the cultural service evaluation of pocket parks . This approach holds greater practical value and provides useful guidance for construction. Over 20% of residents in Liwan Old Town are aged 60 or above, reflecting an ageing population. To achieve vertical spatial equity in cultural opportunities and perception , pocket parks must consider the needs of the elderly. China’s Standard for Urban Residential Areas Planning & Design (GB50180-2018) define a 5-min living circle as 300 m, but considering elderly walking speeds are approximately 80% of adults’ , this research defines 250 m as the effective service area for parks. Using ArcGIS 10.8 network analysis to account for actual walking paths, the service areas of pocket parks were delineated, and subsequent research will focus on the 250-m walking accessibility range (Fig.  ). 
Figure  presents the evaluation framework for pocket parks through cultural opportunities and cultural perception. Cultural opportunities refer to residents’ potential to engage in Lingnan cultural activities within a 5-min walking range of pocket parks, assessed via supply–demand analysis; cultural perception is defined as the aggregated cultural perception scores of all parks in this service area. 

## Data collection

## Field survey data
The research data comes from the Green Space System Plan of Liwan District and field surveys of pocket parks in Liwan old town. Data on park conditions and usage were collected via fixed-point photography. Considering regional characteristics such as working hours and midday nap habits, 16:00–18:00 corresponds to the period when children finish school and the elderly complete their afternoon rest. This period is characterized by the highest intensity of pedestrian activity within the community, suggesting that it may lead to increased usage intensity of pocket parks. Data collection occurred on weekdays during autumn, with temperatures between 20 °C and 30 °C, to obtain the most representative data . At each pocket park, two trained observers conducted simultaneous counts in 15-min cycles; three consecutive cycles were run and the peak value retained. Inter-rater agreement was verified after every cycle—if the difference exceeded 5%, an additional cycle was carried out until consistency was achieved. One observer was stationed at the main entrance, the other at the far end of the site; the compact scale of the parks allowed both to maintain unobstructed lines of sight across the entire space. Where vegetation or structures blocked views, observers adjusted their positions slightly while remaining within the original vantage points. Photographic documentation was taken from each of the four corners toward the interior with a 50 mm lens at the height of 1.5m; a 24 mm wide-angle lens was substituted in narrow or huge parks, and supplementary close-ups were added whenever complex features required detailed recording, ensuring a comprehensive visual archive of every site (Fig ).

## Multi-source data
This research uses some open-access data. Road network data is from openstreetmap.com, excluding expressways and unsuitable roads. ArcGIS 10.8 is used for topological processing and network analysis of the pedestrian network. Population data comes from the latest Seventh National Population Census of China, conducted in 2020, and used a 100-m gridded population dataset generated and openly released by other scholars based on this census . POI data, sourced from Baidu Maps, were cleaned to remove duplicates, irrelevant, and outlier values, leaving around 17,000 entries.

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study protocol was approved by Guangzhou Urban Planning & Design Survey Research Institute’s Human Ethics Committee (No.2022-KY-G08). The SD method uses verbal scales to assess psychological perceptions of landscapes, converting respondents’ feelings into quantifiable data . The entropy method, based on data, objectively determines indicator weights and reduces variable information overlap , and is used to assign weights to factors in the cultural perception evaluation system of pocket parks.

This research designed and implemented two structured questionnaires. For the weighted questionnaire, 20 experts were invited to participate, including university professors, researchers, practitioners in urban and rural planning and landscape architecture, postdoctoral researchers and doctoral students in related fields (Table ). The analytic hierarchy process (AHP) was used to determine indicator weights. The judgment matrix passed the consistency test (CR ≤ 0.1), and the objective weights were calculated using the entropy method. The composite weights were obtained by multiplying the subjective and objective weights and normalizing them. The qualitative factor scoring questionnaire used the semantic differential (SD) method to score qualitative factors. A total of 70 respondents were included, 50 of whom were residents of the Liwan Old Town district, selected through a stratified random sampling, and 20 were experts (Table ). Reliability and validity tests (Cronbach’s α > 0.7; KMO > 0.7 and Bartlett’s test were significant) demonstrated that both questionnaires had high reliability and validity.

## Spatial equity measurement
The Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient measure the percentage of the population benefiting from facility services and have been applied to evaluate facility distribution equity . To assess the spatial equity of cultural service evaluation indicators for pocket parks (including cultural opportunities and perceptions), this research uses the Gini coefficient to quantify inequality and visualizes equity through the Lorenz curve . According to the United Nations Development Program scale, a Gini coefficient below 0.2 indicates "absolute equity," 0.2–0.29 represents "relative equity," 0.3–0.39 is a "reasonable gap," 0.4–0.59 indicates "high inequality," and above 0.6 denotes "significant inequality." Thus, a smaller Gini coefficient reflects more equitable spatial distribution of cultural opportunities and perceptions.

## Spatial distribution pattern
Location entropy evaluates the spatiotemporal concentration of resources . It has been widely applied to assess spatial patterns and equity in public resources and environmental equity , . This research uses location entropy to analyse the spatial distribution of cultural opportunities and perceptions, categorizing the entropy values of 12 subdistricts into five groups using the natural breaks method. The location entropy for each subdistrict in relation to pocket park services (cultural opportunities and perceptions) is calculated as the ratio of the service evaluation of that subdistrict to the average of the entire study area. A value above 1 indicates higher-than-average cultural services, while below 1 indicates lower levels. Higher location entropy values correspond to better cultural service levels in that subdistrict.

## Spatial aggregation pattern
To explore the spatial clustering of cultural service resources in pocket parks, spatial correlation analysis is used. This method effectively identifies clustering patterns, assesses spatial concentration, and determines hotspot distributions . Previous studies have used spatial correlation analysis to examine the spatial clustering patterns of urban green spaces and historical cultural spaces and facilities to investigate urban facility equity. This research uses the Global Moran’s I index to assess overall clustering and the Local Moran’s I index to identify spatial clustering patterns.

## Cultural service evaluation

## Cultural opportunity measurement
This research measures the cultural opportunities of pocket parks using the supply–demand ratio. Building on previous studies that considered the service scope of parks , it improves the single-dimensional methods of evaluating supply by residential land area or population ratio and estimating cultural opportunity demand by demographics . A cultural coefficient is introduced to reflect the park’s internal construction and regional cultural conditions, adjusting the supply–demand ratio. This allows for an accurate measurement of cultural opportunities in pocket parks influenced by Lingnan humanistic characteristics, highlighting the role of culture in achieving social equity and environmental justice. The core idea of calculating the cultural coefficient lies within the supply–demand framework of cultural opportunities, where the ratio between the composite diversity index and the cultural demand gap is used to measure the balance between the richness of cultural supply and the degree of demand fulfilment , .
The Shannon diversity index of facilities and activities, along with its ratio in cultural and non-cultural facilities and activities, helps identify dominant facility or activity types and reflects the quality of cultural supply. A diverse range of cultural facilities and activities enhances the user experience, thus improving the effectiveness of cultural supply. The demand gap, defined as the difference between expected and actual demand , helps uncover unmet cultural needs. Since children and the elderly are the primary park users , they are more likely to engage in cultural activities at pocket parks. Therefore, the population aged 0–14 and over 60 years old is used as the statistical basis for the expected cultural preferences in pocket parks. Participation is measured by the number of people engaging in Lingnan cultural activities, reflecting actual demand. The adjusted cultural opportunity value is normalized, with higher values indicating a better supply–demand match and lower values indicating a shortage of cultural resources. By adjusting the supply–demand relationship with multi-dimensional cultural coefficients, this evaluation can improve the interaction between park usage patterns and residents’ daily habits, preventing supply–demand mismatches or resource concentration, providing balanced cultural resources for various users, and improving the efficiency of pocket park provision in urban planning. (Table ).

## Cultural perception evaluation system construction
Currently, pocket parks, as small-scale urban green spaces, lack a dedicated evaluation mechanism for green space perception. Existing evaluation frameworks primarily rely on metrics designed for large-scale urban parks, such as green space quality , ecological suitability , vitality of green public spaces , recreation level , activity satisfaction , and resident physical activity . To better reflect residents’ preferences for the local characteristics of pocket parks and enhance their practical application, this study optimizes and supplements traditional evaluation metrics by combining literature analysis, multi-source big data, and field research to develop a cultural perception evaluation system for pocket parks.

## Results

## Spatial equity of pocket park service areas
The Gini coefficients for cultural opportunities and perceptions in pocket parks are 0.75 and 0.54, respectively, reflecting significant spatial inequality in cultural opportunities and considerable spatial inequality in cultural perceptions. Specifically, only 47.34% of residents in the study area can access cultural services within a 5-min walking distance from pocket parks, while 52.66% of the population is excluded from effective service areas. Furthermore, 80.52% of residents benefit from 23.27% of cultural opportunities in these parks, while 79.49% experience 52.54% of the cultural perceptions. Overall, the spatial inequality in cultural opportunities is more pronounced than in cultural perception quality. (Fig.  .) 

## Spatial distribution pattern of pocket parks cultural service
A total of 104 pocket parks within 12 subdistricts were evaluated (Table ). In the study area, 8.33% and 16.67% of the subdistricts exhibit very high levels of cultural opportunities and cultural perceptions, concentrated in the northwest. In contrast, 16.67% and 8.33% of the subdistricts show very low levels of cultural opportunities and perceptions, mainly located in the southeast. The high concentration of parks leads to overlapping effective service areas, which do not increase the served population, thus intensifying spatial inequality in cultural service distribution. 
The cultural opportunities and cultural perception levels of pocket parks in Nanyuan Subdistrict are both very high. It has the largest number of pocket parks, and the park facilities are extremely diverse, with a large proportion of cultural facilities, and a high level of participation in cultural activities. The overall Lingnan humanistic atmosphere is strong; the cultural opportunities and cultural perception levels of pocket parks in Lingnan Subdistrict are both very low. Its number is the lowest within the research scope, and its only pocket park is located behind the bus station next to the main road. It is more accessible than lingering. Although it has certain cultural display facilities, it lacks interactivity and landscape appeal, and the overall Lingnan humanistic atmosphere is weak. (Fig.  ). 

## Spatial aggregation pattern of pocket parks cultural service
The results of the global spatial autocorrelation analysis show that the p-values for cultural opportunities and cultural perceptions are both less than 0.01, and the z-scores exceed 2.58, indicating significant positive spatial autocorrelation of cultural services in pocket parks within the study area, and both show significant spatial clustering patterns (Table ). Overall, areas with high or low levels of cultural opportunities or cultural perceptions tend to cluster near other areas with similar values, with the spatial correlation intensity of cultural opportunities slightly higher than that of cultural perceptions. 
The results of the local spatial autocorrelation analysis indicate that regions of high-high (H–H) cluster type for pocket park cultural opportunities and cultural perception quality are relatively concentrated, primarily in the central area and sporadically in the southern region. In contrast, the low-low (L-L) cluster type regions for cultural opportunities cover a larger area and are more widely distributed compared to those for cultural perceptions, primarily in the northern and eastern parts of the study area. Most L-L cluster type regions for cultural perceptions also fall within the L-L cluster type regions for cultural opportunities.
In the central area of the study area, the subdistricts of Caihong, Fengyuan, Jinhua, and Nanyuan contain high-high (H–H) clusters for both cultural opportunities and cultural perceptions. However, their spatial distributions do not fully align. These areas share common features such as high cultural activity participation, diverse cultural facilities, rich historical heritage, and a strong Lingnan cultural atmosphere. Morphologically, the H–H clusters still preserves the fine-grained Lingnan alley-grid and a relatively continuous pedestrian network, while medium-density residential quarters provide usable leftover space for pocket parks. In addition, several neighbourhoods in this belt have already completed urban-renewal projects, so better supporting services and refurbished infrastructure help explain their stronger performance.
By contrast, Xicun Sub-district forms extensive continuous low-low (L-L) clusters. Functionally and geographically, it is constrained by old factory buildings, river arms, railways and elevated expressways that reduce overall accessibility. Also, some neighbourhoods in this area was once self-contained factory-dormitories, so its inward-facing courtyards visually hide pocket parks from outsiders and keep visiting intensity low. The southern edge of Longjin Sub-district shows the same L-L pattern because crowded wholesale markets and as-yet-unreconstructed shanties leave almost no public open ground and hinder routine maintenance. Elsewhere, some pocket parks in these blocks are still under construction and have not officially opened; temporary hoardings and stored equipment create short-term negative impacts, but cultural services are expected to improve once those projects are completed. (Fig.  .) 
To further explore the potential for enhancing cultural opportunities and cultural perception in spatial mismatch, this research mapped the potential sites for pocket parks (Fig.  ). Although some areas are already covered by the 250-m service range of existing pocket parks, the cultural perception levels of these parks are generally low. They should improve service quality by upgrading facilities, strengthening maintenance and enriching cultural activities. Areas not yet covered within the current 250-m service area indicate insufficient supply and require new pocket parks. In the simulation site selection experiment, candidate locations were first identified based on proximity to roads, presence in old neighbourhoods or urban villages, closeness to cultural public service facilities, and availability of underutilized green spaces. Locations overlapping with existing service areas or unsuitable for development (gated residential areas, industrial zones, schools) were excluded, resulting in 48 potential sites. Network analysis in ArcGIS 10.8 was then used to evaluate service coverage, and the ten optimal sites were selected based on the size of their service areas. The results show that in densely populated areas, adding the top 10 pocket parks with the largest 250-m walking distance can maximize the expansion of marginal service areas. The subsequent coverage-based ranking is therefore a second-round filter to maximize immediate equity gains under limited budgets to ensure that the first batch of pocket parks delivers the largest spatial-equity improvement in the shortest time, while follow-up higher-order quality enhancements can be achieved through detailed design guidelines for each pocket park. 

## Discussion

## Main findings and contributions to existing studies
Evaluating the spatial distribution of urban parks based on a single dimension is inadequate for advancing green space equity and environmental justice in well-developed urban areas , . Availability has emerged as a key prerequisite for assessing urban parks in human-oriented urban planning theory and practice , . Cultural equity, as an essential means of achieving environmental equity, is receiving increasing attention in urban development . In this study, areas within 250 m of a pocket park (approximately 5 min’ walk for the elderly) are designated as effective cultural service zones, in alignment with the Guangzhou Urban Green Space System Development Plan . Accurately and comprehensively describing park characteristics is a fundamental step in urban park analysis . Scholars have individually assessed park attributes such as accessibility, recreational quality, and usage intensity , , . This research integrates these factors and uses them to evaluate the cultural services of pocket parks. From the subdistrict governance perspective, the study evaluates the cultural opportunities and perceptions of parks, aiming to uncover the humanistic and cultural features of pocket parks and investigate the role of local culture in achieving green equity.
In the case of Guangzhou’s Liwan Old Town, this research found that there is a degree of spatial mismatch between cultural opportunities and cultural perception in pocket parks. Field surveys revealed a striking paradox: many everyday behaviours that exactly match Lingnan lifestyle traditions are still carried out in cramped or makeshift spaces instead of in the nearby pocket parks. Children practise martial arts on narrow inter-residential open space, elderly men bring their own stools to curb side tree-pits to play Chinese chess, and antique enthusiasts occupy pedestrian lanes to set up stalls. All these activities could naturally occur in pocket parks that already exist within 250 m, yet the parks are bypassed. The observed mismatch is statistically consistent with the H-O-L-P pattern (High opportunity, Low perception) identified in Section " ": physical supply is adequate, but outdated paving, broken seating, lack of shade and absence of management also park form and function design that is inconsistent with residents’ needs reduce the parks’ functional utility and cultural cues below those of self-selected “hard” spaces. This behavioural evidence reinforces the conclusion that maintenance quality, micro-climate design and place-keeping—not merely presence of facilities—govern whether cultural opportunities are actually perceived and used. Urban practitioners therefore need to shift from a “build-and-forget” mentality to continuous, behaviour-oriented stewardship if pocket parks are to realise their full cultural service potential.
As a robustness check, this research also finds that the spatial equity of cultural perception in pocket parks is higher than that of cultural opportunities. Compared with Singapore, where the spatial equity of park environmental quality exceeded that of recreational opportunity, Liwan’s pocket parks also demonstrated higher equity in cultural perception than in cultural opportunity. However, Liwan’s cultural perception equity is even higher—and its opportunity equity even lower—than that of Singapore. This may result from the stronger sense of community identity and bottom-up cultural participation among Liwan residents, which can enhance perceived cultural experience despite the uneven distribution of physical facilities. Conversely, the old town’s dense fabric and limited developable land exacerbate the spatial concentration of cultural supply, thereby widening the gap in opportunity equity. This also may because perception can be enhanced in the short term through design improvements, programming and community engagement, whereas opportunity is constrained by the fixed stock of space and long-term resource allocation. Consequently, raising perception often becomes the more actionable policy lever, which in turn explains why its equity performance currently surpasses that of opportunity. Compared with Nanjing, where pocket parks display high flexibility but highly uneven use intensity, Liwan shows another form of mismatch between supply and use potential—namely a “high-opportunity–low-perception” pattern. This likely reflects the inefficiency in converting abundant cultural resources into experiential quality, largely influenced by the aging park infrastructure, limited activity diversity, and the conservative use behaviours prevalent in the old urban neighbourhoods. This validity and external consistency support the soundness of our metrics and research design. The observed context-specific heterogeneity is likely attributed to variations in regional park supply characteristics, evaluation criteria, and data collection focus. Within this overall consistent pattern, the Liwan-specific spatial mismatch emerges as the innovative contribution of this research. It is important to note that the effective service area of parks in this research covers only half of the Liwan old town area, highlighting considerable room for improvement in the spatial equity of pocket park distribution. So, what has led to the emergence of phenomena such as the above in Liwan Old Town? First, the district’s extremely high built density means that any urban-renewal cycle is necessarily protracted; consequently, the aggregate service area of pocket parks can expand only incrementally. Second, limited public budgets have so far been channelled primarily into boosting the sheer number of facilities, leaving a deficit of long-term stewardship plans and post-occupancy management. Third, earlier green-infrastructure schemes were overwhelmingly top-down, task-driven projects in which designers and administrators engaged minimally with residents and other stakeholders; this communication gap produced a shallow reading of local culture and a misalignment with actual daily needs. Against this backdrop, this research aims to offers an evidence-based diagnostic and a set of actionable guidelines intended to accelerate future pocket park delivery, enhance cultural relevance, and embed sustained community co-management within Liwan’s ongoing renewal agenda.

## Implications for urban park planning and policy
This research provides several insights into the planning and policymaking of Liwan old town and also pocket parks in old urban areas with rich local cultural atmospheres. Firstly, the evaluation system proposed in this study uses existing community governance units (subdistricts) as spatial units to assess cultural opportunities and perceptions in pocket parks. The results formed can guide and help urban green space planning and management teams in Liwan accurately identify spatial clusters with low cultural service levels in pocket parks, leveraging residents’ cultural belonging and utilizing grassroots governance advantages to promote urban green equity through bottom-up improvements. It provides an empirical basis for ongoing and future renewal projects in aging residential compounds across all subdistricts, guiding planners to deliberately infill new pocket parks within currently unserved catchments and to retrofit or refurbish underperforming ones to raise their cultural service quality. Beyond the site-specific findings, this study offers a new lens for evaluating pocket parks: we hope it will inspire other old towns and historic districts to embed local-heritage considerations into their own pocket parks performance-assessment systems, and to develop locally attuned evaluation methods and details built upon—yet not bounded by—our framework. Secondly, given the limitations of land resources, enhancing the urban green space system and park network in park planning aligns with the development and construction needs of old urban areas. Second, land scarcity makes it imperative to treat pocket parks as capillaries that extend the broader green and cultural network , . Incorporating slow traffic systems and community life circles into the planning to enhance the spatial equity of cultural services is a key aspect of this approach .By mediating among stakeholders, we can gradually open selected perimeter walls, reconnect cul-de-sacs within housing estates, and install a coherent way-finding system infused with local iconography. Pocket parks then function as stepping-stone nodes that lead residents from their doorsteps to higher-tier parks, metro stops or major cultural facilities—sometimes acting as forecourts or annexes to those institutions. This strategy enriches the district-wide slow-recreation experience, increases visitation rates and, crucially, seeds more everyday activities in the very spaces that the analysis has identified as under-perceived. Third, pocket parks should be explicitly woven into place-based cultural and heritage conservation strategies. This starts with establishing a shared knowledge base: the planning authority should convene standing workshops that bring together scholars, local residents, designers and heritage agencies to co-produce a Technical Guidelines for the Design of Lingnan Cultural Pocket Parks. The guidelines offer clear positive and negative lists—approved indigenous materials, motifs, plant palettes and construction details alongside inappropriate designs and common easily confused misexpressions—to guide future projects. Yet culture is not static. Beyond celebrating the old, parks can incorporate changeable, digital infrastructures that allow new cultural expressions to be updated seasonally. Long-term maintenance contracts should budget for periodic content refresh, ensuring users encounter novelty on their visit and keeping the transmission of local culture alive rather than frozen in time. To further transform perception, heritage authorities can sponsor quarterly micro-events co-curated with residents, such as excerpts of Cantonese opera, dragon-boat craftsmanship demonstrations. Over time, such programming reframes pocket parks from generic green plots into living cultural exhibition spaces embedded in everyday life.
This research shows that pocket parks play a crucial role in enhancing the cultural opportunities and perceptions in urban parks of old urban areas. Therefore, it is recommended that in high-density, fully developed, and land-constrained old urban areas, pocket parks be constructed with localized cultural characteristics during urban renewal processes. These parks should be standardized and incorporated into the urban green space planning system to promote the equitable distribution of green spaces and cultural services, echoing similar proposals by previous scholars , , . Moreover, when building new pocket parks, careful consideration should be given to their location and scale. While increasing the density of pocket parks can enhance the probability of equitable distribution of access to cultural resources , , excessive density in certain areas leads to overlapping effective service areas. It increases construction costs and does not contribute to improving the spatial equity of cultural services provided by pocket parks.
In the process of this study, the researcher invited 50 residents of the Liwan Old Town to participate in the qualitative factor scoring questionnaire. Also, during the field investigation, conducted random interviews with residents to understand their perceptions of pocket parks, their living experiences, and their insights into Lingnan culture. Although these interviews were not quantified into specific data, they played a significant role in the research process, providing valuable qualitative information and practical support. Through engagement with community members, the research not only enhanced the relevance and practical value of the findings but also made the research outcomes more closely aligned with the actual needs and cultural backgrounds of the local residents. Additionally, the study has initiated preliminary communication with the local street management department, aiming to establish a committee composed of residents, experts, designers, and managers to collaboratively discuss future community improvement projects, including but not limited to pocket park renovations, thereby translating community insights into efficient, equitable and sustainable urban interventions.

## Limitations and future research prospects
While this research conducted a comprehensive evaluation of the internal construction conditions of urban green spaces and regional cultural characteristics to measure cultural opportunities and cultural perceptions, introducing new attempts in research perspectives and methods, several limitations must be acknowledged before the findings are translated into policy, also it is hoped that these limitations can be gradually addressed in subsequent research efforts.
•Data Bias from Single-Time Seasonal Observations and Questionnaire Survey.
The study’s data collection was conducted over a specific period and season, and peak usage periods were selected for analysis. This approach may introduce bias due to temporal and seasonal variations in cultural activities and park usage. The observed patterns may not fully reflect the broader cultural and social dynamics of the study area. Additionally, the questionnaire survey, while aiming to include a diverse range of participants, was limited in sample size relative to the entire Liwan Old Town population. This limitation may restrict the representativeness of the findings and the ability to generalize the results to the broader population. Some pocket parks were also under renovation or construction during the research period, limiting their inclusion in the survey scope, and their coverage rates remain dynamic and subject to change.
• Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP).
The study utilized subdistricts as a unit of analysis, which facilitates the presentation of results at an administrative level and enhances the likelihood of practical application. However, this approach also introduces the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP), which can influence the interpretation of spatial patterns and findings. To mitigate this limitation, the study restricted the use of subdistrict boundaries to the final aggregation and visualization stages. All spatial analyses, including service-area delineation, index calculation, and statistical modelling, were conducted at the pocket park level or on continuous surfaces covering the entire Liwan Old Town. Despite these efforts, the MAUP remains a limitation that may influence the interpretation of the results.
• Theoretical Simplification of Models and Indicators.
Culture is inherently qualitative and context-dependent. The research attempted to quantify cultural opportunities and perceptions using various indicators, aims to approximate the real situation as much as possible, yet there are unavoidable limitations. Like in the “Cultural Coefficient” model the population aged 0–14 and over 60 years old is used as the statistical basis for the expected cultural preferences in pocket parks, this is somewhat biased. The indicators are expected to materialize culture; however, they still cannot fully capture the complex and multi-faceted nature of cultural experiences, which are influenced by a variety of social, historical, and environmental factors.
• Consideration of Other Higher-level Public Spaces and Facilities.
The study focused primarily on pocket parks, but the cultural and heritage landscape of Liwan Old Town includes a diverse range of higher-level public spaces, such as city parks, squares, museums, and pedestrian streets—that also provide cultural services and occupy non-trivial spatial footprints. Their exclusion may over-state the inequity attributable to pocket parks alone (e.g., inflate Gini coefficients) and areas apparently underserved by pocket parks may in fact be adequately served by other existing facilities.

## Conclusion
Previous studies have rarely analysed the spatial equity of micro-scale urban parks, particularly regarding local cultural influence. Few have linked green space equity to cultural social justice. This research addresses these gaps by introducing a methodological framework. Using subdistricts as spatial units and a 250-m walking distance, it proposes a pocket park opportunity measurement method based on cultural coefficients and constructs a pocket park cultural perception evaluation system that integrates the Semantic Differential Method (SD), the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP), and the entropy method. This system provides a comprehensive measure of both the cultural services available to residents and their perceptions of these services. A case study in Guangzhou Liwan old town validates its feasibility. The approach is adaptable to old town with rich cultural context in other cities, and enhances living environments through grassroots governance.
The research found that the spatial equity of cultural perception in pocket parks is higher than that of cultural opportunities. Future urban green space planning in Guangzhou should prioritize supplementing micro green spaces in areas with lower service coverage, improving residents’ access to cultural activities. Additionally, a spatial mismatch exists between cultural opportunities and perceptions. In areas with high cultural opportunity density, cultural perception is relatively low. In compact, old urban areas, incorporating local cultural characteristics into urban renewal, enhancing pocket park construction, and leveraging local culture in park upgrades can maximize cultural service value, improving the quality of urban green spaces.
