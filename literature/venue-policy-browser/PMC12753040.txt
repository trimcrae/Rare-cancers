## ABSTRACT
This scoping review aimed to map the landscape of medical education research (MER) in India from 2016 to 2021, focusing on teaching–learning methodologies. The objectives were to identify demographic trends, analyze research methodologies and their shifts, and inform future research priorities. It was conducted following updated Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines. A systematic search was performed across MEDLINE, Education Resources Information Center (ERIC), and Google Scholar, supplemented by hand-searched journals to select original English-language research articles that addressed teaching–learning methodologies within Indian medical colleges. Data on pertinent variables were extracted. The methodological quality of these studies was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool. From 348 articles screened, 238 met the inclusion criteria. Publication output showed a peak in 2021 (n = 60). A majority of studies originated from private medical colleges (n = 137, 57.6%). The department of Physiology was the most frequent contributor. Undergraduate students were the main participants (n = 205, 86.1%). Most studies were classroom-based (n = 172, 72.3%) and used quantitative, perception-based methods (n = 208, 87.4%). Only 56 studies (23.5%) were rated as high quality. Ethical approval and informed consent were reported in 79.4% (n = 189) and 66.0% (n = 157) of studies, respectively. MER in India has grown in volume since 2016, but significant improvements are needed in methodological rigor, ethical transparency, and instrument validation. It is dominated by quantitative, perception-based studies focusing on undergraduates. Future progress requires in-depth training in research methodologies, improved reporting standards, and stronger institutional support.

## Introduction
Medical education research (MER) is critical for advancing pedagogical practices, curriculum reforms, and educational outcomes in healthcare training. While MER has a long-standing global presence, and India is among the largest global producers of health professionals, the volume and quality of MER publications remain disproportionately low relative to its faculty capacity. The four National Teacher Training Centres (NTTCs) dominated early MER publications.[ ] However, a notable surge in MER publications occurred post 2005,[ ] which can be attributed to regulatory reforms, institutional initiatives, and evolving academic expectations.
The launch of project-based faculty development programs such as the Regional FAIMER Fellowships in the year 2005 and the introduction of project-based advanced courses in medical education (ACME) in 2014 fostered a culture of scholarly dissemination.[ ] The launch of the Competency-Based Medical Education (CBME) curriculum in 2019 introduced transformative opportunities and challenges, necessitating rigorous exploration of implementation barriers, pedagogical innovations, and faculty preparedness.[ , , ] Further, during Covid-19 pandemic, faculty explored alternatives to classroom teaching like e-learning and blended learning. These catalysts have expanded research opportunities and resultant publications.[ ] Despite growing research output, systemic barriers persist, such as methodological limitations and uneven institutional engagement.[ , ]
This evolving context necessitates a clear understanding of India’s current MER landscape. Existing reviews, while valuable, are often fragmented. Dongre and Deshmukh’s[ ] analysis (2006–2015) found most MER studies were quantitative surveys of teaching–learning (T-L) methods, primarily involving undergraduates, but predates full ACME impact, CBME, and the pandemic. More recent reviews, like Jensen et al.’s[ ] on digital education and Kumar et al.’s[ ] on pandemic-era MER, are thematically or temporally constrained, often by database limitations. This highlights the need for a comprehensive synthesis of MER focused on T-L methodologies in India for the critical post-2015 period. A preliminary search of PubMed, Education Resources Information Center (ERIC), the online database for educational research, and JBI Evidence Synthesis, the journal published by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI), was conducted in November 2021 to identify the aforementioned targeted reviews, but no current or in-progress comprehensive scoping review specifically mapping the breadth of T-L methodologies in Indian MER publications from 2016 to 2021 was found underway.
Scoping reviews, unlike systematic reviews that answer specific comparative questions, aim to map existing literature, identify key concepts, and highlight knowledge gaps.[ , , ] Given the broad and evolving nature of research on T-L methodologies in Indian medical education post 2015, we felt that a scoping review is the most suitable approach.
Our research question, therefore, was, “What are the trends in MER focused on T-L methodologies in India from 2016 to 2021 regarding research themes, Institutional and departmental contributions, type of participants, methodologies, outcome measures, analysis and overall quality?” with the following objectives: 1. To analyze demographic trends (research distribution across public/private institutions, disciplines, and stakeholder groups), 2. To map emerging themes (dominant topics, methodologies, and institutional contributions in MER publications), 3. To assess methodological shifts (changes in research methodologies, with a particular focus on incorporation of qualitative and mixed methods approaches in MER), and 4. To inform future research (areas in MER that require further exploration).
Our scoping review is distinct in its comprehensive focus on all T-L methods during the key period of educational reform, its broader search strategy, and its aim to map diverse characteristics including methodological quality and institutional contributions. Its findings are intended to inform policymakers, educators, and researchers, providing directional insights, supporting evidence-informed educational policy, and aligning MER with India’s contemporary curricular reforms.

## Materials and Methods
This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the updated JBI Methodology for scoping reviews[ , ] and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines.[ , ] The study protocol was developed by all authors but was not prospectively registered. Being a scoping review of published literature, an Institutional Ethics Committee/Review Board approval was not mandatory and hence not taken.
The review focused on original research articles published between January 1, 2016 and December 31, 2021. It considered both qualitative and quantitative sources pertaining to studies undertaken in Indian medical colleges (government/public, private, or other recognized medical training institutions) to explore T-L methodologies as a primary focus. These included studies investigating a wide range of pedagogical approaches, strategies, and tools, such as blended learning, e-learning and online learning, flipped classrooms, simulation-based learning (e.g., skills lab, high-fidelity simulations), traditional classroom teaching methods (e.g., lectures, tutorials), laboratory-based learning and practical sessions, self-directed learning (SDL), case-based learning (CBL), problem-based learning (PBL), team-based learning (TBL), integrated learning approaches, studies investigating T-L aspects of AETCOM (Attitude, Ethics, and Communication) module, clinical skills training methodologies and interprofessional education (IPE) involving undergraduate or postgraduate medical students or interns or professionals such as faculty members, or medical consultants.
This scoping review included quantitative studies having experimental designs, analytical observational studies, and descriptive observational studies, provided they reported primary data. Qualitative studies that focused on qualitative data, including, but not limited to, designs such as phenomenology, grounded theory, ethnography, qualitative description, and action research and mixed methods studies that explicitly combined quantitative and qualitative research components to investigate T-L methods, were also included. Short communications or letters to the editor were considered for inclusion only if they presented original data related to MER on T-L methodologies and followed a defined research design.
Sources were excluded if they met one or more of the following criteria: articles not published in the English language, studies focused exclusively on health professions education outside of medicine and if the publication type was editorials, opinion articles or personal viewpoints, commentaries, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, narrative reviews, or other forms of literature reviews, descriptive articles without a clearly defined research methodology or primary data collection (e.g., simple descriptions of courses or programs without evaluation or research components), conference proceedings, abstracts, or posters (unless they were subsequently published as full original research articles meeting other inclusion criteria) and book chapters or books. Other exclusion criteria were articles published outside the specified timeframe, studies not conducted within India or not pertaining to Indian medical education, and studies where T-L methodologies in medical education were not a primary focus.
The search to locate published studies was initiated on February 7, 2022, and the last information was collected on July 7, 2023. A three-step search strategy was utilized in this review as recommended by JBI.[ ] First, an initial limited search of MEDLINE (via PubMed), Google Scholar, and ERIC was undertaken to identify articles on the topic. The text words contained in the titles and abstracts of relevant articles, and the index terms used to describe the articles were used to develop a full search strategy. Then the full search strategy, including all identified keywords and index terms, was adapted for each included database and/or information source. No specific tool was used to identify keywords or index terms, filters, and validation processes. The search strategy was peer-reviewed by two reviewers (HS and SC). Specific validation processes for the search strategy were not conducted beyond peer review. Last, the reference sections of all studies selected for inclusion were then screened for additional studies.
The decision to include only the articles published in English was taken because it is the predominant language for peer-reviewed MER publications in India. Our extensive search yielded no relevant MER articles published in other languages meeting our inclusion criteria, confirming that this approach reflects the accessible scholarly output in this field and aligns with the linguistic proficiency of the reviewers.
The following electronic databases were searched: MEDLINE (via PubMed), ERIC (via ERIC website), and Google Scholar. For Google Scholar, the first 200 results for key search term combinations were screened. Targeted searches of thematic issues of relevant journals were also performed. Key Indian medical education journals, like the National Medical Journal of India (NMJI) and the Journal of Clinical and Diagnostic Research (JCDR), were hand-searched for the entire review period due to their consistent publication of Indian MER. Other journals were considered for hand-searching based on their relevance to the topic as identified during the review. In addition, Google search was done for scoping reviews on medical education and their cross references were screened. The relevant articles meeting the eligibility criteria were included. Sources of unpublished studies and gray literature, such as conference proceedings or relevant institutional websites, were not systematically searched in this review.
A comprehensive list of search terms and the full search strategy for MEDLINE (PubMed), which served as the template adapted for other databases, is provided in . (https://docs.google.com/document/d/1526RrVOUqapU309LAmHL0IDmlbld3UaSVJrN73hSmII/edit?usp=sharing).
Following the search, all identified citations were collated, and duplicates were removed using manual checking within Microsoft Excel. A pilot test of the screening process was conducted by two reviewers (JM and RN) on a sample of 50 titles and abstracts to ensure consistency in applying the eligibility criteria.
The screening process followed a two-step approach. Two reviewers (SC and HS) independently screened titles and abstracts of all retrieved articles against the eligibility criteria. The screening and selection process was managed using Microsoft Excel. Studies that passed this initial screening underwent a full-text review for final inclusion. This was done by three reviewers individually (JM,RN and HS). Reasons for exclusion of sources of evidence at the full-text stage that did not meet the inclusion criteria were recorded [ ].(https://docs.google.com/document/d/1uOjiWzpkyoxA82asgv2LQAvFMIqhVXvdX4_xVLzhvPg/edit?usp=sharing)
Inter-rater reliability for the screening process was assessed on a random sample of 20 articles independently reviewed by two authors. Discrepancies identified during the reliability assessment were resolved through discussion and consensus with a third reviewer.
Data were extracted from articles included in this scoping review [see ] (https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1c4UdAh_27NuYJ-ZI1wtr-gesKz4uka6YK_HScQp4k6M/edit?usp=sharing) by three independent reviewers (JM, HS and RN) using a data extraction tool developed by the reviewers for this study. The data extraction tool was piloted on a sample of 20 included studies by the two primary data extractors (JM and HS), and minor revisions were made to enhance clarity and consistency. For example, ‘perception’ as a frequently assessed outcome was explicitly added and defined in the charting tool following the pilot. The extracted variables were chosen to align with key trends in medical education research.
The extracted variables included: 1. Concept (T-L methods as per search strategy); 2. Trends over time and publication sources (Journal type, year of publication) and authorship patterns; 3. Context (Type of institutions in which studies were conducted, their state-wise location and authorship pattern); 4. Study participants; 5. Study characteristics/methods (Study purpose, Study type, e.g., interventional, descriptive, qualitative, mixed methods), study designs, discipline/subject area, instructional setting, for example, classroom, hospital, skills lab, community, part of project and affiliation, and funding source); 6. Use of perceptions and instrument validity; and 7. Ethical considerations (Reported ethical approval and informed consent status).
While critical appraisal of individual sources of evidence is generally not a mandatory component of scoping reviews,[ ] this review included an assessment of methodological rigor to provide further insight into the landscape of MER in India. The methodological rigor was assessed by two independent reviewers (JM and HS) using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT), 2018 version.[ ] Discrepancies in MMAT scoring were resolved by consensus or by a third reviewer (RN). Studies were subsequently categorized as high, moderate, or low quality based on the number of MMAT criteria met. A score of 0–1 was categorized as low quality, 2–3 moderate quality, and 4–5 high quality.[ ] Any disagreements that arose between the reviewers during data extraction were resolved through discussion or with an additional arbitrating reviewer (SC). Authors of articles were not contacted for missing or additional data.
The evidence is presented in a way that directly responds to the review’s objective and questions. A narrative summary accompanies the tabulated and/or charted results and describes how the results relate to the review’s objective of mapping the landscape of MER in India concerning T-L methodologies. Quantitative data, such as the frequency of study types, designs, participant groups, research themes, and institutional characteristics, were summarized using counts and percentages. Trends in these characteristics over the review period were analyzed. The data are presented graphically (e.g., bar graphs, trend charts) and in tabular form to provide a clear overview of the scope and nature of the literature. All descriptive analyses and data visualizations were performed using Google Sheets and Microsoft Excel.

## Results

## Study inclusion
No deviation from the initial protocol occurred during the conduct of the review. The results of the search and the study inclusion process are presented in a PRISMA-ScR flow diagram[ ] [ ]. Following application of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 110 articles were excluded for various reasons [ and SA2]. Thus, for final analysis, 238 articles were included (SA3).
 The initial proportional agreement for inter-rater reliability was high (90%), and Cohen’s Kappa coefficient demonstrated substantial agreement among raters beyond chance (κ = 0.80), consistent with the criteria of Landis and Koch.[ ]

## Review findings according to variables

## T-L methods as per search strategy
The articles were categorized based on the search terms used during data retrieval. A total of 144 (60.5%) studies were identified using the general search term “teaching-learning methods.” The remaining articles were retrieved using more specific keywords such as “COVID” (n = 39; 16.39%), “flipped and classroom” (n = 20; 8.4%), “integrated learning” (n = 14; 5.88%), “blended learning” (n = 10; 4.2%), “simulation” (n = 6; 2.52%), and “AETCOM” (n = 5; 2.1%) in combination with teaching-related terms.

## Trends over time and publication sources
All articles were original research. The number increased from 43 in 2016 to a peak of 60 in 2021 as seen in . The lowest publication year was 2018 with 23 articles. Most studies were published in discipline-specific journals such as the Indian Journal of Pharmacology (n = 106, 44.54%) or interdisciplinary medical journals like the Journal of Postgraduate Medicine (n = 96, 40.34%). A smaller proportion appeared in medical education-specific journals (n = 36, 15.13%).
 

## State-wise distribution, institutional distribution, and authorship patterns
 depicts the number of publications originated from various states across India. We found out percentage of medical colleges in the state (till 2021) and percentage of publications. A comparison of the proportion of medical colleges with corresponding publication output revealed notable variations across states [ ]. Karnataka accounted for the highest percentage of medical colleges, followed by Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, and Tamil Nadu. However, when publication output was considered, Maharashtra (publication-to-institution ratio of 1.93:1) led, followed by Karnataka (ratio 1.26:1). In contrast, Uttar Pradesh and Tamil Nadu, despite having a high number of institutions, showed relatively low publication percentages with ratios of 0.66:1 and 0.67:1, respectively. Interestingly, union territories and smaller states such as Delhi, Pondicherry, Chhattisgarh, and Assam demonstrated higher publication productivity relative to their institutional base. In contrast, states like Telangana, Madhya Pradesh, and Andhra Pradesh exhibited lower publication output relative to their institutional representation.
 Of the included studies, 101 were conducted in the Government or Municipal institutions, while 137 originated from private medical colleges.
Single-author publications were uncommon. Most studies were multiauthored. Seventy of them were from a single department, 66 from multiple departments within the same institution, 31 were interinstitutional collaborations within the same department, and 46 were from multiple departments belonging to multiple institutions [ ].
Some collaborations lacked clarity regarding the nature of contribution—for instance, studies conducted at a single institution but involving co-authors from different institutions or departments without stating rationale for their inclusion. A few publications included international collaborators (n = 6), though only one study had cross-national data collection (Sr. No. 211; SA 4). In 11 studies, departmental affiliations of co-authors were not specified [ ].

## Study participants
More than 86% studies involved undergraduate students. Other groups included postgraduates (n = 14), interns (n = 6), and faculty (n = 8). A small number included mixed populations (e.g., UG + faculty, or PG + practitioners) [ ].

## Study purpose, types, and designs
Almost all studies (97.9%) stated a clear purpose [ ]. A minor inconsistency was noted where objectives varied across sections within the same article. Authors inconsistently reported study types (SA 4). Among the 238 studies, only 108 explicitly stated a study type [ ]. Upon reviewer reclassification, 157 were found to be interventional, 72 descriptive, 6 mixed-methods, and 2 qualitative. Some studies labeled as observational or descriptive (e.g., Sr. No. 20,60,63,88,122,165,169,201,221,233) involved educational interventions indicating misclassification.
As shown in , study designs were not mentioned in 106 studies. Stated designs included RCTs (n = 4). Two studies were labeled as randomized educational study and randomized cross-sectional study. The other designs mentioned were experimental, quasi-experimental, cross-sectional surveys, and case-control. Only two of the five mixed-method studies mentioned by the authors clearly described sequential designs. For two studies wherein study type was not mentioned by the author, the design was described as mixed (Sr No. 62; Sa 4) and sequential (Sr. No. 124; SA 4). The former was an interventional study with pre-post design, and the latter was a mixed method study (though not mentioned by the authors) with sequential design. There was a mislabeling; for example, some quasi-experimental designs were mislabeled as cross-sectional, observational, or case-control (e.g., Sr. No. 226, 60, 165,140; SA 4), and a mixed method study design was described as cross-sectional instead of sequential (Sr. No. 140; SA 4).
Single-group designs were more than double the ones with comparator groups [ ].

## Departmental representation
The most frequent contributors were departments of Physiology, Community Medicine, Pharmacology, Anatomy, and Biochemistry [ ], followed by Microbiology and Pathology. Clinical disciplines such as Paediatrics, Ophthalmology, Forensic Medicine, Obstetrics–Gynaecology, and Surgery each contributed seven or fewer than seven studies. Five studies were institutional projects not attributed to any specific department [ ]. One study (Sr. No. 49; SA 4) did not mention the name of the department/s where the study was conducted. The corresponding author’s department was identified, but departments of co-authors were not specified.

## Study sites and settings
Though 143 studies were multiauthored from different departments of the same or multiple institutions or the same department of multiple institutions, more than 90% of the studies were carried out at a single site [ ].
Study settings were predominantly classrooms (n = 172), including e-classrooms, workshops, and microteaching [ ]. Online learning was reported in 37 studies, social media involved in 9, and practical/laboratory-based learning took place in 8. Skill labs, community settings, and clinical settings such as OPDs or operation theaters were rare.

## Part of project and affiliation, and funding sources
Most studies (n = 220) were not a part of any project-based program. Ten studies were associated with FAIMER, seven with ACME, and one with the MEU training program at CMC Vellore. Only eight studies disclosed funding sources [ ].
Sources mentioned included State board of Medical Research, Kerala (Sr. No. 119, 169), Institutional Fluid Research Grant, CMC, Vellore (Sr. No. 165), Fogarty International Centre, NIH grant (Sr. No. 210), and Institutional research grants (Sr. No. 121,208,211). One of the articles (Sr. No. 61) acknowledged KS Biomed, Ahmadabad, Gujarat, India, for providing “Anatomage” virtual dissection table (SA 4).

## Use of perceptions and instrument validity
A large proportion of studies (n = 208) collected participant perceptions (SA 4)—most commonly from UG students (n = 191), faculty (n = 42), interns (n = 4), and PG/residents (n = 8). In 58 studies, the term ‘perception’ appeared in the title. A number of studies from different parts of India focused on students reporting their preferences regarding T-L Methods and included first to final year UG students (Sr. No. T-L 3,5, 26,46,48, 56,64, 87,88,110 etc.; SA 3).
Apart from use of questionnaires to collect perceptions, other methods like focus group discussions, interview, SWOT analysis, and informal feedback were used. In one study, after collecting questionnaire-based feedback, NGT was used with faculty as participants.
Among 208 studies, 113 did not mention whether the instrument used to collect feedback/perceptions was tested for reliability and validity [ ]. In the remaining 95, no details were provided by 23. Two studies used validated tools. Methods used for validation included peer review (n = 17), expert input (n = 7), pilot testing (n = 11), literature review (n = 1), face validity (n = 1), and interview (n = 1) or their combinations.

## Ethical approvals and informed consent
Out of 238 articles, the majority mentioned regarding institutional ethics committee (IEC) approval, though less than 1/4th included the approval number (SA 4). No mention of IEC approval was found in 46 studies. Informed consent was documented in 157 studies. Eighty publications did not mention about informed consent [ ].

## Quality of studies
This is depicted in . The percentage of moderate-high quality of articles remained in the range of 53.5–66.67% during 5 years except in the year 2018, wherein its percentage decreased to 47.82. Quality of articles was the highest in 2019, but there was no further improvement with the progressing years [ ].
 

## Discussion
This scoping review aimed to map the landscape of MER focused on T-L methodologies carried out in Indian medical institutions from 2016 to 2021. Of the 368 studies initially retrieved, 238 were found eligible for final analysis, reflecting a growing scholarly interest in T-L innovations, particularly in the post-CBME era.

## T-L methods as per search strategy
The review shows extensive research on diverse T-L strategies, with most publications focusing on general innovations and online learning during COVID-19. In contrast, studies on the AETCOM module were few, likely due to its recent introduction (2019), challenges in assessing attitudinal change, and publication delays. The publication surge in 2021 likely reflects the pandemic-driven shift to online teaching and the urgency to share pedagogical adaptations, a trend also noted by Raynaud et al.[ ]

## Trends over time and publication sources
Beyond medical education journals, specialty (e.g., Indian Paediatrics) and general journals (e.g., Journal of Clinical and Diagnostic Research) also publish educational research. While encouraging, issues like rapid publication, weak methodology, and limited peer review persist. Assigning trained reviewers with relevant expertise can enhance article quality.[ , ]

## State-wise distribution, institutional distribution, and authorship patterns
Private institutions produced more publications than government ones, reflecting their greater number in India—a trend also seen in 2006–2015.[ ] As of 2021, Karnataka had the most medical colleges but ranked second to Maharashtra in publication output. States like Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, and Uttar Pradesh have recently expanded medical colleges, where limited institutional support and facilities may hinder MER scholarship. Cameron et al.[ ] have revealed the same reasons in their scoping review on author development. Clear guidelines on available support and the role of educational scholarship in career advancement, as suggested by Van Melle et al.,[ ] could encourage faculty participation.
Single-author studies were few but raise concerns given the collaborative nature of most educational interventions. Encouragingly, multi-institutional studies are increasing, reflecting growing collaboration, supported by recent NMC authorship guidelines.[ ] However, many such studies lacked details on author roles or affiliations. We support existing recommendations by ICMJE[ ] and high-impact journals urging authors for author contribution statement, which would enhance transparency and justify collaboration.[ , ] Instances where the credited institution differed from the research site highlight the need to acknowledge the actual study venue to uphold academic integrity.

## Study participants
Most studies in this review involved UG students at single institutions, mainly in classroom settings—similar to Dongre and Deshmukh’s findings.[ ] Research with students raises ethical concerns as they may feel pressured to participate. Hence, vigilant ethical oversight by a well-trained IEC is essential.[ ] While over 75% of studies reported IEC approval, 46 did not, raising compliance concerns. Ethics clearance is also required for survey questionnaire and secondary data analysis and before publication.[ ] Across programs and institutions, students consistently preferred interactive, student-centered methods, supporting evidence that active learning enhances understanding and critical thinking.[ , , ] This underscores the need to shift from lecture-heavy teaching toward active engagement.

## Study purpose, types, and designs
Over the 5 years reviewed, quantitative methods dominated, with limited use of qualitative or mixed approaches—likely due to researchers’ familiarity with empirical biomedical designs. However, qualitative and mixed methods are essential for capturing learning nuances and richer feedback.[ , , ] Many studies assessing perceptions via questionnaires would have benefited from qualitative exploration.
Misclassification of study types was common; interventions were often mislabeled as observational or cross-sectional, underscoring the need for better understanding of research design in MER.[ ] Few studies used true mixed-methods designs, and those labeled as randomized trials often lacked methodological detail on randomization, contamination control, or ethics.[ ] Such inconsistencies hinder reproducibility and interpretation, highlighting the need for structured MER training and appropriate study design selection.
Most studies lacked comparator groups. This limits validity despite larger sample sizes, as noted by Gruppen.[ ] Small-scale or pilot studies predominated, usually reporting positive results. Future research should include larger or longitudinal cohorts and promote multi-institutional, multidisciplinary collaborations to strengthen evidence and generalizability.

## Departmental representation
Preclinical departments, particularly Physiology, produced more publications than para-clinical and clinical departments. Clinical faculty often prioritize patient care and biomedical research over MER, as noted by others.[ , , ] While an earlier review[ ] found Community Medicine leading in publications, Physiology has since surpassed it.

## Part of project and affiliation, and funding sources
Project-based faculty development programs like FAIMER and ACME encourage scholarly dissemination of educational projects, yet few reach publication. Strengthening mentorship and institutional support[ , ] may enhance conversion into peer-reviewed outputs.
Funding for MER came from varied sources—intramural, state, and international—but the number of funded projects remains abysmally low, echoing earlier findings.[ ] To foster high-quality MER publications that inform policy, regulatory authorities must provide clearer guidelines and stronger funding support, as also emphasized by Dongre and Pandya.[ ]

## Use of perceptions and instrument validity
Most studies assessed T-L intervention effectiveness through student or faculty perceptions, aligning with Level 1 of Kirkpatrick’s model,[ ] similar to Dongre and Deshmukh.[ ] Few progressed to Level 2, evaluating knowledge or skill gains. Future studies should extend to organizational-level outcomes and adopt longitudinal designs to capture sustained impact across cohorts. While perception surveys provide insights, combining them with objective measures (performance, retention, critical thinking) would strengthen evaluations. Use of validated tools was inconsistent—many reported only Cronbach’s alpha without validity testing, risking unreliable results.[ ] To ensure the reliability and validity of findings, authors should consistently report psychometric properties when using customized or modified instruments.

## Statistical shortcomings
Several issues emerged regarding statistical approaches in MER studies. Many lacked clarity on the expected educational impact of interventions—statistical significance alone may not imply practical relevance.[ , ] Researchers should specify anticipated improvements to ensure meaningful interpretation.[ ] Statistical tests were sometimes applied indiscriminately; analyses should focus on predefined outcomes as multiple unadjusted tests increase false-positive risk.[ , ] Methodological mismatches were common—for example, omitting repeated measures ANOVA or misusing parametric tests—compromising validity.[ , ] Key descriptors, including score ranges, confidence intervals, and effect sizes, were often missing, limiting interpretation.[ , ] Sample size estimation was rarely reported, undermining power and validity in hypothesis-driven research.[ , ]

## Quality of projects
MMAT (2018) appraisal[ ] revealed that most studies were of low to moderate quality, with common gaps in study design, methodological justification, and reporting detail. Strengthening institutional research capacity is essential to enhance MER standards in India. Echoing Gruppen,[ ] advancing MER quality is crucial for improving healthcare practice and patient care.

## Gaps observed and directions for future research
During the 5-year review period, many studies focused on e-learning, largely driven by the pandemic, while fewer addressed integrated teaching or simulations. There is a need for deeper research on simulations—such as immersive models, standardized patients, and their impact on clinical and soft skills, as well as patient outcomes. Social media-based learning for autonomy, reflection, and collaboration also remains underexplored. Most studies were discipline-based and evaluated perceptions or knowledge/skill gains. Future research should emphasize how students acquire disciplinary concepts and thinking. Theoretical frameworks were often absent; educational interventions should include these, ensure rigorous evaluation, and integrate scholarship with practice.

## Recommendations
Based on our findings, a three-pronged approach is proposed to address shortcomings in Indian MER:

## Educational level

Expand faculty training in MER methods (e.g., MEU India initiatives).
Train ethics committees on MER-specific issues, ensuring full-board review of the project proposal and questionnaires even for minimal-risk studies. No waiver in EC approval should be given as suggested by ICMR.[ ] The reviewers should also look for the provisions in the protocol to extend the benefits of T-L method to the comparator group.
Encourage journals to engage reviewers qualified in MER or train the reviewers.

## Managerial level

Promote use of reporting guidelines (e.g., STROBE, COREQ, PRISMA, SRQR, SURGE) in institutional ethics and research training programs.
Foster interinstitutional collaborations for broader applicability.
Provide technical support for study design, data analysis, and manuscript preparation via MEUs.

## Regulatory level

Strengthen recognition of MER for promotions based on quality indicators (e.g., ethics approval, design, innovation).
Mandate ethics approval or exemption reference in publications.
Develop a national database on learner diversity to support multi-institutional studies.
Enable funding for educational research through premier agencies aligned with NEP 2020.

## Limitations of the study
The study was limited by inclusion of articles from a few databases and Indian journals, excluding recent publications (2022–2024) for logistic reasons. Some studies may have been missed due to database restrictions. Stakeholder consultation, though recommended by Arksey and O’Malley[ ] and Levac et al.,[ ] was not included as it is optional under the guidelines followed. The data charting form lacked specific items on statistical methods, preventing detailed reporting of analytical practices; these were addressed generally in the discussion. The review protocol was not registered, though all PRISMA-ScR steps were rigorously followed [ ]. (https://docs.google.com/document/d/1gb7TEmLT4NVrmGnzHZKtwyhRShctvMtYlbp3R5Y9eLI/edit?usp=sharing)

## Conclusion
In conclusion, though MER output in India has grown post 2015, significant gaps persist in methodological rigor, study design, ethics, and tool validation. Strengthening institutional research capacity and training in educational research methods are essential to enhance quality and global impact. Future work should shift from descriptive to clarification studies[ ] that explain why interventions work.
