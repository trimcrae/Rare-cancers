## Abstract

## Background
Community-based rehabilitation (CBR) services are crucial for improving the prognosis of people with severe mental illness (SMI) in China and other developing countries. However, these services face limitations and a lack of stakeholders’ insights. This study examines the barriers and facilitators to implementing CBR services for people with SMI in China to inform future improvements.

## Methods
This study employed a descriptive qualitative design guided by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR). Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 32 stakeholders (16 mental health practitioners, 11 people with SMI, and 5 family caregivers) to explore their perceptions of factors influencing the implementation of CBR services for people with SMI. The interview data were analyzed and reported using the updated CFIR, with MAXQDA 2022 software facilitating the analysis process.

## Results
Within the CFIR, the following key constructs were identified as critical to CBR services implementation for people with SMI: (1) innovation—considering the evidence base, complexity, and cost of the intervention; (2) outer setting—encompassing critical incidents, local attitudes and conditions, partnerships and connections, policies and laws, financing, and external Pressure; (3) inner setting—reflecting structural characteristics, relational connections, communications, and culture; (4) individuals—encompassing both innovation deliverers and recipients; (5) process—focusing on assessing needs and engaging stakeholders.

## Conclusions
Implementing sustainable and effective CBR services for people with SMI in China and similar settings requires early stakeholder involvement and attention to several key factors. These include a robust evidence base, readily accessible and standardized CBR services, enhanced training and team development for service providers, precise service needs assessment, active community engagement, and sustained policy and financial support.

## Introduction
The increasing global burden of severe mental illness (SMI) significantly impacts individuals’ quality of life and challenges public health systems [ , ]. By 2019, an estimated 64 million individuals worldwide were diagnosed with SMI such as schizophrenia and bipolar disorder [ ]. In China, the registered SMI cases reached 6.43 million by the end of 2020, with a reported prevalence rate of 0.46% and a lifetime rate of 16.67% [ ]. Characterized by high recurrence and treatment complexities, SMI imposes a considerable psychological and economic burden on individuals, families, and society, contributing to its status as a leading cause of disability and loss of healthy life years globally [ ]. To address this challenge, the World Health Organization (WHO) has set clear goals in its Mental Health Action Plan (2013–2030) to provide comprehensive, integrated, and responsive mental health and social care services at the community level [ ]. High-income countries have demonstrated the effectiveness of shifting from hospital-centered to community-based rehabilitation (CBR) for people with SMI, emphasizing the importance of continuous care in the community to enhance social functioning and quality of life [ , ].
However, limited access to CBR services for people with SMI remains a challenge in low- and middle-income countries, including China [ ]. In China, a three-tier mental health service network comprising specialized psychiatric hospitals, psychiatric departments in general hospitals, and primary healthcare facilities has been established. However, a structural disconnect persists in practice: hospital-based rehabilitation primarily focuses on emergency care, while primary healthcare facilities are largely limited to basic medication management and follow-up. Systematic rehabilitation programs (including components such as vocational training and social skills development) are currently only piloted in a few developed regions through government-purchased social services [ ]. Data suggest that only 26.08% of mental health institutions offer dedicated rehabilitation or recreational programs, primarily focused on inpatients. This creates a significant gap for people with SMI in the community [ ]. Furthermore, resource disparities, professional shortages, and persistent social stigma contribute to uneven service quality and coverage. The government has issued key policy documents, such as the 2022 Action for the Integration of Mental Rehabilitation [ ] and the 2023 Mental Health Referral Services [ ]. These policies aim to establish a comprehensive rehabilitation service system through enhanced interdepartmental coordination, optimized welfare lottery fund allocation, and government procurement mechanisms.
While previous studies have explored the effectiveness of CBR for people with SMI and its various components [ , ], a critical gap exists. Achieving optimal outcomes of CBR services requires a focus not only on service design [ ] but also on the effectiveness of the implementation process. Particularly scarce are qualitative studies investigating the implementation of CBR services in low- and middle-income countries, especially within the Chinese context [ ]. This study addresses a pressing global and domestic need for strengthening CBR services for people with SMI, using advanced implementation science methods to systematically analyze barriers and facilitators in service implementation. By integrating our findings with practical applications, we aim to enhance rehabilitation effectiveness, equity, efficiency, scalability, and sustainability of CBR services, which will ultimately inform the development and expansion of evidence-based strategies for CBR delivery.
The Comprehensive Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) is a widely used and comprehensive theoretical framework in implementation science. The updated 2022 version of CFIR provides researchers with a systematic approach capable of thoroughly identifying barriers and facilitators, and promoting comparisons and integration across studies, fostering a bridge between research and practice [ ]. In the implementation of CBR services for people with SMI, stakeholders have distinct priorities: managers focus on policies and resource allocation, providers prioritize service sustainability and quality, while recipients emphasize service acceptability and satisfaction. Previous research [ ] has predominantly focused on provider and manager perspectives, neglecting the crucial role of people with SMI and their families. This study aims to provide an in-depth analysis of the barriers and facilitators to the implementation of CBR services for people with SMI in Nanjing, China, through qualitative interviews with providers, managers, and recipients, offering a multidimensional perspective and a solid basis for developing effective collaborative strategies and implementation plans.

## Methods

## Study participants
Participants were recruited using purposive sampling from diverse organizations (government agencies, professional associations, non-governmental organizations, hospitals, and community health services centers) in Nanjing, between October and December 2023. The study included all stakeholder groups (policymakers, practitioners, managers, people with SMI, and family caregivers) involved in the implementation of CBR service for SMI. All participants hold decision-making responsibilities and are integral to the provision of both medical and non-medical services, collectively referred to as the essential stakeholder groups.
For service providers, the inclusion criteria were as follows: they had to work in fields such as psychiatric medicine, psychiatric nursing, community medicine, or community nursing, hold a junior title or above, and have at least three years of work experience. The only exclusion criterion for this group was withdrawal from the study. For service recipients, the demand-side representatives—the group included individuals diagnosed with SMI [ ] and their primary caregivers. People with SMI were clinically stable with essentially resolved psychiatric symptoms (as assessed by psychiatrists) [ ]. Caregivers were required to—cohabitate with the people with SMI, provide primary care for the people with SMI, demonstrate awareness of the condition, have language fluency, and have no history of mental disorders. Similar to service providers, the only exclusion criterion for this group was withdrawal from the study. This study was approved by the ethics committee of Nanjing Medical University (2023-47) before the commencement of this study. All participants provided informed written consent. Our research followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [ ] guidelines. The COREQ checklist is provided in Supplementary File 1.

## Study design
To develop interview guides, we first conducted a preliminary literature review and held discussions within the project team. We developed three draft interview guides tailored to service providers, managers, and recipients respectively. These drafts were reviewed by two psychiatric specialists, and, based on their feedback, we revised the interview content by eliminating redundant questions, refining the logical flow, and restructuring the question sequence. The final interview outline was then developed, which was primarily based on the five domains of 2009 CFIR (1) intervention characteristics, (2) outer setting, (3) inner setting, (4) characteristics of individuals, and (5) process. A summary of the semi-structured interview guides is included in Supplementary File 1.

## Data collection
To identify participants, we leveraged pre-existing contacts with mental health institutions to gain access to relevant stakeholder organizations and their representatives. Following initial contact, we communicated with potential interviewees to ensure a clear understanding of the study’s objectives and context. After obtaining informed consent, we scheduled one-on-one, semi-structured interviews at mutually convenient times and locations. All formal interviews were conducted in Mandarin Chinese (Putonghua) by two trained postgraduate students (JZ and JF) under the supervision of the corresponding author, using the final version of the interview guide. The interviewers, a female and a male both trained in qualitative research, had personal experience interacting with people with SMI. Neither student was familiar with the participants nor had worked at these sites prior to the study. They secured on-site recording consent and ensured optimal audio quality by selecting appropriate interview locations. The average interview duration was 32 min for service providers and 11 min for recipients. No repeat interviews were conducted. Team members comprehensively documented the interviews with contemporaneous note-taking. Any unclear field notes were subsequently clarified and supplemented using the audio recordings. Purposive sampling was employed to recruit 9–17 key informants, with the sample size determined by aiming for a 90% code saturation threshold (defined as ≤ 10% new codes emerging relative to the cumulative code set) [ , ]. Guided by the principle of information saturation, the final sample size comprised 32 participants: 16 service providers, 11 people with SMI, and 5 family caregivers. No participants were excluded or withdrew consent during data collection.

## Analysis
All transcripts were randomly distributed among team members and coded independently by two reviewers. To ensure inter-coder reliability, reviewers then met in pairs to discuss coding assignments and resolve any discrepancies through consensus discussions. After reaching consensus, the final coded transcripts were entered into MAXQDA 2022 for data organization and analysis. We performed thematic analysis using a modified template approach, mapping identified themes to the implementation constructs of the updated CFIR. Additionally, the primary reviewer developed a comprehensive integrative transcript summary to systematize data analysis and refine theme relationships throughout the process. Transcripts were not returned to participants. The codebook mapping themes to CFIR constructs is available as Supplementary File 1.

## Results
Our study engaged a total of 32 participants, comprising 16 service providers (7 practitioners and 9 managers) and 16 service recipients (11 people with SMI, and 5 family caregivers). Tables  and present the demographics of service providers and recipients respectively. The service providers had an average age of 48.81 ± 7.37 years and all had more than 6 years of experience in mental health. Their working background included government agencies (n = 1), professional associations (n = 2), non-governmental organizations (n = 2), and hospital/clinical/community service organizations (n = 13). The people with SMI had an average age of 39.09 ± 12.97 years, and the family caregivers had an average age of 49.4 ± 16.13 years, with the duration of illness or caregiving ranging from 4 to 29 years.

## Domain 1: innovation
In this domain, participants discussed the current CBR service for SMI provided by specialized psychiatric hospitals, primary healthcare facilities, and social organizations. Specialized psychiatric hospitals offer informal rehabilitation activities like music, sports, and recreational therapies, along with life skills training within their rehabilitation wards, supervised by psychiatrists and nurses. However, these programs primarily target inpatients and lack depth and comprehensiveness. Primary healthcare facilities offered limited services within their jurisdictions, including health follow-up, physical exams and brief psychological counselling. Social organizations, which are primarily staffed by social workers and psychologists, offer a wider range of rehabilitation activities. However, they are limited in number and capacity, reaching only a minority of people with SMI. Within this domain, three key constructs—the evidence base for innovation, complexity, and cost of innovation—significantly influence CBR implementation for SMI.

## Innovation evidence-base
The effectiveness of innovation requires robust evidence. Current CBR services suffer from a weak evidence base and limited rehabilitation efficacy. PR07 noted: “There is no evidence-based support, and the standardization and efficacy of current practices remain unclear.” PR11 stated: “The programs lack professionalism.” PR09 commented: “Single-session interventions show limited effects and require sustained reinforcement.” This sentiment was echoed by P02: “I am reluctant to participate in the current rehabilitation activities (at the hospital) because I find them practically meaningless.”
However, the study identified several promising adaptive innovations. For example, informal rehabilitation activities (e.g., music, sports, recreation, life skills training and agro-industrial) conducted in wards, though lacking structured design, fostered a natural and relaxed environment that improved the emotional well-being of people with SMI. PR07 observed: “Card games, TV watching, reading, and chess have positive effects.” P06 remarked, “Exercise improves physical fitness, and playing cards helps keep the mind active.” PR12 added, “Agricultural therapy and life skills training—such as household chores, motor function exercises, and cognitive training—yield favorable outcomes.”

## Innovation complexity
Prior to initiating CBR services, a psychiatric evaluation of people with SMI is mandatory. The cumbersome assessment and service process were highlighted by PR13: “The user’s condition stability must be evaluated, those in acute phases cannot communicate effectively or benefit from rehabilitation.” Corresponding entry criteria are also established by social organizations, where safety concerns restrict access, with PR09 noting: “Daycare recipients are assessed for accident risk and mental health status; high-risk individuals are excluded.” PR14 emphasized: “For fire safety education, we prescreen participants, provide week-long acclimatization training, and ensure safety through a 1:1 support system pairing each people with SMI with a trained volunteer.” Furthermore, all stakeholders expressed concerns about program safety risks and potential liability issues in CBR services.

## Innovation cost
This section examines the affordability of innovative service, including purchasing and operating costs. Initial hardware investments are substantial, as noted by PR08:“Sports equipment requires relatively higher expenditure.” Manpower operational costs are also significant. PR15 explained: “A six-person team incurs approximately 1 million RMB annually - who should bear these costs?” Additionally, PR11 highlighted specialized venue requirements, such as “Agricultural therapy requires farmland acquisition, which involves substantial costs.”
Facilitating factors include shared resources and low-cost activities (e.g., singing and board games). PR15 noted: “Neighborhood committees typically operate activity centers for disabled and elderly populations, where resources can be shared.” PR02 explained: “People with SMI need only basic equipment (e.g., speakers and cards) to initiate self-organized activities.”

## Domain 2: outer setting
In this domain, the seven constructs—including critical incidents, local attitudes, local conditions, partnership and connection, policy and law, financing, and external pressure—emerged as important factors influencing the outer setting for CBR service for SMI.

## Critical incidents
The prevalence of COVID-19 and influenza has limited access to the rehabilitation block, consequently hindering rehabilitation service access for people with SMI. PR02 noted, “The rehabilitation block has remained closed due to the outbreak of COVID-19 over the past three years and the recent upsurge in Mycoplasma pneumoniae infections.”

## Local attitudes
Social stigma and prejudice exacerbate privacy concerns among people with SMI and families. PR15 noted: “The public disclosure of conditions like depression can lead to job loss, prompting many to conceal their illness.” PR09 added: “Some caregivers highly value privacy and feel ashamed of the disease, restrict their family member’s social interactions to prevent privacy breaches or public exposure.” C02 admitted: “My uncle, a township official, prioritizes social standing—he hospitalizes our relative with SMI during unstable episodes to protect his career.” The preference for long-term hospitalization over CBR services among community staff and families may intensify over-regulation in psychiatric facilities. Provider 15 reported: “Despite expanding from 100 to 400 beds in a decade, demand outstrips capacity, and discharges are rare.” C02 expressed, “Hospitalizing my family member eases my caregiving burden as I also care for two school-aged children and two elderly parents.” Misconceptions about rehabilitation persist. C02 asserted: “Rehabilitation is unnecessary; people with SMI can work independently,” while C01 equated rehabilitation with “consistent medication adherence.”
A facilitating factor is employment-oriented rehabilitation. As C08 stated: “My child wants to work and is looking for job opportunities.”

## Local conditions
Geographical constraints hinder CBR implementation. PR13 noted: “Community health centers in suburban areas are underutilized, as residents prefer urban hospitals—unlike remote rural areas with scarce medical resources where compliance is higher.” PR09 emphasized: “We rely on disabled persons’ centers; residential facilities require 300–400 square meters, while day-care spaces are often under 100 square meters, creating safety risks for 10 or more people with SMI.” Moreover, standardized rehabilitation institutions remain scarce, with limited coverage. P05 reported: “I’m unaware of where to find rehabilitation services or what constitutes good service.” PR15 added: “Subdistrict rehabilitation centers have minimal standards, typically just one or two per area.” PR14 noted low utilization rates: “Daily attendance averages 3–5 people with SMI, with some participating in one or two weekly sessions lasting 1–3 hours.” Additionally, insufficient corporate engagement reflects weak social responsibility incentives. Provider 15 explained: “Insufficient tax reductions fail to motivate companies to assume employment risks.”
Several factors facilitate CBR implementation. First, a robust foundation of medical resources supports the system, as PR14 explained: “Our district has the largest psychiatric workforce and highest funding.” Second, the system also benefits from enhanced specialist-primary care linkages through medical consortiums, as PR16 added: “Specialist hospitals deploy senior doctors for community consultations.” Finally, cost-free services ensure greater accessibility, with PR14 noting: “All rehabilitation services here are free.”

## Partnership and connections
The lack of intersectoral cooperation creates disconnections among hospitals, communities, and families in rehabilitation services. PR8 noted: “Inter-agency communication remains fragmented with different departments operating in silos, limiting services to basic guidance at present.” Moreover, safety and stability are prioritized over functional rehabilitation, as PR13 elaborated: “Our partnerships with communities and police primarily target risk identification and hospitalization. Limited resources are allocated to rehabilitation work.” Additionally, public-private partnerships are too costly. PR09 explained: “Private hospitals collaborate more readily due to their promotional needs, while public hospitals face constraints from training requirements, staff turnover, and safety protocols.” Similarly, industrial collaborations are also costly, as PR10 reported: “Weekly factory visits (two to three times) consume half-day trips each, with delayed financial returns (100–200 RMB after two to three months).”
Facilitating factors for CBR implementation include the Association of Persons with Psychiatric Disability and their Relatives emerging as a potential hub. PR09 affirmed: “As a government-funded autonomous team, though unregistered, it best understands the needs of people with SMI.”

## Policies and laws
Despite national policies promoting community rehabilitation development, assessment requirements remain relatively lenient. As PR15 noted: “The national mental health initiative mandates only minimal rehabilitation centers, which currently demonstrate limited functional capacity and progress.”
A critical policy window now exists for service improvement. As PR09 highlighted: “Jiangsu Province has established standardized templates for psychiatric community rehabilitation this year, enabling province-wide agencies to clarify service objectives and enhance quality.”

## Financing
The lack of a public information-sharing platform impedes information flow. As PR09 mentioned, “We sometimes struggle to obtain timely information on city- or province-level civil affairs projects, which have specific assessment criteria and requirements.” Similarly, delayed fund disbursement creates challenges, PR14 emphasized, “Our finances remain severely unbalanced with minimal revenue, while promised government funds remain undelivered.”
Alternative funding avenues include public welfare venture capital, disability federation subsidies, and welfare lottery funds. PR09 stated: “The disability federation provides partial operational funding for ‘Disabled Persons’ Homes, though it is grossly inadequate,” adding: “Proactive capacity-building could enable us to apply for government rehabilitation project funds.” PR14 acknowledged: “No dedicated mental health rehabilitation budget exists—current operations rely solely on welfare lottery pooled funds.”

## External pressure
CBR services face threefold pressures: social stigma, operational sustainability, and resource-demand mismatches. Regarding social perceptions, PR15 noted: “Media disproportionately report psychiatric hospital incidents, while law enforcement and national levels overemphasize risks associated with SMI.” Operationally, government expectations of financial self-sufficiency remain unrealistic. As PR15 acknowledged: “Post-establishment, centers receive no sustained funding yet must maintain operations—this is a currently insurmountable challenge.” PR10 added: “Mandates to admit 8–10 new service users create bed shortages when families refuse discharge.”
Low pressure on policy appraisals offers mitigating factors. PR15 stated: “Future national requirements may mandate street-level rehabilitation centers, but with minimal quotas (1–2 per area).” PR14 emphasized: “As a government-run entity, we self-determine performance metrics with manageable pressure.”

## Domain 3: inner setting
This domain encompasses four structural elements: structural characteristics, relational connections, communication and culture.

## Structural characteristics
The shortage of skilled professionals has long been a major barrier in the field of mental health rehabilitation. PR13 recommended: “The rehabilitation team should include physicians, nurses, therapists, psychotherapists, and social workers.” However, PR09 admitted: “We cannot recruit therapists graduated from special education schools.” Moreover, the utilization rate of existing facilities remains low. PR08 noted: “Limited space restricts the provision of rehabilitation services, leaving only simple activities like watching TV available.” P04 also reported: “Although the facilities in the room are well-equipped, they remain unused due to the lack of personnel.”
A key enabling factor lies in the availability of infrastructure and space. PR15 emphasized: “Having sufficient rooms and space is the most critical point… for sustained investment…” Additionally, training auxiliary personnel could be a viable solution. PR09 proposed: “Some institutions hire family members (e.g., spouses/sisters) as auxiliary staff, who can participate in rehabilitation work after basic training…”.

## Relational connections
A weak network of relationships hinders some social rehabilitation institutions from providing community-based mental health services. PR09 noted: “Many organizations are willing to participate but lack professional guidance and require support.”
At the community level, grassroots support networks demonstrate positive impacts. The formation of the “Caring Assistance Group” and the “Four-Help-One Team” has particularly strengthened community relational connections. PR15 further elaborated on the composition and role of these teams: “We mandate the establishment of a ‘Four-Help-One Team’ in each community village, consisting of community leaders, grid managers, civil affairs staff, and guardians, all working together to care for people with SMI. Meanwhile, the ‘Caring Assistance Group’ is composed of personnel from various departments, including village officials, public security, and civil affairs, all contributing to the harmonious development of the community.”

## Communication
Two facilitators were identified in institutional information exchange: efficient grassroots-level information sharing and flexible informal communication channels. PR13 reported: “We maintain constant communication through established information-sharing networks with community staff and local police stations.” PR06 emphasized: “Contact the relevant person to resolve issues promptly.” PR02 added: “We routinely consult physicians who provide essential clinical guidance.”

## Culture
Current CBR services demonstrate a pronounced treatment-over-rehabilitation bias. PR07 stated: “Pharmacological control at home appears more practical than rehabilitation interventions.” This therapeutic narrow-mindedness persists among professionals, as PR09 acknowledged: “My limited understanding of rehabilitation services, compounded by systemic constraints, leads me to question their efficacy.”
Notably, proactive intervention and home-based rehabilitation models are gaining traction. P06 expressed: “I strongly desire more family involvement.” Meanwhile, P04 emphasized: “The home environment would be more conducive to my rehabilitation.” PR14 confirmed this shift: “We’re increasingly prioritizing proactive community interventions.”

## Domain 4: individual
At the individual level, the support and emphasis from leaders, implementation facilitators, and the implementation team are all crucial drivers of innovation. This section primarily discusses the two main constructs: the deliverers and recipients of innovation.

## Innovation deliverers
The quality of CBR services is highly dependent on the professional competence of healthcare providers. As PR07 emphasized: “Physicians’ specialized knowledge is paramount, particularly their understanding of rehabilitation’s significance in their recovery.” PR02 further specified the experience requirements: “Nurses should possess at least five years of clinical experience, while physicians need even more extensive training.” Beyond technical expertise, service attitude constitutes another crucial dimension of quality care. PR09 highlighted a common challenge: “Volunteers sometimes perceive people with SMI as being too slow, potentially affecting workflow efficiency… Occasionally, they complete tasks themselves rather than involving people with SMI… I address these situations through private communications to prevent compromising their self-esteem.” This concern was echoed by P02, who reported: “Doctors tend to be critical.” People with SMI consistently expressed expectations for healthcare providers to demonstrate both professional competence and compassionate care. While P10 prioritized “knowledgeable and technically skilled” physicians, P11 preferred providers who were “considerate yet straightforward.” P08 valued “kind and approachable” service attitudes. P09 placed particular trust in senior physicians: “I have special confidence in chief physicians - their timely assessment of my condition and prompt adjustment of treatment protocols.” These findings collectively suggest that exemplary rehabilitation professionals must integrate clinical expertise with interpersonal skills to meet people with SMI expectations effectively.

## Innovation recipients
The recipients of innovation primarily refer to people with SMI and their caregivers. Our findings reveal multiple barriers to CBR services adoption, including caregivers’ low expectations (C02: “I feel reassured living with him, though I don’t expect much better rehabilitation”), individuals’ emotional regulation difficulties (P07: “Someone whose mind is overwhelmed with excessive thoughts”; P08: “Someone who can’t control their emotions”), and social withdrawal tendencies (P02: “Someone who doesn’t like talking much”; C05: “Sometimes he’s too introverted to express anything and needs parental supervision”). Additional barriers included unemployment status (P06: “Someone whose unemployment status affects their treatment”), medication side effects (PR09: “Drug side effects influence task completion time”), and family history of illness (PR14: “Individuals with family history and recurrent episodes undoubtedly show poorer rehabilitation outcomes”).
Conversely, several facilitators promoted treatment engagement. As PR13 noted, “Extroverted service users with strong social motivation and interest in novel experiences demonstrate greater willingness to participate in innovative treatments.” Other positive factors included milder symptom severity (C08: “The less severe cases tend to recover more easily”), favorable socioeconomic status (PR13: “Better family economic conditions”), higher education levels (PR02: “Those with higher educational attainment show greater acceptance”), and strong family support (C02: “I avoid upsetting him or arguing - we maintain harmony”). Particularly noteworthy was the observation that “married male individuals with spousal support typically exhibit more optimistic rehabilitation outcomes” (PR10).

## Domain 5: implementation process
In this domain, we address factors related to the implementation process, with needs assessment and engaging identified as key constructs.

## Needs assessment
The field currently lacks standardized assessment tools for evaluating rehabilitation needs. As PR12 noted, “Standardized tools for assessing rehabilitation needs remain insufficient, with evaluations typically relying on verbal communication.” This limitation is further compounded by inadequate needs identification, as evidenced by P04’s report: “The hospital never inquired about my (rehabilitation) needs.” Even when needs are identified, response capacity remains constrained, with PR13 stating: “While follow-up interactions allow us to document their specific needs and personal circumstances, our ability to address these needs remains substantially limited.” PR02 highlighted the current practice of group-based service planning: “Our programs primarily address collective needs, with gender-specific adaptations—male individuals typically engage in physical labor while female individuals participate in fine manual activities.”
Several promising approaches to needs assessment have emerged in practice. PR12 described an observational and experiential methodology: “People with SMI often struggle to articulate their needs clearly. We primarily identify requirements through practical exploration and proactive experimentation, supplemented by direct communication with people with SMI and families to understand their expectations.” This approach may become a basic assessment framework, as explained by the same provider: “We evaluate individuals based on their clinical status, treatment progress, and available social resources, subsequently providing appropriate guidance.”

## Engaging
Participation in rehabilitation services is influenced by both the providers and the recipients. For healthcare providers, the lack of economic incentives poses a significant barrier to mobilization. As PR12 noted: “The key issues are how to charge for services and whether reimbursement is possible. Without adequate financial support, sustained service provision becomes challenging.” However, intrinsic motivation, including professional dedication and career advancement opportunities, serves as a critical facilitator. PR13 emphasized: “Even without additional incentives, healthcare workers will conscientiously fulfill their duties if assigned to rehabilitation work.” PR14 further highlighted academic motivations: “Personal interest in the specialty, publishing papers, conducting research, and utilizing these achievements for professional promotion.”
For people with SMI, low motivation and participation present major challenges. PR02 illustrated: “Some individuals exhibit passivity. Without encouragement or tangible incentives—such as snacks or small rewards—they often resist engaging in activities or treatment adherence.” P11 echoed this sentiment: “Basic rewards like milk and bread are not particularly motivating.” Additionally, social integration difficulties hinder participation, as P11 described: “I couldn’t adapt to group settings, making engagement difficult.” Family involvement is crucial, with C05 stating: “Unless I accompany him consistently, he won’t participate independently.” Intrinsic motivation remains a key factor, as P11 acknowledged: “I’d participate if the activity interested me.” Effective strategies to enhance engagement include multi-tiered incentive systems, accessible participation models, and strong therapeutic alliances. PR05 described: “Top performers receive 100 or 80 yuan deposited into personal accounts, with transparent tracking of earnings.” PR09 emphasized experiential value: “Activities like fruit-picking or tea harvesting provide tangible outcomes—for example, taking home a small box of strawberries fosters a sense of achievement.” PR07 outlined non-monetary incentives: “Award certificates or prize-winning games (e.g., ring toss) where winners keep their rewards.” Accessibility is equally vital; PR13 explained: “Community-based outreach—bringing services to their doorsteps—maximizes convenience and uptake.” Peer influence and longitudinal trust-building further improve compliance. PR03 noted: “Peer endorsements are powerful; if I recommend an activity, others are more likely to follow.” PR16 added: “Familiarity breeds trust—over time, people with SMI develop reliance and willingness to cooperate.”

## Discussion
In this study, we applied the updated CFIR to explore the perspectives of key stakeholders on barriers and facilitators to implementing CBR services for SMI in Nanjing, China. Our findings emphasize the multifaceted nature of implementing CBR services, highlighting the interplay between the innovation itself, the outer setting, the inner setting, characteristics of individuals involved, and the overall implementation progress within the health system.
Particularly in the innovation domain, stakeholder feedback has highlighted the current limitations in evidence base and suboptimal overall effectiveness of rehabilitation services. Choosing appropriate interventions is complex and requires effectiveness and cost-benefit analysis to inform decision-making and optimize resource allocation [ ]. For example, the care farm factories proposed by service providers show effectiveness in enhancing mental health and promoting occupational and social integration, consistent with current research findings [ , ]. This makes them ideal candidates for piloting in rural areas. While social organizations we interviewed have attempted to design rehabilitation programs for people with SMI, these require further refinement in both design and effectiveness evaluation. Published literature on CBR models provides valuable references, including illness management and recovery projects [ ] that cover a range of standardized curricula such as psychoeducation, medication adherence programs, relapse prevention, social skills training, and coping skills training. continuity. To achieve scalable and effective implementation of CBR services in China, future research needs to generate more localized evidence. First, controlled small-scale pilot studies (e.g., therapeutic care farms) should be conducted to develop modular rehabilitation packages integrating horticultural therapy, vocational training, and other evidence-based interventions, with mixed-methods data collection on clinical and psychosocial outcomes. Subsequently, research should transition to cluster randomized controlled trials, incorporating a multidimensional evaluation framework that combines biomarkers, functional assessments, and employment metrics, supported by data visualization techniques to enhance stakeholder interpretation. Finally, dynamic monitoring systems and modular intervention designs should be implemented to balance safety considerations with individualized rehabilitation needs, ensuring feasible large-scale adoption.
In the domain of the outer setting, major public health events, social stigma, insufficient service accessibility, lack of cross-sector collaboration, and inadequate policy and financial support constituted the primary barriers to CBR service implementation. First, the impact of public health crises such as COVID-19 on mental rehabilitation services aligns with global research findings [ ], but this study reveals that such effects exhibit greater persistence and more profound disruption within China’s healthcare system. Second, the issue of social stigma in this study demonstrates unique cultural characteristics, including the influence of “face culture” on healthcare-seeking behaviors and the interaction between negative media coverage and government stability maintenance policies [ , ]. These findings expand current theoretical understanding of mental health stigma. Regarding service accessibility, the lack of readily available standardized community rehabilitation facilities emerged as a major obstacle limiting awareness and utilization of services among SMI patients and their families, driving them toward prolonged hospitalization rather than CBR. Notably, the absence of vocational rehabilitation services further exacerbates this phenomenon. Research indicates that employment serves not only as a crucial goal of mental rehabilitation but also as a key factor in maintaining rehabilitation outcomes [ ]. However, current collaboration between rehabilitation institutions and enterprises remains severely inadequate, with companies reluctant to participate due to concerns about risks and management costs. The fragmentation of cross-sector collaboration is particularly prominent, which relates to the long-standing “hospital-centric” characteristic of China’s mental health service system [ ]. The study finds that existing collaborations primarily focus on risk control rather than functional rehabilitation, providing new evidence for understanding the priorities of China’s mental health services. Of particular concern is the lack of effective referral and collaboration mechanisms among hospitals, rehabilitation institutions, and enterprises in vocational rehabilitation, leaving even work-capable patients with limited access to suitable employment opportunities. Despite these challenges, the study also identifies several localized positive factors, such as medical resource advantages in certain regions and policy innovation attempts. These findings offer important entry points for future intervention design. Specifically, the recent community rehabilitation standards established in Jiangsu Province may serve as a key policy lever to promote service standardization. Based on these findings, we recommend implementing multi-level anti-stigma interventions, with particular attention to media responsibility and workplace discrimination, innovating cross-sector collaboration mechanisms to establish a hospital-community-family rehabilitation network, fostering social enterprise models to enable rehabilitation institutions to generate sustainable income through productive activities, and improving policy support systems including diversified financing mechanisms and service quality monitoring frameworks.
Within the inner setting, the shortage of mental health rehabilitation personnel is a significant barrier to the implementation of CBR services, within the context of China. Current statistics indicate that there are only 3.55 practicing (assistant) psychiatrists and 9.89 nurses per 100,000 people. This limited workforce struggles to meet even basic treatment needs, let al.one the additional demands of CBR services [ ]. Although China has established community supervision mechanisms like the “Four-Help-One Team” and “Caring Assistance Group” to monitor medication adherence and symptom stability, these primarily serve risk-control purposes rather than active rehabilitation (e.g., social functioning or vocational recovery). Despite the presence of a specialized psychiatric hospital in the interviewed areas, the constraints of time and staffing pose challenges to service implementation. Interviewees highlighted the insufficiency of clinical psychotherapists, social workers, and vocational rehabilitation counselors, hindering the development of a comprehensive service system. Moreover, unlike previous studies, our finding revealed that psychiatrists’ primary focus on medication management often limits their understanding of CBR services, potentially hindering their full support and trust in their effectiveness. To address these challenges and enhance the overall quality and effectiveness of services, it is crucial to strengthen training mechanisms for existing staff. Second, expediting the construction of multidisciplinary rehabilitation teams will promote a holistic approach to care [ ]. Finally, raising healthcare workers’ awareness and trust in CBR services is vital for successful implementation [ ]. Future research could develop and validate a “tiered training model” (e.g., standardized basic rehabilitation skill curricula) and examine its effectiveness in alleviating workforce shortages when combined with incentive mechanisms.
At the individual level, the professional knowledge of service providers is crucial for the rehabilitation of people with SMI. which is consistent with previous research [ ]. Interviewees highlighted that the importance of service providers’ attitude and empathy, as these qualities directly impact their willingness to engage in rehabilitation programs, especially considering the varying paces of recovery. From the perspective of service recipients, a history of relapse, educational level, age of onset, emotional regulation, and attitude towards their illness, along with caregivers’ expectations and satisfaction with the rehabilitation progress of people with SMI, are significant determinants in their acceptance of CBR services. In the areas we visited, most guardians are ageing parents with limited energy, further highlighting the importance of community support for these families. Future research could focus on the feasibility of the “family-community collaborative care model”, including skills training for elderly guardians and analyses of the need for supportive policies.
For successful CBR services implementation, accurate assessment of rehabilitation needs is crucial. Since most people with SMI struggle to clearly express their needs, standardized assessment scales are necessary. Research suggests that a proactive approach, in which service providers actively evaluate the needs of people with SMI [ ], is more effective for early identification and intervention, particularly for elderly people with SMI [ ]. However, the willingness of some people with SMI and their families to seek CBR services is low, possibly due to a psychological state of ambivalence, where they both desire effective rehabilitation services while fearing change and uncertainty. While material and psychological incentives might initially increase participation, their long-term effectiveness is unclear. In the long term, continuously enhancing the effectiveness of CBR services and establishing a clear and fair fee structure that promotes transparency and predictability is essential for building trust and reliance. Effective services that demonstrably meet participants’ needs, combined with a transparent fee mechanism, create a foundation for long-term success. Future discrete choice experiments could be conducted to quantify people with SMI and family preferences for service attributes (e.g., price, effectiveness, convenience) to provide evidence for differential payment strategies.

## Strengths and limitations
This study possesses several strengths. First, we achieved extensive recruitment of representatives from diverse stakeholder organizations involved in CBR services for SMI. Their participation provided valuable expert perspectives on the project’s potential scalability. Second, the qualitative research design yielded rich, in-depth insights into topics that remain under-explored in the existing literature. Furthermore, we employed the 2009 CFIR to guide interview development, ensuring a comprehensive exploration of service implementation aspects while avoiding redundancy. Finally, to align with the field’s latest theoretical advancements, we employed the updated 2022 CFIR during data analysis and result discussions. Notably, we documented key issues in detail immediately following each interview and integrated these notes with the transcripts for a more nuanced analysis. However, the study has limitations. Firstly, the recruitment process did not include all potential stakeholders, particularly hard-to-reach service users in remote areas. This may limit the breadth of perspectives and insights gathered. Secondly, several major mental health organizations were unable to participate due to complex approval procedures. Thirdly, some interviews were shorter than expected, potentially reflecting a lack of prior awareness regarding CBR services for SMI. Despite this, we provided a comprehensive introduction to the concept and relevant background information before each interview, ensuring a common understanding among all participants.

## Conclusion
This study contributes to a relatively under-investigated area: the implementation of CBR services for people with SMI. This study highlights the critical role of collaboration with diverse stakeholder organizations in co-designing effective CBR services to improve rehabilitation outcomes for people with SMI. The findings not only inform the expansion and implementation of CBR services for SMI but also offer valuable insights to the broader public health research community regarding key elements, barriers, and facilitators of program implementation. While the potential benefits of CBR programs for SMI are evident, successful implementation hinges on two crucial considerations: firstly, the design of the program must be adaptable to integrate with existing healthcare infrastructure, services, and systems; secondly, early identification of organizations responsible for funding, support, and execution is essential. As most stakeholders prioritize CBR for SMI, further exploration of potential organizations to act as financial and/or delivery agents is necessary. Additionally, ensuring widespread resource availability and ongoing training for frontline service providers is critical for successful program implementation.
