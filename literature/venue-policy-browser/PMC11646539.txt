Transformative innovations require interdisciplinary research, and recent synergistic advancements in the fields of neurology and cardiology offer excellent test cases for why interdisciplinary collaboration is of great public health importance. In stroke prevention, it is increasingly recognized that improved cardiovascular risk factor identification and modification yields benefits realized in neurological outcomes. Blood pressure control for cognitive decline and atriopathy‐related embolic complications were tested in the systolic blood pressure intervention trial memory and cognition in decreased hypertension and atrial cardiopathy and antithrombotic drugs in prevention after cryptogenic stroke trials, respectively. , In acute stroke treatment, the growing use of endovascular thrombectomy has demonstrated that reciprocal knowledge sharing between cardiologists and stroke neurologists is critical to improving patient outcomes. At the same time, research into the prevention and treatment of cardiovascular disease has demonstrated that many chronic diseases, including diabetes, obesity, and dementia, are deeply linked. For example, clinical trial data demonstrating that sodium‐glucose cotransporter inhibitors have major cardioprotective properties consistently show a benefit towards the brain as much as the heart. , Sodium‐glucose cotransporter inhibition is now being tested as a protective strategy against dementia. 

These advances in the prevention and treatment of cardiovascular disease and related conditions such as neurodegenerative diseases can only happen through thoughtful collaboration between researchers and practitioners of different disciplines. Many strides have been made in interdisciplinarity, particularly in stroke care. In the United States, the creation of the American Stroke Association as a division of the American Heart Association in 1997 and the American Heart Association's strategic alliances with other nonprofit organizations have helped to promote a culture of collaboration and partnership. More recently, the concept of brain health has taken center stage at the American Heart Association. 

Yet, significant barriers to interdisciplinary research approaches remain. Despite shared risk factors and a constant interplay between disorders, after a medicine internship, clinical training in cardiology and neurology is nonoverlapping, and our departments, divisions, and institutes are organized in reactive, disease‐based systems instead of by strategic overlap or patient centeredness. In this Viewpoint article, we argue that a concerted effort by academic neurologists and cardiologists (along with experts in preventive medicine, data science, and other related fields) to promote interdisciplinary research is urgently needed to catalyze further research advancements. We also argue that prior efforts to promote interdisciplinary science have not been effective enough in breaking down structural barriers to interdisciplinary collaboration. ## SUPPORT FOR THE CAREER DEVELOPMENT OF INTERDISCIPLINARY SCIENTISTS AND CLINICIAN‐SCIENTISTS

First, better systems to educate and support the career development of interdisciplinary scientists and clinician‐scientists in neurology, cardiology, and other related fields are sorely needed. Academic institutions are interested in cultivating and retaining subject‐matter experts, but the common advice to “dominate a niche” discourages interdisciplinarity and contributes to an academic culture prone to overspecialization; interdisciplinary research between cardiology and neurology should be considered an expertise in and of itself. This is particularly important in the context of the existing landscape for physician‐scientists in neurology, which historically has had a strong representation from traditional bench clinician‐neuroscientists, for whom the pressures to dominate a specific, narrowly defined research area may be even greater. , 

## DISMANTLING STRUCTURAL BARRIERS TO INTERDISCIPLINARITY
Researchers working at the interface of neurology and cardiology may face specific structural barriers: depending on institution and health system structure, cardiology and neurology may be separate academic departments, requiring greater levels of administrative coordination to promote collaboration. Institutions should create centers, divisions, or other administrative structures to bring together neurologists with leading researchers in other related fields (such as cardiology, neurosurgery, rheumatology, or endocrinology), in an interdisciplinary framework, ideally integrating both clinical practice and research, especially as the clinical discipline of neurocardiology continues to grow. , In particular, developing funding models, beginning as early as the initial hiring process, that allow for both departments/divisions to identify their individual financial contributions to share effort easily and transparently and allow for smooth flow of funds would help to break down barriers to interdisciplinary research. Additionally, departments or divisions of cardiology and neurology should create more informal venues to support interdisciplinary engagement between neurologists and cardiologists such as multidisciplinary grand rounds, journal clubs, and case conferences for disorders requiring multispecialty input (eg, management of patent foramen ovale and atrial fibrillation in the setting of high intracranial hemorrhage risk). Additionally, centers or institutes that cross interdisciplinary boundaries and provide administrative homes for interdisciplinary research should be supported. Strategies to address attribution of funding, such as agreements to offer joint appointments to interdisciplinary researchers, could help address issues with competing incentives to attract funding to specific departments.
Mentorship is also a critical component of any strategy to promote interdisciplinary science and clinical care: the combined perspectives of mentors with complementary areas of expertise can be highly effective to allow trainees to develop interdisciplinary skills. Furthermore, effective mentorship arrangements, wherein a trainee is co‐mentored by, for example, a neurologist, a cardiologist, and potentially other individuals such as experts in preventive medicine, vascular biology, data science, etc., could help promote not only the career development of the trainee, but could also provide an environment where mentors could learn from the trainee and from one another to expand their skillsets beyond their areas of primary expertise. To nurture such relationships, both the educational and financial infrastructure to support these collaborative mentorship teams will be needed.

## DEVELOPING FUNDING PATHWAYS TO PROMOTE INTERDISCIPLINARY RESEARCH
Funding agencies ought to develop strategies to specifically promote interdisciplinary research in cardiovascular disease and neurocardiology. Developing funding pathways designed to investigate the intersections between two fields or disease states or providing combined training and mentorship to early‐career investigators would support and encourage interdisciplinary collaboration both for junior and senior researchers. Additionally, funding agencies ought to involve multiple disciplines in the review of grant applications, to ensure that perspectives from related fields are heard when determining how to allocate scarce funding resources. For example, the Clinical Integrative Cardiovascular and Hematological Sciences study section at the National Institutes of Health could benefit from the perspective of a neurologist, and the StrokeNet working groups and clinical trial study section would benefit from the participation of a cardiologist. Both groups could benefit from experts in metabolic disorders, preventive medicine, and renal disease, among other relevant skillsets. Nongovernmental funding organizations should partner to create grant opportunities that specifically address unmet needs that arise in‐between the clear bailiwick of either organization. At the institutional level, divisions or departments of cardiology and neurology should create joint funding mechanisms to support ambitious, early‐stage interdisciplinary research and researchers. Institutes at the National Institutes of Health, such as the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, and the National Institute for Neurological Disorders and Stroke, should fund joint career development grants for promising interdisciplinary clinician‐scientists.
Specific grant opportunities that target emerging areas of overlap between cardiology and neurology should be developed. Areas such as atrial fibrillation identification and treatment, secondary prevention of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease after myocardial infarction or ischemic stroke, effective management of dysautonomic disorders, and evaluation of cardioprotective drugs for neuroprotection after stroke all require the direct involvement of both cardiologists and neurologists to effectively leverage insights from the existing literature and apply emerging knowledge to real‐world clinical settings. , , The objective should be to promote interdisciplinary engagement for all neurologists and cardiologists, not to advance credentialism by limiting interdisciplinary research and practice only to those who have pursued advanced training in multiple disciplines.

## PROMOTING INTERDISCIPLINARY DISSEMINATION OF SCIENTIFIC KNOWLEDGE
Finally, interdisciplinary research is nothing without interdisciplinary dissemination of emerging knowledge. Scientific and clinical disciplines tend to be relatively closed networks, and it can be challenging for important research to penetrate the collective consciousness of a related field. We suggest that cardiology journals deepen partnerships with neurology journals to promote novel strategies for raising awareness of research of relevance to related fields, such as the publication of editorial articles in one field's journal in conjunction with original research publication in another journal. Furthermore, large‐scale scientific meetings ought to develop sessions and symposia that can target interdisciplinary issues and strive to attract leaders from multiple fields to engage in meaningful scientific dialogue. Meetings surrounding the topic of “stroke,” for example, could be an ideal venue for this. Stroke is not a disease per se but rather the end point of another clinical condition, eventually affecting the brain. Alternatively, an atrial fibrillation meeting could invite a neurologist researching the prevention of cardioembolic ischemic stroke to engage in dialogue with experts in detection and treatment or primary prevention of atrial fibrillation.
Finally, the use of social media platforms such as X/Twitter and Clubhouse can potentially help information achieve broader dissemination across multiple scientific audiences. A more active effort to connect neurologists and cardiologists in virtual spaces can help to promote the integration of research and clinical practice networks, especially across geographic borders. Even before the COVID‐19 pandemic, it was challenging to connect researchers and practitioners from different geographic regions, particularly across national boundaries. The COVID‐19 pandemic has only increased the importance of utilizing virtual venues to build and retain diverse professional networks. 

In summary, we argue that concrete steps to promote interdisciplinary research and practice in neurocardiology are sorely needed. We suggest 3 categories of interventions: (1) optimizing tenure and promotion pathways to reward interdisciplinary science, (2) modifying funding strategies and developing funding pipelines to support interdisciplinary research, and (3) implementing novel strategies to promote the dissemination of emerging research across disciplinary lines.
The potential of successfully acting upon these recommendations is enormous: developing a broad network of interdisciplinary researchers in cardiovascular disease has the potential to accelerate both the discovery of practice‐changing knowledge and the successful implementation and cross‐fertilization of ideas in multiple clinical and scientific settings.
