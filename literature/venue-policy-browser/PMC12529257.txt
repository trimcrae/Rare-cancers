A number of international comparative studies have documented that more comprehensive policies are associated with less secondhand smoking in indoor areas, including bars [ , ]. More recently, comparative studies have reported on exposure to smoking in outdoor areas, such as outdoor hospitality venues [ ]. Smoking exposure at children’s playgrounds was found to be less common in countries with more comprehensive tobacco control policies [ ].
To date, national or comparative data on smoking at outdoor sports venues is scarce. Yet, these venues are important given that many children and adults regularly visit sports venues [ ], and participate in activities that are expected to foster their health. In line with the ultimate goal of a smoke-free generation, a broad coalition of Dutch organizations agreed that by 2025, all children should be able to engage in sports in a smoke-free environment [ ].
In this article, we present comparative data on smoking at outdoor football clubs in the Netherlands and Germany. Smoke-free policies, i.e. policies aimed at banning smoking from indoor or outdoor public places, are more comprehensive in the Netherlands, although outdoor sports premises are not yet included [ ]. However, since 2016, several national campaigns have been launched to encourage Dutch sports clubs to become smoke-free [ ]. Therefore, we tested the hypothesis that exposure to smoking is less common at sports clubs in the Netherlands as compared to Germany.

## Methods
We measured the prevalence of smoking at the venues of Dutch and German football clubs. We focused on football clubs since it is the most popular outdoor sport among youth in Europe [ ]. German clubs were visited during spring 2022 on weekend days, in the morning or afternoon. We visited 45 German football clubs in six cities that were each within 250 km from the Dutch border (Düsseldorf, Duisburg, Essen, Koblenz, Mains, and Hannover). Dutch clubs were visited during spring 2023, also on weekend days, in the morning or the afternoon. We visited 116 Dutch football clubs in 20 municipalities that were selected to represent the four main regions of the Netherlands [ ]. Each club was visited by one observer; in total, three persons acted as observer.
The prevalence of smoking at each club was assessed according to a standard protocol that is documented elsewhere [ ]. The observers visited (i) the club entrance, (ii) the terrace or club house, and (iii) the field with the most spectators at the time of observation. At each site, the observer used a standard procedure to count, for at least 5 min, the number of people smoking. The average observation time was 21.0 and 19.6 min at Dutch and German clubs, respectively.
The analysis consisted of a descriptive analysis of the number of smokers observed at the three sites by country. Moreover, we applied multi-level linear regression analysis in which clubs were clustered into municipalities. The number of smokers was the dependent variable, and country was the independent variable. The regression analysis controlled for temperature, rainfall, the number of people present during the time of observation, and the presence of a terrace or club house. The measurement of these characteristics is described elsewhere [ ]. We also measured the presence of anti-smoking signs.
As this study did not involve direct human participation, ethical approval was not required. No data were collected that could be traceable to individual persons.

## Results
The characteristics of the visited clubs are given in . All Dutch clubs had a terrace or club house, compared to 82% of German clubs. Dutch clubs had more visitors (at least 35 visitors in 46% of all clubs), even though weather conditions were less favourable. Of all German clubs, 22% had some kind of anti-smoking signs in any of the sites, mostly indicating that smoking was prohibited. Of the Dutch clubs, 42% showed the large, blue “smoke-free” signs that were designed as part of a national smoke-free sports campaign.

 shows that smoking around the selected fields was less common at clubs in the Netherlands (25%) than in Germany (64%). In contrast, Dutch clubs had more smokers at the entrance (19 vs. 11%) and at the terrace or in front of the club house (44 vs. 19%). The corresponding regression-based estimates are given in . Compared to German clubs, Dutch clubs had on average one fewer smoker around the field (difference: −1.07; 95% confidence interval: −1.87 to −0.25), but about one more smoker at the terrace (0.80; 0.06–1.53), with no substantive differences at the entrance (0.15; −0.13 to 0.43). The same German-Dutch differences were found after excluding Dutch clubs that declared themselves smoke-free.
 

## Discussion
A strength of this study is the use of a standard protocol and the selection of a series of German and Dutch clubs that were sufficiently comparable to allow for statistical analysis. The main weakness was that a one-time observation at a club may not be fully representative of that club. However, this inaccuracy may be expected to average out at the country level, though the observations for Germany were limited to 45 clubs.
The lower rates of smoking around Dutch football fields are in line with our expectations. One possible explanation is that many Dutch clubs had implemented policies to prohibit or discourage smoking at their venues [ ]. However, similar Dutch-German differences were found in analyses that excluded Dutch clubs with smoke-free policies. Other factors may play a role, such as those operating at the national level. Comprehensive national tobacco control policies may have de-normalised smoking in public places, including places that are not formally smoke-free.
Yet, while smoking was less common around the field, it was not less common at the entrances of Dutch sports clubs. This situation resembles that at schools, where smoke-free school premises policies could lead to smoking being displaced to the areas around school gates [ ]. Sports club members have reported similar challenges with members and visitors smoking at their gates [ ].
At Dutch sports clubs, smoking was more prevalent on terraces. This is problematic since football players are likely to visit these areas. Pro-smoking norms may prevail at terraces, as these places are associated with enjoyment and relaxation rather than with sports [ ]. Those wanting to enforce smoke-free rules at sports clubs find this a particularly hard task when people drink alcohol. The situation at football clubs resembles that of bars and pubs, as current restrictions on outdoor smoking in Europe have yet had little effect on smoking exposure on terraces [ , ].
To conclude, despite comprehensive tobacco control policies and smoke-free sports campaigns, smoking rates are not consistently lower at Dutch football clubs than at German clubs. This would imply that children from both countries are about equally likely to see people smoke when they visit their football club. This underlines the need to adopt and enforce smoke-free sports policies that apply to all places of a sports venue, including entrances and terraces. Such club-level policies more be feasible and effective when supported by national policies to ban smoking from terraces elsewhere.

Key pointsThis is the first study that assesses the prevalence of smoking at outdoor sports clubs in countries with contrasting tobacco control policies.
While prevalence of smoking around the fields is lower at Dutch clubs than at German clubs, the prevalence of smoking at terraces and entrances is at least as high.
The results imply that children from both countries may be about equally likely to see people smoke when they visit their football club.
The results underline the need to implement smoke-free sports policies that cover all relevant parts of sports clubs’ premises and entrances.
