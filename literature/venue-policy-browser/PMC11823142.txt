## Objective
This study was conducted to explore the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) status of and compare HIV knowledge, HIV testing, and other healthy behaviors between men who have sex with men and women (MSMW) and men who have sex with men only (MSMO), to provide a scientific basis for targeted HIV interventions for this population.

## Methods
A cross-sectional survey was conducted to collect demographic and behavioral information using questionnaires for statistical analysis.

## Results
Among 1,993 participants, 772 (38.7%) reported having sexual intercourse with women. The results of the multivariate logistic regression model analysis indicated that MSMW had a lower probability than MSMO of reporting HIV knowledge 1 (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]:0.556, 95% CI: 0.409–0.756), knowledge 2 (aOR:0.626; 95% CI, 0.515–0.761), knowledge 3 (aOR:0.569; 95% CI: 0.447–0.724), informing their HIV status to the last casual sex partners offline (aOR: 0.515, 95% CI: 0.358–0.743), HIV testing more than once in the past 6 months (aOR: 0.696, 95% CI: 0.521–0.931), and HIV testing in a hospital in the past 6 months (aOR: 0.696, 95% CI: 0.521–0.931). In contrast, MSMW had a higher probability of post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) intake in the past 6 months (aOR: 2.252, 95% CI: 1.570–3.229), pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) intake in the past 6 months (aOR: 1.630, 95% CI: 1.091–2.434), and cumulative HIV testing more than twice (aOR: 1.917, 95% CI: 1.434–2.563).

## Conclusion
MSMW showed significant differences with MSMO in HIV knowledge and HIV related risky reduction behaviors. It is necessary to enhance awareness and skills on HIV self-testing, PEP, and PrEP among MSM. Encouraging HIV knowledge education and HIV testing service are important for MSMW. PEP and PrEP might be enhanced among MSMO for reducing the risk of HIV transmission.

## Introduction
Acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS) is a systemic disease resulting from human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection [ , ], and the severe situation of the AIDS epidemic remains one of the major public health challenges globally [ , ]. HIV infection among men who have sex with men (MSM) is a significant public health concern with profound implications for society [ , , ]. With the spread of knowledge about AIDS and the increased accessibility of HIV testing services, it has become particularly important to understand the HIV knowledge, testing behaviors, and other health behaviors of different subgroups within the MSM population. The higher HIV infection rates among MSM in certain regions not only strain healthcare resources and social security systems but also have repercussions on the overall health and public health status of the population [ , ].
In recent years, several studies have indicated that there may be significant differences in HIV knowledge, testing behaviors, and other health behaviors between men who have sex with men and women (MSMW) and those who only have sex with men (MSMO) [ , , ]. Understanding these differences is crucial, as it will guide the formulation and optimization of public health policies, ensuring that effective interventions can be tailored to meet the specific needs of different populations. Firstly, exploring the differences between MSMW and MSMO helps to identify risk factors associated with HIV transmission. Different patterns of sexual behavior may lead to varying risks of HIV infection, affecting individuals’ awareness of HIV and the adoption of preventive measures. In this regard, understanding the HIV knowledge levels and testing behaviors of each specific group can assist in designing educational and intervention programs that better meet local needs. Additionally, from the perspective of public health policy, a one-size-fits-all intervention strategy may not meet the needs of all MSM populations. For example, MSMW may face additional risks associated with sexual behavior with female partners, while MSMO may focus more on the risks associated with male partners. Therefore, developing segmented policies and interventions will help improve testing rates, the utilization of preventive measures, and overall health outcomes for these groups.
From 2022 to 2025, we carried out the HIV Behavior Cohort Survey in Zhejiang province, which was an area with a low HIV epidemic in Southeast China. This study focused on MSM to examine the characteristics and temporal evolution of risky behaviors, as well as to investigate independent factors influencing these behaviors. The cohort study encompassed seven cities in Zhejiang province and involved a sample size of 2000 participants. This study aimed to classify the MSM population based on their engagement in sexual activities with women by dividing them into MSMW and MSMO groups. The main objective was to compare HIV-related health behaviors between MSMW and MSMO, with a focus on identifying potential deficits in accessing HIV intervention resources and adopting healthy behaviors. This study aimed to offer new insights into preventing cross-population transmission. Currently, research in this area is limited, and there remains a significant gap in intervention resources for this population. This study will enhance our understanding of sexual behavioral characteristics and HIV-related risky behaviors in both MSMW and MSMO, facilitating the development of targeted interventions for the MSM community.

## Materials and methods

## Research design
A cross-sectional design was employed for this study. Convenience sampling was done to recruit participants and questionnaire surveys were conducted by HIV-prevention social organizations in seven cities (covering over 70% of MSM in the province) in the Zhejiang province of China from October 2022 to February 2023, following standardized training. Recruitment announcements were disseminated on various platforms, including local social networking applications (such as WeChat, QQ, and Blued), online channels (chat rooms and official accounts), and offline settings such as bars, bathhouses, and HIV testing and counselling centres managed by four local community-based organizations (CBOs). All those who saw the recruitment information were asked to go to the CBOs’studio to participate the research during the study period. The research team developed and administered a questionnaire as an electronic survey on online platforms. The questionnaire was pre-tested among 30 participants before the survey started. The survey involved face-to-face interviews and self-administered questionnaires. Face-to-face interviews were conducted at the testing and counselling centres of social organizations, where participants, were guided individually, accessed the electronic questionnaire platform on their mobile devices, provided electronic informed consent, and completed the questionnaire. Participants who were unable to attend in person received assistance from the social organization staff by phone to complete the electronic questionnaire.

## Participants
The study initially enrolled 2,061 MSM individuals. Two participants declined to participate and a total of 2,059 participants completed the questionnaire survey. Following HIV testing, 10 individuals who tested positive for HIV were excluded, along with 53 participants identified through phone number verification as having submitted multiple entries, and three with incomplete data. This resulted in a final sample size of 1,993 MSM. The effective response was 96.8% (1993 of 2059).
The inclusion criteria for participants were as follows: (1) aged 18 years or older, (2) tested negative for HIV antibodies, (3) engaged in male–male sexual activities (anal or oral sex), and (4) provided voluntary consent to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria included the inability to provide informed consent for intellectual or psychological reasons.
Participants were categorized into the MSMW group if they reported engaging in sexual activities with women and men ever and the others. Those who reported that they only have sexual intercourse with men were classified into the MSMO group.

## Ethical approval
This study protocol was approved by ethics committee of the Zhejiang Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention, and this study complied with the declaration of Helsinki and all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of the ethics committee of the Zhejiang Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention (No. 2022-009-01). All participants completed the questionnaire survey after providing informed consent.

## Questionnaire survey content
The questionnaire included questions on demographic characteristics (e.g., age, gender, education level, income, and marital status), HIV/AIDS knowledge, self-assessment of HIV infection risk, receipt of intervention testing and self-testing, pre- and post-exposure prophylaxis practices, and awareness of the HIV infection status of sexual partners (including temporary partners from online and venue-based interactions).

## Definitions
The HIV/AIDS knowledge assessment consisted of three questions. Question 1: Which of the following behaviors poses the highest risk of HIV transmission? Choices included vaginal intercourse with a female, anal intercourse with a female, anal intercourse with a male (correct answer), oral sex with a male, and do not know. Question 2: Which sexual role is the most vulnerable to HIV transmission? Choices included insertive roles, receptive roles (correct answer), equally, both and do not know. Question 3: Does the use of enhancers, such as rush poppers or nitrous oxide capsules, increase the risk of HIV transmission? Choices included yes (correct answer), no, and do not know.
Casual sex partners online: refers to casual sex partners found on the internet (dating application, chat room, et al.); casual sex partners offline: refers to casual sex partners found in bars, parks, bathing pools, or other places.

## Acquiring HIV status
Participants were asked “Did you acquire your partner’s HIV test results before engaging in sexual activity?”

## Statistical analysis
The data were cleaned and analyzed using SPSS software (version 20.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The independent variable was “engagement in sexual activities with women,” with dependent variables including HIV/AIDS knowledge, informed friendships, pre and post-exposure prevention, HIV testing, and perceived risk of infection. Univariate analysis was performed using the chi-square test, whereas multivariate analysis employed binary logistic regression (Backward: LR). Six confounding factors were controlled: age (0: <25, 1: 26 to 40, 2: >40), registry (0: Zhejiang province, 1: other province), type of residence (0: urban, 1: rural), educational background (0: high school and below, 1: college and above), income (CNY/year) (0: ≤90000, 1: >90000), marital status (0: unmarried, 1: divorced/cohabitation, 2: married/cohabitation). This analysis identified differences and relationships among sociodemographic characteristics, HIV/AIDS knowledge, testing behaviors, and informed friendship behaviors in the MSMO and MSMW groups, with significance set at P < 0.05.

## Results

## General characteristics of participants
Among the 1,993 participants in the study, 772 (38.7%) reported engaging in sexual activity with women. Of these, 68.4% (1,368) were aged 26 years or older, 61.4% (1,223) were residents of Zhejiang province; 1,166 participants possessed a college degree or higher, constituting 58.5% of the sample. Furthermore, 551 participants (27.6%) were married or cohabiting. The self-reported sexual orientations comprised 1,203 homosexuals, 597 bisexuals, and 193 heterosexual/undisclosed individuals, accounting for 60.4%, 30.0%, and 9.7%, respectively. Chi-square analysis was used to analyze the sociodemographic characteristics of MSMO and MSMW. The findings in Table  reveal that, compared to MSMO, a higher percentage of MSMW individuals were aged 40 years and above (33.3% vs. 4.8%). MSMW individuals also had a lower percentage of individuals with a college education or higher (43.7% vs. 67.9%), a higher percentage earning over 90,000 CNY annually (36.9% vs. 32.0%), a higher proportion of married or cohabiting individuals (58.4% vs. 8.2%), and a greater number identified as bisexual (50.1% vs. 17.2%), compared to MSMO.

 

## Univariable logistic regression analysis
Chi-square analysis was performed to evaluate HIV-related health knowledge, behaviors, and infection risks of MSMO and MSMW. The results (Table  ) revealed that, in comparison to MSMO, MSMW had lower rates of HIV knowledge, disclosure of HIV status to recent casual sex partners online or offline, hospital-based HIV testing, multiple HIV testing within the last six months, HIV self-testing, and access to HIV self-testing kits. Moreover, MSMW had a higher likelihood of reporting the use of pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) or post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) in the previous six months, undergoing HIV testing more than twice, and perceiving a higher risk of HIV infection as general/high/very high.

 

## Multivariable logistic regression analysis
A multiple logistic regression model was used to explore the relationship between MSMO/MSMW and risk behaviors. The findings from Table  reveal that compared to the MSMO group, the MSMW group reported higher odds of getting cumulative HIV testing more than twice (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.917; 95% Confidence Interval [CI], 1.434–2.563), uptaking PEP in the previous six months (aOR: 2.252; 95% CI: 1.570–3.229), uptaking PrEP during the same period (aOR: 1.630; 95% CI: 1.091–2.434). Compared to the MSMO group, the MSMW group showed lower odds of the correct answer on HIV knowledge 1 (aOR: 0.556; 95% CI: 0.409–0.756), HIV knowledge 2 (aOR, 0.626; 95% CI, 0.515–0.761), and HIV knowledge 3 (aOR: 0.569; 95% CI: 0.447–0.724). Furthermore, the odds of disclosing their HIV status to their last casual sex partners offline were 0.515 times lower among MSMW than among MSMO (aOR: 0.515; 95% CI: 0.358–0.743). MSMW showed a decreased risk of having HIV testing in the past 6 months more than once (aOR:0.641, 95%CI:0.511–0.806) and getting HIV testing from a hospital multiple times in the past 6 months (aOR: 0.696; 95% CI: 0.521–0.931) compared to MSMO.

 

## Discussion
HIV infection can compromise the immune system and increase susceptibility to other diseases and infections, thereby increasing the risk of contracting additional illnesses [ ]. This cross-sectional survey conducted among MSM in the Zhejiang Province provided insights into the characteristics of their engagement in sexual activities with women. Findings from the study showed that among the 1,993 MSM participants, 38.7% were identified as MSMW. This observation suggests that a proportion of MSM have had sexual experiences with women, highlighting the diversity in sexual orientation and behavior within the MSM population. MSMO may demonstrate more proactive health behaviors than MSMW, indicating a distinction between sexual orientation and behavior, with some MSM being more flexible in their sexual activities. Additionally, this implies that some MSM may conceal their sexual orientation due to societal pressure or face challenges in understanding their sexual orientation, emphasizing the significance of investigating cross-population transmission among MSM. Therefore, offering education and support for sexual health and orientation is vital for this community. Different patterns of sexual behavior may influence individuals’ awareness of HIV, willingness to get tested, and behaviors related to prevention. Therefore, an in-depth exploration of the differences between MSMW and MSMO can help develop more effective interventions to promote HIV testing and prevention efforts, enhancing overall public health.
Research has indicated that a higher level of awareness of HIV/AIDS is advantageous in reducing the risk of new HIV infections [ ]. The findings of this study suggest that, in comparison to MSMO, the proportion of MSMW who have knowledge of HIV/AIDS levels 1–3 is lower, signaling variations in HIV/AIDS knowledge comprehension between the two participant groups. The MSMO commonly prioritizes HIV/AIDS prevention and knowledge attainment, showing a tendency to actively search for pertinent information and engage in health education endeavors to bolster their comprehension and awareness of HIV/AIDS. In contrast, MSMW may exhibit less understanding of HIV/AIDS because of factors such as inadequate self-awareness and limited information sources, resulting in weaker preventive awareness. These disparities could lead to MSM underestimating the risks associated with engaging in sexual activities with women, consequently increasing their chances of infection. Hence, the results of this study underscore the critical need to heighten awareness and education regarding the transmission routes and risks of HIV/AIDS in sexual behavior. Improving the dissemination of HIV/AIDS knowledge and sexual health education within MSM communities is paramount for enhancing awareness of infection risks and promoting safer sexual practices.
For the MSM population, especially during sexual encounters with casual partners, it is essential to emphasize the significance of sexual health and disease prevention. The findings revealed a decrease (aOR = 0.515) in the proportion of MSMW who communicated their HIV status to their recent casual sex partners’ offline. When interacting with transient partners, MSMO were more inclined to divulge their HIV status to ensure the well-being of both parties. In contrast, MSMW may hesitate to proactively disclose their HIV status in such scenarios, citing factors such as fear and shame, which could result in information asymmetry and the potential risk of transmission during partner selection and sexual interactions. This highlights the necessity for enhanced accessibility to reliable HIV testing and counseling services for the MSM community, facilitating the easy retrieval of information concerning their own and their sexual partners’ HIV status and the adoption of suitable preventive measures as required.
The MSM population is among the high-risk groups for HIV infection and alterations in HIV testing behaviors in this group are of particular importance [ ]. The results of this study revealed that the percentage of MSMW undergoing HIV testing nearly twice surpasses that of MSMO (aOR = 1.917), suggesting an increase in the number of MSM who have undergone multiple HIV tests after engaging in sexual intercourse with women. This increase may indicate a heightened focus on HIV testing, influenced by factors such as prioritizing HIV prevention strategies or recognizing the significance of HIV testing. Individuals engaging in sexual activities increasingly value HIV self-tests, likely because of an awareness of their increased susceptibility to HIV and a greater willingness to proactively test themselves for the protection of their own and others’ health [ , ]. Notably, research shows that MSMW reported lower odds (0.641) of have HIV testing more than once in the last six months compared to MSMO. MSMO exhibit a higher tendency for regular HIV testing, understanding the importance of prompt testing for early diagnosis and intervention, and showcasing a strong sense of self-care awareness. In contrast, MSMW may delay or avoid HIV testing owing to psychological and societal pressures, resulting in an unknown infection status and increasing the risk of HIV transmission. An increased focus on sexual health information for MSM with numerous sexual partners enables prompt access to risk details and the enactment of suitable preventive measures to diminish the likelihood of HIV infection. This underscores the essential role of sexual health services, including sexual health education, regular HIV testing, and dissemination of AIDS prevention knowledge.
The findings of this study revealed a decrease (aOR = 0.696) in the proportion of MSMW-seeking HIV testing from hospitals in the last six months compared to MSMO. These results underscore the influence of engaging in sexual activity with women on HIV testing rates within the MSM population and underscore the importance of this discovery for public health policies and relevant research. MSMW undergo HIV testing less frequently than MSMO, potentially because of a lack of awareness regarding HIV infection risks among MSM with heterosexual experiences, resulting in a reduced perceived necessity for testing. Moreover, MSMW may have limited exposure to HIV-related information and services, compounded by social and cultural factors that contribute to lower rates of HIV testing. In contrast, MSMO demonstrate a proactive approach in seeking HIV testing services from healthcare facilities or community health centers to maintain timely awareness of their health status. On the other hand, MSMW, who lack initiative or face access barriers to healthcare resources, tend to seek HIV testing less often from healthcare facilities, thus reducing opportunities for self-protection and preventive measures. These findings highlight the importance of considering a variety of sexual behaviors when designing policies and interventions to prevent HIV transmission. It is crucial to increase attention and support for MSM engaged in heterosexual activities in public health awareness campaigns and to effectively allocate medical resources.
PEP or PrEP has proven to lower the risk of HIV infection [ , ]. The study findings revealed a notable increase in the percentage of MSMW using PEP or PrEP in the past six months compared to MSMO. This trend suggests that MSMW are adopting a more proactive approach to PEP as they become aware of their infection risks or initiate PrEP as a preventative measure upon recognizing potential risks early on. This reflects a positive shift in knowledge and actions pertaining to PEP and PrEP. This study highlights variations in the awareness and utilization of PEP or PrEP among MSM, observing that MSM with heterosexual experiences are more inclined to embrace these preventative methods. This underlines the need to address the diverse requirements for disease prevention and control within the MSM community based on individual backgrounds and experiences, enabling customization of targeted prevention strategies and educational initiatives.
This study had some limitations. First, as a cross-sectional study, it can only observe associated phenomena rather than definitively establish causal relationships. Consequently, accurately assessing the true impact of research variables is challenging. The collected data represent a singular time point, making it difficult to track trends in variable changes over time. Second, voluntary participation in questionnaire surveys could introduce sample bias, potentially skewing the results as non-representative of the entire population. Moreover, respondents may exhibit recall biases, complicating the assurance of respondent authenticity and data accuracy through questionnaires and possibly leading to the provision of false information, casting uncertainties on data quality. Hence, these statistical findings must be interpreted with caution. Subsequent research should further investigate the connection between MSMW and the HIV infection rate with the goal of offering more targeted suggestions for interventions and policy formulation. However, it is important to note that the binary classification of MSMW and MSMO may overlook the complexity and diversity of sexual behavior in the real world. Many men’s sexual behavior patterns are not simple; they may involve frequent switching, emotional connections, and clandestine behaviors. Therefore, future research needs to more meticulously identify and understand the subgroups within these categories to avoid oversimplifying the understanding of HIV transmission.
HIV infection in MSM is a significant social concern, posing substantial harm to both infected individuals and society. Combating and curbing the transmission of HIV within this demographic necessitates collaborative efforts from government bodies, social organizations, and individuals. Such efforts should include the enhancement of pertinent knowledge dissemination, provision of healthcare services and support, and advocacy for the public acceptance of and respect for gay men and other male sexual partners.
HIV infection among MSM is a grave societal concern, resulting in substantial harm to both infected individuals and the community. Combating and mitigating HIV transmission within the MSM population necessitates collaborative endeavors involving the government, social institutions, and individuals. Such efforts should encompass bolstering knowledge dissemination, offering healthcare services and support, and fostering public acceptance of and respect for MSM.

## Conclusions
MSMW demonstrated significant disparities compared to MSMO in HIV-related risky behaviors. MSMW show a higher propensity to inquire about the HIV status of their recent casual sexual partners through offline means, alongside a noticeable rise in self-HIV testing rates over the previous six months. Conversely, there has been a significant decline in the percentage of MSMW undergoing two or more HIV tests, as well as in those opting for testing at hospitals, CDC facilities, or utilizing self-testing kits within the same timeframe. This trend implies that MSMW might be at an increased risk of intergroup HIV transmission, highlighting pronounced sexual orientation variances in their HIV testing practices. Consequently, there is a need to enhance the awareness and competencies regarding self-HIV testing within the MSM community, encourage routine self-HIV testing, and advocate for the adoption of self-testing kits. Moreover, it is crucial to improve the promotion and accessibility of HIV testing services for MSM, disseminate information on HIV infection risks within the MSM community, and expand the availability of HIV testing services in healthcare settings to enhance HIV testing rates among MSM individuals. For MSM with prior heterosexual encounters, targeted health education and interventions should be bolstered to deepen their understanding of personal health, elevate prevention awareness, and mitigate the risk of intergroup transmission.
