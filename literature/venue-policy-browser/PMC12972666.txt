## Abstract
Public health asset mapping involves working in a community partnership to form a systematic inventory of a local community’s health-promoting features. Assets include physical amenities such as parks or fitness centers, community clinics, welfare agencies, health-promoting non-governmental organizations, and businesses. A curated list of these resources constitutes an asset map that can be shared to promote better health and better health policies that build on local strengths rather than deficits to address upstream social determinants of health. Procedures for asset mapping must be adapted for local contexts because the identity and focus of assets differ significantly between countries. Asset mapping emerged as an element of an overall approach to asset-based community development (ABCD). However, an expository gap persists between a rich asset-based community development literature and practice-oriented guidance on how to operationalize these processes through coherent asset map design, data collection, analysis, and integration of qualitative insights, especially for the metropolitan context in the public health field. In response, we developed a systematic and replicable five-step guide to systematically map public health assets. Our approach integrates desk research with qualitative insights and produces a structured, evidence-based process for identifying and classifying super-connectors, thereby providing a robust foundation for subsequent knowledge-exchange and implementation. Public health practitioners, researchers, and community leaders can use this guide to identify and mobilize community assets towards co-creating better health policy and better policy implementation.

## Introduction
Asset-based community development identifies a community’s existing strengths to mobilize local partnerships that promote sustainable and self-reliant change. The initial step of asset mapping allows public health practitioners and policymakers to ally with community members to achieve a humanized and holistic public health practice ( ; ). A community-driven asset-mapping strategy encourages multi-stakeholder partnerships and collaborations among community institutions ( ). The process of producing a public health asset map enables a community to come together to address its unique health-related challenges.
The practice of asset-mapping fosters community development and builds new partnerships as a foundation for better and more inclusive health policies. It recognizes communities as active partners with valuable knowledge, networks, and resources, and does not consign people to the role of passive health-policy recipients ( ). As public health practitioners compile asset maps, they identify and work with potential partners to co-design effective public health programs ( ; ). This practice aligns with the Alma-Ata Declaration, which advocates for ‘maximum community participation in the planning of health services’ ( ). An archetype of asset-mapping for community health is the example of Jamkhed, India, where starting in 1970 Dr Raj and Mabelle Arole identified and partnered with social assets to improve water accessibility, livelihood creation, and the deployment of community health workers ( ), leading to tailored, sustainable, scalable, and adaptable solutions that addressed community public-health needs ( ). As a result of developing asset maps, communities can see and develop stakeholder partnerships to achieve local goals by connecting resources across health, education, livelihoods, and social services ( ). performed a scoping review of community health needs and asset assessments in guiding policy decisions and found that they enhance resource prioritization, strengthen stakeholder collaboration, and guide comprehensive health promotion strategies.
The asset-based community development literature in public health spans conceptual, methodological, and applied strands. Conceptually, strengths-based framings ( ; ) position community assets—not deficits (critiqued by ) —as the foundation for mobilizing for improvement. distill three strands of literature on asset mobilization as connecting assets, raising awareness, and enabling assets to thrive. Methodological literature includes structured mapping guides ( ) and case-led processes that link identified needs to mapped resources and action plans ( ). Sectoral typologies extend asset maps beyond health services to multi-domain assets ( ). Applied studies demonstrate the viability of city-scale asset maps ( ), Geographic Information System (GIS)-based access mapping ( ), practice-based health promotion ( ), culturally anchored asset identification ( ), and targeted workshops for improving access among priority groups ( ). Early work combining mapping with community dialogue ( ) further illustrates asset mapping’s versatility. Collectively, these studies show a variety of applications of asset maps—identification, classification, visualization, and selective mobilization.
Despite the vibrant research literature, there remains an expository gap for systematic guidance on how to undertake asset mapping from initial design through to the potential application of asset maps in public health decision-making. Existing studies excel in discrete areas, such as conceptual framing, mapping techniques, sectoral categorization, or community participation, but have not been integrated into a coherent sequence for producing a ready-to-use asset map that aligns objectives, data collection, analysis, and qualitative integration. There remains little operational guidance on how to combine desk-research data with qualitative insights and thus to identify super-connectors in complex urban settings or maintain dynamic and updatable inventories. , highlights the expository gap relating to asset mapping in the current literature.
In response, this study presents a systematic methodological guide for public health asset-mapping and illustrates it in the context of a high-density metropolitan area. Hong Kong presents a compelling setting for this illustration because it is marked by wide income disparities and diversity. Its dual healthcare system—comprising a public sector operated by government authorities and a private sector—further underscores the complexity of mapping community health assets. This how-to guide is designed to support public health practitioners in systematically identifying and documenting assets, while also engaging with the social infrastructure that underpins their use, namely, the community members, networks, and leaders that influence health-seeking behaviors and collective well-being ( ).
To strengthen adaptability and scalability, we developed structured tables summarizing key tips for each process, allowing practitioners to adapt the approach across varying contexts and to scale the application to larger or more complex settings ( , , ). To enhance practicality, we present potentialdilemmas and suggested solutions ( , , ) to help teams troubleshoot challenges during field applications. For clarity, we include plain-language definitions for each procedural term in – and a consolidated set of illustrative examples in , that clarify who carries out each task, what it involves in practice, and its purpose within the mapping process. The worksheet templates ( ) offer ready-to-use tools that can be directly customized for local implementation. applies the 5-step framework to a Hong Kong case example, using short vignettes to demonstrate how community and stakeholder feedback modified decisions at each step, produced concrete outcomes, and was documented for ongoing verification. ## Application of the 5-step asset-mapping guideline in Hong Kong

Context. The 5-step guideline was piloted across six Hong Kong districts (dense urban cores, new towns, and peripheral areas) to test whether a standardized procedure could be applied and adapted through community and stakeholder feedback and documented with an auditable trail.

Step 1 in practice: how community feedback adjusted boundaries and classifications.

In one district, the planning team used Step 1 to define community boundaries and agree on which assets counted were ‘in scope.’ During the first workshop, residents from an housing estate in this district pointed out that the maternal-and-child health center they routinely used lay just outside the administrative district and had been excluded from the draft asset list. In response, the mapping team introduced a 1 km/15-min service buffer and reclassified the center as an in-scope core health asset. This revision was recorded as Boundary Rule v1.1 in the centralized verification-change log and time-stamped as the basis for future quarterly reviews. The new buffer then prompted a targeted scan along the extended boundary, which identified an additional resident-led exercise group previously treated as informal and upgraded it to a community connector. The revised boundary and updated classifications revealed overlapping outreach zones between two non-governmental organizations and led to a coordination meeting to reduce duplication of activities. This vignette illustrates how community feedback in Step 1 directly modified boundary rules and asset classifications, produced concrete changes in the asset lists, and was formally documented for ongoing verification.

Step 2 in practice: how stakeholder feedback refined desk data and triggered re-verification

As the team applied Step 2 to compile desk-based asset data, they used official and non-governmental organization directories to generate the initial list. During this process, a District Health Centre liaison alerted the team that a youth center recorded as ‘active’ in official registries had recently merged and no longer offered regular programs. Rather than deleting the record outright, the mapping team re-coded the asset as ‘status uncertain,’ flagged it for field validation in Step 4, and documented the issue and its source in the centralized verification-change log. The entry was time-stamped and assigned a follow-up date in the quarterly re-verification cycle. This vignette shows how stakeholder feedback during desk compilation led to an immediate adjustment in asset status, prevented silent carry-over of outdated information, and was formally logged and scheduled for re-check, operationalizing the ‘timestamp assets’ and ‘arrange quarterly re-verification’ protocols.

Step 3 in practice: how community workshops corrected tables and improved accuracy

In Step 3, the team transformed cleaned desk-data into draft tables for community review. Printed maps were taken to validation workshops where residents and local guides were invited to annotate the layouts directly. During these sessions, participants circled facilities that had closed or relocated and wrote in several missing sites, including small exercise corners and informal gathering spaces not captured in official directories. In total, 14 misclassified or obsolete assets were corrected before finalizing the digital tables. Each confirmed change was entered by the data steward into the centralized change log, documented under ‘Community validation session 2024 Q3,’ and cross-referenced with the verification log. This vignette illustrates how community feedback in Step 3 directly modified the spatial representation of assets, produced measurable improvements in map accuracy, and ensured that every edit was traceable and time-stamped within the validation–reverification loop.

Step 4 in practice: how qualitative feedback reclassified assets and corrected the map

In Step 4, the team used qualitative interviews and field visits to challenge and refine the desk-based classifications. During analysis, interviewees explained that a temple-run hall previously coded as an active health asset was in practice used mainly as a festival or occasional event venue, prompting its reclassification as a latent/event-triggered asset. Follow-up field visits also confirmed that the youth center flagged in Step 2 had ceased regular operations and now functioned only intermittently on short-term grants, and its status was updated to transient. In total, 37 records were corrected or re-coded based on interview and field evidence. Each change was entered into the centralized verification-change log, linked to the corresponding transcript or field note, and time-stamped. This vignette shows how qualitative feedback closed the loop between community experience and the desk map, yielding more accurate classifications and a transparent audit trail of all revisions.

Step 5 in practice: how stakeholder insights revealed super-connectors

In Step 5, interview and field-note material were reviewed to identify individuals and organizations that routinely bridged sectors and levels of the system. In one district, a District Health Center (DHC) coordinator who regularly linked non-governmental organizations with government clinics was coded as a meso-level super-connector; a long-standing resident leader who mobilized ethnic-minority mothers and elderly tenants around health activities was coded as a micro-level connector with bridging capacity; and a council member serving on several citywide taskforces was classified as a macro-level connector. These designations were entered into the central asset database, tagged by level and reach, and recorded, with time-stamps, in the verification log alongside references to the underpinning interviews, so they can be revisited during subsequent review cycles. This vignette illustrates how stakeholder insights were translated into concrete connector classifications, enriching the map to show not only which assets exist but also who can activate and link them, with each decision documented for future review.

## Step 1. Design and plan the asset map
The success of asset mapping begins with careful design and planning. A critical precondition of design is including community partners in the process. Our guide presumes that a coalition of stakeholders has already coalesced to the point where they can begin to ask a set of critical questions to ensure that the mapping process is structured, inclusive, and contextually relevant ( ). presents the key planning questions for Step 1 and explains their purpose by providing the underlying rationale, suggested response categories, and guidance for adapting the framework to different settings. builds on this by outlining common planning dilemmas and offering corresponding solutions. complements these tables as an editable tool for stakeholders to enter their own district data, roles, and timelines. Together, these tools translate the planning guidance into a concrete and locally tailored implementation plan.

## Step 2. Collect desk data
The initial phase of asset mapping data collection uses desk research to identify community health assets through a thorough review of secondary resources. and outline a structured and step-by-step approach for desk-based assets identification. They outline how to define objectives, confirm data sources, design the search strategy, and apply inclusion and exclusion criteria, and they pair each sub-step with common dilemmas and practical solutions to support implementation across diverse settings. complements and with a standardized template for collecting desk data. In practice, teams use the tables to plan and troubleshoot the desk-data process, while the worksheet ensures consistent and comparable documentation.
Local health data complements the asset lists by offering demographic and health profiles that also help target relevant assets. Examples of resources include government websites and research databases ( ; ; ; ). Having local population health indicators at hand ensures that assets align with measurable health priorities, enhancing the credibility and relevance of the mapping process while evaluating their real-world contribution to community health with specific sub-district level data ( ; ; .).

## Step 3. Analyze and visualize desk data
Theheatmaps are used to visualize service types within asset categories and link them to district-level health strengths derived from community health profiles. This provides a comprehensive view of asset mapping results, allowing assessment of community resources and their contribution to health outcomes. Grayscale gradient maps complement this by comparing the relative stock of public health assets across categories. further supports this process with editable templates and examples for generating customized summary tables, helping teams translate the cleaned desk-data into clear and actionable outputs for community analysis and validation. Data visualizations can serve as a stepping stone to set the stage for qualitative interviews, focusing on strengths in well-performing districts and offering insights into replicating successful practices across other communities.
Having local data in bar charts or other graphics to highlight district-specific health indicators is very helpful as a conversation starter for key informant interviews with community members about relevant local assets. Color codes can highlight high-connectivity nodes, where networks of healthcare providers, non-governmental organizations, and other resources are dense, well-linked, and actively utilized. Conversely, lighter, sparser areas flag gaps in resource availability, indicating districts where public health resources may be underutilized, disconnected, or insufficient to meet community needs.

## Step 4. Collect and analyze qualitative data
Talking to community partners to ask about local resources and assets is critical. and present a multi-step guide to ensure the readiness, rigor, and feasibility of qualitative data collection and analysis, along with tips for adaptability, scalability, and practicality. The process starts with preparation so that the objectives inform what questions to ask, who to include in the sample, and how to approach them. complements this by providing a standardized template for collecting qualitative data. In practice, teams use the tables to design and troubleshoot the qualitative component, while the worksheet ensures consistent documentation that supports systematic analysis and verification. This approach supports comprehensive and reliable insights critical to understanding community asset dynamics. Strategies such as leveraging trusted intermediaries, flexible scheduling, and highlighting mutual benefits are commonly discussed in the literature ( ; ; ). After the encounters with community members, there can be a systematic approach to reviewing the interview transcripts. The scope of this paper precludes a presentation of qualitative data collection and analysis methods. There are many excellent reviews elsewhere ( ; ). Asset-mappers should regularly revisit to compare, cross-reference, and integrate assets from desk research and qualitative sources.

## Step 5. Identify and categorize asset super-connectors

## Step 5.1. Identify super-connectors in the qualitative data
The qualitative data and group discussion often reveal super-connectors: individuals or groups that maintain substantially more ties than average with a wide variety of different people in a district ( ). For example, in Hong Kong, we identified volunteers who regularly phoned households of mentally impaired individuals and schoolchildren in one district. They had a large number of connections with outreach workers and other non-governmental organizations assisting.
To strengthen the conceptual basis, we now interpret these actors through social network theory: they resemble Granovetter’s ‘weak-tie’ bridges that move information across groups ( ) and Burt’s ‘structural hole’ brokers that connect disconnected parts of the network ( ). In community health terms, they also act as bridging social capital and boundary spanners, enabling coordination between institutions, assets, and community leaders ( ). Because such actors increase the utility of other assets and improve reach ( ) we classify them systematically.

## Step 5.2. Categorize asset super-connectors
Super-connectors can be classified into three levels (micro-, meso- and macro) to reflect the scope of their brokerage. At the micro-level, super-connectors operate within neighborhoods or estates; they provide tailored support, carry trusted information, and reinforce bonding ties to individuals or groups, addressing needs and ensuring access to essential services. At the meso-level, super-connectors link community groups to service organizations; they function as healthcare and social care navigators, and bridging actors that facilitate inter-organizational collaboration and service transition. At the macro-level, super-connectors connect district networks to higher-level or cross-district structures; they serve as strategic resource coordinators and perform brokerage roles consistent with structural-hole theory, focusing on systemic solutions, capacity building, and infrastructure development to meet broader community needs. This three-level classification makes explicit how the observed actors map onto established broker concepts in social network theory and why identifying them supports community resilience. provides a practical template for identifying and analyzing asset super-connectors. It prompts teams to record each connector’s role, level (micro, meso, or macro), sectors bridged, and typical activities, and to link them to specific assets. In practice, teams use this worksheet to make visible who can activate or link resources across the system and to prioritize these connectors for engagement and follow-up. , shows an example of applying this analytical approach across districts.

## Discussion
This paper presents a practical, adaptable, and replicable five-step asset-mapping guideline designed for systematically mapping public health assets in large metropolitan areas with complex urban settings and diverse districts. By combining desk research with qualitative approaches, the guideline captures not only tangible resources but also the super-connectors whose interactions and exchanges shape community health capacity.
Readers could also consult the UCLA Asset Mapping Guidelines ( ) and to see other asset mapping guidance with approaches that emphasize stakeholder participation in data collection. Other literature complements our five-step guide by uncovering the collaborative actions between various stakeholders to develop engagement capacity in public health programs, aligning with community strengths. For instance, demonstrated how asset mapping in a community-based participatory research framework helped identify obesity-related resources, enabling targeted referrals and increasing access to local health interventions. Similarly, showed that asset maps allowed clinics to connect underserved populations to neighborhood support services, addressing both health and social determinants of health ( ).
This guide can be adapted and scaled across metropolitan areas by adjusting four core dimensions. (i) Asset inventory scope. Larger metropolitan regions can apply the guide at sub-city levels (e.g. by district or borough) and merge results into a composite map, while smaller cities can conduct a single unified mapping exercise. (ii) Resource calibration. Cities with rich data infrastructures can leverage detailed Geographic Information System (GIS) layers and real-time datasets in the desk research phase, whereas lower-resource settings can use open-source mapping tools and community-led surveys to fulfill the same steps at lower cost. (iii) Modular workflow. Each step of the guide is designed to stand alone, allowing practitioners to select the scale and depth most suited to their local capacity while still preserving the guide’s end-to-end structure. (iv) Governance integration. In highly centralized governance systems, the guide’s stakeholder engagement steps can be coordinated through a single entity, whereas in decentralized systems, each administrative unit can adapt the engagement protocols before aligning outputs to a shared citywide agenda. This flexibility allows for consistent application across cities of varying sizes, governance arrangements, and resource capacities, without compromising methodological integrity.
Although developed for metropolitan contexts, the guide’s core principles can be applied in non-metropolitan areas with appropriate adjustments. First, systematic asset categorization remains central, but categories may need to reflect local realities such as multi-purpose community halls, mobile health units, or informal providers. Second, integration of qualitative perspectives is essential, since participatory methods, including key informant interviews, focus groups, or participatory rural appraisal, can compensate for limited statistical or geospatial data. Third, identification of super-connectors is often even more critical in rural and peri-urban settings, where a small number of actors may bridge multiple sectors and sustain community health capacity. By adapting these principles to reflect local asset types, data availability, and engagement practices, the core methodology remains feasible beyond metropolitan contexts.
After mapping public health assets, community members can address complex health challenges by relying on stakeholders’ expertise and resources ( ; ). When asset-map curators share their findings with their partners, it creates a platform to coordinate resources for addressing a district’s priorities through a collective-impact approach ( ).
One strategy that facilitates the process of community engagement is the Support, Action, Listen, Transfer-Community Life Competence Process (SALT-CLCP) model, which provides a communicative framework to promote inclusive policies to address public health issues ( ). Once researchers have shortlisted assets and stakeholders suitable for engaging with communities, SALT-CLCP engages participants to find a common identity relating to their organizational and individual narratives and shared goals. Then, using the spoken and written narratives of workshop participants, the workshop facilitator will define their common objective and perform rounds of self-assessment to collectively devise an action plan that takes stock of these assets and stakeholders’ current standing in their public health policymaking. SALT-CLCP workshop participants will then execute their action plan and take stock of their results as they address their public health concerns ( ). Asset-based participatory practices exemplify active implementation of the Alma-Ata Declaration and the Astana Declaration in 1978 and 2008, respectively ( ). Community participation that includes human rights and concern for equity is best assured when people become involved in their community’s health policy formation ( ).
Even though the approach can help communities identify strengths, link them to local priorities, and open space for dialogue across sectors, regulatory and organizational rules can further restrict recognition of community needs ( , ). Some stakeholders hesitate to share struggles, which reduces opportunities to uncover local vulnerabilities ( ). Participation often skews toward institutional actors because gatekeepers control access, and voices from the broader community are left out ( , ( ). Data sharing is another challenge: reluctance to release information, inconsistencies between sources, and the unstable nature of grant-funded programs all make it hard to keep maps current and reliable ( ; ). Even when dialogue occurs, links between community insights and actual resource allocation or policy response remain weak, especially in fragmented urban systems ( ; ). Thus, community asset mapping works best when it is inclusive and transparent.

## Conclusion
This guide presents one approach to replicable asset-mapping that allows context-based modifications. As the guide is applied, other public health asset-mappers can evaluate their community settings to enumerate their healthcare resources to support community empowerment.
As asset mapping gains wider application as a core practice in community health, it will be transformative for health policy and planning. Pivoting from a focus on problems and deficits to include an inventory of solutions and strengths is a powerful antidote to unfounded charges of elitism and paternalism in public health. It is envisaged that this guide can help practitioners, researchers, and policymakers design and implement impactful asset mapping projects in other contexts. Asset maps can assist communities in using their latent strengths for innovative solutions to pressing public health challenges by fostering collaboration.
