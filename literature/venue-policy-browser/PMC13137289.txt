## Abstract

## INTRODUCTION
Neighborhood “third places” are increasingly studied as contextual determinants of cognitive health, yet the reliability of geospatial datasets is poorly understood.

## METHODS
We evaluated Advan Research, Data Axle, FourSquare, and the National Establishment Time Series (NETS) across five categories: cafes/coffee shops, civic/social organizations, libraries, performing arts/museums, and recreation centers/gyms. Bayesian multilevel logistic regression models estimated locational accuracy and categorical validity for 13,168 coder ratings of 4876 unique businesses. Qualitative content analysis examined reflections from 18 coders.

## RESULTS
Advan showed high locational accuracy (≥95%), with Data Axle and FourSquare performing well for most categories and NETS the lowest. Inaccuracies stemmed from outdated or incorrect addresses and non‐fixed locations. Advan, Data Axle, and FourSquare performed moderately well for categorization (70% to 97%) and NETS the worst. Misclassification reflected ambiguous purposes, misleading names, and uncertainty around third places.

## DISCUSSION
Study‐specific dataset selection, triangulation, cleaning, error calibration, and clearer third place conceptualization are critical to strengthen neighborhood‐based dementia research.

Keywords: built environment, business establishments, data validity, dementia risk, exposome, geospatial data, location accuracy, mixed‐methods evaluation, social infrastructure, third place categorization

## BACKGROUND
Neighborhoods are increasingly recognized as central to the “exposome” – an array of physical, chemical, social, political, and economic exposures that have multilayered interactions with human health. Among emerging attempts to crystallize connections between the exposome and health, Cognability advances findings on neighborhoods and dementia risk through a socioecological model. , Third places – informal sites where people gather outside of home and work – are central to this framework. Sites such as parks, coffee shops, libraries, and recreation centers are theorized to support cognitive health behaviors including education, physical activity, cognitive stimulation, social engagement, and reduced stress (Figure  ). , 

 Growing evidence links third places to dementia risk through these pathways. For example, access to senior centers, social organizations, eateries, , museums, performing arts venues, parks, recreation centers, and retail are positively linked to cognitive health outcomes. Crucially, third places offer greater mechanistic specificity than broader neighborhood measures. Summary indicators like neighborhood socioeconomic status provide useful but often conceptually circular characterizations (e.g., people who are economically vulnerable are also freighted with health vulnerabilities ), while measures like retail density remain too coarse to guide action (e.g., treating a neighborhood dense with coffee shops as equivalent to one dense with tobacco stores). In contrast, direct measurement of third places captures the specific establishments that support social engagement, cognitive stimulation, physical activity, stress reduction, and other health behaviors relevant to risk for Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD).
Producing evidence on the associations between third places and ADRD relies on geospatial data that can accurately characterize neighborhoods. These data are often too prohibitive for research teams to construct from scratch; thankfully, third‐party providers increasingly make contextual data available at varying price points and levels of accessibility. For example, the National Establishment Time Series (NETS) database provides coverage of 87.5 million establishments from 1990 to 2022. These business microdata are widely used by academic researchers and government agencies either through direct point‐level data purchase or secondary data products, often tabulated at the Census tract and/or zip code level. Other frequently used data products include Data Axle, which advertises itself as having the industry's most complete coverage of small‐ and medium‐sized businesses for companies seeking detailed location data. FourSquare's OS Places dataset leverages machine learning to synthesize over 16 billion check‐ins, third‐party data, and web crawls to create expansive point‐of‐interest (POI) data intended to “drive innovation” in the geospatial community. , Advan, which uses SafeGraph's POI universe, analyzes cellphone location data for traffic to businesses and neighborhoods. , , Today, Dewey provides access to both Advan and SafeGraph via paid subscriptions.

## RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: We reviewed the interdisciplinary literature on neighborhoods, social infrastructure, and cognitive health, in addition to prior studies evaluating geospatial datasets, using traditional databases (PubMed, Google Scholar). Prior work demonstrated significant associations between neighborhood “third places” and cognitive outcomes but rarely interrogated the reliability of underlying contextual data.

Interpretation: Our findings demonstrate that contextual datasets vary substantially in locational accuracy, categorical validity, temporal resolution, and coverage of third places. This study highlights a key consideration of quantitative neighborhood‐based dementia research: Error is unavoidable, even in widely used and well‐regarded contextual datasets. By foregrounding business establishment data construction, geolocation errors, third place classification tradeoffs, and purpose‐oriented correctness, this study advances methodological rigor in research on the exposome.

Future directions: Future research should evaluate spatial and demographic patterning of dataset errors, incorporate temporality and situational uses of third places, integrate visitation data, and develop conceptually grounded third place classifications to strengthen research on neighborhoods and dementia risk.

Still, uncertainty remains regarding the accuracy of these databases. While a limited set of analyses compares third‐party and the US Census Bureau's data products (e.g., studies comparing NETS with Business Dynamics Statistics , ), deeper dives into the quality of these pre‐constructed sources remains largely unexplored. Lacking an understanding of the reliability of these datasets constitutes a critical knowledge gap that has only grown as their use has proliferated across sectors. Academics, urban planners, and policymakers rely on these data to assess and plan interventions centered on place‐based determinants of health, such as access to alcohol outlets, place visitation‐associated risk for depression and cardiovascular disease, and overall mobility patterns. While companies offer variable descriptions of their data to promote transparency, paywalls, proprietary algorithms, and intellectual property protections often restrict data users’ ability to evaluate errors, biases, and ontologies.
To advance the field, it is essential to evaluate the accuracy, completeness, and epistemological foundations of these contextual datasets. In this study, we compared four US contextual databases with varying geographic and temporal coverage, price points, and POI classification systems: NETS, Advan, FourSquare, and Data Axle. Our team investigated how accurately these databases captured third places of interest to inform and advance research on the exposome and ADRD risk.

2.

## METHODS
2.1.

## Focal third places
Third places are informal public gathering spots outside of home (first place) and work (second place) that foster community, social connection, and a sense of belonging. Introduced by sociologist Ray Oldenburg in The Great Good Place, these sites include coffee shops, libraries, bars, parks, gyms, and community centers where people can informally interact. They are vital for community life and ideally promote social cohesion and offer a neutral, comfortable environment for people to gather, connect, and build relationships. Third places support both individual and collective health and wellbeing. , 

For this analysis, we chose five focal categories: cafes and coffee shops, civic and social organizations, libraries, performing arts sites and museums, and recreation centers and gyms. Hypothesized ADRD‐relevant mechanisms articulated in the Cognability framework guided the selection of these categories (Figure  ). Recreation centers/gyms represent the physical activity pathway; civic/social organizations and cafes/coffee shops stimulate social engagement; and libraries and performing arts sites/museums capture cognitive stimulation (engagement in creative, complex, and learning activities , , , , , ). Prior quantitative analyses have identified significant associations between these categories and cognitive outcomes. , , , , , We also intentionally selected categories spanning public institutions (e.g., libraries), non‐profit organizations (e.g., civic/social groups), and private commercial establishments (e.g., chain coffee shops) to assess how different data‐generation processes may influence measurement accuracy for theoretically meaningful social infrastructure. The research team collaboratively developed a working definition for each focal third place category, and examples of sites included versus excluded regarding belonging to the conceptualized third place category (Table  ). We constructed definitions by first articulating the hypothesized mechanism relevant to ADRD risk and then identified place characteristics most likely to activate those pathways.
 
2.2.

## Contextual datasets
We selected four national geospatial data sources to compare (Table  ). They represent a range of price points, level of temporal coverage, and degree of penetration into current exposome research.
 2.2.1.

## Advan research
Advan Research compiles data for nine million establishments from multiple sectors, including retail, hospitality, consumer services, energy, technology, healthcare, and financials, at both monthly and weekly intervals from 2019 to 2025 across the United States, Canada, and the United Kingdom. Advan Research uses multiparameter models to analyze panel cellphone location data aggregated at both the place of interest and Census block group levels. This dataset contains business name, location (street address and latitude/longitude), start and end time for measurement, number of visits, number of unique visitors, dwell time, and related visits to other brands. Advan Research uses the 2017 North American Industry Classification System (NAICS) codes to classify these establishments.

2.2.2.

## Data Axle Business
Data Axle Business compiles annual historical company data from 1997 to 2024, drawn from Infogroup's US Business Database covering 24 million establishments across the United States. Locations covered include churches, eating and drinking establishments, hotels, professional services, provider services, and medical facilities. This dataset contains information about company name, location information (mailing address and latitude/longitude), employee counts, and sales volume. Data Axle Business uses both Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) and NAICS codes to classify these establishments.

2.2.3.

## FourSquare
FourSquare draws data from a variety of sources, including web crawls, data syndicators, and partnerships with data providers. It reported coverage of more than 105 million commercial establishments in 2025 across 247 countries and territories. The businesses in this dataset include arts and entertainment, business and professional services, community and government, dining and drinking, health and medicine, landmarks and outdoors, retail, sports and recreation, and travel and transportation. This dataset contains business name, location (street address and latitude/longitude), entered/refreshed/closed dates, contact information (email, website, telephone number), and commercial rating. FourSquare provides a classification of establishments across more than 1000 categories.

2.2.4.

## NETS
The NETS database provides annual counts of establishments recorded and is updated each January from 1990 to 2022, capturing over 87.5 million private, public, and governmental establishments across the United States. NETS is compiled by Wall & Associates using both employer and non‐employer business establishment data from Dun & Bradstreet, which draws on information from secretaries of state, Yellow Pages, court records, credit inquiries, and direct telephone contact. This dataset contains business name, location (street address and latitude/longitude), headquarter linkages, year business started, years when business was active, employment, and estimated annual sales. NETS uses both SIC (1990 to 2022) and NAICS (1990 to 2022) codes to classify these establishments.

2.2.5.

## Third place data selection
We coded third places based on our derived category definitions (Table  ) rather than relying on how each data source classified them. Because classification systems differ across datasets (Table  ) – and none were designed explicitly for third place academic research – we developed dataset‐specific strategies to identify comparable categories. For Advan Research and Data Axle Business, we relied on NAICS codes to identify cafes/coffee shops, civic and social organizations, libraries, performing arts sites and museums, and recreation centers/gyms. For FourSquare, we used their proprietary place codes that most closely aligned with our conceptual definitions. For NETS, we used SIC codes as a finer‐grained classification system compared to NAICS. Table  presents the detailed codes used across all three systems.
 These decisions were neither neutral nor automatic. NAICS and SIC codes were developed for industrial and economic reporting rather than to capture everyday social infrastructure. This misalignment introduces fundamental tensions in any effort to map theoretically relevant categories onto secondary data – a task that is unavoidable for researchers considering using these, or similar, large‐scale geospatial business databases. For example, identifying independent and chain cafes/coffee shops requires selecting NAICS codes 722515 “snack and nonalcoholic beverage bars,” in addition to big chains like Starbucks, which are coded under 722513 “limited‐service restaurants.” However, this latter category also includes delicatessen restaurants, pizza delivery shops, family restaurants, takeout eating places, fast‐food and fast casual restaurants, takeout sandwich shops, and limited‐service pizza parlors. Removing “limited‐service restaurants” undercounts cafes and coffee shops, but including it significantly muddies the category with fast‐food outlets. One might also consider NAICS code 722330 “mobile food services” – which includes coffee carts, but also many other types of food carts as well such as trucks selling ice cream, cookies, popcorn, hamburgers, chicken, pizza, and specialty cuisine. Our coding approach therefore describes the fit (and misfit) between our theoretically derived third place categories and how places are listed in real‐world business classification systems.

2.3.

## Analytical approach
2.3.1.

## Overview
We evaluated the accuracy of business locations and categorical assignments across four contextual datasets: NETS, Data Axle, FourSquare and Advan. Within each data source, we evaluated 250 randomly selected sites from five focal third place categories: cafes and coffee shops, civic and social organizations, libraries, performing arts venues and museums, and recreation centers and gyms. This yielded 5000 unique sites for review. Our plan was for three coders to independently evaluate each site, leading to a target sample of 15,000 business ratings.

2.3.2.

## Research team
The coding team consisted of 16 undergraduate, graduate, staff, and postdoctoral researchers from four universities (University of Colorado Boulder, University of Minnesota, University of Michigan, and Florida State University), supervised by four faculty members (authors A.D.J., D.R., J.M.F., and M.H.E.). Team members had training across multiple disciplines including geography, sociology, gerontology, and public health, as well as qualitative, quantitative, and geospatial methodologies.
Team members represented a range of identities (Table ), including variation in age (20 to 41 years old), gender (female, male), race/ethnicity (Asian, Black/African American, Hispanic/Latinx, Middle Eastern or North African, White), and years lived in the United States (0.5 to 42 years). This diversity supported more comprehensive and contextually sensitive coding.

2.3.3.

## Coding process
All coders participated in standardized training, which included synchronous training sessions with faculty leads and a detailed written training guide ( ) developed by authors M.H.E. and J.M.F. to promote consistency across reviewers. Coders independently completed a training dataset of 25 randomly selected establishments (five per focal third place category) to practice evaluating the following four dimensions:

Belong: Do you believe a given business conceptually belongs within its assigned third place category?

0 = no, I do not believe that this establishment fits within this category

1 = yes, I do believe that this establishment fits within this category

Belong_confidence: How confident you are in your assessment of whether an establishment is properly categorized. In other words, this measure should describe how certain you are in the value you chose for “belong.”

0 = I am not confident in my assessment

1 = I am confident in my assessment

Location_correct: Is the establishment located at its given street address and/or latitude/longitude coordinates?

0 = no, this business is not or never was located at this address

1 = yes, this business is or was at some point located at this address (Note: The data may be a few years old; therefore, businesses may have closed and/or changed locations since the point in time that these data represent. In these cases, please record a “1” if you find compelling evidence to suggest that the establishment in question was located at its listed address at any point in time. If you are uncertain, please mark “0.”)

Notes: Use this area to record notes about any particular business: its fit, its location, etc. Feel free to write in anything of interest here to help you keep track of broader trends you discover in the data.

Open‐ended free text

Authors met after practice coding to discuss their experiences, improve third place categorizations, and clarify coding guidelines. Author D.R. then anonymized and distributed coding assignments to ensure the independence of coder evaluations. Each coder was assigned between 832 and 948 sites. The team met weekly to review quantitative progress, troubleshoot emerging issues, and engage in qualitative discussions of ambiguous or challenging sites. Faculty leads regularly reviewed coding sheets and weekly reflections to ensure consistency in coding practices and adequate progress.

2.4.

## Final analysis
2.4.1.

## Quantitative
Our final analytical sample consisted of n = 13,168 ratings of n = 4876 unique businesses sampled from the four candidate datasets. Ratings were provided by 16 coders, with one of the 17 planned raters leaving the project before beginning the evaluation process. Fifteen of the raters completed 99% to 100% of their evaluations, while one completed approximately 28%. Overall, 71.8% of businesses were assessed by three distinct raters, 26.4% by two raters, and 1.8% by a single rater.
With these data, we focused on estimating both of the following:

Prlocationcorrect=yes|dataset,proposedcategoryPrbelong=yes|dataset,proposedcategoryThe former summarizes the accuracy of each dataset's geospatial coordinates, while the latter captures how frequently the businesses located in our sample match their intended third place coding (e.g., for proposed category = Cafes and Coffee Shops in dataset = Advan, how often our NAICS recipe from Table  recovered a business from Advan that our research team deemed a cafe or coffee shop). Importantly, this means Pr(belong = yes) reflects a joint process: the accuracy of each data source's provided labels and the precision of our scheme mapping those labels to target third place categories.
We fit Bayesian multilevel logistic regression models to estimate our target quantities. In separate models – Model 1 and Model 2, respectively – we regressed our location and categorical belonging indicators on the dataset a business was sampled from, its proposed category, and their interaction, with rater‐ and business‐specific random intercepts included as well. We derived estimates of our focal probabilities from the models’ posterior distributions, evaluated at average rater and business effects. All model priors were constructed to be weakly informative to help regularize results, and models were fit using the brms package in R. 

2.4.2.

## Qualitative
We used qualitative content analysis to interpret the weekly and final reflection data of 16 coders and two faculty supervisors with qualitative expertise (authors A.D.J. and J.M.F.). Team members followed the prompt: Each week, please reflect on your coding experience. Include the categories you assessed, overarching observations about belonging and location accuracy, and any issues, uncertainties, or notable patterns you observed. Weekly entries varied in length from a few sentences to multiple paragraphs (approximately one page of text) and addressed team member experiences and observations of coding the five third place categories across four contextual datasets.
Authors J.M.F. and A.D.J. followed a systematic process of coding followed by identifying themes and patterns. This approach facilitated deep immersion in the data, which enabled new insights to emerge into rater‐ and category‐specific decision‐making. J.M.F. and A.D.J. developed themes inductively without imposing preconceived structures. Guided by our research aims – How accurately do contextual datasets capture third places? What systematic errors and mechanisms underlie misclassification? – we worked systematically and iteratively through the data. As noted earlier, J.M.F. and A.D.J. followed a general inductive approach so that findings could emerge from dominant and recurring themes in the raw qualitative data.
The analytic process involved five steps. First, J.M.F. and A.D.J. independently read all qualitative reflections data to gain a sense of the whole. During this immersion, J.M.F. and A.D.J. wrote notes, documented preliminary impressions, and recorded potential codes. Second, J.M.F. developed a coding scheme: an inductive approach in which codes flowed directly from the data. J.M.F. discussed these codes with all co‐authors to refine the framework. Third, J.M.F. read all data and assigned it to relevant codes, with additional input from A.D.J. to ensure completeness and accuracy. Fourth, authors A.D.J., M.H.E., D.R., Y.S., and J.M.F. reviewed material to share thoughts, impressions, and takeaways. We refined and grouped codes into themes that meaningfully organized material around central organizing concepts. Fifth, J.M.F. finalized category names and definitions, summarized themes in writing, and selected exemplar quotations. All co‐authors reviewed and refined the qualitative results.
We enhanced methodological rigor through multiple strategies: (1) peer debriefing with colleagues outside the core study team to review analytic decisions and interrogate potential biases, (2) negative case analysis to search for and integrate contradictory examples, and (3) maintaining a clear audit trail documenting each step from data organization through theme development. 

3.

## RESULTS
While the majority of places were accurately classified and geocoded to the correct location, there was wide variation across datasets and third place categories, often produced through a set of repeating errors. Libraries were the overall cleanest and most consistent category: The location names were usually correct and addresses complete, and any issues tended to be minor (e.g., street addresses were incomplete, but geographic coordinates were correct). Civic and social organizations tended to be the least correctly identified across datasets.
The task was more challenging and nuanced than initially expected. Coding for W.K. “made [her] more aware of how messy real‐world data on third places can be, especially when we try to connect categories, locations, and ideas of belonging.” Coder S.L. wrote:

Although each entry in the dataset provided a name, theoretical categorization, and a geographic coordinate, the act of verifying these details established that location and belonging are not objective attributes but interpretive outcomes which are molded by inconsistent data sources, differential platform representations, and theoretical ambiguities entrenched within third place typologies… Assessments of “accuracy” or “belonging” vary across place types and what this means for measuring third places.
Below we discuss consistent strengths and challenges across the contextual datasets for location accuracy and third place categorization.
3.1.

## Location accuracy
Table  and Figure  describe our findings regarding the accuracy of the addresses provided in each dataset. The former displays parameters from a multilevel model of location accuracy, while the latter plots predicted probabilities that an average rater found agreement between a business's listed and actual location.
 The addresses provided by each data source were generally accurate with measurable variation across contextual data source and third place category. Advan, for instance, provided highly accurate geolocational data across all categories, with all location accuracy estimates hovering at or above 95%. Data Axle and FourSquare performed well in most areas, with each having a single category falling below a 75% accuracy rate: Pr(location correct) = 0.695 for Data Axle's accounting of civic and social organizations and 0.606 for museums and performing arts in FourSquare. NETS demonstrated the worst performance overall, with particular inaccuracies in reporting civic and social organization addresses. Model estimates suggest that NETS provided accurate geocodes for this type of third place only 35.6% of the time.
Our qualitative analysis provides insight into the underlying processes generating these evaluations among our coder rater pool. The most common reasons for inaccurate geocoding were (1) outdated listing information (including establishment closures); (2) missing, incomplete, or incorrect addresses; and (3) not a fixed establishment location.
3.1.1.

## Outdated listing information
Coders frequently encountered establishments that were temporarily or permanently closed or had moved to a new location. As Coder Y.S. noted: “There are temporal lags between historical data and current locations.” While some establishments were indeed open during the year of contextual data tabulation (e.g., 2024 for Data Axle and Advan, 2022 for NETS), other coders noted instances where businesses “closed quite some time ago” (Coder B.D.) before the year of contextual data tabulation. Reviewers also encountered confusion when one contemporary source stated that the establishment was closed, but another source suggested potential activity. As coder S.L. wrote: “The use of multiple data sources [to check establishments], including Google Maps, Yelp, Apple Maps, and MapQuest, showed that each platform exercises its own epistemic authority, often producing conflicting representations.” Multiple coders cautioned that MapQuest often contained outdated information for now‐closed businesses. In some instances, establishments did not have any information online, prompting questions about whether they ever existed and how they originally entered the dataset. For Coder A.J.B.: “My biggest observation and issue was how did these data sets find these places and information? A lot of these places are either closed, never existed, or don't have much online information at all.”
Ambiguous operational status made location assessments uncertain. Coder S.Z. wrote as follows:

Outdated or inaccurate information is prevalent throughout the datasets. Many places have changed ownership, purpose, or cease to exist, yet their old data remains. Even when websites are still available, it's often unclear whether an organization is still operating or its current role. I never saw the raw data. However, if the raw data did not provide the scraping date, this raises a deeper question about the temporality of data: how do you encode a place that once evoked a sense of belonging but no longer does?
Coders reflected on the dynamic nature of third place openings/closings and the need for temporal awareness when approaching these data.

3.1.2.

## Missing, incomplete, and incorrect addresses
Wrong addresses were often less than 16 kilometers (~10 miles) from the actual location but could range from under 1.5 kilometers (around a mile) to more than 160 kilometers (~100 miles) off. Performing arts sites and museums was the category coders qualitatively identified as frequently flagged for street address issues. Common errors included missing street addresses and zip codes, missing leading zeros (e.g., zip code listed erroneously as “2108” instead of “02108”), or incorrect addresses (e.g., correct except for the wrong N/S/E/W directional indicator). This was also the case for some cafes and coffee shops, though correct latitude and longitude coordinates often helped to find the right location in these instances. Establishments across all categories listed a post office (PO) box location as their address. Other common errors included the wrong city or a state listed as “NA” (not applicable). Latitude and longitude coordinates were often more reliable than street addresses. However, they also sometimes referred to the wrong location.
The co‐location of businesses made it difficult to tell whether the location was current, such as a coffee shop inside a large grocery store or library. In a few instances, civic organizations were listed inside city halls, government administration buildings, and police stations. Coder D.A.M. wrote: “There might be occasional community engagement in these places, but it is not consistent and it is more likely that these locations are where the planning for such events happens, not the hosting of them.”
The most frequent source of location inaccuracy was businesses using a residential address instead of a front‐facing third place establishment. Coder L.V. noted: “A lot of the civic organizations listed don't have a central location, and when you look at the location, it is a house (presumably, that of the organization head).” Coder Y.S. further speculated: “The wrong address could be due to a home location or PO box address for tax filing, particularly for civic organizations and personal businesses (e.g., personal trainers).” Coders reflected during weekly meeting discussions about businesses that truly operate out of homes, or do not have a permanent fixed location. Coder B.D. wrote as follows:

Civic/social organizations likely have “chapters” but may not have a brick and mortar space… This is fascinating to me given there are additional costs to business owners/chapter leaders and there may be patterns of private residences associated with civic organizations which may have community meaning but may be missed as third places.
Coders regularly checked Zillow listings for residential addresses, in addition to street imagery for external signage or confirmation of operational businesses. Personal trainers nearly always used a home address, which led to concerns about environmental exposure misclassification. As Coder D.A.M. reflected on gyms and recreation centers:

The biggest issue with this category lies with the fact that personal trainers, and often owners of gyms, will use their home address as their business address despite not seeing clients at that location. These folks cannot use the private or public gyms that they often rent space in to see clients as the business filing site, so they use their personal addresses for their business. This discrepancy is important because researchers interested in place‐based effects will be off by an entire city or Census tract for the location.
Residential addresses introduced ambiguity in assessments and deeper conceptual questions about public access expectations of community‐facing third places. As coder S.L. noted: “If a business is operated from a private residence, its classification as a third place becomes ambiguous: third places, by definition, are typically understood as publicly accessible, socially neutral, and community‐oriented gathering environments.” While a home‐based Limited Liability Company (LLC) may serve commercial functions, it does not necessarily provide the open, interactive, and social setting characteristic of traditional third places.

3.1.3.

## Unfixed establishment location
Coders noticed when activities generally did not occur at a listed address. For example, civic and social organizations listed conference and event centers as addresses, but it was difficult to tell whether this location regularly hosted events, gatherings, or on‐site services. Mobile workout services appeared in the recreation center/gym category, and event planning companies provided a base address. Coder L.S. described one such instance with humor:

A notable example that stood out to me was [redacted] the Balloon Twister, listed as a museum/performing arts venue. While I do not want to go out on a limb here and say what [he] does is not performing arts, that would simply be wrong, especially after watching a video of him dressed as a clown making a balloon bouquet at a birthday party, he travels from party to party. Never stationary. If you tried to find [redacted] at his listed address, you would find him off partying at another house.
Additional tricky instances included businesses that move, such as coffee trucks and musical theater or circus shows.

3.2.

## Third place categorization
The next set of results describes our team's ability to accurately translate the classification codes provided by data providers into more narrow, precise, and substantively meaningful third place groupings. Parameter estimates of a multilevel logistic regression of categorization accuracy and corresponding predicted probabilities are presented in Table  and Figure  , respectively. Categorization accuracy rates were generally lower than location accuracy rates, reflecting the fundamentally more nuanced and subjective nature of the former task. FourSquare – which constructed its own proprietary place classification system – generally performed well, with Pr(belong = yes) ranging from 0.705 to 0.969 across target third place categories.
 Data Axle, Advan, and NETS – which relied on bespoke combinations of the broad NAICS and SIC codes provided by the data maintainers – produced more variable results. Advan displayed high categorization hit rates at or above 0.84 for all categories except coffee shops/cafes, which displayed an estimated Pr(belong = yes) of 0.488. Data Axle performed well across most categories, with proposed gyms and recreation centers, libraries, and civic/social organizations being classified correctly more than 80% of the time. However, cafes/coffee shops and museums/performing arts establishments were correctly classified 50.8% and 63.0% of the time, respectively. NETS categorization rates were highest among libraries, gyms/recreation centers, and museums/performing arts – all of which produced probabilities at or around 0.70. Accurate classification was lowest among cafes/coffee shops and civic/social organizations, both of which hovered around 0.50.
As noted earlier, coders qualitatively observed that libraries were nearly always accurate. Large‐chain establishments were also often accurate for cafes/coffee shops and gyms/recreation centers. Multiple patterns contributed to unclear or incorrect third place categorizations: (1) inadequate information available for assessment, (2) not a place where people physically gather, (3) ambiguous establishment purpose, (4) misleading naming conventions, (5) place not providing expected services, (6) struggles with third place conceptualization, and (7) uncertainty given coder positionality.
3.2.1.

## Inadequate information available for assessment
Some establishments lacked specific and adequate information to make a confident assessment. As Coder L.V. wrote: “I've noticed that for a lot of the businesses that I can't find anything on, there's only a MapQuest entry but nothing else confirming the existence.” Non‐profits and foundations (classified under civic/social organizations) were often tricky, as Coder D.A.M. reflected:

In many cases, the names of the listed nonprofit organizations were so vague that it was impossible to discern it from hundreds of others that were listed under the same name. Occasionally one could cross‐reference the name with the city or location, but often the location was a residential address or a PO Box. Additionally, there are a handful of websites that provide more in‐depth public information about registered nonprofit organizations such as Idealist or Guidestar. These sites offer little to no clarification of the type of services provided by the organization or the accurate working location; there is incomplete and very minimal information provided on these websites.
FourSquare (blinded for coders as dataset 4) included user‐generated sites that led to reviewer confusion. For Coder A.B.: “I would say that dataset 4 had some of the most random inclusions, for example this week I had one named ‘Camp‐out for jay‐z tickets wi.’” Coder B.D. wrote: “What is going on with dataset #4? ‘On our way to see the BIEBS’ linked to a highway, and ‘almond joy has nuts’ – user input madness. ‘Abookandalatte,’ clearly just in someone's room.”

3.2.2.

## Not a place where people physically gather
Coders looked for third places where people could enter and gather. The most frequent belonging error for libraries was Little Free Libraries. Coder L.S. noted:

There were quite a few “free little library” boxes listed as libraries. Some were located in residential neighborhoods, often mounted on trees or outside residential homes; however, these boxes do not have places for people to sit and read, and connect with others. They are typically places people walk by daily, which may make them conducive to community building through the distribution of knowledge and entertainment. Most often, these boxes are places people stumble upon by happenstance.
Other common issues included nonprofit civic and social organizations that only had a website and not a physical space. Gyms and recreation centers “were notably messy … although chain gyms, bowling alleys, and branded training facilities (e.g., yoga or CrossFit studios) tended to be accurate. Many records reflected personal trainers or small wellness businesses listed at home addresses, swimming pool contractors, or private apartment complex pools that are not publicly accessible by the community” (Coder M.S.). Gyms/recreation centers and cafes/coffee shops also included online‐only or mobile services, as Coder A.J.B. reflected:

I think some of the entries are not places but are online or come‐to‐you services. For example, there was a [redacted] fitness that I believe is more of a mobile fitness that claims it brings the fitness to you. Another one in the cafe and coffee shops category is called [redacted] LLC which looks to be a home bakery and caterer. The Facebook page says she has dreams of owning a cafe.
Coder D.A.M. reflected on the overall challenge of the civic/social organization category:

The primary complicating factor with this category lies in the inclusion of nonprofit organizations. This type of business spans an incredibly large array of business categories that do not have consistent location‐based community engagement. These entities may occasionally host in‐place community engagement events, but they are inconsistent and not at the location listed for the business itself. In fact, often these nonprofits were listed under residential addresses, not locations zoned as businesses. Here are some examples of the types of nonprofits included in this category: private foundations (religious and non‐religious based), nonprofits that raise funds for kids with intellectual disabilities and award them to families in a community, nonprofits to support families in the Air Force, professional associations (accountants, homebuilders etc.), Friends of Libraries nonprofits, holding companies for nonprofit funds, academic or public publishing nonprofit companies, nonprofits that provide in‐home care services for adults with disabilities, symphony foundations, schools themselves and the booster clubs within schools, nonprofits that provide counseling services, addiction treatment centers. These businesses might hold an event once a year at a concert hall or a community center, but they are not themselves sites that “foster community engagement.” It seems that because these data are technically civic‐oriented, by nature of their nonprofit status, they are included.
Coders widely commented on whether establishments fit third place ideals of public availability and accessibility. As coder Y.S. wrote: “It is hard to define social infrastructure as some establishments such as civic organizations and gyms are located in private buildings or not public‐facing. University/prison libraries are not open to the public.” Coder K.S. questioned: “Would a library in a high school still be a third place? (personally coding as belong 0)… Do dance schools count as gym/recreation centers since lessons usually cost money, not necessarily a third place in the same sense as a public gym would be, since you have to pay for ‘membership’ in this third place? (currently coding as belong 1).” Weekly team meetings featured regular discussions of public versus private third places. Coder B.D. wrote: “A golf course or [redacted] lounge is a third place but not one that all members are welcome to, even if it is in their neighborhood.” Team member J.M.F. commented on “economic bias for what's included in the performing arts and museums category (e.g., opera houses). These sites are not often situated in low‐income neighborhoods. Other places may double‐up in these communities (e.g., coffee shops that host poetry slams).”

3.2.3.

## Ambiguous purpose
Multiple establishments had vague and broad names, accompanied by little to no information online. This made it difficult for coders to determine the third place categorization. Coder Z.L. compared across categories:

I like coding the cafe/coffee shop category because it is so easy to tell. Especially when you see a name like “Starbucks,” you are sure that it belongs to a cafe/coffee shop with one hundred percent confidence! You only need to spend some time deciding the accuracy of the address, but it takes less time. Libraries are my second favorite category because of the same reason. My least favorite category is civic/social organizations. In most cases, you can only find an old website which has not been updated for years. You have to click everything you can on the page to find limited useful information. It takes a really long time and after that, you have to say you are not confident (put “0” in the box) about your judgment.
As coder S.L. elaborated: “The civic/social organizations category was particularly expansive and included everything from volunteer groups and family foundations to government offices and business LLCs … These examples highlight the difficulty of determining belonging when the category itself is conceptually broad or inconsistently applied.” Coders similarly questioned whether childcare services and parks listed under the civic/social organizations category belonged. Some establishments crossed multiple categories, such as historical societies, which could count as both civic organizations and museums.
Museums and performing arts sites were the category with the second highest frequency of ambiguous establishments. For example, coders debated whether stadiums counted as performance venues (since some stadiums host concerts, though they more often host football games or other professional sports). Wedding venues, bars with stages/shows, and individual artists were noted as ambiguous. Coder M.S. recorded: “Dance schools were particularly ambiguous, as some operated more like recreation sites than public performance venues.” Coders searched websites to determine whether “ticket exchange sites” belonged or whether stores selling artwork counted as a performing arts venue. Coder S.Z. wrote: “Some art institutions do not show art to the public but instead promote artists or manage other museums, which feels more like a business.” She reflected on difficulties deciding which category was the best fit: “Should surfing or meditation count as gyms and recreation centers? Does a bowling alley count as a gym? The ambiguity of organization purpose is one recurring challenge. Many institutions blur the lines between different categories.”
Gyms and recreation centers included difficult‐to‐categorize personal trainers, dance studios, medical spas, bowling alleys, and shooting ranges. Coders debated whether these sites promoted health behaviors that were “pretty different than a run‐of‐the‐mill neighborhood gym” (coder L.S.). For coffee shops and cafes, coders visually inspected menus and online photos to determine whether the primary purpose was coffee shop or general restaurant, leading to uncertainty in scoring. Weekly coder meetings included lively discussions about whether fast‐food chains (e.g., McCafe), ice cream shops, smoothie shops, juice bars, bubble tea, bagels, donuts, and bakeries belonged. The coffee shops and cafes category started to diverge from classic notions, informed by each coder's lived experiences and perspectives.

3.2.4.

## Misleading place name conventions
Coders observed naming algorithms possibly used to help construct the datasets that muddied correct third place categorization. For civic and social organizations, for instance, business names appeared to be pulled in because of key words such as “society,” “social,” “liberty,” “foundation,” and “center.” Restaurants with “cafe” in their names were often labeled as coffee shops, even when they were full‐service restaurants. Business names with fitness‐adjacent words such as “body” were categorized under gyms and recreation centers, leading to some erroneous classifications such as autobody car shops and “body salon” spa/massage/beauty services. Coder S.Z. reflected: “Those patterns reveal some fundamental logic error (or bias) on the scraping process from the data provider.”

3.2.5.

## Place not providing expected services
Across all categories, establishments technically fit the category name but not the expected type of service or activity. For libraries, for example, the consistent error was “Friends of [a local] Library” being included. This is a non‐profit organization that often raises funds for and volunteers at the library. This category also occasionally included book wholesalers, foundations and other non‐profits, bars, college organizations and buildings (e.g., student centers and consultants), government administrative spaces, and online‐only libraries.
Museums and performing arts sites often included gift shops and some commercial recording studios and entertainment agencies. Coder Y.S. noted: “Museums and performing arts categories showed the most extensive misclassification, capturing stadiums, fields used for occasional events, individual artists, event planners, event centers, business centers, study programs, churches, civic organizations, vehicle appraisers, wedding venues, game stores, restaurants, farms, and non‐profit organizations.”
Coder K.H. shared the surprising inclusions of “American Idol audition” and “[name redacted]’s bed” as third places. Coder A.J.B. wrote:

Museum and performing arts categories often get some random entries that are kind of connected. For example, I have seen some artist names and concerts for musicians that aren't really establishments but do sort of connect to the category. Such as Ice Cube in Indio CA, “On our way to see the BIEB” which I believe may have been a Justin Bieber concert, or [redacted] who is an artist whose art was located in the gallery at the specific location.
Coders frequently flagged civic and social organizations for potential misclassification, stating that they were often the most complex and time‐consuming to verify. As noted earlier, many non‐profits and foundations were not customer‐facing or did not offer a service at a site. Additional establishments that coders flagged as not fitting with the categorization included religious organizations, bars, government offices, automobile storage facilities, consulting firms, environmental services, charter schools, tutoring services, Internet companies, recreation centers, home‐improvement services, fire stations, museums, and retail establishments. Some establishments led to deeper conversations, as noted by team member A.D.J.:

One research assistant found a civic organization that had online materials that suggest perhaps it is a group of individuals with extreme, shared attitudes and corresponding, collectivist behaviors. This raised a question among our coders: is a cult a third place? While we are not tasked with determining whether or not this organization rises to that standard, it was an interesting discussion about collectivism and social belonging.
For gyms and recreation centers, the most common misclassification was businesses providing personal care and health/wellness, such as laser treatments, injectables, skincare, massages, nails, and broader spa services. Additional non‐fitting establishments included swimming pool contractors or maintenance companies, physical therapists, and private apartment gyms/pools.
Coders questioned whether certain coffee shops and cafes should be classified based on the theoretical function of the category (e.g., informal social gathering), the commercial identity of the business, or the literal availability of coffee/tea. This category included many types of retail food establishments such as restaurants, fast‐food outlets, ice cream and dessert shops, grocery stores, bars, coffee roasters, and coffee delivery services. Coder S.L. reflected:

Our weekly coder meetings played a critical role in navigating these ambiguities. Through collective discussion, we developed shared heuristics for evaluating borderline cases, such as distinguishing between cafes and restaurants, between gyms and wellness services, and between arts venues and art businesses. These discussions emphasized “accuracy” not being fixed, but negotiated among coders based on theoretical clarity, platform evidence, and agreement about the intended meaning of categories. If I truly remember, we discussed whether, for example, fast‐food chains like McDonald's or Dunkin’ should be coded as cafes simply because they serve coffee. Similarly, places like [redacted] Cafe, which appeared to be home‐based or unlicensed, forced us to consider whether non‐traditional or informal businesses could meaningfully qualify as third place. These collaborative reflections helped reduce coder variability but also highlighted the interpretive effort involved in determining belonging.

3.2.6.

## Struggles with third place conceptualization
Coders struggled to determine whether sites fostered socialization, community, or other third place ideals. In practice, categorical “belonging” did not always align with the conceptual definition of a third place. Coder W.K. reflected: “During coding, I noticed a gap between the conceptual idea and the actual classification work. It was easy for the boundaries to become blurred. In many cases, it was hard to say clearly “this is” or “this is not” a third place.” Coder M.S. wrote:

Many entries – especially in the coffee shop, gym, and civic/social categories – represent businesses or organizations that may not function as social infrastructure or third places in practice. Studies relying on these datasets should therefore treat them as imperfect proxies and explicitly acknowledge that classification and geocoding errors, as well as the inclusion of sites with limited or unclear public‐facing functions, which may bias estimates of neighborhood social infrastructure.
The mismatch between an establishment's categorical label and its third place conceptualization may vary across data providers due to the ontological choices each company makes when compiling the data. Coder S.L. wrote: “Third place identification is mediated by commercial platform infrastructures, making belonging partially a product of platform categorization rather than a physical reality.”
Coders questioned how dimensions of access attenuate an establishment's belonging and refined third place categorization for this project. For example, coder DAM wrote:

As a team we decided that gyms in apartment complexes do not fit in this category, but I think the breadth of this category does include them. Choosing to exclude apartment gyms for this category because they are exclusive to those that live in the complex is adding an additional defining layer that is not included. That being said, the fact that there is a factor limiting access to this third place (residence at the complex) does shape the availability of gyms in these particular locations, and for these particular populations. So, do we change the working definition to “open to all”? More importantly though is the need to consider the factors that shape access that this example reveals, such as affordability, acceptability, and accessibility.
Coder Z.L. questioned who third places serve: “For example, a university library or coffee shop within the campus. If the research group is university students, of course, they can be considered as third places. But what if the research targets are kids or older adults? Will they go to the university library or campus coffee shop? I will put a question mark here.” This led to a team discussion about how what is “correct” can depend on your research question, analytical approach, and study conceptualization of third places and neighborhood social infrastructure.
Coder B.D. questioned what happens if researchers underestimate community resilience because social infrastructure is not tagged to a particular location or adequately counted. For example, a multisite commercial area (e.g., strip mall, industrial park, office building) can house multiple businesses. But a recreation center or church counts as just one establishment, even if in reality it houses multiple organizations and activities within its boundaries (e.g., educational classes, childcare, gym, pool, concerts). The depth of social infrastructure is difficult to assign quantitatively using the available data. In her final reflection, coder S.Z. wrote:

I've come to realize that the biggest challenge isn't merely data inconsistency, but the conceptual instability of the classification itself. The lines between museums and performing arts, libraries and archives, and civic organizations are blurred, with some overlap. Art schools, community theaters, historical societies, and even religious institutions often play multiple roles simultaneously. Determining their “attribution” forces me to confront the fact that any classification system is a simplification, and some ambiguity cannot, and perhaps should not, be eliminated. By the project's conclusion, I realized that uncertainty itself possesses analytical value. My hesitations, revisions, and objections to the coding manual reflected the interpretive work inherent behind seemingly objective data. People's understanding of “third place” or “community belonging” depends on personal experience, academic training, and cultural assumptions. In this sense, coding is not merely data cleaning; it is a social act that reveals how belonging is constructed, debated, and presented unevenly. Ultimately, the coding experience emphasizes that classification is not about pursuing absolute accuracy, but about understanding complex realities while acknowledging its limitations. Patterns of ambiguity, mislabeling, and interpretive disagreements collectively highlight how fragile category boundaries are when faced with the fluidity and lived‐in nature of social spaces.

3.2.7.

## Uncertainty given coder positionality
Coders reflected on how their distinct identities, knowledges, perspectives, and experiences shaped coding assessments. For example, coder Z.L. sought clarity in group meetings given unfamiliarity with American culture and nomenclature. She wrote: “I did not know what a ‘PO Box’ is and its difference from the ‘real address’ because we do not have that in China.” Coders often relied on their lived experiences to make assessments, such as coder A.J.B.:

I got a Berlin Raceway in Marne MI for museums and performing arts which is a racetrack for motorsports. I believe it likely got in this category ’cause sometimes these things have music performances at them. I also know that growing up in Florida the Daytona International Speedway has a little museum attached and does sometimes hold events that are not motorsports races, such as Rockville, which is a rock festival.
For coder L.S. from Chicago (Illinois): “A lot of the bars back home, especially in the Boystown neighborhood that is known for its queer nightlife scene, host drag bingo for charity, host kickball leagues, and chess nights. Could these be considered civic organizations then?”
Coder W.S. generally excluded fast‐food chains from the coffee shops and cafes category but made an exception for one in particular given personal knowledge:

Panera Bread was a special case. I treated Panera as belonging to “Cafes and Coffee Shops.” One reason is that it has a sip club, so coffee is not just an extra item but part of a main product line. Another reason is that Panera provides tables and seating where people can stay, rest, and interact. This matches more closely with how I understand a cafe‐like third place.
Overall, coding was a highly interpretive process. Coder S.Z. reflected: “I realized that determining whether the category is correct or not is far more interpretive than I originally thought. The seemingly simple task at first transforms into an ongoing process of negotiating meaning, influenced by ambiguity, missing information, and my own biases.”
In his final reflection, coder S.L. wrote:

In the end, these coding experiences demonstrate that the measurement of third places requires more than point data and category labels. Location and belonging are shaped by inconsistent datasets, interpretive coder judgment, and the inherent fluidity of contemporary informal social spaces. This suggests the need for refined third place measurement approaches that incorporate metadata on business operational status, differentiate between formal and informal establishments, and grapple more explicitly with the epistemic limits of platform‐based verification.

4.

## DISCUSSION
This study evaluated four commonly used contextual datasets to identify neighborhood third places with implications for research on social infrastructure, neighborhoods, and dementia risk. Our findings suggest variable strengths and limitations across datasets that may attenuate associations or alter their direction, magnitude, or spatial patterning – particularly in small‐sample analyses where locational precision and categorical validity are paramount.
4.1.

## Tradeoffs between coverage and accuracy
A central tension identified in our analysis is the tradeoff between coverage and accuracy. Advan, for example, demonstrates high rates of locational and categorial accuracy for the establishments they include, but it is not a comprehensive list given its focus on mobility patterns. In contrast, NETS offers more comprehensive tracking of establishments nationwide (e.g., ∼90 million versus Advan's ∼9 million) – but at the cost of higher rates of misclassification and locational error. Prior research attributed NETS misclassification to inconsistent assignment and limited granularity of self‐reported SIC codes, as well as error due to address changes. 

4.2.

## Temporality and the ephemeral nature of third places
Our findings highlight the temporal and situational nature of social infrastructure, which is not well captured by static data. Operating hours impose objective constraints on when social interaction and other cognitive health behaviors are possible. Prior work on food environments demonstrated that temporal accessibility can meaningfully shape exposure and behavior, , , suggesting similar dynamics for third places and cognitive health.
Beyond operating hours, many establishments function as third places only ephemerally, depending on programming, clientele, and situational use. A restaurant may become a social club during weekly trivia nights; a McDonald's may serve as a de facto coffee shop during discounted “senior coffee” hours. , , , These examples underscore that third places are often defined less by fixed physical spaces than the social practices that animate them – a fluidity that sits uneasily alongside rigid classification systems.
Temporality has important implications for ADRD research design. Because dementia risk accumulates across sensitive periods in early, mid, and later life, researchers must consider whether third place exposures are being measured at etiologically relevant time points. For midlife‐focused studies where many modifiable dementia risk factors are most consequential (e.g., physical inactivity, depression, obesity, excessive alcohol consumption), capturing contemporaneous third place environments may be critical to find meaningful associations. In contrast, studies of late‐life cognition must contend with the possibility that current neighborhood third places may not reflect historically relevant exposures that shaped earlier cognitive reserve. This raises a key challenge for existing cohorts, since many lack detailed address histories and linked historically archived, high‐resolution third place data. As a result, researchers often rely on cross‐sectional contextual measures that may misclassify cumulative or past exposures and potentially attenuate or distort estimated associations. To strengthen causal inference, exposome research should prioritize (1) reconstructing longitudinal and detailed neighborhood exposure histories where possible, (2) incorporating temporally sensitive contextual datasets, and (3) explicitly aligning neighborhood exposure measurement windows with hypothesized sensitive periods across the life course.

4.3.

## Classification systems are constructed and imperfect
Our results demonstrate that classification systems such as NAICS and SIC, developed for economic and regulatory purposes, are imperfectly aligned with how people use third places in everyday life. The relatively low “belong” rates for certain categories partially reflect structural limitations of these classifications systems rather than failures of the datasets themselves.
Extracting meaningful third place categories requires careful, non‐automated decision‐making. Researchers must make consequential choices about which codes to include or exclude, facing tradeoffs between precision and completeness. For instance, we combined NAICS codes 722515 (snack and nonalcoholic beverage bars) and 722513 (limited‐service restaurants, including Starbucks) to identify cafes and coffee shops in Advan and Data Axle. A supplementary model regressing belonging indicators on data source, NACIS code, and their interaction finds Pr(belong = yes) rates of 0.63 to 0.88 for 722513 but only 0.03 to 0.04 for 722513 in Data Axle and Advan, respectively. A narrower list of NAICS codes provided higher precision, but at the cost of missing chain cafes like Starbucks that function as important third places.
These challenges raise a broader need for conceptual clarity about what constitutes a “third place” in exposome research. Our findings underscore that ambiguity in third place categorization reflects a deeper conceptual tension between defining places by form (e.g., business type) versus function (e.g., the social engagement they enable). Such definitional choices materially shape measurement validity across datasets. Clarifying these dimensions is essential to align theory, measurement, and interpretation.
Data providers may aid research by offering pre‐classified third place groupings organized by everyday use rather than industry type. FourSquare's proprietary classification system may provide a model for researcher–data maintainer collaboration in such schemes.

4.4.

## Purpose‐oriented correctness and use‐case specificity
An important implication of this work is that the “best” dataset depends on research purpose. Research on third places and dementia risk, for instance, often prioritizes location accuracy of social infrastructure, particularly at small spatial scales. In this context, locational correctness may outweigh categorical precision since even misclassified establishments can function as third places for some populations. From this perspective, Advan's high locational accuracy may make it desirable despite incomplete coverage. In contrast, studies focused on economic activity may reasonably prioritize comprehensive establishment counts, making NETS more appropriate despite higher error rates. These distinctions underscore “purpose‐oriented” correctness: Evaluate data quality relative to analytic goals rather than a one‐size‐fits‐all standard.
Future work should assess how correctness varies spatially, as existing evidence suggests that data quality differs across urban–rural gradients. Prior studies indicate that coverage varies across socioeconomic groups, potentially due to differences in device usage or privacy behaviors. , 

4.5.

## Limitations and future directions
First, our training process, selection of focal third places, and coder positionalities shaped coding decisions, reflecting broader epistemological challenges in measuring social infrastructure. Second, our study identifies false positive error – whether an included business is incorrectly coded – but not false negatives, where real‐world establishments are absent from a database entirely. Third, while we utilized SIC codes within NETS due to their finer‐grained classification system, future research could assess whether applying NAICS codes within NETS improves cross‐dataset comparability or alters third place estimates. Finally, this study focused on the measurement and validity of third place categories within contextual datasets, as opposed to empirically determining which specific third places strongly influence ADRD outcomes. Future studies are needed to further develop and validate a mechanistically grounded typology of ADRD‐relevant third places (e.g., see Finlay et al., 2026 ).
While best practice geocoding requires manually reviewing categorizations and re‐matching addresses for quality control, extensive data cleaning is not feasible for large‐scale analyses. This study highlights that error is unavoidable in contextual data, even in widely used and well‐regarded datasets. Such error is not merely technical but particularly consequential for ADRD research. Cognitive decline and dementia emerge from a complex generative process involving multiple factors – for example, genetic, behavioral, social, and environmental – making it difficult to cleanly isolate the contribution of any single factor. In explaining outcomes like these, where discrete effects of interest are likely modest relative to total variation and many competing causes operate simultaneously, measurement error can be the difference between detecting a real association and missing it entirely or between correctly sizing an effect and producing an estimate that misleads intervention planning. Given the absence of an effective cure for ADRD, prevention is critical. For promising interventional pathways like those routed through third places, getting measurement “right” is a precondition to produce accurate scientific narratives about ADRD and the policy implications from such studies.
Rather than striving for an unattainable perfect measurement, researchers must become comfortable working explicitly with error through creative, construct‐aligned strategies. For example, the frequent listing of business owners’ home addresses rather than establishment locations motivates probabilistic corrections, such as cross‐referencing listed coordinates against residential zoning data to down‐weight likely home‐address observations before aggregating to tract‐level exposure summaries. Similarly, validation exercises like ours can be used to directly calibrate formal measurement error models. This may enable researchers to appropriately carry through measurement uncertainty into their model estimates rather than treating noisy counts as precise, which may greatly affect estimation in small‐area analyses common to neighborhood–ADRD research. Moreover, qualitative insights can directly inform pre‐filtering conditions. If, for example, ethnographic evidence suggests that chain coffee shops are not routinely used as sites for socialization, researchers interested in parsing how ADRD risk develops via diminished social interaction can filter those sites before analysis to reduce noise in the measurement of a focal construct. More generally, triangulating across multiple datasets, building error calibration directly into the inferential workflow, and incorporating qualitative observations into quantitative modeling decisions can help align measures of third places with the social processes that they are intended to capture.
Ultimately, improving how we measure social infrastructure is not merely a technical exercise – it is foundational to understanding how neighborhoods support cognitive health through social, behavioral, and biological pathways across the life course. By foregrounding the limits of our tools while pushing for conceptual and methodological innovation, this work aims to strengthen future research on the exposome and ADRD risk.
