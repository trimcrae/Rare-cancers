## ABSTRACT

## Introduction
Access to early‐phase cancer clinical trials is heavily skewed toward urban patients treated at tertiary centers, creating significant inequity for regional/rural (R/R) populations. For early drug development (EDD) trials, travel requirements are a major barrier to R/R participation. Growing public and governmental policy pressure on pharmaceutical stakeholders and trial centers has driven the exploration of innovative solutions to improve access. Among these, the teletrial model, which has been effective in later‐phase studies, presents an opportunity to address recruitment challenges in phase 1 trials. This article provides practical guidelines for implementing teletrials in EDD.

## Methods/Results
This review outlines key operational, regulatory, and logistical considerations for phase 1 teletrials, covering site evaluation, ethics and governance, resource allocation, and funding models. Several teletrial frameworks are presented, tailored to the varying capabilities of satellite sites and the complexities of early‐phase trials. Lessons learned from successful pilot initiatives are integrated into the recommendations to help guide site conversion and trial design.

## Discussion
Phase 1 teletrials are a necessary evolution in clinical trial conduct, driven by increasing demands for equitable access to life‐saving therapies. Challenges, including resource‐intensive set‐up, cost management, and oversight requirements, are acknowledged. However, with proper planning and stakeholder collaboration, teletrials offer significant benefits: increased trial recruitment, improved R/R patient access, and reduced geographic disparities. The expansion of teletrials will be crucial for meeting the recruitment challenges posed by biomarker‐driven and rare‐disease studies while maintaining safety and scientific integrity.

Teletrial guidelines for early‐phase oncology drug development. Centralised phase 1 trial oversight at a tertiary hub is extended to regional and rural satellite sites using telehealth, shared governance, and standardised processes. This approach reduces travel burden and geographic inequity while maintaining safety, protocol compliance, and scientific integrity.

## Introduction
One third of Australia is rurally based [ ]; however, regional status is consistently associated with under‐provision of services and poorer health outcomes [ , ]. This urban–rural health disparity extends to the area of trial access which is almost exclusively centered in major academic centers. A comprehensive study on failed trial recruitment involving >51,000 cancer patients, showed “no trial at center” was the cited cause of half of respondents. The second cited reason was patient refusal, particularly due to travel requirements [ ]. These issues explain current practice, where 70% of cancer patients polled express interest in trial participation but less than 5% do so [ ]. Forgoing a large potential recruitment pool is counterproductive in the context of mounting biomarker‐driven subpopulations and rarer cancer subtypes [ ]. Triallists are instead recommended to develop new strategies in order to remain competitive and answer the demands of the market and growing governmental interest and investment in expanding regional access to trial provision.
Given geography has been cited consistently as the main barrier for both patients and referrer. Approaches that allow the patient to be treated closer to home would be anticipated to have the highest yield when considering new trial conduct design. A strong example of such an approach is the teletrials model [ ]. A “teletrial” involves a primary site utilizing telehealth to engage satellite sites to enhance recruitment but maintaining appropriate experienced oversight. This model has shown success globally, in particular in the United States [ ], Canada [ ], and Australia [ ]. Clinicians, referrers, and patients appear overall satisfied with this model of care [ ] and there is a broad policy move in favor of expansion of teletrials across health systems [ , , ].
The first teletrial model was developed in 2016 [ ] with substantial sponsor input from AbbVie Pty Ltd, Janssen, Novartis, Eli‐Lilly, and Pfizer. The first investigator‐initiated teleoncology trial that brought this model into clinical practice took place in 2018. TARGET‐TP, a randomized oncology trial of thromboprophylaxis, was specifically chosen due to its low complexity and toxicity profile [ ]. In this trial, a quaternary site provided principal investigator (PI) oversight over two regional satellite centers directly responsible for recruitment, consent, and intervention. The success of this trial and others inspired development and ethical approval of several further teletrial satellite centers that delivered tangible clinical data without safety signals [ , , , , ].
In light of these encouraging findings and with no clear safety signals, it is reasonable to explore the applicability of the teletrials model in earlier phases of drug development, given it is at this stage that greater access is most needed [ ]. Similar to the United States and Canada, Australian geography frequently presents issues of regional access and as a result, governmental and institutional policy has supported the creation of a set of guidelines and practical tools to meet the specific requirements of teletrials (health.gov.au/resources/collections/the‐national‐teletrials‐compendium). The advent of COVID‐19 accelerated these plans, in generating a test case, allowing the realization of these policies and their practical application.
We recently published a case report on the world's first successful phase 1 teletrials patient [ ]. This follow up review intends to develop a framework for centers contemplating expanding their teletrial portfolio, drawing on practical experience from several successful teletrial startups.

## Methods
This section describes practical aspects of conversion of a site to a teletrials hub.
2.1.

## Description of Primary Site
Our network serves a sizeable metropolitan area in Melbourne, Australia with five satellite sites as well as a rural catchment extending a 500 km radius with the regular accommodation of interstate trials referrals. Its active phase 1 portfolio is supported by a dedicated phase 1 clinical and research team. One third of phase 1 patients currently recruited live greater than 1 h from the center (10–370 km range). Our five most regular regional referring centers are based an average of 178 km from the primary site.

2.2.

## Preparatory Work
Given the wide geographical area served by the center, significant interest in the teletrial model commenced from 2016, provoking policy changes with strong regional and governmental support for the project. Initial success with low complexity pilot teletrial cases prompted further funding, complimented by the establishment of practical guidelines for the teletrial model in 2018/19. Secured funding for a dedicated teletrials nursing post was established in 2019 with the initial intent to expand the center's phase 3 portfolio to regional sites. Ethics and governance boards were consulted over this time period to prepare for unique requirements posed by the teletrial model and several industry sponsors were engaged in policy decisions as well as to gauge general interest in the viability of the model. The advent of a global pandemic in early 2020 accelerated the project by 6 months and also allowed the role to extend into earlier phases of drug development in order to meet the needs of our site.

2.3.

## Pilot Case
For further details, the reader may refer to our previous publication [ ]; however, in brief, our center was able to successfully treat a young mother from interstate with metastatic cervical cancer via a novel phase 1b sponsored teletrial model. The main features in the amended clinical trial research agreement pertained to additional administrative costs, which were covered by the sponsor as well as addressing privacy clauses. An ethics resubmission was passed using a modified version of the original approved consent form. In terms of governance contracts, the main issue centered on adequate PI oversight and an acceptable framework for this was agreed upon.

2.4.

## Costs and Outcomes
The patient was treated for 7 months at satellite site before eventually progressing, with no requirement to visit the primary site during that time, avoiding $7800 in travel/accommodation costs, hitherto covered by the sponsor. No major toxicity or safety signals were shown with the patient proceeding to third line options. Additional costs to the sponsor were a second ethics submission fee, research governance fees at primary and satellite site as well as extension of insurance and indemnity. Additional costs to the primary site were the full time equivalent higher‐grade nursing salary of 4 working weeks in order to establish this partnership, the full time equivalent of 2 working weeks required by the trials lead for the department, and an additional 16 h of study coordinator time as primary nursing supervisor for the satellite site. However as was shown upon further successful teletrial recruitment at the primary site with three subsequent patients in separate trials, initiation costs and personnel hours decreased substantially with experience.

## Results/Recommendations
This pilot study followed quickly by several other successfully recruited patients, provided the opportunity to explore the practicalities of the teletrial model in the phase 1 setting. Upfront awareness of initiation costs and in particular the investment of an expanded workforce is paramount to success. It is strongly recommended that candidate sites charge appropriately higher fees to cover the marked escalation of resources required.
Acceptance of governance and ethical considerations as well as agreement on standardized operating protocols should be in place prior to specific trial negotiations.
The necessity of standardized processes and adequate data capture across sites will be aided substantially by mutually accessible electronic documentation and storage.
It is critical the proposed teletrial model have clear oversight protocols in place with contingency plans for expected and unexpected deviations from this.

## Potential Operational Models for Teletrial Design
Multiple models may be conducted at the primary site depending on capacity and trials experience of each satellite site, the sponsor's perceived risk, and the complexity of each individual trial. The nature of the relationship between primary and satellite however always remains collaborative as opposed to more traditional single‐site models. We would suggest the primary site recruit the first patient for trial, regardless of geography of the patient; the patient could then be transferred to the satellite site once practical experience is gained.

## Discussion
Applying the teletrial model to phase 1 trial conduct has been shown possible [ ]. A practical guideline has been given to demonstrate how this was achieved and essential points that must be considered for future trialists in order to implement similar models at their center. Several modified models are offered to the reader to allow for variability in risk aversion and trial complexity.
5.1.

## Benefits of Phase 1 Teletrial Models for the Sponsor: Cost, Efficient Resource Allocation, Greater Reach
Escalating cost of drug development from 1 billion $ in the 1990s to 2.6 billion $ in the past decade [ ] inform the field of an untenable future. Teleoncology has been shown globally to reduce costs with one teleoncology model leading to 58% of projected travel reimbursement, resulting in a major net saving for the model regardless of extra set up and maintenance costs [ ]. Kansas University group concluded positive cost analyses [ ]. The teletrial model allows sponsors to include less experienced sites given the oversight provided by well‐established primary sites also allowing for shorter ethics and governance waiting times. It allows PI/delegates to conduct site visits on behalf of the sponsor, perform training, collate data for two sites and potentially activate satellite sites only patients are identified. It also dramatically increases the prospective catchment of potential patients: especially attractive in rare biomarker studies.

5.2.

## Recognition of Increasing Demand in Regional Areas for Trials
Ample provision of high‐quality oncology trials is rare in regional cancer centers. If available at all, the majority will typically comprise of low intensity phase III studies [ ]. Rural patients express strong interest in phase 1 trials [ ], sponsors desire shorter recruitment times, and regulators mandate broader representation [ , ]. The overwhelming barrier to recruitment of a rural patient is travel time and the teletrial model solves this [ ]. In this pilot test case [ ], the patient was able to work, provide childcare, and avoid 20 flights and 10 overnight accommodation stays during time on study. This example of improved patient‐centered care is highly likely to result in marked improvement in recruitment numbers from their historically low rates [ ].

5.3.

## Teletrials Provide New Opportunity to Tertiary Sites
From the perspective of the primary site, this project required significant staff hours and diverted resources. Trial centers need to be realistic in their costing structure for this labor‐intensive process. Sites may have the opportunity to access grant funds for minority group engagement, which would likely provide the venture capital to establish the necessary preparation for a teletrial program before engaging with sponsors. We would anticipate maintenance of ongoing costs would be covered by additional teletrial‐related fees charged to sponsor. However, in contracts with tighter budget margins and collaborative trials, cost sharing is a more likely model and the center will need to actively pursue other avenues of equity‐of‐access and regional development funding to make up the gap in costs. Cost modeling and sustainable income is essential for this model to also work for the primary site; however, with proper planning, sites should ultimately increase revenue potential.

5.4.

## Risk/Benefit Considerations in Phase 1 Teletrials Models
The teletrial model still remains in relative infancy. Particularly in phase 1, the standpoint has always been safety above innovation, which breeds a conservative approach. For example, other dose escalation models have been proven more effective and cheaper than the standard 3+3 model but this remains the only design in 98.4% of 1200+ phase 1 trials [ ]. Such conservatism is motivated by the need of phase 1 triallists to ensure safety and efficacy signal to be fully evaluated and support appropriate decision making in early drug development. This primary direction must be balanced in the context of eroding public acceptance, as large phase 1 trials offer increasingly attractive treatment options to otherwise terminal patients [ ]. Teletrials and their place in drug development are an extension of this access‐safety trade‐off debate requiring clinicians to balance the ethics, in this case, of geographic discrimination but avoiding compromise of scientific rigor and safety [ ]. A clear reason for success in this pilot case is that the subject had controlled cancer, and low toxicity from the drug. It is likely that a more complex case would have required the primary site to maintain its treating role and it is difficult to envisage how complex first‐in‐human studies could ever be delegated to small regional centers.
However, with the emergence and demand for telemedicine in general, it is probable teletrial uptake will expand and it is incumbent on tertiary leaders to determine how to do this safely and how to navigate the unique issues this approach will face. This case is one of many potential innovations to improve consumer experience on trial, such as wearable devices to negate multiple ECG reads, improving consent using media, Patient Reported Outcomes performed via app [ ], all with the intention to move toward patient‐centered care.

6.

## Conclusion
The field of phase 1 research is experiencing a transformative shift to larger, more effective and less toxic treatment options likely demanded by an increasingly vocal patient group. The rapid uptake of the teletrial model in Australia has already commenced in later phase trials recruitment [ ]. The oncological community needs to adapt to this emerging model and develop strategies to run more accessible early‐phase trials where the clinician and sponsor still feel comfortably indemnified, adequately remunerated and the patient receives safe oversight and care. Fully engaging the currently underserved and wasted potential of a center's complete catchment area is becoming feasible with the added catalyst of a global pandemic to inspire new innovative strategies to shorten the long‐suffered gap of rural disparity.
