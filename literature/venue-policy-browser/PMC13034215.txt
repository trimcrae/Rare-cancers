## Abstract

## Introduction
Globally, men who have sex with men (MSM) have a higher HIV risk, with transmission rising in many Asian countries. Limited information on MSM in Bhutan hampers HIV prevention efforts. This study estimates the size of the MSM population and examines risk behaviours at the district and national levels.

## Methods
A cross-sectional community-based survey and population size estimation exercises were conducted in Bhutan from November 2019 to December 2020. MSM in 9 of 20 districts in Bhutan were recruited using a hybrid sampling approach, combining peer-referral snowballing, venue-based outreach and online recruitment through MSM-oriented platforms. A standardised face-to-face questionnaire collected demographic and HIV-related data, while population size estimation methods, including the service multiplier, unique object multiplier and wisdom of the crowds, complemented the survey. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Board of Health of Bhutan, and verbal informed consent was obtained.

## Results
Among 273 MSM surveyed, 85.7% had their first sex before age 15; 17.4% sold sex and 22.1% exchanged sex for goods. Only 40.9% had tested for HIV in the past year, 25.0% attended HIV education, 18.5% faced discrimination at health services and 22.0% experienced violence for being MSM. Bayesian synthesis estimated 1463 MSM (95% CI 1006 to 2030) in the study area, with a national extrapolation of 1726 MSM.

## Conclusions
Our study estimated that MSM comprise approximately 1.58% of urban men in Bhutan who originate from diverse geographic areas and sociodemographic backgrounds. Findings inform HIV and sexually transmitted infection (STI) services for MSM through target setting and effective resource allocation, emphasising the need for targeted HIV prevention addressing low testing rates and high-risk behaviours. Overcoming healthcare discrimination requires tailored interventions and stronger collaboration between health authorities and MSM communities.

## WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC
Although men who have sex with men (MSM) are regarded as high risk for HIV, the lack of adequate data on population size estimation and risk behaviour of MSM in Bhutan has hindered effective targeted intervention.

## WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
This study identified 1726 MSM in Bhutan, which is about 1.58% of the total urban men aged 15 years and above. The study also showed low HIV testing rates, high sexual risk behaviour and healthcare discrimination.

## HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY
This study recommends the need for targeted HIV intervention, stigma-free healthcare and policy reforms to improve MSM-inclusive HIV services in Bhutan. This data will also guide programme designs through effective resource allocations and target settings to optimise HIV interventions and improve health outcomes for MSM.

## Introduction
 Globally, men who have sex with men (MSM) face a significantly higher risk of HIV infection than the general or heterosexual populations. Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) data report that MSM are at 28 times higher risk of HIV infection compared with heterosexual populations of the same age and gender. Asia, in particular, may be witnessing an increase in HIV incidence among MSM, despite achieving reductions in other populations. 
However, data on MSM in the Kingdom of Bhutan (Bhutan) are scant. Only after Bhutan joined a regional Global Fund grant to address HIV among key populations in 2012 were MSM included in surveillance activities and prioritised for HIV prevention programmes. , MSM status has not been systematically recorded for diagnosed HIV cases in Bhutan. Consequently, it remains uncertain if HIV infections are concentrated among MSM in Bhutan, following the pattern observed in other Asian countries. The limited data available also suffer from small sample sizes. For example, Bhutan’s 2016 integrated bio-behaviour survey of a small sample found zero HIV infections and imprecisely measured risk behaviours. Other situational analyses indicate conditions conducive to HIV transmission within certain populations, including MSM, to the extent that they were prioritised in Bhutan’s national strategic plan for 2017–2023. , Stigma and discrimination experienced by MSM may hinder them from accessing timely HIV and sexually transmitted infection (STI) testing and treatment services, elevating the risk of poor sexual health outcomes. For example, some studies from Bhutan highlight the social and internalised self-stigma MSM experience, resulting from experiences at health services and from society at large. 
Addressing HIV infection among MSM in Bhutan requires data to fill some basic knowledge gaps. As a baseline to set appropriate targets and allocate resources, an estimate of the number of MSM in Bhutan is vital. An understanding of the basic demographic composition of the population is also required to help identify which groups of MSM may be at higher risk. Finally, measures of risk and preventive behaviours are essential to assess the unmet needs of MSM in Bhutan’s prevention programmes. To help fill these data gaps, we undertook a twofold study in Bhutan, including (1) a cross-sectional, community-based survey of MSM in several districts and (2) a size estimation exercise to determine district and national-level numbers of MSM.

## Methods

## Study design and setting
This cross-sectional study was conducted in Bhutan, which had an adult population of 537 728 in 2017 from 13 November 2019 to 31 November 2020. We selected nine of the country’s 20 districts as the study area. These included the most populous districts (Thimphu and Chukha) and at least one district in each of the three macroregions (Central, Southern and Eastern). The study area included 63.4% of the total population and 80.7% of the urban population of Bhutan. As the survey’s goal was to measure the prevalence of key indicator variables, an a priori desired sample size was calculated to obtain point estimates within a wide range with acceptable precision, using a 95% CI. Assuming a range of point estimates from 5% to 50%, a sample size of 300 provided a margin of error of ±2.5% to ±5.5%.

## Study population
MSM were eligible whether Bhutanese or non-Bhutanese if they were born male or female and currently identifying as male, aged 18 years and above, who reported engaging in anal sex with another male in the past 12 months, irrespective of sexual orientation. Though the primary focus of eligibility was on anal sex with male partners, it is important to note that sexual diversity among the participants included other sexual behaviours, such as oral and potentially vaginal sex, particularly among bisexual and heterosexual MSM. While eligibility was defined by anal sex with another man, other questionnaire items broadly asked about sex; however, the respondent chose to define it.

## Recruitment of participants
To reach diverse segments of the MSM population, we used a peer-directed, hybrid recruitment method facilitated by peer outreach workers. Peer outreach workers recruited initial participants from their networks of clients, who then made referrals from their social networks, producing a snowball sample. The sample also included MSM whom the peer outreach workers recruited through direct messaging using lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer/questioning, and intersex (LGBTIQI+) oriented online platforms, including geo-locating dating apps such as Grindr and Bardoo. Additionally, some MSM participants were recruited through outreach at physical venues (eg, bars and karaoke clubs). Eligible MSM were interviewed face-to-face or online. A brief, standardised questionnaire collected demographic characteristics, sexual risk behaviours, access to HIV prevention information, use of prevention services and experiences of stigma, discrimination and violence due to being MSM. The questionnaire also included items related to the population size estimation methods described below. An incentive of Ngultrum (Bhutan’s currency) 500 (~US$6.79) was given in the form of cell phone airtime for their survey participation, and Nu 200 (~US$2.72) in airtime was given for successful referrals of eligible MSM to the survey. A response rate could not be calculated due to peer-directed recruitment.

## Survey measures
This study included a 24-item structured questionnaire. Demographic measures included age, marital status, sexual identity, sexual orientation and gender of sexual partners. Behavioural measures captured age at first sexual experience, number and types of sexual partners, condom use, alcohol use during sex and engagement in commercial or transactional sex. HIV-related information included testing history, STI testing, knowledge of testing sites and their attendance in HIV prevention awareness programmes. Stigma and discrimination were assessed using items on MSM identity disclosure, experiences of stigma, discrimination in healthcare settings and violence due to MSM identity (detailed survey measure and coding in ).

## Data collection and analysis
The survey integrated multiple methods to estimate the population size of MSM. The methods undertaken were similar to those conducted for surveys and population size estimation exercises for transgender women and transgender men in Bhutan, previously published and briefly described here. As recommended by UNAIDS, multiple population size estimation methods are used to minimise the risk of bias inherent in using only one method. The population size methods used in the present study were the wisdom of the crowds, the service multiplier and the unique object multiplier. 
The results of these three methods were synthesised into one estimate using a Bayesian approach. We extrapolated the population size estimates calculated for the nine study districts to all 20 national districts using a proxy indicator variable and logistic regression. Survey data were analysed using Stata V.14, presenting key indicators as percentages of the MSM respondents who answered each question. Given Bhutan’s small population size, data cells with four or fewer persons are presented as ‘<5’ in tables and text to ensure confidentiality.
Wisdom of the crowd: the wisdom of the crowd method asked survey participants their best guess on the number of MSM living in Bhutan. The mean response was taken as the population size estimate with a 95% CI. We used the mean, rather than the median, with an IQR because the median, upper and lower quartiles happened to be the same number.
Service and unique object multipliers: the service multiplier method is based on leveraging a count of MSM clients using a specific service against the survey estimate of the proportion of MSM in the population who report using the service. The total population size (N) is given by the number of clients using the service (S) divided by the proportion of respondents in the survey who report using the service (P) as N=S/P. A 95% CI is calculated using the capture–recapture approach. For the present study, the service count was obtained by the number of MSM in the membership of an LGBT community-based organisation. The unique object multiplier method uses the same formula, with the service client count being replaced by a count generated by distributing a memorable object to MSM in the target area. Two weeks before the survey, outreach workers/interviewers distributed a unique key chain to MSM they encountered in physical venues. The questionnaire then asked respondents if they received the key chain.
Bayesian synthesis of estimates: a Bayesian anchored multiplier method developed by Wesson et al in 2018 and 2019 combines the results from the wisdom of crowds, service multiplier and unique object multiplier methods into a single estimate with a 95% credible interval. The process builds on a ‘prior’ estimate (in this case, the full range of estimates assuming a uniform distribution) and the best fit of the three calculated estimates above and their 95% CIs.
Extrapolation: the synthesised population size estimate for the total study area was apportioned to each of the nine districts based on the district of residence reported by MSM survey respondents. To extrapolate the results from the nine study districts to the entire 20 districts of Bhutan, we assessed the correlations between the size estimates in the nine districts and measures (‘proxies’) available for all districts. Potential proxies were available from the National Statistics Bureau and the Ministry of Economic Affairs. Potential proxies included measures such as the number of licensed bars, population density, adult male urban population, distance from the capital and the number of entertainment venues. Linear regression models were applied to assess the strength and significance of correlations. The proxy with the highest adjusted R2 for its correlation with MSM was chosen as the proxy to model the MSM population size in the 20 districts. The 20 modelled district estimates were summed to produce the national estimate of the number of MSM in Bhutan.

## Results
 presents demographic characteristics and indicators of HIV-related risk and preventive behaviours of 273 MSM participating in the community-based survey. The mean age was 28.8 years (SD 7.6). All (100%) participants were assigned male sex at birth and identified as male. The majority (97.1%) identified as gay or bisexual, with 55.3% identifying as bisexual. Notably, 20.7% of participants were married to female partners. While anal sex with male partners was an inclusion criterion for the study, participants engaged in various sexual practices, including oral and vaginal sex, particularly among those who identified as bisexual or were married to female partners. As specific types of sex acts were not asked with each question, responses reflect the participant’s own interpretation of the term ‘sex’.
 Concerning risk indicators, 17.4% had provided sex for cash, and 22.1% of participants had received cash or other goods or support for sex in their lifetime. Alcohol consumption was reported by 70.3% of MSM, of whom 56.4% had sex under the influence of alcohol. The majority (85.7%) first had sex under the age of 15 years, and the sexual network was diverse. Nearly two-thirds (63.1%) reported having sex with both men and women, while 33.2% had sex with a transgender person in their lifetime. The mean number of sexual partners reported in the last 30 days was 5.8 (SD 9.0). Consistent condom use was low with spouses (<5) and steady partners (24.8%), moderate with casual (43.0%) partners and higher with paying (62.5%) and transactional partners (64.4%).
In terms of preventive behaviours, nearly three-quarters (72.2%) knew where to access an HIV test; more than half (58.0%) reported ever testing for HIV, with under half (40.9%) testing in the last year. Engagement with other preventive behaviours was low, with only 20.0% attending an HIV educational event, 14.4% ever talking to an outreach worker and 30.5% ever testing for an STI. A majority (63.0%) of participants reported that their MSM status had not been disclosed to anyone. Nonetheless, one-third (35.4%) reported they had experienced stigma due to being MSM often or sometimes, 18.5% experienced discrimination at health services and 22.0% experienced violence due to being MSM.
 shows the results of the three population size estimation methods for MSM and the Bayesian synthesis of these results. The mean response of survey participants on the number of MSM in Bhutan (ie, the wisdom of the crowd method) was 1313 (95% CI 552 to 2072). The service multiplier estimated 1769 (95% CI 669 to 2869) MSM in Bhutan based on a count of 74 community-based organisation (CBO) members and 4.182% of survey respondents reporting being members. The object multiplier method estimated 1284 based on 47 key chains distributed to MSM, and 3.660% of survey respondents reported receiving a key chain. The Bayesian synthesis of these three estimates, using the complete range of results with a uniform distribution as the prior, resulted in 1463 MSM (95% credible interval 1006–2030).
 Using the estimates of MSM within each of the nine districts, the proxy indicator variable of the number of licensed bars in each district had the highest adjusted R2 for its correlation with the MSM population size in each district (R2=0.6811, p=0.004). Combinations of proxy variables did not improve the model fit over the number of licensed bars alone. The linear regression model for MSM population size in all 20 districts of Bhutan was given as N=0.99×number of bars—125.35. shows the model-fitted numbers of MSM for each district. Summing the numbers for each district produced a national estimate of 1726 MSM in Bhutan. Notably, every district in Bhutan was mentioned by MSM respondents as their birthplace in the survey (data not shown due to small cell sizes).
 

## Discussion
We conducted the largest survey of MSM in Bhutan to date, providing a description of the population and benchmarks for risk and preventive behaviours, including their size estimates at the district and national level. Several risk and preventive indicators are worth noting. First, recent HIV testing among MSM was low, with fewer than half of MSM testing in the last year, which falls well below the 90% UNAIDS target. Similarly, only a small proportion of MSM have ever had contact with an HIV prevention programme. Furthermore, while anal sex was an inclusion criterion for this study, many MSM respondents, particularly those identifying as bisexual or heterosexual and engaged in various sexual practices, highlighted the complexity of their sexual risk and preventive behaviours due to both same-sex and opposite-sex encounters. This aligns with previous national data showing that most MSM in Bhutan are married and have engaged in sexual relationships with both male partners and their female spouses. Global evidence also suggests that MSM and women are less likely to be HIV positive than MSM only, but significantly more likely than men who have sex with women exclusively. In addition, behaviours such as partner concurrency and condomless sex increase their risk for acquiring HIV and STIs and for transmission to their male and female partners. , 
The majority of MSM had a young sexual debut, low consistent condom use, sex under the influence of alcohol and a high number of sexual partners in the last 30 days. The latter indicator, especially in the context of a small population, suggests tightly connected sexual networks and partner concurrency—factors that can amplify HIV and STI transmission. , The risk of HIV for MSM in Bhutan is exacerbated by the use of alcohol during sex in the context of very low condom use, as noted in the global literature. , We also acknowledge the potential bias of under-reporting of risk practices for a range of reasons, including lack of trust, homophobia and heterosexism, barriers noted widely in the literature as influencing behavioural reporting. , The findings of our study support this potential under-reporting that two-thirds of MSM experienced stigma, one-third faced discrimination at health services, and one in five reported they were confronted with violence due to their MSM status. Findings from several global studies also suggest that stigma associated with MSM status may limit the uptake of HIV prevention, treatment and care services. , This will hinder progress towards the goals of control and elimination of HIV. 
Furthermore, our population size estimation exercise confirms the presence of MSM throughout Bhutan, likely in all 20 districts, and constituting 1.6% of the urban male population aged 15 years and older in the country. We believe our estimate is credible, having been generated through multiple methods with high consistency in results. Results compare closely to an estimated 1.7% of urban men found in a 2006 BSS survey and a 1.7% estimate for China. The estimate is also above the UNAIDS minimum estimate of 1.0% of adult males for countries as a whole and the 1.5% suggested estimate for areas that experience higher in-migration, such as principal urban centres. 
Data gaps for some behavioural indicators, such as pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) use (which was not available in Bhutan at the time of the survey) and detailed condom-use patterns, also limit the completeness of our risk assessment. Nonetheless, several considerations must be noted when interpreting our estimates. While we used multiple methods to overcome limitations inherent in each method separately, as recommended by UNAIDS guidelines, it is possible that all methods we used may have biases in common. For example, all three methods may pertain only to more visible MSM who reside in urban areas of Bhutan and are socially and community-connected. All empirical data were collected in the main towns of the nine study districts. Our estimates may not account for MSM who are isolated, living in rural areas and lack connections to other MSM. The use of the snowballing technique, which relied on peer referral, may have suffered from selection bias by disproportionately recruiting MSM who are socially connected while under-representing more isolated individuals. The extrapolation from proxy data in nine study districts to the entire country of Bhutan also assumed a linear relationship between the number of bars and the number of MSM. While the correlation was strong in the nine study districts, a different relationship may exist between the presence of bars and MSM in rural areas. Additionally, HIV prevention programmes prioritise MSM who are accessible through their sexual networks, namely, those who can be reached through peer outreach, peer referral, online messaging or self-referral on gaining relevant health information. Therefore, our estimates may represent the more ‘reachable’ part of the MSM population. Nonetheless, we believe our numbers to be the most reliable to date for the development of targets for HIV programmes for the MSM population in Bhutan. However, future studies may need to include MSM in rural areas of Bhutan to provide more comprehensive estimates.
On a positive note, this research improved collaboration between the Ministry of Health and LGBT CBOs, providing data to guide HIV prevention for MSM in Bhutan. It introduced innovative recruitment techniques and strengthened relationships for future MSM-friendly prevention, testing and care programmes.

## Conclusions
Our population size estimation exercise in Bhutan highlights the presence of MSM in all districts, constituting approximately 1.6% of the urban male population aged 15 years and older and an estimated 1726 MSM nationally. This provides, for the first time, a reliable denominator for setting concrete national coverage targets for HIV prevention services, including PrEP, condom programming and routine HIV testing. While our estimates are credible and robust across multiple methods and consistent with other estimates in the region, a potential bias towards urban and socially connected MSM in our data warrants caution. The snowball-based recruitment approach may have introduced selection bias by only reaching socially connected MSM, while under-representing more isolated individuals. Such bias may result in underestimation of prevention indicators. Our finding of a low testing rate (40.9%) despite high prevalence of sexual risk behaviours, such as early sexual debut and commercial sex, may in fact be underestimated. Stigma, discrimination and experiences of violence may further inhibit accessing services, highlighting additional structural barriers that must be addressed. Tailored interventions and stronger collaboration with community-based organisations are essential. The diversity in sexual practices, including bisexual and married MSM engaging with both male and female partners, emphasises the complexity of sexual networks and associated risks. HIV prevention programmes must address these dynamics through MSM-friendly services and innovative outreach strategies. Strengthening access to care and support services will be crucial to reducing HIV transmission and improving programme effectiveness in Bhutan.
