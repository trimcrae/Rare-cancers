## Background
Long wait times at health facilities negatively affect contraceptive access and cause dissatisfaction with care. Conventional data collection methods, such as population-based surveys and exit interviews, may not accurately capture wait times due to methodological challenges including recall and social desirability bias.

## Methods
We compared mystery client observations conducted in all public facilities in Kisumu County, Kenya with data from a population-based sample of women of reproductive age (18–49, n = 744) in Kisumu County. We compared recalled wait times from women who used public facilities in the last year for their last contraceptive method with wait times recorded by mystery clients (n = 401) presenting as first-time family planning users, and analyzed quotes from mystery clients who mentioned long waits.

## Results
Most mystery clients reported wait times between 1-2 h (49%) or over 2 h (33%), whereas most women surveyed in their homes recalled being seen within 30 min at their most recent visit for family planning (74%). When stratified by facility type, mystery clients waited the longest at dispensaries and basic health centers (median wait time of 110 min) and the shortest wait times at primary and secondary care hospitals (median of 82.5 min). In the survey, women recalled waiting a median of 15 min at basic health centers, 20 min at dispensaries, and 30 min at hospitals. Common causes of long waits reported by mystery clients in qualitative data included late facility openings, late providers, or prioritization of other patients. More than half of mystery clients reported spending less than 5 min with providers (59%), compared to only 8% of women surveyed.

## Conclusions
Triangulation of data between different sources can provide a more nuanced understanding of long wait times, their causes, and how they impact contraceptive seekers. We find that in comparison, these different methods of data collection answer distinct questions about wait times, time spent with provider, and their impacts on contraceptive seekers. Both forms of data are useful to policy makers and stakeholders. We recommend data collection efforts aimed at improving quality of services and adherence to national guidelines consider supplementing standard data collection methods with mystery clients.

## Introduction
Access to contraception is essential to women’s health and contraceptive autonomy– but women in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) often face facility-level barriers to achieving their desired contraceptive use [ , ]. Even after arriving at facilities, individuals can encounter long wait times and short visits with providers, both of which may prevent them from accessing their desired method of contraception [ – ].
A long-standing concern among family planning researchers and policy makers is how women perceive– and are impacted by– wait times at facilities. Across LMICs, women report that long wait times decrease patient satisfaction and can result in women leaving the facility before they can be seen by a provider [ , – ] For women attempting covert contraceptive use in particular, long wait times increase the difficulty of obtaining a method or prevent future attempts at contraceptive use, as women could be recognized at a facility, or their time away from home could raise questions [ ]. Research with family planning providers has also explored concerns about long queues, which can result in pressure on providers to see patients quickly, offer group counseling instead of individual counseling, and in some cases, not counsel on all contraceptive methods [ – ] Long wait times and limited time spent with providers can also strain relationships between providers and community members, and drive family planning clients to the more expensive private sector [ , ]. Taken together, long wait times and abbreviated counseling sessions negatively impact contraceptive care, and are therefore important issues to understand when making improvements to family planning programs [ , – ].
Despite the importance of monitoring wait times, researchers often rely on general recall from patients, sometimes weeks or months after the contraceptive visit, to assess wait times. While this can provide valuable information on long-term perceptions and memories of wait time, it may tell us little about how long patients are actually waiting. While perceptions of wait times are important, actual wait times are more useful as a metric for directly assessing improvement in quality of care at facilities, and ensuring facilities have adequate resources, including staff, to meet the needs of their patients. Additionally, it can be difficult to collect data from patients or providers on reasons for long wait times using these methods. If data is not collected immediately after the visit, patients may have forgotten details about how long they waited or the reason they had a long wait at the facility, resulting in recall bias. However, if data is collected from patients at facilities, the presence of researchers or knowledge that patients are being recruited for exit interviews at that time may change provider behavior, often referred to as the Hawthorne effect. Social desirability bias may prompt patients to report lower wait times at exit interviews, or providers to self-report lower average wait times at their facilities, especially when negative provider behaviors, such as absenteeism or excessive socializing with coworkers, contribute to the long wait times and patient frustration [ ]. If a high burden of non-medical tasks (such as paperwork or cleaning), issues with facility staffing, or other administrative issues are contributing to long wait times, social desirability bias may negatively influence provider willingness to discuss frustration with their administrators [ , ].
Many of these methodological measurement challenges for obtaining empiric estimates of wait time can be avoided by using mystery clients: specially trained data collectors who observe service delivery at facilities under the guise of seeking care, without revealing their status as researchers [ ]. Mystery clients are therefore able to report on issues such as wait times and quality of care without facing issues of recall bias or the Hawthorne effect altering provider behavior. Previous research using mystery clients to quantify wait times in Kenya found that, on average, mystery clients seeking family planning services waited 74 min to be seen, even when they arrived exactly when the facility opened [ ]. Little is known, however, about how data collected by mystery clients compares with responses to population-based survey questions about facility wait times and time spent with providers. The objective of this paper is to triangulate data from two distinct data sources– mystery clients and a population-based survey of women of reproductive age– to better understand how women perceive wait time and the amount of time spent with a provider.

## Methods

## Parent study
Data for this analysis come from a randomized controlled trial implemented in Kisumu County, located in Western Kenya. The parent study collected individual and facility-level data to evaluate a quality improvement intervention to increase contraceptive access. For the present analysis, we use two sources of data collected prior to the intervention: (1) individual-level data collected among a representative sample of adult women of reproductive age (18–49) in Kisumu County, and (2) mystery clients observations conducted across all 137 public-sector healthcare facilities offering family planning in Kisumu County. Ethical review and approval for this study was provided by The Maseno University Ethics Review Committee (Reference number: MSU/DRP/MUERC/01054/22) in Kisumu, Kenya and the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill (Study number: 21-2217).

## Data collection

## Women’s individual-level questionnaire
Baseline data were collected between May and October 2022 among a representative sample of adult women of reproductive age (18–49) residing in Kisumu County [ ]. Trained female enumerators explained the study, obtained informed consent, and then administered a structured questionnaire. The questionnaire captured data on demographic characteristics, reproductive history, and contraceptive use and behavior, including at their last visit to a facility for contraceptive care (Supplementary File S1.) All data were securely recorded on encrypted, password-protected tablets using the Survey CTO platform.
Of the 2,484 women who participated in the parent study’s women’s questionnaire, 1,988 had ever gone to a facility to obtain a contraceptive method. We excluded those who received methods from private facilities or from a facility outside of Kisumu County (n = 619), had last sought contraceptive services more than a year ago (n = 623), and two women who were missing data for wait time or amount of time spent with a provider for a final sample of 744 women. We conducted a sensitivity analysis to examine if our results would change significantly if we restricted the sample to those who had last visited the facility in the past year versus in the last month and saw no statistically significant or meaningful differences.

## Mystery client observations
Twelve female mystery clients of reproductive age were selected based on their performance on a memory test and their demographic profiles, to ensure strong recall among a mix of mystery client profiles of different parities (including no children) and marital statuses. Mystery clients were trained to present as first-time family planning users seeking a specific (assigned) preferred contraceptive method (IUD, implant, injectable, or oral contraceptive pill) at all 137 public-sector facilities in Kisumu County. Mystery clients visited one public facility per day, Monday– Friday, and arrived just before the facilities officially opened at 8:00 AM, to record staff arrival. All visits were completed between May-July 2022. Each facility received three visits from three unique mystery clients. To verify that mystery clients were at their assigned facility before 8:00 AM, mystery clients were required to WhatsApp a GPS location to their field supervisor before 8:00 AM, or that day’s visit was canceled. Mystery clients were instructed to present as a first-time family planning user and wait until they were seen by a family planning provider. Mystery clients waited until the last possible moment to end a visit, citing context-appropriate excuses about changing one’s mind, needing to obtain partner approval, or being concerned about invasive procedures. Within 30 min of leaving the facility, the mystery clients were in communication with the field supervisors, and completed an electronic questionnaire on an encrypted, password-protected tablet. The mystery clients completed 411 total visits. Ten observations were removed for having missing data for most of the questionnaire because the facility remained closed the entire day of the visit, for a total of 401 completed observations. After visits, mystery clients returned to the research office to debrief with their field supervisors, ensure that there was consistency between their description of the visits and their entered surveys, and process any visits that had been distressing to the mystery clients. Mystery client data was reviewed daily for quality checks. Informed consent for the mystery client visits were not obtained from individual providers to avoid bias, but was obtained from the facility directors. The county Department of Health granted permission to access health facilities and conduct this research.

## Measures
On the women’s questionnaire, participants were asked about their last visit to obtain family planning, and for the details of the facility. For that most recent family planning visit, to estimate approximate time spent waiting to see a healthcare provider for family planning counselling and the amount of time spent with a provider, participants were asked “How long did you wait to see the provider?” and “How long did the provider spend with you?” Answers were recorded in hours and minutes. To understand if the client perceived the wait time as long or short, participants were asked: “How did you feel about the amount of time you waited? Would you say it was very short, somewhat short, somewhat long, or very long?”
Mystery clients discreetly recorded the exact time of arrival to the facility, the exact time they were seen by a provider, the length of the visit in minutes, and their time of departure from the facility (Supplementary File S2). Mystery clients used a variety of techniques to record exact times, including taking notes in their phones, capturing a phone screenshot of their lock screen to have a timestamp of important steps in the visit (provider arrival, start of visit, etc.), and memorizing the time on digital watches. Mystery clients were instructed not to write in notebooks to avoid being identified as a mystery client. When filling out the survey, mystery clients were asked: “What time did you arrive at the facility this morning?” “What time did the providers start seeing family planning patients?” “What time did your FP counselling session with the provider BEGIN (i.e. at what time did the provider begin talking to you about family planning)?” and “What time did you FP counselling session with the provider END (i.e. what time did the provider stop talking to you about family planning?” Mystery clients were instructed to provide all time answers in hours and minutes and using the 24-hour clock, with no rounding. We used these times to calculate the amount of time in minutes that mystery clients waited to be seen after the official opening time of 8:00 AM, and the length of the contraceptive consultation. Because mystery clients arrived before the facility opening time, they also recorded if the facility opened promptly at 8:00 AM and the exact time providers began seeing patients. Some facilities (specifically larger hospitals) were open 24-hours; however, these facilities do not offer family planning for 24 hours. In these cases, mystery clients recorded what time the family planning room was opened and family planning services were offered. All mystery clients completed long-form comments about their visits.

## Data analysis
For both actual clients and mystery clients, we estimated the wait time in minutes, categorizing responses into meaningful intervals and calculating the median wait time and interquartile range across all participants, and disaggregated by facility type. We used the same approach to estimate time spent with providers. All analyses were conducted in Stata version 17.
We conducted a deductive thematic analysis of the qualitative mystery client data from the 720 long-form comments using Dedoose [ – ]. Each day after data collection was complete, three study team members (SC, EG, AM) read through the mystery client comments for clarity and quality checks. SC, KT, BB, and LS developed a deductive codebook for coding the mystery client data based on the questionnaire (Supplementary File S2) that focused on details of the visit, including attitudes toward wait times and time spent with provider. For this paper, we deductively identified instances where mystery clients noted long wait times influencing their opinion on the quality of care they or other patients received, and their perception of whether providers were rushing their family planning consultations. Mystery client illustrative quotes were chosen and reviewed in study team meetings.

## Results

## Sociodemographic characteristics
Of the 744 survey respondents who had last obtained a method of contraception at a public facility (and did so within the last 12 months), 80% were currently using a method (n = 598), with implants and injectables being the most commonly used methods and making up 32% and 54% of the method mix respectively (Table  ). Slightly more than half of the women surveyed were under 30 years of age (52%), and the majority (83%) were either married or living with a partner as if married. Most of the women in this sample had previously given birth (99%). Regarding mystery clients, 33% were under 30 years old, slightly more than half (58%) were married, and one third (33%) were nulliparous (Table  ). A higher proportion of mystery clients were nulliparous compared to women surveyed (33% vs. 1%). The distribution of facility level was also different between mystery clients and women surveyed, as mystery clients visited every public facility in Kisumu County three times. In women surveyed, 35% of visits were at dispensaries, 27% were at basic health centers, and 38% were at primary or secondary care hospitals. Among mystery clients, 45% of visits were to dispensaries, 38% were to basic health centers, and 16% were to primary or secondary care hospitals.

 

 

## Wait time and time spent with provider
Compared to mystery client visits with exact recorded times, women in the population-based survey reported lower wait times. Most mystery clients waited between 1 and 2 h (49%) or over 2 h (32%), whereas most of the women surveyed in their homes reported being seen within 30 min (74%; Table  ). The median wait time for women surveyed was 20 min (IQR: 10–45), compared to 106 min (IQR: 70–135) for mystery clients. When stratified by facility type, at dispensaries mystery clients waited a median of 110 min (IQR: 75–139) and women reported a median wait of 20 min (IQR: 10–30). At basic health centers, mystery clients waited a median of 110 min (IQR: 80–140), and women reported a median wait of 15 min (IQR: 5–30). At hospitals, mystery clients reported their lowest median wait time of 82.5 min (IQR: 51–110), but women surveyed reported their longest median wait of 30 min (IQR: 10–60).

 

According to mystery clients, most facilities (72%) had no medical providers present by 8:00 AM, the time providers are supposed to start seeing patients, and nearly half (54%) had no staff, medical or otherwise, present at the posted opening hour of 8:00 AM (Table  ). Mystery clients reported that most facilities started seeing family planning patients between 9:00–10:00 AM (49%) or between 10:00–11:00 AM (31%), with only 16% of facilities seeing family planning patients before 9:00 AM.

 

In the mystery clients’ short form comments, they expressed a range of attitudes toward their long waits. When providers were seen “storytelling” with each other or wait times were exacerbated by late openings or absent providers, mystery clients expressed frustration. However, when providers were visibly busy with other work-related tasks, such as patient care or cleaning, mystery clients found more patience for their long waits, with one mystery client recommending that future mystery clients should be “patient enough” due to the schedule of the clinic:

“The next mystery client should be patient enough. Once the providers and CHVs [community health volunteers] come they start with prayers and health talks that take an hour. After that they start seeing the clients who are there for their 1 st clinic and women who have brought kids for clinic. Even though I was number one, I waited for a long time, more than an hour. Some clients were even complaining because they came early, and they have not been attended to.”– MC 3.

For other mystery clients, provider behaviors that caused long wait time were frustrating. Some mystery clients reported that providers actively avoided seeing patients, were rude to patients when they asked to be seen, or spent significant amounts of time “storytelling,” or engaging in non-work-related chats. One mystery client wrote:

“The providers at this facility are so insensitive. The waiting bay was fully packed with patients waiting to be attended to in various departments, but the providers were busy storytelling and catching up with each other for more than one hour. This shocked me for sure.” - MC 7.

When mystery clients felt that rules of fairness were not being observed– for example, attending to patients who had arrived later first or pulling specific people out of queue– this impacted their perception of quality of care.

“Even though I was the first client for family planning, they selected who to attend to first. They never followed the queue as it was. They attended to two people who came after me just because they were familiar to the providers.”– MC 9.

Mystery clients observed that long wait times had direct impacts on patient care and patient willingness to wait. A mystery client who waited for over 4 h reported:

“The family planning room had two providers who were busy on other things and not attending to patients. My enquiries made them lock themselves inside the room. They would leave shortly and come back again and lock it despite admitting to me that that is the venue where I was to be served. I was later told the nurse serving antenatal is the one who attended to me. By this time, the family planning queue was really long, with other patients getting impatient and going back home. I overheard that since its 1200hrs already the provider may not offer family planning patients the services before [mid-afternoon].”
– MC 5.

These long waits sometimes resulted in providers rushing with patients in order to complete attending to all those waiting in the long lines. In the women’s survey, 92% of women recalled spending more than 5 min with providers, but only 41% of mystery clients recorded a visit of more than 5 min with providers (Table  ). We conducted a sensitivity analysis to see if both wait times and time spent with provider were similar between those who had last visited a public facility for family planning within the last month versus in the last year, and our estimates changed by no more than 2% for wait times or time spent with providers (data not shown).The median length of time spent with providers for mystery clients was 4 min, compared to a recalled 15 min from women in the population-based survey. Stratified by facility type, at dispensaries, mystery clients recorded spending 3 min (IQR: 2–6) with providers compared to women, who recalled 10 min (IQR: 5–25) visits (Table  ). At basic health centers and hospitals, mystery clients reported spending 4 min (IQR: 2–6) with providers and women recalled visits of 10 min (IQR: 5–30). At hospitals, women recalled their longest visits at 15 min (IQR: 10–230), and mystery clients reported spending 4 min (IQR: 3–6) with providers.

 

Mystery clients reported that rushed pace of providers sometimes impacted the quality of care they received:

“She was hurrying while handling me because she wanted to attend to women who brought their kids to the clinic and at the same time getting ready to attend to some other male patients. She didn’t explain much about the method, she said that if I wanted more explanations then I had to go back to the facility later.”– MC 4.

Another mystery client, who was seen for a total of two minutes, wrote:

“The facility opens early but the providers came very late. And even though they came late, none of them seemed to bother. Everyone was busy doing their own things, yet clients were there waiting. The session was very short simply because a large number of clients were waiting, and he also seemed to be in a hurry going for an outreach. You were therefore being offered whatever you want without discussion.”– MC 8.

## Discussion
There were stark differences in the reported wait times and time spent with providers between mystery client observations and women surveyed in their homes who were recalling their last family planning visits at public facilities in Kisumu County, Kenya. Mystery clients recorded longer wait times and shorter consultations with providers, which suggests that recalled data from surveys may underestimate wait times and overestimate time spent with providers. Less than 10% of mystery clients were seen within the first half hour the facility opening at 8:00 AM, with almost half of mystery clients being seen between 9:00–10:00 AM and more than a third seen after 10:00 AM, despite having arrived before the facilities were scheduled to open at 8:00 AM.
Compared to previous research conducted in the same county in Kenya between 2018 and 2019 that also used mystery clients, wait times have increased while time spent with providers has decreased [ ]. While only 14% of mystery clients in 2018/19 waited at a facility for more than two hours, 33% of our mystery clients waited more than two hours to be seen [ ]. Similarly, there was an increase in the percentage of mystery clients who spent less than 5 min with a provider (20% in 2018/19 vs. 59% in current study) [ ]. While this is not out of the ordinary for countries in which patients are seen as they arrive without appointments, we must note that our mystery clients were presenting as first-time family planning clients [ ]. While shorter visits may be expected for patients who are being seen for method continuation, the Kenyan National Family Planning Guidelines state that service providers should at least briefly review all available methods of family planning with clients and take medical history to assess for contraindications before offering a method [ ]. Still, if we assume that some percentage of the women surveyed were seeing providers just for continuation of methods, we would expect to see shorter visits in the survey than those reported by mystery clients.
Contributing factors to long wait times for family planning patients included late facility openings, late provider arrival, prioritization of other patient populations, and other negative provider behaviors, like ignoring clients to socialize. Many of these factors, especially late opening and provider absenteeism, are consistent with previous research on causes of long wait times and short visits with providers in other studies, both in Kenya and in similar contexts in Africa [ , , ]. The consequences of long wait times and short visits with providers found in our study are also consistent with other studies: clients, who have often expended time and income to travel and wait at facilities, often feel disrespected by the long waits and view this as one aspect of poor quality of care [ , , , ]. In the Kenyan context, long wait times and rushed visits with providers also influence how women make choices between using public facilities, where family planning should be freely available, and accessing private facilities, where the financial barriers are higher but the perceived quality of care is also higher [ ].
Our comparative analysis of wait times and time spent with providers was limited by the inherent differences between our two data sets. Wait time for family planning clients may vary by time of day; while mystery clients all arrived at the clinic opening times, we do not know what time women arrived at the facility. Still, our mystery clients were not the only ones waiting; as shown in the qualitative descriptions, mystery clients were often waiting with other contraceptive seekers in long queues for providers to arrive. The mystery client methodology also limits comparisons to ‘real’ client behavior, as mystery clients were assigned to visit specific facilities and remain there until seeing a provider, while respondents on the women’s surveys are able to select which facilities they would like to visit and can leave and choose another facility if they are not seen in a timely fashion. Additionally, mystery clients presented (if they were specifically asked; not all were) as first-time family planning users, and with our household survey data, women were not asked if their most recent visit for family planning was their first visit for family planning. Still, as mentioned above, first time users should have been counseled on all methods, which would ideally have resulted in longer visit times for mystery clients, not women surveyed. However, our analysis is strengthened by the standardization of the mystery client’s behaviors and their abilities as data collectors, as we have less biased data on how long mystery clients waited and how long they spent with providers given they recorded visit data in real time.
Our ability to triangulate between two data sources– a population-based survey that asks women to recall their wait times and mystery client data with exact time measurements collected by professional enumerators within the same facilities identified by women in the population-based survey– elucidates the unique strengths and weaknesses of each data collection approach and how they may be applied to answer different questions about quality of care in facilities. In population-based surveys, women are asked to recall details of a visit that may have occurred several months before. On one hand, this allows for insight into women’s perceptions over time of wait times, which may be more useful for understanding the extent to which facilities are meeting patient expectations. On the other hand, recall bias likely impacts accurate reporting of wait times. Additionally, participants may under-report negative experiences, long wait times, and dissatisfaction with long wait times due to social desirability bias. While qualitative data routinely shows dissatisfaction with long wait times, quantitative data does not always show reflect this [ , ]. Mystery client data allows for less biased estimates of opening times, wait times, and time spent with providers, which can be directly compared to national guidelines and Ministry of Health expectations for facility opening and operational times. Data reported by mystery clients also does not face recall or social desirability bias. However, experiences reported by mystery clients may not reflect the experiences of actual clients, as mystery client behavior could potentially differ from that of real patients. For example, mystery clients were expected to arrive at facilities before their scheduled opening time of 8:00 AM, whereas real patients are likely operating with the knowledge that public facilities rarely start seeing patients at the posted opening time. Women in Kisumu seeking family planning therefore may have already adapted to a lack of predictability with opening times, wait times, and time spent with providers, and thus may not face the same barriers that mystery clients reported. For example, one study examining how Kenyan women made decisions about which method of contraception to use found that women often decided on a method before arriving at facilities, and chose specific sources of contraception based on this predetermined decision [ ]. In those cases, short visits with providers may be preferrable, as the goal of the visit is not to get counseling, but rather to obtain a pre-determined method. Future research should ask patients about their perception of the time spent with providers, and if it felt rushed or adequate.
Given that data from the population-based survey reflects women’s perceptions of wait times, it’s important to consider whether long wait times reported by mystery clients are of actual concern for women in Kisumu. Our qualitative mystery client data provides important context: mystery clients reported that actual clients in lines complained about long waits, and in some cases, left facilities entirely. This is supported by other research in this context, for example, a pilot of social accountability interventions in public facilities in Kisumu County in 2020 in which community members and providers selected long wait times as one of several quality-of-care issues with family planning services to address via the intervention [ ]. One reason we may see vast differences in wait times in the two data sources is because mystery clients were instructed to wait until they saw a provider, but the women’s survey data would not capture those women who left facilities because they had to wait too long and received their family planning services in locations with shorter wait times, especially if they went to private facilities instead. We asked women in household surveys about the last time they successfully obtained a method of contraception, and therefore do not capture failed attempts to obtain a method, or if they experienced access barriers like unpredictable facility opening times.
Further, mystery clients and population-based survey participants may measure time spent waiting or time spent with a provider differently. Mystery clients were given specific instructions about how to measure wait times (from when the facility opened at 8:00 AM) and time spent with provider (only time spent in consultation with a provider about family planning methods). Women surveyed may have, for example, counted wait times as the time from when the facility actually opened (at, say, 10:00 AM) and the time they were seen, which may lead to underestimates compared to mystery clients. Additionally, women may have counted time spent having their weight and blood pressure checked at intake as part of their time spent with provider, despite these intake procedures not including any counseling. These different measurement approaches likely contribute to differences in reported times by mystery clients and women surveyed.
This study adds to the knowledge of biases in population-based survey estimates of wait times and the validity of these estimates when compared with data collected the day of service by professional enumerators. In comparison, it is clear that these two forms of data, while both asking questions about wait times and time spent with providers, actually answer very different questions. The women’s survey data likely does not actually answer the question of how much time women are spending waiting to be seen by providers in the way that the mystery client data gives us down-to-the-minute wait time estimates. The women’s survey does give us estimates of how long women think they waited and how long they may estimate they will wait again in the future, and offers an assessment of how burdensome women find these wait times. This data is useful for understanding the burden that long wait times place on women, and how much time women expect to have with a provider. To answer questions about actual weight times and time spent with the provider, other forms of data are needed. We would recommend that when gathering data to improve family planning services and increase adherence to national guidelines and standards for quality of care, researchers and policy makers should consider supplementing standard data collection tools, like population-based surveys and client exit interviews, with mystery client methods to receive more accurate data on wait times and time spent with providers. Additionally, mystery clients can provide qualitative data about their experiences seeking care that can add nuanced observation of how real patients may be affected by quality-of-care issues. When promoting use of public facilities for family planning services, it is important for stakeholders, especially governments and healthcare providers, to be aware of how long wait times and short visits with providers burden patients and impact perceived quality of care and willingness to utilize public facilities.
