## Summary

## Background
Having more cognitive activities may prevent dementia, but its evidence of modulating the functional brain network is limited. This randomised controlled trial (RCT) investigated the effect of increased cognitive activity participation on the default mode network (DMN) in older adults who had already been having regular cognitive activity participation and experiencing subjective cognitive decline (SCD).

## Methods
Community-living Chinese individuals aged 55–75 years with regular practice of Chinese calligraphy and screened positive for SCD (but negative for mild cognitive impairment or dementia) were randomly allocated to either the intervention or control group. Over 6 months, the intervention group doubled their weekly calligraphy practice time, while the control group maintained their usual amount of practice. The primary outcome was functional connectivities (FCs) of DMN, with pre-specified regions of interest including medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), inferior parietal lobe (IPL), hippocampal formation (HF), posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), and lateral temporal cortex (LTC). FC changes were compared using repeated measures multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA). This study is registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR1900024433.

## Findings
Between 15 January 2020 and 31 December 2021, 112 individuals consented and completed the baseline assessment. The participants, who had a mean age of 66.3 (SD 4.3) years, with 83 (74%) being women, had been practising calligraphy for an average duration of 9.7 years before enrolment and, in the preceding six months, for an average of 3.1 hours per week. 96 (86%) completed the post-intervention fMRI scan. Significant between-group differences were observed in the FCs between mPFC and right LTC (group difference = 0.25 [95% CI = 0.06–0.44], p = 0.009), mPFC and right IPL (0.23 [0.06–0.39]; p = 0.007), left HF and right LTC (0.28 [0.002–0.57]; p = 0.04), and left HF and right IPL (0.34 [0.09–0.60]; p = 0.009).

## Interpretation
Our findings, which reveal positive neuromodulatory effects with increased calligraphy practice, highlight the importance of engaging more in cognitive activities in late life for better brain health.

## Research in context

## Evidence before this study
We reviewed the available literature on PubMed, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and Google Scholar from the inception of the databases to 30 June 2023 to identify randomised controlled trials of late-life participation in cognitive activities on default mode network. The following searched terms were used, and the search was limited to studies published in English: “aged” or “older adults”, “intellectual” or “cognitive activity”, “cognitive training”, “cognitive intervention”, “default mode network”, “functional connectivity”, and “cognitive function”. Evidence from the retrieved studies showed that single- or multi-domain interventions that involved cognitive components led to better cognitive function in older adults who are at risk of dementia. However, neuroimaging evidence of cognitive activity participation strengthening default mode network is limited and inconclusive.

## Added value of this study
This study investigated the effect of increasing cognitive activity participation on default mode network in community-living older adults who had already been having regular cognitive activity participation and experiencing subjective cognitive decline (but without mild cognitive impairment or dementia). We found that doubling the amount of cognitive activity participation strengthened some of the functional connectivities within the default mode network. Questions remain as to whether the same benefit is observed in older adults without prior regular engagement in cognitive activities and whether other dementia biomarkers can be modified with greater participation in cognitive activities.

## Implications of all the available evidence
This study demonstrates the neuromodulatory effect of cognitive activity participation in older adults who are not yet having mild cognitive impairment or dementia. Our findings highlight that engaging more in cognitive activities may serve as a safe, practical, and useful non-pharmacological intervention for maintenance of the functional integrity of brain network in older adults at risk of dementia.

## Introduction
As the global population ages, dementia emerges as a growing health concern. With the long-term safety and cost-effectiveness of amyloid-targeting monoclonal antibodies yet to be established, failure to address dementia prevention could have serious health implications and pose a substantial burden on societies.
Among various potential lifestyle interventions, participating more in cognitive activities has shown promise in slowing cognitive decline, , , , , , possibly through stimulating mental processes and optimizing cognitive reserve. , However, whether cognitive activity participation modulates functional connectivities (FCs) of brain networks is not fully understood. Given that default mode network (DMN)—a functionally interconnected set of different brain regions most active at rest and during internal thought processes but inactive during external goal-orientated tasks—correlates with the clinical symptoms, spatial distribution of pathologies, and progression of Alzheimer's disease, , , , , , , it is crucial to investigate whether participating in cognitive activities can strengthen the DMN or reverse functional network dysfunction.
Adding to the complexity, a recent randomised controlled trial (RCT) of cognitive activities reported paradoxical findings—the control group, which engaged in crossword puzzles, exhibited a stronger DMN, whereas the DMN in the intervention group, which received computerized cognitive training (CCT), was weakened. This unexpected result underscores 3 key implications: 1) given the high dropout rate in their CCT group, it is important to implement an intervention that is cognitively demanding while also being culturally relevant and well-received by older adults; 2) care needs to be taken when designing the active control, such that its cognitive stimulation is minimal; and 3) these intriguing findings highlight the intricate relationship between cognitive activity participation and brain network modulation, emphasizing the pressing need for further investigation.
Given the challenge of developing an active control that matches the engagement of behavioural interventions without stimulating any cognitive domain, our approach to establishing a causal link between cognitive activity participation and the DMN involves examining whether doubling the amount of cognitive activity participation strengthens the DMN in individuals already engaging in cognitive activities regularly. Doubling cognitive activity exposure can be conceptualized as increasing the intensity of a workout for the brain. Analogous to the physiological principle of muscle hypertrophy in response to more physical exercise, the cognitive domains engaged in cognitive activities could potentially improve with more practice, leading to overall cognitive function benefits. Moreover, since DMN is associated with a range of cognitive functions, many of which are engaged during cognitive activities, the increased cognitive demands from more cognitive activity participation could potentially strengthen the FCs of DMN, reflecting the cognitive benefits of cognitive activities.
In this trial, we chose Chinese calligraphy handwriting as the cognitive activity of study. As one of the “Four Arts” traditionally mastered by intellectuals in China and currently widely practised by older adults in the Chinese community, Chinese calligraphy requires a unique blend of skills. It involves not only the artistic ability to create aesthetically pleasing characters but also a high degree of concentration to maintain consistency in the motion of the brush, the stroke order, the rhythm, and the space on the paper. The practice of calligraphy engages multiple cognitive domains, including attention, memory, spatial orientation, and executive function, and necessitates fine motor control for eye-hand coordination. , , , While there are different styles in Chinese calligraphy, each with its own characteristics and purposes, they all involve the use of a brush, ink, an inkstone, and paper. This adds another layer of complexity to the practice, as the individual must also master the use of these tools.
In this RCT, we aimed to investigate whether doubling the amount of calligraphy practice could strengthen the FCs of DMN among community-living older Chinese adults who had been practising Chinese calligraphy handwriting regularly and experiencing subjective cognitive decline (SCD) but without mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or dementia. SCD is characterised by a self-perceived cognitive decline, despite normal performance on objective cognitive assessments, and cannot be explained by other physical or mental disorders. Compared to MCI, which involves measurable changes in cognitive performance noticeable to the individuals and others but does not significantly impair daily life, SCD not only serves as an earlier indicator of dementia but is also associated with a higher risk of progression to MCI—approximately one in four individuals with SCD progresses to MCI within four years, and individuals with SCD are nearly twice as likely to develop MCI as those without ; therefore, SCD may present a more opportune window for interventions to prevent or slow cognitive decline. The findings of this RCT might support and extend the previous literature that increased engagement in a familiar cognitive activity, such as calligraphy, could potentially serve as a safe and effective lifestyle intervention for maintaining the functional brain network in older adults at risk of dementia.

## Methods

## Study design
This was a two-arm, parallel-design, assessor-blind RCT conducted in Hong Kong from 1 January 2020 to 31 December 2022, with enrolment from 15 January 2020 to 31 December 2021. Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects before recruitment, and the protocol was published previously. 
The trial was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR1900024433). Ethics approval was obtained from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Chinese University of Hong Kong (CREC Ref. No.: 2019.054). This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards as laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

## Participants
Subjects were enrolled through advertisement in community centres. Potential candidates underwent a thorough screening process to ensure that they met the following criteria: age 55–75 years, ethnic Chinese, living in the community, regular practice of Chinese calligraphy handwriting, and SCD.
Regular calligraphy practice was defined as having at least 1 hour of Chinese calligraphy per week in the past 6 months. There were no restrictions on the form of Chinese calligraphy, but subjects were expected to practise in a quiet environment, and their calligraphic writing should involve brush handwriting by tracing the strokes and structures of Chinese characters displayed in a mixture of traditional calligraphic styles. 
SCD was defined by 1) scoring above the cutoff for MCI in younger-old adults with high education level (i.e., cognitive test performance within the normal range) in the Hong Kong Chinese version of Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and 2) scoring positive in the Abbreviated Memory Inventory for Chinese, a locally validated screening tool for SCD.
Exclusion criteria included non-Chinese ethnicity; living in care homes; having history of stroke, traumatic brain injury, Parkinson's disease, MCI, or clinical dementia; taking drugs that affect cognition (e.g. benzodiazepines, medications for dementia); having history of depression, bipolar affective disorder, psychosis, or substance abuse; already attending other cognitive intervention programs or having regular participation in painting (≥1 hour of painting per week); significant impairments in communication or writing; no prior calligraphy practice; and having contraindications for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).
After baseline assessment, subjects were randomised to either the intervention or control group in a 1:1 ratio as per protocol. 

## Interventions
Both groups engaged in calligraphy practice for 6 months. The control group practised calligraphy as usual—maintaining the same frequency, duration, style, and practice environment at home. The intervention group also practised as before, but with the requirement to double the amount of time of practice each week.
Adherence to intervention was monitored by instructing subjects to send a snapshot of their work and report the amount of time spent on calligraphy handwriting to the research assistant via WhatsApp or email after each practice session. The research assistant was instructed to acknowledge receipt of the work and record the practice time, without providing any feedback on the subjects’ work.

## Neuroimaging outcomes
The primary outcome was FCs of DMN. Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) data were acquired using a 3T MRI with an 8-channel SENSE head coil (Achieva, Philips Medical Systems), with subjects instructed to relax and hold still without falling asleep. A series of 3D high-resolution T1-weighted structural images and axial T2-weighted gradient echo-planar images were collected for each subject. rs-fMRI data were pre-processed using CONN release 20.b and SPM12 (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12). The pre-processing pipeline included realignment with correction of susceptibility distortion interactions, slice timing correction, outlier detection, direct segmentation and MNI-space normalization, and smoothing. The rs-fMRIs were realigned using SPM realign and unwarp procedure to the first scan of the session with a rigid transformation and resampled using b-spline interpolation. Temporal misalignment was corrected using slice-timing correction procedure. Then rs-fMRIs and T1-weighted MRIs were normalized into standard MNI space using SPM unified segmentation and normalization algorithm. Finally, rs-fMRIs were smoothed using spatial convolution with Gaussian kernel of 8 mm full width half maximum (FWHM). In addition, rs-fMRIs were denoised using a standard denoising pipeline including the regression of potential confounding effects characterised by white matter, cerebrospinal fluid, motion parameters, outlier scans, and linear trends within each run, followed by bandpass frequency filtering of the BOLD time series between 0.01 Hz and 0.1 Hz to reduce the impact of low-frequency drifts and high-frequency noise. Based on the functional anatomy of DMN, , the following brain areas were pre-specified as regions of interest (ROIs): medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), inferior parietal lobe (IPL), hippocampal formation (HF), posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), and lateral temporal cortex (LTC). Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to evaluate the FC between each ROI; a greater value indicates stronger connectivity.

## Cognitive function
Standardized locally validated neuropsychological tests were conducted by blinded assessors. Global cognition was measured using the Chinese version of the Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale-Cognitive Subscale (ADAS-Cog) and MoCA; lower ADAS-Cog scores and higher MoCA scores reflect better global cognition. Working memory was assessed using the Digit Span Backward test; recent memory and learning using the 10-min Delayed Recall test and Word Recognition test; attention using the Digit Span Forward test and Part A of Trail Making test; and executive functioning using the Part B of Trail Making test and Category Fluency test.

## Other variables
Self-reported information on the subjects' prior experience in calligraphy practice (number of years and number of hours per week), sociodemographics (age, sex, educational level, and employment status), physical morbidities (assessed by the Chronic Illness Rating Scale), and other types of cognitive activities and physical exercises performed (assessed by the Lifestyle Questionnaire) were collected. Additionally, structural MRI data were processed automatically using the AccuBrain® (BrainNow Medical Technology Company Ltd.) to generate the Alzheimer's Disease-Resemblance Atrophy Index (AD-RAI), an MRI-based machine learning-derived biomarker of brain atrophy in AD. AD-RAI is superior to medial temporal lobe atrophy, hippocampal atrophy, or blood biomarkers alone in detecting early AD and predicting conversion of SCD to MCI. , The AD-RAI ranges from 0 to 1, with a higher value indicating more resemblance to atrophy.

## Adherence
The adherence rate was calculated by dividing the actual amount of time subjects practised calligraphy by the target amount of time.

## Adverse events
An adverse event checklist was used to identify any health problems associated with our intervention during the trial. Procedures for handling adverse events and unblinding were described previously. 

## Assessment schedule and venue
The cognitive and neuroimaging outcomes and the covariates were assessed before (T0) and after intervention (T1). Adherence and adverse events were assessed at T1. The MRI scan was performed at the teaching hospital, while the rest of the assessments were performed at our research centre.

## Sample size calculation
Power analyses were conducted with G∗Power 3.1. Based on the prior results of cognitive training in people with MCI, which found a moderate effect size (0.68) in strengthening FC between hippocampus and left superior frontal lobe, a sample size of 70 would achieve adequate power (0.80) with alpha 0.05 (two-tailed) to identify significant between-group differences in FC. However, as the participants of this study were younger and did not have MCI, we anticipated a larger effect size (1.02). Nevertheless, because the control group would continue with their usual calligraphy practice, we adopted a more conservative estimate by reducing the anticipated increase by 50%. Hence, assuming an effect size of 0.85, a sample of 46 would be required to identify significant between-group differences in FC changes. To account for multiple comparisons (25 pairs of FCs) and potential attrition, we adjusted alpha to 0.002 and inflated the sample size by 20%, respectively. Consequently, a total of 112 subjects were required for this study.

## Statistical analyses
Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics (IBM). Repeated measures multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was used to evaluate the outcome changes between groups, with time and group as the independent variables, and FCs of DMN or cognitive functions as the dependent variables. Intention-to-treat analysis and Bonferroni correction were adopted. To better appreciate the changes in cognitive scores measured on different scales, a Z score was computed for each subject using the mean and standard deviation (SD) of that variable prior to the analysis. Correlation analyses were performed to test if the cognitive changes were associated with the FC changes. Sensitivity analyses were conducted for the analysis of study completers (i.e., per-protocol analysis). Paired t-test was performed to compare within-subject's FCs before and after intervention. Exploratory analyses were performed to identify subgroups with better treatment response on FC, considering that factors such as age, years of calligraphy practice, amount of weekly practice, and AD-RAI score at baseline might influence FC changes. Furthermore, post-hoc paired t-tests were performed to identify any functional hemispheric asymmetry at baseline for FC changes with significant group-by-time interactions on unilateral (rather than bilateral) hemisphere. Descriptive statistics were used to identify any significant differences in adherence rate and adverse events between groups.

## Results

## Baseline characteristics
A total of 127 individuals approached us. Out of these, 124 completed screening. Three individuals did not meet the study criteria, and nine others chose not to participate further. Hence, 112 subjects provided written informed consent and completed baseline assessment ( ). Throughout the study, five subjects (4.5%) dropped out, and 96 (85.7%) completed the follow-up fMRI scan. 
Our sample consisted of individuals with a mean age of 66.3 (4.3) years, with 83 (74.1%) females, and an average of 14.8 (3.6) years of education. At baseline, the mean ADAS-Cog and MoCA scores were 5.4 (3.7) and 28.5 (1.2), respectively. Prior to enrolment, participants had been practising calligraphy for an average duration of 9.7 years and, in the preceding six months, for an average of 3.1 hours per week. Other baseline characteristics, including sociodemographic factors, physical morbidities, other health behaviours, and severity of brain atrophy, were described in . 

## Neuroimaging outcomes
By 6 months, nearly all the FCs of DMN in the control group weakened, whereas those in the intervention group either strengthened or showed a lesser degree of weakening ( ). Statistical significance was reached in the between-group differences of the following FC changes: mPFC and right LTC (intervention vs control = 0.08 vs −0.17; group difference = 0.25 [95% CI = 0.06–0.44]; p = 0.009), mPFC and right IPL (0.07 vs −0.15; 0.23 [0.06–0.39]; p = 0.007), left HF and right LTC (0.08 vs −0.21; 0.28 [0.002–0.57]; p = 0.04), and left HF and right IPL (0.13 vs −0.21; 0.34 [0.09–0.60]; p = 0.009) ( and ). 

## Cognitive outcomes
Global cognition remained relatively stable at 6 months in both groups, with no statistically significant differences noted between the two groups ( , and in online ). Nevertheless, among various cognitive domains, significant between-group differences were observed in the changes in working memory (intervention vs control = 0.20 vs −0.23; group difference = 0.43 [95% CI = 0.10–0.75]; p = 0.01). Between-group differences of changes in other neuropsychological test scores did not reach statistical significance. 

## Correlation analyses
Changes in the z-score of the ADAS-Cog total score correlated significantly with FC changes between mPFC and right LTC (r = −0.24; 95% CI = −0.42 to −0.04; p = 0.02), mPFC and right IPL (r = −0.22; 95% CI = −0.41 to −0.02; p = 0.03), and left HF and right LTC (r = −0.21; 95% CI = −0.39 to −0.01; p = 0.04).

## Other analyses
Sensitivity analyses of FCs of DMN and cognitive functions in the study completers ( in online ) were consistent with the primary analysis. Strengthening of the above FCs were not simply explained by changes over time ( ).
The effect of doubling calligraphy practice on FC strengthening was observed regardless of age ( ), prior experience in calligraphy practice ( ), amount of prior practice ( ), or the degree of brain atrophy ( ), although the ROIs where FCs were strengthened varied between subgroups.
No significant leftward functional hemispheric asymmetry was found in the post-hoc analyses of the FCs of mPFC and left HF with LTC and IPL at baseline ( ).

## Adherence
The adherence rate was 100% (IQR = 79.3%–109.6%) for the intervention group and 107% (94.8%–126.8%) for the control group. The target amount of practice for the intervention and control groups was 5.5 (4.0) and 3.4 (2.3) hours per week, respectively, and the actual amount achieved by these two groups was 5.7 (3.4) and 3.7 (2.7) hours of practice per week. Although the subjects practised calligraphy slightly more than the target amount (an extra of 11.4 min/week in the intervention group, and an extra of 13.7 min/week in the control group), the increased amount of practice did not differ from the target amount (p = 0.85), and the difference in the amount of actual practice between the two groups still reached statistical significance (p = 0.002).

## Adverse events
21 (19.6%) subjects reported adverse effects; nevertheless, all were mild and self-limiting. The most common ones were fatigue (n = 11; 10.3%) and muscle soreness (n = 11; 10.3%). The occurrence of adverse events did not differ significantly between the two groups ( in online ).

## Discussion
In this RCT involving older adults who practised Chinese calligraphy regularly and experienced SCD, we found that doubling the amount of calligraphy practice led to strengthening of FCs between specific nodes within DMN. These observed improvements were not limited to subjects of younger age, with more years of experience or weekly practice in calligraphy, or with lesser brain atrophy. Our findings demonstrate the potential of increased cognitive activity participation in modulating the functional brain network in older adults at risk of dementia.
Compared to previous studies, , the present RCT provides novel neuroimaging evidence that participating more in cognitive activities has a positive impact on the DMN. Our findings on better working memory with more calligraphy practice are also consistent with another RCT which examined the cognitive benefits of calligraphy handwriting in older adults with MCI. Moreover, we found that improvement in the ADAS-Cog performance is associated with FC strengthening. This suggests that not only are the two related, but FC might be more sensitive than cognitive tests in detecting the changes brought about by calligraphy practice in SCD. Taken together, our findings highlight the importance of promoting active participation in cognitive activities as part of a lifestyle intervention for better brain health in older populations.
Despite maintaining practice, the control group showed deterioration in FCs of DMN. As our subjects had already been practising calligraphy for, on average, nearly a decade, these findings suggest that continuous participation may no longer provide sufficient stimulation to the brain. However, the intervention group, which doubled their practice, demonstrated stronger DMN FCs and better working memory. These findings suggest that increasing exposure to accustomed cognitive activities has some potential to slow FC dysfunction and cognitive decline in older adults who have not yet developed MCI or dementia. The high adherence rate and absence of serious adverse effects further support that participating more in cognitive activities could serve as a safe and effective non-pharmacological intervention for cognitive and network maintenance in community-living older adults.
We found that increasing calligraphy practice leads to stronger FCs of DMN at rest, particularly in mPFC and hippocampal formation with temporal cortex and inferior parietal lobe. Although this RCT did not include a task-based fMRI study or examine other networks, we speculate that these brain regions are co-activated during internally focused calligraphy tasks (such as recalling words, relating to knowledge of character style and form, reflecting on prior practice experiences, and envisioning the final piece of work) and de-activated during externally focused cognitive processes (such as monitoring and correcting writing errors). , Increased calligraphy practice may thus induce neuroplastic changes in brain circuitry, leading to re-organization and strengthening of DMN, improved connectivities within and between networks, and optimized activities during resting state.
Interestingly, we observed strengthened FCs on the right (as opposed to the left) parietal and temporal lobes with mPFC and hippocampus. Given the greater vulnerability of the left hemisphere in AD and the right-handedness of our subjects, one would expect intervention to ameliorate deficits on the left hemisphere. However, our subjects were cognitively relatively unimpaired, with no baseline evidence of leftward functional asymmetry. Moreover, the direction of asymmetry changes across different life stages, with greater FC over the right hemisphere in adulthood for visuospatial and attention processing. As calligraphy handwriting involves visuospatial coordination, attention, and non-verbal memory, which are associated with the non-dominant parietal and temporal lobes, increased practice might help older adults preserve the healthy rightward functional advantage.
This study had several strengths. We used a validated questionnaire to screen for SCD, and we excluded MCI, dementia, and, importantly, conditions that mimic SCD. Our subjects were randomised properly, with all baseline characteristics well-balanced between groups. Despite the need to double the amount of calligraphy practice for 6 months, adherence rate was high, attrition rate was low, and adverse events were mild and self-limiting. The risk of introducing social interactions was minimized, as our subjects practised calligraphy alone at home. Standardized validated neuropsychological tests were used to assess cognitive outcomes, with alternate versions used in follow-up to minimize the practice effect.
Nevertheless, this study had some limitations. Firstly, our subjects were ethnic Chinese, well educated, cognitively relatively healthy, and experienced in Chinese calligraphy handwriting, so caution is needed when applying our findings to other older populations. Further studies are needed to examine if the observed benefits from increased participation in calligraphy practice, or other cognitive activities such as painting, sculpting, and knitting, are consistent across different ethnic groups and cultural backgrounds. The practice of calligraphy handwriting is not exclusive to China. In fact, Western calligraphy, which originated as early as the Latin writing system, is still practised today, typically commissioned for private purposes such as wedding invitations and memorial documents. Similar to Chinese or Islamic calligraphy, Western calligraphy scripts adhere to strict rules and shapes, with each character having a precise stroke order. Given the universal nature of calligraphy across cultures, it is highly plausible that participating in other forms of calligraphy could also confer similar cognitive benefits to older adults. Secondly, our RCT was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, which might have an impact on the participants' cognition, behaviour, and their adherence to indoor activities. The enrolment period (and thus the trial period) of this study was extended by a year because subject recruitment was temporarily halted. However, due to the zero-COVID policy in Hong Kong, the number of COVID-19 cases among our subjects was low (2 in each group). While the precautionary measures such as travel restriction and home quarantine implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic might have contributed to the high adherence rate of calligraphy practice, it is crucial to note that a substantial deterioration of cognition in older adults was observed during the pandemic, possibly explained by major dementia risk factors, such as reduced exercise, loneliness, and diminished cognitive stimulation, being more prevalent during the pandemic. This observed continuous decline in cognition underscores the importance of engaging in more indoor cognitive activities to maintain cognitive health in older adults during the pandemic. Thirdly, we did not examine the effect in older adults without prior calligraphy experience. Nor did we include a control group that did not engage in calligraphy or any other cognitive activities. As this study was intentionally designed to examine the benefits of increasing participation in a familiar cognitive activity, rather than the effect of adopting a new cognitive activity, we deliberately recruited older adults with regular calligraphy practice and designed our control group to maintain their usual calligraphy practice. Nevertheless, given that 2 out of 5 older adults in Hong Kong do not participate in any cognitive activity in late life, and considering the large variety of cognitive activities, we recognise the importance of embarking on new studies to explore the effect of taking up a new cognitive activity on FCs, to discern whether different activities contribute to enhancements in distinct networks and cognitive domains, and to test whether increasing the complexity (instead of the dosage) of practice or employing AI technology to tailor the training difficulty based on the individual's ability can assist older adults in optimizing their brain health. Such understanding would help us in developing personalized interventions tailored for different at-risk older adults. Fourthly, in this study, the intervention only lasted for 6 months, and there was a lack of a follow-up period after the intervention to ascertain the sustainability of the cognitive benefits. It would be interesting to investigate if a longer exposure to increased calligraphy practice results in greater cognitive benefits and neurobiological changes, how long these effects will last after stopping this intensive cognitive workout, and what could be done for older adults to maintain their brain networks and cognition when FCs or cognitive functions begin to decline again. In particular, we are interested to determine whether another course of intensive participation in cognitive activities, akin to a booster dose in vaccination, could restore the cognitive benefits that have waned over time, provided that the individual remains free of mild cognitive impairment or dementia. Fifthly, this trial selected DMN as the network of interest and did not include other neural networks or biomarkers. Although various functional brain networks are also associated with cognitive decline in dementia, DMN is the most studied in Alzheimer's disease, and its evidence of changes with cognitive decline in Alzheimer's disease is most consistent across studies. Nevertheless, in addition to fMRI, the use of other neuroscientific techniques, such as electroencephalography (EEG) or functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), could potentially provide a more comprehensive understanding of how cognitive activities improve brain health. While this study found FC strengthening with increased calligraphy practice regardless of differences in age, prior calligraphy experience and amount of practice, and degree of brain atrophy, care needs to be taken when interpreting these results as the subgroup analyses were exploratory in nature and not adequately powered. Lastly, exploring the feasibility of implementing such interventions in different socio-economic contexts, as well as their cost-benefit ratios, can help to understand the possibility of implementing such interventions in a wider population. Given that calligraphy is easy to be implemented, involves minimal cost, can be practised alone at home, and does not cause any serious adverse effects, the cost-benefit ratio is likely to be favourable.

## Conclusions
Increased Chinese calligraphy practice leads to some neuromodulatory effects on DMN in older adults with prior regular practice and SCD. Engaging more in cognitive activities may serve as a safe, practical, and useful non-pharmacological intervention for maintaining the functional integrity of brain network in older adults at risk of dementia.
