In Africa, the prevalence of HAP exposure has surged, with 75% of households subjected to harmful pollution levels. This problem disproportionately impacts rural or marginalised urban communities, particularly women and children, thereby exacerbating existing health disparities. In Nigeria, over 80% of households use unprocessed solid fuels or kerosene for cooking, leading to HAP, a significant risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD). HAP is associated with elevated rates of non-communicable diseases, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, stroke, and hypertension. HAP is strongly correlated with elevated blood pressure (BP), increased heart rates, and subsequent CVD. The relationship between BP and ambient air pollution is well documented. Similarly, the association between elevated BP and increased HAP, particularly from indoor biomass combustion, has also been reported. Clinical trial evidence demonstrated that transitioning from the use of solid fuels to clean cookstoves can significantly reduce pollutant emissions and BP. A 2019 systematic review showed that the effects of clean cookstoves on BP were significant for both systolic and diastolic BP. However, clean stove use only limits exposure by reducing emissions from fuel consumption. Achieving optimal health also requires transitioning to the use of clean fuels. Bioethanol, a clean fuel successfully used in African countries, presents a viable alternative to kerosene and solid fuels alongside liquefied petroleum gas (LPG). 
Transitioning to cleaner cookstoves and fuels requires a deep understanding of the cultural, socioeconomic, and individual user needs among community stakeholders. Several barriers impede the adoption of clean fuel-clean stoves (CF-CS), including stove incompatibility with local cooking practices, insufficient heat generation, safety concerns, and the persistence of stove-stacking when households adopt CF-CS but continue using traditional equipment due to relative fuel costs and cooking requirements for specific dishes. 
The Household Air Pollution Intervention Network (HAPIN) trial tested an intensive LPG intervention in Guatemala, India, Peru, and Rwanda. The trial achieved large reductions in personal exposure to fine particulate matter and carbon monoxide among pregnant women and adult women compared with control households that continued using biomass. Despite these exposure gains, the primary clinical outcomes showed no clear improvements over the trial period. The main HAPIN reports found no significant effects on infant birth weight and child pneumonia, and ancillary analyses in adult women did not detect reductions in BP after up to 18 months of LPG use. These results highlight a persistent implementation gap. Clean fuel programmes can lower exposure substantially, yet real-world health benefits may depend on sustained adoption, consistent exclusive use, affordability, and alignment with local cooking needs.
Therefore, understanding community perspectives on clean fuel and stove adoption is essential. Stakeholder views on fit with local cuisines, heat output, safety, fuel cost and reliability, and the tendency toward stove stacking directly influence uptake and sustained use. Insights from end users, community leaders, front-line health workers, and household decision makers can inform the design of implementation strategies that are acceptable, feasible, and scalable in resource-constrained settings such as Lagos, Nigeria.
In this study, we engaged community stakeholders and households in Lagos, Nigeria, to identify the challenges and facilitators for implementing CF-CS interventions in their communities, guided by the Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, and Sustainment (EPIS) framework. Specifically, we sought to understand how contextual and innovation factors influence perceived fit and acceptability during the pre-implementation phase, what barriers and facilitators should inform implementation strategies, and which stakeholder-recommended approaches could support initial adoption of CF-CS.

## Methods

## Conceptual framework
The EPIS framework guided our theoretical approach (see ). This framework describes the implementation of evidence-based practices (EBPs) as a multi-phase process (pre-implementation, implementation, and post-implementation phase) influenced by contextual factors. These contextual factors may be external or internal to the organisation, community, or individuals implementing the EBP or innovation. This study was conducted during the pre-implementation phase (Exploration and Preparation stages), which guided our data collection and analytical approach. For the Exploration stage, the health needs of a community were explored, and information obtained was used to refine the proposed CF-CS intervention. The Preparation phase was used to identify challenges and facilitators of CF-CS implementation within the outer (environment external to the implementation context) and inner (characteristics within the implementation context) contexts of the community sites. This information is then used to assess adaptation needs and develop a detailed plan that addresses potential challenges and capitalises on identified facilitators. 
 

## Study design
We used qualitative framework analysis guided by EPIS to address the goals of this study. Specifically, we conducted focus group discussions (FGDs) and in-depth semi-structured interviews (IDIs) to inform the needs assessment or pre-implementation phase. We used the COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research (COREQ) to guide transparent reporting of study conduct and findings.

## Study setting, participants, and sampling
Stratified, geography-based purposive sampling was used for participant recruitment. The study included 32 peri-urban communities distributed across all five administrative divisions of Lagos State, where the use of unprocessed solid fuels for cooking is frequent, to capture heterogeneity in the implementation context. shows the geographic distribution of sampled communities by division.
 In partnership with regional Medical Officers of Health from the five divisions, who serve as community health representatives, we purposefully recruited 128 key stakeholders from the 32 communities via local community development associations and known authority figures between February and March 2022. We purposefully sampled the key stakeholders due to their extensive knowledge, active engagement, and valuable insights drawn from their direct experiences living in the communities. Their deep involvement provided a comprehensive understanding of the local context, ensuring the relevance of the data collected. We chose this approach over other types of sampling because purposeful sampling allowed us to focus on individuals whose expertise and experience were essential for gaining in-depth and context-specific insights relevant to the CF-CS intervention.
The stakeholders comprised community influencers, traditional rulers, religious leaders, market women leaders, youth leaders, members of the community health committee, and staff of the primary healthcare centres (PHCs). Participants were recruited to ensure adequate representation of all key stakeholders from community leaders and households within each division. Stakeholders represented a sub-population of those who would most likely use CF-CS within the study’s target community. Stakeholders were excluded if they were unable to provide consent or were not from the five divisions of Lagos state.

## Participant eligibility
We defined clear eligibility criteria to ensure that participants reflected the local context and had relevant perspectives on household energy and health. All participants provided verbal informed consent prior to data collection, and language accommodations were available in English and Yoruba. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are outlined below.

## Inclusion criteria
Age 18 years or older.
Resident of one of the five administrative divisions of Lagos State at the time of data collection.
Holds a relevant stakeholder role such as community leader, religious leader, market leader, youth leader, or household decision maker.
Able to participate in English or Yoruba.
Provides verbal informed consent.

## Exclusion criteria
Unable to provide informed consent.
Not residing in one of the five administrative divisions of Lagos State during data collection.

## Sample size
We based the estimation of the sample size on best practices for maximising informational power (i.e., the more information shared that is related to the study goals, the lower the number of participants needed) of qualitative research. Information power is determined by the specificity of the study goals, the use of theory, and dialogue quality. Smaller sample sizes are required with higher specificity on each element. It is recommended to begin with 20 participants and to add to the sample, as needed, to maximise information power. For this study, sampling was planned with the expectation that the initial targets would be sufficient to achieve thematic saturation, including saturation across relevant subgroups such as stakeholder types and geographic divisions. Ultimately, we interviewed 128 key stakeholders, approximately 25 participants per division, allowing for robust representation and ensuring thematic sufficiency across domains.

## Interview structure
Medical Officers of Health, in collaboration with members of the research team, approached potential participants. If interested, study procedures were explained, and verbal informed consent was obtained before the focus groups and individual interviews were conducted. Participants were given the option to participate in either FGDs or IDIs. The choice between these two formats was contingent on the number of participants willing to be interviewed on the day of the session and their comfort level with participating in a group discussion. We used both sex-stratified FGDs and in-depth individual semi-structured interviews to balance breadth and depth of perspectives. Focus groups elicited community norms, shared language, and cross-stakeholder facilitators and barriers relevant to implementation, while individual interviews enabled private discussion of sensitive topics such as household budgeting for fuel, safety experiences, and intra-household decision-making that may be influenced by social desirability in a group. Separate sessions were conducted for males and females to capture diverse perspectives and enhance openness and candour within each group. This approach aimed to ensure a more comprehensive understanding of the community’s needs and views. Before the interviews, the research team conducted an informational session to demonstrate the CF-CS features. This session included instructions on installing fuel canisters, turning the stove on and off, cooking, and storing the fuel.
Ten FGDs consisting of 102 stakeholders and 26 IDIs were conducted at town halls, PHCs, and church halls, which were centrally located within each division to encourage representation from all communities. Two FGDs, one for men and the other for women, were conducted in each of the five divisions. The mean focus group size was 10 participants per group. Stakeholders who did not participate in focus groups were invited to IDIs. Both FGDs and IDIs were held on the same day and at the same venue for each division. Interviewees represented various stakeholder groups, including religious leaders, market women, and youth leaders.
Focus groups and semi-structured interviews, each lasting approximately 60 min, were digitally audio-recorded, with field notes taken during the sessions. Each focus group was moderated by one team member who was trained in qualitative research, with a second team member serving as note taker; moderation rotated among the Nigerian-based study team members (RO, DA, TA, FA, OO and FO) under AA’s oversight. In total, 10 focus groups and 26 individual interviews were conducted. Focus groups and interviews were conducted in the participants’ preferred language, English or Yoruba, which are the local languages spoken by most participants. Drawing on their medical expertise and public health training, interviewers contributed to healthcare delivery and built trusted relationships within communities across the five divisions, thus equipping them with a deep understanding of community dynamics. In addition to the audio recording, detailed notes were taken by the study team. Study team members transcribed the audio content verbatim and translated the transcripts into English when required, saving all transcripts in text documents (Microsoft Word). Documents were stored on a secure server and uploaded to Dedoose, an online qualitative coding programme, for subsequent coding and analyses. No financial incentives were given to stakeholders to participate in focus groups or interviews.

## Researcher positionality and reflexivity
Recognising that our roles as university-affiliated researchers and clinicians could create power dynamics, we mitigated this by having Nigerian community-based team members lead data collection in the preferred language spoken by participants, using neutral prompts, reiterating confidentiality and voluntariness, clarifying that participation would not affect services or benefits, and conducting sessions in familiar community spaces.

## Qualitative interview guides
The interview guides for focus groups and individual interviews were based on the Exploration and Preparation phases of the EPIS framework, with modifications to focus on CF-CS use. The questions addressed participants’ views on their cooking behaviours, knowledge of clean fuels and clean stoves, potential challenges and facilitators to using these cleaner cooking options, and their willingness to purchase and use them if available. Example prompts included “What advantages would clean cookstoves have in your community?”, “What challenges might you face using a clean cookstove?”, and “How much would you be willing to pay for a stove and for monthly fuel?”, including preferences for financing options.
The interview guide questions also explored community leaders’ perceptions of the programme’s value and its potential effects on health. For leaders, we asked, “How could your role support adoption in your community?” For household adults, who make daily cooking decisions, questions explored their ability to use the clean cookstove, their cooking practices, the affordability of the clean cookstoves and ethanol fuel, the barriers they perceive to the use of CF-CS, and their understanding of the purpose of the CF-CS programme and its value. For household decision makers, we asked, “How would family members react to using a clean cookstove?” and “What follow-up visit frequency would be acceptable?” All participants were asked to provide suggestions on how they could support CF-CS adoption within their respective communities.
To ensure cultural relevance and clarity, interview guides were co-developed with the Nigerian field team. Prior to implementation, guides were pilot tested with members of the research team and with community health workers from Lagos State University College of Medicine, who regularly serve the target communities, allowing us to refine wording, remove redundancies, and strengthen alignment with the local context. Guides were administered in English or Yoruba with verbal informed consent. Sample interview guides are provided in .

## Data analysis and coding
We used the EPIS framework to guide inductive and deductive qualitative coding and analysis. Analyses were conducted within a constructivist and interpretivist stance with a pragmatic focus on generating actionable insights for implementation. Four research team members (DO, SM, RO and AA) were responsible for coding the transcripts. All team members possess experience working within the Nigerian context and engaging with Nigerian communities. All four research team members first underwent data familiarisation, a process during which the researchers became immersed in the details of multiple sources of data to gain a general understanding of the content and to document initial impressions. This involved reading through 1–2 transcripts. We then began inductive open coding, identifying concepts directly from the data and iteratively grouping them into preliminary categories through ongoing comparison and team discussion.
Next, the team identified emergent themes across multiple data sources while anchoring the interpretation within the EPIS model to ensure a structured and theory-driven approach. Themes were continually refined and compared with each other. Coders then indexed the codes, during which the team members further immersed themselves in the data to refine identified themes and sub-themes and summarised the data in an analytical framework. Next, the information was reduced into understandable but brief summaries of what was voiced by stakeholders. Finally, researchers synthesised and interpreted the data, comparing themes and sub-themes against the original transcripts, field notes, and audio recordings to ensure appropriate context. This inductive phase was followed by a deductive phase in which we organised and interpreted these emerging categories using the EPIS framework to understand how determinants aligned across inner setting, outer setting, and bridging factors.
Inter-rater reliability was assessed and determined with a subset of the data and repeated until satisfactory agreement among raters was achieved (~80% of coded data). Discrepancies in coded data were resolved by consensus and adjudication by SW, a senior research professor guiding the study. After systematically reading all transcripts, each was coded into concepts reflecting what was voiced. Established procedures to enhance the trustworthiness of the analysis were used, including analyses of codes that did not fit the coding scheme, the development of an audit trail documenting analytical decisions, and cross-checking coded transcripts with the research team. A detailed analysis of the data generated a conceptual model (see in the Discussion section of this manuscript) that elucidates challenges and facilitators to the uptake of CF-CS in participating communities. Coding and data management were conducted in Dedoose. Data were collected and analysed until thematic saturation was achieved. The decision to conclude data analysis at the point of saturation ensured a comprehensive understanding of the challenges and facilitators associated with CF-CS use across households in Lagos.
 

## Results

## Stakeholders’ (participants) demographics
Purposeful sampling yielded near-equal representation across the five divisions (Badagry, Epe, Ikorodu, Lagos Island and Ikeja) and across key stakeholder groups. Overall, 128 participants were recruited, the majority through focus groups. Participants were predominantly Yoruba, with additional representation from other ethnic groups, and demographic characteristics were broadly similar across divisions and between focus group and interview participants. A total of 26 individual interviews were conducted, with five per division except for Ikeja, which had six. summarises demographic characteristics by division.
 Several challenges to implementing a CF-CS intervention were identified by stakeholders and are discussed below.

## Themes associated with challenges to CF-CS implementation

## Stove stacking
Participants reported using a variety of fuels, including LPG, firewood, kerosene, and charcoal. These fuels were often used simultaneously within the home, with kerosene and firewood being the most common combination and major contributors to HAP. Interviews indicated that households engaged in stove stacking as a functional strategy informed by the type of dish being prepared and the cost of refilling LPG. A female FGD respondent from Ikeja explained, “We use gas stoves; we also use charcoal stoves. I use both of them together. If I use that gas, I fill one cylinder for 11 000 naira (~$11). So if I use charcoal to cook soup, I use gas to warm it.” Functional considerations such as required heat intensity, cooking duration and fuel cost, rather than taste per se, appeared to drive concurrent stove use, highlighting stove stacking as an adaptive strategy shaped by both cooking requirements and fuel affordability.

## Cost/price of CF-CS
There were concerns about the cost of the clean stove, which serves as a barrier to CF-CS use among participants. A female IDI respondent from EPE stated, “I’ll discuss with my people, i.e., other community members, to decide on the range that we think it [clean stove] should be sold for… everyone now knows that the cost of everything has gone up, but [we will buy] if they can sell it to us for 25 000 Naira (~$20). We know that things are now expensive.” Participants shared the opinion that the stove was expensive and beyond their purchasing power. The consensus was that the proposed price of the cookstoves was too high, with a few respondents expecting CF-CS to be given freely for a trial period. The maximum amount participants were willing to pay ranged from 5000 naira (~US$3) to 30 000 naira (~US$20), whereas the minimum monthly wage in Nigeria is 58 000 naira (~US$38).
Participants were also concerned about the long-term stability of the stove and biofuel prices. In one of the FGD interviews, a male respondent from Ikeja remarked, “Then those things we will be using for this stove should be available and affordable. We don’t want a situation whereby next year there will be inflation. You know that’s what happened to gas, the price kept increasing, and people are going back to firewood and charcoal.” Participants compared the CF-CS stove innovation to the introduction of LPG in Nigeria. They noted that when LPG was first introduced, it was affordable, but as more people began to use it, the price increased over time and is now considered expensive by the community. Participants expressed concerns that the clean stove and its biofuel might follow the same trajectory.

## Fuel availability
The availability and accessibility of CF-CS fuel were crucial factors that participants stated would influence their use of CF-CS. In one of the IDIs in Ikorodu, a female respondent explained, “I will use it on a regular basis if the ethanol is available. Buying the clean cookstove is not the problem, it is the availability of the ethanol. To make it attractive to people, it will encourage people to buy it.” An important concern pertained to the continued availability, accessibility, and affordability of ethanol fuel within the community. Of particular concern was the potential scenario where ethanol might not be readily obtainable from local depots, thereby posing a challenge to sustained adoption of CF-CS. This issue is compounded by the introduction of CF-CS as a novel technology that relies on ethanol, a fuel that is new to this community. Interview respondents complained about the unavailability of kerosene due to the fuel shortage across the country and the rising cost of LPG. Therefore, a switch to ethanol would be feasible, but only if the fuel were available at accessible locations within their neighbourhoods. Participants noted that clean fuel would be considered ‘stable’ only if it was consistently stocked at neighbourhood depots or markets, was readily accessible within their area, and remained affordable; with these conditions satisfied, they would be willing to use the stove frequently.

## Concerns about durability, accessibility and maintenance of the CF-CS
Respondents raised concerns about the stove’s durability and the associated maintenance costs. Additionally, respondents expressed worry about the availability of repair services outside of the study team’s office if the stove were to get damaged over time. For example, a male FGD respondent from EPE pointed out, “The disadvantage I can see there is that as they have brought it now, if the materials beneath get spoiled or damaged it may be difficult to source them and other spare parts…Because it is metal it can easily get rusted when water pours on it while cooking.” Another significant concern was the quality of the stoves; counterfeit products with inferior components could potentially pose safety risks and performance issues for consumers.

## Trust in institutions and new technologies
Several respondents expressed scepticism towards clean fuel and stove programmes introduced by external organisations, reflecting broader mistrust in institutional systems. Participants sought reassurance regarding the safety and reliability of the stove, asking whether it had been successfully used in other countries or communities within Nigeria. For instance, a male FGD respondent from Ikeja remarked, “The reason I said no is that you are still advertising black market. If someone is sick and you recommend a drug, the question will be ‘have you tried it before?’ It has not been practicalized, hence I am saying no………” Many participants voiced reluctance to be 'guinea pigs’ for this new technology. Concerns were also raised about child safety and whether the research team had fully disclosed any potential side effects of the stove.

## Perceived culinary concerns
Participants expressed concerns about the ability of the use of the CF-CS stove to cook traditional dishes that require extended cooking times and high heat delivery. For example, an IDI participant from Ikorodu stated, “It depends on what I will cook, but if I decide to cook beans, you know it takes about 1 hour and 40 minutes. I'm not sure how easy that will be with the CF-CS stove. If I use it, I will use it daily.” There was also concern about the fuel efficiency and capacity of the stove, with worries that fuel cells might need frequent replacement. This could lead to increased costs, storage needs, or the challenge of purchasing fuel more frequently in an uncertain distribution environment.

## Equipment safety
Participants raised concerns about the potential dangers associated with handling the equipment. A male FGD respondent from Ikeja expressed, “But does it explode like other cooking gas? And this ethanol, where will we be sourcing it from that our people will not start pouring other fuels into it?” There were additional concerns that if ethanol fuel was not readily accessible, people might resort to using alternative fuels, potentially leading to accidents and household disasters.

## Themes associated with facilitators of CF-CS implementation

## Payment flexibility
Despite concerns over cost constraints and the likelihood of price reductions, all participants expressed willingness to purchase the stove if alternative payment methods were available aside from upfront costs. Respondents cited dissatisfaction with the high cost of other fuels and indicated that the ability to use the cookstove at a reduced price compared with alternative fuels would sway their decision. For example, a male FGD respondent from EPE suggested, “We could adopt a community approach, where each ward receives three stoves at a time through a cooperative society. This way, we could sign for the stoves and pay gradually in installments, starting with an initial payment, and specifying when we can settle the balance. When others see us using the stoves, they may inquire about where we obtained them.” Participants demonstrated their willingness by proposing methods to reduce the stove’s cost to more affordable levels or offering instalment payments to alleviate financial burdens. While other funding suggestions such as mortgages, thrift cooperatives (ajo), or microfinance companies were mentioned, they were generally poorly received due to participants’ reluctance to pay any form of interest on loans.

## Test drive CF-CS
CF-CS was introduced to participants as a new technology, prompting them to express a preference for testing it before committing to purchase the cookstoves. A female respondent from an IDI in Ikorodu stated, “If they see it and I’m using it and they like it, they will request for it. None of my family members will discourage me from using it. They will be eager to get one.” Participants noted that similar test drives were employed when introducing LPG into the community. Moreover, participants were willing to use the testing period as an opportunity to promote CF-CS to other households in their neighbourhood, contingent on the stove’s performance during the trial.

## Excitement to use CF-CS
Respondents expressed excitement about adopting the new cooking technology, describing it as a “marvel.” They conveyed gratitude and optimism for the potential introduction of CF-CS to their community, emphasising its anticipated ease of use and portability among community members once implemented. For instance, a male FGD respondent from EPE remarked, “It is with immediate effect that the community members will change their cooking method because this clean cook stove is far better than gas cooker……. This new style of cooking is the best way of cooking because it is stress free and portable.” After the demonstration, participants praised CF-CS for its innovation, highlighting its improved fuel efficiency and ability to cook food faster compared with other fuels like charcoal and firewood. They described CF-CS as attractive, stylish, and “thoroughly modern.”

## Preference for enhanced cooking equipment
Participants highly valued the promise of improved cooking equipment offered by the CF-CS compared with other stoves, particularly pressurised LPGs, which are prone to equipment discolouration. For instance, a male FGD respondent from EPE remarked, “Regarding the clean stove we’re discussing, charcoal stains our cooking pots, and gas cookers also blacken them. But this clean cookstove doesn’t pose such hazards. We know gas emissions can be harmful to health and shorten lifespans, unlike this clean cook stove, which has no such Wahala [trouble] or hazard in using it.” Additionally, participants expressed a strong preference for fuels that do not emit hazardous soot and smoke, which can lead to respiratory issues. They highlighted that ethanol, when burned, does not stain pots, unlike solid fuels. Health benefits were infrequently raised spontaneously. When mentioned, participants described less smoke in the kitchen, fewer coughs and eye irritation, and easier breathing, emphasising improved comfort. For example, a female IDI respondent from Badagry stated, “That if we use it, it will reduce the risk of pollution and it reduces some ailments like hypertension. For example, the way we use charcoal, that we inhale fumes and we will be coughing, therefore, with the help of this clean cookstove, it won’t allow such to happen.”

## Community leader endorsement
Community leaders expressed readiness to promote the adoption of CF-CS among their community members. They demonstrated confidence in their ability to explain the purpose of CF-CS, encourage its adoption, and engage key stakeholders to facilitate widespread use. For example, a male FGD respondent from Ikeja stated, “Apart from cooperatives, we can use the church, the mosques, the market, and the Community Development Committee as vital community platforms. For instance, pastors can identify church members and inform them about purchasing cookstoves, ensuring those interested are enlisted and committed. The same approach applies to mosques and the wider community.” Furthermore, due to the innovative features of the stove, many expressed eagerness to use it and serve as promoters or community influencers to endorse CF-CS within their communities, leveraging their positions.

## Subsidies and business opportunities from CF-CS dissemination
Respondents expressed interest in subsidies for CF-CS, noting that while they were willing to use the stoves, their enthusiasm would be significantly boosted with financial support or subsidies. They argued that subsidised prices would likely lead to higher adoption rates among households. For example, a female FGD respondent from Lagos stated, “If it is subsidized, people will love it because it is quite useful.”
Additionally, some participants expressed willingness to act as retailers and promote CF-CS within their social circles. Beyond domestic use, a few suggested enhancements, such as increasing the stove size and expanding its capacity for commercial purposes, are needed. Some participants proposed engaging in marketing and acting as intermediaries for both the biofuel and the stove, seeing it as a viable commercial opportunity. For instance, a male FGD respondent from Lagos remarked, “Those of us known in the community could act as intermediaries, buying and connecting with salespersons when community members want to purchase. This would improve accessibility.”
Stakeholders identified a total of seven challenges and seven facilitators across multiple thematic areas, as detailed in below. These themes have been mapped to correspond with the EPIS constructs and domains, which serve as the foundational framework guiding the analytical approach in this study.
 The application of the EPIS framework allowed for a systematic categorisation of challenges and facilitators in CF-CS implementation, addressing both system-level (outer context) and organisational-level (inner context) factors. During the exploration phase, stakeholder concerns, such as cost and fuel availability, were identified as potential barriers to adoption. The preparation phase highlighted key facilitators, including subsidies and payment flexibility, as well as bridging and innovation factors linking the inner and outer contexts. ## Discussion
In this study, we conducted a needs assessment to explore challenges and facilitators influencing stakeholders’ adoption of CF-CS use in Lagos, Nigeria. Stakeholders identified several factors that influenced both their own and their communities’ attitudes toward using the CF-CS. The four main findings are (1) the cost and price of the CF-CS as well as affordability of the fuel compared with alternatives; (2) the constant availability of fuel and accessibility of the fuel within the neighbourhood or walking distance from their home; (3) technological perception, usability, and durability of CF-CS, and (4) community influence and business potential to facilitate uptake of CF-CS.
Costs and fuel stability emerged as the central adoption barriers. With a minimum monthly wage of about US$38, a stove priced at US$15 to 25 represents a substantial upfront expense, and price volatility for fuels reinforces household caution. These constraints suggest that financing and price protections are necessary complements to technology rollout. Practical options include instalment plans through cooperatives or microfinance, targeted subsidies for low-income households, and trial periods that lower perceived risk at purchase.
Concerns about reliable access to ethanol reflect Nigeria’s history of fuel disruptions and the novelty of ethanol as a cooking fuel in these settings. Without dependable last-mile distribution, exclusive use is unlikely, and stove stacking will persist. Programme design should therefore secure supply chains, place fuel at routine points of purchase within neighbourhoods, and budget for ongoing monitoring of stockouts and prices.
Perceptions of usability and durability were mixed. Speed and cleanliness were valued, but doubts about safety, longevity, and capacity for long-simmer dishes tempered enthusiasm. These views point to the need for hands-on demonstrations tied to after-sales support, clear safety guidance, and user training that addresses cooking tasks requiring sustained heat. Trust is likely to grow if users experience dependable performance and local service when problems occur.
Community leadership can accelerate diffusion. Religious and civic leaders are trusted information sources and could serve as visible early adopters, advocates, and even micro distributors that close last-mile gaps for fuel and stove parts. Partnering with these leaders, while tracking uptake and service metrics, can align community influence with reliable access and reinforce consistent use.
Our findings are like those of other CF-CS programme implementations in Nigeria and Ghana. For example, in a study conducted in Nigeria, following the introduction of the bioethanol stove, researchers assessed the acceptability and willingness to pay for CF-CS among participants and found that one-third of the pilot participants expressed a willingness to purchase the stove for the minimum price of US$42, and bio-ethanol sales data suggested sustained but non-exclusive use of the CF-CS (i.e., stove stacking). Just as stakeholders in our study emphasised the potential impact of cost on CF-CS utilisation, a study in Ghana, a region similar to Nigeria in West Africa, also identified cost and maintenance as crucial factors driving CF-CS adoption among users. 
Similar to concerns raised in our study about usability, durability, and cooking practices, a study conducted in Ethiopia found that despite reduced HAP and subsidised clean cookstoves among low-income households in Addis Ababa, stove stacking continued as part of their cooking practice (i.e., the use of other types of cookstoves in combination with the clean cookstove). Conversely, in a study examining birth outcomes and sustained usage of bioethanol cookstoves in an urban Nigerian city, over time, there was a significant decline in traditional kerosene stove usage among participants using bioethanol stoves. Approximately 84% of these participants gave away their kerosene stoves before the conclusion of the study (56% within the first month of enrolment), suggesting that the bioethanol stove replaced the kerosene stoves. 
Our analysis highlights that the cost of the cookstove, the availability, and the long-term sustainability of ethanol, especially beyond the initial research phase, are critical considerations for individuals in peri-urban communities of Lagos targeted by a CF-CS intervention. Our study provides a context-specific exploration of the barriers and opportunities to CF-CS adoption in low-income peri-urban settings like Lagos, Nigeria. It offers a nuanced understanding of how affordability can impact uptake in communities with limited disposable income, while also presenting tailored strategies to mitigate these challenges, such as payment plans, community-based financing cooperatives, the creation of distribution networks, and the use of community leaders as advocates, intermediaries, and influencers for CF-CS and the ethanol fuel.
While there was enthusiasm for the innovation, stakeholders also expressed mistrust and scepticism about the cookstoves and the fuel. These insights directly informed how the full CF-CS programme was designed (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05048147. Registered 17 September 2021). The implementation strategy was developed collaboratively with community teams in Nigeria to ensure cultural relevance, transparency, and trust. Subsidies were incorporated to reduce the financial burden of stove and fuel adoption, and fuel distribution networks were established with local distributors to ensure reliable access. In addition, community health workers and local champions were recruited to serve as liaisons between households and the study team, helping to address concerns and strengthen community buy-in. These strategies are being evaluated as part of the broader ongoing CF-CS trial. This community-driven approach underscores the importance of leveraging trusted local leadership to improve acceptance, reduce scepticism, and support equitable access to clean cooking technologies.
Interestingly, discussions around health benefits directly linked to transitioning to cleaner cooking fuels and stoves were relatively sparse. However, when mentioned, they were perceived as potential facilitators of clean cookstove adoption. Stakeholders recognised that these cookstoves offer a solution to health risks associated with solid fuels. The limited salience of health benefits in interviews likely reflects immediate priorities of cost and fuel reliability, despite established links between HAP and respiratory and cardiovascular harms and the large exposure reductions achieved with LPGs in HAPIN, where clinical endpoints were mixed. This underscores the need to pair health messaging with concrete assurances about affordability and stable supply.

## Strengths
One major strength of this study is that the stakeholders interviewed also served as key influencers and respected decision-makers within the communities. These respected leaders are trusted voices in the communities where CF-CS implementation is planned.
Another notable strength of this study is its diverse regional sampling. Lagos State, known for its cosmopolitan nature encompassing urban, peri-urban, and rural areas, offered a broad spectrum of perspectives. By sampling from the five major divisions, we ensured comprehensive coverage of contextually relevant nuances that could impact CF-CS implementation across Lagos State.
Additionally, the study situates its findings within the broader context of CF-CS implementation research across similar settings in Nigeria, Ghana, and Ethiopia. By comparing our findings with existing studies, our study highlights consistent patterns in adoption barriers (e.g., cost, fuel availability, stove stacking, etc) while also identifying unique local challenges. This comparative approach strengthens the evidence base for understanding CF-CS adoption dynamics in African contexts and underscores the need for contextually tailored strategies to address these barriers and promote uptake.

## Limitations
Our study has several limitations. First, stakeholders’ views of CF-CS use were based on a demonstration conducted by the research team a few hours before their interviews, as none of the stakeholders had used the CF-CS previously. This was their first introduction to the technology. In addition, although the interview guides were pilot tested with the research team and community health workers familiar with the target communities, we were not able to pilot test them directly with members of the intended study population during the study period. As a result, some user-specific nuances may not have been fully captured. Future interviews will include stakeholders who have used the CF-CS for an extended period and can provide deeper insight into its utility.
Second, the purposive, geography-stratified selection of 32 peri-urban communities across all five divisions of Lagos was designed to capture contextual heterogeneity rather than statistical representativeness. This approach may introduce selection bias and limit generalisability, although we sought to mitigate this by partnering with divisional Medical Officers of Health and recruiting diverse stakeholder roles within each community. Additionally, while Lagos is cosmopolitan and varies between urban, peri-urban, and rural designations, the generalisability of these findings to other contexts and populations within and outside Nigeria is limited. Most of the identified contextual factors that influence stakeholders’ uptake of an intervention may only exist within the implementation climate of the intervention and are thus not generalisable.
Third, the number of individuals approached who declined or did not respond after accepting participation was not systematically recorded, which limits the ability to assess selection bias and recruitment feasibility.
Fourth, member checking of transcripts or preliminary findings was not conducted, which may limit confirmability. Mitigation strategies included team-based coding, triangulation across data sources, maintenance of a documented audit trail, discrepant case analysis, and regular analytic debriefings. Emerging interpretations were also vetted with community health workers and members of the research team familiar with the study communities to support validation and contextualisation. Nonetheless, the absence of direct participant member checking remains a limitation.
Finally, it’s important to note the potential discrepancy between stated preference and revealed preference. Although stakeholders may have expressed intent and enthusiasm to purchase and use CF-CS during interviews, their actual behaviour once the CF-CS implementation begins might not align with their verbal responses. This inconsistency will be explored in subsequent analyses following the completion of the CF-CS implementation trial.

## Implications
Implications of our study are that if the main barriers to CF-CS implementation are addressed, including ensuring the fixed and variable costs of CF-CS are within household budgets, and the bio-ethanol fuel is adequate for cooking needs, successful adoption of CF-CS is likely. However, cooking/eating is a basic need which would prioritise the use of less expensive, unhealthy fuel options to address short-term needs for daily living, offsetting any perceived benefits from transitioning to healthier fuel use. Therefore, the policy requires establishing distribution chains, fuel cost stability with subsidies, and production infrastructure for CF-CS to be adopted. Additionally, mistrust of CF-CS can be addressed through community-driven marketing by local leaders and early adopters, which could help foster wider acceptance of the technology. However, if the equipment is faulty, fuel prices are too high, or fuel is unavailable, community trust could quickly be undermined. Finally, stove stacking is a protective strategy for persons to satisfy daily household needs for cooking in an unstable fuel economy, and often health benefits are ignored to satisfy short-term needs for daily living, which ultimately will influence CF-CS adoption.
Results from this study informed a community mobilisation strategy designed to enhance the adoption and sustainability of CF-CS use by building community readiness, mitigating social and structural barriers, and promoting healthy behaviours to achieve improved health outcomes. Adopting CF-CS would ultimately reduce HAP and improve cardiovascular health outcomes in these vulnerable populations.
Future research should go beyond brief demonstrations to evaluate real-world sustained use and health-relevant outcomes over time, using longitudinal cohorts or pragmatic trials that test affordability strategies, fuel supply reliability, user support, and after-sales service. Studies should incorporate objective measures of use and exposure where feasible, such as sensor-confirmed stove use and personal particulate matter 2.5 micrometers (PM2.5) or carbon monoxide monitoring, alongside household surveys. Implementation evaluations should report adoption, fidelity, reach, cost, and equity impacts and compare ethanol with other clean fuels under local cooking conditions. At the policy level, partnerships with health and energy agencies should be tested to integrate clean cooking into routine primary care and community programmes, while cost-effectiveness should be modelled under realistic pricing and supply scenarios to guide scale-up. Building on this work, our team has begun a cluster randomised trial in Lagos to evaluate a community mobilisation strategy for clean cookstoves and clean fuels, registered at ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05048147 on 17 September 2021, with the protocol published in BMJ Open 2025.

## Conclusion
Stakeholders identified actionable barriers and enablers to CF-CS adoption, including trust in implementers, affordability, and reliable fuel supply. Programmes in peri-urban Lagos should address these factors and align technologies with local cooking practices to reduce HAP exposure relevant to CVD risk. These insights informed the community mobilisation strategy for the CF-CS rollout in Lagos and can guide similar efforts in comparable settings.
