## ABSTRACT

## Aim
To describe interventions to mitigate weight bias among practicing healthcare providers and examine their effectiveness.

## Design
An integrative review.

## Methods
The framework of Whittemore and Knafl (2005) was utilised to identify and synthesise studies of weight bias interventions. Covidence reference management software facilitated screening using predetermined eligibility criteria that is, published in peer‐reviewed journals, reported in English, and described outcomes for weight bias mitigation interventions among healthcare providers with a professional practice licence. We evaluated study quality using Joanna Briggs Institute's Critical Appraisal Tools for qualitative and quantitative studies.

## Data Sources
Five databases were searched (CINAHL, Embase, EBSCOhost, APA PsycINFO, and Scopus) in July 2024.

## Results
Sixteen articles met the inclusion criteria. Four themes emerged: (1) active learning lessens weight bias more than passive learning, (2) multicomponent approaches yield better outcomes, (3) healthcare providers are willing to change their practices, and (4) explicit attitudes and beliefs are more amenable to change than implicit ones. Four sub‐themes within theme one included the use of in‐person workgroups; films, computer‐based, and podcast delivery; written modules; and physical characteristic modifications, such as the use of an adiposity empathy suit. Intervention designs using a theory‐based, active learning approach with repeated sessions were most successful at decreasing negative weight‐biased attitudes and beliefs.

## Conclusions
Interventions that effectively reduce weight bias among practicing healthcare providers vary, yet evidence suggests that weight bias mitigation is achievable. These interventions offer strategies to improve patient‐centred care among patients living with obesity.

## Impact
Healthcare providers often display weight bias. This review synthesises information on effective interventions for reducing bias, which may limit the subsequent negative patient outcomes associated with it.

## Reporting Method
The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses was the structure used for this paper.

## Patient or Public Contribution
There were no patient or public contributions.

## Quality Appraisal
The first author evaluated the studies using the Joanna Briggs Institute's (JBI) Manual for Evidence Synthesis. The questions in the tools provide a comprehensive evaluation method to determine if the selected studies are methodologically rigorous (Aromataris and Munn  ). Studies that were selected for review were either qualitative or quantitative; critical appraisal checklists are devised to understand the thoroughness associated with each category. The studies were then rated on their designs. According to JBI, a study's dependability should be assessed for academic and reporting rigour. Based on the number of positively answered questions, the studies were then ranked as high, medium, low, or very low‐quality. After answering the JBI questions, thirteen studies were rated as high quality; three studies were rated as moderate quality. See Table  for ratings.

## Data Abstraction and Synthesis
The matrix table (Table  ) shows the data extracted from each article, including authors, year, country, study purpose, design, sample, results/effectiveness and JBI quality assessment. After building the matrix table, the first (GSM) and second (MTT) authors identified patterns in the findings and performed a thematic analysis similar to the qualitative analysis methods of nurse‐anthropologist, Leininger (Leininger  ): (1) collecting, describing, and documenting the data, (2) identifying and categorising descriptors, (3) performing patterning and contextual analysis, and (4) identifying primary themes and recommendations (Wehbe‐Alamah and McFarland  ). Analysis was conducted with attention to the aims of the study: to describe the weight bias mitigation approaches and discuss their effectiveness among practicing healthcare providers.

## Results
The 16 included studies were published between 2011 and 2024; 12 were quantitative (n = 3 RCT; n = 5 prospective single cohort; n = 4 prospective multiple cohort), and four were qualitative. See Table  . All but one study provided detailed participants' demographics (Rincon‐Subtirelu  ). Three studies utilised a theory‐based intervention (Blackburn and Stathi  ; Setchell et al.  ; Wijayatunga et al.  ). Sample sizes ranged from N = 7 to 506. Most studies included participants from medicine (n = 6), nursing (n = 5), or physiotherapy (n = 4); dietetics was represented in one study, and six studies included more than one type of healthcare provider. Moreover, the studies included multiple healthcare specialties and settings, that is, general medicine, family medicine, public health practices, suburban community hospitals, and large urban academic medical centres within several countries. Seven countries and four continents were represented in the review, providing a broader perspective on weight bias intervention. Six studies were from the United States, four studies were from Canada, two studies were from Australia, and one study each was from Israel, New Zealand, Switzerland, and the United Kingdom. The studies were mostly short‐term, that is, 4 weeks to 12 months.
3.1

## Weight Bias Intervention Approaches and Their Effectiveness
Results from aim one and two are reported concurrently via themes and sub‐themes. Workshops and in‐person group discussions (Baillargeon et al.  ; Berman and Hegel  ; Blackburn and Stathi  ; FitzGerald et al.  ; Gujral et al.  ; McVey et al.  ), watching videos (Blackburn and Stathi  ; FitzGerald et al.  ; Koran‐Scholl et al.  ; Wijayatunga et al.  ), reading self‐directed computer‐based learning modules (Allison et al.  ; FitzGerald et al.  ; Gujral et al.  ), wearing an adiposity empathy suit (Hales et al.  ), and listening to podcasts and participating in group discussions (Heidebrecht et al.  ) comprised the different approaches used to mitigate weight bias. Effectiveness was determined by whether the intervention successfully improved weight bias attitudes and beliefs. Several studies successfully modified either weight bias attitudes or beliefs but reported challenges to changing both (Allison et al.  ; FitzGerald et al.  ; Gujral et al.  ; Rincon‐Subtirelu  ).
Four themes were identified through the analysis phase: (1) active learning improves weight bias more than passive learning, (2) multicomponent approaches yield better outcomes, (3) healthcare providers are willing to change their practices, and (4) explicit attitudes and beliefs are more amenable to change than implicit ones. Four sub‐themes relating to the specific strategies used were also identified, including (a) in‐person workgroups; (b) films, computer‐based formats, and podcast delivery; (c) written modules and (d) physical characteristic modifications. These four subthemes are discussed under Theme 1 and provide specific examples of the active approaches that were more influential at mitigating weight bias.

3.2

## Theme 1: Active Learning Improves Weight Bias More Than Passive Learning
3.2.1

## In‐Person Workshops
In‐person workshops allowed for integration of multiple formats of weight bias interventions and were used by a majority of the studies (n = 11). Moderate‐sized, face‐to‐face group meetings were reported (Baillargeon et al.  ; Berman and Hegel  ; Blackburn and Stathi  ; FitzGerald et al.  ; Gujral et al.  ; McVey et al.  ), as well as smaller, more intimate groups (Hales et al.  ; Heidebrecht et al.  ; Rincon‐Subtirelu  ; Setchell et al.  ). Studies integrating in‐person workshops with cognitive (thinking), affective (feelings), or psychomotor (skill development) learning domains and active learning approaches were more successful in reducing weight bias attitudes and beliefs (Baillargeon et al.  ; Blackburn and Stathi  ; Hales et al.  ; Heidebrecht et al.  ; McVey et al.  ; Rincon‐Subtirelu  ; Setchell et al.  ) compared to more passive approaches (Falker and Sledge  ; Gujral et al.  ). Incorporating all three learning domains in discussion forums and interactive webinars at a two‐day collaborative experience resulted in significant improvements in weight bias attitudes of physicians that were retained 1‐year post‐intervention (Baillargeon et al.  ). Focusing on the cognitive and affective domains during discussion forums with 106 physicians resulted in over 75% of the participants correctly describing multiple effects of weight bias on patient outcomes; this approach also showed a reduced tendency to hold patients responsible for their weight, and significant benefits from a single workshop were reported (Berman and Hegel  ). Prior to an in‐person meeting, listening to podcasts that examined the complexities of living with obesity, the effects of bias experiences, and the subsequent health detriments improved weight bias attitudes among practicing clinicians at a large hospital in urban Canada (Heidebrecht et al.  ).
Among physiotherapists, positive effects of weight bias interventions with in‐person workshops were generally noted. Physiotherapists reported changes to clinical practice and improved attitudes toward people living with obesity; they also better understood their weight bias tendencies when they met in small groups (Setchell et al.  ). However, during a one‐day seminar with large, in‐person workgroups compared to an online intervention, in‐person participants' weight bias beliefs improved somewhat, but their negative weight bias attitudes worsened (Jones and Forhan  ). Discussions at the seminar may have inadvertently introduced stigmatisation and bias when physiotherapists focused on the practical aspects of bedside care for people living with obesity (Jones and Forhan  ). Overall, using an in‐person workshop effectively reduced weight bias attitudes and beliefs in several types of healthcare providers, especially when a multi‐component, active engagement approach was used.

3.3

## Films, Computer‐Based, and Podcast Delivery
Films, computer‐based, and podcast weight bias interventions were delivered with in‐person workgroups or online using computer‐based learning (CBL) platforms. Trigger films (i.e., short 2 to 4‐min videos meant to elicit complex feelings), vignettes, and videos were used in six studies (Allison et al.  ; Blackburn and Stathi  ; FitzGerald et al.  ; Koran‐Scholl et al.  ; Sherf‐Dagan et al.  ; Wijayatunga et al.  ) and showed mixed effectiveness. While some showed only a partial reduction in weight bias attitudes, that is, in two subscales of the tool (Sherf‐Dagan et al.  ), others reduced negative weight bias attitudes and beliefs when films were added to interactive, qualitative discussions (Blackburn and Stathi  ). Using films, CBL platforms, and podcasts increased convenience, and a larger number of specialties and participants could take part in the intervention (Allison et al.  ; Falker and Sledge  ; FitzGerald et al.  ; Gujral et al.  ; Heidebrecht et al.  ; Koran‐Scholl et al.  ; Sherf‐Dagan et al.  ; Wijayatunga et al.  ).
Trigger films were used to explore simulated physician‐patient interactions (Blackburn and Stathi  ; Koran‐Scholl et al.  ) and to examine healthcare providers' feelings toward people living with obesity; vignettes emphasised the challenges people living with obesity experienced with their obesity diagnosis (FitzGerald et al.  ). Although general practitioners had conflicting thoughts about the care of people living with obesity, they better understood how to approach patient discussions after an intervention with a discursive framework and trigger films (Blackburn and Stathi  ). Despite carefully crafting and delivering trigger films and videos, these methods did not always reduce negative weight bias attitudes and beliefs (FitzGerald et al.  ). Similarly, strictly watching a video without active engagement and discussion resulted in no change in attitudes and beliefs (Wijayatunga et al.  ).
Nonetheless, some asynchronous methods positively changed attitudes with repeated interventions that used short, interactive videos without group discussions (Allison et al.  ; Koran‐Scholl et al.  ). Completing various learning modules over 6 weeks resulted in a positive change in weight‐biased attitudes among physiotherapists (Allison et al.  ). Using seven podcasts to convey the lived experiences of weight bias provided participants a strong knowledge base for in‐person discussions that led to strong emotional reactions, self‐reflections, and a desire to change practices (Heidebrecht et al.  ). Although using videos and vignettes alone was not as successful as using videos with interactive discussions, successful outcomes occurred without discussions when repeated intervention sessions were incorporated.

3.4

## Written Modules
Two studies delivered their weight mitigation efforts through CBL platforms (Falker and Sledge  ; Gujral et al.  ) and found mixed results, particularly with maintaining positive changes in weight bias beliefs over time (Gujral et al.  ). Bariatric sensitivity training modules highlighted weight bias attitudes, obesity knowledge, and the likenesses rather than the differences between people living with and those living without obesity (Falker and Sledge  ). Additionally, when comparing an institution with bariatric sensitivity training modules already in place to one that did not have these types of training modules, weight bias attitudes were improved in institutions with bariatric sensitivity training modules, but negative weight bias beliefs persisted (Gujral et al.  ). Although no significant changes occurred in participants' awareness of weight bias and negative stereotypes when using written formats in two studies, anecdotal evidence from discussion throughout an institution and at the bedside suggested stereotyping declined and the use of more inclusive terminology occurred (Falker and Sledge  ; Gujral et al.  ). The results from using these written modules indicate that increased knowledge about obesity is possible; however, no clear improvements were made in weight bias attitudes and beliefs.

3.5

## Physical Characteristic Modifications
One weight bias intervention used psychomotor learning with an adiposity empathy suit that modified participants' anthropomorphic measurements to align with those of people living with obesity (Hales et al.  ). Six nurses and one physiotherapist performed daily activities while wearing the suit for ~2 h (e.g., walking up and down stairs, tying shoelaces, shopping, and taking public transportation). Qualitative interviews revealed that participants positively changed their attitudes and beliefs, including being more empathetic and less critical of people living with obesity. Participants noted an improved understanding of the physical difficulties, social isolation, and withdrawn feelings experienced by people living with obesity, which was a unique perspective for these healthcare providers (Hales et al.  ).

3.6

## Theme 2: Multiple Approaches Provided Better Outcomes
Overall, studies that used a multi‐component approach, for example, discussion forums and interactive webinars (Baillargeon et al.  ); videos and discourse analysis (Blackburn and Stathi  ); lectures and discussion groups (Jones and Forhan  ; McVey et al.  ); lectures, reading materials, videos, and group discussions (Rincon‐Subtirelu  ); short, self‐guided videos, case studies, and reflection methods (Allison et al.  ; Heidebrecht et al.  ; Koran‐Scholl et al.  ); and podcasts followed by discussion (Heidebrecht et al.  ) showed an improvement in weight bias attitudes and beliefs, with one exception (Jones and Forhan  ). Of the studies that used a single‐approach intervention, reduced weight bias attitudes and beliefs were reported qualitatively, and outcomes occurred in relatively small groups (Blackburn and Stathi  ; Hales et al.  ; Setchell et al.  ), limiting generalisability. Several studies demonstrating effectiveness incorporated discussion groups in one session (Blackburn and Stathi  ; McVey et al.  ) or in multiple sessions (Baillargeon et al.  ; Rincon‐Subtirelu  ). Using a multi‐faceted approach in multiple sessions (Baillargeon et al.  ) or in one session (Berman and Hegel  ) decreased negative weight bias attitudes and beliefs in physicians, but not in nurses even in repeated sessions (Baillargeon et al.  ; Koran‐Scholl et al.  ). However, a multi‐component approach in nurses who provide direct hands‐on care to people living with obesity resulted in weight bias improvements when psychomotor learning was used (Hales et al.  ).

3.7

## Theme 3: Healthcare Providers Are Willing to Change
Modifying healthcare providers' practices to include a more patient‐centered approach is essential for developing empathetic patient‐provider relationships (Pearl et al.  ). Healthcare providers can be more supportive of their patients when understanding how weight bias impacts overall health of the patient and taking time to reflect on their personal bias. An essential outcome of approaches to mitigate weight bias is that healthcare providers improve their practices when interacting with people living with obesity. A majority of included studies evaluated the likelihood of their participants to modify their empathetic and supportive practices (Baillargeon et al.  ; Berman and Hegel  ; Falker and Sledge  ; FitzGerald et al.  ; Gujral et al.  ; Hales et al.  ; Heidebrecht et al.  ; McVey et al.  ; Setchell et al.  ). Additionally, the confidence needed to change healthcare providers' attitudes and beliefs toward people living with obesity was an important component of the discussion (Allison et al.  ; Baillargeon et al.  ; Berman and Hegel  ; Gujral et al.  ; Hales et al.  ; Koran‐Scholl et al.  ). Although many participants reported an understanding of weight bias, they were challenged by the complexity of discussing weight during patient interactions (Baillargeon et al.  ; Setchell et al.  ). Learning about weight bias and the best approach to patient encounters increased participants' confidence in addressing obesity (Baillargeon et al.  ; Koran‐Scholl et al.  ; Setchell et al.  ). Interestingly, physicians, but not nurses, reported increased confidence in changing practices when discussion forums, interactive webinars, and collaborative sessions were used (Baillargeon et al.  ). Yet, when an innovative approach with an adiposity empathy suit incorporated empathetic experiences with discussion groups, nurses were more thoughtful about their practices toward people living with obesity (Hales et al.  ). Intriguingly, when podcasts were used prior to meeting in‐person, all 20 participants showed a more empathetic approach to caring for people living with obesity (Heidebrecht et al.  ).
Group interactions positively impacted practice changes that led to greater inclusivity (Berman and Hegel  ; McVey et al.  ) and increased participants' desires for more intervention sessions with role‐play and ways to change behaviours (Heidebrecht et al.  ; McVey et al.  ). With an increased sensitivity to patients' body images (Hales et al.  ; Setchell et al.  ) and a deeper understanding of themselves (Setchell et al.  ), participants were more inclined to modify their approaches and ensure patient experiences were positive. Overall, healthcare providers strived for patient‐centred care and focused on the needs of patients living with obesity more when healthcare providers understood their challenges.

3.8

## Theme 4: Explicit Attitudes and Beliefs Are More Amenable Than Implicit Ones
Explicit attitudes and beliefs improved more than implicit ones, but these outcomes were not consistent. Targeting explicit attitudes and beliefs through detailed presentations and interactive discussions led to participants gaining weight bias knowledge, examining their biases, and increasing their willingness to modify bedside practices (Allison et al.  ; Berman and Hegel  ; McVey et al.  ). Semi‐structured interviews measuring explicit weight bias attitudes revealed reduced weight bias attitudes and beliefs (Hales et al.  ; Setchell et al.  ). Yet, studies failed to change explicit weight bias attitudes in healthcare providers with self‐directed computer‐based learning approaches only (Sherf‐Dagan et al.  ) or when using a single‐event video‐based approach (Wijayatunga et al.  ).
Implicit weight bias attitudes are subconscious (Rubino et al.  ) and were less responsive to interventions. FitzGerald et al. ( ) discovered worse implicit weight bias attitudes toward people living with obesity when compared to attitudes toward patients with mental illness; in fact, participants were more interested in changing their bedside responses to patients with mental illness than people living with obesity. Dietitians did not change implicit weight bias attitudes after a single‐video approach (Wijayatunga et al.  ). However, repeated intervention sessions improved implicit attitudes, on average, among paediatric healthcare providers; those who reported obesity bias post‐intervention said this was likely due to their personal history of obesity or their frustration when patients were not successful with weight loss (Rincon‐Subtirelu  ).

## Discussion
This integrative review is the first synthesis of the literature to describe weight bias mitigation interventions specifically for practicing healthcare professionals, who are key to ensuring patient‐centered care among people living with obesity. Various weight bias mitigation interventions—in‐person workshops, video platforms, written modules, podcasts, and physical appearance changes—have been used to improve weight bias attitudes and beliefs. These four subthemes were described under Theme One, which highlights how active learning approaches improve weight bias more strongly than passive learning approaches. Yet, how the information was delivered was important. When a discussion was included, the videos' content positively improved weight bias attitudes and beliefs, as was reflected in the second subtheme under Theme One. Workshops appear efficient, although the logistics of reaching large numbers of participants may be challenging. The reviewed evidence suggests that implicit attitudes and beliefs are more difficult to change than explicit attitudes and beliefs, as described under Theme Four. Moreover, the degree and duration of the resulting weight bias reduction are not well understood.
As Theme Two explained, the benefits of multi‐component approaches that included trigger films, vignettes, video‐based learning, and podcast strategies show effectiveness in student groups and have the potential to reach larger audiences. Trigger films were an effective method to educate nursing students when coupled with adequate debriefing (Molloy et al.  ). Group activities and films improved weight bias beliefs in kinesiology students (Wijayatunga et al.  ) and medical students (Persky and Eccleston  ; Poustchi et al.  ), which corresponded with our findings, as described in the first subtheme under Theme One and in Theme Two. Yet, randomised controlled trials using video learning for doctors in training, nurses, nutritionists, and dietitian students yielded mixed results (Swift et al.  ). Others have not found any changes in weight bias stereotyping of physicians, medical students, nurses, or nursing students when using trigger films (Nickel et al.  ). Podcasts are an innovative way to discuss multiple topics, provide an asynchronous approach, and show promise in educating healthcare providers (Newman et al.  ). These data suggest that more research is needed to determine the best technology‐based approach for reducing weight bias.
As we highlight under the fourth subtheme, physically modifying the participant's appearance with an adiposity empathy suit effectively increased understanding of the patient's physical and social experiences, positively modified attitudes, and increased understanding of social isolation and withdrawal (Hales et al.  ). However, this strategy has significant limitations. Using an adiposity empathy suit is neither practical nor possible for larger numbers of participants, even though healthcare providers were willing to change their practices after simulated experiences, as Theme Three indicates. Empathy suits have been implemented in similar research in evaluating attitudes toward aging, with mixed results (Akpınar Söylemez et al.  ; Lavallière et al.  ; Mandegari Bamakan et al.  ).
Of the 40 different weight bias measurement tools that have been published (Lacroix et al.  ), 8 were used to determine intervention effectiveness; no dominant tool was identified. Tools were generally designed to focus on attitudes and/or beliefs. Attitudes are feelings or emotions described as affective in nature, while beliefs are cognitively‐based; something that is accepted, considered to be true, or held as an opinion (Fishbein and Raven  ). The distinction between these two outcome variables is an important consideration when evaluating weight bias interventions because intervention effectiveness may differ based on the variables.
As described in Theme Four, changing implicit weight bias attitudes and beliefs with interventions may pose a more significant challenge than improving explicit weight bias attitudes and beliefs (Baillargeon et al.  ; McVey et al.  ). Shifting implicit bias is a challenge in discussions of racial disparity (Hagiwara et al.  ; Saluja and Bryant  ), infectious disease roundtables (Andrasik et al.  ), and culturally‐focused dialogues about patient care (Chapman et al.  ). Moreover, implicit bias is more difficult to recognise in oneself (Shah and Bohlen  ). Improving ingrained, implicit bias likely requires more in‐depth, consistent, and repeated interventions (FitzGerald and Hurst  ; Nadolsky et al.  ).
Curricula with comprehensive content on understanding obesity aetiology, effective communication between patients and providers, and discussions of lifestyle modifications may be beneficial in reducing overall weight bias in healthcare providers (Auckburally et al.  ; Swift et al.  ). Inadequate training and the lack of knowledge about obesity can result in negative interactions between various healthcare providers and people living with obesity (Auckburally et al.  ). Obesity education for trainees and practicing healthcare providers is gaining prominence, and calls are growing for training programme modifications (Allnutt et al.  ; Arvelo‐Rodríguez et al.  ). Trainees may enter into practice with preformed ideas about people living with obesity and require more intensive efforts to change these ideas (Puhl et al.  ).
4.1

## Practice Implications
Professionals who are responsible for the ongoing training of healthcare providers must have a comprehensive understanding of effective approaches that support patient‐centered care (Wong et al.  ). This review synthesises effective weight bias interventions for hospital‐based educators, providing evidence‐based strategies to develop and evaluate weight bias reduction activities. While the interventions themselves are crucial, employing valid assessment strategies is frequently highlighted as essential for continuous professional development (Marceau et al.  ). The approaches outlined here serve as a framework for further professional programme development in this area.

4.2

## Research Implications
This review synthesised data and identified gaps on the most effective approaches for designing weight bias reduction interventions among practising healthcare professionals. Only three studies used a randomised controlled trial design (Allison et al.  ; Sherf‐Dagan et al.  ; Wijayatunga et al.  ), and three studies included theory‐based interventions (Blackburn and Stathi  ; Setchell et al.  ; Wijayatunga et al.  ). Future research should incorporate these rigorous methods with larger samples, more diverse healthcare providers, and engagement with professional organisations. Six studies included nurses, and interventions were inconsistently effective. Interventions must be further tested for their ability to reduce weight bias among nurses with whom patients spend the most time. Moreover, because only three studies incorporated a theoretical framework, theory‐guided interventions should also be examined. Although beyond the scope of this review, cultural distinctions and social determinants of health that may drive obesity development and affect healthcare providers interactions require additional research because people who are living with obesity are viewed differently in various cultures (Puhl et al.  ).
The impact of social determinants of health and different cultural perspectives on body weight is important to consider. Providing healthcare professionals with education about obesogenic settings, including how social factors such as neighbourhood safety, access to fresh food, and healthcare inequalities drive obesity, is essential (Williams et al.  ). Moreover, integrating diverse cultural perspectives on obesity can strengthen patient‐centered, culturally sensitive care by fostering greater respect and satisfaction in patient‐provider interactions (Tucker et al.  ). In addition, there is increasing interest in exploring the connections between global adiposity trends, climate change, and the broader carbon footprint (Koch et al.  ). Comprehensive weight bias education should incorporate all these critical elements.
Future research should test the best package of intervention components for weight bias mitigation in practicing healthcare providers suggested by this review—innovative video formats and group‐based, in‐person approaches where in‐depth discussions during repeated interactions can occur over time. Supporting nursing practices with a foundation in methods to mitigate weight bias and emphasise patient‐centred care will lead not only to improved bedside outcomes but may impact population‐based health as well.

4.3

## Strengths and Limitations
This is the first review focusing on practicing healthcare providers. We synthesised research from seven countries, which supports the generalisability of these findings; however, given our methods and inclusion criteria, we may have missed relevant global studies. Most studies in this integrative review were rated a high quality, and a health sciences librarian assisted with the search, which strengthens the rigour of our method. Despite using five databases to search for literature and the ancestry approach, some studies may have been missed, limiting the review's comprehensiveness. Evaluation of the study quality was conducted by the first author (GSM) only in order to expedite the process, increasing the possibility of a biased quality evaluation; however, two authors conducted the study screening, analysed the information, and developed themes.

5

## Conclusions
Interventions used to reduce weight bias among practicing healthcare providers are diverse, and effective approaches require a multi‐faceted approach. Available evidence suggests that improving weight bias attitudes and beliefs is achievable through active learning methods, such as group‐based, in‐person workshops. Additionally, implicit bias was more resistant to improvement than explicit bias. Importantly, most healthcare professionals were interested in and willing to change practices that reflected weight bias. Innovative approaches, such as wearing an adiposity empathy suit, using targeted podcasts, and delivering a combination of methods over time should be tested to determine their value in reducing weight bias among healthcare providers.
