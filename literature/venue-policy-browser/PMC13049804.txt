## Background
Maternal and infant mortality in Indiana remain above national averages, largely due to inequities in healthcare access. This study evaluates a pilot Pop Up Pregnancy & Family Village, a community-based, one-stop-shop model designed to improve maternal and infant health by integrating health and social services.

## Methods
A mixed-methods evaluation guided by the RE-AIM framework (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, Maintenance) incorporated demographic tracking, vendor service metrics, attendee surveys, focus group, and written feedback.

## Results
The Village reached high-burden zip codes, engaging pregnant individuals and families, though postpartum participation was lower. Vendors reported delivering 299 services, 147 referrals, and 37 enrolled in programs, indicating strong utilization. Attendees expressed satisfaction, trust in providers, and increased confidence in navigating resources. Community feedback highlighted the need for expanded postpartum support, culturally tailored foods, improved event logistics, and wider outreach.

## Conclusions
The Village’s success rested on trust-building, cultural responsiveness, and community ownership. Co-design with local stakeholders ensured services reflected the lived experiences of families facing health disparities. This pilot demonstrates the promise of community-driven interventions to transform care delivery in underserved settings. Future efforts should strengthen postpartum resources, broaden outreach, and improve operations to promote equitable engagement and sustained impact.

## Significance
Maternal and infant mortality rates remain disproportionately high in Indiana, reflecting persistent inequities in healthcare access, especially for underserved communities. Prior research highlights the benefits of community-based models in addressing social determinants of health, yet few interventions integrate health and social services in a single, trusted setting. This study adds to the literature by demonstrating the feasibility and impact of a co-designed, community-driven “one-stop-shop” model that fosters trust, cultural responsiveness, and meaningful engagement. Findings underscore the potential of Pop Up Pregnancy & Family Villages to reduce disparities and reimagine maternal and infant health care delivery in high-burden communities.

## Background
Maternal and infant mortality remain critical indicators of systemic health inequities. Globally, more than 700 women die each day from preventable pregnancy-related complications, roughly one every two minutes [ ]. The United States stands out among high-income countries for its disproportionately high maternal mortality ratio, driven by fragmented care, inadequate prenatal and postpartum coverage, and entrenched racial disparities. Non-Hispanic Black women face maternal mortality rates two to three times higher than non-Hispanic white women, reflecting systemic inequities, implicit bias, and broader socioeconomic barriers [ ].
Within the US, state-level insights provide further depth into these challenges. The 2024 Indiana Maternal Mortality Review Committee (MMRC) report documented 72 pregnancy-associated deaths occurring in 2022, corresponding to a pregnancy-associated mortality ratio of 100.1 per 100,000 live births. Additionally, 12 deaths (17.5 per 100,000 live births) were pregnancy-related, directly attributed to pregnancy complications or conditions exacerbated by pregnancy. The MMRC classified 83% of pregnancy-associated deaths as preventable, emphasizing substantial opportunities for intervention through improved healthcare delivery and policy enhancements [ ]. In addition to high maternal death rates, infant mortality rates in Indiana have consistently been higher than the US national average [ ]. St. Joseph County (SJC) is one of the counties with the highest infant mortality rates in the state. The Fetal Infant Mortality Review (FIMR) Program in SJC, Indiana, was established in 2015 and has been systematically reviewing cases of infant deaths and stillbirths since October 2016 to identify underlying risk factors and implement targeted community interventions aimed at improving maternal and infant health outcomes. Despite significant efforts, infant mortality rates in SJC remain notably higher than state and national averages, highlighting persistent health disparities influenced by race, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and access to healthcare services [ ].
To address these disparities, we piloted the Pop Up Pregnancy & Family Village (the Village) model in SJC, focusing on high-burden communities of South Bend and Mishawaka. Adapted from a successful San Francisco initiative [ , ], the Village offers holistic, community-centered care. Priority zip codes 46544, 46545, 46619, 46628, and 46615, representing half of county births and 61% of infant deaths, were targeted for intervention, reflecting concentrated need and the importance of equitable, locally informed strategies. While this paper focuses on evaluating the pilot Village events, the detailed processes of co-design, implementation, and partnership development are further elaborated in a companion paper focused on the creation and operationalization of the Pop Up Pregnancy & Family Village model.

## Planning overview
The Village was developed through a multi-year, community-engaged planning process conducted throughout 2023 and 2024, prior to its pilot launch. The design of the Village was intentionally informed by local maternal and infant health data, formative qualitative work with community members, and sustained stakeholder engagement across public health, healthcare systems, and community-based organizations.
Early planning was grounded in formative community engagement conducted through the SJC FIMR program and the Birth Equity & Justice Workgroup.

## Adaptation of an established model and local co-design
The Village was adapted from the San Francisco Pop Up Village model, a community-based initiative established in 2016 to address perinatal inequities through low-burden, anti-racist, and holistic service delivery in public spaces. The San Francisco model emphasizes cross-sector collaboration, culturally responsive programming, and community ownership, offering services that extend beyond clinical care to include wellness activities, arts and cultural engagement, support for expecting fathers, and access to healthy foods. The decision to implement the Village model in SJC was driven by converging empirical and community-based evidence as well as community partnership buy-in. While informed by this model, the SJC Village was not replicated wholesale; rather, it was co-designed to reflect the specific sociocultural, structural, and healthcare context of SJC. Beginning in December 2023, the study team convened regular planning meetings with a diverse group of stakeholders, including representatives from the county health department, healthcare systems, community-based organizations, social service agencies, and advocacy groups. While researchers from the San Francisco Pop Up Village were consulted on the SJC pilot, it was the local stakeholders that contributed to decisions regarding the Village’s goals, priority populations, service priorities, event logistics, and evaluation strategies. This iterative planning process emphasized shared decision-making and responsiveness to community-identified needs.

## Identification of core service areas and trust-building design
Through bi-monthly partner meetings and community input, six core service areas were identified, named, and defined for inclusion at each Village event: (1) Healthy Minds: Managing Stress & Building Support; (2) Health Insurance Assistance; (3) Community Connections & Resources; (4) Pregnancy & Family Health Education; (5) Your Healthcare Connections; and (6) Family Wellness, Fitness & Fun. These core service areas are directly mapped onto gaps identified through health cafés and FIMR data.

## Materials and methods

## Design and setting
This study used a mixed-methods approach to assess the feasibility and acceptability of the pilot Pop Up Pregnancy & Family Village (the Village) in St. Joseph County (SJC), Indiana, guided by the RE-AIM framework. The evaluation focused on early indicators of implementation viability rather than health or clinical outcomes and examined whether the Village could be successfully delivered in high-burden communities and perceived as accessible, useful, and trustworthy by participants and partners. Quantitative data assessed participant characteristics, geographic reach, service utilization, and participant-reported acceptability, accessibility, person-centered care, and trust, while qualitative data from focus group discussions, open-ended participant feedback, vendor surveys, and post-event planning meetings contextualized implementation processes, perceived value, and sustainability considerations across RE-AIM domains.
Two Village events were held in August and September 2024, with each event being held on a Saturday from 1 to 5 PM. The Village is comprised of six core service areas: (1) Healthy Minds: Managing Stress & Building Support; (2) Health Insurance Assistance; (3) Community Connections & Resources; (4) Pregnancy & Family Health Education; (5) Your Healthcare Connections, and (6) Family Wellness, Fitness & Fun. Each core service area had multiple community partners, known as vendors, providing services in each designated area. Services ranged from health insurance enrollment, talking with doctors in a group setting, blood pressure checks, mental health screenings, fitness classes, and various health education topics such as car seat safety, safe sleep, breastfeeding, labor advocacy, among many others. A general welcome table was at each location’s entrance, staffed by volunteers. Volunteers were also located throughout the venue and acted as wayfinders, offering general assistance. A Pop Up Pregnancy & Family Village guide was provided at the welcome table as a resource for families and community members to navigate various local and state resources (e.g. food pantries, transportation assistance, employment centers, etc.) as well as space to write notes under each core service area. While the resource guide supported participants’ navigation and engagement during the event, it was intended as a practical tool rather than a formal component of the study evaluation and was not used as an outcome measure in the analysis.
The two events were held in South Bend and Mishawaka, the two communities within our targeted zip codes. Mishawaka and South Bend, both situated within SJC, are communities experiencing notable health disparities characterized by significantly higher infant mortality rates compared to state and national averages [ , ]. Mishawaka contains zip codes 46544 and 46545, while South Bend encompasses zip codes 46619 and 46628.

## RE-AIM evaluation framework
The RE-AIM framework evaluates health interventions across Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance, emphasizing accessibility, adoption, and sustainability beyond clinical outcomes (Table  ) [ ]. In this study, Reach was measured through a check-in system using colored star stickers by zip code to track engagement of pregnant, postpartum, and family/other participants. Volunteers at the Welcome Table would have every person that entered the Village provide a star to their respective zip code (must have stayed at location at least 6 months) and were provided a meal ticket.
 Effectiveness was assessed using Vendor Data Forms documenting services, referrals, and enrollments. These forms were provided to every vendor at their table prior to the Village start time and were instructed to return them to the project manager at the end of each event. Services were defined as direct, on-site interactions or activities provided to participants during the event (e.g., health screenings, education sessions, counseling, fitness or wellness activities). Referrals were defined as facilitated linkages or recommendations made by vendors to external programs, providers, or resources for follow-up beyond the event. Enrollments were defined as instances in which participants completed sign-ups or were formally registered into programs, services, insurance plans, or care pathways during the Village.
Adoption was evaluated through vendor satisfaction surveys and a focus group discussion (FGD) with guests after the first event. Vendor surveys were sent after each event using University of Notre Dame Qualtrics and included questions related to communication, overall logistics and planning for each event, and asking for any additional feedback. Questions were intentionally kept anonymous and brief to improve completion rates. The FGD was held at the first Village event using a voice recorder to capture participants’ feedback. The FGD guide was semi-structured and informed by FIMR data to ensure the barriers that had been identified prior to the Village creation were getting addressed and was conducted by one of the researchers.
Implementation was examined using a Person-Centered Care (PCC) survey [ ] and participant feedback collected through graffiti walls highlighting successes, challenges, and gaps. The PCC was integrated into the attendee survey and included constructs such as respect, privacy, ability to understand information provided, among others. The Graffiti Wall included three large, brightly colored poster boards spread throughout the event to capture three questions: “What is working well?”, “What is missing?”, and “How can we improve?”. Attendees could answer these questions anonymously using multi-colored post-it notes and markers that were provided under each posterboard.
Maintenance was addressed through post-event planning meetings, where vendors and stakeholders discussed feasibility, partnership integration, and strategies to sustain and adapt the Pop Up Pregnancy & Family Village model. Meetings included researchers presenting preliminary results and seeking open feedback. For the meeting in August, after the first event, vendors were instructed to consider quick, realistic changes that could be made prior to the September event. During the following meeting, after both events had passed, vendors and stakeholders discussed more long-term goals.

## Data collection & analysis
To assess the adoption and implementation dimensions of the RE-AIM framework, a structured attendee survey was developed using a review of the literature, feedback from the planning committee, and adapted survey questions from the evaluation team at San Francisco (unpublished data). Attendee surveys were completed in a digital format using tablets and the University of Notre Dame Qualtrics Survey Software, and were available in English and Spanish. Informed consent was obtained from each participant at the start of the attendee survey in Qualtrics. Anyone who completed the attendee survey was provided a $15 gift card. Demographic variables collected included the type of visitor (currently pregnant, postpartum within 12 months or 24 months, family member, or other), zip code of residence, gender, age, race, education level, and insurance status. To evaluate the experiences of the visitors, questions were included on accessibility (convenience of location, ease of access), acceptability (usefulness of the Village and confidence in access to services), person-centered care (respect, privacy, feeling heard), and trust in the service providers. Four-point Likert scales (strongly agree, agree, disagree, strongly disagree) were used for the assessment of acceptability, person-centered care, and trust.
Characteristics of participants were compared between areas of residence (South Bend, IN, or Mishawaka/Other) using chi-square tests. Data from completed attendee surveys were analyzed using SPSS v29.
Further methods to assess the adoption dimension of the RE-AIM framework was a FGD that was conducted on-site. The FGD guide was designed based on existing literature and preliminary qualitative data, ensuring a structured approach to capturing community perceptions of healthcare accessibility and the program’s potential impact. Each attendee in the FGD was provided with a $15 gift card or diapers and consented prior to participation. To analyze the FGD, two researchers independently conducted a thematic analysis to assess the feasibility and acceptability of the Village [ ]. This method enabled the identification, interpretation, and synthesis of patterns within participant narratives, offering deeper insights into maternal healthcare access, social support networks, and systemic barriers. Following transcription of the audio-recorded FGD, two researchers independently conducted open coding, refining emergent themes through an iterative, collaborative process. Once thematic saturation was achieved, a finalized codebook was developed to codify themes and standardized definitions, enabling structured thematic categorization. Comparative analyses of independently derived themes reinforced inter-rater reliability, while cross-validation with diverse members of the research team, spanning program stakeholders and external qualitative researchers, further contextualized findings.

## Results

## Reach

## Color-coded stars
The demographic analysis, stratified by zip code, elucidated distinct patterns in community participation within targeted geographic locations, as shown in Table  . Primarily, individuals classified as Family/Other represented the predominant segment of the total participant population (n = 70 out of 101), suggesting broad community and familial engagement with the initiative. Pregnant participants constituted the next most substantial demographic group (n = 20), whereas the Postpartum cohort reflected comparatively limited representation (n = 11). This program intentionally concentrated on four specific zip codes: 46619, 46628, 46545, and 46544, due to their identification as priority areas. Among these, zip code 46619 exhibited particularly high participation (n = 18), characterized by equitable distribution across all demographic categories, indicative of effective outreach and successful community integration. Conversely, zip code 46628 demonstrated moderate engagement (n = 10), maintaining representation in each demographic category yet signaling potential for deeper programmatic penetration. Zip codes 46545 (n = 3) and 46544 (n = 9) exhibited notably lower participant numbers. Cumulatively, these findings highlighted the heterogeneity of participant representation across the targeted neighborhoods.
 

## Effectiveness

## Vendor data form
The number of vendors from the August Village event totaled 26, with seven from Community Connections & Resources, four from Family Wellness, Fitness, & Fun, four from Pregnancy & Family Health Education, four from Health Insurance Assistance, five from Your Healthcare Connections, and two from Healthy Minds. Similarly, the September event had 30 vendors with two additional vendors from both Pregnancy & Family Health Education and Health Insurance Assistance. The combined analysis of vendor data provided several key findings. Overall, there were a total of 299 services, 147 referrals, and 37 enrollments recorded across all vendors for both events (see Appendix ). The totals for the Vendor Form represent the number of times individuals received services, referrals, or enrollments across all vendors, meaning individuals may be counted multiple times if they accessed different services, received different referrals, or enrolled with multiple vendors. The vendors with the highest number of services received were those from the Family Fitness & Fun core service area, followed by Your Healthcare Connections. For referrals, the Healthy Minds core service area and Health Insurance Assistance were among the highest utilized. Regarding enrollments, the Community Connections & Resources core service area had significant activity. These findings suggest concentrated vendor usage patterns and opportunities to improve engagement and cross-vendor collaboration.

## Adoption

## Vendor survey
The vendor feedback survey for the first pilot Village held in August 2024 included a total of 11 participants out of 26 vendors. These vendors represented various core service areas, including Pregnancy & Family Health Education (3 participants), Family Wellness, Fitness, & Fun (3 participants), Community Connections & Resources (2 participants), Healthy Minds (2 participants), and Your Healthcare Connections (1 participant).
Vendor satisfaction ratings were generally positive across event components (n = 11). Event setup and the vendor checklist received the highest ratings, with the majority of vendors reporting being very satisfied (7/11 and 9/11, respectively). Pre-event communication, vendor sign-up, and event timing were rated favorably overall but skewed toward somewhat satisfied responses rather than very satisfied. Event promotion received the lowest ratings, with most vendors reporting somewhat satisfied (8/11), two reporting not satisfied at all, and only one reporting very satisfied. Feedback from the August vendor survey was used to inform operational adjustments for the subsequent pilot event. While a formal vendor survey was not administered in September, vendor feedback was collected and documented through structured discussion during the September planning meeting.

## Focus group discussion
The focus group discussion (FGD) involved nine participants representing various community segments, including pregnant individuals and family members. As indicated in Table  , participants’ ages ranged from mid-30s to early 40s, predominantly drawn from zip codes 46613, 46619, and 46628, highlighting targeted involvement from priority communities in St. Joseph County.
 Analysis of this discussion revealed five key themes and 14 subthemes. The five themes included (1) Beneficial Services, (2) Successful Reach of Priority Audience, (3) Targeted Promotion, (4) Resource/Service Gaps, and (5) Operation Feedback. Table  includes all of the corresponding subthemes and quotes for each theme and subtheme. Participants underscored Beneficial Services, notably appreciating access to health care providers, informative educational discussions on pregnancy and postpartum care, valuable guidance on insurance options, and designated entertainment areas for children. The theme of Successful Reach of Priority Audiences emerged, encompassing resources specifically beneficial to pregnant and postpartum women, inclusive spaces catering to children’s engagement, and supportive resources tailored explicitly to male partners, particularly addressing their mental health needs. The importance of Targeted Promotion surfaced clearly, with participants emphasizing the effectiveness of social media platforms such as Facebook and TikTok, local news media, and significant reliance on word-of-mouth recommendations. Nevertheless, participants identified critical Resource and Service Gaps, expressing notable concern over the absence of alternative support providers like midwives and doulas, essential community services such as electricity assistance and smoke detectors, and broader medical services, including diabetes screenings and immunizations. Finally, practical Operational Feedback was offered, recommending venues that are more capacious to accommodate privacy and accessibility concerns, more culturally sensitive and pregnancy-appropriate food options, and affirming the utility of the Pop Up Pregnancy & Family Village guide as a valuable navigational resource.
 

## Attendee survey
A total of 40 attendees participated in the attendee survey. Attendees were divided into two groups based on location of residence: South Bend (n = 25) and Other (n = 15). Participants from Mishawaka were included in the Other group due to the small sample size (n = 5). Characteristics of participants for the attendee survey are shown in Table  . Overall, 40% of attendees were currently pregnant or had given birth within the last 12 months, 72.5% were female, 35% were Black or African American, 50% had graduated from college or higher, and 42.5% had private or employer health insurance. Statistically significant differences existed in race/ethnicity and insurance status based on the location of residence. Participants from South Bend were more likely to be Black or African American and use Medical/Medicaid as their primary insurance.
 Acceptability was one of the subset scales within the attendee survey given to participants at the 2024 Pop Up Pregnancy & Family Village, shown in Table  . There were no statistically significant differences observed; however, there was an overwhelming response of acceptability from the total participants. At least 82.5% of the participants responded Strongly Agree or Agree for each of the questions on this portion of the attendee survey, demonstrating a high level of acceptability for the Village.

## Graffiti wall
Feedback gathered from attendees through the Graffiti Walls at the Village ranged from 2 to 12 comments being provided under each posted question at each event and indicated positive experiences, clear areas for improvement, and specific requests for additional support. Attendees appreciated the friendly, helpful nature of volunteers and vendors, the availability of health screenings, and the comprehensive panoply of resources provided, particularly highlighting informative discussions with healthcare providers and the welcoming atmosphere. However, they noted several gaps, including the need for more family-centered amenities, increased participation of dads, additional parent support groups, and practical assistance with transportation, finances, and offering vaccinations. Attendees at the Mishawaka location specifically emphasized the lack of doulas, scheduling during the University of Notre Dame football game weekends, and Lamaze classes.

## Maintenance
Vendor feedback from the planning meeting after the two pilot events highlighted several key insights and recommendations. Vendors agreed that the Village model was feasible based on the pilot’s success, emphasizing the value of relationship-building among vendors and deeper community connections. Recommendations included adjusting event hours, preferably to morning slots, to accommodate family schedules, particularly children’s nap times. They suggested enhancing attendance by including doctors or healthcare professionals to attract more participants. The creation of a master schedule to avoid competition between classes was proposed. Vendors emphasized improved promotion strategies, such as involving community influencers (providers, lactation specialists, doulas) and showcasing testimonials from participating mothers through interviews, photography, and videography. They noted challenges with vendor contracts and attendance justifications, suggesting collaborations with existing community events. Additionally, vendors recommended strategically selecting venues near subsidized housing or near bus lines, and varying locations and times to engage diverse communities more effectively.

## Discussion
The Pop Up Pregnancy & Family Village in St. Joseph County, Indiana, is a feasible and acceptable community-engaged, effective intervention to provide families with access to services and resources that could support maternal and infant health care delivery. This study demonstrates the Village’s potential as a community-based model for addressing gaps in health care delivery and social drivers of health associated with maternal and infant health disparities in St. Joseph County, Indiana. While pop-up and mobile service delivery models are not novel, this study demonstrates the Village’s unique contribution as an intentionally integrated, maternal and child health focused model that builds upon and complements existing health and social service delivery systems rather than operating as a stand-alone or emergency response mechanism. By embedding services within trusted community spaces and coordinating cross-sector partners, the Village advances prior pop-up models toward a more sustained, systems-oriented approach to maternal and infant health equity.
Our findings align closely with evidence from San Francisco’s Pop Up Village model, which demonstrated that trust-building, cross-sector collaboration, and culturally responsive design are central to successfully engaging underserved pregnant and parenting populations [ ] Strong participation from priority zip codes, particularly 46619 and 46628, underscores the effectiveness of locally tailored outreach and venue selection, reinforcing the importance of situating services within communities bearing the greatest burden of maternal and infant mortality. These findings parallel broader evidence from community-driven maternal health interventions in low-resource settings, including Ghana, where locally tailored, community-based maternal health packages have demonstrated improvements in service utilization among underserved populations [ ].
Findings from child health pop-up clinics in South Queensland, Australia further contextualize the Village’s success by demonstrating that pop-up models can meaningfully increase access to preventive and developmental services for families experiencing high vulnerability, particularly when clinics reduce logistical barriers related to transportation, scheduling, and cost [ , ] These studies emphasize that flexibility, geographic proximity, and service bundling are key mechanisms through which pop-up models improve reach, mechanisms similarly reflected in the Village’s ability to connect families to clinical, social, and educational supports in a single encounter. Unlike many child or general preventative medicine focused pop-up models, however, the Pop Up Pregnancy & Family Village integrates maternal health, infant care, and broader family-centered services across the perinatal continuum, extending the utility of pop-up design beyond episodic pediatric outreach.
Insights from pop-up COVID-19 testing initiatives further reinforce the importance of community engagement, relational trust, and unconventional models of healthcare delivery in achieving uptake among historically underserved populations. The Final Inch study in Ohio illustrates that the effectiveness of pop-up service delivery hinges less on the novelty of the model itself and more on the presence of trusted messengers, culturally responsive communication strategies, and responsiveness to community-identified needs [ ] These principles were echoed in participant feedback from the Village, particularly regarding the desire for alternative maternity providers such as doulas and midwives, as well as expanded access to diabetes screening, immunizations, and other supportive services.
Participant feedback consistently reinforced the importance of culturally responsive and comprehensive care, identifying critical gaps such as the limited availability of alternative maternity providers like doulas and midwives, as well as broader community and medical services, including diabetes screening and immunizations. Opara et al [ ]. emphasized that culturally informed approaches substantially enhance maternal healthcare utilization, reinforcing the necessity of tailoring interventions to the nuanced cultural, socioeconomic, and health-specific contexts of targeted populations. Operational feedback further emphasized practical yet essential aspects of program implementation, such as the strategic choice of venues and culturally appropriate food offerings. Nijagal et al [ ]. similarly identified logistical considerations as essential to successfully addressing participant comfort, privacy, and overall program effectiveness. Integrating this logistical awareness into future iterations of the Pop Up Pregnancy & Family Village model will likely enhance participant experiences, thereby sustaining and potentially expanding community participation.
Moreover, the use of the RE-AIM framework was instrumental in systematically evaluating the Village’s impact across multiple dimensions critical for sustainable public health interventions. RE-AIM provided a structured mechanism to assess the accessibility, stakeholder adoption, implementation fidelity, and long-term sustainability potential of the program [ ] The structured evaluation approach highlighted critical areas for refinement, such as enhancing postpartum participation, diversifying service offerings, and strengthening promotional efforts, all of which are essential for the continued evolution and improvement of community-based maternal health programs.
Despite promising data, our study had several limitations. The evaluation involved a relatively short timeframe, had a limited sample size for the attendee survey, and only one site was able to host a focus group discussion, restricting generalizability and assessment of longitudinal impacts. Future research should incorporate extended evaluation periods, larger and more diverse participant cohorts, and longitudinal analyses to robustly assess long-term behavioral and health outcomes.

## Conclusion
The Pop Up Pregnancy & Family Village demonstrates considerable potential as a feasible and acceptable model for addressing gaps in healthcare delivery through integrated, community-engaged care. This evaluation highlights critical areas of success, including community engagement, cultural sensitivity, and comprehensive service delivery, while simultaneously identifying specific opportunities for improvement in outreach, logistical planning, and service expansion. Continuous refinement informed by community feedback, systematic implementation of culturally responsive practices, and strengthened operational logistics are necessary steps toward enhancing maternal and child health equity in St. Joseph County and beyond.
