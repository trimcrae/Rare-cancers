## Abstract

## Background
Pacific Peoples face increasing rates of metabolic diseases, yet interventions for healthy dietary change are rarely focused on Pacific communities or centered around the cultural values and socio-cultural environment of Pacific Peoples.

## Objective
In this scoping review we aimed to systematically identify enablers and challenges of dietary change for Pacific Peoples in Aotearoa, New Zealand (AoNZ), and Australia.

## Data Sources
Medline (OVID), Embase (OVID), Scopus, Indigenous health (informIT), CINAHL (EBSCO), NZ Research, and Web of Science databases were searched from inception until July 2021.

## Data Extraction
We performed this review according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) Checklist, Joanna Briggs Institute methodology. Of 10 963 articles identified, 19 were selected for review. Eight articles focused solely on Pacific Peoples and 11 included Pacific Peoples alongside other groups. Seventeen articles reported consultations with Pacific communities, utilized Pacific research methodology, or included authors of Pacific heritage.

## Data Synthesis
The Pacific Health model “Kato Polopola” (food basket) was utilized to synthesize the findings. This model includes 12 domains reflecting values commonly shared across Pacific Island cultures: family, community, church, and concepts, impacting the relationship between Pacific Peoples and food.

## Results
Key enablers to engaging in positive dietary change centered on cultural values of Pacific communities: inclusion of the whole family; use of a holistic approach to physical and spiritual health; the significance of food in preserving cultural traditions; and co-design and facilitation performed with the Pacific Community. Crucially, the main challenges identified were primarily related to the social determinants of health and well-being.

## Conclusion
Despite alarming health outcomes for Pacific Peoples living in AoNZ, this scoping review highlights the paucity of research addressing healthy dietary change for Pacific communities. Pacific-led research, based on Pacific values, is essential to promote meaningful change, alongside addressing the social determinants of health. This review provides a valuable basis for future research.

Keywords: Pacific Peoples, Pacific Islanders, dietary interventions, diet modifications, dietary change

## INTRODUCTION
Pacific Peoples make up 9% of the total population of Aotearoa New Zealand (AoNZ), around 450 000 people. In Australia, Pacific Peoples make up 1.6% of the total population, which is approximately 415 033 people. However, Pacific Peoples are overrepresented in rates of long-term metabolic conditions and multimorbidity. , According to the Health New Zealand Status Report 2024, Pacific Peoples have a life expectancy of 6 years less than Europeans living in AoNZ and a similar lower life expectancy compared to the general population in Australia. Pacific Peoples residing in both AoNZ and Australia are 2 times more likely to experience obesity and 3 times more likely to have type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) compared to the general population, with T2DM being particularly concerning because Pacific Peoples are more likely to develop T2DM at a younger age, resulting in complications such as early mortality, cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, lower-limb amputation, and retinopathy. These statistics are alarming and require immediate attention to find solutions to improve health outcomes of Pacific Peoples.
In the broader context of metabolic health, following a healthy diet and engaging in regular physical activity can prevent or delay the onset of T2DM in people with impaired glucose tolerance, particularly if dietary patterns include primarily plant-based foods and a Mediterranean-style diet. However, little is known about the optimal dietary pattern for Pacific Peoples living in AoNZ and Australia.
Factors influencing metabolic health for Pacific Peoples in AoNZ and Australia are multifaceted, and developing strategies to implement change can occur only with recognition of the factors that have led to inequities in health and the historical events that have shaped the health of Pacific Peoples living in both AoNZ and Australia. Changes in the food environment, a scarcity or absence of traditional foods, food insecurity, and a movement into cities, away from a village and farm-based lifestyle, alongside institutional racism, job insecurity, and restricted or complicated access to healthcare, contribute to the health and well-being of Pacific Peoples living in AoNZ. In addition, in Australia low levels of health literacy and health-seeking behavior, limited understanding of healthcare services, lack of culturally tailored health promotion targeting Pacific communities, genetic factors, and generational disadvantage compared to people of other ethnicities were identified as other contributing factors. Here, we provide some background on these factors to establish the context for the scoping review.

## BACKGROUND

## Definition of Pacific Peoples
Pacific Islander is a term used to describe the Indigenous Peoples from the Pacific regions of Polynesia (Hawai’i, Tonga, Samoa, Cook Islands, Niue, Tokelau, Tuvalu, and AoNZ), Melanesia (Fiji, Vanuatu, Papua New Guinea, Solomon, and New Caledonia), and Micronesia (Marshalls, Nauru, Kiribati, Guam, and Palau). The term “Pacific Islander” is an identity label that transcends national boundaries on the basis of cultural backgrounds and similarity of experiences. The term “Pacific Islander” is exclusive of Māori, who are the Indigenous Peoples of AoNZ. The term “Pacific Peoples” is unique to AoNZ and is used to describe a diverse group of people living in AoNZ who have either migrated from the Pacific Islands or who identify with the Pacific Islands due to ancestry or heritage. It is important to acknowledge that Pacific Peoples share a common Pacific heritage, but there is also diversity and representation of the separate groups that are linguistically, culturally, geographically, and politically distinct from each other. , 
While many Pacific Peoples still live on their original islands, substantial numbers have migrated to larger societies, mostly Australia, the United States, and AoNZ. According to the 2021 Australian census, Pacific Peoples make up 1.3% of the total population, while in the United States Census of 2022, they made up 0.3% of the population. In AoNZ Pacific Peoples make up 9% of the population and are the fourth largest ethnic group. The 7 largest Pacific groups in AoNZ are Samoan (48% of Pacific Peoples), Tongan (22%), Cook Islands (21%), Niuean, Fijian, Tokelauan, and Tuvaluan. The Cook Islands, Niue, and Tokelau groups have a special political relationship with AoNZ that grants them automatic eligibility to a status equivalent to an AoNZ citizen. 
In contrast, the largest Pacific Island populations residing in Australia are Indigenous Māori from AoNZ (46%), Samoan (20%), and Tongan (9%). Other Pacific Island communities residing in Australia include Fijian Indian, Indigenous Fijian, Niuean, Cook Islanders, Ni-Vanuatu, Papua New Guinean, and Tuvaluan. In Australia, Queensland has the largest population of Pacific Peoples, approximately 147 928 people, with a higher percentage of Pacific people aged 0-29 years. 
Most Pacific Peoples in AoNZ (66%) were born in AoNZ. Of those Pacific Peoples born elsewhere, 60% have lived in AoNZ for 10 years or more. In addition, there are 19 000 Pacific-resident workers in AoNZ for some part of the year for the Recognised Seasonal Employer (RSE) scheme, working in horticulture and viticulture. Although traditional Pacific Island life is predominantly centered around rural villages, most Pacific Peoples living in AoNZ reside in urban areas, with only 7.2% living rurally. The city of Auckland, AoNZ, has the most significant urban concentration of Pacific Peoples (260 400 population) in the Pacific Rim. 

## History of Pacific Peoples in AoNZ New Zealand
Between 1950 and 1980, many Pacific Peoples migrated to AoNZ, supported by the AoNZ government and the manufacturing industry. These migrants to AoNZ sought job opportunities and better education for their children, which they believed were key to success in life. Jobs were mainly low-skill and low-wage positions, which made housing affordability a challenge. Pacific communities gathered in lower-rent suburbs, mostly in Auckland and Wellington. However, housing suitability and affordability, combined with lower wages, is a challenge that remains today. 
A downturn in the AoNZ economy in the early 1970s resulted in high unemployment rates that disproportionately affected Pacific Peoples. As a result, the government at the time launched an intensive police enforcement campaign to search and arrest Pacific Peoples who had stayed past the expiration date of their visas. These raids were known as the “Dawn Raids,” as they occurred very early in the morning. The raids were traumatic and frightening experiences for Pacific families and had a lasting negative impact on Pacific communities within AoNZ. What followed was government-endorsed and police-enforced systemic racism, during which police spot checked and arrested people on the street that looked like Pacific Peoples even if they were not. The Pacific Peoples were the only group subjected to this enforcement campaign, despite the knowledge that the highest percentage of overstayers in AoNZ at the time were people from the United Kingdom. This discrimination according to race had a significant impact on the lives of Pacific Peoples. , 
In AoNZ today, many factors contribute to the health inequities experienced by Pacific Peoples. These include racism and discrimination when accessing health services, low rates of homeownership, household crowding leading to high levels of avoidable housing-related hospitalizations, educational poverty, low incomes, and unemployment. The pay equity gap between Pacific Peoples and other ethnic groups across all job sectors in AoNZ is significant and cannot be fully explained by qualifications and skill level. Pacific women experience the largest pay equity gap among all groups in AoNZ. According to Statistics New Zealand the median individual Pacific Peoples’ net worth in 2021 was NZ$28 000, while it was NZ$439 000 for Europeans living in AoNZ. Pacific Peoples experience the highest levels of material deprivation, with 21% compared to 7% of AoNZ Europeans. Additionally, 26% of Pacific children live in households experiencing material hardship. 

## History of Pacific Peoples in Australia
Pacific Peoples began migrating to Australia in the early 1860s to work on Queensland’s sugar plantations, with many workers reportedly brought there by force. In the 1960s and 1970s the government introduced multicultural policies, followed by 1980s and 1990s migration policies that allowed AoNZ citizens preferential entry to Australia, which led to the growth of Pacific communities. 
The 2021 Australian census data revealed that the situation for Pacific Peoples in Australia is somewhat similar to that in AoNZ. In Australia, despite working similar hours, Pacific Peoples earn less than the general population. This disparity may be attributed to the types of employment the groups typically engage in. Pacific Peoples are underrepresented in professional, educational, and scientific fields, while they have higher representation in healthcare, social assistance, and manual labor roles. They are also more likely to be unemployed or seeking full-time work. Approximately half of the Pacific Peoples in Australia live in regions that experience moderate levels of socioeconomic disadvantage. 
Moreover, Pacific Peoples tend to have larger families, with more children compared to the general population, resulting in greater childcare responsibilities. These responsibilities are often shared within extended families and the wider Pacific Community, reflecting the collectivist nature of Pacific cultures. Additionally, there are higher incidences of multiple families living in the same household among Pacific Peoples compared to general population. Although having multiple generations under one roof aligns with cultural norms, it may also indicate pressures related to cost of living. Furthermore, Pacific Peoples experience higher rates of unstable housing and are more likely than the general population to reside in public housing. 
On a positive note, in Australia the percentage of Pacific children completing year 12 of high school is 63.2%, which is comparable to the completion rate of 59.1% in the general population. 

## Values of Pacific Peoples
Despite social and economic disparities, Pacific Peoples living in AoNZ and Australia have strong communities, based on values brought from their home countries. The document Yavu: Foundations of Pacific Engagement New Zealand 2022 describes the values important to Pacific Peoples, including collectivism, consensus, family, love, reciprocity, respect, and spirituality. Pacific Peoples are communal people, and their worldview is driven by characteristics perceived as acceptable to the community, which include teamwork, co-operation, and working together to achieve common goals. Decisions are made on the basis of consensus, where possible, by consulting everyone who ought to be consulted. For many Pacific Peoples, love is inherent in all that they do and how they behave, in the showing of care, kindness, concern for others, and putting others before themselves. Family can extend beyond the nuclear family and is inclusive of the village (community), which brings a sense of belonging and identity. Reciprocity refers to the value of relationships and obligations between individuals or groups when working toward a shared purpose. Pacific Peoples are taught to respect one another from an early age. Most Pacific Peoples hold their spiritual faith, religious practices, and cultural customs and protocols in the highest regard. Cultural identity and well-being are intricately entwined with patterns around food and dietary behavior, with the preparation and consumption of food deeply embedded in cultural practices of social exchange, obligation, and cohesion for household, family, and community life. Food serves as a way to bring family and communities together, and it is regarded as a reward for hard work and a source of comfort and pleasure. When migrating to another country, Pacific Peoples use food rituals to help preserve the form and retain connections to strengthen culture within the new community. Food and food practices represent tradition, identity, and togetherness. Although some food and food practices are less healthy, they persist due to the emotional and cultural gratification they provide. 

## Pacific Peoples: View of Health
Health for Pacific Peoples is holistic and encompasses physical, mental, social, and spiritual well-being, as well as identity, land, cultural values, roles, and responsibilities. Compared to a Western biomedical perspective, the Pacific Peoples view health as “collective,” that is, it entails being able to provide for their families and communities and ensure the well-being of their families, rather than just focusing on individual physical health. 
To reflect the health perspectives of Pacific Peoples, Pan-Pacific research methodologies have been established to ensure that research aligns with the values of the communities being studied. One example of this strategy is the Fonofale health model developed by Pulotu-Endemann (2001). In this model the shared values and beliefs of many Pacific ethnic groups are incorporated and frequently used in research projects involving individuals of multiple Pacific ethnicities, thus ensuring that the epistemology of one Pacific ethnic group does not hold privilege above those of other groups. 
In addition, ethnic-specific Pacific research models have been developed to capture the perspectives of specific Pacific ethnic groups. Examples include the Kūki Airani/Cook Island, Tivaevae model by Maua-Hodges (2003), the Tokelauan, Te Vaka Atafaga health model by Kupa (2009), and the Meihana health model by Pitama et al. (2014), to name a few.
For this review, we utilized a more recently developed Tongan health model, Kato Polopola (food basket), developed by Muimuiheata (2021). This model emerged from a PhD thesis, a qualitative study that explored the relationship between food and living with diabetes, from the perspective of a Tongan population living in urban AoNZ. Although Kato Polopola is an ethnic-specific health model it can be used as a Pan-Pacific research methodology by employing other research tools such as the “give way rule.” In this review authors gave priority to the perspectives of Pacific Peoples by adopting the give way rule, whereby if any cultural differences in interpretations of data arose, the views of the Pacific advisor who holds Pacific knowledge relevant to that particular Pacific ethnic group would take precedence. 
The Kato Polopola health model was selected for this review because it reflects the shared values and beliefs of many Pacific ethnic groups and captures their perspectives on food and health, aligning with the objectives of this review
The objective of this scoping review was to systematically locate and review the literature on the enablers and challenges of dietary interventions and dietary change to optimize the health of Pacific Peoples living in AoNZ and/or Australia.

## METHODS

## Protocol and Registration
To identify key concepts and research gaps in existing literature on a specific topic, a scoping review method was chosen because this method provides a useful approach for mapping the literature. This scoping review was conducted according to a previously registered protocol (Open Science Framework 10.17605/OSF.IO/AJCXK), which was informed by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews. This review follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Scoping Review extension (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines and the relevant PRISMA checklist is included in . 

## Research Reflexivity
The authors of this scoping review are from diverse backgrounds and research experience. The first author (T.A.) is of Cook Island Māori and Pākehā (AoNZ European) descent and is also a registered dietitian and doctoral candidate and is supported by a Pākehā (A.P.-S.) Research Fellow, a Tongan (S.M.) Registered Dietitian and emerging Pacific researcher, 2 Pākehā research supervisors (R.H., J.K.), 3 Pākehā research collaborators (C.B., K.C., M.W.-M.), and 2 Indigenous Māori research collaborators (C.M., B.J.). All members of the team contributed their knowledge and expertise regarding the health of Indigenous Māori and Pacific Peoples and performed qualitative research to develop this scoping review. Throughout the review, the authors have described how the enablers and the challenges of Pacific Peoples engaging with dietary interventions and change in studies are positioned within the worldview of Pacific Peoples.

## Search Strategy
We originally intended to include both Indigenous Māori and Pacific Peoples in this analysis; however, after the initial search for data, we decided to conduct separate reviews. This enabled Indigenous Māori of AoNZ to exercise their “tino rangatiratanga” (self-determination) within research by drawing on their Kaupapa Indigenous Māori methodologies. This approach also enabled other Pacific methodologies to be utilized for this review. The key search terms “food,” “diet,” “nutrition,” AND “intervention,” “programme” encompassing both “Indigenous Māori” OR “Pacific Peoples populations” were used to systematically identify English-language articles published in peer-reviewed journals across 6 databases (Medline [OVID], Embase [OVID], Scopus, Indigenous health [inform IT], CINAHL [EBSCO], NZ Research, and Web of Science) from their inception until July 27, 2021. In addition, relevant articles were sought in the reference lists of the included articles, including theses. The search strategy for each database is detailed in .
The initial search resulted in 10 963 articles. After removing 4455 duplicates, 2 reviewers (A.P.-S., M.W.-M.) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved articles using Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia), and 6175 articles were excluded as shown in the PRISMA flow diagram ( ). The full-text articles were independently assessed by T.A. and A.P.-S., who applied the population, concepts, and context (PCC) and the inclusion and exclusion criteria depicted to the research question described in . To ensure consistency, n = 18 (46%) eligible articles were reviewed by both researchers. All discrepancies were resolved by consensus, and input was sought from a Pacific researcher (S.M.) who shared the same Pacific ethnicity as one of the populations included in the article on which T.A. and A.P.-S. sought guidance. The give way rule was applied. A total of 19 articles were selected for the review. Because only a small number of articles (n = 8) focused solely on Pacific Peoples, articles that included Pacific Peoples alongside other ethnic groups were also included (n = 11).
 

## Data Charting Process
Data extraction was carried out from eligible articles using a template created by the authors on Microsoft Excel. The data were then separated into 2 tables to reflect the 2 main questions of the review: (1) What are the enablers that facilitate dietary interventions and healthful change? and(2) What are the challenges that come with dietary interventions and healthful change? This process was iterative, and authors modified the template as they became more familiar with the articles selected. Accuracy was ensured by both researchers (T.A. and A.P.-S.) checking each other’s data extraction. Articles that reported more than 90% data of Pacific Peoples were considered Pacific Peoples only articles. The extracted data included details on first author, publication, aim, methods, location, population studied, intervention, and enablers and challenges to dietary interventions or change. Additionally, the quality of the methodology employed by the studies when engaging with Pacific Peoples was taken into consideration and guided by the Health Research Council (HRC) Pacific Health Research Guidelines (2014) (see and   ).
 

## Synthesis of Results
To synthesize the data for this review we used a Tongan health model, Kato Polopola (food basket), developed by Muimuiheata (2021) ( ) because this health model interweaves Pacific Peoples’ values with Pacific Peoples’ views on relationships between food and health.
 The Kato Polopola model has 12 domains that are symbolized by coconut leaves woven to make a food basket. Five of these domains reflect the Pacific values that are commonly shared across Pacific Island cultures: Fāmili (family), those living within a household; Kāinga (extended family and the community); Kāinga lotu (church); Laumālie (spirituality); and Loto (heart), the connection with others. The other 7 domains identify key concepts from a Pacific perspective that impact the relationship between Pacific Peoples and food: Sino (body), including physical well-being; Àtamai (mind) knowledge and understanding; Ongo (emotional); Taimi/Tā (time), relating to the past and the present; Pule’anga (government and services/policy); and ′Atakai/Vā (environment/space). 
The rim of the basket symbolizes Anga Faka–Tonga (meaning Tongan nature or the Tongan way) or Anga Faka–′Ulungaanga (meaning characteristics of a culture) that binds and is interwoven with all aspects of Pacific values and relationships with food. 
Identified enablers and challenges were separately mapped onto the visual figure of the Kato Polopola model by T.A., in accordance with the themes that emerged from the data sets (see ). A talanoa, which means having a discussion, and is a well-recognized concept across the Pacific Islands, occurred between the researcher who developed the Kato Polopola model (S.M.) and the reviewers of the selected articles (T.A. and A.P.-S.) to gain a deeper understanding of the model. A talanoa is an extension of the Pacific Community practices of having face to face conversations for passing down traditional knowledge or exchanging of ideas and developing collaborations. In the context of this review, a talanoa was employed to engage in collaboration of data mapping. The collaboration of data mapping was an ongoing iterative process with T.A., A.P.-S., and S.M. (developer of the Kato Polopola Model), until data mapping was agreed to be completed (see ). The iterative process was also applied when data overlapped more than 1 conceptual domain, for example Taimi/Ta (time) and Anga Faka—“Ulungaanga” (characteristics of culture). The pre-existing conceptual domains of the Kato Polopola model provided a lens through which to view, collate, and summarize the data. Doing so in this study provided a narrative review of how the available literature reports on enablers and challenges of dietary interventions and healthful change for Pacific Peoples.

## Key Enablers for Pacific Peoples to Engage With Dietary Interventions and Dietary Change
This scoping review highlights the finding that developing dietary interventions reflecting shared cultural values is essential for Pacific Peoples to actively engage and achieve positive dietary changes. Importantly, an intervention must align with the Pacific value of collectivism, involving all family members living in the same household. , , , , , , To promote a holistic approach to health and well-being, the intervention should integrate physical activity , and spirituality and reflect the cultural values of Pacific Peoples. , , , , 
The quality of the relationship between a facilitator and participant was identified in this review as an important enabler that encourages Pacific Peoples to engage with interventions. , , , The findings of this review demonstrated that facilitators who understand the diverse cultures and worldviews of Pacific participants and/or share a similar cultural background can build meaningful relationships with them. , , , For instance, in their diabetes prevention study (2021), Firestone et al. found that engaging the Pacific Youth from the community as facilitators can motivate the Pacific Community to participate. This approach aligns with the cultural values of collectivism and love.
Co-designing interventions with the targeted Pacific Community was also highlighted as a key enabler in this review. , Pacific Island groups are linguistically diverse and possess a variety of cultural traditions. Therefore, involving the specific Pacific Community in the co-design process ensures that their culture and values are respected throughout the design and implementation of the intervention. Additionally, ensuring that the intervention is accessible to the targeted community in terms of both affordability and location can be achieved through this collaborative approach. , , 
The review process also highlighted the benefits of engaging with community church groups as an essential enabler. , Such benefits suggest that interventions were important that enhanced knowledge of traditional Pacific foods, including their preparation, and provided practical dietary advice for the management of T2DM. , , , The supportive, collective unit of the family and extended family were depicted as a key enabler for Pacific Peoples to engage with dietary interventions and make dietary changes, particularly families living within the same household who were able to establish and reinforce healthy dietary changes. , 

## Key Challenges for Pacific Peoples to Engage With Dietary Interventions and Dietary Change
This scoping review highlighted key challenges for Pacific Peoples seeking to engage with dietary interventions, and healthful dietary changes stem from the socioeconomic environment in which many Pacific Peoples in AoNZ and Australia reside. This environment included low-income employment that required multiple jobs, inflexible work hours, and shift work, all of which limited the ability to attend interventions. , , , The review also identified the cultural norm of the collective responsibility among Pacific Peoples to prioritize caring for family members such as children and elderly grandparents, as well as fulfilling obligations to the Pacific Community and church. , , , These responsibilities often took precedence over attending the dietary interventions.
Other key environmental challenges identified by the review as having an impact on healthful dietary changes were the influences of the promotion of unhealthy foods by the media. Additionally, the review indicated that situations in which family members living in the same household, , , , , , as well as the broader Pacific Community , , , and church, , do not support healthy dietary changes, create further obstacles to making these changes. Food insecurity (that is, a limited or uncertain availability of nutritionally adequate and safe foods) was another key challenge to making healthy dietary change. 
Food consumption and cultural identity are deeply intertwined for Pacific people living in AoNZ and Australia. Therefore, maintaining cultural identity while adhering to healthy eating guidelines posed a challenge. For example, socioeconomic pressures to provide food for large families would lead to unhealthy food choices to ensure that traditional customs around food were upheld. The review emphasized the importance of food sharing in Pacific culture as a means of expressing cultural identity and reflecting the core values of collectivism, reciprocity, and the well-being of family and community.

## DISCUSSION
In this scoping review we aimed to investigate factors that enable or challenge engagement with dietary interventions and healthful change for lifestyle-related health conditions among Pacific Peoples residing in the Western countries of AoNZ and Australia, who have disproportionate morbidity and mortality due to metabolic diseases. The study findings reflect the experiences of Pacific Peoples primarily living in AoNZ and Australia.
Key enablers for Pacific Peoples to engage with dietary interventions and make dietary changes included interventions that reflect Pacific cultural values, prioritize strong facilitator–participant relationships, and are co-designed with the targeted community. Additionally, these interventions should support and grow the knowledge of traditional foods and food preparation and provide practical, healthful dietary advice for the management of T2DM. The key enablers identified from this scoping review are similar to those reported in the existing literature, such as the Mana Tū Diabetes Programme by Tane et al. (2021), who found that culturally tailored interventions were more effective in supporting Indigenous Māori and Pacific Peoples living with T2DM than general healthcare services in AoNZ that offer support for individuals living with T2DM. Similarly, in the Prediabetes Intervention Package (PIP) study, Abel et al. (2021) observed that the quality of the participant’s relationship with the health professional made a difference in health-seeking behaviors. In the study by Akbar et al. (2022), a co-design approach with the target Pacific Community was utilized to develop a framework to explore diabetes self-management of Australian Pacific women living with T2DM, and their findings highlighted the significant role of family and community in providing support to women for their diabetes self-management. Similar to this review, a systematic review and narrative synthesis by Matenga-Ikihele et al. (2021) explored the characteristics of behavior change interventions used among Pacific people. These researchers determined that important characteristics in the interventions were meaningful relationships with Pacific communities obtained using community-based participatory research and culturally centered interventions, with Pacific facilitators and the use of talanoa for the delivery of the interventions. 
An evaluation of a community-based program for the prevention of chronic disease among Māori and Pacific Island communities living in Queensland Australia reported similar results of positive impact on behavioral changes for the community from a culturally tailored program delivered by multiethnic health workers. Furthermore, these authors identified the need for developing culturally appropriate policies, a culturally responsive workforce, and long-term funding to embed these successful initiatives into the healthcare system. 
The supportive, collective nature of the family is key to establishing and reinforcing healthy dietary changes within the household. However, Pacific communities are faced with challenges. The increased responsibility and duty of Pacific Peoples to care for their families is amplified by the existing health inequities of Pacific Peoples living longer with co-morbidities and dying early compared to Europeans living in AoNZ. This situation places a greater burden on Pacific families, limiting their time and resources for participating in dietary interventions and dietary change. In addition, Pacific Peoples are 3 times more likely to live in the most socioeconomically deprived areas in AoNZ and Australia, and data from Australia, the United States, and the United Kingdom have demonstrated that low-income populations are less able to adopt healthful dietary change. , 
This review highlights the finding that interventions should empower the Pacific Community not only with knowledge about diabetes and other metabolic conditions relevant to the community but also with support needed to overcome socioeconomic challenges. This empowerment requires a comprehensive system-wide approach involving government policy implementation and support. The social determinants identified in this review that hindered participation in dietary interventions and change were similar to those reported by Pacific Peoples when engaging with healthcare services in AoNZ and prediabetes intervention studies by Abel et al. (2021), such as inequitable access to healthy food, food insecurity, financial security, transportation, availability of services (accessible locations, opening hours, the ability to take time off work to attend), and the availability of childcare services. , 
Food insecurity was also identified as another key challenge to making healthy dietary change possible. A study by Carter et al. (2010) identified food insecurity as contributing to poor nutritional outcomes, including inadequate dietary intake resulting in iron deficiency, anemia, and obesity, and thus further perpetuating poor health outcomes for Pacific Peoples.
In this review we emphasized the significant role the environment has in supporting healthy food choices to prevent and manage chronic health conditions, including diabetes, and the importance of policy and government systems in enabling sustainable dietary change and engagement with dietary intervention programs. Similarly, a prediabetes intervention study by Abel et al. (2021) emphasized the significance of supportive food environments in facilitating dietary changes. In this study Abel et al. observed that participants were more likely to recognize the personal, interpersonal, and healthcare influences on the food environment, rather than the more complex social, environmental, and policy factors crucial for promoting a healthy food environment. Similarly, the articles in this review briefly addressed social, environmental, and policy influences on dietary intervention and changes. This limited focus may have been due to the specific aims of the studies included in this review and their study designs, which did not include exploration of this area. Therefore, exploring the impact of these broader factors represents a potential area for future research.
Although reported as a challenge for Pacific Peoples to engage with healthcare services in AoNZ, institutional racism was not identified as a challenge in this review. This may be because the articles in this review focused on the participants’ experience or perceived experience of the intervention rather than a wider societal and environmental system–based view. Furthermore, the methodology of the articles in this review may not have been developed to explore institutional racism.
Striking a balance between preserving traditional customs, such as bringing food to community and church gatherings and promoting healthy lifestyle choices was a significant challenge. Pearce et al. (2007) found a strong association between neighborhood deprivation and geographic access to fast food outlets in AoNZ, which contribute to unhealthy eating patterns. Coupled with long working hours, limited time for food preparation resulted in the making of unhealthy food choices (eg, fast foods) by Pacific Peoples to ensure that they continued to uphold their traditional customs of bringing food to community gatherings, including church gatherings, as an integral part of their cultural heritage.
This review highlighted the importance of dietary interventions that should equip Pacific communities with knowledge and support to overcome challenging socioeconomic environments that often hinder their ability to engage with dietary interventions and adopt healthy eating habits.

## Strengths and Limitations
The strengths of this scoping review include the methodology used to conduct the search and the methods used to identify themes and report findings. Using a combination of JBI methodology, the Pacific health Kato Polopola (food basket) model allowed for flexibility in data extraction, charting, and analysis while maintaining a systematic and transparent approach to the review. As a Pacific Health framework, the Kato Polopola model shares key similarities with the pan-Pacific health model commonly used in health research, known as the Fonofale model. Both models provide a holistic view of health, in which culture encompasses all domains. Each model includes similar areas, such as family, spirituality, physical health, mental well-being, environment, and time. This makes the Kato Polopola model suitable for analysis with a scoping review.
The largest population of Pacific Peoples outside of the Pacific Islands resides in AoNZ, which makes the identified literature representative of the key enablers and challenges of dietary interventions and change for lifestyle-related conditions among Pacific Peoples living in AoNZ and Australia. Research with Pacific Peoples based elsewhere was not included, and it is possible that some important messages were missed. However, we are confident that with the inclusion of all research based in AoNZ and Australia a large number of Pacific Peoples were represented, and the identified themes consistently mapped to the Kato Polopola model. A limitation of the study was the minimal inclusion of gray literature, which may explain the lack of studies focused on Pacific Peoples.

## Gaps in the Literature and Future Research
A key finding of this review is the underrepresentation of Pacific Peoples’ perspectives in published literature, with only 5 articles identified that focused on dietary interventions specifically designed for this population and addressed the research question. Similarly, the review by Matenga-Ikihele et al. (2021) also reported a lack of studies focused on behavior change interventions to improve health for Pacific Peoples living in AoNZ and Australia. This paucity of research demonstrates institutional racism of inaction in the face of need. 
In addition, for the studies in which participants were found to identify with different ethnic groups we were unable to isolate challenges and enablers unique to Pacific Peoples. This limitation further supports the need for research in this area.
While the reviewed articles included collected feedback from participants, future research should also include insights from facilitators, because understanding different perspectives can enhance the quality of interventions. Moreover, engaging with those who declined to participate in postintervention interviews can help reduce bias in feedback collection.

## CONCLUSION
In this scoping review we have identified the factors that enable and challenge dietary interventions and change for Pacific Peoples residing in AoNZ and Australia. The Pacific population is facing a critical and increasing incidence of metabolic diseases that are directly associated with early morbidity and mortality. Therefore, it is imperative to carefully consider the key enablers for managing healthy dietary change, as well as preventing metabolic conditions, in the context of the cultural values of the Pacific Peoples. The findings of this scoping review will provide valuable insights for future research that aims to design and implement a dietary and lifestyle intervention program aimed at preventing and managing cardiometabolic conditions among Pacific Peoples.
