## Abstract

## Background
Mental disorders represent a major global health burden, yet undergraduate psychiatric training in many low- and middle-income countries remain suboptimal. In Nigeria, misalignment between regulatory frameworks and international best practices has limited the prioritisation of psychiatry in medical education.

## Aims
To provide a nationwide overview of undergraduate psychiatric training, structure and assessment methods in Nigerian medical schools.

## Methods
A cross-sectional survey of accredited Nigerian medical schools was conducted between June and July 2024 using a 28-item questionnaire administered electronically to psychiatric departmental heads or lecturers. Descriptive analysis was performed using STATA 17.0.

## Results
Of 48 accredited medical schools, 40 (83.3%) had psychiatric lecturers who could complete the questionnaire, while 39 participated (response rate: 97.5%). Standalone departments of psychiatry were present in 64.1% of schools, although nearly half employed only one to three psychiatrists. Most schools (76.9%) offered no psychiatric teaching in the preclinical years, and 94.9% had no independent assessment of behavioural sciences. During the clinical years, 46.2% provided four weeks of psychiatric clerkship, while only 15.4% met the WHO recommended eight weeks. Emergency and community psychiatric exposure was largely absent. Although 59.0% of schools used OSCE/OSPE for clinical assessment, only 28.2% conducted psychiatry as a standalone examination, and just 17.9% allocated full independent weighting (100%) at the final MBBS level.

## Conclusion
Undergraduate psychiatric training in Nigerian medical schools remains insufficient in scope, duration, and priority, with substantial variability across institutions. Harmonised and enforceable regulatory reforms aligned with international best practices are urgently needed to strengthen psychiatric education and improve mental health competencies of future doctors.

## Introduction
Mental disorders constitute a significant global health burden, with depressive disorders ranking as the second leading cause of years lived with disability (YLDs) in 2019 [ ]. An estimated 450 million people worldwide live with mental illnesses, yet access to care remains limited, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa [ ]. In Nigeria, a national survey reported a lifetime prevalence of psychiatric disorders of 12.1%; however, only 8% of individuals with moderate-to-severe mental illness received treatment [ ]. This substantial treatment gap reflects the global shortage of mental health professionals and infrastructure highlighted by the World Health Organisation (WHO) [ ]. Nigeria faces one of the most critical shortages, with fewer than 300 psychiatrists serving a population of over 200 million—a situation further exacerbated by the emigration of trained specialists [ , ].
Undergraduate psychiatric medical education has been identified as a key strategy for bridging this gap by equipping future doctors with the skills, knowledge, and attitudes required to deliver quality mental health care [ ]. Globally, the structure and delivery of undergraduate psychiatric training vary widely. International bodies such as the World Psychiatric Association (WPA) and the World Federation of Medical Education (WFME) advocate for a competency-based medical education (CBME) framework that emphasises curricular integration, acquisition of soft skills, problem-based learning, and milestone-based assessments [ – ]. Core competencies include effective communication, empathy, reflective listening, and a holistic approach to care—skills that are essential not only in psychiatry but across all medical disciplines [ , ]. These organisations further recommend early exposure to psychiatry, extended training durations, innovative teaching methods, and independent formative and summative assessments, including multiple-choice questions, objective structured clinical examinations (OSCEs), and entrustable professional activities (EPAs) [ , – ].
In Nigeria, the regulation of medical schools is jointly undertaken by the National Universities Commission (NUC) and the Medical and Dental Council of Nigeria (MDCN), with each body playing distinct but complementary roles [ , ]. Historically, both regulatory bodies have conducted separate accreditation exercises for medical schools. However, in mid-2025, the Federal Ministry of Education resolved that the NUC and MDCN should conduct joint accreditation exercises for medical schools instead of separate reviews [ , ]. It is anticipated that implementation of this policy will commence soon.
The NUC oversees the academic and institutional aspects of university education, including medical programmes. Its responsibilities include approving and licensing universities, accrediting degree programmes such as the MBBS/MBChB, setting minimum academic standards for medical curricula, and monitoring institutional capacity to deliver these programmes [ ]. In contrast, the MDCN focuses on the professional and clinical dimensions of medical and dental education and practice. Its mandate is to ensure that graduates are competent and safe to practise. To achieve this, the MDCN accredits medical and dental schools from a clinical training perspective, approves teaching hospitals and other clinical training sites, and sets standards for medical and dental training, particularly with respect to curriculum content and clinical competence [ ].
Both the NUC and MDCN curricula historically classified psychiatry as a subspecialty under internal medicine [ , ], a position that aligns with practices in several sub-Saharan African countries where psychiatry may even remain optional during undergraduate medical training [ , ]. This contrasts sharply with practices in developed countries such as the United States (US), the United Kingdom (UK), Canada, and Australia, where psychiatry is recognised as a core specialty with structured, mandatory training in medical schools [ ].
During the preclinical years, the MDCN curriculum recommends that medical psychology (behavioural sciences) be taught alongside the basic medical sciences in the first or second year of study [ ]. The curriculum specifies course content and expected learning outcomes for medical psychology-related topics [ ]. However, the guidelines do not stipulate the duration of teaching (in hours or weeks) nor clearly specify who should teach the course, despite requiring the presence of a mental health department and at least one full-time psychiatrist as part of the minimum accreditation standards for medical schools [ ]. Conversely, the NUC Core Curriculum and Minimum Academic Standards (CCMAS) for Medicine and Dentistry (2023) is silent on the requirement for psychiatrists in medical school accreditation but prescribes the teaching of a “community orientation/psychology” course in the first semester of the 200 level [ ].
This community orientation/psychology course carries a total of five credit units and comprises five subjects—medical sociology, man and his environment, medical psychology, history of medicine, and evolution of medical education in Nigeria—each weighted as one credit unit [ ]. Of these, medical psychology is allocated 30 lecture hours, while the remaining subjects are each allotted 15 lecture hours [ ]. Based on the course content and logical classification, behavioural sciences—being a broader construct—would encompass medical sociology, medical psychology, and man and his environment, amounting to three credit units and a total of 60 lecture hours. Paradoxically, the NUC curriculum situates the community orientation/psychology course under the Department of Community Medicine, to be taught by community health physicians, and further specifies that four of the five credit units should be carried forward into the clinical years and merged with community medicine, while remaining silent on the disposition of the final credit unit [ ]. Additionally, although community orientation/psychology is taught in the first semester of the 200 level, it is not recognised among the core and compulsory basic medical science courses—such as anatomy, physiology, and biochemistry—that are routinely examined during the preclinical years (200 and 300 levels) in medical schools [ ]. This contrasts with the early advocacy by Lambo (1961), who recommended that medical psychology be taught during the preclinical years, be examinable, and carry equivalent weight to anatomy and physiology in the second MB (first professional) examination [ ].
During the clinical years, the MDCN guidelines acknowledge that eight weeks are required for psychiatric training but paradoxically recommend only four to six weeks of clinical psychiatric posting [ ]. Meanwhile, the NUC curriculum approves four weeks of clinical psychiatric clerkship, compared with 20 weeks each allocated to paediatrics and obstetrics and gynaecology [ ]. In terms of credit allocation, the NUC guidelines assign a total of eight credit units to psychiatry, compared with 36 credit units each for paediatrics and obstetrics and gynaecology, 47 credit units for community medicine, 60 for internal medicine, and 66 for surgery [ ].
In 2012, the United States Agency for International Development (USAID), through the Health Systems 20/20 project and in collaboration with the MDCN, NUC, and other key stakeholders, facilitated a comprehensive review of the undergraduate medical curriculum in Nigeria in alignment with global standards [ ]. This revised curriculum separated psychiatry from internal medicine, recognising it as a standalone specialty, and reaffirmed earlier recommendations for teaching medical psychology during the preclinical years, as proposed by Lambo in 1961. It further recommended eight weeks of clinical psychiatric clerkship, to be split between the second semester of the fifth year and the first semester of the final year, with an independent examination distinct from general medicine [ ]. However, more than a decade after these recommendations were issued, they have neither been formally adopted nor implemented by the regulatory bodies, as evidenced by their omission from the 2023 NUC CCMAS guidelines [ ].
Notwithstanding this policy inertia, psychiatry faculty members in some Nigerian medical schools—such as the University of Ibadan; University of Ilorin; Obafemi Awolowo University, Ile-Ife and University of Benin, Edo State—have succeeded in implementing the eight-week clinical psychiatric postings and independent assessments (at 100% level) within their institutions. However, a universal policy change championed by both regulatory bodies remains essential to ensure uniformity and timely compliance across all Nigerian medical schools.
Comparisons with other developing and developed countries further underscore these disparities. In the US, medical students typically receive 60–90 h of behavioural sciences teaching during preclinical years and eight weeks of clinical psychiatric clerkship (equivalent in duration to paediatrics and obstetrics), while in the UK, students receive approximately 80 h of psychology teaching and 12 weeks of clinical psychiatric posting [ ]. Ethiopia provides about 20 h of behavioural sciences teaching during the preclinical years and eight weeks of clinical psychiatric clerkship during the fourth and fifth years [ ]. In Uganda, undergraduate psychiatric training includes basic and applied psychology teaching in the first three years and five weeks of clinical psychiatric clerkship in the fourth year [ ]. India prescribes structured behavioural sciences teaching comprising 40 lecture hours, four weeks of clinical psychiatric clerkship, and a two-week internship in psychiatry [ , ].
Consequently, psychiatric education in many African medical schools remains limited in duration and depth, often characterised by inadequate clinical exposure and, in some cases, a lack of adequate number of trained psychiatrists as teaching faculty [ , , ]. A study by Uwakwe et al. [ ] demonstrated incomplete implementation of undergraduate psychiatric guidelines across Nigerian medical schools [ ]. Among the 13 schools surveyed, 11 (84.6%) offered no teaching in medical psychology during the preclinical years. Only six schools (46.1%) implemented the NUC-recommended four-week clinical psychiatric posting; three schools (23.1%) provided six weeks of posting; one school (7.7%) had eight weeks of posting; while one school (7.7%) had no psychiatric posting at all. Furthermore, more than half of the medical schools (53.8%) did not carry out teaching in psychiatric ward rounds, and a large majority (n = 11, 84.6%) did not conduct independent examinations in psychiatry for the students at the end of training [ ]. Although this study (conducted 10 years ago) was limited by its small sample size (n = 13) and recruitment of participants was at a postgraduate examination venue, it nonetheless provided representation across Nigeria’s six geopolitical zones and highlighted significant gaps in psychiatric education [ ].
Evidence indicates that undergraduate clinical experiences and the role modelling of teaching faculty significantly influence medical students’ decisions to pursue psychiatry as a career [ ]. These experiences interact with other determinants of career choice, including students’ attitudes toward psychiatry, teaching methods, duration and quality of clinical exposure, opportunities for electives and enrichment activities, and individual personality traits [ , ].
Since the publication of the Uwakwe et al. study in 2015 [ ], the number of accredited medical schools in Nigeria has increased substantially. Consequently, updated data on training structures, adherence to standards, and evaluation methods are required. The present study therefore seeks to build on previous research [ ] by including a larger number of accredited medical schools across Nigeria and directly recruiting trainers involved in undergraduate medical education.
Furthermore, discrepancies remain between NUC and MDCN provisions for undergraduate medical education, with both bodies according insufficient priority to mental health training. These provisions fall below international standards and global best practices and are inconsistently enforced, allowing medical schools without any psychiatric training for their medical students to be accredited [ , , ]. This ultimately may affect the students’ future competence in providing basic mental health care, integrative mental health services at the primary care level, and specialization choice in psychiatry [ , , , ]. There is therefore an urgent need to review/harmonise regulatory provisions in line with international best practices [ ].
This nationwide survey aims to provide an updated overview of the structure and evaluation of undergraduate psychiatric training in Nigerian medical schools. Regular audits of psychiatric education are essential for monitoring curriculum implementation and guiding reforms. The findings of this study will identify both progress and persistent deficiencies with implications for the competence of future doctors and the strength of Nigeria’s mental health workforce. Ultimately, the study will contribute to the growing literature on medical education in Nigeria and inform strategies to strengthen psychiatric training, with broader implications for improving mental health care delivery nationwide.

## Methods

## Study design and setting
This was a cross-sectional, descriptive study among accredited Nigerian medical schools offering undergraduate medical education.

## Study population and Sampling
At the time of the study, there were 48 medical schools approved by the National Universities Commission (NUC) and with partial or full accreditation from the Medical and Dental Council of Nigeria (MDCN). The inclusion criteria were the accredited medical schools with psychiatric lecturers either in a department of psychiatry or a department of medicine who consented to participate in the study and completed the survey questionnaire, and these were the essential necessities for participating in the study.

## Data collection
Data were collected between June and July 2024 using a structured Google Form questionnaire designed for this study. The survey instrument was a 28-item self-administered questionnaire designed for this study, structured into three sections (see Appendix 1):

Basic institutional information—such as university name, location, type, and number of psychiatrists employed.
Structure of training—such as whether psychiatry functions as a standalone department, the extent of psychiatric input into pre-clinical training and examinations, the duration and timing of clinical clerkships, and the availability of psychiatric services.
Evaluation and assessment methods—including approaches to theory and clinical examinations, whether psychiatry is examined independently or in conjunction with internal medicine, and the weighting of psychiatry marks within overall medicine scores.

The questionnaires were distributed via email or WhatsApp to each school’s Head of Department (HOD) of psychiatry, and in the absence of an HOD, another psychiatrist involved in undergraduate teaching was approached to respond. One respondent (the HOD or designated psychiatrist) represented each participating institution, who provided written informed consent before completing the questionnaire. Thirty-nine out of 40 schools that met the inclusion criteria consented and completed the survey questionnaire. Eight schools did not have psychiatric lecturers in a department of psychiatry or a department of medicine who could sign informed consent and fill out the questionnaires; hence, they were excluded. The authors were aware that no psychiatric training of any sort occurred in the affected eight medical schools, and they also did not send their medical students to any other psychiatric facilities for such training.
To maximise participation, reminders were sent weekly (Fridays) through individual eligible respondents’ email or WhatsApp over 8 weeks.

## Ethical considerations
Approval for the study was sought from the Institutional Review Committee of the Kwara State University Teaching Hospital, and the approval protocol number was KWASUTH/IRC/246/VOL.II/106. This study was conducted using the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were informed about the study’s voluntary nature, and informed consent was obtained electronically. Only those who provided informed consent proceeded with the questionnaire. Institutional responses were anonymised and stored securely to maintain confidentiality throughout the study.

## Data analysis
Data was analysed using STATA 17.0. Categorical variables were summarised using frequencies, while continuous variables were described using mean and standard deviation. Descriptive comparative analysis was performed to understand similarities and differences across different institutions regarding the demographic characteristics of medical schools, training structure and evaluation methods in each medical school.

## Results
Of accredited medical schools (n = 48), 40 (83.3%) schools had under their employment the presence of psychiatrists as part of clinical lecturers training medical students, while only eight schools (16.7%) were without psychiatrists. Thirty-nine out of 40 accredited medical schools with psychiatric input responded to our survey, yielding a response rate of 97.5%. Federal and state-owned medical schools represented 43.6% and 38.5%, respectively, while only 9 (17.9%) were privately owned medical schools. Medical schools with standalone departments of psychiatry constituted 64.1%, and 48% of these standalone psychiatric departments have been in operation for at least 11 years or more. Nearly half (48.7%) of the medical schools have at least one to three psychiatrists (Table  ).
 With regard to the structure of training, the majority (76.9%) of medical schools had no psychiatric teaching (behavioural sciences) for students in the pre-clinical years, and nearly all (94.9%) had no independent assessment of students in behavioural science–related courses at the end of the pre-clinical years (second MB examination). During the clinical years, fewer than half of the schools (46.2%) provided four weeks of psychiatric clerkship experience. Four schools (10.3%) offered less than four weeks of psychiatric posting, while six schools (15.4%) provided eight weeks of clinical psychiatric clerkship. Nearly half of the schools (48.7%) conducted clinical psychiatric clerkship rotations at the 500 level, while 33.3% did so at the 600 level. (Fig.  ; Table  ). Most psychiatric departments in medical schools ran psychiatric subspecialty services like general adult psychiatry (97.4%), consultation-liaison psychiatry (92.3%), addiction psychiatry (79.5%) and child and adolescent psychiatry (66.7%), but nearly all were deficient in the provision of emergency psychiatry (92.3%) and community psychiatry (94.9%) for their student’s experience and learning (Table  ).
 Clinical evaluation and assessment varied across medical schools. However, most schools adopted standard evaluation methods like theory and clinical examination in the assessment of their students. Specifically, of theory assessments, most schools (76.9%) assessed their students using a combination of multiple choice questions (MCQ) and written theory exams (like Long Answer Question [LAQ] and Short Answer Question [SAQ]); 17.9% adopted MCQ alone; and 5.1% had only written theory exams. Regarding clinical assessments, one-fifth (20.5%) of the schools had no clinical exams in psychiatry, while the majority (79.5%) conducted psychiatric clinical assessments for their students. For the clinical exams, 20.5% of schools still provided a traditional long case, short cases or oral methods of clinical assessment, while most schools (n = 23, 59.0%) provided a clinical examination in the form of an Objective Structured Clinical Examination and Objective Structured Picture Examination (OSCE/OSPE). Furthermore, 11 (28.2%) medical schools conducted their psychiatric examination as a standalone, but only 7 (17.9%) medical schools had an independent weighting score of 100% for psychiatry at the 600 level final professional MB examination. Of those medical schools (n = 29) that had psychiatric assessment under internal medicine, most of them (n = 20, 69.0%) allotted psychiatry a weighting score of 10–20% of the total medicine mark (Table  ).
 

## Discussion
This nationwide survey provides an updated and comprehensive overview of the structure and evaluation of undergraduate psychiatric training in Nigerian medical schools and reveals persistent gaps between regulatory provisions, institutional practices, and international best standards. Despite modest progress compared with earlier reports [ ], psychiatric education in Nigeria remains underprioritised, inconsistently implemented, and insufficiently resourced, with important implications for the competence of future doctors and the country’s mental health workforce.
A key finding of this study is the relatively high proportion of accredited medical schools with psychiatric input (83.3%) and the predominance of standalone departments of psychiatry (64.1%). This represents an improvement over the earlier study by Uwakwe et al. [ ] and suggests growing recognition of psychiatry as a distinct discipline within Nigerian medical education. However, the continued absence of standalone psychiatric departments in some Nigerian medical schools contravenes MDCN provisions, which mandate dedicated mental health departments, short-stay wards, community psychiatry linkages, and collaboration with primary care [ ]. Weak regulatory enforcement likely contributes to these gaps, highlighting the need for stronger oversight by both the MDCN and the NUC.
In addition, nearly half of the existing departments are staffed by only one to three psychiatrists, reflecting severe human resource constraints within training institutions—a challenge previously reported in earlier research [ ]. Such limited staffing can restrict the scope of teaching, supervision, service delivery, and innovation in undergraduate psychiatric training. There is therefore a clear need for deliberate workforce expansion strategies, including targeted recruitment, retention incentives, and the development of academic psychiatry career pathways, to strengthen departmental capacity and ensure long-term sustainability.
The near absence of psychiatric teaching during the preclinical years is one of the most striking deficiencies identified in this study. More than three-quarters of the surveyed schools reported no teaching in behavioural sciences or medical psychology, and almost all lacked independent assessment of these courses at the second MB examination. These findings mirror earlier evidence [ ] and reflect a persistent misalignment between the NUC and MDCN curricula, as well as poor enforcement of existing guidelines. Early exposure to behavioural sciences is essential for shaping students’ attitudes, fostering empathy, and developing communication and psychosocial skills that are foundational across all medical specialties [ ]. However, in many Nigerian medical schools, the limited behavioural sciences teachings are embedded within the community medicine curriculum rather than housed in mental health departments, and are often taught by community physicians, with accrued credit units allocated to community medicine [ ]. Reinstating and strengthening examinable behavioural sciences or medical psychology in the preclinical years—supported by clearly defined teaching hours, learning outcomes, and appropriate evaluation by psychiatric departments should therefore be prioritised in future curriculum reforms by NUC and MDCN.
During the clinical years, the duration of psychiatric clerkship remains markedly inadequate in most institutions. Fewer than one in six schools provided the internationally recommended eight weeks of clinical psychiatry, while nearly half offered only four weeks, and some even less. Although several institutions met the NUC requirement of a four-week psychiatric posting, this standard itself falls short of the WHO recommendation of a minimum of eight weeks and lags behind practices in many high-income countries as well as some low- and middle-income settings [ , , , , , ]. Such shortened postings limit students’ exposure to the full spectrum of psychiatric disorders, multidisciplinary care, and longitudinal patient management, thereby undermining competence and confidence in mental health care [ ]. In light of these gaps, harmonised regulatory enforcement mandating a minimum of eight weeks of clinical psychiatric clerkship across all Nigerian medical schools is urgently needed, particularly within the framework of the newly approved joint NUC–MDCN accreditation policy.
Although most psychiatric departments reported offering subspecialty services such as consultation–liaison, addiction, and child and adolescent psychiatry, the near-universal lack of emergency and community psychiatry exposure is concerning. Given that the majority of mental health care in Nigeria occurs in emergency rooms, primary care settings, and community contexts [ ], this training gap further limits graduates’ preparedness for real-world practice. Curricula should be revised to explicitly require student exposure to emergency and community psychiatry, leveraging partnerships with general hospitals, primary health centres, and community-based services where specialist facilities are unavailable.
Encouragingly, this study demonstrates a gradual improvement in assessment practices, with over half of the schools adopting OSCE/OSPE formats for clinical evaluation. This trend aligns with global moves toward competency-based medical education and supports more objective assessment of clinical skills, communication, and professionalism [ , ]. Nonetheless, significant gaps remain. One-fifth of the schools reported having no clinical examination in psychiatry, and fewer than one-third conducted psychiatry as a standalone examination. In schools where psychiatry was subsumed under internal medicine, its contribution to the final mark was minimal—typically not exceeding one-fifth of the total score in nearly all (28 of 29) affected institutions—thereby reinforcing perceptions of psychiatry as a marginal discipline.
Compared with an earlier Nigerian study, in which fewer than one-sixth of schools conducted independent psychiatric examinations and psychiatry contributed less than one-sixth of the final mark in most affected schools [ ], the current findings suggest modest progress but persistent structural limitations. Similar patterns have been reported internationally. Byrne et al. [ ] found that nearly all Irish medical schools used MCQs and/or extended matching questions alongside theory-based assessments, with 83% conducting clinical examinations using OSCEs. Notably, only one of six Irish schools (16%) lacked a clinical psychiatric examination, a figure comparable to the one-fifth (20%) observed in the present study [ ]. Given the strong influence of assessment on learning priorities and students’ perceptions of a specialty [ ], independent summative assessment of psychiatry with adequate and standardised weighting should be implemented nationally to enhance its educational and professional value.
The variability observed across institutions reflects longstanding discrepancies between NUC and MDCN provisions and the absence of effective monitoring mechanisms. The continued accreditation of medical schools that fail to meet even minimal psychiatric training standards perpetuates inequities in graduate competence and undermines national mental health goals [ , ]. The recent decision to implement joint NUC–MDCN accreditation exercises presents a critical opportunity to address these gaps. This process should be used to harmonise and improve on the curricula, enforce minimum standards for psychiatric training, and link accreditation status to demonstrable compliance, rather than mere documentation.
Beyond competence in general medical practice, undergraduate psychiatric exposure has implications for workforce development. Evidence consistently shows that positive undergraduate experiences, role modelling, and adequate clinical exposure increase the likelihood of choosing psychiatry as a career [ , ]. In a country facing an extreme shortage of psychiatrists [ ], improving the quality, duration, and visibility of undergraduate psychiatric training may contribute indirectly to recruitment into the specialty. Medical schools should therefore support enrichment activities such as student psychiatry interest groups, electives, mentorship programmes, and research exposure to enhance engagement with the discipline, as applicable in some jurisdictions [ , ].
This study also highlights the need for regular national audits of undergraduate psychiatric education. Since the expansion of accredited medical schools in Nigeria, up-to-date data are essential for tracking progress, identifying persistent deficiencies, and informing policy decisions. Institutionalising periodic nationwide reviews, supported by regulatory bodies and professional associations like the Association of Psychiatrists in Nigeria (APN), would promote accountability and continuous improvement in psychiatric medical education.

## Strengths and Limitations
A major strength of this study is its nationwide scope and high response rate, with participation from 39 accredited medical schools across Nigeria, providing broad geographic and institutional representation. By directly recruiting psychiatrists involved in undergraduate teaching, the study offers firsthand insights into the structure, delivery, and assessment of psychiatric training within medical schools. In addition, the use of a structured and pretested questionnaire enhances the consistency and reliability of the data collected, while comparison with existing national guidelines and international standards allows for meaningful contextual interpretation of the findings.
However, the findings should be interpreted in light of certain limitations. The study relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to reporting bias. The exclusion of medical schools without any psychiatrists who could complete the online survey questionnaires—although necessary for ethical and practical reasons—may have led to an underestimation of the true extent of deficiencies in undergraduate psychiatric training nationwide. The cross-sectional design also limits causal inferences. Furthermore, the study did not evaluate the specific lecture hours or content of behavioural sciences courses offered during the preclinical years, which could have provided additional insight. Despite these limitations, the study provides the most up-to-date and comprehensive overview of undergraduate psychiatric training in Nigeria to date. Future studies should assess the students’ competencies or perceptions of their training, which are critical for evaluating training outcomes.

## Conclusion
This nationwide survey demonstrates that undergraduate psychiatric training in Nigerian medical schools remains inadequate and inconsistently implemented, despite growing recognition of psychiatry as a standalone specialty in many institutions. Deficiencies persist across the preclinical and clinical years, particularly in early exposure to behavioural sciences, duration of clinical psychiatric clerkship, availability of community and emergency psychiatric training, and the conduct and weighting of independent psychiatric assessments. These gaps fall below international standards and contribute to the continued production of medical graduates with limited mental health competencies.
There is an urgent need for harmonised and enforceable regulatory reforms by the NUC and MDCN to prioritise psychiatry within undergraduate medical education. Aligning curricula with global best practices—especially mandating examinable behavioural sciences in the preclinical years, a minimum of eight weeks of clinical psychiatric posting, and independent summative assessment—will be critical. Strengthening undergraduate psychiatric training is essential not only for improving the mental health competencies of all doctors but also for building a resilient mental health workforce capable of addressing Nigeria’s substantial and growing burden of mental illnesses.
