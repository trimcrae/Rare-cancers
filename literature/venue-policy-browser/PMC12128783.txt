KhannaRitu http://orcid.org/0009-0009-0875-4724GobinMaya Uptake of STI and HIV testing delivered in sexual health services is lower among these groups and concerns regarding confidentiality, stigma and discrimination are barriers to testing in these settings. 
Outreach services including community-based point-of-care testing (POCT) and innovations such as HIV and STI self-sampling kits and HIV self-testing kits have become increasingly accessible, proving to be acceptable and effective in reaching first-time testers. These approaches are valued for confidentiality, privacy and convenience, creating potential to encourage testing among individuals reluctant to engage in testing within sexual health services. Nonetheless, people from black communities continue to be under-represented among intervention users, suggesting that these interventions, when delivered in isolation, fail to overcome additional testing barriers such as risk awareness, cultural norms, stigma and fears of HIV-positive status reported among black communities around HIV and STIs. 
Sustained efforts are required to increase HIV and STI testing to reverse the longstanding trends in infection prevalent within black communities and achieve the goal to end new transmissions of HIV by 2030. Successful interventions must therefore be informed through a comprehensive understanding and consideration of the main facilitators and barriers to testing. While existing literature reviews have assessed the effectiveness of different approaches to HIV/STI testing, insights from interventions aimed at improving HIV/STI testing for black communities in the UK are limited.
Peoples’ willingness to engage with testing interventions is determined by a range of factors related to the individual, intervention and wider environment of delivery. Behavioural models offer valuable frameworks for understanding and predicting behaviour, in turn informing the design and implementation of health interventions. The Capability Opportunity Motivation Behaviour (COM-B) model is one such model, suggesting behaviour results from the interactions between an individual’s ability (physically as skills and psychologically as knowledge) to enact the behaviour (capability), their motivation to engage with the behaviour (motivation) and the physical or social environment that makes the behaviour possible (opportunity). The COM-B model provides a comprehensive, flexible and intuitive approach of behaviour change, being increasingly used in the development and evaluation of a broad range of interventions. The COM-B model therefore serves as an advantageous framework in understanding HIV/STI testing facilitators and barriers, offering invaluable insights to inform the development of tailored interventions.
The objectives of this review were to summarise interventions developed to increase HIV/STI testing among black communities in the UK, using the COM-B framework to describe the perceived barriers and facilitators of these interventions at both the participant-level and service provider-level.

## Methods
A scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute Framework of Evidence Synthesis and Preferred Reporting Item for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for scoping reviews guidelines. This review was adapted from a student dissertation project for an intercalated BSc, for which a protocol was drafted. Due to the nature of the review, the protocol was not registered.

## Search strategy and screening
A Population Intervention Comparison Outcome (PICO) framework ( ) informed the search strategy, and defined the inclusion and exclusion criteria ( ). Studies were included if they reported on the effectiveness of interventions to improve HIV/STI testing among black communities in the UK, in the English language, published from January 2000 onwards. This was to provide comment on current testing and technology guidelines. Authors of conference abstracts were contacted via email for full-text papers or reports and were included if sufficient information was extractable. Internal migration studies, those solely measuring anthropometric aspects of HIV/STI testing in the population and review papers were excluded.
 A systematic search of six databases (CINHAL, EMBASE, MEDLINE, PubMed, SCOPUS and Web of Science) using Medical Subject Headings terms, free text and combining terms related to the PICO, was performed in November 2023 ( ). Search results were imported into Endnote and references were de-duplicated by RK. Title, abstract and full-text screening were completed by RK and MG independently according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria ( ).
 Snowball searches in the references lists of relevant studies and review papers were undertaken by RK and MG to identify relevant primary publications. Discrepancies in the screening were resolved through discussion.

## Data extraction, synthesis and appraisal of quality
Data were extracted by RK and MG independently using a standardised extraction template and included: study aims and design; intervention characteristics (setting, participants, development, implementation); theoretical framework employed in intervention design; main findings (including quantitative and qualitative measures of effect) and conclusions/recommendations. Extracts were compared, with any differences in opinion resolved through discussion.
Due to the heterogeneity of the studies, the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool was used to appraise their quality. Studies available as abstracts or presentations only were not appraised due to the limited details available. Quality appraisal was undertaken solely to support the interpretation of findings, with no numerical scores assigned to studies ( ).
The intervention characteristics and outcomes for all included studies (full-text papers, abstracts and presentations) were summarised ( and ). Inductive thematic analysis was used to identify prominent themes, which were subsequently classified into the COM-B model’s six subcomponents (described above) for participants and service providers ( ). 
 

## Results

## Study characteristics
Twenty-two references met the inclusion criteria ( ); 11 were abstracts/presentation slides, nine were original research articles and two were reports. Four studies were published before 2010, 15 between 2010 and 2020 and three from 2020 onwards. Eleven studies were in Greater London, six in other UK cities, four UK wide and one England only. Study methodology varied; 13 studies were observational (quantitative descriptive), six mixed-method evaluations, two qualitative and one randomised controlled trial. Ten of the 11 studies with full-text papers were of high quality and one was of medium quality ( ). Five of the six quantitative studies had insufficient information to determine the risk of non-response bias.
Nineteen studies focused on HIV only, two on both HIV and STIs (chlamydia, gonorrhoea and syphilis) and one on STIs (chlamydia and gonorrhoea) only. Eleven studies specifically targeted people from black communities, while the remainder included individuals from black communities as part of interventions to increase testing among individuals at high risk of infections.

## Intervention development
In eight studies, intervention development was informed by service providers (healthcare and community organisations) only (one), service users/target populations only (five), or both service providers and service users/target populations (two) Input was gathered via focus group discussions, 1:1 interviews, service user feedback collected via a website, an incentivised service evaluation survey and the inclusion of representatives from the intervention settings in the study steering group. 
Service providers and service users/target groups identified actual and perceived barriers to testing that were subsequently incorporated into the design of the intervention. In two studies, contributors were invited to test or provide feedback on the intervention. Three studies had interventions explicitly informed by behavioural change models. 

## Intervention design
There were two main intervention types: testing provision and health promotion. Twelve studies included both testing provision and health promotion, six studies focused on testing provision only and four studies on health promotion only. 

## Testing provision
Testing consisted of HIV POCT (seven), HIV self-sampling (five), HIV self-testing (four) and STI self-sampling (three). HIV testing method was unreported in two studies. 
Sixteen studies evaluated testing provision in one setting type only: community settings including further education colleges (seven), online ordering platform (five), primary care (two), online sexual health service appointment (one) or vending machines (one). Three studies offered testing in two different settings types. 
In three studies, HIV testing was offered as part of wider health checks, such as migrant screening for infectious diseases (tuberculosis, HIV and hepatitis B and C) in primary care, new patient health checks in primary care and NHS health checks in community settings. 

## Health promotion
Sixteen studies consisted of health promotion interventions to increase knowledge of infections, understanding of risk and awareness of opportunities for testing. Reported methods included community outreach (three), and local and national media (print/broadcast/social) campaigns (five). 
Four studies adopted more direct approaches to health promotion such as during 1:1 consultations (one), small group sessions (one) or targeted text (SMS) messages (two). Four studies implemented a multifaceted approach involving local stakeholders, fora and media channels to both promote intervention and provide health education. 

## Barriers and facilitators

## Capability
Limited knowledge about infections, sexual health services and testing interventions was a psychological capability barrier reported by both participants and service providers. Inadequate involvement of local communities in the dissemination of educational and promotional materials, alongside the provision of English-only resources, contributed to the low awareness and understanding among participants for some interventions. In contrast, interventions with extensive public awareness campaigns (health education and testing access information) distributed via community organisations and leaders, local media channels, peers and social networks were considered to be effective at increasing knowledge in the target populations. 
The lack of HIV expertise in testing, treatment and communicating HIV test results described by non-specialist service providers in primary care and community settings contributed to their lack of confidence to offer HIV POCT testing services. Providers in community and primary care settings reported confidence and subsequent willingness to deliver testing for interventions that included skills-based training, education and robust referral pathways. 
Technical support provision, guidance on self-sampling, delivered to testing intervention participants, were physical and psychological capability facilitators of testing. These resources helped address challenges such as inexperience in using the kits, lack of skills and limited access to technical support, which reduced readiness to use the HIV self-sampling testing interventions. 
Service provider and user involvement in the intervention design informed the content, framing and dissemination of health messages and development of other resources needed to enhance psychological and physical capability. Insights gained through engagement activities contributed to the development of interventions that were perceived as culturally appropriate and relevant to both service providers and target users.

## Opportunity
Stigmatisation, due to interventions directly targeting individuals from black communities, was identified as a social opportunity barrier to testing. Failure to provide rationale for targeting people from black communities in one intervention resulted in feelings of being pigeon-holed and unfairly singled out. Concerns regarding stigma were also linked to the intervention setting and provider-patient relationship; people from black communities were reluctant to accept testing within sexual health services due to associated stigma. Black communities expressed willingness to access testing delivered by trusted providers, such as primary care professionals or community organisations where pre-existing relationships helped destigmatise STI and HIV. HIV POCT interventions delivered in primary care and community settings were perceived as more accessible and acceptable to the target populations when compared with testing delivered in sexual health services. 
Testing interventions integrated into existing services, for example, new patient health checks, migrant screening and routine appointments, were reported to facilitate testing for individuals who were first-time testers, unlikely to visit sexual health services or did not identify themselves at risk of infection. This was perceived to help normalise testing. Health education and awareness campaigns, along with interventions delivered through local individual and group partnerships, also contributed to the normalisation of STI and HIV testing. 
Participants’ decisions to test were reportedly influenced by the mode of testing, with barriers related to the waiting times for results from self-sampling kits, and need for confirmatory tests for POCT. The provision and delivery of self-sampling and self-testing kits through a range of mechanisms, for example, postal services, click and collect and vending machines, were particularly valued for their convenience, accessibility, privacy and confidentiality. 
The experiences of the target groups gathered as part of the intervention development identified trusted non-traditional settings and novel approaches to the testing delivery overcoming opportunity barriers related to stigma and access. 
Environmental opportunity barriers perceived to limit intervention reach included inadequate intervention targeting, as a result delivery in settings infrequently accessed by people from black communities, lack of IT systems to identify individuals from black communities (based on ethnicity and/or country of birth) and inability to establish partnerships with black community organisations. Increased consultation time, limited staff capacity and difficulties recruiting personnel to deliver interventions were environmental opportunity barriers identified in primary care and in the delivery of HIV POCT in the community, raising concerns about their impact on the delivery of other services. 

## Motivation
Low perceived risk of infection, related to beliefs that HIV is an infection of ‘others’ and a lack of knowledge about the signs and symptoms of infection, influenced decisions regarding the acceptance of testing. Fears of testing positive was often reported as a barrier for black communities in accessing and accepting testing. The ability to access immediate support from trusted service providers at the time of testing was viewed as a facilitator of testing. 
The concerns of community and primary care providers regarding their lack of expertise in managing high-risk cases and delivering results were cited as motivational barriers to offering testing. Providers voiced reluctance to offer POCT, due to concerns that service users were potentially being pressured to test and were not psychologically ready for the results. Providers were worried that testing policies based on ethnicity had the potential to offend service users, and felt uneasy about targeting groups based exclusively on ethnicity. 
Interventions that offered information about STIs/HIV, addressed misconceptions regarding risks and consequences and enhanced confidence in self-sampling played a key role in motivating individuals to access testing. 
Non-invasive or self-sampling testing methods were more acceptable to the target population, seen as less embarrassing than traditional testing methods. Additionally, testing conducted outside of sexual health services (in community settings, primary care or via online platforms) was viewed as increasing privacy, alleviating concerns of being seen or judged for accessing sexual health services. 
The opportunity to provide health education and offer testing to high-risk groups who otherwise did not access sexual health services motivated service providers to deliver interventions. Their confidence and willingness in offering testing was increased through education and training provision, resources and the implementation of referral pathways to specialist services. 

## Discussion
This scoping review provides an overview of interventions implemented in the UK since 2000 to increase STI and HIV testing in people among black communities, using the COM-B model to explore the facilitators and barriers that influence testing uptake. Research in this area has increased over the past 15 years, with notable emphasis on interventions to enhance access to HIV testing.
The application of the COM-B model provided a coherent framework for understanding facilitators and barriers experienced by people from black communities and service providers regarding various testing interventions. This review underscores the limitations of interventions focused solely on one aspect of behaviour change, such as access to testing (environmental opportunity) or health promotion (capability), as they overlook the interplay of capability, opportunity and motivation that influences behaviour.
The exploration of participants and service providers experiences offers practical insights for intervention planning and design. This review highlights the need for multifaceted testing interventions that educate individuals about infections and testing (capability), ensure privacy for testers (motivation) and normalise testing through integration into trusted existing services delivered by appropriately trained staff (opportunity). The review also identifies the importance of including black communities, local community and health sector organisations in all stages of intervention design and implementation. This is necessary to develop interventions that are acceptable to both communities and providers. 
This review identifies clear gaps in the evidence base which are areas for future action. Over the past two decades, interventions targeted at people from black communities have primarily centred on HIV testing. This emphasis likely reflects advances in HIV testing technologies and guidelines advocating for expanded testing provision beyond sexual health services and antenatal clinics, driven by the ambition to achieve HIV elimination. Our review highlights the necessity for interventions aimed at increasing both STIs and HIV testing. Evidence-based practice, combined with stakeholder insight, is crucial to the design of testing interventions. Half of the interventions included in this review were solely available as conference abstracts, with the majority of study designs being case studies of small-scale, time-limited pilots of diverse and innovative interventions. The lack of evaluations presented as project reports or research articles represents a missed opportunity to understand and learn from the experience of others.
Misconceptions about the infections, individual susceptibility and eligibility and opportunities for testing and treatment have previously been reported as barriers to testing, highlighting the importance of health promotion as a key component for any testing intervention. Local communities have a role to play in the design of the wider health promotion interventions to ensure messages are culturally appropriate, relevant, trusted and mitigate further stigmatisation. Additionally, delivering health promotion interventions through local organisations, involving local partnerships and community leaders, proves effective in increasing awareness and prompting action within communities. Other more novel approaches to health promotion, such as SMS messages and digital media interventions, hold promise for youth engagement and should be considered as part of a wider suite of health promotion initiatives. 
The expansion of self-sampling and self-testing services has helped overcome barriers to in-person testing reported by black communities. While testing uptake is high, completion and return of samples or reporting of self-test results are often suboptimal, possibly attributed to perceived inability to use the kits correctly or anxieties related to testing outcomes. Preferences for in-person testing have been described for people from black communities, yet stigma and privacy concerns continue to hinder willingness to access testing in sexual health services. Community organisations and primary care are trusted providers of health promotion and interventions, serving as acceptable settings for STI and HIV testing interventions, for both staff and black communities. The provision and integration of testing into existing services and programmes may have the potential to normalise STIs and HIV testing and overcome barriers to online testing. This includes lack of access to support and counselling, as well as concerns regarding perceived stigma and the risk of being ‘outed’ when testing in sexual health services. Additionally, sexual health services face increasing pressure due to funding cuts and surges in service demands. Interventions delivered in alternative settings have the potential to be financially sustainable, while reducing demand on sexual health services. Such interventions must however be co-designed with local services to achieve seamless integration with sexual health services and ensure appropriate and timely access to care. Sufficient staffing levels, and provision of comprehensive skills and knowledge-based training for staff are therefore essential to uphold the credibility of interventions, alongside willingness of services and staff to deliver them.
This review is strengthened by its comprehensive search strategy, including the review of citations and follow-up with authors to obtain project reports. The inclusion of conference abstracts guaranteed a more complete understanding of the range of testing interventions implemented in the UK since 2000. However, this approach may pose limitations, as information and insights surrounding the facilitators and barriers experienced with those interventions were limited. The review included studies not exclusively focused on black communities, and for some studies, it is unclear to what extent the facilitators and barriers identified in those studies were experienced by this demographic. The review was not limited to papers reporting on facilitators and barriers to testing, potentially leading to under-reporting of key challenges experienced by people from black communities. Additionally, the COM-B model focused on individual behaviour change, providing limited exploration of wider structural factors necessary for long-term change, which are often more challenging to address.

## Conclusions
The current review provides valuable insights for the development of future interventions necessary to reduce the burden of infections in black communities, and to achieve zero HIV transmissions by 2030. Multifaceted interventions, co-designed with and by local communities, are crucial to address the range of barriers and facilitators experienced by these communities. Local organisations and leaders play a vital role in these efforts, emphasising the importance of local partnerships in delivering effective testing interventions. The intention to end new transmissions of HIV by 2030 provides a clear focus for actions to enhance early HIV diagnosis and treatment. Similar attention and prioritisation of interventions for STIs is therefore essential in reversing the rising tide of infections.
