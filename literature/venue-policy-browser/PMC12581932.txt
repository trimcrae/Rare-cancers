## ABSTRACT

## Introduction
Alcohol‐related user‐generated content (UGC)—created by influencers and peers—appears to play a central role in shaping young people's alcohol‐related attitudes and behaviours. However, the nature and extent to which UGC facilitates the promotion of alcohol remains under‐examined. There is limited evidence on how alcohol companies utilise social media platforms to engage users and incentivise the promotion of their brands and products. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the nature and extent of alcohol promotion via UGC on Instagram and TikTok posts.

## Methods
This content analysis examined 1848 public user‐generated Instagram (n = 1148) and TikTok (n = 700) posts, focusing on major alcohol tags and hashtags over a 7‐year period. Posts were systematically coded for account type, sentiment, product placement and indicators of marketing (e.g., sponsorship or undisclosed marketing).

## Results
Of the posts analysed, 37% originated from influencers accounts. Five percent were identified as sponsored content, while 10% showed characteristics of undisclosed marketing. Half featured alcohol as the main focus, with 64% including branded products. Most posts (78%) expressed pro‐brand sentiment, 53% showed positive drinking experiences and 20% portrayed camaraderie. Only 2% contained anti‐brand sentiment or depicted negative consequences of alcohol consumption.

## Discussion and Conclusions
The disparity between disclosed (5%) and suspected undisclosed promotional content (10%) highlights transparency and regulatory issues. UGC predominantly carried a celebratory tone, reinforcing a pro‐alcohol narrative that influences young users' attitudes towards drinking. Stronger regulatory frameworks, independent monitoring and content transparency tools are urgently needed to mitigate young people's exposure to covert alcohol promotion.

## Introduction
Young people are frequently exposed to alcohol‐related content on social media platforms [ , ] and such exposure has been associated with earlier onset of drinking, increased alcohol consumption, intent to drink and change in alcohol attitudes [ , , , , , , , ]. Excessive alcohol consumption remains a major public health issue, particularly among young people. Young people who begin drinking at an early age are at a significantly higher risk of developing alcohol dependence and experiencing serious injuries [ , ]. Additionally, adolescence is a critical period for brain development, during which alcohol can have especially detrimental effects, potentially disrupting cognitive and emotional development [ ].
Recent studies have indicated that adolescents and young people are not fully aware of the influence that online marketing has on alcohol‐related harms [ , ]. This is likely to be more evident with user‐generated content (UGC). Alcohol‐related UGC encompasses content created by individuals that highlights alcohol consumption, endorsements, or drinking culture. This includes self‐created posts (e.g., sharing personal experiences with alcohol), peer‐generated content (e.g., posts shared by friends or followers that others interact with) and influencer‐generated material, such as promotional posts, paid partnerships or casual posts. Emerging research indicates that UGC has a major influence on young people, which is thought to be attributed to observing peers (or influencers) and linking alcohol use with positive feelings, attitudes and behaviours [ , , ]. Alcohol brands leverage users, who act as unofficial brand ambassadors, through incentivising organic, peer‐to‐peer UGC to increase brand awareness [ ].
Influencer marketing has also rapidly evolved as a primary strategy for alcohol and other unhealthy commodity brands [ , ]. Influencers, defined as ‘individuals who amass large followings on social media and exert significant influence over their audience through engaging content’ [ , p. 1], generate appealing and relatable content that normalises alcohol consumption, blending advertisements seamlessly into everyday life [ ]. This tactic amplifies reach and engagement, subtly reinforcing positive brand associations and influencing social norms around drinking [ ]. The line between paid media and UGC is often blurred [ ], as is the distinction between a digital creator with a large following and an influencer making it quite challenging to detect [ ]. One may use their platform to explicitly or implicitly shape audience decisions, while the other might focus solely on content creation without intending to influence purchasing behaviour.
There are numerous social media platforms that vary in format, content style and user interactions. Instagram and TikTok have been highlighted as particularly influential in visual brand promotion [ , , ]. Instagram is a social media platform on which users create, post and share photos and short‐form videos. Instagram has risen rapidly in popularity since its global release in October 2010, with over 2 billion monthly active users in 2023 [ ]. The popularity of people creating content (i.e., UGC) on Instagram has changed the way alcohol is promoted on social media, and brands have adopted approaches to directly connect with users [ ]. This includes competitions to encourage brand tagging [ ]. Tagging (using the @ symbol, e.g., @canadianclubaus) and hashtagging (using the # symbol, e.g., #smirnoff) is used to increase the visibility of posts to brand accounts and other users [ ]. Posts containing a higher number of tags and hashtags may receive increased engagement through likes, comments and views [ ]. This has coincided with Instagram's new features to retain and engage young users, and to increase teenagers time spent on the platform [ , ]. This combination of alcohol brands using social media and social media adapting to young users is likely to have an impact on young people's exposure to the promotion of alcohol.
TikTok is also a social media platform where users create, post and explore short (typically less than 60 s) videos. It is reported that TikTok's carefully tailored algorithms make it the most addictive social media platform [ ]. TikTok has risen rapidly in popularity since its global release, with its global monthly active users rising from around 55 million in January 2018 to close to 700 million in July 2020 [ ], and recent figures suggest that there are now more than 1.5 billion monthly active users [ ]. However, TikTok is particularly popular among adolescents, with 41% of Australian teens aged 14–17 and 30% of those aged 12–13 being users [ ]. While TikTok's alcohol‐related guidelines and policies prohibit alcohol brands directly advertising or promoting alcohol‐related content [ ], alcohol advertising and promotion are taking place via influencers, UGC and other indirect promotion [ , , , , ]. This includes overt displays of product placement. For example, Guégan et al. [ ] found that 73% of TikTok posts by bartender influencers displayed alcohol brands. Numerous studies examining user sentiment on social media have also reported strong pro‐brand and pro‐alcohol messaging [ , ]. Therefore, UGC has been identified as a mode of alcohol promotion requiring further investigation [ ].
Both alcohol and social media are popular among young people in Australia. In Australia, where this study was set, per capita alcohol consumption is among the highest in the world [ ], with 31% of Australians aged 14 and older reporting engaging in risky drinking behaviour in 2022 [ ]. Similarly, social media penetration is substantial, with 16.8 million Instagram users aged 13 and above (representing 62.1% of the population) and 9.73 million TikTok adult users aged 18 and above (comprising 35.9% of the population in 2024 [ ]). Despite the large numbers of Australian young people that use TikTok and Instagram, there is very little data on how alcohol companies are leveraging these social media platforms to engage and encourage users to promote their brands and products.
1.1

## Current Study
The main aim of this study is to examine the nature and extent of alcohol promotion via UGC on Instagram and TikTok posts. Three research questions were addressed:RQ1: What are the reach and engagement metrics of the collected alcohol UGC?
RQ2: What is the prevalence of marketing disclosures and indicators of undisclosed alcohol promotion in UGC?
RQ3: How is alcohol depicted in UGC on Instagram and TikTok, with regard to sentiment, user experiences and product placement?

## Methods
2.1

## Research Design
This study analysed UGC on Instagram and TikTok for alcohol promotion, focusing on major alcohol tags and hashtags over a 7‐year period. Instagram and TikTok were chosen due to their popularity among young people and the platforms use of alcohol‐related hashtags and tags in UGC. This study was approved by Deakin University's Faculty of Health Human Ethics Advisory Group (HEAG‐H 161_2023).

2.2

## Research Team and Positionality
This study was informed by an interdisciplinary team with expertise in digital marketing and alcohol public health research, including youth drinking and alcohol policy. We acknowledge that our backgrounds, professional experiences and technological understandings shape our interpretations of UGC. As researchers of varying ages and experiences, we recognise that alcohol promotion on social media is embedded within broader cultural, commercial and platform‐specific dynamics. In conducting the content analysis, we remained attentive to the complex interplay between commercial interests, influencer strategies, platform algorithms and organic user expression.

2.3

## Search Strategy
The search strategy involved two methods: (i) brand tag searches on Instagram; and (ii) hashtag searches on Instagram and TikTok. Tagging (using the ‘@’ symbol) allows users to directly engage with brands by mentioning the official account in their posts (e.g., @gordonsginau). This can prompt brand interactions such as likes, comments, or shares, increasing a post's visibility. Tag searches were only conducted on Instagram, as TikTok prohibits official alcohol brand accounts under its content guidelines. Hashtags were used to identify relevant posts across both platforms. Hashtags make content discoverable to broader audiences and are commonly used to reference specific brands (e.g., #smirnoff, #smirnoffvodka).
To select relevant brands, we first identified the most popular types of alcohol among young Australians based on the National Drug Strategy Household Survey and the Australian Secondary Students' Alcohol and Drug Survey [ , ]: Beer and Spirits (including ready to drink (RTD) or pre‐mixed alcohol). We then used market share rankings to select the top eight RTD/spirits brands and top four beer brands in Australia [ , ], resulting in 12 brands in total. This amount of selected top brands ensured a manageable yet comprehensive dataset while still covering key industry players. RTD brands dominated the selection, reflecting their higher consumption rates among young people [ , ]. We identified 14 official brand accounts across these 12 brands for Instagram tag searches (see Table  ).
 To identify the most commonly used hashtags for these 12 alcohol brands, we reviewed brand websites, captions from brand Instagram posts and online advertising. This process yielded 21 brand‐related hashtag, 20 on Instagram (e.g., #canadianclubaus and #keepwalking) and 18 on TikTok (e.g., #vodkacruiser, #victoriabitter; see Table  ). Most hashtags were consistent across platforms, except for three: #canadianclubaus and #jimbeambourbon appeared only on Instagram, while #wildturkey was used on TikTok as a substitute for #wildturkeybourbon, which did not appear on that platform. To capture broader trends, an additional seven general alcohol‐related hashtags were included (e.g., #beer and #vodka). In total, data were collected from 14 alcohol brand tags on Instagram, 27 hashtags on Instagram and 25 hashtags on TikTok.
 
2.4

## Data Collection
All tagged posts were retrieved from the ‘Tagged’ section on each brand's official Instagram account. Hashtag searches were conducted using the ‘Top’ posts filter on both Instagram (now known as your ‘For You’ page) and TikTok to capture the most popular or trending UGC, which is most likely to generate high exposure and engagement. For each hashtag and tag search on each platform, the first 28 top posts by platform users—excluding posts by brand‐owned media accounts—were collected for analysis. This number was deemed feasible and accounted for the fact that some hashtags had limited content, helping to avoid skewing findings towards more frequently used hashtags. Posts were limited to the period between January 2017 and 2024. Most posts originated from 2023 to 2024, likely reflecting more current trends and platform engagement. Collection involved copying the URL links into an Excel spreadsheet. These URLs directed to UGC photos and videos, including Instagram Reels and short‐form videos. No media content was downloaded or screenshotted; visual and textual content was accessed directly via the URLs for coding. Instagram Stories, which disappear after 24 h, were excluded due to their limited visibility. Duplicate posts were deleted, and additional URLs were collected where necessary to meet the sample size. All searches were conducted with an Australian IP address and using clean web browsers by making a new user profile for each platform to reduce algorithmic influence. This approach aligns with prior studies on hashtag‐based content analysis [ , ]. For each post, we recorded metrics including posting date, likes, comments, views, shares and follower count of the content creator as a proxy for reach.

2.5

## Coding Variables
2.5.1

## Reach and Engagement
Drawing on prior studies of sentiment, product placement and influencer content [ , ], each post was systematically coded for key variables aligned with our study aim and research questions (see Table  for the codebook). First, views, likes, comments and shares were recorded to gauge engagement; thereby informing the study's aim of understanding the reach of these alcohol‐related posts.

2.5.2

## Type, Source and Prominence
Second, we identified sponsorship or paid content by noting any explicit disclosure (e.g., ‘#ad’, ‘#sponsored’ and ‘#paidpartnership’) or official brand labelling, as well as brand‐specific hashtags or references to promotions or competitions—thus fulfilling the aim of examining the extent of brand‐driven UGC. Where content creators have been paid to post the content, the content creators who posted these videos were classified as a ‘paid’ brand account; the type of content creator (e.g., influencer) and their number of followers recorded. This provided information about the actions of brands in encouraging the creation of UGC and of the possible reach of these videos.
Third, the profile of each content creator was accessed to determine the type of account and assess for indicators of commercial affiliation or promotion. Accounts were categorised as: (i) personal (individual users with no promotional focus); (ii) business (e.g., pub, bar or bottle shop accounts); or (iii) influencer (e.g., bartenders, alcohol reviewers, lifestyle/travel influencers, celebrities or sportspersons). Influencer accounts were identified based on factors such as high follower counts, verified status, professional branding, or a pattern of sponsored content. Follower counts were also recorded to assess possible reach, aligning with RQ1. Where strong brand presence was evident without formal disclosure, we coded for potential undisclosed marketing, contributing to our understanding of influencer marketing practices. These posts were flagged if they displayed prominent branding, frequent brand mentions, or influencer‐like behaviour/accounts (e.g., discount codes, brand tagging in multiple posts, a history of alcohol‐related posts/or an alcohol focused account [e.g., bartenders or alcohol reviewers] or a large following) without a formal disclosure label (e.g., #sponsored). UGC stimulated by alcohol promotions, campaigns or competitions were also identified by brand‐specific hashtags (e.g., #BrandChallenge), direct references to ‘enter to win’, mentions of brand‐led campaigns or venue price promotions (e.g., bars, pubs and bottle shops). Fourth, we noted alcohol presence, branding elements and product images to explore the nature and features of UGC (e.g., whether it resembled traditional advertising).

2.5.3

## Nature and Sentiment
Fifth, overall sentiment towards the brand was coded as pro‐brand, anti‐brand or neutral, based on textual and visual cues (e.g., captions, emojis or hashtags), providing insight into public attitudes. Depiction or sentiment towards alcohol was identified using the categories listed below based on a previous analysis of UGC on Instagram and TikTok content [ , ]. These categories provided basic classifications of user‐generated videos/photos that involve alcohol:Irrelevant: Videos/photos that do not relate to the hashtag or brand outside the use of the hashtag.
Pro‐brand: All videos/photos with an indication that the user was paid to post the content, as well as content portraying a positive attitude towards the brand or products (e.g., through celebratory video content or positive text, emojis or hashtags in the caption that praise the brand).
Anti‐brand: Videos/photos portraying a negative attitude towards the brand or products (e.g., through video content or negative text, emojis or hashtags in the caption that contain disparaging language, negative or comedic mocking of the brand).
Neutral: Videos/photos that relate to the hashtag or brand but do not portray a positive or negative attitude towards the brand or products (e.g., no overt positive language that promotes/praises the brand nor anything negative/disparaging).

Lastly, the nature of posts was coded based on prior analyses of user‐generated content, with separate codes applied for positive experiences (e.g., drinking enjoyment, having fun), camaraderie (e.g., friendship, familiarity, closeness and party scenes) and negative experiences (e.g., hangovers, physical injuries and economic consequences) [ ].

2.6

## Data Analysis
Descriptive statistics were used to determine the extent of alcohol branding and product placement in posts; the distribution of sentiment towards alcohol and the nature of video/photo content; the average number and range of views, likes, number of comments and shares per post.

2.7

## Interrater Reliability
The first 250 posts were cross coded by two researchers (A.C. and N.L.) across several hashtags and tags that included video‐based and image‐based posts on Instagram. Percentage agreement was calculated for each code, with all codes achieving an inter‐coder agreement of at least 80%. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion (A.C., N.L. and M.D.), and the codebook was refined accordingly. The remaining posts were coded by A.C., with regular meetings held between A.C. and M.D. to review coding decisions and ensure consistency.

## Results
3.1

## 
RQ1: Alcohol‐Related Posts' Reach and Engagement
A total of 1848 posts were collected (28 posts per hashtag/tag), 1148 on Instagram and 700 on TikTok. The number of followers of included brand accounts ranged from 5507 (@gordonsginau) to 529,000 (@johnniewalker) (Table  ). The range of the number of followers of Instagram hashtags varied from about 1000 (#vbbeer) to approximately 85.8 million (#beer), while on TikTok, hashtags ranged from 47 (#vbbeer) to 11.9 million (#gin) (Table  ). Posts received a median of 57,900 views (TikTok video posts only), 318 likes, eight comments and 36 shares (Table  ). In the study, the majority of posts were made in 2023 and 2024, comprising 74% (n = 1362) of the total. Specifically, 42% of posts were from 2024 (n = 772) and 32% from 2023 (n = 590).
 
3.2

## 
RQ2: Type, Source and Prominence of Posts
Thirty‐seven percent of posts were from accounts deemed as influencers (n = 685); 40% were from individual/personal accounts (n = 742) and 23% were from business accounts (n = 420). Of the 1848 posts, 5% were identified as sponsorship or paid posts (n = 88), indicated by the inclusion of an ‘official’ label/paid partnership banner or hashtags indicating as such (e.g., #ad, #spon, #collab or similar). Ten percent (n = 180; Table  ) were identified as potential undisclosed marketing, indicated by heavy product placement, history and nature of user account behaviour such as having previous alcohol partnerships, being an alcohol review account, bartender/alcohol review account, being a verified account and the number of followers. More than half of the posts (n = 951) had alcohol as the main focus of the post (e.g., alcohol reviews, alcohol memes), followed by 21% (n = 392) that had alcohol clearly visible in the foreground, and 16% not visible (n = 293). Three percent of Instagram posts were liked by alcohol brands (n = 58), and 3% were both liked and commented on by an alcohol brand (n = 48).

3.3

## 
RQ3: Nature and Sentiment of User‐Generated Content
Sixty‐four percent of posts included branded alcohol product/s (n = 1182) and 30% included only branding element/s such as logos (n = 544). Alcohol brand engagement, including stimulating the generation of content through the use of hashtag challenges, was present in 3% of posts (n = 47) while price promotions, including promotion of regular prices and promotions of limited‐time offers, were included in 1% of posts (n = 21). The majority of posts had pro‐brand sentiment (n = 1444; 78%), while 2% of posts had anti‐brand sentiment (n = 38). Fifty‐three percent of posts showed positive experiences with drinking (n = 980) while 19% showed camaraderie, including party posts (n = 353). Thirteen percent of posts portrayed humour (n = 226; Table  ). Intoxication was present in 4% of posts (n = 80), while the consumption of multiple drinks was in 9% of posts (n = 173). Negative consequences, including alcohol dependence and alcohol‐related harm, were present in 2% of posts (n = 39). Well‐known celebrities were in 2% of posts (n = 43) while well‐known sportspeople were included in 1% of posts (n = 25; Table  ).

## Discussion
4.1

## Principal Findings
This study aimed to examine the nature and extent of alcohol promotion via UGC on Instagram and TikTok. We found that alcohol UGC using brand hashtags and tags has an overt pro‐brand sentiment (78%), with a majority including branded products (64%). We examined the prevalence of paid posts on Instagram and potential undisclosed marketing across both platforms and found a greater proportion of suspected undisclosed marketing (10%) compared to sponsored posts (5%). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically examine a broad range of both Instagram and TikTok major Australian alcohol brand‐related tags and hashtags for sentiment and marketing elements and identifying undisclosed marketing. Previous studies have analysed sentiment or portrayals within specific alcohol‐related hashtags, focusing on either Instagram [ ] or TikTok [ , , ], while others have centred on influencer posts and the presence of marketing disclosures [ ].
An important finding of this study was the relatively small percentage of disclosed sponsored posts (5%) compared to the greater proportion of suspected undisclosed promotional content (10%). The literature lacks a comprehensive outline of potential indicators for undisclosed marketing on social media, beyond general associations with influencers, analysis of engagement metrics and overt displays of pro‐brand sentiment [ , ]. This form of marketing has substantial influence; however, as previous research indicates, establishing direct commercial links between influencers and brands is challenging given the transparency and accountability issues from the platforms, influencers and alcohol brands themselves [ ]. Hendriks et al. [ ] found that posts with sponsorship disclosures yielded fewer engagement metrics such as likes and comments than posts without such disclosures, highlighting the potential incentives by influencers to not disclose partnerships. This lack of transparency hinders efforts to discern genuine user content from commercially driven posts. Moreover, the fact that genuine content often serves as a form of brand promotion—evidenced by the overt presence of pro‐brand sentiment in 78% of posts—underscores the persuasive impact of UGC more broadly.
Alcohol‐related UGC on social media predominantly conveyed alcohol consumption as a positive and social experience. More than half of posts depicting positive drinking experiences and one‐quarter showcasing camaraderie, such as party scenes. Branding elements were also common, with three‐fifths of posts including branded products. The emphasis on positive experiences and brand endorsements further illustrates a dominant pro‐alcohol narrative. This finding aligns with previous research where pro‐alcohol sentiment including positive experiences and humour were highly prominent [ ], particularly among influencer and commercial accounts with vested interests in the promotion of alcohol [ ]. This alignment of alcohol with positive social experiences has been found to influence young users' attitudes towards drinking to increased consumption [ , , ]. This type of peer‐to‐peer promotion, including by influencers, has also been shown to be more engaging than direct paid posts [ ], encouraging users to associate positive experiences with specific brands, particularly among young people who are highly susceptible to social media influence [ ].
In addition to branded and promotional content, the findings also provide a snapshot for UGC featuring intoxication and negative consequences, with 9% of posts showing multiple drinks consumed, 4% explicitly displaying intoxication and 2% displaying negative consequences. The relatively high amount of posts depicting multiple drinks consumed and intoxication is notable, particularly when accounting for the fact that Snapchat has been highlighted in previous research as the primary social media platform where young people post heavy consumption and negative consequences [ , ]. This has been attributed to the increased privacy and ephemeral nature of posts [ , ]. Intoxication and binge drinking were often framed as positive or humorous experiences, a trend also noted by Russell et al. [ ] who also observed a scarcity of posts depicting negative consequences.

4.2

## Implications for Policy and Practice
This study underscores the significant impact of alcohol promotion through UGC on Instagram and TikTok, highlighting the public health risks, regulatory challenges and the need for proactive measures from platforms and policymakers. Online alcohol advertising in most countries globally is currently poorly regulated, often relying on industry‐self regulation, which is commonly undermined by the industries themselves [ , ]. In countries like Australia, alcohol industry self‐regulation has proven to be ineffective, underscoring a need for a strong regulatory framework capable of establishing and enforcing stringent advertising standards [ ]. The Évin Law in France and the Alkoholloven in Norway are examples of strong regulatory frameworks that have resulted in population‐wide decreases in alcohol consumption [ , , ]. However, social media marketing has presented its own challenges. Both countries have encountered issues with violations among alcohol brands and affiliated influencers on social media [ , ]. Ultimately, our findings on the prevalence of undisclosed marketing across platforms, alongside the pervasive pro‐brand sentiment of drinking experiences, reveal a powerful, hidden form of alcohol promotion.
Social media platforms must be held accountable for the promotion of alcohol‐related content and the lack of transparency. There is a notable conflict of interest between social media companies' pursuit of profits and public health that requires regulation [ ]. The prominence of pro‐brand sentiment and branded products on TikTok highlights how poor these social media companies are at enforcing their guidelines. Regular audits and transparency tools by the social media platforms are essential to help platforms detect and label paid advertisements and UGC that may function as undisclosed advertising [ ]. Such tools could help young users distinguish between organic content and potentially harmful promotional content, allowing for more informed engagement and reducing the risk of covert influence on drinking behaviour. Understanding these dynamics would allow regulators to design targeted interventions to mitigate the impact of hidden marketing practices, ensuring that consumers, particularly younger people, are adequately protected from undue influence by alcohol promotions disguised as UGC. Research has found that the current privacy and advertisement settings on social media platforms including Instagram do not suppress exposure to alcohol‐related branded content [ ]. Regulating social media platforms processes largely controlled by algorithms is made more difficult as there is no international regulation regulating how digital consumer data is analysed or sold for commercial purposes [ ].

4.3

## Limitations
There are limitations to the study. The Instagram and TikTok algorithms influence the display of posts, particularly the ‘top posts’ search results, which can affect replicability. The search strategy and data collection methods were constrained by algorithmic limitations and the inability to access private accounts, whose posts could not be quantified. Furthermore, Instagram's and TikTok's algorithms and search functions are not transparent, which restricts researchers' ability to systematically and reliably retrieve consistent data. While the study aimed to capture recent trends by focusing on data from January 2023 onward, the time frame was expanded back to 2017 due to limited post availability for certain tags. This adjustment introduces variability, as trends and norms in alcohol promotion may have shifted over time, particularly pre‐ and post‐COVID‐19. Consequently, findings may reflect outdated practices rather than current ones, affecting the relevance of insights for 2023–2024. However, this was mitigated as the majority of the posts (74%) were within 2023–2024. Only a limited number of posts (first 28 per hashtag/tag) were analysed for each search term. This sampling approach may not be fully representative of the breadth of content related to alcohol promotion across the entire platform. However, unlike previous studies, we sought to broaden our scope across multiple brands. The reliance on manual selection and coding of posts also opens up potential for selection bias, even if unintentional.
The study infers undisclosed marketing based on factors like product placement, account type, follower count and history of posts. This indirect approach may not fully capture undisclosed marketing practices, as it relies on observable indicators rather than concrete evidence of brand partnerships. Some genuine UGC may be misclassified as marketing, or actual marketing posts may go undetected if they do not exhibit these traits. Although interrater reliability was reported as 80% or above for the first 250 posts, coding categories like pro‐brand or anti‐brand sentiment remain somewhat subjective; although there have been several studies that have used similar methods [ , , ]. The study focuses on trends among Australian young people and relies on hashtags and tags popular in Australia. However, trends in alcohol‐related UGC may differ internationally. Our findings may not apply universally to global audiences or other age demographics, limiting their broader applicability.

5

## Conclusion
This study contributes to the growing body of evidence on the nuances of alcohol promotion in UGC, particularly in an environment where there are blurred lines between UGC and paid promotion. By exploring the specific content features and patterns of engagement associated with undisclosed marketing, these findings can inform policies aimed at promoting transparency of alcohol marketing on social media platforms.
