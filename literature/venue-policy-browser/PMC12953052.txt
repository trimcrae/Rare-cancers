## Abstract

## BACKGROUND
Dementia stigma impedes timely diagnosis in low‐ and middle‐income countries. This study examines how cultural, social, and structural factors influence stigma in Malaysia's multiethnic context.

## METHODS
In‐depth interviews with 18 caregivers in urban and rural Malaysia were analyzed thematically using the Integrated Dementia Stigma Framework.

## RESULTS
Stigma forms a self‐reinforcing cycle. Families attribute symptoms to “madness” or normal aging, prompting concealment (micro). Cultural norms like filial piety prescribe family caregiving as a moral duty, reinforcing isolation (meso). Structural stigma, including clinical dismissal and service scarcity, validates family inaction and worsens urban–rural inequities (macro). Two interlocking pathways emerge: a legitimation pathway where medical dismissal confirms family hesitation and an entrapment pathway where cultural obligation and poor infrastructure trap caregivers.

## DISCUSSION
Dementia stigma in Malaysia drives low diagnosis rates. Multilevel interventions integrating public education, culturally appropriate community support, and healthcare reform are essential to break the cycle.

Keywords: Dementia, Integrated Dementia Stigma Framework, Klang Valley, Multilevel stigma cycle, Malaysia, Sabah

## INTRODUCTION
Over 60% of the 55 million people living with dementia reside in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), where an estimated 90% of cases remain undiagnosed, with stigma consistently identified as a key barrier to recognition and help seeking. Stigma prevents individuals from acknowledging symptoms and seeking the help they need to maintain a good quality of life, contributes to discrimination, and reinforces chronic underinvestment in dementia services. , 

Stigma is shaped by language, culture, religion, and broader structural conditions. In many communities, cognitive decline is framed as shameful or a moral failing rather than a medical condition. , , , , , Cultural norms such as filial piety in Chinese families , and birrul walidain in Muslim households reinforce expectations that dementia care should remain with the family. , , , , Understanding how stigma manifests in LMICs is therefore critical for designing effective interventions that promote timely diagnosis.
Studies of minority communities in Western countries demonstrate how cultural norms can influence dementia stigma even within well‐resourced healthcare systems. , , However, little is known about how these dynamics operate in LMICs, including Malaysia, where healthcare access, infrastructure, and cultural expectations differ substantially. , Additionally, with its multiethnic and multireligious population, Malaysia provides a valuable context in which to explore the intersecting dynamics that influence stigma in dementia.
Malaysia is experiencing rapid population aging, with over 300,000 people living with dementia and projections indicating that this number will rise to half a million by 2050. As the prevalence of dementia increases, stigma will continue to delay recognition, diagnosis, and help seeking, placing additional strain on families and an already resource‐constrained healthcare systems. Although dementia has gained recognition as a national public health priority, dementia‐related activities and services remain concentrated in urban centers, particularly Kuala Lumpur and Selangor. Existing studies consistently report poor public and professional knowledge, misconceptions of dementia as normal aging, stigma, uneven service provision, and limited care infrastructure. , , , , , , , , , However, these studies did not examine how cultural diversity, socioeconomic disadvantage, and rurality shaped stigma experiences and help seeking.
Sabah, one of Malaysia's poorest regions, has a population of 3.7 million, with the indigenous Kadazan‐Dusun as the largest group, followed by Chinese and Malays. , Around 50% of Sabah's population lives in rural areas, and the older population (aged 65 and over) has doubled over the past 6 years to 231,100. Despite rapid aging, far less is known about dementia in Sabah. This limited evidence base, combined with an underdeveloped health infrastructure, reflects Malaysia's rural‐urban divide and highlights the need for geographically sensitive research.
To address these gaps, this paper presents the findings from a qualitative study exploring dementia stigma among families in Klang Valley (urban district) and Sabah (rural region), examining how stigma influences access to dementia care, and support across ethnic communities.

2.

## METHODS
Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Research Ethics Board, Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia (National University of Malaysia) (Ref. JEP‐2024‐658).
2.1.

## Recruitment
Participants were recruited between September and December 2024 in Klang Valley and Sabah. Eligible participants were family caregivers of a person living with dementia, aged 18 or older. Paid carers were excluded. Recruitment was facilitated through a local dementia organization and care home provider in Sabah, and community networks and professional contacts in Klang Valley, with additional snowball sampling in Sabah. Eighteen participants were recruited (nine per site). In Klang Valley, participants included three Malays, three Chinese, and three Indians. In Sabah, the participants included five Kadazan‐Dusun, three Chinese, and one Malay. Written informed consent, including audio recording, was obtained from all participants. In Sabah, persons living with dementia were often present during the interviews but were not study participants. An incentive of RM90 (∼US$20) was offered for participation.

## RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: While a formal systematic review was not conducted for this study, a comprehensive scoping of the existing literature was performed to situate our research within the Malaysian context. This involved a targeted search of available evidence, which confirmed that the landscape of dementia research in Malaysia is limited and geographically concentrated. The majority of existing studies were conducted in the federal states of Kuala Lumpur and Selangor, with no published research identified from the Borneo state of Sabah. Critically, our scoping confirmed a significant gap in the literature: No study in Malaysia specifically investigated stigma in dementia. Furthermore, there is a clear absence of research that examines how stigma operates and interacts at individual, community, and structural levels in a multiethnic context. This study is, to our knowledge, the first in Malaysia to apply a comparative urban–rural design to explicitly investigate these multilevel interactions and address the identified research gap.

Interpretation: Our findings provide a novel, integrated analysis of dementia stigma in Malaysia, demonstrating how it functions as a self‐reinforcing system rather than a series of isolated barriers. Application of the Integrated Dementia Stigma Framework allowed us to systematically map stigma across individual (micro), community (meso), and structural (macro) levels. A contribution is the identification of two specific pathways – the legitimation pathway (where micro‐level doubt is validated by macro‐level healthcare dismissal) and the entrapment pathway (where meso‐level cultural norms are reinforced by macro‐level service scarcity) – that explain how different levels of stigma interact to perpetuate delay in help seeking and isolation. Additionally, our comparative design reveals that stigma is not a monolithic experience but is profoundly stratified by geography and socioeconomic status. We provide new evidence that structural factors, such as urban–rural health infrastructure inequities, are fundamental drivers that intensify individual‐ and community‐level stigma, particularly in under‐resourced states like Sabah.

Future directions: This study outlines several critical avenues for future research. First, longitudinal studies are needed to track how stigma evolves over the dementia journey and how the identified pathways impact long‐term caregiver wellbeing and patient outcomes. Second, research must intentionally include the perspectives of persons living with dementia, especially in the early stages, to provide a complete understanding of the stigmatization process. Third, the intersection of stigma

 with other social determinants, such as poverty, gender, and education, requires further investigation to identify the most vulnerable subgroups. Fourth, the Integrated Dementia Stigma Framework has facilitated a more indepth and naunced understanding of stigma experienced by people living with dementia and their families across multiple levels. Finally, and most urgently, implementation research is needed to develop and test targeted interventions designed to tackle the identified pathways. Specific research questions include:
Does training primary care doctors in rural areas to provide a validating, non‐ageist response effectively break the legitimation pathway and increase diagnosis rates?
Can community‐based support programmes, co‐produced with families, successfully reduce isolation and caregiver burden in tackling the entrapment pathway?
To what extent do multilevel campaigns increase awareness of dementia, leading to stigma reduction at all levels?

2.2.

## Data collection tool
A semi‐structured interview guide, informed by a need‐based approach and the Candidacy Framework, was used to explore experiences of symptom recognition, help seeking, and access to care. Prompts were used to examine how stigma shaped these processes, including labeling of dementia, anticipated and enacted stigma, concealment, and the influence of cultural norms, religious beliefs, and social expectations on help seeking and caregiving. The interview guide was adapted linguistically and culturally for use across ethnic groups. Interviews were conducted in participants’ preferred language (Malay, English, Chinese, or local dialects), using culturally meaningful terms to facilitate discussions.
Interviews lasted up to 60 min. In Sabah, all interviews were conducted in person by the first author (JNWL, a Sabahan). In Klang Valley, six interviews were conducted online and three in person, by ethnically matched researchers (NSG for Malay, CZT for Chinese, and TM for Indian carers).

2.3.

## Transcription and translation
All interviews were audio‐recorded, transcribed verbatim, and subsequently translated into English for analysis. Transcription and translation were undertaken by the interviewers, who are also members of the research team.

2.4.

## Data analysis
Data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis, following Braun and Clarke's approach. Analysis began with familiarization through repeated reading of the transcripts. Initial inductive coding was conducted to capture participants’ meanings and experiences without imposing predefined categories. Coding was undertaken by JNWL and TM, with regular discussion with the research team to refine codes. NVivo software was used to support data management and organization. Although some interviews in Sabah were conducted in the presence of person living with dementia, only data generated by caregivers were analyzed.
Following inductive coding, codes and preliminary themes were subsequently mapped onto the Integrated Dementia Stigma Framework. This approach facilitated a theoretically informed interpretation across multiple levels, that is, micro (individual and family), meso (community), and macro (structural) levels. This analytic process was both inductive and deductive: Themes emerging from the data were examined in relation to the framework, while the framework sensitized the analysis to cross‐level interactions without constraining theme development. All authors reviewed and reached a consensus on the final thematic structure and cross‐level analysis.

2.5.

## Research positionality and reflexivity
Reflexivity was actively maintained throughout the study. Researchers engaged in regular meetings to discuss and reflect on how their positionalities, disciplinary backgrounds, and prior assumptions about dementia and stigma might influence data generation and interpretation. Analytic decisions were discussed collaboratively to ensure that themes were grounded in participants’ accounts rather than researchers’ expectations.

2.6.

## Integrated Dementia Stigma Framework
Several theoretical frameworks have been developed to conceptualize and examine stigma. Link and Phelan (2001) conceptualized stigma as a dynamic process of labeling, stereotyping, separation, and discrimination. The health‐related stigma framework examines the temporal and relational dimensions of stigma, differentiating anticipated stigma (e.g., fear of judgment), enacted stigma (e.g., overt discrimination), and internalized stigma (e.g., self‐blame). , , Perspectives on structural and systemic stigma emphasize how discrimination is institutionalized through policies and resource allocation and practices across healthcare and social services. In LMICs and among ethnic minorities, these frameworks have been applied to explore the influence of cultural norms, values, and social meanings on the experience of stigma, , , , , where the reality of lived experiences, influenced by intersectional factors, is neglected. To enable examination of how everyday beliefs and family practices are interacting with cultural norms and healthcare systems to produce self‐reinforcing cycle of stigma affecting dementia recognition, help seeking, and care, a multilevel framework is needed. Through the integration of the three complementary theoretical perspectives, the Integrated Dementia Stigma Framework offers a framework to analyze stigma across micro (individual and family), meso (community and social networks), and macro (structural and systemic) levels (Figure  ).
 In this study, we apply the Integrated Dementia Stigma Framework to examine a multilevel process of stigma experienced by families living with dementia. A multilevel stigma framework is particularly suited to the Malaysian context, where dementia experiences are shaped by the intersection of ethnicity, religion, family structures, and uneven healthcare provision. In a multiethnic and multireligious society, stigma is not only produced through individual beliefs but also reinforced by community norms around caregiving and by structural inequities in access to diagnosis and care, especially between urban and rural regions. A multilevel approach allows these intersecting influences to be examined together rather than in isolation.

## FINDINGS
Of the 18 caregivers/participants, 15 were women, the majority of whom were daughters (Table  ). Most participants were caring for female relatives with dementia. Significant disparity in formal diagnosis was observed, whereby eight of the nine families in Klang Valley reported having a diagnosis, compared to only two in Sabah, which were discovered coincidentally during other health appointments. Furthermore, five families in Klang Valley had a healthcare background, while none in Sabah did.
 Applying the Integrated Dementia Stigma Framework, stigma was identified across all levels. The following sections first detail the barriers (Table  ), followed by findings from the cross‐level analysis.
 3.1.

## Micro level: individual and family dynamics
At the micro level, stigma emerged through personal beliefs and family interactions, shaping how dementia was initially understood and responded to within the household. These processes reflected internalized stigma and formed the foundation for later community and systemic responses.
3.1.1.

## Labeling dementia as madness or normal aging
All participants, including an Indian caregiver with a medical background, reported difficulty recognizing or accepting a dementia diagnosis. Framing the condition as madness like “chi zing” or “otak rosak,” generated fear and shame, prompting families to hide the symptoms. In contrast, dismissing it as normal aging or “nyanyuk” and “sakit tua,” minimized its urgency. This paradox cultivated a pervasive silence that delayed help seeking.

“In Chinese… we called it ‘chi dai zing'or ‘chi zing’ … It means crazy. The family is embarrassed; they keep it hidden.” (Chinese C2, Sabah)

“It was described as ‘nyanyuk’ (senile) … ‘sakit tua’ (old‐age illness).” (Malay P1)

“When it happened to a loved one, it was still shocking and difficult to accept … We thought it was normal aging …” (Indian P1, Klang Valley)

3.1.2.

## Misattribution and stereotyping
Many families frequently misinterpreted dementia symptoms as intentional behavior, attributing them to “laziness,” “stubbornness,” or “being evil.” This intensified interpersonal conflict, fueling frustration and blame. Families in Klang Valley often progressed to a medical understanding and received formal diagnosis, compared to a non‐diagnosis in Sabah due to persistent poor awareness.

“She is being lazy and stubborn, just lying in bed and refusing to get up unless she needed the toilet.” (Kadazan P4, Sabah)
“She shouted at me, called her ‘jahat’ (evil)… then we realized… it's the dementia.” (Malay P1, Klang Valley)
“We didn't recognize her stories of seeing a child in her room as symptoms. We thought she was just being defiant and refused to sleep in her room.” (Chinese P2, Klang Valley)

3.1.3.

## Internalized stigma and denial
Many participants internalized social stigma, which manifested as denial and avoidance. This emotional self‐protection delayed help seeking and normalized dementia as an unspoken burden rather than a health concern. This internalization rendered the condition invisible within households, perpetuating diagnosis delays.

“My father … forbade us to tell anyone.” (Chinese C3, Sabah)
“The biggest barrier was my own denial.” (Indian P3, Klang Valley)
“It's a taboo … They don't talk about it.” (Malay P1, Klang Valley)
“They refused to accept that she can no longer be independent.” (Chinese P1, Klang Valley)

3.2.

## Meso level: community and social relationships
At the meso level, stigma within social networks and cultural norms led to anticipated judgment, social withdrawal, and rigid caregiving expectations, which reinforced micro‐level stigma and increased isolation.
3.2.1.

## Anticipated stigma and concealment
Some participants feared that revealing a dementia diagnosis would trigger gossip or judgment. Anticipated stigma led families to actively conceal the condition, a defensive strategy as families withdrew from community support and delayed seeking professional care.

“… only our closest relatives knew about my grandmother's condition.” (Indian P3, Klang Valley)

3.2.2.

## Social withdrawal and isolation
Stigma of dementia often extended beyond the family, leading to community exclusion. Friends, neighbors, and religious peers withdrew from contact with the person living with dementia. Social withdrawal operates as both cause and consequence of stigma. In anticipation of society's reaction, families preemptively isolated themselves to avoid shame, while communities reciprocated by withdrawing support, creating a feedback loop of exclusion that intensified the caregiving burden.

“My mom … keeps herself in the house. She didn't want people to know her condition. Her temple friends don't want to visit her.” (Chinese P2, Sabah)

3.2.3.

## Cultural norms reinforcing stigma
Cultural values strongly shaped caregiving practices. For many participants, filial piety required families to keep dementia hidden to protect family honor, and caregiving was seen as the sole responsibility of the nuclear family. These norms reinforced silence and intensified caregiver burden, creating a situation where some caregivers felt trapped, unable to seek external help without violating cultural expectations.

“Because of filial piety … the family is embarrassed, so they don't say dementia.” (Chinese P2, Klang Valley)

“Only the immediate family can care … In‐laws are not expected.” (Kadazan P5, Sabah)

3.3.

## Macro level: structural and systemic barriers
Stigma was embedded in healthcare systems and institutional practices, creating structural barriers that reinforced stigma at other levels.
3.3.1.

## Healthcare dismissal
Many participants, especially in Sabah, reported that the doctors dismissed dementia symptoms as normal aging or misdiagnosed them, a reflection of institutionalized agism, where dementia was seen as an inevitable part of aging.

“When we went to clinic … they said she's already old, so leave it.” (Chinese P2, Sabah)

“The doctor discouraged assessment, saying she was too old.” (Malay P3, Klang Valley)

“All the doctors misdiagnosed mum as having schizophrenia.” (Chinese P1 and P2, Sabah)

3.3.2.

## Lack of health services
A critical shortage of specialists and services, particularly in Sabah, left families without a formal diagnosis or support. These families faced major logistical barriers to accessing healthcare and care support. The lack of visible, accessible care reinforced community beliefs that dementia was not a medical issue but a private family problem that was left undiagnosed.

“Where? Which doctor can we go to?” (Kadazan P4, Sabah)

“Don't know any specialists … people don't know where to go. Mum was prescribed anti‐psychotic medicine by a primary care doctor.” (Chinese P3, Sabah)

3.3.3.

## Inadequate facilities
Available care facilities were often under‐resourced or misaligned with dementia care needs or prohibitively expensive, failing to provide support even when accessed.

“Many of the dementia facilities are in name only … They give food but don't look after people with dementia.” (Chinese P1, Klang Valley)

“There is only one dementia care home in Sabah, and they have limited places.” (Chinese P1, Sabah)

3.4.

## The vicious cycle of stigma: a cross‐level analysis
Our analysis demonstrates that stigma at the micro, meso, and macro levels did not operate in isolation but interacted to form a vicious, self‐reinforcing cycle that hindered help seeking and perpetuated dementia stigma among families in our study. Two interconnected pathways emerged from the data.
3.4.1.

## Pathway 1: Legitimation pathway – from family doubt to medical validation of inaction
This pathway began at the micro level with a family's initial interpretation of symptoms of dementia as normal aging. This misinterpretation was then legitimized by a dismissive encounter with the macro‐level healthcare system.

Micro‐level trigger: Participants observed memory loss in loved ones and labeled it as “sakit tua” (old‐age illness) and thought that “it was just aging…” This created doubt about whether medical help was warranted.
Macro‐level validation: Seeking answers, some participants consulted the doctor about the symptoms. The doctor, reflecting systemic agism, dismissed their concerns, stating, “she's already old, so leave it.” Or “she is old, no need to put her through the examination.”

Resulting feedback loop: The macro‐level response from the doctor validated the micro‐level stigma. The participants’ doubt was transformed into a legitimate medical fact.
In this pathway, the participants’ initial hesitation was confirmed, and help seeking was decisively abandoned. The system's failure reinforced the belief that dementia was not a medical condition but an inevitable decline that ultimately perpetuated the cycle of delay in help seeking.

3.4.2.

## Pathway 2: Entrapment pathway – cultural mandates and systemic abandonment
This pathway illustrated how meso‐level cultural norms interacted with macro‐level neglect to intensify micro‐level caregiver burden and isolation.

Meso‐level pressure: Cultural norms of filial piety dictated that caregiving was a private, family duty, creating pressure to conceal the condition to protect family honor (“Because of filial piety … the family is embarrassed, so they don't say dementia”).
Macro‐level failure: The family sought support but encountered a void, a lack of specialists (“Not enough specialists…,” lack of dementia care services (“only one care home in Sabah”), and no clear referral pathway (“where do we see a doctor about dementia?”).
Micro‐level outcome: Trapped between cultural expectations that forbid disclosing the problem and a system that offers no viable solutions, the caregiver was left to cope completely alone. This was compounded by meso‐level social withdrawal (“The temple friends don't want to visit her”), intensifying the caregiver's burden and isolation.
The pathway resulted in entrapment, where the only culturally and structurally permissible option was to bear the burden silently and privately.
The discussed pathways demonstrated that stigma was cemented through the mutual reinforcement of everyday beliefs (micro), cultural norms (meso), and systemic failures (macro). Consequently, families did not merely face multiple barriers; they were caught in a vicious cycle where micro‐level fear, meso‐level pressure, and macro‐level neglect interacted to reinforce one another, resulting in inaction and intensified care burden.

4.

## DISCUSSION
Consistent with studies from both LMICs and Western contexts, , , , , we found that families’ initial interpretation of dementia symptoms served as a pivotal point in the help‐seeking process. The dual framing of dementia as either “madness” (prompting shame and concealment) or “normal aging” (diminishing urgent medical attention) creates a paradoxical situation for families. These findings echo global evidence on the denial and trivialization of dementia and is mirrored in local expressions of distress and caregiver burden. Notably, even participants with healthcare backgrounds struggled with these interpretations, highlighting the pervasive influence of sociocultural norms and the limited knowledge of dementia as a disease among healthcare professionals. , 

At the meso level, cultural values such as filial piety and family honor positioned caregiving as a private moral duty, discouraging disclosure and external help seeking, patterns consistent with cross‐cultural findings in other Asian communities. , , , Crucially, these norms did not merely coexist with stigma; they amplified its effects by making non‐disclosure the morally “right” choice, thereby intensifying caregiver burden and isolation.
Macro‐level structural stigma was most evident in Sabah, where dismissive healthcare responses and a severe lack of services legitimized family inaction. This systemic neglect validated community‐level misconceptions (meso level) and individual‐level denial (micro level).
Another key finding was the identification of two interlocking pathways – the legitimation pathway and the entrapment pathway – which illustrated how micro, meso, and macro factors interact to produce a vicious cycle of stigma. The legitimation pathway (micro → macro → micro) showed how families’ initial doubt (“just aging”) was validated by macro‐level ageist dismissal, transforming hesitation into medically endorsed inaction, resulting in non‐diagnosis. The entrapment pathway (meso → macro → micro) showed how meso‐level cultural norms (“keep it in the family”) combined with macro‐level service scarcity to trap caregivers in isolation and distress. Together, these pathways illustrate a mutually reinforcing cycle in which beliefs, cultural norms, and structures interact to perpetuate stigma and exacerbate caregiver burden.
Stark urban–rural disparities were clear: Nine of the 10 persons with dementia in Klang Valley had a formal diagnosis, compared to only two in Sabah. This gap highlights how poverty, underdeveloped infrastructure, and remote geography exacerbate stigma.
The presence of caregivers with healthcare backgrounds in Klang Valley, and their absence in Sabah, reveals that stigma is not uniformly spread out in society but is stratified by intersecting social structures like social position, education, and geography. In Klang Valley, caregivers with professional or familial exposure to healthcare systems possessed greater health literacy, social capital, and confidence in navigating medical institutions. In contrast, the absence of such resources in Sabah meant that stigma operated with fewer counter‐narratives. Here, limited education, lower socioeconomic status, and rural isolation intersected with cultural norms to entrench misattribution, concealment, and delayed help seeking. These findings highlight that dementia stigma is not experienced uniformly but is stratified by intersecting social structures – particularly class, education, and geography – that determine who can access knowledge, recognition, and validation. Therefore, addressing dementia stigma requires not only cultural and attitudinal interventions but also structural strategies that reduce socioeconomic and geographic inequities in health literacy and care access.
4.1.

## Implications for practice and policy
The findings of this study point to several implications:

National multilevel dementia awareness campaigns must actively counter harmful labels like “crazy/madness” “nyanyuk”) with medically accurate narratives.
Training for primary care doctors and nurses is critical to address ageist attitudes and break the cycle of structural stigma.
Community‐based support should be co‐produced with local stakeholders and families.
Policymakers must address the urban–rural inequity divide by investing in primary health clinics and dementia training for health workers in states like Sabah.

4.2.

## Strengths and limitations
A key strength of this review is the use of a theoretical framework to analyze stigma across multiple levels. The comparative design between an urban and rural site is novel for Malaysia. The research team's effort to ensure ethnic representation and to match interviewers to participants' ethnic backgrounds strengthened cultural sensitivity. A limitation is the sole inclusion of caregivers to the exclusion of the perspectives of persons living with dementia. Generalization to other parts of Malaysia may not be possible as experiences of other states may differ. Finally, the cross‐sectional design captures experiences at one point in time and may not reflect how stigma evolves throughout the dementia journey, and more research is needed to examine the intersectionality of socioeconomic factors on stigma.

5.

## CONCLUSION
Dementia stigma in Malaysia is a multilevel system. It begins with a label within the family, is amplified by community expectations/norms, and is cemented by systemic neglect. Further, the legitimation and entrapment pathways show how this system delays help seeking and diagnosis and perpetuates isolation. Tackling this issue requires coordinated, multilevel interventions that break these self‐reinforcing cycles.
