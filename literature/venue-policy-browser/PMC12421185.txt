https://orcid.org/0000-0002-6153-1273BoakyeDorothy Serwaa https://orcid.org/0000-0001-9308-6031AdjorloloSamuel In Ghana, where approximately 25 000 children and adolescents are living with HIV, the mental health burden is particularly concerning, with studies reporting high emotional difficulties, regardless of sociodemographic factors and perceived socio-economic status. The high mental health burden has been attributed to the interplay of multiple, complex factors, including biological factors, psychosocial stressors, stigma, medication challenges and disclosure concerns. 
Despite this significant burden, mental health interventions specifically designed for YPLHIV in Ghana and similar resource-constrained settings remain limited in availability, accessibility and cultural appropriateness. A critical barrier contributing to this gap is the severe shortage of trained mental health professionals in Ghana and other low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs). Ghana has approximately 0.1 psychiatrists and 1.4 psychiatric nurses per 100 000 population, far below the WHO-recommended minimum of 1 mental healthcare worker per 10 000 population. This workforce shortage is compounded by the concentration of available mental health professionals in urban areas, leaving rural and peri-urban communities, such as the Lower Manya Krobo Di,strict with minimal specialised mental health resources. The gap between the need for mental health support and available services is particularly pronounced in rural and peri-urban areas, where specialised mental health resources are scarce and HIV-related stigma often limits help-seeking behaviours. This treatment gap, exacerbated by the shortage of qualified professionals, undermines the potential for achieving optimal health outcomes in a population already facing significant vulnerabilities and challenges.
Multiple family group therapy (MFGT) represents a promising intervention that has demonstrated effectiveness in addressing mental health challenges among adolescent populations in various contexts. MFGT has been successfully implemented in several settings, including urban communities in the United States for children with conduct disorders and disruptive behaviour difficulties, and family group interventions have shown promise in African contexts, such as South Africa for families affected by HIV and Rwanda for YPLHIV. Additionally, preliminary implementations have been documented in other sub-Saharan African settings, including Uganda and Zimbabwe, for addressing mental health challenges among YPLHIV. By simultaneously engaging multiple families in therapeutic processes, MFGT offers potential advantages in resource-constrained settings by efficiently utilising limited professional resources while leveraging peer support mechanisms.17 
 20 However, despite its theoretical promise, few studies have examined the feasibility of implementing MFGT specifically for YPLHIV in sub-Saharan African contexts, where unique cultural, social and healthcare system factors may significantly influence implementation success. 
Although implementation science literature has identified general barriers to mental health intervention delivery in low-resource settings, including workforce limitations, financial constraints and stigma, there remains a critical gap in understanding the specific contextual barriers that might affect MFGT implementation for YPLHIV in Ghana. This gap is particularly significant, given that implementation success heavily depends on identifying and addressing context-specific barriers during the planning stages. Several additional MFGT processes require deeper contextual understanding: cultural adaptation of therapeutic content to align with Ghanaian family dynamics, implementation sustainability within local healthcare resource allocation systems, contextualising group facilitation approaches to respect cultural communication norms and power dynamics, tailoring therapeutic content to address Ghana-specific psychosocial stressors affecting YPLHIV and developing culturally appropriate evaluation metrics that reflect local conceptions of well-being. 
Triangulating perspectives from YPLHIV, caregivers and healthcare professionals is methodologically essential as each stakeholder group contributes unique resources to understanding MFGT implementation barriers. YPLHIV provide experiential knowledge about living with HIV and preferences for intervention formats, caregivers offer insights on family dynamics and resource constraints affecting participation and healthcare professionals contribute systemic knowledge about institutional capabilities and operational constraints. This multi-stakeholder approach reveals how barriers manifest differently across groups, identifies convergent concerns that require priority attention and uncovers potential implementation tensions, ultimately enhancing the acceptability and sustainability of interventions within the specific Ghanaian context. 
This study addresses these gaps by exploring the contextual barriers that could potentially affect the implementation of MFGT to address mental health challenges among YPLHIV in HIV-prevalent communities in Ghana. By engaging multiple stakeholders—YPLHIV, caregivers and healthcare providers—through focus group discussions (FGDs), this study aimed to identify and understand the multilevel factors that could influence implementation success. Findings from this study will contribute to implementation science literature by illuminating the complex interplay of factors affecting mental health intervention implementation in resource-constrained settings and will inform the development of contextually appropriate implementation strategies for MFGT and similar interventions targeting YPLHIV in Ghana and comparable settings.

## Theoretical framework
The study’s theoretical framework combines implementation science models with socio-ecological perspectives. The framework provides essential structure for understanding the complex, multilevel factors influencing intervention implementation in resource-constrained settings. At its foundation, the study on the consolidated framework for implementation research (CFIR), a comprehensive meta-theoretical framework that synthesises implementation theories to identify factors that might influence intervention implementation. The CFIR’s five major domains—intervention characteristics, outer setting, inner setting, characteristics of individuals and process —align closely with the study’s findings. The identified resource constraints reflect outer setting challenges, organisational instability corresponds to inner setting factors, while participation barriers relate to characteristics of individuals. This alignment with CFIR demonstrates the framework’s utility in structuring the investigation of implementation barriers in this specific context. This study focufour on our of the five CFIR domains: (1) intervention characteristics, examining how MFGT’s design features, such as group format and family involvement, might affect acceptability; (2) outer setting, investigating how community stigma, transportation infrastructure and socioeconomic factors influence accessibility; (3) inner setting, assessing how organisational culture, available physical facilities and resource allocation affect implementation capacity; and (4) characteristics of individuals, exploring how participant readiness, disclosure status and group participation comfort affect.
The study further enriches its theoretical foundation by incorporating socio-ecological perspectives, recognising that implementation barriers exist at multiple levels of the social ecology. This approach acknowledges that successful implementation requires addressing factors at individual (adolescent anxiety in groups), interpersonal (caregiver–adolescent dynamics), organisational (facility limitations), community (stigma concerns) and policy levels (healthcare system priorities). The socio-ecological lens helps explain why single-level interventions often fail and supports the study’s conclusion that multi-level, coordinated approaches are necessary.
This integrated theoretical framework provides a solid foundation for understanding the complex interplay of factors influencing MFGT implementation in Ghana. By combining established implementation science frameworks with socio-ecological perspectives, the study offers a nuanced theoretical lens for examining implementation challenges in resource-constrained settings and developing contextually appropriate implementation strategies.

## Methods

## Study setting
This study was conducted at Atua Government Hospital, located in the Lower Manya Krobo District in the Eastern Region of Ghana. This district has one of the highest HIV rates in Ghana, consistently reporting rates above the national average. The Atua Government Hospital serves as a major healthcare facility in the district, providing comprehensive HIV care services to approximately 200 YPLHIV. The hospital houses a dedicated adolescent HIV clinic that operates weekly, offering antiretroviral therapy, routine health monitoring and limited psychosocial support services. The facility was selected owing to its substantial YPLHIV population, established relationships with potential participants and the absence of formalised mental health interventions specifically targeting YPLHIV, making it an appropriate setting for exploring implementation barriers to MFGT.

## Study design
This study employed a qualitative exploratory design using a multi-stakeholder approach to capture diverse perspectives on potential barriers to MFGT implementation. Qualitative methodology was chosen for its ability to provide a rich, contextual understanding of complex phenomena such as implementation barriers. This qualitative study adheres to international reporting standards, with data reported following the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist ( ).

## Study participants and sampling
Three distinct stakeholder groups participated in this study: YPLHIV, caregivers of YPLHIV, and healthcare professionals involved in adolescent HIV care. Purposive sampling was used to recruit participants who could provide informed perspectives on potential implementation barriers based on their experiences with HIV care services at the Atua Government Hospital.
For YPLHIV participants, the inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) confirmed HIV-positive status, (2) age 12–22 years, (3) awareness of their HIV status, (4) receiving care at the Atua Government Hospital and (5) capable of providing assent with caregiver consent for those <18. Caregivers were eligible if they were the primary caregivers (for at least 6 months) for a YPLHIV who received care at the Atua Government Hospital. Healthcare professionals were included if they had at least 1 year of experience providing direct services to YPLHIV at the facility.
The final sample comprised 12 YPLHIV (aged 12–22 years), 13 caregivers and five healthcare professionals (ie, two nurses, one nurse/counsellor, one nurse/data manager and one social worker). This multi-stakeholder approach enabled triangulation of perspectives across groups with different roles in the potential implementation of MFGT.

## Data collection instruments
Two complementary data collection instruments were developed for this study: an FGD guide and an in-depth interview (IDI) guide. Both instruments were developed based on the CFIR and reviewed by experts in implementation science and adolescent HIV care.
The FGD guide included open-ended questions exploring potential barriers to MFGT implementation across five domains: (1) intervention characteristics (eg, What aspects of multiple family group therapy might be challenging to implement in this setting?), (2) outer setting (eg, What community factors might affect participation in the programme?), (3) inner setting (eg, What institutional factors might affect implementation?) and (4) characteristics of individuals (eg, What concerns might adolescents or families have about participating?). Please see for the guides for FGDs and IDIs for YPLHIV, caregivers and healthcare workers, respectively.
The interview guide contained similar domains but allowed for more in-depth exploration of specific implementation concerns from individual perspectives. The predominant language at the study location is Dangbe, necessitating careful linguistic adaptation of all study instruments. Following the WHO’s translation protocols, the research team implemented a comprehensive bidirectional translation process—converting materials from English to Dangbe and back—to preserve both cultural and conceptual accuracy across languages. The instruments were pilot-tested with two adolescents, two caregivers and one healthcare provider who were not included in the final sample, with minor refinements made based on their feedback. Modifications to the guide also allowed for the introduction of probes and prompts.

## Data collection procedures
Data were collected between February and March 2025 through a series of FGDs and IDIs. For YPLHIV, two FGDs were conducted (one with four participants and one with five participants), grouped to ensure age-appropriate discussions (12–16 and 17–22 years, respectively). For caregivers, two FGDs were conducted with 6–7 participants each. Healthcare professionals participated in one FGD with all five members.
Additionally, three IDIs were conducted with YPLHIV to achieve data saturation. During the iterative analysis process following the FGDs, the research team identified that certain individual-level barriers and personal experiences related to HIV status disclosure and psychological readiness for group participation required deeper exploration to reach theoretical saturation. These interviews captured more nuanced perspectives that might not emerge in group settings due to the sensitive nature of HIV-related discussions and adolescent concerns about confidentiality in the presence of peers. All FGDs were conducted in private rooms at the Atua Government Hospital, and the interviews were conducted online via telephone calls or Google Meet video conferencing to ensure confidentiality and participant comfort.
FGDs lasted 60–90 min, whereas interviews lasted 45–60 min. All FGD sessions were led by the first author, a Ghanaian clinical nurse and PhD candidate who brings expertise in qualitative methodology, adolescent HIV and mental health through her academic position as a lecturer. Supporting the facilitation was a research assistant who performed dual roles: providing language translation and capturing detailed field notes that documented group interactions, non-verbal communication patterns and emotional expressions. To ensure linguistic inclusivity, participants were encouraged to express themselves in their preferred language—whether English or Dangbe—with the facilitator offering immediate translation when participants chose to speak in a local language. With participant consent, all sessions were audio-recorded. After each session, the research team held debriefing meetings to discuss emerging themes and adjust data collection strategies as needed. This iterative process enabled continuous refinement of the interview guide and ensured responsiveness to participants’ narratives. Data saturation—the point at which no new information or themes emerged—was used as a guiding principle in determining the adequacy of the sample size (n = 30). Saturation was assessed during team debriefings and was considered reached when subsequent interviews no longer yielded novel insights, affirming the completeness and depth of the data collected.

## Rationale for employing both focus group discussions and in-depth interviews
This study employed both FGDs and IDIs to maximise data richness while addressing stakeholder-specific needs. FGDs were the primary method, as they facilitate dynamic interactions, enable exploration of consensus and divergent viewpoints and mirror the collective nature of MFGT itself. IDIs were conducted specifically with YPLHIV participants as a purposeful supplement to FGDs to address privacy concerns around sensitive HIV-related topics and explore individual barriers that might not emerge in group settings while allowing deeper exploration of themes already identified in the FGDs. Together, FGDs and IDIs allowed for a comprehensive exploration of themes, capturing both collective and individual perspectives and strengthening the credibility and richness of the findings.

## Data analysis
Thematic analysis using a contextual approach was employed to analyse the data. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and, where necessary, translated into English by bilingual research team members. The analysis process followed six steps as outlined by Braun and Clarke : (1) familiarisation with the data through repeated reading of transcripts, (2) generating initial codes using both deductive codes from the CFIR framework and inductive codes emerging from the data, (3) searching for themes by collating codes into potential themes, (4) reviewing themes for internal homogeneity and external heterogeneity, (5) defining and naming themes and (6) producing the report.
QDA Miner software was used to facilitate data management and coding. Initial coding was conducted independently by two researchers, who then met to compare codes and resolve discrepancies. Themes were developed iteratively through collaborative discussions between the two authors, with careful attention to how barriers manifested across the three stakeholder groups. The analysis focused on identifying both convergent perspectives (barriers recognised across multiple stakeholder groups) and divergent perspectives (barriers unique to specific stakeholder groups). The identified themes were systematically mapped to CFIR domains during the analysis. Two researchers independently coded barriers according to CFIR constructs, with discrepancies resolved through discussion. This mapping revealed that barriers spanned three CFIR domains: outer setting (resource constraints and stigma concerns), inner setting (organisational challenges) and characteristics of individuals (participation barriers), whereas no significant barriers were identified within the intervention characteristics or process domains. The CFIR framework guided the organisation of identified barriers into broader theoretical constructs while allowing for emergent themes specific to the Ghanaian context.

## Rigor and reflexivity
Several strategies were employed to enhance the trustworthiness of the study findings. Credibility was strengthened through triangulation of data sources (three distinct stakeholder groups) and methods (FGDs and IDIs). Member checking was conducted by sharing preliminary findings with representative participants from each stakeholder group and incorporating their feedback into the final analysis. Dependability was enhanced through maintaining a detailed audit trail of methodological decisions and analytical processes.
Reflexivity was practised throughout the research process. The research team, comprising two Ghanaian researchers with backgrounds in mental health, HIV care and implementation science, regularly engaged in reflective discussions about how their positionalities might influence data collection and interpretation. A reflexive journal was maintained to document these discussions and their effect on methodological decisions. The research team’s expertise and reflective approach facilitated careful examination of assumptions and biases that might influence the interpretation of findings.

## Ethical considerations
The study received ethical approval from the Ghana Health Service Ethics Review Committee (004/07/24), with additional permissions granted by the Eastern Regional Health Directorate and hospital administration. Ethical safeguards included obtaining informed consent from adult participants and both caregiver consent and adolescent assent for minors, using participants’ preferred languages. Confidentiality was protected through anonymised identification numbers and secure data storage. Owing to the sensitive nature of the topic, a clinical psychologist was present during data collection to address any emotional distress. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without affecting their access to care. The study took extra care to accommodate the needs of YPLHIV, employing age-appropriate communication methods during recruitment and interviews. To reduce participation barriers, transportation stipends (150 GHS) were provided, and refreshments were offered during FGDs to foster a supportive and comfortable environment.

## Patient and public involvement
None.

## Findings

## Sociodemographic characteristics of the participants
The demographic profile showed that the majority of the YPLHIV participants were female (83.3%) and aged between 15 and 19 years (58.3%), with most currently attending school and having completed at least junior high school. Awareness of their HIV status varied, with 41.6% knowing for 1–5 years. One-third lived with grandparents, and their caregivers were mostly married (33.3%) or widowed (25%). The group of five healthcare professionals was gender-diverse and primarily composed of nurses with varying years of experience in HIV care, and several had received training in mental health or psychosocial support. Caregivers were predominantly female (92.3%) and Christian (100%), aged between 25 and >60 years. Most had completed junior high school or higher, with biological mothers being the largest caregiver subgroup (38.5%). Nearly 40% had provided care for >10 years, and 61.5% did so without any additional support services ( ).
 

## Themes and subthemes
The thematic analysis of FGDs with YPLHIV, caregivers and healthcare providers reveals the multifaceted barriers that could impede successful programme implementation ( ).
 

## CFIR domain: outer setting

## Theme 1: resource and logistic constraints
Participants (healthcare workers) identified several resource limitations that could hinder effective implementation.

## Subtheme 1.1: Transportation costs and geographic accessibility
Transportation cost and geographic distance emerged as a significant barrier largely because some of the YPLHIV and their caregivers do not live close to the hospital where they could walk. These individuals often travel on public transport to access healthcare, which strains their finances and scheduling of their daily activities owing to the lack of travel schedules by the public transportation system. The key challenge to transportation encompasses both the financial burden of transportation and the geographic distance from the facility, given that the majority of them come from poor socio-economic backgrounds.
As illustrated by one healthcare professional below:
I understand the importance of these meetings for the clients, but…you know, some of these people don't live around…. They don’t have transportation. Yes, they don’t have transportation to come for a meeting. (Healthcare worker 1)
The transportation and geographic accessibility challenges highlighted by healthcare workers are strongly echoed and validated by both caregivers and adolescents, demonstrating a shared understanding across all stakeholder groups about this significant barrier. For families in the Lower Manya Krobo District, transportation costs represent not merely an inconvenience but a substantial economic burden that directly affects their ability to consistently participate in an intervention like this, compounded by the geographic distance many must travel:
Sometimes by Sunday, you might be facing financial difficulties, which means you will have to miss a meeting. (Caregiver 3)

## Subtheme 1.2: inadequate physical facilities
The FGDs revealed significant concerns about the venue for implementing the MFGT. Participants highlighted challenges relating to the size of the venue to accommodate a high number of adolescents and caregiver dyads as well as the appropriateness of the venue to support the intervention in terms of ventilation. These indeed represent significant potential barriers towards the effective implementation, expansion and scale-up of the MFGT. As reported by one of the participants:
As we sit here, we’re all feeling hot; do you understand? So, bringing in this is a very good thing, but then, this space of ours is not big enough… What if in a few months to come, we are getting more than 100 adolescents coming in. We can’t; this place is too small. (Healthcare worker 4)
The immediate discomfort (‘we’re all feeling hot’) demonstrates how environmental conditions can negatively affect the therapy experience. Uncomfortable participants may be less engaged, less likely to return for future sessions and less able to focus on the therapeutic content being delivered.

## Subtheme 1.3: inadequate funding to support implementation
The FGDs with healthcare workers highlighted financial constraints as a significant barrier to implementing and sustaining the MFGT programme over time, particularly when the pilot phase has officially ended. Participants openly acknowledged the ongoing financial requirements for programme operation and expressed concerns about the availability of adequate funding sources to support these needs beyond the initial implementation period. Their concerns reflect their experiential wisdom regarding programme sustainability challenges in resource-constrained settings such as Atua.
This kind of program needs consistent funding. We’ve seen many good initiatives start with enthusiasm but then gradually fade away when the initial resources dry up. Without dedicated financial support, we’ll struggle to maintain the quality. (Healthcare worker 3)
This perspective highlights how frontline healthcare workers’ practical experience with programme sustainability challenges represents a crucial knowledge resource for implementation planning, emphasising the need for sustainable funding mechanisms to be established before programme initiation rather than as an afterthought.

## Theme 2: stigma and confidentiality concerns
Stigma remains a significant barrier to engaging adolescents and families in HIV-related interventions.

## Subtheme 2.1: public identification concerns
Our discussions with the healthcare workers revealed significant apprehension about how the MFGT programme might inadvertently identify participants as people living with HIV, some of whom might be hiding their identity and status from others. This concern represents a critical psychosocial barrier that could severely affect programme participation and effectiveness.
[The] Krobo area is known to be an HIV area. That’s what people are aware of, so I don’t know the name that we put on the programme so that people will not say this is an HIV-infected people meeting. (Healthcare worker 4)
The fear of being recognised as associated with HIV reflects the persistent reality of stigma in the community and demonstrates how implementation strategies must address not just logistical challenges but also deeply rooted social concerns. Without addressing these identification concerns, even well-organised programmes may struggle with recruitment and retention.

## Subtheme 2.2: community stigma concerns
The FGDs revealed pervasive anxiety about HIV-related stigma as a fundamental barrier to participating in the MFGT programme. Participants articulated detailed concerns about how gathering for programme activities might trigger community scrutiny and lead to stigmatisation, regardless of the meeting location chosen:
When people come to [the] hospital, for instance, they will be asking what are these people coming to do? (YPLHIV 6)
These stigma concerns reflect the enduring social consequences of an HIV diagnosis in this community context. Without addressing these deep-seated fears, even the most well-designed implementation strategies risk being undermined by participants’ reluctance to risk public identification with HIV. Similarly, community-based venues raised parallel concerns:
When you mention a space within the community, it raises concerns because meeting in a public area might expose us to people we know, which can make us feel uncomfortable or worried about our privacy. (Caregiver 2)
This concern suggests that any regular gathering pattern, regardless of location, might eventually be recognised and stigmatised by community observers. For adolescents, these stigma concerns carried significant psychological weight:
I have a concern about being seen by others while attending the programmes. I am afraid it could lead to stigmatisation or people making assumptions about us. (YPLHIV 10)
These stigma concerns suggest the need for implementation strategies that actively counter or disguise the HIV-specific nature of the programme.

## CFIR domain: inner setting

## Theme 3: organisational capacity and engagement barriers
Several practical challenges were identified that could affect the programme’s success.

## Subtheme 3.1 attendance reliability
Healthcare providers expressed anxiety about the gap between participants’ stated intentions to attend and their actual follow-through, highlighting attendance reliability as a critical implementation challenge.
My fear is when we’re trying to get to the people, and then they’re not turning themselves in for the programme… The person tells you today that when you call me, I’ll reliably come, but when the D-Day comes, you call – they won’t come. (Healthcare worker 2)
YPLHIV and caregivers further described other competing social responsibilities, such as educational commitments, market day conflicts and cultural expectations regarding family emergencies that can interrupt consistent attendance:
Please, now Saturdays and Sundays, too, we go for extra classes. (YPLHIV 8)
There are times that we may be required to be present at family meetings, marriage ceremonies and funerals. Hmmm, so coming for the programme on such days may be challenging. (Caregiver 13)
This concern about attendance consistency reflects the reality that therapeutic interventions require regular participation to achieve their intended outcomes. Even the most well-designed programme cannot be effective if participants don’t attend sessions consistently, making attendance reliability a fundamental implementation barrier that requires targeted strategies.

## Subtheme 3.2 organisational instability
The FGDs revealed concerns about the unpredictable nature of institutional priorities and resource allocation within the healthcare system. The healthcare workers expressed anxiety about how organisational changes might undermine programme implementation and sustainability over time, highlighting institutional instability as a significant barrier to long-term programme success.
I’m concerned that in the future, if we’re not careful, we may be told that the space is needed for another ward or a different project. This has happened before here in Atua Hospital. (Healthcare worker 1)
This statement reveals sophisticated foresight about institutional dynamics based on experience. It also reflects awareness that even well-implemented programmes remain vulnerable to shifting institutional priorities beyond the control of programme staff. Without addressing these structural vulnerabilities, the MFGT programme risks disruption regardless of its therapeutic value or initial implementation success.

## CFIR domain: characteristics of individuals

## Theme 4: individual and behavioural participation barriers

## Subtheme 4.1 anxiety and social discomfort in group settings
Participants, particularly adolescents, expressed anxiety about communicating in group settings and suggested alternative formats that might better accommodate their comfort levels. These concerns about group dynamics highlight important considerations for the implementation of a programme that fundamentally relies on interpersonal exchange. This discomfort with group settings reflects both developmental characteristics of adolescence and the sensitive nature of HIV-related discussions:
Sometimes, when certain people are around, others may feel unable to speak up—or they might choose to remain silent in the presence of others. (YPLHIV 7)
This concern about group communication led some participants to suggest alternative delivery formats:
Maybe you can create a group on the phone [and] then you connect those [who] want to join. I mean those who will be joining this project. Then, every Sunday, we can meet online… There is no need for us to come there (venue). (YPLHIV 11)
The discussions further revealed that group composition significantly affects comfort levels:
Just that I don’t want them (the parents/caregivers) to be in our midst while we are having a conversation, maybe I will be shy of them. (YPLHIV 10)
This statement specifically identifies discomfort with intergenerational group settings where parents are present. The significance of these group setting concerns demonstrates how format preferences directly affect programme engagement. Successful implementation strategies must strike a balance between the therapeutic benefits of group interaction and the psychological realities of participant comfort.

## Subtheme 4.2 delayed status disclosure
The FGDs reveal a significant disconnect between WHO recommendations for HIV status disclosure to children (a gradual process from ages 6 to 12) and actual practices in Atua, where even adolescents as old as 15 remain unaware of their status. This gap represents not merely a deviation from clinical guidelines but a critical implementation barrier for the MFGT programme, which requires participants to be aware of their HIV status. The healthcare worker’s observation identifies a widespread cultural practice of delayed disclosure that directly restricts the pool of eligible adolescents, emphasising the need for strategic interventions that address disclosure support as a prerequisite for successful programme implementation.
The WHO recommends starting the process of disclosing HIV status to children by the age of 6 years and completing it before they turn 12, but here in Atua, even in children who are 15 years old, their status has not been disclosed to them. (Healthcare worker 1)
Without targeted strategies to support status disclosure, a substantial portion of adolescents who might benefit from the programme will remain excluded by eligibility criteria designed to prevent accidental disclosure.

## Discussion
This study provides valuable insights into potential barriers that can affect the effective implementation of the MFGT to address mental health burden in YPLHIV in Ghana’s Lower Manya Krobo District. The findings reveal four interconnected barrier themes: resource and logistic constraints, stigma and confidentiality concerns, implementation challenges and participation barriers. These findings align with established implementation science frameworks while highlighting context-specific challenges in Ghana.
The resource and logistic constraints identified, particularly transportation challenges and inadequate physical facilities, echo findings from other low-resource settings implementing HIV interventions. Ridgeway et al and Rachlis et al similarly identified transportation costs as a significant barrier to HIV care engagement in sub-Saharan Africa. In the Ghanaian context, these barriers are exacerbated by the socioeconomic circumstances of many YPLHIV families, making consistent participation particularly challenging. The physical facility limitations also raise concerns about programme scalability and participant comfort, factors that directly affect engagement and retention.
Stigma emerges as a pervasive barrier across all stakeholder groups, manifesting as fear of public identification and community stigmatisation. These findings align with previous research, which found that HIV-related stigma significantly affects intervention uptake among adolescents in sub-Saharan Africa. However, this study provides deeper insight into the specific community context of the Krobo area, where HIV stigma appears particularly entrenched and where regular gatherings for MFGT could inadvertently reveal participants’ HIV status. This stigma dimension reflects how implementation barriers can be deeply rooted in social and cultural contexts beyond mere logistical challenges.
The implementation challenges identified, including attendance reliability and organisational instability, reflect common barriers in the CFIR. The attendance concerns highlight the gap between intention and action, complicated by competing priorities, such as educational commitments and cultural obligations. Organisational instability points to the vulnerability of programmes to shifting institutional priorities, a challenge documented in other implementation studies but particularly relevant in resource-constrained settings where competition for space and resources is intense.
Participation barriers, including adolescents’ anxiety in group settings and delayed status disclosure, further complicate implementation efforts. The finding that adolescents experience anxiety and social discomfort in group settings raises critical questions about the fundamental acceptability and appropriateness of MFGT’s inherently group-based format for YPLHIV. This concern is particularly significant because sustained engagement and therapeutic benefit in MFGT depend on participants’ willingness to actively engage in group discussions and share personal experiences. The discomfort expressed by adolescents, particularly regarding intergenerational group settings where parents are present, suggests that the traditional MFGT format may require substantial adaptation to accommodate the unique developmental and psychosocial needs of YPLHIV in Ghana. Research with adolescent populations in group-based interventions has documented similar challenges and effective mitigation strategies. Studies of group interventions for HIV-affected adolescents in African contexts suggest that age-appropriate groupings and peer support mechanisms significantly improve participation and comfort levels. Evidence from group support interventions in Uganda and South Africa demonstrates that incorporating structured warm-up activities, establishing clear confidentiality guidelines and utilising peer co-facilitators help reduce anxiety and enhance engagement in group-based interventions for HIV-affected youth. The disclosure challenges represent a particularly significant barrier, as the gap between WHO disclosure guidelines and local practice in Atua limits the pool of eligible participants. These findings highlight how cultural practices around disclosure can directly affect intervention implementation, requiring pre-implementation work focused on disclosure support.
Successful implementation of MFGT for young people living with HIV in Ghana requires simultaneously addressing multiple barriers through integrated solutions. Transportation vouchers, rebranding the programme to reduce stigma, adopting hybrid delivery models, establishing parallel disclosure support and securing formal institutional commitments would collectively enhance programme viability. These approaches must balance addressing logistical challenges with mitigating deeper social and cultural barriers specific to the Ghanaian context.
The findings underscore the need for flexible implementation rather than rigid protocol adherence, recognising the tension between intervention fidelity and necessary cultural adaptation. Pre-implementation work focused on status disclosure and stigma reduction may be essential prerequisites, while addressing organisational instability through systemic support will be crucial for sustainability. By embracing these adaptations while maintaining core therapeutic elements, MFGT can be effectively tailored to meet the unique needs of young people living with HIV in this setting.

## Implications for future interventions and implementation policies
The findings from this study have several important implications for future mental health interventions targeting YPLHIV in Ghana and similar resource-constrained settings. First, they highlight the necessity of conducting thorough pre-implementation assessments to identify potential barriers specific to local contexts before introducing new interventions. Such assessments should engage multiple stakeholders, as this study demonstrated the value of triangulating perspectives from healthcare providers, adolescents and caregivers.
Second, the findings suggest that implementation policies should adopt staged approaches that address foundational barriers before full programme rollout. For example, disclosure support programmes and community stigma reduction initiatives may need to precede MFGT implementation. This sequenced approach would create a more conducive environment for intervention success and ensure that resources are not wasted on premature implementation efforts.
Third, the study findings call for flexible funding models that accommodate transportation support, venue improvements, technology enhancements and programme sustainability as essential components of intervention implementation rather than optional add-ons. Policy makers should recognise these elements as core implementation costs rather than peripheral expenses, particularly in resource-constrained settings where such barriers can completely undermine programme viability.

## Limitations of the study
Despite its valuable insights, this study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the study was conducted at a single site in the Lower Manya Krobo District, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other regions of Ghana with different cultural, economic and healthcare contexts. The specific concerns around stigma in the Krobo area, for instance, may differ in magnitude or nature from those in other regions. Second, the study involved a relatively small sample size, which, although acceptable for qualitative research aiming at depth over breadth, may still limit the transferability of the findings. Third, there was an oversampling of female adolescents living with HIV and caregivers, reflecting sex patterns in care engagement at the study site. However, this may have resulted in under-representation of male perspectives, which are critical for a comprehensive understanding of family and sex dynamics in HIV care. Fourth, one focus group had only four participants due to unexpected absences. Despite producing meaningful data through standard procedures, the smaller size potentially limited the breadth of viewpoints and interactive dynamics typical of larger groups. Fifth, the study relied on anticipatory perspectives rather than observations of actual implementation challenges, as participants were discussing potential barriers to a programme that had not yet been implemented. Although this approach provides valuable foresight, it may not capture all the barriers that would emerge during actual implementation, and some anticipated barriers might prove less significant in practice. Finally, the study does not explicitly quantify or rank the relative importance of identified barriers, which could help prioritise implementation strategies. Without such prioritisation, it remains unclear which barriers might have the most significant effect on implementation success and which might be more easily addressed with limited resources.

## Conclusion
This study illuminates the complex barriers impeding MFGT implementation for YPLHIV in Ghana, revealing that successful implementation requires addressing practical challenges alongside deeper social and structural concerns. The findings demonstrate how established implementation frameworks must be contextually adapted to local cultural, economic and healthcare realities. For effective implementation, policymakers should adopt comprehensive approaches that address multiple barriers simultaneously while maintaining core intervention components, with pre-implementation work on disclosure support and stigma reduction serving as essential prerequisites. By systematically addressing these contextual barriers, future implementation efforts can bridge the gap between evidence-based interventions and successful real-world application, ultimately improving mental health outcomes for this vulnerable population in resource-constrained settings.
