However, research has shown that ChatGPT's large language models (LLMs) exhibit Western cultural characteristics reflective of the Anglosphere and Protestant Europe, with the United States serving as a representative example ( ). Responses and recommendations from AI models with cultural characteristics may result in varying creative performance outcomes for native and foreign-culture users, due to differences in thinking processes and criteria for evaluating creativity ( ).
The United States and China lead AI development, yet their AI models differ in more than just technical architecture; they also encode distinct cultural worldviews that influence how humans interact with them. If ChatGPT is biased toward English-language-dominant Western culture, what types of cultural biases would be observed among non-Western-developed AI models, such as ErnieBot—a leading AI model in Chinese-language-dominant world with over 300 million users ( )? According to the foundational theory of linguistic relativity, the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis ( ), language functions as a cultural carrier, shaping human behavior, cognition, and worldview. Cultural psychology further defines human culture as unique systems of information and meaning that shape people's social orientations and cognitions ( ). For example, Hofstede et al. ( ) describe culture as “the programming of the mind which distinguishes the members of one group or category of people from another.” Similarly, each AI model reflects distinct “thinking processes” shaped by the culture of its training data. Considering that ErnieBot's LLM is trained on datasets grounded in the Chinese language ( ), a cultural carrier of Chinese knowledge, practices, and preferences ( ), the Chinese AI model is likely to reflect biases influenced by the Chinese culture.
While existing research has primarily examined Western-developed AI models such as ChatGPT, research on cultural biases in non-Western models remains limited ( ). This imbalance is important to address for three reasons. First, recognizing linguistic and cultural nuances across AI systems is critical to ensuring equitable and effective use by all users. Second, the dominance of Western LLMs in the literature does not provide adequate insight into the experiences of users working with non-Western models, which may lead to different outcomes. Third, cultural biases embedded in AI models can also affect performance in collaborative settings such as cross-cultural creativity. Given AI's global deployment, underrepresenting non-Western models risks reinforcing Western-centric conclusions and distorting our understanding of cultural bias and user experiences. Moreover, existing studies have yet to systematically identify the cultural dimensions along which Western and non-Western AI models diverge or have they examined how these cultural biases impact human–AI collaborative performance. To address this gap, our study presents a direct comparative analysis of a United States–developed model (ChatGPT) and a China-developed model (ErnieBot), linking culturally embedded differences to their downstream effects on human–AI creative collaboration. Understanding these effects is critical, as cultural (mis)alignment may shape the quality, relevance, and adoption of AI-assisted work in real-world contexts.
Creativity is shaped by cultural frameworks that influence how ideas are generated, encoded, and evaluated ( , ). When human and AI collaborators share culturally aligned cognitive frameworks, they are more likely to exchange and build on ideas using compatible symbolic systems, contextual references, and evaluative criteria—enhancing the applicability of outputs in local contexts. Conversely, cultural misalignment introduces diverse perspectives that may foster novelty but can also reduce contextual appropriateness. Language illustrates this dynamic: Chinese characters are information dense and often carry layered meanings, encouraging holistic processing, whereas English words are typically more context dependent, relying on linear and compositional structures for interpretation ( ). These linguistic contrasts reveal how cultural meaning systems shape the construction and understanding of ideas, highlighting the importance of cultural alignment in effective creative collaboration.
Our first goal is to deepen current understanding of cultural biases in AI models by providing a comparative analysis between ChatGPT and ErnieBot (study 1). Their large user bases highlight the significance of understanding the strengths and weaknesses of these AI models, as well as the role of human–AI cultural alignment in driving their effectiveness. By analyzing the responses of the two AI models to psychometric scales established in cultural psychology, we investigate whether LLMs of AI models can be considered “cultural beings” that reflect values, norms, and cognitive tendencies corresponding to the cultural backgrounds of their training data. According to the cultural schema theory ( ), repeated cultural experiences shape individuals’ cognitive framework, which in turn influences how new information is processed and interpreted. Yiu et al. ( ) argue that LLMs are powerful tools for cultural transmission. If AI models exhibit responses that are consistent with the cultural framework embedded in the training datasets, it shows that AI models go beyond processing language neutrally to reflect and reinforce cultural features, raising implications on whether AI models represent a particular cultural context.
Our second goal is to explore the effects of cultural (mis)alignment in human–AI creative collaboration by examining how American and Chinese users interact with culturally coherent and incoherent AI models in the context of local and global creative tasks. Interestingly, existing literature offers competing predictions regarding the impact of cultural alignment on creativity. On one hand, users working with culturally aligned AI models may exhibit better creative performance on local tasks (creative idea targeted at local audience) due to enhanced focus and immersion (i.e. a state of psychological flow), facilitated by a deeper understanding of the local task context ( ). On the other hand, drawing on the positive effects of multiculturalism on creativity—which thrives on diverse cultural knowledge ( )—users may achieve higher creative performance when interacting with culturally misaligned AI models for global tasks (creative idea targeted at global audience). We empirically test these two competing hypotheses through an experiment (study 2) and a field study (study 3).
Moreover, we propose that the effects of cultural alignment on human–AI creative collaboration are mediated by psychological flow, defined as a state of deep absorption and engagement in an activity, characterized by focused attention, intrinsic enjoyment, and a balance between challenge and skill, a recognized precursor to creativity ( ). When users and AI models share culturally grounded norms, values, and cognitive tendencies, collaboration requires less effort to reconcile divergent perspectives or interpret unfamiliar frames of reference ( ). With fewer cognitive resources spent on resolving misalignments, more can be dedicated to elaborating and refining new ideas, thereby enhancing creativity in contextually relevant tasks. In parallel, this cognitive ease is accompanied by heightened experiences of enjoyment and immersion in the task, consistent with the conceptual definition of psychological flow as unobstructed engagement. In contrast, cultural misalignment can introduce interpretive challenges that disrupt concentration and break the immersive experiential state. Accordingly, we conceptualize psychological flow as an overarching construct encompassing both a cognitive-attentional dimension and an affective-experiential dimension ( ). Given that creative contexts involve both cognitive and experiential processes, approaches that privilege one dimension over the other may provide an incomplete account of the consequences of human–AI alignment. An integrated perspective of psychological flow enables a comprehensive investigation of how cultural alignment enhances human–AI creativity.

## Empirical strategy
To examine how cultural alignment between humans and AI influences collaborative creativity, we adopted a three-study approach. Study 1 establishes cross-cultural differences in norm, value, and cognition orientations as manifested in LLMs, demonstrating that these models encode distinct cultural tendencies. Study 2 employed an experiment to test whether alignment between users’ and AI models’ cultural backgrounds influences creativity outcomes in local and global tasks. Study 3 extended and replicated these conditions in a naturalistic field setting using dyadic data from supervisor–subordinate pairs, thereby enhancing the robustness and ecological validity of the findings. (Study 1 did not include any human participants; hence, no IRB was required. Studies 2 and 3 were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Singapore Management University with approval number IRB-24-061-A045(324); with informed consent obtained from all participants.) Descriptive statistics and correlations of key variables of studies 2 and 3 are presented in Tables and .
To investigate potential cultural biases in AI model outputs in study 1, we focused on ChatGPT-3.5/4 and ErnieBot-3.5/4. ChatGPT's training data are largely rooted in Western contexts ( ), whereas ErnieBot's are primarily Chinese ( ). Cultural psychology research has highlighted significant differences in cultural norms, values, and cognitive tendencies between American and Chinese cultures ( ). Given these insights, we expected ChatGPT and ErnieBot to reflect norms, values, and cognitive tendencies of American and Chinese cultures, respectively. To test cultural bias in AI models, we used the multiple-choice prompt approach, specifically employing the zero-shot technique, where no prior training or examples were given before presenting the multiple-choice prompt. This method allowed us to test the AI models in their most natural form. Multiple-choice questions serve as a key benchmark in AI model evaluation, assessing model capabilities across diverse domains through explicit reasoning and comparison using natural prompting strategies ( ).
To assess the extent to which a society enforces social norms, we used the six-item cultural tightness scale developed by Gelfand et al. ( ), predicting that ErnieBot would score higher on cultural tightness than ChatGPT. For measuring cultural values, we employed the 26-item CVSCALE, predicting that ErnieBot would score higher than ChatGPT on three key dimensions—collectivism, power distance, and long-term orientation—based on existing research of value differences between Americans and Chinese ( ). For completeness, we also included the other two dimensions of masculinity and uncertainty avoidance. To evaluate cultural influence on cognitive tendencies, we adopted Morris and Peng's ( ) 21-item cultural attribution scale, which examines external attributions (e.g. “America's extremely individualistic, selfish values corrupt foreign students”) and internal attributions (e.g. “Perpetrator was mentally imbalanced because his life consisted only of work, without other activities to relieve stress”). We predict that ErnieBot will make more external attributions but less pronounced difference for internal attributions than ChatGPT.
To examine the interactive effect of human–AI cultural (mis)alignment and task locality on creativity in study 2, we conducted an experiment with 385 participants—the sample size was determined a priori to achieve 80% power and a small effect size of 0.15—who were randomly assigned to conditions of cultural alignment versus misalignment between users (American versus Chinese) and AI models (ChatGPT versus ErnieBot), as well as task locality (local versus global). Creativity was assessed through an idea generation task, where participants were asked to create a compelling elevator pitch to attract investment for a new business. Cultural alignment in study 2 is operationalized by nationality rather than by directly measuring individual values—an approach that may overlook within-country variability in cultural orientations. However, this operationalization is justified by the normative function of culture. Culture primarily influences behaviors through shared norms and practices that are widely accepted within national groups. It represents a tradition of knowledge and practices passed down across generations and collectively embraced by members of a society ( , ). In interactions with in-group members, individuals tend to draw on these culturally shared ideas to establish common ground, reinforcing the assumption that others within the group endorse similar values. Supporting this view, Zou et al. ( ) demonstrated that both Chinese and US participants acted in ways consistent with their shared cultural values rather than their personal values, especially in social judgment and decision-making contexts.
In addition, the business pitch tasks were selected because they required participants to encode ideas persuasively for a target culture's audience, making linguistic and cultural framing particularly salient. For local tasks, shared cultural knowledge between users and AI could facilitate more resonant and contextually appropriate proposals. For global tasks, however, potential misalignment could introduce alternative cultural frames that, while enhancing novelty, may also reduce usefulness. This design allowed us to test how cultural alignment shapes creative collaboration across contexts that differentially emphasize cultural specificity versus universality. Pitching business ideas offers strong ecological validity, as it reflects a common real-world activity in entrepreneurial, corporate innovation, and investment contexts, where creativity must balance novelty and usefulness. The task is also well suited for manipulating local versus global framing, since the content of a pitch can be readily adapted to different markets while keeping its structure consistent. Furthermore, business pitches can be evaluated by raters with investment experience, ensuring domain-relevant judgments of both novelty and usefulness.
Two evaluators rated the pitched ideas on novelty (i.e. originality of ideas) and usefulness (i.e. relevance, inclusion of unique selling points, and appeal). The two evaluators in study 2 each have over 23 years of investment-related experience, routinely assessing entrepreneurial proposals, market-entry plans, and corporate innovation pitches. Both evaluators hold senior positions in finance and consulting, equipping them to evaluate pitches in local and global contexts using informed, real-world criteria. To measure psychological flow, we used the 10-item scale adapted from Bressler and Bodzin ( ) and Jackson et al. ( ). While psychological flow and task engagement are related, the psychological flow scale captures both cognitive-attentional and affective-experiential processes that distinguish it from mere task engagement; greater task engagement may lead to more useful output without necessarily reflecting reduced cognitive load or a genuine experience of immersion. Psychological flow is considered a specific and optimal form of engagement characterized by reduced cognitive effort and deeper experiential qualities such as absorption, intrinsic enjoyment, merging of action and awareness, and distortion of time ( ). Scale items include “I did things naturally without thinking too much,” “I really enjoyed what I was doing,” and “I was in the zone.”
In study 3, we replicated the interaction effect of human–AI cultural (mis)alignment in a naturalistic field study. We recruited 194 supervisor–subordinate dyads from an enterprise software development company in China. Employees self-reported their workplace AI usage over a 2-week period, including details on the AI model used, frequency, tasks performed, and task locality. During the same period, each employee's direct supervisor assessed their creative performance. Details on methodologies are reported in the .

## Divergent cultural characteristics
Results from Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (study 1) show that AI models trained in different LLMs exhibit cultural norms, values, and cognition of the respective cultures (Table and Fig. ). Consistent with prior literature comparing Chinese and American cultural characteristics ( ), both versions of ErnieBot reported: higher cultural tightness (P < 0.05, 95% CI [0.00, 0.00]); higher collectivism (P < 0.05, 95% CI [0.00, 0.00]), power distance (P < 0.05, 95% CI [0.00, 0.00]), and long-term orientation (P < 0.05, 95% CI [0.00, 0.00]) than the respective versions of ChatGPT. ErnieBot-3.5 also reported significantly lower uncertainty avoidance (P < 0.05, 95% CI [0.00, 0.00]; MeanErnieBot-3.5 = 20.58, MeanChatGPT-3.5 = 22.08) than ChatGPT-3.5. However, ErnieBot-4.0 reported significantly higher uncertainty avoidance (P < 0.05, 95% CI [0.00, 0.00]; MeanErnieBot-4.0 = 25.00, MeanChatGPT-4.0 = 24.76) than ChatGPT-4.0. The effects of uncertainty avoidance therefore differ in opposite direction between version 3.5 and version 4 models, indicating no consistent overall effect. ErnieBot-3.5 also reported significantly lower masculinity (P < 0.05, 95% CI [0.00, 0.00]; MeanErnieBot-3.5 = 15.82, MeanChatGPT-3.5 = 17.68) than ChatGPT-3.5. With regards to cognitive tendencies, ErnieBot-4.0 reported higher personal attribution (P < 0.05, 95% CI [0.00, 0.00]; MeanErnieBot-4.0 = 32.66, MeanChatGPT-4.0 = 26.62) than ChatGPT-4.0. This finding is unexpected; however, research indicates that cultural bias in attribution is more pronounced and prevalent in external attributions than in internal attributions ( ). Indeed, both versions of ErnieBot reported significantly higher situational attributions than the respective versions of ChatGPT (P < 0.05, 95% CI [0.00, 0.00]).
 

## Cultural alignment and local tasks enhance creativity usefulness
Using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, we observed significantly higher creativity usefulness effects (study 2): (i) in participants using culturally aligned AI models compared with those using culturally misaligned AI models (W = 2.45, P < 0.05, 95% CI [0.01, 0.02]); (ii) in participants performing local tasks compared with those performing global tasks (W = 1.80, P = 0.07, 95% CI [0.07, 0.08]); (iii) in participants using culturally aligned AI models to perform local tasks compared with those using culturally misaligned AI models to perform local tasks (W = 4.48, P < 0.05, 95% CI [0.00, 0.00]) and to participants using culturally aligned AI models to perform global tasks (W = 4.15, P < 0.05, 95% CI [0.00, 0.00]; see Table and Fig. for the above results). Notably, we replicated the above results using regression analysis (Table ). Separately, we did not find any significant interaction effect between cultural (mis)alignment and task locality on creativity novelty in study 2.
 We then analyzed the data in terms of American and Chinese subsamples. Results in both subsamples show that participants using culturally aligned AI to perform local tasks report significantly higher creativity usefulness compared with (i) participants using culturally aligned AI for global tasks (American: W = 3.18, P < 0.05, 95% CI [0.00, 0.00], Chinese: W = 2.73, P < 0.05, 95% CI [0.01, 0.01]) and (ii) participants using culturally misaligned AI for local tasks (American: W = 2.63, P < 0.05, 95% CI [0.01, 0.01], Chinese: W = 3.85, P < 0.05, 95% CI [0.00, 0.00]) (see Table and Fig. A and B for the above results). Both samples showed nonsignificant results in participants using culturally misaligned AI for global task.
 Moreover, we conducted another study in the real-world context (study 3) to validate and extend findings. Results from the OLS regressions showed that participants using culturally aligned AI models scored better creative performance evaluations by their supervisors than participants using culturally misaligned AI models (b = 0.41, SE = 0.11, P < 0.05; see model 1 in Table and Fig. ). However, after adding control variables into the regression model, the effect of culturally aligned AI use became nonsignificant (b = 0.19, SE = 0.14, P = 0.19), whereas the effect of culturally misaligned AI use became marginally significant (b = −0.22, SE = 0.13, P = 0.09; see model 2 in Table ), suggesting that at least one of the control variables was suppressing the effect of culturally misaligned AI use. Further investigation showed that after removing AI use frequency from the model, the effect of culturally misaligned AI use became nonsignificant (b = −0.13, SE = 0.11, P = 0.24) and the effect of culturally aligned AI use remained significant (b = 0.31, SE = 0.12, P < 0.05; see model 3 in Table ). Although including AI use frequency as a control produced a suppressor effect, we argue that AI use frequency does not alter the true impact of culturally aligned AI on creativity for two reasons. First, AI use frequency correlated more strongly with culturally aligned AI (r = 0.58) than culturally misaligned AI (r = 0.31), suggesting that culturally aligned AI was used more as it might feel easier and more intuitive, whereas culturally misaligned AI was often avoided and its harm was masked until AI use frequency was controlled. Second, since AI use frequency is conceptually downstream of culturally aligned AI, adjusting for AI use frequency removes part of culturally aligned AI's genuine effect. Therefore, excluding AI use frequency captures the total influence of human–AI cultural alignment, yielding a more reliable estimate of its impact on creativity. Even though study 2 did not measure AI use frequency, given the results in study 3, study 2's findings represent a more conservative test of our hypothesis.
 

## Psychological flow as the mediator
Mediation analyses of the experimental dataset (study 2) using SPSS Process Macro revealed participants using culturally aligned AI models for local tasks perform significantly better for creativity usefulness through a state of enhanced psychological flow than participants working with culturally aligned models on global tasks or with culturally misaligned models on local and global tasks. Specifically, human–AI cultural alignment had a significant indirect effect on creativity usefulness via experienced psychological flow (indirect effect = −0.07, SE = 0.03, P < 0.05, 95% CI [−0.13, −0.02]; Table ). In addition, local (versus global) task had a significant indirect effect on creativity usefulness via psychological flow (indirect effect = −0.09, SE = 0.03, P < 0.05, 95% CI [−0.16, −0.40]; Table ). These findings were partially replicated in our field study (study 3). Specifically, mediation analysis showed a marginally significant indirect effect of human–AI model cultural alignment on creativity through experienced psychological flow (indirect effect = 0.09, SE = 0.05, P = 0.07, 95% CI [−0.00, 0.21]; Table ). Although local (versus global) task had a significant indirect effect on creativity usefulness via psychological flow (indirect effect = 0.13, SE = 0.06, P < 0.05, 95% CI [0.01, 0.24]; Table ), psychological flow emerged as a partial mediator in a competitive mediation. This finding suggests that the effect of psychological flow is consistent with the theoretical framework, but there is the likelihood of an additional mediator driving the direct effect ( ). We observed similar results in OLS regression (Table ), where model 1 shows using culturally aligned AI models increased creativity (b = 0.41, SE = 0.11, P < 0.05), and when psychological flow is entered into the regression in model 4, the effect of using culturally aligned AI weakened (b = 0.26, SE = 0.09, P < 0.05), and psychological flow emerged as a stronger predictor of creativity (b = 0.63, SE = 0.07, P < 0.05). Finally, psychological flow remained a significant mediator (b = 0.60, SE = 0.08, P < 0.05) after adding in the control variables to model 5.
 In supplemental analyses, we further decomposed psychological flow into cognitive-attentional (clarity, focus, and reduced effort) and affective-experiential (enjoyment and immersion) dimensions. Correlation between cognitive-attentional and affective-experiential dimensions were moderately strong (study 2: r = 0.58; study 3: r = 0.68), showing they capture intertwined aspects of psychological flow. We then re-estimated all mediation models using each dimension. Cognitive-attentional flow consistently mediated effects of cultural alignment (study 2: indirect effect = −0.07, SE = 0.03, P < 0.05, 95% CI [−0.13, −0.02]; study 3: indirect effect = 0.12, SE = 0.05, P < 0.05, 95% CI [0.03, 0.22]) and task locality (study 2: indirect effect = −0.05, SE = 0.03, P < 0.05, 95% CI [−0.11, −0.01]; study 3: indirect effect = 0.13, SE = 0.06, P < 0.05, 95% CI [0.02, 0.24]), whereas affective-experiential flow was a significant mediator in the bounded creative task of study 2 (cultural alignment: indirect effect = −0.04, SE = 0.02, P = 0.07, 95% CI [−0.08, 0.00]; task locality: indirect effect = −0.08, SE = 0.03, P < 0.05, 95% CI [−0.15, −0.03]), but not study 3 (cultural alignment: indirect effect = 0.02, SE = 0.05, P = 0.67, 95% CI [−0.06, 0.12]; task locality: indirect effect = 0.08, SE = 0.05, P = 0.14, 95% CI [−0.02, 0.17]). Together, these results indicate that cognitive ease alone cannot account for the experiential immersion and intrinsic engagement that emerge when task conditions allow it, making psychological flow the more comprehensive mechanism. The differences in statistical outcomes for affective-experiential flow between the two studies are likely due to variations in task context. Study 3's field context involved diverse, self-selected tasks performed over 2 weeks, which likely diluted the affective-experiential component of psychological flow compared with the structured, immersive task in the experimental setting of study 2.
To further assess the robustness of the cognitive-attentional and affective-experiential decomposition, we conducted additional data-guided analyses using principal component analysis (PCA) to examine how flow items empirically load onto the two dimensions. As reported in , these PCA-guided decompositions and corresponding mediation analyses converge on the conclusion that the effects of human–AI cultural alignment on creativity are not reducible to cognitive ease alone, but instead reflect a broader psychological flow state in which cognitive-attentional and affective-experiential processes operate in tandem, with their relative prominence shaped by task context.

## Theoretical implications
This research provides a new perspective on AI research by uncovering the cultural biases inherent in AI models. First, through a comparative analysis of United States–developed ChatGPT and the Chinese-developed ErnieBot, we offer empirical insights into the cultural biases present in LLMs trained on different languages. Just as human beings develop cultural schemata ( ), AI models trained by culturally encoded datasets appear to function as cultural beings, internalizing and manifesting culturally specific tendencies. Specifically, ChatGPT reflects American cultural norms, values, and cognitive tendencies, whereas ErnieBot embodies Chinese cultural norms, values, and cognitions. These results are largely consistent with the well-documented cultural differences between American and Chinese societies in psychology research. To the best of our knowledge, our research is one of the few demonstrations of cultural biases in a Chinese LLM, revealing that LLMs from other cultures are also susceptible to cultural conditioning, much like ChatGPT. This expands the scope of existing research, which has predominantly focused on American models, to include Chinese LLMs. Our findings highlight the need for more inclusive and diverse methodologies in the development and evaluation of AI systems, ensuring their effectiveness in fostering global collaboration.
Second, we expand creativity research by examining how varying cultural alignments between humans and AI models in human–AI collaboration influences creative performance. We found that to the extent that AI models possess their own “culture,” cultural alignment with users can enhance creativity usefulness by fostering a heightened state of experienced psychological flow. This effect is especially pronounced when the task is intended for a local audience, highlighting the importance of cultural alignment between users, AI models, and the creative tasks. Although prior research found that human–human collaboration often benefits from cultural diversity through access to novel cultural knowledge, mutual adaptation, and broader perspectives ( , ), this advantage did not appear to extend to human–AI collaborations. In fact, cultural misalignment between humans and AI models significantly reduced creative efficiency, especially in global tasks, as AI models may lack the ability to engage in dynamic cultural adaptation. Overall, our findings show that, at the current stage, human–AI cultural diversity does not mirror human–human cultural diversity for creative collaboration. The impact of diversity likely extends beyond content diversity, where AI excels, to include deeper dimensions shaped by cultural motivations and values. Additionally, the ability of AI to engage in debate and dissent remains uncertain. Since a key advantage of diversity is its facilitation of dissent and task conflicts, which foster creative abrasion and enhance creativity, our finding highlights a limitation in the current capabilities of AI models.
It is theoretically noteworthy that while cultural alignment in human–AI collaboration enhances the usefulness dimension of creativity via the experience of psychological flow, the novelty dimension did not appear to improve. One interpretation is that when human and AI share the same culture, the mutual cultural understanding facilitates the psychological flow and exchange of culturally consistent information that would benefit solving a local problem (usefulness). Indeed, Zenk et al. ( ) found that psychological flow experience has a positive impact on creative usefulness in addition to other components of creativity. However, this cultural alignment inhibits the exchange of culturally inconsistent or contradictory information and knowledge. One possibility is that culturally aligned human and AI collaboration can become an “echo chamber” of sorts wherein culturally consistent information reinforce one another, reducing the level of novelty in the ideas generated. In other words, cultural biases in AI models might constrain or channel creative outputs in predictable ways, aligning with cultural norms but potentially stifling creative thinking.
Third, the finding that humans experience greater psychological flow during collaborative tasks with culturally aligned AI models highlights the importance of AI interfaces in enhancing user experience and efficiency. In studies of human–human collaborations, cultural alignment is often associated with better rapport, trust, and mutual understanding ( ). This dynamic likely translates to human–AI interactions as well, though the impact appears limited to specific dimensions of collaborative work. Nevertheless, it is clear that cultural alignment influences not only technical outcomes (e.g. creativity) but also the psychological and emotional aspects of human–AI collaboration. To the extent that an AI model serves as an agent that can augment human abilities, psychological flow is likely to occur when the AI model behaves predictably and aligns with the user's goals, thereby reducing cognitive load and fostering affective-experiential immersion in the creative task. These insights open avenues for developing AI interfaces that enhance user engagement and performance.

## Policy and governance implications
Beyond advancing theoretical understanding of cultural alignment in human–AI collaboration, our findings also hold important implications for AI policy and governance. The evidence that AI models embody culturally embedded norms, values, and cognitive tendencies suggests that policy and governance frameworks must move beyond generic principles of fairness and transparency to account for cultural specificity. In particular, the finding that alignment enhances creative usefulness but risks reinforcing “echo chambers” highlights the dual challenge for regulators: enabling culturally adaptive systems while safeguarding against insularity. To that end, we offer the following suggestions:
First, training data transparency should be a cornerstone of AI governance. While companies may resist full disclosure of proprietary datasets, policymakers can at minimum require the publication of high-level summaries of training corpora that describe cultural and linguistic composition. Such transparency would allow stakeholders to identify cultural blind spots, assess potential biases, and hold developers accountable for how cultural schemata shape model behavior. Second, adaptive alignment protocols should be encouraged. Governing bodies could establish standards for models to dynamically adjust to diverse cultural contexts without erasing differences. For example, technical guidelines may include evaluation benchmarks for cultural adaptability, alongside incentives for research into systems that balance local resonance with global interoperability. Third, monitoring and mitigation of cultural silos could be prioritized. To prevent fragmentation of global AI use, governance frameworks should incorporate auditing mechanisms that track when cultural tailoring inadvertently reinforces isolation, limiting cross-cultural knowledge exchange. Such oversight can ensure that cultural adaptation enhances inclusivity rather than reproduces digital divides.
Taken together, these measures underscore the need for AI governance that is both culturally aware and universally oriented. By embedding requirements for training data transparency, adaptive alignment, and silo monitoring into governance structures, policymakers can promote AI systems that are not only technically reliable but also equitably accessible across diverse populations. In this way, our findings contribute directly to ongoing debates on universal alignment—the aspiration that AI systems should serve humanity broadly, rather than privileging particular cultural vantage points. Far from being a purely academic issue, cultural alignment is therefore central to shaping the equitable, responsible, and globally interoperable deployment of AI.

## Limitations, future research, and conclusion
While our research provides important insights for AI theory development and governance, we acknowledge certain limitations that highlight promising directions for future research. First, as the findings are specific to the AI models being studied, they may not be generalized to other AI models due to variations in training data, algorithms, and evolving architectures ( ); moreover, future AI models may differ significantly in behavior, capabilities, and collaboration potential ( ). Future research can explore cultural schemata of AI models developed in different cultures. The ongoing race between the United States and China is likely to see reduced cost of developing AI models ( ), making it easier for countries to train AI systems that reflect their own cultural features. This rapid expansion of AI models and the cultural schemata encoded in them (i.e. AI as a cultural being) raise important questions about how culturally distinct AI models will influence cognitive patterns and ideological perspectives on a global scale. Also, the current study is bounded by the scope of the creative tasks and human–AI collaboration scenarios used in the experiments, the relatively short duration of the tasks that may have captured only the exposure effect, and the field study conducted with one enterprise software company in China. Further research may investigate different types of collaborations between human and AI models to replicate their effects on creativity across organizational, industry, and national contexts.
Additionally, the AI models in our research were tested in their native forms without influence through prompting. However, prior work shows that prompting can shift the cultural orientations of AI models and highlights the limitations of using multiple-choice questions to evaluate the values and opinions manifested in LLMs, as we did in study 1 ( , ). Future research could test our hypotheses using more nuanced evaluation frameworks that consider factors such as the use of multiple-choice versus open-ended formats, the prompting methods employed, and the nature of LLM responses, enabling a more comprehensive assessment of values and opinions in LLMs. Another promising venue of research is to investigate whether AI models are capable of dynamically adapting their responses to users’ cultural backgrounds, and how such adaptation might enhance both novelty and usefulness in creative collaboration. Moreover, examining the effects of cultural alignment across different types of tasks would provide deeper insight into the broader impact of human–AI cultural fit.
Considering that our study did not directly investigate the mechanisms underlying cultural misalignment between users and AI across different cultural dimensions assessed through self-reports, we acknowledge the limitation of not isolating the specific cultural factors that might account for variations in the pursuit of creative usefulness and novelty. Prior work suggests that collectivism may influence preferences for consensus-based versus individualistic idea generation, whereas power distance may drive individuals to prioritize feasible ideas that align with authority's preferences rather than challenge norms ( , ). Without directly measuring these dimensions, it remains unclear whether misalignment in values such as collectivism (e.g. consensus-oriented AI versus individual-oriented user) or power distance (e.g. directive AI versus autonomous user) explains the observed patterns. Future research should disentangle these mechanisms and examine how individual reports of cultural value congruence or divergence influences creative collaboration outcomes.
Finally, government censorship might play a role in influencing our results. However, the role of government censorship in shaping AI cultural schemata is better understood as part of the contextual environment in which models are developed. AI models trained under strict content moderation and government-imposed restrictions may exhibit constrained perspectives, omitting controversial or politically sensitive viewpoints. Such conditions can lead to AI-generated outputs to align more closely with state-sanctioned ideologies, potentially reinforcing certain cultural worldviews while suppressing others. In the present research, this contextual factor is unlikely to have substantially influenced our findings, as our measures focused on basic psychological responses and behaviors with little political content. Nonetheless, future research could directly examine how different content moderation policies shape the cultural narratives encoded in AI models and their implications for human–AI collaboration and creative expression.
Despite these limitations, this research highlighted that LLMs reflect the cultural context of their training data and underscored the role of cultural alignment in enhancing creative psychological flow and efficiency, while critically evaluating the potential benefits of cultural diversity in human–AI interactions. Just as AI begins to mirror human intelligence, we found it is also reflecting human cultural cognition, norms, and values. With major AI models reflecting different cultural foundations, users may gravitate toward the ones reflecting their own cultural backgrounds. This may lead to a cultural fragmentation of AI usage, and such fragmentation may in turn lead to silos, where interoperability and communication become complicated as different models from different cultural backgrounds might interpret or prioritize information differently. This research informs the global community on the importance of considering cultural differences during AI usage, especially when engaging in creative tasks.
