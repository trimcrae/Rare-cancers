## Abstract

## Objective
To compare the research performance and publishing practices of the Top 50 and Next 50 dental schools ranked by the QS World University Rankings by Subject: Dentistry, and to identify institution‑level bibliometric characteristics associated with higher ranking performance.

## Methods
QS Dentistry subject ranking data (2023–2025) were used to identify 100 dental schools, stratified into Top 50 and Next 50 groups based on multi‑year ranking stability. Institution‑level bibliometric indicators for 2016–2025 were retrieved from SciVal, using Dentistry (ASJC) as the subject filter to define the literature set. Twenty indicators capturing research productivity, citation impact, collaboration patterns, policy influence and journal quartile distribution were analysed. Group comparisons used Welch’s t‑tests for individual indicators and χ2 tests for distributional comparisons (collaborative mode; journal quartiles). False discovery rate correction was applied within each analytic family.

## Results
Across 2016–2025, the Top 50 schools outperformed the Next 50 in 10 indicators, including scholarly output, citation count, citations per publication, policy‑document citations and multiple collaboration‑impact metrics (all FDR‑adjusted P < .05). The Top 50 schools published a significantly higher proportion of their output in Q1 journals (58.0% versus 52.6%) and engaged more frequently in international collaboration (47.0% versus 44.3%). Correlation analysis showed that citations per publication positively correlated with international collaboration, Q1 publishing and top‑percentile citation indicators. Exploratory analyses showed that the ratio of scholarly output in Q1 journals declined stepwise from the Top 10 to ranks 12–20, 21–50 and 51–100.

## Conclusion
Top‑ranked dental schools are distinguished by greater publication volume, higher citation impact, stronger international collaboration and more frequent publishing in Q1 dental journals. These research findings offer directions for dental schools aiming to enhance their research performance and competitiveness within the QS ranking framework.

Key words: Dentistry, QS World University Ranking, SciVal, Citation analysis, Academic performance, International collaboration

## Introduction
Global university rankings have become increasingly influential in shaping the strategic priorities of higher education institutions. Among these, the QS World University Rankings by Subject: Dentistry is regarded as one of the most widely consulted and impactful league tables for dental schools worldwide. Institutions frequently use QS rankings to benchmark academic reputation, research quality and international visibility, while students, faculty and policymakers rely on them to guide decisions about enrolment, recruitment, and resource allocation. Despite their popularity, QS rankings are complex composite measures that incorporate multiple indicators. This multidimensionality prompts an important question: Which research‑related factors are most closely associated with high performance in QS dentistry rankings?
Our previous investigation addressed this question at the individual level by analysing whether the presence and performance of top 2% scientists, recognised according to the standardised citation indicators developed by Stanford University, correlated with QS scores among the global top dental schools. That study demonstrated that institutions hosting a larger number of highly cited scientists also tended to achieve higher QS scores, suggesting that outstanding individual researchers may contribute meaningfully to institutional visibility and ranking performance. However, rankings are ultimately computed at the institutional level, but not solely on the basis of individual scientific achievements.
One major knowledge gap that remained was how high‑ranking dental schools differed from lower‑ranking schools in terms of their institution‑level bibliometric profiles. While it can be assumed that top‑ranked schools publish more, collaborate more internationally and achieve higher citation impact, there has been no systematic benchmarking across a sufficiently large and balanced sample of dental schools. In particular, the field lacks large‑scale comparative evidence on research output patterns (e.g. journal quartile distributions), collaboration structures, citation impact and policy‑relevant outputs, all of which may influence institutional visibility and ranked performance.
To address this gap, the present study examined 100 global dental schools, partitioned into the Top 50 and Next 50 based on the QS subject rankings for the most recent 3 years (see the Methods section for details). By analysing institution‑level bibliometric data from the last 10 years, we performed a comprehensive comparison of research productivity, research impact, collaboration patterns and journal‑level publishing behaviour to reveal differences between the two groups of dental schools. Apart from contributing empirical insights, this study addresses the growing practical need for university leaders, dental faculties, and national policymakers to seek data‑driven strategies to enhance global competitiveness. By identifying actionable, evidence‑based take‑home messages for dental schools aspiring to enter or rise within the Top 50 of the QS Dentistry ranking, our findings may support more informed institutional planning, academic development, and strategic investment.

## Methods

## Determination of the Top 50 and Next 50 dental schools
On 13 February 2026, “QS World University Rankings by Subject: Dentistry” data versions 2023-2025 were downloaded from its official website. These three annual lists contained a total of 121 unique dental schools, with some schools appearing only once or twice and some schools fluctuating between the top 50 and the next 50 below that over those 3 years. The initial definition of a Top 50 dental school was a school ranked within the top 50 in ≥2 out of 3 years (n = 54). Of the remaining 67 schools, 20 were newly listed in 2025 only and ranked below the top 50, and another school was no longer listed in 2025. These schools were excluded, and therefore 46 schools were initially defined as the Next 50. To balance the group sizes, 4 schools with the worst ranking performance from the Top 50 were moved to the Next 50. All these schools had 1 year ranked beyond 50, and the other 2 years’ rankings were close to the bottom of the Top 50 (e.g. 49 or 50). Ultimately, each group contained 50 schools.

## Data extraction of institution-level bibliometric indicators
Institution-level bibliometric indicators were exported from the Elsevier SciVal electronic database, which is powered by Scopus data. The ‘Compare > Benchmark all metrics’ function was chosen. The 100 institutions were searched for and selected from the institution list. One dental school, ACTA (Academisch Centrum Tandheelkunde Amsterdam), is a joint venture between the University of Amsterdam and Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam. Hence, ACTA was manually defined using the ‘Group of institutions’ function. Afterwards, data were limited to the last 10 years (2016-2025), and the subject area was limited to Dentistry according to the ASJC (All Science Journal Classifications) code. By doing so, institutional-level bibliometric indicators were computed based on papers published in dental journals, the methodology used by QS Subject Ranking. 
Twenty bibliometric indicators (called ‘metric’ in SciVal) were recorded: scholarly output (commonly known as publication count), citation count, CPP, field-weighted citation impact (FWCI), cited publications percentage, scholarly output cited by policies, international/national/institutional collaboration percentage, single authorship percentage, international/national/institutional collaboration/single authorship impact (i.e. CPP per category), percentage of output in the top 1%/10% citation percentiles, and percentage of publications in the Q1/Q2/Q3/Q4 journal quartile by CiteScore.

## Link to QS indicators
To clarify potential circularity, we mapped our bibliometric indicators to indicators used by QS‑subject ranking. One indicator, namely CPP, is similarly used by QS. QS also uses an indicator called International Research Network (IRN) which records sustained partnerships with schools in other countries that result in ≥3 joint papers published in a 5-year period. Although IRN is conceptually related to international collaboration percentage used by our study, readers should notice that IRN is currently not used in QS Dentistry ranking. Outside QS indicators are policy citations, journal‑quartile distribution, collaboration impact metrics and field‑normalised metrics (FWCI).

## Statistical analysis
We compared the Top 50 and Next 50 dental schools for each independent bibliometric indicator using Welch’s two-sample t-test (two-sided; unequal variances). Effect sizes were reported as Hedges’ g and interpreted as small (0.2), medium (0.5) and large (0.8). Meanwhile, χ2 tests were used to evaluate the distribution of scholarly output by collaborative modes (international, national, institutional and single author) and by journal quartile (Q1-Q4). Effect sizes were reported as Cramer’s V and interpreted as small (0.1), medium (0.3) and large (0.5). The comparisons were done in 3 panels: 2016-2025 (full period), 2016-2020 and 2021-2025.
Meanwhile, Pearson correlation tests were performed to evaluate the relationship among all bibliometric indicators using data from the full period (2016-2025).
All analyses were conducted using Python. Since many tests were conducted within each analytic family (all t-tests in a panel; two χ2 tests in a panel; all correlation pairs), we controlled the expected proportion of false positives using the Benjamini–Hochberg False Discovery Rate (BH–FDR) procedure. Hence, FDR-adjusted P values were computed and reported throughout the entire manuscript. For simplicity, all FDR-adjusted P values are simply referred to as P values. Results with P < .05 were considered statistically significant.

## Exploratory analysis by dividing the 100 dental schools into 4 groups
To further evaluate the differences between the schools, we divided them into 4 groups: Top 10 (actually 11 schools due to ties), 12–20, 21–50 and 51–100. Welch’s ANOVA was performed to check if there were significant differences between the groups for the aforementioned independent bibliometric indicators. Chi-squared tests were used to evaluate the distribution of scholarly output by collaborative modes (international, national, institutional and single author) and by journal quartile (Q1-Q4). The comparisons were done with the full period data only (2016-2025). The tests were similarly adjusted for multiple comparisons using the BH–FDR procedure.

## Results

## Main results across 2016-2025
Across the entire survey period (2016-2025), significant group differences were found for 10 bibliometric indicators. Notably, the mean total citation count of the Top 50 dental schools nearly doubled that of the Next 50 (25420.5 ± 13263.2 vs 13,067.2 ± 3,358.0, P < .001). The mean number of scholarly output being cited by policy documents from the Top 50 was 1.76 times that from the Next 50 (75.6 ± 41.6 vs 43.0 ± 19.0, P < .001). Besides, the Top 50 on average published 1.64 times more than the Next 50 (1,380.9 ± 929.7 vs 840.5 ± 314.7, P = .001). The Top 50 also had significantly higher mean CPP, national collaboration impact, institutional collaboration impact, single authorship impact and percentage of output in the top 1% and top 10% citation percentiles with modest to large effect sizes ( ). At the same time, the collaborative patterns and journal quartile distributions were significantly different between the Top 50 and the Next 50 ( ) (both P < .001), with the Top 50 having a larger ratio of international collaboration (47.0% vs 44.3%) and scholarly output in Q1 journals (58.0% vs 52.6%).
 Correlation tests indicated that the overall CPP was significantly positively correlated with 10 bibliometric indicators ( ), notably percentage of international collaboration (r = 0.50, P < .001), international collaboration impact (r = 0.89, P < .001), percentage of output in the top 1% citation percentile (r = 0.86, P < .001), percentage of output in the top 10% citation percentile (r = 0.73, P < .001), and percentage of publications in Q1 journals (r = 0.48, P < .001). Meanwhile, CPP was significantly negatively correlated with percentage of national or institutional collaboration, as well as percentage of publications in Q2-Q4 journals.
 

## Secondary results for subsets of 2016-2020 and 2021-2025
For the first half of the survey period (2016-2020), significant group differences were found for 5 bibliometric indicators. The mean total citation count of the Top 50 dental schools doubled that of the Next 50 (18,739.8 ± 9,891.2 vs 9,263.5 ± 2,543.0, P < .001). The Top 50 dental schools had more CPP (34.1 ± 10.6 vs 28.2 ± 10.2, P = .027) and higher institutional collaboration impact (22.8 ± 6.7 vs 19.1 ± 6.7, P = .027). The Top 50 also had higher mean scholarly output (589.4 ± 411.5 vs 352.6 ± 122.2, P = .002) as well as scholarly output cited by policy documents (58.6 ± 32.8 vs 32.8 ± 14.4, P < .001).
For the second half of the survey period (2021-2025), significant group differences were found for 3 bibliometric indicators. The Top 50 schools had a higher mean scholarly output count (791.5 ± 572.9 vs 487.9 ± 220.8, P = .004), scholarly output cited by policy documents (17.0 ± 10.5 vs 10.2 ± 6.6, P = .002) and citation count (6,680.7 ± 3,820.8 vs 3,803.6 ± 1,427.3, P < .001).
Moving from 2016-2020 to 2021-2025, both Top 50 and Next 50 schools showed an increased ratio in international collaboration, with the former being more prominent (Top 50: 44.3% → 49.0% vs Next 50: 42.9% → 45.3%). One notable difference was that the Top 50 decreased their scholarly output ratio with national collaboration while the Next 50 had an increased ratio in this collaborative mode ( ). Meanwhile, both Top 50 and Next 50 schools increased their publication ratio in Q1 and Q2 journals, but the Top 50 had the largest increase in Q1 journals whereas the Next 50 had the largest increase in Q2 journals. The distributions by collaborative mode and journal quartile were both significantly different between the Top 50 and the Next 50 during both periods (Cramer’s V = 0.02-0.07: small effect sizes, P < .05).
 

## Exploratory results
When schools were divided into 4 groups, significant intergroup differences were found for scholarly output count (P = .001), citation count (P < .001), scholarly output cited by policy documents (P < .001) and institutional collaboration impact (P = 0.012). shows the statistics for the 4 groups. Post hoc pairwise comparisons showed that the Top 10 schools had significantly higher mean scholarly output counts and institutional collaboration impact than schools ranked 51-100. The top 10 schools had significantly higher mean citation counts and scholarly output cited by policy documents than schools ranked 21-50. The differences between the top 10 schools and schools ranked 12–20 did not reach statistical significance. Chi-square test results showed that there were significant differences in the distribution of scholarly output based on both journal quartiles and collaborative modes (both P < .001) ( ). The top 10 dental schools had a higher ratio of international collaborations, whereas the other 3 groups had more national and institutional collaborations. Meanwhile, the ratio of scholarly output in Q1 journals declined stepwise from the Top 10 to ranks 12-20, 21-50 and 51-100.
 

## Discussion
This study provides a large, multiyear comparison of institution‑level bibliometric indicators between the Top 50 and Next 50 dental schools ranked by the QS Dentistry subject ranking. Several clear and consistent patterns emerged across the full 10‑year period and the two 5‑year subperiods, offering important insights into the research behaviours associated with high QS performance.
First, publishing in high‑impact (Q1) dental journals is a defining characteristic of the Top 50 dental schools. Top‑ranked schools tended to publish a larger share of their work in high‑impact journals, consistent with stratified publishing practices. This pattern aligns with the strong positive correlation we observed between CPP and Q1 proportion, reinforcing the established link between journal prestige, visibility and citation performance. Publishing in higher‑impact journals may therefore play a dual role, not only enhancing citation rates but also improving QS citation‑based metrics.
Second, international collaboration was consistently higher among the Top 50 schools across all periods. Our findings support the widely observed phenomenon that internationally co‑authored papers attract more citations and are more likely to appear in high‑impact journals. , , Strengthening structured international partnerships may thus be a practical route for schools aiming to enhance both their research quality and their global reputation.
Third, the Top 50 schools produced substantially more scholarly output that was cited in policy documents. Although policy citations are not currently a QS indicator, they reflect real‑world influence and may enhance visibility to global stakeholders. , For institutions aiming to strengthen their societal impact, cultivating research programs with clear clinical, translational or public health relevance may be valuable.
Fourth, citation impact matters on top of publishing volume. Although the Top 50 schools produced more papers overall, group differences in citations per publication, top 1% citation percentiles and top 10% percentiles suggest that higher‑ranked schools excel by generating more influential work. This mirrors our previous finding that institutions hosting more high‑impact scientists tended to achieve higher QS scores, and echoes a previous study that found multiple Top 2% dental scholars concentrated at elite institutions. 
Fifth, patterns observed in the full 10‑year dataset were also present in the 2016-2020 and 2021-2025 subsets. This suggests that the distinctions between Top 50 and Next 50 schools are systemic and persistent rather than short‑term fluctuations. It also reflects that the top schools may enjoy cumulative advantage, otherwise known as the Matthew effect, and that institutional prestige could relate to institutional stratification as a governmental policy and serves as a catalyst to promote their status further. Notably, both groups increased their proportion of Q1 output and international collaborations over time, but the Top 50 expanded faster in Q1 publishing, whereas the Next 50 expanded relatively more in Q2 publishing. This divergence may reflect differences in academic networks, research capacity or institutional strategy. However, readers should note that there are multiple journal rankings, and some niche topics may not have dedicated Q1 journals. 
For dental faculties, the aforementioned results highlight institution‑level factors associated with impact, such as network breadth (international partners), venue mix (share in top‑quartile dental journals) and knowledge translation (policy‑document citations). Actions that strengthen research mentorship, multicentre clinical studies and global consortia may improve both capacity and visibility, with downstream implications for education, specialist training and oral health equity.
Readers should be aware that amongst the bibliometric indicators discussed above, CPP is directly related to the citation-based indicator adopted by the QS ranking formula for dentistry. One may argue that the percentage of output in the top 1% and top 10% are metrics highly related to CPP and thus potentially closely tied to QS dentistry ranking. Other than these, indicators such as percentage of output in Q1 journals, percentage of international collaboration, citation by policy documents and publishing volume are independent of the QS dentistry ranking formula mechanics.
To summarize, our cross‑sectional findings suggest that dental schools in higher-ranking tiers are usually associated with higher research output shares in Q1 journals, more frequent international collaborations and higher citation impact. However, no causal inferences could be attributed to the study design. Also, the significant group differences in the output distribution in terms of journal quartiles and collaborative modes demonstrated a small effect size. Policymakers should understand that QS ranking also depends on other nonbibliometric indicators such as academic reputation and employer reputation.
The study has several limitations common to bibliometric research. To begin with, SciVal/Scopus coverage varies across regions and disciplines, which may affect schools with strong local or nonindexed journals. Further, institutional naming variations (e.g. multi‑campus dental schools) and rebranding (e.g. Tokyo Medical and Dental University has recently merged with another school to become Institute of Science Tokyo) potentially introduce data irregularities that may not be completely corrected. Besides, even though bibliometric indicators relate to QS performance, they do not capture the full QS methodology such as academic and employer reputation surveys. Whereas the current dataset aligns with the QS methodology, it captures all papers published in dental journals regardless of whether the authors come from the dental faculty or not, and ignores papers authored by the dental faculty but published in nondental journals. Moreover, multidisciplinary journals have multiple ASJC codes so that their designated journal quartile by CiteScore is not restricted to dentistry only, but is rather the best q1uartile across all assigned categories. Readers should note that there are 6 subcategories (3501-3506) under the umbrella category of General Dentistry (3500), so more journals are likely to be in Q1 than when they are classified by the system of Journal Citation Reports (JCR) Journal Impact Factor (JIF). As a validity check, we compared Scopus CiteScore-based quartiles with JCR JIF quartiles considering the overall Dentistry category using 2023-2024 journal data. The two systems still showed substantial quartile agreement (κ = 0.69-0.72) and very strong JIF-CiteScore correlations (ρ = 0.90-0.92), confirming that our findings regarding quartile distributions are relatively robust. Meanwhile, correlation analyses included multiple interrelated metrics which might potentially have multicollinearity. Finally, as Goodhart Law states, ‘when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure’, which means publication and citation metrics may become less useful or distinguishable if the academia collectively aims to achieve better performance in terms of these metrics. 

## Conclusions
Top‑ranked dental schools consistently outperformed the next tier in Q1 publishing, international collaboration, policy‑relevant outputs and high‑impact citation indicators. Moreover, exploratory analyses showed that the ratio of scholarly output in Q1 dental journals declined stepwise from the Top 10 to ranks 12-20, 21-50 and 51-100. These findings suggested practical, data‑driven pathways for dental schools seeking to strengthen their scientific influence and rise within the QS Dentistry ranking.
