On May 13, 2025, in partnership with the Alzheimer's Association of the United States, the World Dementia Council (WDC) held its inaugural Satellite Summit in Lima, Peru. This summit, the culminating event in the WDC's Latin America and Caribbean series, convened key stakeholders to discuss the public policy landscape across the region and how advocacy can be leveraged to advance efforts around dementia prevention, quality care, and research.
1.

## INTRODUCTION
Lenny Shallcross, executive director of the WDC, opened the meeting by emphasizing both the opportunity and urgency to address dementia through an integrated approach, including public health, policy, research, medicine, clinical practice, and more. Dr. Joanne Pike, president and chief executive officer of the Alzheimer's Association of the United States and chair of the WDC, shared her hope that the meeting would deepen understanding of how research can be effectively translated into policy and action. She also highlighted the importance of this meeting in being a venue to foster global dialogue, share diverse perspectives, and help adapt dementia strategies to meet the unique needs of individual countries across Latin America and the Caribbean.

2.

## DEMENTIA IN LATIN AMERICA AND THE CARIBBEAN: THE REGIONAL CHALLENGE
Co‐chairs of the meeting, Dr. Paulo Caramelli, professor of neurology at Federal University of Minas Gerais, and Dr. Ricardo Allegri, director of the Aging and Memory Center at FLENI, introduced the first session focused on regional challenges related to dementia.
Dr. Nilton Custodio, professor of medicine, Instituto Peruano de Neurociências, highlighted key challenges in the region, including clinical diagnostics, biomarkers, and access to innovative treatments for Alzheimer's disease (AD). He emphasized that many diagnostic tools are developed in the United States or European nations and are applied locally without appropriate cultural or linguistic adaptation. Consequently, these assessments yield inaccurate and unreliable results. Dr. Custodio stressed the need to adapt cognitive tests and diagnostic processes to ensure they are valid, relevant, and appropriate for the population they are intended to assess. This is proving to be a substantial challenge in the region and one that is central to the improvement of dementia care and research.
Dr. Allegri echoed the challenges surrounding dementia diagnosis and access to new treatments in the region, reiterating the importance of ensuring that diagnostic tools and protocols are culturally adapted to reflect the diversity of the region. He also added the lack of prevention measures and access to biomarkers as major barriers to progress. Given the region's disproportionately high prevalence of dementia risk factors, there is an urgent need to implement effective risk reduction strategies. However, although prevention efforts are cost effective in the long term, support from the public and government for such efforts is lacking.
Dr. Daisy Acosta, associate professor of geriatrics at Universidad Nacional Pedro Henriques Urena in the Dominican Republic, discussed the challenges surrounding workforce and training. In the Caribbean, there is a shortage of trained professionals capable of conducting clinical trials, as well as a lack of data analysts to advance research and inform policy change. Dr. Acosta stressed the need to develop and make accessible more technical training to equip the local workforce with the necessary skills, while also creating more professional opportunities within the region to retain talent and advance dementia‐related efforts.
Another major barrier is the limited availability of geriatric training centers, which contributes to the shortage of qualified dementia care professionals. Stigma also remains a significant issue. Many families are reluctant to share or acknowledge a dementia diagnosis with their loved ones. This stigma can lead to ethical and legal issues and hesitancy in obtaining a diagnosis, ultimately leading to lower detection and diagnosis rates across the region.
To address these challenges, public education is paramount. Educating communities and the government on AD awareness can help reduce the stigma and advance efforts to address the disease. AD associations across the region also play a critical role in promoting education, raising awareness, and destigmatization.
Despite these barriers, speakers emphasized the wealth of talent and opportunity within Latin America and the Caribbean. Investing in the local workforce and building upon the skills, expertise, and dedication of those already living in the region is vital to advancing dementia research, care, and policy efforts.

3.

## THE ECOSYSTEM IN LATIN AMERICA: DEMENTIA RESEARCH
Dementia research in Latin America is scarce. The number of scientific publications, grants, and clinical trials taking place in the region is very low. There is an urgent need and significant opportunity to increase Latin America's representation in the global research space. The following session examined public policy challenges in conducting research and clinical trials in the region and discussed how regional and international collaborations can help strengthen the research ecosystem.
Dr. Maria Carrillo, chief science officer and medical affairs lead at the Alzheimer's Association of the United States, emphasized the Alzheimer's Association's commitment to investing in research, scientists, and training programs across Latin America. LatAm FINGERS is a prime example of successful regional collaboration and clinical trial execution within Latin America that can serve as a model for others. Dr. Carrillo stressed the importance of creating more training opportunities for the next generation of dementia researchers as well as the need to listen to Latin American researchers and stakeholders to understand how to best support them, ensuring efforts are aligned with local needs.
Dr. Rema Raman, professor of neurology at the Kirk School of Medicine at the University of Southern California, described her role in helping establish the National Institute on Aging and Alzheimer's Association‐funded Institute on Methods and Protocols for Advancement of Clinical Trials in ADRD (IMPACT‐AD). This program aims to educate and train the next generation of clinical trial researchers and future principal investigators in the field of AD and related dementias clinical trial research. Importantly, the goal goes beyond scientists, providing training for entire research teams. Dr. Raman noted recent efforts to expand this work globally in partnership with the Global Brain Health Institute. She emphasized that while the dementia burden in the region is growing, there is growing momentum and opportunity to address the disease by building on initiatives like LatAm FINGERS.
Dr. Gustavo Sevlever, professor of pathology at FLENI, stressed the importance of translating science and research into easily understood messaging for the public that can be used to gain support and increase awareness. Dr. Sevlever also noted the need to train future doctors and researchers in both geriatrics and clinical trial methodology as a way to generate knowledge on new dementia therapeutics. He also called for expanding high‐quality regional data resources.
Dr. Eduardo Zimmer, assistant professor, Universidade Federal do Rio Grande do Sul, Porto Alegre, emphasized the need to strengthen and consolidate scientific research in Latin America by conducting disruptive research and innovative studies. He also highlighted the importance of investing in medical education to improve dementia prevention and treatment.
The group discussion underscored the many unique strengths of the region, including a shared language, which can facilitate collaboration in advancing dementia research. They also encouraged the engagement of students and young professionals. Their energy and enthusiasm make them vital partners in advancing local research efforts.

4.

## THE ECOSYSTEM IN LATIN AMERICA: PREVENTION
Building upon the previous session, speakers then discussed dementia prevention across the region, including the science of prevention, understanding the Latin American and Caribbean context, and opportunities for public policy interventions.
Dr. Lucia Crivelli, head of neuropsychology at FLENI, opened the session and called on fellow researchers and health professionals to act urgently on risk reduction. Through LatAm FINGERS, researchers are working to identify, validate, and adapt risk reduction strategies specific to Latin America with the goal to inform future public policy. Dr. Crivelli also made a call to action to create complementary dialogues between dementia treatment and risk reduction so as to not silo them from each other.
Dr. Andrea Slachevsky, associate professor at the University of Chile, highlighted the importance of focusing on prevention across the entire life course, including among people who have already been diagnosed, as risk reduction strategies may help slow the progression of the disease. It is also important to consider and address the social determinants of health that may influence dementia risk to reduce disparities across the population. Finally, Dr. Slachevsky emphasized that risk reduction efforts must engage health‐care providers, policy makers, researchers, and the public to maximize impact.
Dr. Heather Snyder, senior vice president of medical & scientific relations at the Alzheimer's Association of the United States, spoke to the value of small, measurable wins in driving momentum for dementia risk reduction. Dr. Snyder reflected on the success of global collaborations like the 10/66 research program and LatAm FINGERS, which have laid the groundwork for harmonized research and interventions. She also underscored the critical role of global collaboration, combination therapies, and policy engagement, stressing the need to align research, action, and local priorities to continue driving progress in dementia prevention and risk reduction.
During the discussion, speakers shared the significance of cross‐sector collaboration in advancing risk‐related efforts on cardiovascular disease and diabetes. The upcoming results from LatAm FINGERS will provide additional insights into risk reduction strategies and the long‐term impact of lifestyle intervention on cognition. Participants also drew lessons from successful public health efforts like tobacco reduction and stressed the need for clear, accessible messaging, like the Alzheimer's Association's 10 Healthy Habits for Your Brain campaign, to promote brain health.
Prevention efforts must occur across the entire life course, including younger and mid‐life populations, as prevention is more effective when started early, although it is never too late to act. AD associations across Latin America and the Caribbean are also invaluable resources that can be leveraged to help spread awareness and drive action on risk reduction.

5.

## THE DECADE AHEAD
The following session explored how to advance dementia research and progress in the coming decade, while also elevating dementia as a public health and policy priority.
Dr. Lea Grinberg, the John Douglas French Alzheimer's Endowered Professor at the University of California, San Francisco, celebrated the significant acceleration of dementia research across Latin America within the last 10 years, but noted that recent political shifts could affect this, so researchers need to find new ways to sustain the progress. She also reiterated the importance of retaining researchers within the region to reduce brain drain and advance local research. Dr. Grinberg also pointed to biomarkers, especially blood and saliva, as promising tools to expand research and diagnosis in the area.
Dr. Ana Luisa Sosa, professor at the UNAM Faculty of Medicine in Mexico, stressed the need for continued collaboration in dementia research, particularly involving and training young scientists over the next decade. The growth of regional consortiums like LatAm FINGERS and the 10/66 research group have significantly advanced dementia knowledge in the region. Over the next 10 years, Dr. Sosa called for researchers to gain a greater understanding of region‐ and country‐specific dementia risk factors, like malnourishment among pregnant women and maternal education levels. She also advocated for positioning dementia as a public health priority to drive prevention efforts and long‐term health‐care savings.
Dr. Claudia Duran‐Aniotz, co‐director of the Latin America Brain Health Institute in Chile, highlighted the importance of empowering young Latin American researchers through multidisciplinary collaboration and creating opportunities for the next generation of dementia researchers to return to and work in Latin America. Dr. Duran‐Aniotz stressed the need to develop Latin America–specific biomarker data and explore new biomarkers beyond the standard amyloid/tau/neurodegeneration framework. She advocated for more research focusing on younger populations and prevention strategies and emphasized the vast research opportunities available in the region.
Speakers then shared immediate actions to accelerate dementia progress, including retaining locally educated researchers, analyzing existing consortium data, engaging and inspiring new students in the field, and training students in consortium research documentation and academic paper writing.
After the presentations, attendees discussed the importance of clear, adaptable communication strategies tailored to diverse audiences to improve awareness. They also highlighted the need to address the gender gap in the research field, suggesting that building partnerships and consortiums among female researchers in the field can help promote equity and opportunity.

6.

## ADVANCING ADVOCACY
During the summit's last session, speakers focused on the role of advocacy in driving progress for people living with dementia and their caregivers and how national and international organizations can support these efforts. Broadly, this session focused on a key question: What are the most effective ways to transform research into action with the help of government and society?
Dr. Celene Pinheiro, president of the Brazilian Alzheimer's Association (ABRAS), shared the work of ABRAS, which engages the public and government to advocate for people living with dementia and their caregivers. ABRAS is also working to promote the development of a national plan to address dementia, noting that although there are some current laws in place focused on this population, greater emphasis is needed on risk reduction, public education, and awareness efforts.
Diego Aguilar, Regional Director Americas at Alzheimer's Disease International, highlighted the critical role of civil society in advancing dementia efforts across Latin America. Drawing on examples from the response to the human immunodeficiency virus epidemic, he emphasized the need for stronger investment in civil society leadership, collaboration, and advocacy capacity. Mr. Aguilar also stressed the importance of support groups as the foundation of many AD organizations and called attention to the vital leadership role of women in this work. He also urged stakeholders to work across sectors to drive progress for people living with dementia.
Dr. Christopher Butler, clinical reader in global brain health at Imperial College London, emphasized the need to align research with advocacy to drive meaningful policy change for dementia in Latin America. He argued that research must be participatory and involve patients, caregivers, policy makers, and society, from design to implementation. He highlighted Impact Salud, a UK‐funded initiative in Peru working across four regions to improve dementia diagnosis, care, and policy through community‐based, locally adapted interventions. The project integrates technology, caregiver support, and economic evaluation to strengthen health systems and advocacy. Dr. Butler called for building advocacy skills among researchers and clinicians, promoting patience, persistence, and strategic partnerships across sectors.
During the discussion, participants emphasized the role of society in dementia awareness and care, proposing grassroots strategies to reduce stigma and promote inclusion. Suggestions included engaging youth groups like Scouts, leveraging community leaders, and using communication via local radio, churches, and easy‐to‐understand materials. Others stressed the need to amplify indigenous voices, expand partnerships between researchers and the state, and build regional structures to uplift aging and dementia as public health priorities. The session concluded with calls to strengthen networks, develop local research capacity, promote prevention, and secure sustainable funding sources.

7.

## CLOSING REFLECTIONS
The summit co‐chairs concluded the event with calls to strengthen the response to dementia in Latin America and the Caribbean through fortifying partnerships among government, researchers, and the public. Key priorities identified for the future included building awareness to reduce stigma, expanding research capacity through infrastructure and training, and integrating dementia within existing public health and risk reduction strategies. They closed by stressing the importance of leveraging the many regional strengths, such as shared language and successful models of cooperation, to advance action on dementia.
