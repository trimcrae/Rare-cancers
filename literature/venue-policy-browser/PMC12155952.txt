## Summary
This paper argues for the need for an integrated approach towards cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) and mental health (MH) disorders. Despite non-communicable diseases (NCDs) being a major public health problem and thus a prime concern for primary health centres, the strong interplay between CVDs and MH conditions as two major NCDs is largely overlooked. Management for MH and CVDs tends to be dichotomized between mental and physical health. This paper continues a publication by a Southeast Asian panel by using Indonesia as a national case study. The paper describes the results of a discussion by an interdisciplinary team of national experts on barriers to NCD management in current policy and clinical implementation. The thematic review provides a basis for the recommendation for an integrated cardiovascular and mental health approach to optimize policy for NCD prevention and management.
Keywords: Cardiovascular disease, Expert opinion, Health policy, Mental health, Non-communicable disease

## Introduction
Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) account for 62% of total deaths and approximately 48% of mortality in persons below 70 years in Southeast Asia, a region whose population is predominantly under-65. , , NCDs in productive age have compounding financial and social implications. In 2016, Indonesia—the region's most populous country—had 1,365,000 premature deaths, 73% from NCDs. Without appropriate action, the region will bear the impacts from the 21% predicted rise of NCDs-related mortality in the next decade. Despite noticeable improvements in health profile (i.e., gains in life expectancy and communicable disease outcomes), the prevalence of NCDs is rising, increasing the economic and health burden. , Moreover, Indonesia still faces a double burden of communicable disease (CDs) and NCDs that varies across provinces. Despite a decline in infectious disease from 1990 to 2019, tuberculosis, diarrheal disease and lower respiratory infections are still main sources of disability adjusted life years (DALYs), while mortality and DALYs from NCDs and their risk factors account for larger proportion of health loss. 
Among NCDs, cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of mortality, accounting for approximately 35% of total deaths. The nine diseases that are considered catastrophic in Indonesia include heart disease, stroke, and diabetes mellitus. Ten diseases with the highest mortality rate include stroke, heart diseases, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension. Although mental health (MH) disorders such as depression can cause severe personal and social consequences, it is somehow neglected from the list of catastrophic diseases. Mortality rate due to suicide is also neglected, despite it being the main cause of death among young people around the world. Hypertension, as one of the most common conditions, interlinks CVDs and MH conditions. Stress can increase blood pressure, and subsequently the risk for stroke and heart disease.
Stress and MH conditions still carry a stigma, limiting assessment and treatment. A recent study from Indonesia estimated a suicide underreporting rate of 859.10%, the highest observed in a nationally representative sample. Even though MH treatment is largely covered in the Indonesian Universal Health Coverage (UHC), self-harm and suicide are excluded, and are often masked under diagnoses such as organophosphate intoxication, asphyxias, or injuries.
Individuals struggling with stress and MH conditions face major barriers to recovery. , Patients are often reluctant to seek professional help for fear of social, work/study-related discrimination or judgement, which often causes further isolation and suffering, worsening conditions. Human rights may even be an issue, e.g. even though the Constitutional Court ruling has granted voting rights to individuals with mental disabilities, its execution remains hindered by various administrative and infrastructure issues. 
To address challenges related to MH, collaborative efforts between the government and other key stakeholders are necessary. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends educational strategies to correct myths and misconceptions, as well as contact strategies to shift negative societal attitudes through direct interactions with individuals experiencing mental health conditions. The Ministry of Health promotes education and social engagement to reduce mental health stigma to combat the tendency to label persons with MH conditions. Comparatively, individuals suffering from high blood pressure, for example, are not referred as “hypertensives” with “cardiovascular disability” that precludes high-stress positions, despite the risk of stroke or heart attack. In primary health centres, CVD and MH are often segregated in a dichotomy between mental and physical health. Patients have their blood pressure checked and treated while MH tends to be neglected. Ideally, stress levels, mood, and cognitive processes or behaviour could also be monitored and managed.
CVDs and MH often coincide and have convergent risk factors. Stress and various mental disorders are interlinked with modifiable risk factors unhealthy diet, inadequate physical activity, tobacco and alcohol use, increasing risks of CVDs and other NCDs internationally , and in Indonesia, , , contributing to DALYs. The INTERHEART study, involving 52 countries, reported that psychological factors, such as depression, stress, and significant life events are attributable risk factors for acute myocardial infarction. Likewise, the INTERSTROKE study involving 32 countries revealed that among ten potentially modified CVDs risk factors associated with approximately 90% of the population attributable risk (PAR) for stroke, psychosocial factors has an odds ratio of 2.20 (1.78–2.72, 95% CI). Depression is also correlated with diabetes mellitus, with reduced activity, dietary changes, and risk of complications. , , , , , , Recent empirical evidence demonstrates that risk factors for NCDs are frequently clustered and strongly associated with psychological distress even from the age of 16–18 years. 
Despite NCDs being a major health issue, as many as 30% of Indonesians with moderate-to-high cardiovascular risk have not received proper treatment and counselling. Economic disparity, healthcare access, and fragmented management contribute to insufficient NCD management. , , This paper calls for integrating CVD and mental health care to prevent and treat NCDs effectively.

## Methods
This paper was developed by a team of experts in clinical and community health across Indonesia. Leaders of national medical organizations representing different disciplines, including cardiology, psychiatry, psychology, community medicine/family medicine, internal medicine and endocrinology, clinical nutrition, pharmacology, pharmacy, some of them additionally representing consumers. The movement began in the meeting “NCDs in Focus” in Bangkok, Thailand on 3–4 August 2019, which brought together a panel of experts from across Southeast Asia. Insights were collected using Workmat® methodology–a poster-based exercise to optimize interaction among the participants. , This initial meeting resulted in a paper using thematic analysis on gaps in NCDs management. Following the regional meeting, a national meeting for Indonesia was conducted in Jakarta in early 2020, which generated a consensus on the need for integrated NCDs management. The progress towards this paper was halted during the COVID-19 pandemic, which in itself exacerbated MH and CVDs problems, highlighting the importance for this work to be resumed. The team has launched a concerted effort to qualitatively document barriers to NCDs management from a policy perspective, using Indonesia as a national case study. The interdisciplinary team of national experts collectively reviewed the barriers to NCD management in current policy and clinical implementation.
The resulting thematic review provides the basis for the development of the recommendation for an integrated cardiovascular and mental health approach to optimize policy for NCD prevention and management.

## COVID-19 and NCD management
The COVID-19 pandemic caused bereavement, job/income loss, social isolation, and other stressors, affecting mental health, exacerbating existing mental and cardiovascular disorders, and increasing alcohol/drug use, insomnia, and anxiety. COVID-19 also lead to neurological and MH complications. People with pre-existing cardiovascular, mental, and substance use disorders were also more vulnerable to COVID-19. COVID-19 could also resulted in cardiac injury and severe disease, need for intensive care, and mortality. 
Fear of contracting COVID-19 in healthcare settings led to a significant reduction in visits for necessary care for NCDs. At the same time, the COVID-19 demonstrated potential strengths that could be tapped into for NCD management, such as risk-communication programs, vaccination program mostly rolled out through the Puskesmas; teleconsultation by applications and mobile phones that provided information, reduced anxiety, and assisted the sick at home, as well as neighbour support to provide daily meals and over-the-counter medication to maintain physical distancing. The surge in telemedicine services now offer a potential solution for maintaining continuity of care. However, integration and reimbursement of telemedicine services remain limited, leaving patients with fragmented options that often lack data privacy and security safeguards or are not integrated into national healthcare systems. 
The COVID-19 pandemic also significantly altered Indonesia's health expenditure landscape, with a dramatic increase in total health spending from 1.6% of GDP in 2019 to 2.6% in 2021. This surge was primarily driven by public health spending channelled through the UHC's infrastructure; with COVID-19 response expenditures representing a substantial portion—45% in 2020 and 72% in 2021—of all non-COVID public health spending. This likely impacted spending on other essential health services and programs. In the aftermath of the pandemic, long-term CVDs and MH disorders are also a concern that require further attention, investigation and management.

## Theme 1: mapping of framework, regulations, and policies
The World Health Organization (WHO) has established a global strategy for the prevention and control of NCDs, whose objective is to initiate and integrate national policies for the prevention and control of NCDs across all government departments. It emphasises interventions to reduce modifiable risk factors for NCDs and encourages partnerships to prevent and control NCDs. At the regional level, the guiding principles for NCDs prevention and control by the WHO Regional Office for Southeast Asia also promotes integrated multi sectoral works, including sectors outside healthcare, and integration to address NCDs as a group of diseases. This comprises comprehensive promotive and preventive approach to modify the population's lifestyle as well as the treatment and care. 
The Indonesian government has initiated social health insurance since 2014. By the end of 2024, the Indonesia's Universal Health Coverage (UHC) has covered more than 279 million of the country's population, making this system the biggest single-payer UHC system in the world. The payment system uses a capitation system based on performance for primary health care and a case-based group (CBG) system for hospitals, which covers medical consultation, diagnostic, procedure, and medication fees, including some preventive services. It is expected to provide affordable and effective healthcare services to accommodate diverse health needs. , The UHC is expected to move to universal risk coverage (URC), which stresses the elimination and control of proximal risk factors/and lifestyle such as high stress, unhealthy diet, lack of exercise. This aims to lower the direct risk of CVDs and other NCDs. Preventive and curative management targets to reduce the number of patients with dyslipidaemia, hypertension, and smoking habits, as factors with the highest population attributable risk (PAR) to CVDs. The approach needs to adequately address the MH aspect of such lifestyle factors.
Despite the fact that hypertension is a significant risk factor for CVDs-related mortality, , only 18.1% out of approximately 34% of the population with hypertension took antihypertensive therapy. , While dyslipidaemia management guidelines have been issued by several international bodies such as the European Society of Cardiology (ESC), the European Atherosclerosis Society (EAS) and), the American College of Cardiology (ACC), the American Heart Association (AHA), the applicability of such guidelines for Indonesian population has not been ascertained.
In terms of a dietary approach, in 2013, the Indonesian Ministry of Health established a regulation requiring processed food products and fast-food products to put nutritional information (glucose, sodium, and fat) on the product. The health information on the label read “glucose consumption of more than 50 g, sodium consumption of more than 2000 mg, or total fat consumption of more than 67 g per day increase the risk of hypertension, stroke, diabetes, and heart attack”. This regulation supports the WHO statement to reduce modifiable risk factors NCDs. Additionally, in 2016 the Ministry of Health initiated a national committee to manage cardiocerebrovascular diseases, which issued the national standard of CVDs prevention and promotion to apply in the country. The Ministry of Home Affairs also set a regulation for local governments to assure the implementation of minimum health service standards, including health promotion, disease prevention and early detection, and treatment for diseases. 
From the many different risk factors, smoking is a serious issue in Indonesia. Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) for NCDs aim to reduce one-third of premature mortality from NCDs through tobacco and alcohol control and to achieve UHC, Indonesian smokers spent 11% of their total income on average on tobacco consumption. The treatment cost of smoking in Indonesia amass to US$2177 million or about 2.5% of Indonesia's gross domestic product (GDP). To control tobacco consumption, the government has increased tobacco tax and broadcasted the harm caused by smoking. The government has also implemented non-smoking areas in most public places and working areas within the city or regency. 
In addition, the government has shown support for the global strategy to prevent NCDs is by initiating a national committee for cancer control as well as a multisectoral effort by the Ministry of Health, Ministry of Home Affairs, and local government under the Community Healthy Life Movement. A strategy under the acronym of “CERDIK” (smart) campaign persuades people to: 1) come for regular medical check-ups, 2) stop smoking, 3) engage in more physical activities, 4) consume a healthy diet, 5) get adequate rest, 6) manage stress. 
In February 2025, the Indonesian MoH has launched free medical check-ups for every citizen on their birthday (Cek Kesehatan Gratis—CKG) as an NCDs screening program at the Puskesmas (community health care centre at every district), which includes assessments for body mass index (BMI), blood pressure, blood sugar, and cholesterol, highlighting the importance of prevention and early detection. Uptake for the program is increasing, with a positive yet lukewarm response from the community. The check-up covers points related to CVDs, such as blood pressure as well as cholesterol testing, previously conducted by indication and referral from the general practitioner. Mental health is still largely neglected, aside from the blanket message “to reduce stress” without much instruction, compared to hypertension, for example, which indicate counselling on low-sodium meal preparation. More follow-up for abnormal findings is also needed, with a clear pathway for comprehensive treatment.
The effect of MH disorders on CVDs and vice versa tend to be neglected despite strong correlations. Hypertension, an unhealthy diet, and tobacco use cannot be separated from MH aspects. Hypertension and other CVDs are often attributed to stress, yet there is inadequate effort to integrate comprehensive stress management as a part of healthcare services. , Unhealthy diets are also common ways of compensating for stress that should otherwise be directed towards healthy physical activity within a more comprehensive mental and physical health approach. Tobacco cessation requires MH services to help people overcome addictions and deal with related psychosocial problems. , , , Having a chronic disease in itself may lead to MH conditions if not addressed properly.
Furthermore, more attention must be paid to patients with MH conditions and CVD comorbidities. Mental disorders may affect people for most of lives. Mental disorders increases the risk of having other NCDs, as well as increases rates of morbidity and mortality. An Indonesian study found that 88% persons with schizophrenia reported cardiometabolic risks, yet only 6.8% of these had access to care. Moreover, a study of 4310 veterans found that more than 30% of patients with bipolar disorders were diagnosed with hypertension, while 23% and 17% had dyslipidaemia and diabetes, respectively. CVD prevention and management thus need to be an integral part of the service for patients with MH disorders. Standardised health services are available for patients with hypertension, diabetes mellitus, as well as mental disorders. Yet, there is still a lack of long-term integrated care that takes into account the individual and the family's needs. This is a severe loss since implementation of integrated care especially in primary care that considers locality-specific priorities and resources could potentially improve both clinical and economical outcomes. 

## Theme 2: real-life applications in primary care

## Theme 2a: the impact of financial incentive schemes on the quality of care
While Indonesia promises universal health coverage (UHC), its implementation has faced various problems, particularly: 1) financial difficulties, 2) inadequate supply and distribution of health workers, and 3) increasing number of NCDs despite persisting number of infectious diseases, as well as the effects of the previous pandemic. Inadequate quality of primary care facilities, insufficient medical supplies, and lack of robust information systems also compound the obstacles to sustaining quality UHC in Indonesia. Since 1996, the aim to provide adequate continuous and comprehensive care for patients and community has also propelled the establishment of family medicine as a specialization following medical school, yet up to now there is still only 500 family medicine specialist and 300 fellows from the 11 study programs for family medicine specialist at faculties of medicines across the Indonesian archipelago.
The low government allocation to the health sector is a crucial reason for the financial problem in the implementation of UHC. The World Bank reported that Indonesia spent only 3.1% of its gross domestic product (GDP) in 2016 for health. , Although the government has increased this from 2.9% in 2014, this figure remains lower than the mostly-cited recommendation to spend at least 5% of GDP on the health sector. This problem becomes more complicated as NCDs mean additional costs for UHC due to their slow progression and long duration. In 2010, before UHC, cardiovascular problems were estimated to have cost of US$1.56 billion in out-of-pocket spending for households’ health expenditure and indirect burden. This number was expected to increase by 34.4% to US$2.09 billion by 2020. , Indonesia has now implemented the Omnibus health law, which removed the mandatory spending for health. Inappropriate health programme expenditures has lowered government allocation for CVDs and MH programmes.
Capitation based on the performance payment systems has also caused problems in primary health care. The system uses rates excellent performance based on the following indicators: a minimum contact rate of 150‰, outpatient referral rate of non-specialist cases of no more than 2%, and a minimum of 5% of chronic disease care program in whom the participants are under control. In order to get 100% capitation, primary health centres are required to fulfil all of these indicators. To overcome this problem, in some cases, some primary health centre's providers do not include all diabetic or hypertensive patients in their chronic disease care programme, let alone patients with MH mental disorders. By excluding challenging patients, they could demonstrate a false 5% ratio of controlled participants in the chronic disease care. For patients who need further referrals, the UHC system uses the collegium recommendation for the payment of certain diseases without considering other aspects of the majority of the Indonesian CBG payment system to hospitals. This misinterpretation of scientific recommendation utilised as a foundation of the UHC payment system may cause payment discrepancies for healthcare providers. Inadvertently, such financial approaches create a disincentive for a substantial proportion of individuals with CVDs and MH disorders or risk factors to be diagnosed and treated. Low awareness and non-compliance with treatment remain problems for blood pressure control, in addition to an insufficiently allocated budget for prevention. 
Furthermore, the maldistribution of health service centres, both primary and secondary, and of health professionals throughout Indonesia remains a problem. Although the implementation of UHC reinforces the optimisation of primary health care utilisation, preventive and promotive efforts are still inadequate, while there is a large number of unnecessary referrals and a lack of quality of care at the primary level, particularly for lifelong, long-term chronic conditions. , This, in turn, results in higher rates of unnecessary hospitalisation, and thus higher healthcare costs for people with NCDs, especially those with comorbidities. 

## Theme 2b: availability, accessibility and competency issues
Managing the large number of patients with chronic diseases also poses a challenge. A primary health centre in Jakarta can handle up to 800 patients day with a limited number of health professionals. This situation means that each patient gets little time to receive adequate information about their disease and its management. The Indonesian government tried to overcome this with the implementation of chronic disease management program (Program pengelolaan penyakit kronis–Prolanis). This program prioritizes the management of diabetes mellitus and hypertension, and includes health consultation, monitoring, home visit, education group, drug education, and gymnastics. However, there is a lack of standard operating procedure, resources, and funding. There is also a lack of informed and shared decision-making scheduled sessions on diagnosis and treatment through counselling, home visits, palliative care, and transitional care. Patients thus seek information from relatives, friends, community members, social media or the internet, creating a discrepancy between the goals of the patient and the health professionals.
Furthermore, patients with mental health problems often have more difficulty accessing health centers. Depression, for example, is often missed among patients with other NCDs such as CVDs, which in turn worsens potential outcomes. There is still a tendency for a fragmented health system that separates physical health from mental health management. Despite the fact that diagnosis and treatment of many mental health conditions such as depression and schizophrenia are listed under the competency of general practitioners, there is still a tendency to focus on physical ailments and consider mental health to be the realm of a separate professional. Managing depression, for example, is not always considered part of the role of primary care physicians/family medicine specialist. Some are reluctant to prescribe psychopharmacology, and most lack the time to provide counselling, with some clinics seeing 80 patients a day.
Some primary health centres in Jakarta have employed psychologists to provide counselling, but consultations are limited to referrals, without generalized screening and outreach services. Stigma also creates a barrier for patients who may refuse seeing a psychologist due to the fear of being considered mentally ill. Patients may feel more comfortable when consulting a family doctor.

## Theme 2c: psychosocial barriers
NCDs impact not only the patient, but also the family. Patients with diabetes mellitus and hypertension are encouraged to change their diet, which may affect the meal preparation for the entire family in the same household. Similarly, individual decision-making is often overruled by the family. Social influence also plays a role. Higher levels of education do not guarantee compliance with the standard treatment. Accepting a chronic disease is not easy, and denial is commonly encountered. When the diagnosis finally sinks in, some patients condemn themselves for their disease and lose motivation to make healthy changes. In clinical practice, some patients with NCDs discontinue medical treatment and choose alternative medicine or herbal medications.
In Indonesia, mental health improvement faces an enormous challenge, including the lack of useful epidemiological data on mental disorders, ineffective service planning and resource allocation, and the prevalent stigma it causes, leading to prejudice and discrimination. Similar to in the case of other NCDs, traditional healers and religious leaders hold an important role for treatment of mental disorders, which are still sometimes seen as rooted in spiritual affliction, of external (spirits, jin, etc.) or internal (lack of faith and interaction with God) sources. Thus, collaboration between professionals is crucial to improving the control and management of NCDs and CVDs, for example, the United Kingdom's mental health strategic partnership proposed a liaison psychiatry service in all local hospitals, modification of smoking cessation services for people with MH disorders, and early identification of MH disorders in people with long-term condition to improve the integration between physical (including NCDs and CVDs control) and mental health management. 

## Theme 2d: Patient and Public Involvement for Co-designing healthcare
Healthcare services are built to improve the patient outcomes. In order to achieve better outcomes, there is a shift from traditional involvement of patients and caregivers in the evaluation of customer satisfaction into co-designing in each phase of creating and redesigning the healthcare services. Patient and public involvement (PPI) in designing and improving healthcare services has the potential to improve efficiency, quality of care, clinical outcomes, quality of services, satisfaction, perceived value and accessibility. This approach can be applied in various levels of healthcare organizations and in both higher income countries and lower-middle income countries.
While PPI has been implemented and received positive enthusiasm from the patients and caregivers in Indonesia, its implementation is still limited to mental health projects only, such as mental health festivals, youth mental health literacy tool, and task shifting in primary care for family interventions for psychosis. It requires several adjustments besides extending the implementation to NCDs patients, such as considering the unique cultural view of communal involvement that goes beyond the patient and caregiver individuals in Indonesia. In line with the WHO recommendation, it also needs to be developed for various stakeholders, including local legislators, health-care professionals, educators, community leaders, besides the individuals with personal experience of NCDs and mental illness themselves. These guides should consider important factors like engaging key local stakeholders, the availability of resources, and the existing health system arrangements at the local level. In order to ensure effective knowledge transfer and implementation, the guides should be provided in local languages and dialects.

## Future directions and recommendations for the role of primary care physicians in NCDs control
Greater initiatives are needed to strengthen the prevention of NCDs risk factors to control the increased incidence of NCDs, and, in turn, lower the cost of treatment. A paper on Africa similarly argues for NCD integration with MH as well as injuries into all levels of healthcare, especially at the community level, is crucial for effective management, to cover the areas as listed in .

## Areas to cover in NCD integration with MH.85
1.Service-Level Screening—Early detection at patient contact points.
2.Health Promotion & Prevention—Implementing strategies to address these conditions.
3.Workforce Training—Retraining health workers for better identification and management.
4.Community Health—Utilizing community health volunteers for routine management through task shifting.
5.Policy Advocacy—Engaging decision-makers to implement supportive measures.
6.Data Strengthening—Improving data collection and disaggregation for better planning.
7.Implementation Research—Understanding best approaches despite healthcare limitations.
8.Social Mobilization—Designing community-sensitive awareness campaigns for sustainability.
Specifically, the policy gaps need to be addressed by fostering Patient and Public Involvement (PPI) and multisectoral engagement through “whole of government” and “whole of society” approaches. A “health-in-all policies” approach could be used to address issues with financing, accessibility, efficiency and improved quality of health services. The proposed solutions may include.
•Enhancing the uptake of the national free medical checkup (Cek Kesehatan gratis—CKG) screening programme as well as integrating MH assessments such as Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) for children, Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) for adults, and Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) for seniors, as well as screening for risk factors such as family history of mental disorders, domestic violence/child abuse and neglect, psychosocial problems, etc.
•The screening should also lead to national trajectory programs using chronic-care model for patients screened with (1) pre-NCDs (to have scheduled counselling meeting with nutritionist, health cadres, family doctors, and allied health professionals including psychologists or mental health counsellors to approach healthier lifestyle habits including a specific stress management program and social worker for reach out services and assistance to overcome real-world stressors), (2) patients with early and stable NCDs, including MH conditions (to have scheduled counselling meeting with family doctors, hospital specialists, and other related health professionals, to obtain optimal adherence and early check on complications), (3) patients with terminal NCDs or catastrophic illnesses including MH conditions (to have scheduled serial home-visits with family doctors, palliative care program, and other related health professionals to obtain optimal transitional care from home-hospital-home).
•Integration of mental health risk assessment and stress prevention and management with Prolanis (chronic care program) in primary care settings, within the national trajectory programs of any levels of NCDs.
•National programs to incentivize persons, communities, educational institutions and workplaces to promote healthy habits such as not smoking, getting adequate physical activity, consuming a healthy diet, fulfilling the adequacy of Vitamin D level, balancing the gut microbiome, enhancing parenting skills, and enhancing positive social interactions and community engagement to improve both mental and cardiovascular health.
•Legislation to limit the use of NCDs-risk factors (e.g. smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, trans-fat use, salt intake, interpersonal violence, stress prone workplaces, unhealthy urban planning/designs, etc.).
•Capacity building of primary care physicians, such as family medicine practitioners and other primary healthcare workers in controlling NCDs including MH conditions, facilitating stress management and reducing the burden of disease as well as the government's budget for the health sector.
•Utilising Kleinman's explanatory model interview during assessment and treatment may help clinicians understand and address any cultural meaning and illness narrative that may hinder treatment, particularly the patient and family choice in treating NCDs and MH disorders with traditional medicine or spiritual healing methods.
•Larger implementation of Patient and Public Involvement (PPI) in co-designing comprehensive healthcare services which takes promotion and prevention beyond providing information.
We describe suggestions that are particularly relevant in the Indonesian context, which may also be considered for other nations in the region, tailored to specific situations. Singapore, for example, has been in the forefront in recognizing nationwide policy approaches to prevention of disease and health promotion, such as in dietary recommendations and regulations of food and beverages. At the same time, for explicit integration of MH and CVD could be beneficial throughout the region and even beyond.
In conclusion, this paper addresses the urgent need to identify and rectify deficiencies in the prevention and management of NCDs, offering recommendations to facilitate a more integrated approach to cardiovascular and mental health for better quality of life. These recommendations provide a starting point for policy development and the formation of a specific task force to conduct more in-depth exploration. Furthermore, a focused health economic study, including a cost-effectiveness analysis, is strongly recommended to develop sustainable and impactful policies that effectively reduce the burden of NCDs and improve population health.
