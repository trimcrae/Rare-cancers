YuanZheming . As of September 2022, more than 620 million infections and 6.5 million deaths have been reported . Chinese mainland has implemented a series of measures supported by PCR nucleic acid-testing (NAT) to contain the pandemic, significantly mitigating the impacts of the COVID-19 in terms of suffering, lives lost and socioeconomic progress , . To detect cases as early as possible and prevent the local outbreaks caused by the imported viruses, Chinese mainland has implemented a regular NAT sampling strategy in low-risk areas, referred to as Regular-1/7 in this paper, since April 2022; this strategy tests everyone every seven days and tests 1/7 of the population daily, considering the testing capability. Once one or more positive cases are detected, the Regular-1/7 sampling strategy will switch to citywide NAT strategy, which tests everyone within two days after local outbreaks. However, Regular-1/7 sampling strategy is extremely expensive and time-consuming. Even if 1:10 pooled testing , is widely adopted, even if the cost and the testing time are reduced to 4 yuan RMB and 0.5 h per person, and even if only 509 million residents in first, second and third-tier cities are covered, the annual testing cost and time will be as high as 100 billion yuan RMB and 13 billion hours, respectively.
Several NAT strategies have been proposed to alleviate the burden of large-scale testing. One widely adopted approach is the group testing strategy introduced by Dorfman , which involves pooling multiple samples for testing. This method significantly reduces the overall cost of NAT. Further strategies have been developed to optimize the pool size for regions with varying infection rates – . Feng et al. established an efficient method known as the fragment-length identification of pooled nucleic acid samples (FLIPNAS) , where all subsamples (n = 8) are uniquely labeled and tested simultaneously within pooled samples. FLIPNAS effectively addressed the key limitation of the Dorfman-based group testing method that still requires individual retesting of the positive sample pool. In addition, Girón-Pérez et al. proposed a pooling strategy that utilizes saliva samples as a substitute for nasopharyngeal swabs . He et al. introduced a dynamic testing site deployment strategy that reduces costs by minimizing the number of NAT sites . While these methods reduce costs either by decreasing the number of PCR tests or by limiting the number of testing sites, they do not consider reducing costs by minimizing the number of subjects tested, thereby decreasing both time and economic burdens.
Social interactions between individuals can be represented as a network, where nodes correspond to individuals and edges represent contacts between them. Traditional compartmental models, such as the SIR (susceptible, infected, recovered) model, assume disease transmission occurs in a homogeneous population. However, real-world contact patterns during epidemics are heterogeneous . The topology and evolution of real-world networks are governed by robust organizing principles , and contact-based networks are characterized by pairwise interactions that generate scale-free, disassortative, and hierarchical structures . For instance, Ziff A. L. and Ziff R. M. analyzed the spread of COVID-19 through the lens of fractal kinetics, revealing an underlying fractal or small-world network of connections between susceptible and infected individuals . Similarly, Feehan et al. have found that certain demographic groups exhibit significantly higher contact rates than the rest of population . Previous studies have shown that individuals with more contacts are more likely to become infected and spread disease , . This implies that prioritizing the sampling of hub individuals within a network could lead to earlier detection of outbreaks and more effective disease control , .
Therefore, by focusing on the priority detection of hub individuals in the contact network (Intensity of social activity), while also accounting for the degree of social overlap and the time interval since each individual’s last NAT, we propose a novel regular sampling strategy termed Weighted Hub and Time Sampling (WHTS). This strategy aims to quickly identify hidden infections in low-risk areas, reduce the alarm time, and help prevent the spread of highly infectious diseases within the population. The modelling study demonstrates that WHTS sampling strategy can significantly reduce testing costs and the alarm time and shows potential applicability to infectious diseases beyond COVID-19.

## Methods

## Regular-1/7 and Random-1/7
Regular-1/7 is a periodic NAT sampling strategy employed in low-risk areas, wherein each individual is tested once every seven days. This approach is equivalent to randomly sampling one-seventh of the population each day without replacement, considering account the immense complexity of large-scale NAT processes. In contrast, Random-1/7 serves as an alternative reference sampling strategy, where one-seventh of the total population is randomly sampled daily with replacement.

## Key concepts of WHTS
Selecting more representative individuals from the population is crucial for reducing the number of tests while maintaining the effectiveness of sampling. Since diseases spread through contact networks, the disease preferentially reaches individuals with many contacts . The first key concept we introduce is social activity intensity (ISA), which represents the number of contacts an individual has within a crowd. In social network models, ISA is also called the degree of a node. The higher the ISA, the greater the likelihood of sampling that node . For instance, in Fig.  a, the degrees of B, G, and H are 5, 4, and 4, respectively. The larger the individual ‘s degree, the higher the intensity of its social activity. Therefore, testing individual with high social activity intensity (e.g., B, G, and H) may be more efficient than testing those with lower intensity (e.g., A, C, D, E, F, I, J, and K).

 

Drawing on the principle of the minimum-Redundancy-Maximum-Relevance (mRMR) algorithm in feature selection , individuals selected for testing should not only have high social activity intensity but also exhibit lower redundancy among them to ensure broader coverage across the population. We introduce the second key concept, degree of social overlap (DSO), which measures the redundancy between candidate nodes and previously selected nodes based on shared neighbors. If a candidate node has high social activity intensity but shares many neighbors with already selected nodes, its probability of being sampled should be reduced. For instance, in Fig.  a, B and G share three neighbors (D, E, F), and B and H share two neighbors (E, F). The degrees of social overlap between B-G and B-H are 3 and 2, respectively. Once B is selected for NAT, we would prefer to choose H over G as the next testing sample, even if G and H have the same intensity of social activities.
Finally, considering the cumbersome nature of the nucleic acid testing process, an individual’s willingness to participate in subsequent tests decreases significantly after being tested on the same day. Therefore, we introduce the third key concept: Time interval since each individual’s last NAT. As shown in Fig.  b, when the sampling period is 7 days, if an individual is tested today, their probability of being selected for testing in the following days will gradually increase from 0 to 1 over the 7-day period, given the sample coverage and willingness to be tested.

## Model simulating
Traditional compartmental models, such as SIR , assume disease transmission occurs in a homogeneous population, where all susceptible individuals have the same probability of becoming infected. However, this assumption does not accurately reflect real-world social contact patterns, particularly when population structure is influenced by spatiotemporal factors such as quarantine measures and socioeconomic activities . The contact network model extends the SIR framework by incorporating interactions between individuals, allowing for a more realistic representation of complex epidemic dynamics , , .
This study utilized three types of contact network models to simulate the spread of the epidemic, including Scale-free network , Small-World network , and Random network . A defining characteristic of scale-free networks is that most nodes have only a few connections, while a small number of nodes, known as hubs, are connected to many. The degree distribution in scale-free networks follows a power-law. In this study, the Barabási-Albert (BA) model was employed to generate the scale-free networks . The small-world network represents an intermediate form, transitioning from a regular network to a random network, and exhibits the small-world phenomenon, where increasing the reconnection probability p transitions the network from regular to random. When p = 0.06, the community structure is at its strongest , . The Watts-Strogatz (WS) model was used in this study to generate small-world networks (p = 0.06). In a random network, edges are selected completely randomly with equal probability, and the degree distribution follows a Poisson distribution. The Erdos-Rényi (ER) model was used to generate random networks .
For the three social network models, we created network consisting of N nodes with mean degree of the network is MD where all nodes were initially set to the susceptible (S) state. To simulate external input cases in low-risk areas, a specified number of imported cases (NIC) in the infected (I) state were introduced from outside the network. Each infected node was randomly connected to MD nodes in the existing network. The virus transmission interval was calculated on a daily basis, with the network updated once per day. An infected (I) node had a probability of transmission (PT) to susceptible nodes connected to it. After a predefined recovery period (γ), the infected nodes transitioned to the removed (R) state. Nodes in the R state could no longer be infected or transmit the virus. Each day, nodes selected for nucleic acid testing were tested to determine if they were in the I state, with infected nodes potentially being misclassified as S due to a False-Negative Rate (FNR). The simulation continued until the nucleic acid test identified the first node in the I state. The number of days the virus had spread, as well as the number of nodes transitioning to the I and R states, were recorded. We compared the performance of the WHTS-1/f sampling strategy with the Regular-1/7 sampling strategy in terms of cost and alert time. The default parameters reflected the characteristics of the SARS-CoV-2 virus prevalent in mainland China. Furthermore, by adjusting network model parameters such as N, MD, PT, NIC, γ and FNR, we simulated a broader range of infectious diseases and evaluated the effectiveness of the WHTS sampling strategy under different scenarios.

## Results
For the BA model, under the default parameters (Fig.  a), the simulation results with 1,000 repeats show that: (1) for WHTS-1/7 and Regular-1/7, the alerting times are 2.8 ± 2.0 days and 3.8 ± 2.2 days (p = 5 × 10− 27), and the number of infections at alerting are 4.0 ± 8.6 cases and 6.2 ± 14.2 cases (p = 2 × 10− 5), respectively. WHTS-1/7 can alert one day earlier and has a smaller standard deviation in the number of infections. This indicates that WHTS-1/7 is more sensitive and robust compared to Regular-1/7. Additionally, the efficiency of Random sampling is lower than that of Regular and WHTS. For Random-1/7, the alerting time and the number of infections at alerting are 4.2 ± 2.7 days and 7.6 ± 18.1 cases, respectively. (2) for WHTS-1/14 and Regular-1/7, the alerting times are 4.2 ± 1.9 days and 3.8 ± 2.2 days (p = 0.0001), and the number of infections at alerting are 7.0 ± 13.8 cases and 6.2 ± 14.2 cases (p = 0.19), respectively. This shows that WHTS-1/14 can save half of the cost and is comparable to Regular-1/7 in terms of alerting time and the number of infections.

 

In the WS model, the WHTS strategy demonstrated superior performance compared to the Regular-1/7 approach (Fig.  b), largely due to the pronounced community structure of the WS model. For WHTS-1/7 and Regular-1/7, the average alerting times were 2.7 ± 2.3 days and 3.9 ± 2.8 days (p = 1 × 10-34), respectively. And the number of infections at the time of alert were 3.0 ± 2.5 and 4.0 ± 2.8 cases (p = 1 × 10-16), respectively. WHTS-1/7 was able to issue alerts approximately one day earlier and exhibited a smaller standard deviation in the number of infections. Notably, while WHTS-1/14 halved the number of tests, its alerting time (3.2 ± 2.4 days) and number of infections at the time of alert (3.3 ± 3.2 cases) only increased slightly. Remarkably, even when the WHTS strategy tested only one-thirtieth of the individuals, it achieved detection performance comparable to the Regular-1/7 strategy. This indicates that in the WS model, WHTS-1/30 can reduce testing costs by no less than 76% compared to the Regular-1/7 strategy.
In the ER model, the detection effect of the WHTS-1/7 strategy is not much different from that of the Regular-1/7 method (Fig.  c). For WHTS-1/7 and Regular-1/7, the average alerting times were 3.5 ± 1.8 days and 3.9 ± 2.2 days (p = 9 × 10-5), with the number of infections at the time of alert being 4.0 ± 2.7 cases and 3.7 ± 2.7 cases (p = 4 × 10-4), respectively. However, when WHTS-1/14 tested only one-fourteenth of the samples, both the alerting time and the number of infections during the alert period increased significantly, rising to 5.6 ± 2.6 days (p = 2 × 10-53) and 6.4 ± 4.6 cases (p = 5 × 10-34), respectively. This result is not surprising, as random networks lack the hub characteristics of social contacts, including the heterogeneity or community structure of the network.
We first increased the susceptible population size (N) from 50,000 to 1,000,000, and observed that the average period to detect hidden communicators (alerting time) using WHTS-1/14 did not increase with population size, but stabilized between 4 and 5 days (Fig.  a). Factors such as an increase in the network degree, probability of transmission, and the number of imported cases all contribute to the rapid spread of infectious agent among susceptible populations. Consequently, both the WHTS-1/14 and Regular-1/7 can detect hidden communicators earlier (Fig.  b-d). As the number of infections increased, for instance, with an increase in the number of initial imported cases to 6 (Fig.  d), the Regular-1/7 could detect positive cases earlier than WHTS-1/14 by testing more individuals. However, WHTS-1/14 also identified positive cases early in the epidemic, with an average detection time of 3.2 days or less. As illustrated in Fig.  e, an increased false-negative rate of NAT delays the detection of hidden communicators. When the false-negative rate of NAT increased from 20 to 60%, the average alerting time for Regular-1/7 increased from 3.39 ± 2.06 to 4.94 ± 2.96 days. Similarly, the average alerting time for WHTS-1/14 increased from 3.91 ± 1.61 to 5.41 ± 2.75 days. Lastly, we extended the recovery time (γ) to 15 days (Fig.  f), but observed no significant changes in the alerting times for either the WHTS-1/14 or Regular-1/7 testing methods. This is because the average alerting time for both methods remained less than 7 days. In other words, in the 1000 transmission event simulations, most of the simulation processes concluded before the infected individuals returned to the susceptible (S) state. Collectively, with the increase of N, MD, PT, NIC, γ and FR, the alerting time dynamics show a parallel trend between WHTS-1/14 and Regular-1/7. This indicates that WHTS-1/14 can achieve promising results under different scenarios and infectious diseases, even though it samples half as many people as the Regular-1/7.

 

## Discussion
Rapidly discovering hidden infections and reducing alarm time with large-scale NAT technology in low-risk areas are critical to stopping the spread of highly contagious epidemics . The WHTS sampling strategy offers a promising alternative to the Regular-1/7 sampling strategy, particularly in dynamically adapting to the evolving epidemiology of COVID-19 and other infectious diseases. By integrating the intensity of social activity, the degree of social overlap, and the time interval since the last NAT, the WHTS-1/14 sampling strategy can save half the cost, and the WHTS-1/7 sampling strategy can alert 1.0 days in advance compared to the Regular-1/7 sampling strategy.
The WHTS sampling strategy relies on real-world daily contact networks, and reconfiguring such networks is a significant challenge. There are several digital contact tracing approaches that can aid in reconfiguring the network. (1) Mobile network data. Numerous studies have utilized mobile phone data to track user trajectories and construct contact networks, which describe daily interactions between individuals. These include GPS location data, call records , Near Field Communication protocol , Bluetooth Low Energy protocol , and Radio-Frequency Identification techniques . However, these technologies also face challenges such as susceptibility to various tracing errors, high economic costs, restrictions on personal freedom, and potential negative impacts on mental health and well-being . (2) Venue quick response (QR) code scanning , . People are required to scan a venue QR code before entering key venues such as supermarkets, stations, schools, hotels, hospitals, and crowded places. (3) Mobile health apps, such as daily reporting app for the Covid-19 contact tracing. These apps should operate on a voluntary basis and ensure full protection of personal privacy.
The effectiveness of the WTHS sampling strategy lacks validation from real-world data. Due to the significant economic lost, as well as the extremely low mortality and severity rate of the current COVID-19 variant, Chinese mainland canceled the regular NAT policy on December 7, 2022, and no longer organizes large-scale NAT by administrative region. Nowadays assessing the real-world implementation of the WHTS strategy is difficult.
In conclusion, WHTS sampling strategies not only enhance early detection but also achieve significant cost reductions. By adjusting network model parameters, we also demonstrated the effectiveness of the WHTS-1/14 sampling strategy under different infectious diseases and scenarios. This more cost-effective and sensitive sampling strategy can facilitate the broader implementation of large-scale NAT in more countries and regions during outbreaks of highly contagious diseases with long incubation periods, providing a useful approach for future epidemic control. If the WHTS strategy can be further integrated with optimized pooled testing – , strategic site deployment , and contact tracing – , it is expected to more effectively curb the spread of the epidemic while reducing prevention and control costs.
