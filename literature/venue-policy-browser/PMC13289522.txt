## Abstract
Community action focused on sociocultural and environmental influences to prevent alcohol and other drug (AOD) use and related harms is a global priority. Despite this recognition, understanding of effective community‐level approaches is limited. This umbrella review aimed to develop a unifying term to define and describe community‐level AOD approaches and summarize lessons learned from implementation. We searched Embase/MEDLINE, PsycINFO, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, two online registries and reference lists of included studies. Eligible reviews focused on community‐level approaches addressing AOD use or harms. Two authors independently screened articles and completed full‐text review and data extraction. We identified 87 reviews, spanning three decades. We propose the term Comprehensive Community Initiatives (CCI) to define community‐level AOD approaches. CCIs involve multiple coordinated initiatives implemented across a community and are categorised into four types and eleven subtypes. School‐based activities, the most common initiative subtype, were reported by reviews to be most effective when interactive and peer‐focused, while fear‐based approaches reportedly lack impact. Meaningful community engagement was central across recommendations to improve effectiveness. We summarize lessons learned for each initiative subtype, including recommendations, barriers, facilitators, and moderators of impact to guide future implementation.

## Key Highlights

Comprehensive Community Initiatives comprise at least two activities from 11 initiative subtypes.
This paper summarises lessons learned from 87 reviews spanning 30 years.
Using our definition will promote a shared language and improve the clarity of future research.

 Four systematic reviews (Carson et al.,  ; Gates et al.,  ; Kingsland et al.,  ; Langford et al.,  ) and 10 narrative reviews (Bruce,  ; Burton et al.,  ; Das et al.,  ; O'Mara‐Eves et al.,  ; Pederson et al.,  ; Sellers et al.,  ; Thomas et al.,  ,  ; Wakefield,  ;  ) were of high quality. Full results of the quality analysis are available in Supplemental Material.
The included reviews, along with their associated community focus, AOD focus, the different initiatives that were addressed, and review quality is provided in the Supplemental Material. Most reviews included multiple types of substances, though alcohol was the most frequently assessed, and included location‐based communities, or had a limited focus to young people. The systematic reviews assessed 264 evaluation studies of community‐level approaches with low overlap (corrected covered area of 1.33). Each review in support of any claim described in the results was considered to contribute to the certainty of evidence, as each claim was likely supported by separate primary studies.

## Aim 1: Defining Community‐Level Approaches—Comprehensive Community Initiatives (CCI)
A total of six reviews defined “community,” and two themes were developed during thematic analysis as definitional elements of community (see Supporting Information for details). Theme one related to groups of people within a geographic area; and theme two related to groups of people with shared characteristics other than location, often referring to culture or identity.
In total, 70 definitions of “community approaches” were extracted from the included reviews (see Supporting Information Material for details), and three definitional themes were developed. Theme one: community approaches involve multiple types of initiatives typically designed to address risk and protective factors of either the individual or the community. Theme two: community is engaged during the design and/or implementation of the initiatives. However, the level of community engagement varied on the spectrum outlined by Thompson and colleagues ( ). This spectrum spans from outreach and education designed and delivered by non‐community members such as researchers, implemented with community advice or approval, to true partnership following methods of CBPR and involving a strong bidirectional relationship. Theme three: the approach is designed to be accessible by the whole community without restriction by anyone.
Following thematic analysis, the term Comprehensive Community Initiatives (CCI) was developed as an encapsulating term to describe community‐level AOD approaches to prevent or reduce AOD use or harms. We define CCI as an approach that is (1) comprehensive and comprises at least two initiative subtypes (referring to the 11 subtypes described below) and (2) that are implemented to reach a community to prevent or reduce AOD use or harms. This definition excludes individual‐level approaches and those involving a single initiative subtype. We define community as referring to more than one resident of a particular locality (e.g., a town or city) or to groups of people identifying to a set of shared characteristics (e.g., First Nations people or students).

## Aim 1: CCI Framework
Through thematic analysis, we developed four types and 11 subtypes of initiatives for inclusion in the framework (Figure  ). Initiative types related to: the setting in which they were delivered, including school‐based, vendor‐based, workplace‐based, or health service‐based initiative subtypes; the provider responsible for implementation, including coalition‐led or police‐led initiative subtypes; the people targeted by the intervention, including the parenting‐related initiative subtype; or the type of activity, including mobilisation, policy‐related, media campaigns, and infrastructure‐related initiative subtypes. Details of the activities that comprise these 11 initiative subtypes are provided in aim 2.
 

## Aim 2: Description of initiatives and lessons learned

## Setting‐based initiative type

## Initiative subtype—School‐Based activities
The school‐based initiative subtype was reported to be the most common set of activities within the context of CCI (Carson et al.,  ; Cuijpers,  ; Evans et al.,  ; O'Mara‐Eves et al.,  ; Stockings, Bartlem, et al.,  ). Schools may be involved in primary prevention to implement new policies, activities, or a change in curriculum along with teacher training relating to AOD prevention. Schools also allow for codesign and evaluation of CCI initiatives with young people. Langford et al. ( ) cited that one common method for codesign was to establish a school‐based working group or committee which may also include parents or community.
School‐based initiatives as part of CCI are separate from the typical approaches used within schools to address AOD use which largely focus on the individual student (Gorman & Speer,  ), do not involve evidence‐based programs (Pentz,  ), and are not in focus here.
School‐based activities were reported by reviews to be most effective when interactive and peer‐focused, while fear‐based approaches reportedly lack impact (Table  summarizes lessons learned).
 

## Initiative subtype—Vendor‐based activities
The vendor‐based initiative subtype includes activities implemented in locations where alcohol or tobacco, or cannabis in locations where sales are legal, are sold (Stockings, Shakeshaft, et al.,  ). Prominent examples include: changes in serving policy, like reducing local promotions that reduce the price of alcohol or tobacco, or changing hours of operation; risk assessments and improvements to the venue environment to improve patron safety, like using alternatives to glass and/or having clear exits; introducing patron banning; and, training in responsible service (Brennan et al.,  ; Jones et al.,  ; Kingsland et al.,  ; Stockings, Shakeshaft, et al.,  ). Participation by vendors in these initiatives may be voluntary, though community‐level policies and state or national regulations may mandate participation by enforcing certain standards or placing limits on sales. Mandated participation is considered in the policy‐related initiative subtype and not addressed here.
Vendor‐based activities were reported by reviews to work best when paired with compliance monitoring and framed as beneficial for business (Table  summarizes lessons learned).
 

## Initiative subtype—Workplace‐based activities
The workplace‐based initiative subtype includes activities relating to the introduction of workplace policies and procedures; and/or the provision of education, training, or services within the workplace that aim to prevent or reduce the use of AOD in and around the workplace (Sorensen et al.,  ). Initiatives that often occur outside of CCI under workplace management practices due to the association between employee substance use, injuries, and productivity loss (de Oliveira et al.,  ), are not considered here.
Only two reviews reported any recommendations around the implementation of workplace‐based activities. One high‐quality narrative review noted that any such activity must ensure that it is culturally relevant to all populations within the workplace (Power et al.,  ). One critically low‐quality systematic review noted that workplace activities have limited reach to those who are unemployed, so they must be combined with other initiatives (Hill et al.,  ).

## Initiative subtype—Health service‐based activities
The health service‐based initiative subtype includes activities involving health services which provide treatment or harm reduction strategies that are: (1) face‐to‐face with individuals or groups, delivered in fixed locations (Burton et al.,  ), or involve mobile outreach (Jiwa et al.,  ); (2) by remote delivery through the internet by application on any device, telephone, self‐help materials, or medication (Secker‐Walker et al.,  ); and/or (3) prescription of pharmaceuticals used in substance use cessation such as nicotine replacement therapies (Hill et al.,  ; Lantz,  ). One additional review considered activities that upskilled health service staff (Haegerich et al.,  ). In the context of CCI, health‐service initiatives typically involve training workers across multiple locations in the delivery of a certain service, like Screening Brief Intervention and Referral to Treatment (SBIRT; Stockings, Shakeshaft, et al.,  ); or the use of a service that reaches whole communities, like cessation support “Quitlines” (Secker‐Walker et al.,  ).
Health service‐based activities SBIRT and telehealth approaches, were reported by reviews to improve accessibility and cost‐effectiveness, especially when culturally tailored (Table  summarizes lessons learned).
 

## Provider‐led initiative type

## Initiative subtype—Community coalitions
The community coalition initiative subtype refers to activities related to the coordinated collaboration between organizations and individuals with skills, expertise, and a shared commitment to reducing or preventing AOD use (Giesbrecht et al.,  ). Coalitions act to change the physical, social, economic, and legal environment that shapes AOD use within communities (DeJong & Hingson,  ). The rationale for membership varies but assumes a mutual benefit for the community and coalition members through the pooling of resources, growth in networking, and promotion of cultural competence (Hutchison & Russell,  ; Krakouer et al.,  ; Kreuter et al.,  ). Specifically identified members included, youth and family representatives that primarily promote education through schools (Lantz,  ; Pentz,  ; Porthé et al.,  ); health services to provide evidence‐based treatment (Secker‐Walker et al.,  ; Stockings, Shakeshaft, et al.,  ); and alcohol retailers, police, and local councils to promote and enforce local policies (Jones et al.,  ; Porthé et al.,  ; Stockings, Shakeshaft, et al.,  ). In addition, many coalitions also involved community members with lived experience or respected leaders and elders (Hutchison & Russell,  ; Kreuter et al.,  ; Zakocs & Edwards,  ).
Coalitions faced difficulties at all stages, from early planning and formation, to community assessment, action planning, generating resources, taking action, and sustaining the effort (Roussos & Fawcett,  ). Many coalitions fail to get established (Kreuter et al.,  ; Wandersman & Florin,  ). A major difficulty was meeting the resource requirements for sustainability as coalitions typically take several months to form, and even longer to effect change (Secker‐Walker et al.,  ).
Coalitions were reported by reviews to succeed with diverse membership and strong leadership but often fail due to resource and consensus challenges (Table  summarizes lessons learned).
 

## Initiative subtype—Police‐led activities
The police‐led initiative subtype involves activities related to law enforcement. These include: penalties for AOD‐related crime; information dissemination and problem solving; deterring crime through overt presence like targeted inspections for compliance by AOD vendors, or increased presence in areas or times associated with AOD risk; establishing roadside checkpoints to conduct AOD testing (Aguirre‐Molina & Gorman,  ; Blewden & Spinola,  ); and providing data for evaluation of call‐outs, crime rates, or reports of AOD‐related accidents and injury (Droste et al.,  ). Two reviews also considered that police could be involved in CCI through initiatives that targeted incarcerated individuals; however, there were no published evaluations of CCI with this initiative that assessed impact on AOD (Evans et al.,  ; Stockings, Shakeshaft, et al.,  ).
Police‐led community engagement and roadside checkpoints were reported by reviews to outperform punitive measures like increased penalties for drug‐related crime (Table  summarizes lessons learned).
 

## Receiver‐based initiative type

## Initiative subtype—Parenting‐related activities
The parenting‐related initiative subtype refers to activities that focus on building parenting practices and may involve interactions with parents to deliver training, counseling, and advice on how to support their children. It may also be delivered without interaction through information or self‐help “homework” (Langford et al.,  ). In the context of CCI, it is more common for these activities to involve parental interaction. Outside of CCI, parenting‐related activities are more likely to not involve interaction (Flay,  ). This initiative subtype typically targets parents with children of school age, especially high school (10–16 years) (Ballester et al.,  ; Pentz,  ; Petrie et al.,  ; Spoth et al.,  ).
Parenting‐related activities are based on research conducted in the 1990s and early 2000s. This shows that parents have a major influence on their children who respond to reinforcement of desired behaviors and clearly defined behavioral expectations (Flay,  ; Nation et al.,  ; Stockings, Shakeshaft, et al.,  ). However, this research is rapidly evolving and the reliance on outdated research may limit the generalization to present day (Stockings, Shakeshaft, et al.,  ).
Interactive parenting programs with multiple sessions and school partnerships were reported by reviews to be more effective than passive information delivery (Table  summarizes lessons learned).
 

## Activity‐based initiative type

## Initiative subtype—Community mobilisation

Community mobilisation initiative subtype involves activities related to community outreach and engagement in various forms (Giesbrecht et al.,  ). This initiative subtype is most active in the early stages of CCI implementation, bolstering the formation of coalitions and the development of community support for the wider CCI objectives through petitions (Stockings, Bartlem, et al.,  ). Mobilization activities primarily include information dissemination through holding one‐off community events, introducing ongoing “alternative” substance‐free activities for youths, or through more active outreach such as door knocking (Hawkins et al.,  ; Stockings, Bartlem, et al.,  ).
Mobilization activities were reported by reviews to enhance sustainability when community‐designed, though they require substantial resources (Table  summarizes lessons learned).
 

## Initiative subtype—Policy‐related activities
The policy‐related initiative subtype involves activities related to two main types of community‐level policy that may be advocated: regulatory policy, and programmatic policy (Pentz,  ). These types of policy‐related activities are mostly relevant to alcohol or tobacco but may include other substances where sale and consumption is legal. Regulatory policies are formal laws, regulations, or ordinances that address the supply and demand for AOD. The include policies that: impact the supply of AOD focus on banning the production of illicit substances, limiting sales, or encouraging the sale of lower strength products; limit the sale of legal substances include, restricting the density of vendors by granting licenses to sell in venues as well as the placement of vending machines, restricting operating hours or days, raising the minimum legal purchasing age, or holding vendors legally responsible for harm caused to customers (Burton et al.,  ; McGrath et al.,  ; Pentz,  ); or impact the demand for AOD focus on restricting the exposure and content of advertisements, banning certain sponsorship, or increasing sales price (DeJong & Hingson,  ).

Programmatic policies are those aimed to support the institutionalization of the CCI activities by raising or mandating funds for CCI and requiring the standardized implementation of activities (Pentz,  ). These activities primarily involve advocating for a policy that mandate all schools within a community to budget for the implementation of an AOD prevention program into the curriculum.
Deterrence policies are also relevant and act to dissuade individuals from AOD use. Deterrence policies primarily relate to changes in the punishments surrounding substance use, and instigating roadside checkpoints and thresholds for what is considered to be “driving under the influence” (DeJong & Hingson,  ). Other less common deterrence policies include banning substance use in certain public locations, and instituting public health warning labelling on legally sold AOD products (Burton et al.,  ).
Unlike other activities, instigating policy changes is likely out of scope for most CCI and falls under state or federal governance (McGrath et al.,  ; Stockings, Shakeshaft, et al.,  ). Although enacting policy change may be out of scope, raising awareness and educating the public on local concerns and risk factors will promote the likelihood that a policy is enacted by demonstrating public sanctioning (Stockings, Shakeshaft, et al.,  ).
Policy initiatives were reported by reviews to be most effective when coherent, community‐endorsed, and combined to shift social norms (Table  summarizes lessons learned).
 

## Initiative subtype—Media campaigns
The media campaign initiative subtype included any promotional activities typically focusing on legal substances (alcohol or tobacco) or illicit substances (most commonly cannabis) (Aguirre‐Molina & Gorman,  ). The promotional activities operate through a specific media, like publications, promotions, advertisements, flyers, posters, newspapers, billboards, television, radio, and social media (Carson et al.,  ), and are distributed to specific individuals or entire communities (Aguirre‐Molina & Gorman,  ; Jones,  ; O'Mara‐Eves et al.,  ; Pentz,  ).
The objective of media campaigns is to raise awareness and community support for the CCI objectives such as by modelling substance refusal skills or promoting positive alternatives to drug use (DeJong & Hingson,  ; Flay,  ; Pentz,  ).
Media campaigns with high‐quality, emotionally engaging content and interactive elements were reported by reviews to outperform those with fear‐based messaging (Table  summarizes lessons learned).
 

## Initiative subtype—Infrastructure‐related activities
The infrastructure initiative subtype refers to activities involving risk assessments and environmental changes that aim to increase the safety of being in a certain location, including the installation of surveillance cameras, increasing the available light, or neighborhood clean‐ups and attempts to improve housing; or the safety of traveling from a certain location by improving public transport options, or providing volunteer drivers (Burton et al.,  ; Droste et al.,  ; Graham,  ; Hingson & Howland,  ; Jones,  ; Langford et al.,  ; Stockings, Shakeshaft, et al.,  ). CCI with infrastructure‐related activities were relatively rare.
Reviews reported that environmental changes improved safety but rarely reduce substance use, while ride services showed more promise than designated driver programs (Table  summarizes lessons learned).
 

## DISCUSSION
We conducted an umbrella review to synthesize the vast literature on community‐level AOD approaches, aiming to develop a unifying term, provide a framework of relevant initiatives, and update lessons learned. There was significant contextual heterogeneity across reviews and definitions which limited comparability and synthesis. This heterogeneity reflected the breadth of activities, and the varying extent to which the reviews discussed each activity. Despite this, our analysis developed the term CCI, where two or more of initiative subtypes are implemented to prevent or reduce AOD use or harms to a community, referred to as residents of a particular locality (e.g., a town or city) or people with shared characteristics (e.g., First Nations people or students). We recommend adopting this term and its supporting framework to promote clarity and consistency in the field.
Many lessons learned were presented, most notably the importance of community engagement to promote effectiveness. Firstly, practitioners should engage with community to understand the normative beliefs about AOD use and to foster a supportive social context (Aguirre‐Molina & Gorman,  ; Flay,  ; Hawkins et al.,  ; Hutchison & Russell,  ; Jiwa et al.,  ; Krakouer et al.,  ; Merzel & D'Afflitti,  ; Nagorcka‐Smith et al.,  ; Pentz,  ; Spoth et al.,  ; Stevenson & Mitchell,  ). This aligns with social influence theory around substance use, which identifies targeting social norms and peer influence as mechanisms for program effectiveness (Sánchez‐Franco et al.,  ). In addition, recent work on community coalition development highlights community norms and politics as important factors influencing program effectiveness (Wang et al.,  ).
Second, practitioners should engage with community to ensure the cultural relevance of activities, particularly when these activities involve workplaces (Power et al.,  ) or health services (Hill et al.,  ; Jiwa et al.,  ; Krakouer et al.,  ; Minichiello et al.,  ). This may be best achieved by including culturally representative community members during planning and implementation (Hawkins et al.,  ; Hutchison & Russell,  ; Krakouer et al.,  ; Merzel & D'Afflitti,  ; Nagorcka‐Smith et al.,  ). This advice is consistent with the results of a recent meta‐analysis of prevention programs targeting US Black, Hispanic, and Native American adolescents which found a significant impact in preventing or reducing substance use (g = −0.20) among programs that included cultural representation among those involved with planning and implementation (Bo et al.,  ). To achieve this representation, Bo et al. ( ) recommend that CCIs employ the culturally sensitive framework proposed by Bernal and Sáez‐Santiago ( ).
Third, community should be engaged during designing and implementing activities to foster connection, a sense of ownership, and sustainability within the community (Aguirre‐Molina & Gorman,  ; Minichiello et al.,  ; O'Mara‐Eves et al.,  ; Pentz,  ; Stockings, Shakeshaft, et al.,  ). This aligns with research highlighting the positive relationship between sense of community and well‐being (Stewart & Townley,  ). In addition, research on community health promotion strategies suggests that ownership helps develop and optimize existing community capacities and enables participation (Trisnowati et al.,  ).
Fourth, community should be engaged and empowered with the goal to increase capacity to respond to substance‐related harm (Jones et al.,  ; Krakouer et al.,  ; Minichiello et al.,  ; Nagorcka‐Smith et al.,  ; Stevenson & Mitchell,  ). Recent research demonstrates that indicators of community capacity, specifically collaboration, commitment, and knowledge or skills, are associated with effective prevention and health promotion interventions (Birgel et al.,  ). Moreover, community readiness, including willingness and preparedness to act, is critical for AOD prevention efforts (Wade et al.,  ). Collectively, these findings align with the principles of community‐engaged research (April et al.,  ), reinforcing engagement as foundational for effective and sustainable CCIs.
Building on the focus of community engagement, the next step is for communities and funders to understand which combination of initiatives are most likely to have a positive impact (Jones,  ). The reviews supported several initiative pairings, most commonly the combination of school‐based and parenting‐related activities (Allen et al.,  ; Ballester et al.,  ; Cairns et al.,  ; Carson et al.,  ; Foxcroft & Tsertsvadze,  ; Haegerich et al.,  ; Hutchison & Russell,  ; Jackson et al.,  ; Langford et al.,  ; Marshall et al.,  ; Müller‐Riemenschneider et al.,  ; Petrie et al.,  ; Spoth et al.,  ). Langford et al. ( ) note that this combination aligns with the World Health Organization's Health Promoting Schools Framework, offering an “ecoholistic” model, influencing individuals through curriculum, leveraging the school environment for credibility, and reinforcing health messaging within the broader community. In addition, a recent umbrella review that focused on AOD CCI impact highlighted how this combination was frequently found to have a positive significant impact, especially when complemented by a media campaign (Gates et al.,  ). However, no gold standard CCI has emerged, underscoring the need for further research (Allen et al.,  ; Cibich et al.,  ; Coggans & Watson,  ; Gates et al.,  ; Geia et al.,  ; Hill et al.,  ; Norman & Turner,  ; Pentz,  ; Sanchez‐Puertas et al.,  ; Spoth et al.,  ; Stockings, Shakeshaft, et al.,  ; Walsh,  ; Webb et al.,  ).
As with any umbrella review, results were limited by the content and quality of included reviews (Fusar‐Poli & Radua,  ). Most reviews were rated as critically low or low quality, often lacking clear search strategies, inclusion criteria, or detailed descriptions of included studies. These limitations influenced the balance of evidence across initiative subtypes and favored school and parenting initiatives, which have been extensively researched. Conversely, workplace‐based and infrastructure‐related activities require further investigation. While review quality may not uniformly affect all recommendations, a quality assessment was included to denote areas in need of rigorous research. Finally, our discussion of moderators of impact reflects associations identified as important in the reviews but may not necessarily represent statistically significant relationships or be supported by high‐quality evaluations.

## Implications for policy, practice, and research
From a policy perspective, the shared definition we propose here enhances clarity and could inform funding decisions and guiding documents such as the World Health Organization's 2022–2030 Alcohol Action Plan (2021). This plan currently promotes “community action” without defining the term or recommending activities. Incorporating our proposed definition and framework would provide policymakers with 11 initiative subtypes to consider. Similarly, there is limited guidance on how to go about involving community (Collins et al.,  ), compounded by the challenge of accurately measuring the extent of involvement (Boursaw et al.,  ). However, recent work has explored factors associated with effective engagement, highlighting the importance of transparent communication and awareness of disparities among members in available resources and motivations for participation (Halsall et al.,  ).
For practitioners, this shared language supports standardization and enables meaningful comparisons, improving activity selection, delivery, and evaluation. Research exploring CCI activity selection demonstrates that communities tend to select school‐based and vendor‐based activities and rarely consider activities from other initiative types (Shakeshaft et al.,  ). Selections are often made without regard to effectiveness, reflecting limited capacity for evidence synthesis and a prioritization of community autonomy over evidence‐based practice (Giesbrecht et al.,  ; Shults et al.,  ; Stockings, Bartlem, et al.,  ). Importantly, in addition to the evidence‐supported initiative pairings already discussed, the reviews also noted activities to avoid which are not supported by evidence, such as designated driver programs or increasing penalties around drug use. In addition, reflecting the central importance of community to CCI, practitioners should consider an ecological perspective. This regards social determinants of health, such as poverty, social exclusion, health disparity, and inequalities in resources, as important targets for substance use prevention (Constanzo,  ; Fishbein & Sloboda,  ; Trickett,  ). In this context, three of the identified 11 initiative subtypes are relevant: policy, especially social justice‐oriented; structural/environmental transformation to address inequalities in access; and coalition formation to foster inclusive involvement. Although these components are supported by the defining principals of CBPR (Collins et al.,  ), and the tenants of community psychology, future research is needed to determine the efficacy of such CCIs.
Finally, for researchers, there is substantial diversity in methods for community engagement, and best practices remain unclear (Haapanen & Christens,  ; Kaufman et al.,  ). There is a need to further explore CBPR methods for designing and implementing CCIs, evaluate initiative combinations under varied community contexts, and develop robust evaluation frameworks that can attribute effects within multi‐component interventions. Additionally, most primary studies were published before or during the early 2000s, and reviews often focused on quantifying CCI impact rather than describing processes around implementation. Consequently, priorities for future research include clarifying the initiatives included, and factors associated with impact, such as community setting, context, and the extent of community involvement. These insights for policy makers, practitioners, and researchers all underscore the need for a consistent definition and evidence‐based strategies moving forward.

## Conclusion
This umbrella review synthesized the heterogenous literature focused on community AOD prevention. To date, it offers the most extensive description of community‐level approaches and their constituent initiatives and activities. Future work should build consistency by (1) adopting our definition and proposed framework, (2) considering which of the 11 initiative subtypes are most relevant, and (3) consulting our summary of lessons learned during planning. Rigorous evaluation of CCIs is essential to strengthen the evidence base and identify initiative combinations most likely to reduce AOD use and related harms. Importantly, this evaluation should continue to measure moderators of impact using consistent terminology and outcomes.
