An emerging venue for PP has been second/foreign language (L2) education given its emotional essence. While many studies are exploring various aspects of PP in English as a foreign language (EFL) contexts, they are mostly correlational. Nevertheless, teachers’ perceptions and experiences of PP use in EFL classes have remained under-explored. To fill this gap, this study aimed to examine Chinese EFL teachers’ perceptions about the use, benefits, setbacks, and requirements of applying PP in L2 education. To this end, a sample of 21 Chinese EFL teachers with various educational and pedagogical profiles attended a semi-structured interview. The findings indicated that Chinese EFL teachers perceived various benefits for PP use including its capacity to generate positive emotions, stop and reduce negative emotions, improve professional expertise and feelings, establish positive, friendly, and interactive learning environments, and strengthen one’s character. Concerning challenges, it was found that PP application had been tough in China because of its logistic demands, teacher-related and learner-related factors (e.g., personality, needs, and openness to diversity/change), polices and rules, and the need for training. Moreover, the findings showed that PP use requires positive classroom climates, attitudes, and relationships, along with special training, emotional literacy, individualized instruction, school support and resources, and a humanistic teaching style. The study discusses the findings and implications for EFL teachers and educators, who can realize the benefits, challenges, and requirements of PP use in L2 classes.

## Introduction
Throughout the history of second language acquisition (SLA), several theories in psychology have come about to shape the teaching and learning of second/foreign language (L2) education [ , ]. For the most part, such theories focused on the nature of L2 education, which was the transfer of linguistic information via input, output, and interaction [ ]. Moreover, the field has been largely obsessed with the cognitive side of L2 learning leaving no room for emotions and affect in the process of L2 education [ – ]. However, the emotional essence of L2 education pushed the boundaries ahead by placing emotions and inner feelings at the heart of language learning [ , , ]. This shift toward emotions was further strengthened by ‘the affective turn to applied linguistics’ and ‘emotionology’ [ , ]. As a consequence, rapid developments in the psychology of L2 teaching and learning emerged in light of affective dimensions, which were long poorly studied and misunderstood as unscientific [ , ]. Also, research on the psycho-affective aspects of L2 education was mainly preoccupied with negative emotions and interpreted one’s functioning only in terms of a deficiency model by ignoring the positive side of life [ ]. As put by Seligman, educational psychology at that time came close to be “victimology” by focusing on victims and injuries rather than strengths. As a reaction to the dark side of emotions, positive psychology (PP) emerged at the turn of the millennium to initiate a change in educational psychology and social sciences by calling for a re-orientation of attention from the worst things to the best qualities in life [ ]. Technically, PP is the scientific study of what goes right in life from birth to death [ ]. This movement highlights positive emotions, traits, and workplaces to secure one’s functioning and flourishing [ ].
Given its approved potential, depth, and breadth in general education and other fields, PP has received a rising interest in the context of L2 education as well [ , – ]. This integration into L2 education is due to the fact that SLA is by nature an emotionally driven process (Richards 2020). Following the launch and flowering of PP in L2 education, the adynaton “when pigs fly” has changed into ‘the time of flying elephants’, which refers to conceding the presence of emotions in L2 education. In the past decade, the ever-present focus on negative emotions like stress, anxiety, burnout, and demotivation was replaced by positive constructs such as love, joy, gratitude, resilience, buoyancy, engagement, optimism, hope etc. [ , ]. The current body of knowledge on the application of PP to English as a foreign language (EFL) contexts is mostly confined either to correlational studies among PP constructs in face-to-face or online modes of delivery [ , – , ] or theoretical-conceptual studies that aimed to justify the injection of PP into L2 classes, [ , ], While these studies facilitated the ground for PP growth and popularity in applied linguistics, they turned a blind eye to what actually happens and needs to happen for its effective integration into L2 education. There is insufficient research (if any) on the real-life experiences and perceptions of EFL teachers regarding PP use. The existing studies on EFL teachers are again limited to correlations among teacher-related PP factors. This study then goes beyond such prior correlational and conceptual research in scope and depth by empirically focusing on how PP is actually implemented by EFL teachers and its benefits, challenges, and requirements as significant gaps to be uncovered. To cast light on this area, this qualitative study aimed to examine Chinese EFL teachers’ perceptions and experiences of PP use with a focus on its benefits, difficulties, and preconditions of effective injection. The study can move the literature from theory and association among PP factors to actual practice of this booming movement.

## Literature review

## The positive psychology movement: descriptions, theories, and models
PP is a school of educational psychology intended to break the hegemony of linguistic and cognitive perspectives of learning around the turn of the twenty-first century. The first ideas of positive-oriented psychology were put decades ago, but the explicit emergence of PP appeared in the words of Martin Seligman in 1998. Seligman, as the founder of this movement, called for strengths and potentials instead of overgeneralizing negativity and problems [ ]. As a science, PP rests on three pillars of ‘positive experiences/emotions’, ‘positive personality traits’, and ‘positive institutions/environments’ that promote positive development [ ]. PP aims to help individuals, groups, and organizations to develop their sense of functioning and well-being based on scientific research [ ]. It is education for both learning and happiness [ ]. PP does admit the existence and role of negative factors and abnormalities in life and education, but it strongly adheres to the positive side [ ]. It disagrees with the dichotomization of psychology into negative and positive psychologies. Yet, prefers a positive model in contrast to the deficits model. PP expands individuals’ and institutions’ resources and contributes to their flourishing [ ]. It is essential to note that PP is not a radical paradigm shift, but it is just a transition and development from repairing what is worst to creating what is best [ ]. PP is concerned not only with happiness, but also with resilience, development, flourishing, and fulfillment in any area of life comprising education [ ].
PP is theoretically underpinned by various models and theories. The first theory is Fredrickson’s [ ] Broaden-and-Build Theory, which highlights the role of positive emotions in one’s flourishing and performance. It posits that positive emotions can broaden thought-action repertoires, prevent negative emotions, increase psychological resilience, establish personal resources, and generate psychological and physical well-being in individuals. In contrast, negative emotions can damage all those areas. This theory rests on positive emotions because these forces can contribute to supreme short-term and long-term functioning [ ]. The second theory behind PP is the Rhetorical/Relational Goal Theory (RRGT), which considers various rhetorical and relational goals for both teachers and learners in the classroom that they try to achieve [ ]. RRGT suggests that rhetorical and relational goals should be concurrently met to boost learning. Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) is the third underlying theory of PP. It emphasizes on the social nature of learning that emerges from interactions among individuals, environment, and behavior [ ]. SCT also underscores the interaction of cognitive, social, environmental, and personal factors in shaping one’s behavior. It enlightens PP by adding a sociocultural dimension to it. Bowlby’s [ ] Attachment Theory (AT) is the fourth related theory here. AT accentuates affective bonds among people, which can shape their relationships, behaviors, feelings, experiences, and work engagement [ ]. This theory calls for teacher-students’ sense of immediacy and rapport in the classroom that foster various areas of L2 education. Other than these theories, different frameworks have also been proposed in the literature the most popular of which is the PERMA model, which draws on five pillars of well-being, namely Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, and Accomplishment [ ]. Based on the PERMA model, other models have been introduced, as well. One such model is the EPOCH framework, which is made up of five elements: Engagement (E), Perseverance (P), Optimism (O), Connectedness (C), and Happiness (H) [ ]. Another one is the SEARCH model which includes Strengths (S), Emotional management (E), Attention and Awareness (A), Relationships (R), Coping (C), Habits and Goals (H) [ ]. The last related framework is the PROSPER framework, which emphasizes seven key elements of well-being, namely Positivity, Relationships, Outcomes, Strengths, Purpose, Engagement, and Resilience [ ]. All these frameworks unanimously protect teachers and students against negative emotions and enhance their positive feelings and academic performance [ , ]. However, their application to L2 education contexts is yet to fully fledge.

## Positive psychology and L2 education: the impacts on teachers and learners
Given its benefits and potentialities in other disciplines, PP found its way into L2 education with the rising concern for an effective movement [ , ]. PP began a renaissance in L2 education by disregarding pure linguistic and cognitive views in support of an emotion-based education [ ]. The traditional polarity of emotions into positive and negative emotions gave its place to PP which endorsed negativity, but invested mostly in positivity [ , ]. The study of positive emotions added a new spirit to L2 education in the sense that feelings of enjoyment, meaningfulness, support, and immediacy overwhelmed teachers and students [ ]. Urged by the promises of PP, many L2 researchers empirically studied different PP-related constructs such as enjoyment, hope, happiness, grit, engagement, trust, love, optimism, pride, and so forth [ – , , , , – ]. Seminal studies have also been carried out on the interactions among positive emotions. For example, Botes et al. [ ], argued that foreign language enjoyment (FLE) significantly correlates with willingness to communicate (WTC). Derakhshan et al. [ – ] ran a multinational study on the interaction among loving pedagogy, creativity, and work engagement of EFL teachers. They reported significant correlations among these three constructs. Such studies had been pure qualitative and one-shot rather than deeply ingrained in life experiences.
Moreover, Jin et al. [ ] suggested that PP approach can reduce Chinese EFL learners’ anxiety levels. Mercer et al. [ ] used PP to promote teachers’ professional well-being. Wang et al. [ ] proposed a practical model for applying PP to L2 education highlighting teacher-related, learner-related, context-related, and cultural factors. The literature on PP is mostly conceptual rather than being driven from practitioners’ actual implementations and experience of PP practices in L2 classes. Concerning online L2 education, Derakhshan et al. [ ] examined 240 Iranian EFL learners’ perceptions of boredom and reported 10 practices inducing boredom in online L2 classes. Theoretical accounts and review studies on PP are also raising to encourage future studies (e.g., [ , , , , ]). In a phenomenographic study in Iran, Ghiasvand and Sharifpour [ ] examined the construct of ‘love’ as a cardinal positive emotion and claimed that without love L2 education is not education at all. Other contributions of PP constructs to L2 learners pertain to their ability to increase students’ motivation, interest, creativity, and academic performance (e.g., [ – ]). Positive emotions and PP have also been declared to shape EFL teachers’ professional development and success [ – ]. To move this area to the world of L2 assessment, Derakshan et al. [ – ] explored EFL teachers’ and learners’ perceived emotions during L2 assessment practices. They reported different positive and negative emotions and their associated regulation strategies.
Recently, research agendas on PP have called for innovative approaches to study the dynamism of PP constructs in light of chaos complexity theory [ ], systems-informed positive psychology [ ], retrodictive qualitative modeling (RQM) [ ], latent profile analysis [ ], the ecological perspective [ ], and the idiodynamic method [ ]. However, these advances are still unclear and complicated to apply by many L2 researchers, who work on the interaction between PP and L2 education. The realization of PP in online communities and in relation to technologies is also reported by Derakhshan’s [ ] influential theoretical synthesis of previous research. Despite a wealth of research on various PP constructs in correlational, theoretical, qualitative, and structural equation modeling (SEM) studies, few studies (if any) have practically explored EFL teachers’ experiences and perceptions of PP use in L2 classes. There are some intervention studies on the use of PERMA and EMPHATIC models in L2 education (e.g., [ , , ]. However, EFL teachers’ perceptions about the actual use of PP as a whole have remained under-explored. To fill the gaps, this qualitative study tried to uncover the benefits, challenges, and requirements of PP use in the context of China. It attempted to answer the following research questions:What are the benefits of positive psychology use from the perspective of Chinese EFL teachers?
What are Chinese EFL teachers’ perceived challenges of positive psychology use in L2 education?
What requirements need to be met for positive psychology use from the perspective of Chinese EFL teachers?

## Method

## Participants
As for the purpose of this study, 21 Chinese EFL teachers were selected through a convenience sampling procedure. The sample included two genders; 10 males (48%) and 11 females (52%). They were all EFL teachers from China. The age of the participants ranged from 23 to 48. They reported to be teaching at various institutes across different proficiency levels in China. Concerning their major, the teachers had studied applied linguistics (8), English literature (6), Translation (4), and Business English (3). Their educational degree ranged from MA (n = 11) to PhD (n = 10). All of them were conscious of the objectives of this study and were fully aware of their participation in this study.

## Instruments

## Semi-structured interview
To explore Chinese EFL teachers’ perceptions of PP use, a semi-structured was employed in this study. The interview questions were developed by the researchers according to the research objectives and the understanding of the constructs in the literature. Three experts were later invited to double-check the language and content of the questions. After applying their comments, five open-ended questions were finalized (Appendix). The respondents were required to answer all the questions in English in a face-to-face interview session. During interviews, they could ask for more clarifications. Each interview took around 30 min. The interviews were audio-recorded using a voice recorder.

## Data collection
Encouraged by the potentiality of semi-structured interviews to collect data related to perceptions and lived experiences, the researchers in this study decided to explore a cohort of 21 Chinese EFL teachers’ perceptions and experiences of PP use through interviews. The researchers, first, planned their study and wrote some key objectives considering the implementations, experiences, perceptions, benefits, challenges, and requirements of PP use in L2 education. Proportionate with these goals, they developed 8 interview questions initially. However, experts’ opinions and comments in the pre-testing phases led to the deletion of 3 questions with vague meaning and structure and overlapping scopes with others. The five interview questions were asked interactively in a face-to-face interview with 21 Chinese EFL teachers, who previously agreed to attend the session individually. After 10 days, the whole interview data were collected from the participants. They had already signed a consent form and were assured of their privacy concerns. The interview data were then transcribed by the researchers carefully. The errors and types were detected and rectified. In the face of a misunderstanding, they called the participants to explain their responses. The data were then sorted per participant and interview question for the final qualitative analysis.

## Data analysis
To analyze the data gathered through interviews, the researchers used thematic analysis following Braun and Clark’s [ ] six-step model (Fig.  ). First, the researchers went through the data to get familiar with the responses and possible themes. Then they managed to enlist some initial codes. Afterward, they combined and juxtaposed initial codes to produce large themes to condense the data as well. As an example from the iterative coding processes and pattens, ‘PP makes teachers and learners connected’ and ‘teacher-student rapport and relationship enhance through PP’ were mixed together to produce the fifth benefit of PP use labelled ‘PP improves teacher-student relationship’. Subsequently, the major themes were reviewed once more before being finalized. After that, names and labels were selected for each theme. Finally, a report of findings including sample responses was created by the researchers. 
To ensure maxims of trustworthiness and data rigor, the researchers asked the Chinese EFL teachers to examine and give feedback on the extracted themes and do a member-checking. Similarly, a second coder with PhD degree checked the themes to calculate inter-coder reliability. After one week, he returned his analyses and the researchers estimated the inter-coder reliability by Cohen’s Kappa Coefficient whose results were satisfactory (r = 0.78). Principles of transferability and confirmability were ensured by deeply explaining and describing the demographics and contextual information of the participants as well as stages of data collection and analysis. With such details, future researchers can replicate this study in other contexts. The researchers were the collectors and analysts of the data in this study. They excluded their previous experiences and perceptions of PP use throughout the study.

## Findings

## The benefits of PP use for L2 education
To answer this question, the researchers examined the third interview question, which focused on the benefits of PP use. The results of the thematic analysis culminated in seven recurrent themes during the interviews (Fig.  ). The participants unanimously believed that PP use has had various benefits for EFL teachers and learners in different areas. The most frequently repeated theme was that PP produces different positive emotions and reduces negative emotions among teachers and learners. In this regard, T5 stated, “for students, PP can increase motivation, engagement, and overall well-being, which can, in turn, improve language acquisition”. Likewise, T7 declared “for teachers, PP is conducive to the development of teaching, and can guide and improve the language learning effect and all-round development of students”. Taking a similar approach, another teacher said “other than the promotion of positive outcomes like interest, mental health, self-confidence, and engagement, PP can eliminate negative emotions in L2 classes, especially sense of boredom, anxiety, and pressure” (T9). The contribution of PP to teacher professionalism, happiness, and satisfaction was another extracted theme as evidenced by T11, who maintained that “PP increases teachers’ desire for professional development and experiencing positive emotions like happiness in their job. These positive feelings finally promote their level of satisfaction with their work”. 
The role of PP in harmonizing and humanizing L2 learning environments was also frequently posed by the participants. For example, T15 claimed that “a learning environment that cares for emotions and feelings enlightens both the process of learning and the climate in which it occurs”. It can also “add joy to the classroom, which invigorates teachers and learners” (T18). Two themes concerned the interpersonal relationships between teachers and students, which can be augmented by PP use. As put by T20, “the application of PP can change students' stereotype of teachers, so that students can get close to teachers and form an immediate relationship in the classroom”. Moreover, the potential of PP in “shaping and improving teachers’ and learners’ interpersonal communication skills like clarity, immediacy, credibility, stroke, etc.” was also pinpointed by Chinese EFL teachers. The last benefit of PP use in Chinese EFL classes was its contribution to character strength rather than deficiency. To support this benefit, a teacher contended that “PP focuses on positivity and strong points of people, hence it can lead to character strength rather than sticking to weaknesses and problems” (T10). In sum, the benefits of PP use in Chinese EFL classes could be divided into its potential to generate positive emotions, stop and reduce negative emotions, improve professional expertise and feelings, establish positive, friendly, and interactive learning environments, and strengthen one’s character.

## The challenges PP use in L2 education
The fourth interview question was analyzed for this research question. The results indicated that Chinese EFL teachers had perceived and experienced different challenges in the course of using PP in their classes. Specifically, six challenges for the implementation of PP were found (Fig.  ). The first theme was related to the limitations in time and budget of L2 education and courses. As stated by T8, “in China, often English language classes are not allocated enough time to take methods and approaches that emerge in the field. Moreover, some approaches like PP require budget and financial support, which is not observed that much at the moment”. The next challenge had to do with differences among learners regarding their personality and needs for attending English classes. In this regard, some participants mentioned “the application of PP largely depends on individual differences and the perceived and desired needs of learners. Some learners may be more open to innovation and psychology, while others oppose talking about emotions” (T21). Another frequently posed challenge was “the demand for receiving specific training for an effective integration of PP into L2 classes” (T3). Another teacher argued “teachers need to be trained on the key principles and practices of PP before using them in their English classes. Otherwise, reverse outcomes may appear” (T13). 
Furthermore, strict policies and resistant educators in English language institutes may put PP use at the risk of failure since “inflexible rules and stakeholders avoid innovative approaches to L2 education including PP” (T4). Another significant challenge mentioned by the respondents was “sociocultural conditions” that impose specific particularities on English language classes in different countries. In support of this them, a teacher declared “the successful implementation of PP into L2 classes considerably depends on the social, cultural, and religious conditions and beliefs of a given context. Countries with dogmatic cultural beliefs may not welcome PP” (T17). These challenges can be compressed to ‘logistic demands of PP use’, ‘teacher-related and learner-related factors such as personality, needs, and openness to diversity/change’, ‘polices and rules’, and ‘the need for training’.

## The requirements of PP use in L2 education
To answer this research questions, the researchers analyzed the last interview question that examined Chinese EFL teachers’ perceptions about the requirements of PP use. The results of thematic analysis of the interviews pointed to eight frequently mentioned requirements (Fig.  ). Two important and recurrent themes were the need for “positive classroom climate” and “positive interpersonal relations” between the teacher and students. As stated by T15, “a positive and friendly environment along with a close rapport between the teacher and his/her students are the very basic requirements of PP use”. Another teacher mentioned “PP is applicable only in case the classroom atmosphere is positive and there is an immediate relationship between teachers and learners in a school” (T7). Teachers were also suggested to be “emotionally literate to effectively detect and regulate positive emotions in L2 classes” (T2). Such literacy and awareness is claimed to be “achievable in PP-specific training courses that are provided for EFL teachers in some contexts” (T19). Another requirement concerned the necessary supports and facilities that PP application demands in EFL classes. In this regard, T13 maintained “PP revolves around individuals’ emotions, traits, and needs. Hence, teachers require support, time, and resources from schools to use such an innovation”. A part of this requirement is due to the idea that “PP is a movement that focuses on individualized and learner-oriented instruction, where each and every student’s feeling matters” (T9). 
Moreover, the participants argued that the presence of “positive attitudes toward change” in teachers and students can also foster the application and efficacy of PP in EFL contexts. As stated by T20, “first and foremost, a positive attitude toward change instead of resistance and attachment to traditional methods, is needed for PP use in L2 classes. With negative attitudes it is not possible to see positive outcomes from an imposed PP integration”. Another teacher said “attitudes and mindsets shape teachers’ and students’ acceptance or rejection of PP use” (T4). The final extracted theme concerned the need for “a humanistic teaching style” on the part of teachers. As claimed by T21, “the core principle of PP is caring for the individuality of students and the importance of their emotions. So, PP is best applied in light of humanistic teaching styles rather than authoritative and teacher-centered approaches”. To conclude, the results of interview analysis showed that the effective application of PP to EFL classes requires positive classroom climates, attitudes, and relationships, along with special training, emotional literacy, individualized instruction, school support and resources, and a humanistic teaching style.

## Discussion
This qualitative study was conducted in the context of China to unveil EFL teachers’ perceptions and experiences of PP use in their classes in terms of its benefits, challenges, and requirements. The results of thematic analysis revealed that PP use in EFL classes had different benefits for teachers and learners including its capacity to generate positive emotions, stop negative emotions, improve professional expertise, establish positive learning environments, and strengthen one’s character. The findings are empirically in tune with various studies in the literature that approved the potential of PP to the production of positive emotions and prevention of negative ones [ ,  , , , , ], among others). The contribution of PP to professionalism and humanizing learning atmosphere is also in accord with Richards [ ], who carried out influential studies on the close linkage of English language teachers’ emotionality and professional development, which later softened learning environments. The findings are theoretically consistent with broaden-and-build theory of emotions that highlights the transferability of positive emotions to other emotions and thought-action repertoires. Teachers’ knowledge of PP and high emotional literacy may explain the findings of the current study. It seems that the participants had already been familiar with the contributions of PP to L2 education. Such awareness might have been acquired from teacher training courses or actual teaching experiences. Another justification could be Chinese EFL teacher positive attitude toward the psychology of L2 education and their perceived need for innovating L2 learning via PP. The emotional nature of L2 education may also be a reason for such a zest for emotionality and PP use.
This study also evinced that the application of PP in EFL classes entails three categories of challenges. They were related to the logistics (time, budget, and facilities), teachers’ resistance rate and need for training, learners’ personality traits, contextual conditions, and institutional policies/rules. These challenges are reflections of Wang et al.’s [ ] study, which positioned an agenda on PP practice in EFL/ESL contexts and enumerated several teacher-related, learner-related, and context-related factors determining the practice of PP. The extracted challenges show that the participants have had a broad overview and understanding of PP use in L2 education since they pointed to micro, meso, and macro-level challenges. This might be due to their research and teaching experiences related to PP. They may have gained such an understanding by reading PP articles and books or implementing this approach in trial and error detecting the possible setbacks of its application. Teachers’ pedagogical content knowledge mixed with educational psychology knowledge may also explain the findings. Concerning the requirement of PP use, it was found that positive classroom climate, positive attitude, and positive relationships are needed for the effective application of PP in Chinese EFL classes. This is theoretically consistent with SCT, which emphasizes on the social-interactive nature of learning and AT which highlights close bonds between teachers and learners in academia. Additionally, focusing on relationship concurs with the R element of PERMA model, SEARCH model, and PROSPER model, where the relationship is a key component. Moreover, positivity and positive attitude are in line with the positive emotion element of PERMA model and the positivity element of PROSPER model. This signifies the familiarity of the participants with the theoretical foundations of PP. Their previous education and training may be the logic behind such a comprehensive image of the requirements of PP. In a similar manner, the findings give credence to Wang et al.’s [ ] agenda in that the proposed requirements are associated with teachers, learners, and sociocultural factors in one way or another. This study also indicated that for successful PP use, Chinese EFL teachers need PP-related training and literacy, individualized instruction, school support and resources, and a humanistic teaching style. These requirements echo the collective and joint practice of PP demanding efforts from teachers, schools, and principals in terms of providing training programs, support, and equipment. This is justifiable by the AT and sociocultural perspectives of PP that defend the shared nature of learning approaches (PP in this case). The participants’ awareness of the logistics of PP use and their own context’s necessities can explain the findings, as well. It seems that the participants had the practical experience of using PP because they highlighted the need for individual-based instruction and special teaching styles for PP. Their openness to diversity and change in L2 pedagogy may also support the interpretations of this study. The findings can also be attributed to Chinese EFL teachers’ agency to innovate and renovate L2 education methods. The sample of this study seems to have had sufficient autonomy and support to practice PP.

## Conclusion and implications
In light of this study, it can be concluded that PP is an applicable school of psychology in various contexts and disciplines including L2 education. Depending on the provision of micro, meso, and macro level requirements, PP can incur numerous benefits for EFL teachers, learners, and schools. It can generate positive emotions, reduce negative emotions, professionally develop teachers and teaching, and humanize the overall classroom culture and climate in EFL settings. Based on the findings, it can also be argued that the EFL context of China has had a positive view of PP movement providing sufficient support and training opportunities of EFL teachers. Such a backbone may have made them understand the nuts and bolts of this shift toward positivity and emotionality in L2 education. Another conclusion could be the idea that PP is applicable to various contexts in case the teachers, as frontline forces, are trained and aware of the uses, setbacks, and necessities of this approach. Moreover, it can be asserted that effective use of PP in L2 education requires a broad view of factors related to teachers, learners, resources, sociocultural conditions, and professional support from authorities.
In view of these contentions, the current study can offer implications for theory and practice in different ways. Theoretically, the study expands the literature on PP and L2 education by providing practical insights from teachers’ real-life experiences of applying PP rather than exclusively sticking to conceptual suggestions. L2 teacher psychology and teacher education theories may also develop in light of the findings. Practically, EFL teachers, teacher educators, policy-makers, and school managers. The findings can inform teachers of a new teaching approach that puts emotions at the center of all instructional efforts. Hence, EFL teachers can use this study to shift from traditional L2 teaching approaches to innovative and emerging ones. They can figure out the benefits and practical ways of employing PP in L2 classes to bring about positive and optimal academic outcomes. Teacher trainers may benefit from this study and run workshops and training courses for EFL teachers in which the principles and practices of PP are fully explained to EFL teachers. The benefits and practical approaches to PP injection can be explained to pre-service and in-service teachers in such training courses. Furthermore, the study can be insightful for language policy-makers in that they can make decisions regarding the injection of emotionology and educational psychology to L2 education. They can revise existing plans and methods of L2 education in light of PP. Policies that ignore emotionality can be replaced with PP-informed ones. Another beneficiary camp is school managers, who can realize the benefits of PP use in English classes if they provide the required resources and facilities for EFL teachers. School managers may foster the integration of PP into L2 classes by supporting teachers via a positive school climate and professional development supports. Despite these takeaways, this study suffered from some limitations. First, the sample size was small, which constrained the generalizability scope of the interpretations. Large scale studies can be done to reach a more comprehensive image of actual practice and experience of PP and L2 education. Broader sampling is also an advised strategy to address the scope limitation of the present research in the future. Another limitation was the use of a single research instrument, while narratives, observations, focused groups, and reflective journals could complement the interviews. Future scholars are then invited to use mixed-methods designs to triangulate the data. The voices of teachers and students can be integrated in a single study as well. Another limitation concerns the one-shot nature of data collection, which overlooks the dynamism of PP use over time. Future studies, consequently, can focus on the developmental and longitudinal process of PP integration and injection into L2 education. Semester-long or full-year case studies are suggested to unveil the actual implementation and outcomes of PP practices in L2 education using multiple sources of data across time periods. Interventional studies on how PP practices may promote EFL teachers’ professionalism and identity are also recommended. The role of culture and teacher-related factors (e.g., age, gender, teaching experience, and university degree) were ignored in this study. Hence, further investigations can be done on the mediating role of such factors in the applicability of PP by EFL teachers. The application of PP principles and practices to emotionality research in emerging modes of education, including artificial intelligence (AI) mediated ones, is also an interesting line of future research [ , ].
