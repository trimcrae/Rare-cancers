## Abstract

## Objective
Understanding patients’ wishes and preferences during hospitalisation is a crucial component of care. We identified a gap related to documentation of advance directives and patient preferences for care and focused on ensuring appropriate goals of care discussions were occurring and documented. Our aim was to improve the documentation of advance care planning notes to include 80% of targeted hospitalised patients.

## Patients and methods
Hospitalised patients in two community hospitals were included. We performed serial Plan-Do-Measure-Act cycles. The first intervention introduced the ‘surprise question’ during an afternoon huddle. Intervention 2 emphasised documentation of the advance care planning note. The third intervention used a structured approach led by administrators at daily multidisciplinary huddles and identified patients with an Elderly Risk Assessment score of 16 or greater as targets for advance care planning documentation.

## Results
From a baseline performance under 10%, we increased to greater than 80% of patients with Elderly Risk Assessment scores of 16 or higher having documented advance care planning. We were able to sustain this performance over subsequent years.

## Conclusion
A structured approach that identifies a targeted population at higher risk of mortality, and implementation of a checklist at a daily multidisciplinary huddle provided sustained improvement in advance care planning documentation. This provides the opportunity for improved patient care that is aligned with their values and preferences.

Keywords: Hospital medicine, Patient-centred care, Healthcare quality improvement

## WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC
External metrics identify unanticipated in-hospital mortality as a negative assessment. Many patients may not have adequate documentation of goals of care to indicate the limitations they may want, ensuring their care is aligned with their values. Care concordant with patient values and preferences is a vital part of hospital care. Identification of those goals reaches beyond code status, and there is an opportunity at time of hospitalisation to document advance care planning, improving the current and future episodes of care.

## WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
Our study describes a set of interventions aimed at increasing documentation of advance care planning notes for patients with acute hospitalisations. We identified that a simple intervention of a checklist during a daily huddle was able to promote increased completion of advance care planning documentation.

## HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY
Improved documentation helps to align care provided with patient wishes and values. Hospital care is often fragmented and a durable document that is easily accessible within the electronic health record provides an opportunity to have continuity regarding a stressful topic. From a practice perspective, it may increase utilisation of palliative and hospice services, as more patients are able to express their wishes beyond code status. This, in turn, can result in lower overall healthcare spending, improved concordance with patient preferences and satisfaction with care.

## Introduction
 In late 2019, we identified a higher observed-to-expected mortality rate in one of our hospital system’s community hospitals. Based on our casemix index, more patients were dying than would be expected based on their acuity. To understand this better, we undertook a root cause analysis. This revealed many contributing issues. One recurring theme was that of patients with multiple hospital admissions and readmissions prior to their ultimately terminal admission, during which they died. Most of these patients, despite a high burden of disease, had no advance care planning (ACP) documentation. Had it been present, this may have prompted initiation of palliative and hospice care for patients with a high burden of disease whose wishes were congruent with this approach. Because the observed-to-expected mortality metric excludes hospice-status patients, we initiated a project to match resources with patient wishes and thus improve hospital performance and provide patient-centred care.
A systematic review by Brinkman-Stoppelenburg et al identified several outcomes related to increased advanced care planning, including decreases in hospital utilisation, reoperation, days in hospital, mechanical ventilation, procedural interventions, pressors, dialysis, intensitve care unit (ICU) admission, tube feeding, laboratory and radiographic studies and cardiopulmonary resuscitation. There were increases in hospice care, withdrawal from dialysis, patient quality of life, with some studies showing increased ICU use and longer hospitalisation. The effect of advanced care planning likely depends on patient factors including age and comorbidities. Despite low rates of use for frail older adults, ACP has been shown to have a positive effect on patients as they approach the end of life, enables greater access to palliative and hospice consultation and has shown improved concordance with patient preferences at end of life. Another study showed an improved sense of control, peace of mind and facilitating communication with family members about end of life. 
Similar to prior studies, we found that the documentation of ACP notes was low for admitted patients. These notes provide continuity of care planning at a critical time and facilitate adherence to patient wishes.
The actual documentation of goals of care and efforts to improve this are uncommon in the literature. A Canadian group identified that despite a policy to have all patients get updated documentation of goals of care, only 63% had this documentation and the quality was variable. Other studies identified the low rates of documentation of advanced directives and opportunities to move beyond code status to a more descriptive and thorough understanding. Prior studies that described interventions to increase documentation include one undertaken in nursing facilities by social workers (baseline 20% documentation of code status documentation increased to 40%), another in a large hospital setting using similar multidisciplinary rounds (baseline 41% with advanced care planning increased to 75%), use of a medical record prompt for nursing home admissions (increased from 4% to 63%). 
The Elderly Risk Assessment (ERA) score incorporates risk factors for hospitalisation of older adults in the outpatient setting. Specifically, age, marital status, hospital days in the last 2 years and comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, stroke, congestive heart failure/coronary artery disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cancer and/or dementia) ( ). As we considered potential ways to identify patients for whom it would be appropriate for advanced care planning in intervention 3, prior experience with ERA by an improvement team member, and availability in the electronic health record (EHR) led to the selection of this score. The ERA score has been validated to predict risk for subsequent hospital visits after an emergency department (ED visit but not for our use case. 
 We aimed to increase documentation of ACP notes for hospitalised patients. Our initial goal was for >80% of targeted hospitalised patients to have an ACP note. This patient population evolved over the course of the project. Initially, all hospitalised patients were included. It became clear the team felt the efforts should be focused on a narrower population and we moved towards the use of ‘surprise question’, followed by the ERA score. The final criteria specified the goal of >80% of patients with an ERA score greater ≥16 to have an ACP note.

## Patients and methods

## Context
Our project took place at two community hospitals in small cities within a rural area that is part of a healthcare system with 17 community hospitals and a large academic centre in the Midwestern USA. Hospital 1 (H1) and hospital 2 (H2) are licensed for 51 and 22 medical/surgical inpatient beds, with an annual census of 3900 and 1800 medical admissions, respectively. Both hospitals provided general medical/surgical care with no medical subspecialty consultations, while H1 also offered intensive care services. Both hospitals were staffed by physician hospitalists and nurse practitioners/physician assistants. Case Mix Index for H1 is 1.15 and for H2 is 1.32. The intervention was implemented sequentially, starting in H1 and diffused to H2 after 12 weeks.
H1 had an existing daily ‘discharge huddle’ to ensure appropriate planning for hospital dismissal was occurring. This multidisciplinary group was facilitated by a local administrator and used a checklist of topics to cover and occurred during all weekdays. This huddle was identified as a venue to drive improving ACP, H2 did not have a similar huddle at the time the project was formulated.
Patients and public were not included in the planning of the intervention nor in discussions around dissemination. The question of how we can improve ACP does have a direct impact on the quality of patient care, in particular as it relates to providing care that is concordant with patient values and preferences. It was implicit in our goal that aligning care with patient preferences was the desired outcome and we felt that this would be an acceptable pursuit from the patient’s perspective.

## Intervention(s)
Improvement team members included regional and local hospital physicians, nursing and administrative leaders, physician quality champion, quality improvement specialist, social worker and care coordinators.

## Intervention 1 (February 2020)
The hospitalist leadership team added discussion of code status and ACP documentation as an item in the checklist for review at the daily multidisciplinary team huddle led by the site administrator. Code status and ACP documentation were audited for every hospitalised patient on the medicine service, daily. Patients to approach for goals of care discussions were selected by using the ‘surprise question’. The physician or advanced practice provider caring for the patient determined whether they would be surprised or not if the patient passed away within 12 months. Patients for whom death would not be unexpected (eg, not a surprise) were to be offered goals of care discussions and their code status was updated in the EHR and on the tracking report used at the huddles

## Intervention 2 (June 2020)
The ACP note was emphasised as an expected outcome, rather than documentation of only code status. In July 2020, regional hospital leadership transitioned to a new physician lead who took on championing the project. This individual worked closely with the quality improvement specialist and interfaced with the front-line care teams to promote the work and gather feedback.

## Intervention 3 (November 2020)
The ERA score was introduced to objectively identify patients with more complex illness and risk of subsequent hospitalisation. Patients with an ERA score greater than or equal to 16 were expected to have an ACP note documented. The score was calculated automatically and included in the patient list that was used to guide the huddle.
H2 joined the project in late 2020, and initiated work during Intervention 3, with the same processes as H1, using the ERA score.

## Control phase
Daily huddles continued with use of the checklist and identification of patients using the ERA score, tracking of rates of completion, with the same multidisciplinary format. No additional interventions were enacted during this time and the team continued to monitor ACP note completion.

## Study of the intervention(s)
We relied on a local administrator to serve as a scribe. They attended huddles to collect data and reinforced the use of the checklist. This individual provided feedback to the improvement team on the group dynamic and engagement to continue refinement and intervention iteration.
Outside the efforts put forth by this team, there were no other initiatives aimed at changing the way goals of care or ACP were used.

## Measures and analysis
Our primary measure was confirmation of an ACP discussion, initially via the checklist and subsequently the documentation of an ACP note in the EHR.
The presence of an ACP note was initially manually extracted by our quality improvement coordinator. Later, a report was created to automate the process.
Qualitative data were collected ad hoc by the huddle administrator and physician champion and reported to improvement project leaders.
Descriptive statistics were used to assess the quantitative data and understand the changes in the use of the ACP note over time with each intervention.

## Patient and public involvement
We did not have a formal mechanism for patient and public involvement in this intervention. However, our goal was to improve the alignment of care provided with patient goals and preferences, as such they were integral to our work.

## Results
We found minimal use of ACP notes prior to starting the improvement project. Baseline data from June 2020 show that only a single ACP note was filed at H1, and at H2, there were none.
The trajectory of uptake of ACP documentation is demonstrated in .
 The interventions 1–3 in H2 resulted in an increase in documentation in patients with an ERA>16, from a baseline ranging from 0% to 6% in the 4 months prior to the intervention, to >80% by month 2.
After the initiation of intervention 2, the use of ACP notes remained low until September. Although it was not an intentional intervention for this project, we note that a new regional leader for hospital medicine started the role in mid-August 2020. In September 2020, the numbers begin to increase and with intervention 3, the formal adoption of the ERA score as prompt for ACP documentation in November 2020, the goal of 80% is met.
These results were sustained through September 2022 at both hospitals with >80% adherence without further interventions.
Qualitative feedback from the team demonstrated a theme of concern for lack of familiarity with how to have conversation and use the resources within the EHR. An unexpected result of the early interventions included concerns raised by staff that the conversations may not be appropriate. In response to feedback, the improvement team partnered with hospice to provide education to the hospital clinicians and increased discussion among the multidisciplinary group as to which patients were most appropriate for ACP documentation.

## Conclusion

## Summary
We found through iterative refinement that documentation of ACP notes could be improved and sustained over time. Key interventions included an updated leadership team that included a strong regional leader and champion for the project and shifting to an objective criterion for the indication to engage in goals of care discussions and create the documentation—the ERA score. Prior to the final intervention of using the ERA score to identify patients, the number of patients with ACP notes was very low. After implementation of this criterion, the team quickly met the goal of 80% and regularly exceeded it, at times reaching 100%.
An unintended consequence of this project was that the hospice services did not have enough resources to evaluate all the patients being referred. The chronic underutilisation of this resource had led to sparse staffing that did not reflect the true needs of the communities when care concordant with patient values was being provided. An opportunity to increase services and staffing was identified.

## Interpretation
We believe that the objective approach to identifying patients who would benefit from ACP, creating a team accountable for performance and achieving improved documentation is feasible for most hospital systems. We acknowledge that strong leadership and a dedicated champion can have a tremendous impact on the outcome of a project. The impact of a new regional hospital leader cannot be ignored as a special cause variation that likely had a lasting impact on the success of the project.
Despite broad acknowledgement that ACP is beneficial to patient populations we found few prior studies that looked at interventions to increase ACP for older adults and hospitalised patients. We believe that our intervention reflects a process that many other settings could emulate. Our outcome is patient-oriented and care team-oriented and lays the groundwork for care that is concordant with patient values.
The cost to our system to implement this intervention was relatively low. We had already implemented the discharge huddle and adding an item to the checklist for accountability was negligible. We did not measure financial outcomes with this intervention and no specific funding was allocated.

## Limitations
This project took place at two small hospitals. It is possible that this limited scope may reduce generalisability to other settings. Manual extraction of some data points, particularly earlier in the project, may have introduced unintentional errors. As noted, leadership change during the project may have been a factor that increased uptake of a new initiative, and we are not able to quantify the effect of this variable.
Rather than using the ACP note as a surrogate for goals of care conversations, we considered a using hospice consultation, however, we felt this focused too much on patients who are approaching end of life and did not include patients who were not yet in this phase. Code status was also considered, but the ACP note framework provides additional nuance, and we felt the outcome was more valuable.

## Discussion
There is inherent value in establishing the patients’ goals of care and values as they age. ACP conversations are an opportunity to engage with patients and their loved ones about the eventuality of the end of life. Subsequent documentation of the conversation provides a lasting record so that those wishes can be followed. Regardless of any objective effect on observed-to-expected mortality, we hope that by having this conversation we facilitate adherence to patient desires and provide care that is concordant with their values. The use of the ACP note as a marker for improved care is rooted in the importance of having the conversation and incorporation of appropriate documentation is intended to provide a best-practice—communication to the whole care team and those downstream who may care for the patient. In a hospital system with rotating staff—on service for 1 week and off service for the next—the integrity of the medical record is valuable. ACP documentation provides successive teams direction on how to approach potential scenarios. Working on this project allowed our hospital medicine teams to work more closely with our colleagues in hospice and contributed to a positive culture change.
We received informal feedback that providers were uncomfortable with goals of care conversations. They indicated that they did not feel they had adequate training around goals of care conversations or end-of-life discussions. There were times during the project when team members felt unclear about who was responsible for having the discussions with patients/family members—the clinician team, nursing, social work/case management or hospice team. The ERA score was not one that had been used in this setting previously and learning to use the score was another barrier that was encountered. Finally, the workflow for documenting an ACP note in the EHR was another task that required additional education to the team.
The need for improved education around ACP has been identified in prior studies and our sites are no exception. Educational efforts have been found to be one factor that contribute to successfully increasing goals of care conversations and would likely augment our efforts. The Serious Illness Conversation Guide (Ariadne Labs, Boston, Massachusetts, USA) is a free and widely used tool for clinicians to structure conversations around goals of care. Residencies have demonstrated efforts to incorporate ACP into their curricula. , 
We have had excellent sustained results from this project. This speaks to the value of the care provided, the way the team incorporated ACP conversations into the daily work, and the accountability check at the huddle. Key team members included the administrative partners who led the huddle and kept the momentum of this project going, even when faced with staffing crises and pandemic surges, and clinician champions who can interface between the front-line staff having the conversations and the administrative team.
