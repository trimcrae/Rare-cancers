## ABSTRACT
This study examined how community mental health case managers, most of whom were mental health nurses, modified their day‐to‐day practice to strengthen the therapeutic relationship within community services. Using a participatory action research design across 10 centres in Catalonia, Spain, 24 case managers and 105 patients engaged in two action‐reflection cycles. Data from reflective diaries and focus groups were analyzed using inductive thematic analysis. Through cyclical observation and collaborative reflection, participants identified key facilitators and barriers to developing the therapeutic relationship and co‐designed two practice innovations: (1) jointly‐created informational materials clarifying case‐management scope and expectations, and (2) regular mixed patient‐professional reflective groups focused on the therapeutic process. Despite structural pressures, heavy workloads, service fragmentation, and limited opportunities for professional reflection, the participatory approach supported sustainable, relationally focused practice change grounded in patient‐provider dialogue. Embedding structured reflective spaces within routine care emerged as a practical mechanism to consolidate therapeutic alliance and advance recovery‐oriented practice. While findings apply across disciplines, they are particularly relevant to nursing‐led and nurse‐participating case‐management teams, in which continuous therapeutic contact, coordination functions and relational expertise intersect. The study offers a replicable framework for enhancing person‐centred care in community mental health settings, and it underscores the value of nurse‐involved co‐creation and structured reflection to maintain therapeutic presence, align expectations and integrate lived experience into ongoing quality improvement.

## Participants
This study was conducted with 26 mental health case managers working across 10 community mental health centres in the Barcelona metropolitan area, in Spain. Each centre provides mental health services within a defined geographic catchment area, with staffing levels based on population density, resulting in teams of 1 to 6 case managers.
After the presentation to the coordinator of the case management programme at the representing institution, an information meeting was held to invite participants to participate, provided they met the following criteria: (a) they had been in the case management programme for at least 1 year, and (b) they were not on sick leave or absent during the study. Of the 26 professionals available, 24 participated and began the PAR. The remaining invited case managers declined to participate, citing personal reasons or professional demotivation.
The participating case managers were organized according to their usual workplace, and their direct coordinator assisted all of them. Due to structural changes in the company's organizational chart and various professional relocations, only 22 of the initial 24 case managers completed the two full PAR cycles.

2.3

## Data Collection
Multiple qualitative methods were used to collect data across the different stages of the PAR. In the initial contextual analysis, comprehensive meetings were held with the case management team's coordination service. These meetings provided a space to discuss the workers' current situation, the service's status, and the most common challenges encountered in daily clinical practice. Summaries of these meetings and daily ad hoc observations were used to develop a guideline document for participants.
This document briefly explained the PAR phases and actions and the participant's role in the research, and offered a tutorial on completing the self‐recorded reflective diaries. It also explained the three areas for reflection: language, action and context, to facilitate comprehension of the observation of the therapeutic relationship.
The reflective diary was the primary tool for recording the actions decided upon within the PAR framework. Its main objective was to track participants' reflections in order to improve their clinical practice and the self‐reflective process it generated. Participants were provided with a diary template (Appendix  ) to facilitate data recording. They completed and submitted this to the research team, thereby creating an audit trail that enhanced the credibility of the co‐generated findings (Creswell  ).
Group‐based data collection techniques—specifically, focus group discussions—were implemented to stimulate collective reflection, using a widely trusted qualitative research method to elicit interactive, multi‐voiced insights (Nyumba et al.  ; Moreno‐Poyato et al.  ). Each PAR cycle included up to 2 reflective group sessions held in a meeting room in the hospital and moderated by the principal investigator and another team member. The sessions were recorded using an audio device and later transcribed. Additionally, in situ notes were taken during the sessions, and field notes were recorded as audio, capturing the research team's impressions at the end of each discussion. The average duration of the focus groups was 2 h. During these sessions, the findings derived from the reflective diaries were validated, and group discussions were encouraged to propose improvements in the therapeutic relationship, which were subsequently evaluated in the second phase of the PAR cycle.

2.4

## Data Analysis
Data were analyzed using inductive thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke  ), which is appropriate for PAR, in which themes emerge from participants' reflective accounts rather than from predetermined categories. This involved semantic coding of diary entries and focus group transcripts, followed by collaborative categorization to identify overarching patterns in the development of therapeutic relationships.
The analysis proceeded by identifying content, coding and categorizing the primary themes, and tracking their evolution across the stages of PAR. During the initial phase, descriptive codes were assigned based on semantic content. These codes were subsequently organized into subcategories to reflect various combinations of the identified codes. In the final stage, overarching categories were formulated to deepen understanding of the text. The analytical process was further strengthened through researcher triangulation, involving the lead author and other team members, who collaboratively verified the results in a reflective, critical and consensual manner. This approach underpinned a rigorous coding process (Merriam and Tisdell  ). Additionally, the analysis team used the Dedoose software for data analysis (Dedoose  ).
This study adhered to the principles of credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability, ensuring the overall trustworthiness of qualitative research (Polit and Beck  ). The research team included experts in mental health (clinical team) alongside individuals trained in qualitative methodologies and experienced in similar studies (methodological squad). This diverse composition, reflecting varied ontological perspectives, enhanced neutrality by encouraging input from all members. The team maintained an open, consensus‐driven and critically reflective approach. Data collection methods were thoroughly documented to ensure reliability, and participants received ongoing feedback throughout the process. At the start of each stage, a summary of the analysis was shared with participants for validation, thereby reinforcing the authenticity and credibility of the data. Additionally, the use of multiple data collection methods and the involvement of various researchers ensured data triangulation. Reflexivity was a key element throughout the study, supported by continuous critical analysis. The research team maintained a balance of ‘insider’ and ‘outsider’ perspectives. While the lead researcher's background as a Mental Health Nurse and former Case Manager at the institution facilitated rapport, the co‐investigator, an external Senior Lecturer, provided an ‘outsider’ perspective to ensure methodological distance. Potential biases were managed through continuous reflexive dialogue within the research team and a consensus‐driven triangulation process. Crucially, the researchers held no hierarchical or supervisory authority over the participants, who perceived the team as independent. The study's extensive, collaborative nature also enhanced the transferability of the findings across all participating centres. To account for positionality, researchers recorded their impressions and reactions during participant interactions and incorporated these reflections into the analysis. All professionals signed an informed consent form and agreed to participate voluntarily in the study. To recognize the effort required for study participation, due to possible work overload, participants were relieved of their regular care duties while attending the study‐group sessions. Participants were assigned a code to track their diaries, ensuring the anonymity and confidentiality of the data collected. Additionally, the principal investigator established a generic study email account to manage the diaries, which were accessible only to them. The healthcare ethics committee of the representing institution approved the study.

## Findings
Of the 24 case managers who enrolled, 22 completed the full participatory action research cycle (a retention rate of 91.7%). The sample had a mean age of 40.8 years (SD = 6.1, range 28–49), and women comprised the majority (68%, n = 15). The professionals represented diverse disciplinary backgrounds: nine were mental health nurses, five were social workers, five were occupational therapists, four were social educators and one was a psychologist. Collectively, the group had an average of 5.7 years (SD = 2.4) of experience in mental health case management, and all but five had received prior formal training in therapeutic relationships.
3.1

## Stage 0: Contextual Analysis of the Case Management Programme
The aim of this phase was to increase understanding of the reality of care practices among all the case managers in the programme. The observation lasted 2 months and included two meetings with the programme coordinator, resulting in a comprehensive overview of the study's context. The importance of helping the case managers understand the PAR process was also clearly stated.
It was concluded that despite physical differences across regions, disparities among teams in the reference community mental health centres and varying workloads, the care model and its limitations were similar across regions. Establishing a therapeutic alliance, followed by an ongoing therapeutic relationship, was identified as the primary approach to managing these patients in the community. This approach facilitated a shared understanding of the need to enhance the impact of that relationship.

## First Cycle
4.1

## Stage 1: Planning
The first cycle of the PAR began with the first focus group (FG). The research team shared the results obtained in Stage 0 and reviewed the dossier in accordance with the guidelines for daily clinical practice observations. Additionally, the reflective journal was introduced as a tool for recording reflections.
The initial group aimed to understand the meaning of the TR in case management and participants' experiences with this phenomenon throughout their professional careers. Participants later identified three everyday situations in which they wanted to analyze the therapeutic relationship to have more knowledge:
Admission to the case management programme.
A support visit that included a third party.
A discharge visit from the programme.

The TR must be built once you get to know that person. The most important aspect of the first visits is conveying to the individual that I am genuinely interested in learning more about them. (CV2)

There are moments of accompaniment that have a much greater impact on the relationship because they hold a deeper meaning for the individual. (ESP1)

One of my concerns is not only how you establish and maintain the connection but also how you disengage and facilitate the discharge. (CER2)

4.2

## Stages 2–3: Action and Observation
After the first FG, participants returned to their daily practice for 2 months. Using the provided guide document, they observed their interactions during community visits. Following the observations, interactions were recorded in reflective journals. A total of 24 journals indicated that during the programme's first visits, participants chose environments close to the patient, such as the patient's neighbourhood or home. Concepts such as active listening, respect, presence and empathy fostered the creation of an initial bond, enabling the achievement of personal recovery goals through TR, grounded in autonomy rather than paternalism. Regarding support visits, TR was repeatedly used to link individuals with others. The setting was often perceived as more hostile, making tone of voice a key factor in encouraging patient engagement. Most records highlighted TR's role in mediating during support visits, with physical proximity enhancing dialogue. For discharge visits, a comfortable space was again chosen for the patient, and TR was utilized to review the objectives achieved during the period of enrollment in the programme. This highlighted the patient's autonomy and justified the discharge. Participants also emphasized the need to set limits within TR upon discharge and strongly recommended telematic support during the gradual disengagement process.

4.3

## Stage 4: Collective Reflection
After analyzing the reflective journals, the results were shared in the second FG, which took place over 2 months. The participants validated the observations. A key consensus was that referring entities' lack of understanding of the case management programme hindered the development of an effective TR. Participants agreed that in some cases patients were enrolled not voluntarily but through coercion upon hospital discharge. They collectively recognized the need to improve this aspect of their practice. Another central area for improvement was identified: the effectiveness of collective reflection spaces in FG sessions. Listening, sharing ideas and proposing improvements were highly stimulating for enhancing TR use. Consequently, participants suggested establishing stable reflection groups to improve case management practices. Participants were asked to submit written reflections or proposals via email for consideration in the following cycle.

## Second Cycle
5.1

## Stage 1: Replanning
This phase lasted approximately 2 months. Initially, the principal investigator analyzed the case managers' proposals and communicated the results to them. The third FG aimed to refine strategies and reach a consensus on implementing them. The first strategy focused on enhancing the impact of TR at the beginning of the patient's engagement with the programme. The goal was to address limited awareness of case management services, increase voluntary referrals and improve participants' effectiveness. To address this, participants decided to redesign the programme's informational brochures (Figure  ). These brochures would serve a dual function: informing patients about the programme's existence and educating mental health services about available resources, the target population, the recovery‐based care approach, and the referral process, thereby reinforcing the principle of voluntary participation.That is why I mentioned the brochure. It is a way to ensure that you provide the patient with something prepared in advance during the first visit, while also allowing the referring professional to review it. Over time, they gain a basic understanding of how we work, through simple and clear information, right? (CV3)

The second improvement strategy involved creating reflective supervision spaces every 6 months, where participants unanimously agreed that patients should be included as expert voices in assessing the effectiveness of TR.Before starting, another option could be to ask the patient what helped them in their process—those who engaged well and those who did not—what worked and what did not. (GAR4)

Given the large patient volume, the improvement intervention was organized by service areas, grouping smaller regions to collect more data. It was decided that these reflection groups would take place quarterly to ensure continuous service improvement. Five new supervision spaces were established, and case management nurses committed themselves to organizing the calendar, recruiting patients, and leading the sessions. The research team was asked to participate in the initial meetings.
 
5.2

## Stages 2–3: Action and Observation
Following the Replanning phase, the research team informed the programme coordinator about the proposed improvements. The process lasted approximately 2 months. Participants documented their activities in informal notes and voice recordings, which were submitted to the principal investigator for analysis and review. To improve TR's impact at programme entry, a team of five reviewed old brochures, redesigned them and presented the revised versions to the hospital's communication department and programme coordinator. The new brochures were approved for production and distribution. Meanwhile, reflective supervision spaces were organized. Professionals introduced the initiative to patients, invited them to participate, scheduled sessions, and informed the directors of all relevant community mental health centres. Venues were prepared, including refreshments, to create a welcoming and secure atmosphere. Four out of five planned groups took place; the last was not feasible due to geographical distance and patients' lack of electronic means for telematic participation. Sessions were recorded and transcribed for further analysis (see Table  ). Patients emphasized the importance of individualization, flexibility and patience in building TR. They also stressed the importance of empathy, honesty and a genuine desire to help.I need to know that you are there. We are sensitive beings. (GAR4.2)

Participants expressed concerns about difficulties they encountered during programme admission and discharge, suggesting ways for improvement, including extending the enrollment period, increasing the intensity and frequency of interactions and enhancing the discharge process. Both patients and professionals agreed that case management and TR‐driven care require a vocational commitment.
 
5.3

## Stage 4: Final Reflection and Conclusions
During the 3 months following the implementation of improvements, participants sent their impressions to the lead researcher, who analyzed them and compiled them into a presentation for the fourth FG. The main objective of this session was to reflect on and discuss the implementation of strategies in clinical practice and the overall impact of the PAR process on participants' perceptions of the TR. Regarding the implemented strategies, participants expressed satisfaction with the results from the informational brochures and reflective spaces, validating their usefulness in enhancing TR within the programme:I think I had fallen into a kind of inertia, right? I was not paying close attention to all the elements that contribute to how visits are conducted, where they occur and with whom. Personally, this process helped me greatly in that regard, as these components and factors have a significant impact on fairness. However, when you have so much work and are just getting through it, I was not analyzing or properly caring for these aspects. So, in that sense, it has been a learning experience for me. (ESP2)

Regarding the spaces created in collaboration with patients, participants reiterated that receiving feedback from those they cared for was crucial in fostering awareness of their daily practice:It helped me become more aware of… how I perform my work. I liked having feedback on how people perceive it, because it also shows you which paths you are following correctly and which ones you might need to reconsider. (CAST2)

Case management nurses affirmed that they had incorporated TR as the central axis guiding their interventions, particularly in the areas where they had practiced self‐observation:Preparing the nest—it is like… taking the patient to a place where they feel comfortable, right? And it is incredible how, in all the areas they work in, they instinctively look for these spaces… I had never noticed it before, but it caught my attention when we kept our diaries. (ESP2)

They noted that raising awareness about this aspect and patient validation had given them greater confidence in their practice. They also emphasized the importance of highlighting TR's reciprocal nature and the need for ongoing feedback on its status. The overall opinion about participating in the study was very positive. However, they also acknowledged feeling an increased workload in the more organizational aspects of the study, such as meetings, journaling and travel. Additionally, they expressed concerns about whether the strategies could be sustained in the future, given turnover among temporary professionals and the extent to which the project had been institutionally validated:Let us say it sheds light on aspects you normally would not consider. However, the question is whether that light will fade again over time, right? That is why it would be ideal to repeat these processes periodically. Ultimately, it is all about pausing, analyzing oneself and receiving feedback on the quality of what you are doing, because ultimately, that is the key, isn't it? (CER3)

At the end of the fourth FG, conclusions were drawn about the two PAR cycles and their usefulness, comprehensively closing the process. An infographic summarizing the results was created and shared with all participants, who valued it as a meaningful takeaway (see Figure  ).

## Discussion
This study aimed to explore the process of change in the clinical practice of mental health case managers in community‐based mental health services, specifically their participation in an action research process to improve the therapeutic relationship. Results indicate that creating structured spaces for professional reflection promotes a greater awareness of relational care dimensions, leading to more person‐centred and recovery‐oriented practices (McCormack and McCance  ; Hamovitch et al.  ).
The findings suggest that the therapeutic relationship is not static but rather a malleable practice jointly produced by micro‐contexts, power dynamics and structural conditions. Participatory approaches enable professionals to move beyond routine‐driven care, allowing them to critically examine how their communication, attitudes and relational skills influence therapeutic engagement (Hartley et al.  ; Moreno‐Poyato et al.  ). Participants reported enhanced self‐awareness and confidence, particularly in navigating critical moments such as admissions, joint visits and discharges, consistent with research highlighting the attitudinal component as central to TR development (Tolosa‐Merlos et al.  ).
Admission visits were deliberately relocated to patient‐selected ‘nests’ (neighbourhood benches and home kitchens) where active listening, unhurried presence and autonomy‐oriented language fostered an initial bond. Other studies have shown that inpatient nurses' eye contact, body orientation, and vocal tone fluctuate with ward geography and shift tempo, supporting the argument that space itself is an active ingredient in relational care (Cleary et al.  ; Cleary and Horsfall  ).
Joint visits, conversely, were perceived as more hostile. Case managers compensated by modulating tone of voice and increasing physical proximity to the patient, using TR to mediate between the individual and family, psychiatrist or social worker. The literature describes a strategy in which consumers perceive the alliance as stronger when clinicians help them integrate with external stakeholders rather than exclude them (Kornhaber et al.  ).
The discharge visit emerged in our data as a uniquely delicate negotiation of autonomy and loss. Another dissertation reported that clients experienced the TR as ‘thinner’ at discharge, especially when separation felt abrupt (Roebuck  ). Participants feared ‘pulling the rug’ from patients who had come to rely on weekly contact, a loss‐anxiety repeatedly noted by consumers (Kornhaber et al.  ). By institutionalizing a phased, telematically supported disengagement and inviting patients to narrate the goals they achieved, staff converted discharge into a reciprocal rite of passage that emphasized autonomy and justified separation. The practice aligns with recovery‐oriented approaches (Anthony  ) and is operationalized in community mental‐health studies that emphasize shared decision‐making and self‐direction (Ventosa‐Ruiz et al.  ).
The two strategies implemented—redesigning informational brochures and organizing mixed patient‐professional reflective groups—addressed key barriers previously identified in the literature, such as limited patient understanding of case management programmes and the need for genuine spaces for encounter and dialogue (Nilsen et al.  ).
The admission brochure established transparency and voluntary engagement, thereby strengthening the TR at first contact. It directly addressed the moral tension created when involuntary hospitalization precipitates coerced referral, a barrier repeatedly associated with weaker engagement (Browne et al.  ). The brochure was created locally with input from both clinicians and patients. This approach has been shown to double self‐referral rates in recent studies (Chambers et al.  ). Our second major output—the co‐creation of quarterly reflective supervision groups that include patients as expert auditors—extends the participatory logic in Spanish mental‐health units (Moreno‐Poyato et al.  ) and in Australian community teams (Chambers et al.  ). Patient feedback contributed to validating and refining professional practice. It conferred professional legitimacy by stating that lived experience is an indispensable source of quality assurance.
Despite these positive impacts, several persistent structural challenges were identified. Heavy workloads, organizational fragmentation and limited resources constrained both the establishment of strong TRs and the sustainability of the improvement strategies (Hanrahan et al.  ; Ovretveit et al.  ). However, the findings of this study served as a catalyst for a significant shift in clinical perspective, leading the case managers of the institution to autonomously develop a TR‐centred document tool following the project's conclusion. This tool has since been adopted as the primary framework for organizing the recovery process, effectively embedding the relational focus into the institutional culture. Nevertheless, for these changes to be fully sustained and inclusive, the institution must provide the necessary resources—such as protected time and dedicated spaces—to ensure that these reflective cycles move beyond staff‐only meetings and consistently reintegrate the patient's voice. This suggests that while a change in mindset can trigger structural innovation, long‐term sustainability remains dependent on ongoing institutional endorsement and resource allocation (Ministerio de Sanidad  ).
6.1

## Strengths and Limitations
The limitations of this study include its focus on a single regional context, which may affect transferability to other settings. Furthermore, while patient involvement was central to the PAR cycles, the depth of participation varied across stages. Patients played a key role in the reflective spaces, providing the foundation for co‐designing interventions and validating emerging findings. To ensure clinical viability, the research team facilitated the final technical synthesis and strategy selection, keeping them grounded in the participants' collective input. This iterative validation process ensured that findings resonated with the service users' lived experiences, while highlighting a path for future research to further flatten hierarchies in the co‐evaluation of mental health interventions (Corrigan and Oppenheim  ; Wiles et al.  ).
Second, the study experienced participant dropouts during the project, primarily due to changes in service management by the participating institution. These dropouts may have influenced the final composition of the sample and the continuity of the participatory process, potentially limiting the depth and richness of the data collected.
Nevertheless, the study was strengthened by methodological rigour, adherence to COREQ guidelines (Tong et al.  ), and triangulation of data sources and perspectives, enhancing its credibility and dependability.
Future research must move beyond single‐stakeholder accounts and deliberately co‐create knowledge about community‐based mental‐health care with the very people who give and receive it. Longitudinal, participatory studies are needed that simultaneously focus on the voices of service users, caregivers and mental‐health case managers, treating each group as equal epistemic partners.

## Relevance to Clinical Practice
The findings of this study have several implications for mental health nursing practice in community mental health services. Firstly, they highlight the importance of creating nursing‐led structured spaces for professional reflection to enhance self‐awareness and improve the quality of the therapeutic relationship. Such spaces allow mental health nurses to refine their therapeutic use of self—a core nursing competency—and critically assess how their attitudes and relational skills influence therapeutic engagement. This process is essential for delivering highly individualized, person‐centred care.
Second, the co‐development of informational materials about the case management programme empowers nurses to act as facilitators of transparency and trust from the initial point of contact, reinforcing the nursing‐patient therapeutic relationship.
Third, the introduction of mixed reflective groups provides a feasible model for nurses to integrate service users' voices into clinical improvement, aligning with contemporary recovery‐oriented nursing models.
However, for these nursing interventions to become sustainable, organizations must formally support them and ensure that structural conditions—such as adequate time, resources and staff stability—required for relational nursing care to remain the operational core of recovery settings.

8

## Conclusion
This study demonstrates that participatory action research can be a valuable approach for enhancing the therapeutic relationship in community mental health case management. The participatory redesign of informational materials and the establishment of mixed reflective groups increased professionals' awareness of relational care and facilitated a shift toward more person‐centred practices.
However, sustaining these improvements requires ongoing institutional support, organizational flexibility and integration of reflective practices into routine care. Furthermore, future initiatives should deepen patient involvement, moving from consultation toward genuine co‐research partnerships.
Strengthening the therapeutic relationship through participatory and reflective methodologies is a promising approach to enhancing the quality and person‐centredness of community mental health care, ultimately improving patient experiences and outcomes.
