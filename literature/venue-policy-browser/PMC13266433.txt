## ABSTRACT

## Background
Multimorbidity, ≥ 2 chronic conditions as per the World Health Organization, can complicate aging in place and lead to earlier transitions to nursing homes. To inform supportive service intervention planning in naturally occurring retirement communities (NORCs), which are areas where large proportions of older adults reside, we examined clustering patterns of specific chronic conditions among NORC residents.

## Methods
We conducted a cross‐sectional analysis of a population‐based cohort of community‐dwelling NORC residents aged ≥ 66 years as of January 1, 2022, in Ontario, Canada. A provincial NORC registry of high‐rise buildings was linked with individual‐level health administrative records by postal code to identify NORC residents. Multimorbidity (≥ 2 of 17 measured chronic conditions), sociodemographic, clinical, and health service use (HSU) characteristics in NORC residents were assessed. Baseline descriptive statistics of the NORC cohort were compared by multimorbidity status, overall, and by sex, using standardized differences (STD). Latent class analysis (LCA) identified groups (known as classes) with similar patterns of co‐occurring chronic conditions.

## Results
Among 200,565 older NORC residents (63% female, 89% in urban settings), 84% lived with multimorbidity. Multimorbid residents were older (mean 78 vs. 74 years; STD 0.58) and more had low‐income status (19% vs. 9%; STD 0.27) than non‐multimorbid counterparts. LCA resulted in a 6‐class solution. Class 1 (13% of the cohort with multimorbidity) was characterized by conditions including osteoarthritis, osteoporosis, cancer, and mood disorders, class 2 (36%) by hypertension, osteoarthritis, osteoporosis, cancer, and mood disorders, class 3 (23%) by diabetes, class 4 (10%) by cardiovascular conditions, class 5 (8%) by respiratory conditions, and class 6 (10%) contained above average prevalences for 16 conditions and had increased HSU, including unscheduled emergency visits (48%).

## Conclusion
Findings suggest a substantial burden of multimorbidity in NORC residents and reinforce the importance of designing programs in NORCs to help older adults with multimorbidity age in place.

Keywords: health service use, multimorbidity, naturally occurring retirement community, population aging, programs

## Summary

Key Points
○Supportive service programs in naturally occurring retirement communities (NORCs) are an emerging resource to keep older adults aging in the right place. A deeper understanding of multimorbidity patterns in NORC residents could support tailored chronic disease prevention and management programs.
○Among older NORC residents, latent class analysis identified 6 classes of multimorbidity with varying sociodemographic and clinical characteristics and health service use.
○Bolstering place‐based supportive services with resources that prioritize system navigation and integrated care can efficiently support those with multimorbidity who live in the community.

Why Does This Paper Matter?
○Many older adults live in Naturally Occurring Retirement Communities (NORCs), but their health needs are understudied. Findings suggest a substantial burden of multimorbidity in NORC older residents, indicating a necessity to tailor supportive service programming to address needs and help older adults with multimorbidity age in place.

## Introduction
As global life expectancy is increasing and the number of adults aged ≥ 60 years is projected to reach 1.4 billion by 2030 [ ], the importance of fostering healthy environments that support the dynamic nature of aging is critical. By 2040, 25% of Canadians will be aged ≥ 65 years [ ]. A 2024 National Institute on Aging report found 80% of Canadians, aged ≥ 50 years, preferred to remain in their homes and communities for as long as possible [ ]. Despite many adults' desire to age in place, many experience financial, social, and/or health issues that may not make it feasible or safe [ ]. For example, older adults with self‐reported poor/fair health are twice as likely to express uncertainty about their living conditions as those in good health [ ].
Multimorbidity, ≥ 2 co‐occurring chronic conditions as per the World Health Organization [ ], has been established as a barrier to aging in place [ , ]. Increasing multimorbidity, the number and complexity of individual conditions, can result in frailty, disability, and functional decline, impacting a person's ability to remain at home safely [ , ]. In diverse contexts, multimorbidity has been identified as a predictor of earlier nursing home (NH) transitions and hospital readmissions in older adults [ , ]. Across all age groups the prevalence and complexity of multimorbidity are increasing, presenting new challenges to support the aging population [ , ]. Multimorbidity is most common in those with less support to manage their health and live independently, specifically older adults of increasing age, from low‐income backgrounds, and who live alone [ ]. Lastly, multimorbidity patterns and prevalence differ across sexes and are more common in females than males [ ]. Compared to men, women are 4.2, 1.5, and 1.6 times more likely to have diagnosed osteoporosis, dementia, and access services for mood and anxiety disorders, respectively [ ]. On average, women over 65 years live longer and spend more time in poor health than men, which is exacerbated by structural disadvantages (e.g., more Canadian older women live below low‐income cut‐offs).
Naturally occurring retirement communities (NORCs), housing developments or high‐rise buildings that house large proportions of older adults [ ], present a venue of under‐tapped potential for supportive services programs (SSP) to support those with multimorbidity. Despite representing a sizable portion of community‐dwelling older adults [ ], population‐level research is limited. Ontario NORC residents have been characterized as older, more likely to be female, low‐income, and having more chronic conditions, compared to non‐NORC older adults [ ]. Many chronic conditions, such as hypertension, are amenable to upstream interventions that include lifestyle modifications [ ]. NORCs provide an opportunity to deliver SSPs that leverage the co‐location of older adults and address health and social needs, especially multimorbidity management.
To inform planning and evaluation of equity‐promoting multimorbidity interventions in NORCs, this population‐based cohort study examined the prevalence of multimorbidity and clustering of conditions within NORC residents in Ontario, Canada. Latent class analysis (LCA) guided the identification of meaningful subgroups of multimorbidity, as understanding prevalent clusters of chronic conditions can support targeted SSP design. We also examined if sex‐based differences were present in multimorbidity clustering to further tailor programming.

2.

## Methods
2.1.

## Study Design, Data Sources, and Population
Our population‐based cohort study included all community‐dwelling Ontario residents, aged ≥ 66 years, whose postal code as of the index date, January 1, 2022, matched a postal code in the NORC registry (Defined as NORC Residents) [ , ]. Inclusion beginning at age 66 allowed for 1 year of prescribing data from the Ontario Drug Benefit (ODB) database, which adults become eligible for at age 65 years.
A provincial NORC registry of high‐rise buildings, created by the University Health Network's (UHN) OpenLab [ , ], was linked via postal code with individual‐level health administrative records at ICES (Table  ). ICES (formerly the Institute for Clinical and Evaluative Sciences) is an independent, nonprofit research institute whose legal status under Ontario's health information privacy law allows it to collect and analyze healthcare and demographic data, without consent, for health system evaluation and improvement. The use of the data in this project is authorized under section 45 of Ontario's Personal Health Information Protection Act (PHIPA) and does not require review by a Research Ethics Board.
The NORC registry used postal code information from Ontario's Registered Persons Database (RPDB) to identify NORC high‐rise buildings, defined by UHN's OpenLab as buildings where ≥ 30% of residents were older adults aged ≥ 65 years, and at least 50 older adults resided in the building [ , ]. The NORC registry excluded nursing and retirement homes and continuing care facilities but included social housing for older adults. Postal code at index date was used to ascertain NORC residents.
We excluded individuals if they were (1) non‐Ontario residents at index date; (2) < 66 or > 105 years; (3) admitted to a NH, retirement home, or complex continuing care within 6 months prior to index date.

2.2.

## Multimorbidity
We identified the prevalence of 17 chronic conditions (at index date): acute myocardial infarction (AMI), asthma, (any) cancer, cardiac arrhythmia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), congestive heart failure (CHF), chronic coronary syndrome, dementia, diabetes, hypertension, nonpsychotic mood and anxiety disorders, other mental health (e.g., schizophrenia, personality disorders, substance abuse), osteoarthritis, osteoporosis, renal failure, rheumatoid arthritis, and stroke using health administrative datasets (Table  ) [ ]. These datasets were linked using unique encoded identifiers and analyzed at ICES.
While the 17 conditions do not represent all possible chronic conditions which could impact a person over the life course, they were selected for their high prevalence, economic burden, and clinical importance, representing substantial conditions from a population‐level epidemiological perspective [ , , ]. Validated ICES algorithms were used to ascertain AMI, asthma, CHF, COPD, dementia, diabetes, hypertension, and rheumatoid arthritis [ , , , , , ]. For remaining conditions, we adopted a similar approach to the derivation algorithms (i.e., at least one diagnosis recorded in acute care (Discharge Abstract Database (DAD)), or two diagnoses recorded in physician records (Ontario Health Insurance Plan Claims Database (OHIP)) within a two‐year period) to define the conditions. Each chronic condition was defined with all available retrospective health administrative data to ascertain condition status, with the exception of episodic conditions for which a limited lookback period was used to more accurately reflect care received by residents [ ]—AMI (1 year prior to index), and Cancer, Mood Disorders and Other Mental Health (2 years) [ ]. Moreover, this approach is consistent with other population‐based health administrative data research in Ontario [ , , ]. The full set of diagnostic codes, data sources, and definitions are provided in Table  .
Multimorbidity was defined as the co‐occurrence of any ≥ 2 [ ] of these chronic conditions as of index date. The number of chronic conditions was also grouped by levels of 2, 3, 4, or ≥ 5.
Chronic conditions were presented in tables/figures according to Canadian Chronic Disease Surveillance System guidelines [ ].

2.3.

## Cohort Characteristics
NORC residents' individual‐ and neighborhood‐level sociodemographic information, clinical, and health service use (HSU) characteristics were determined. The Registered Persons Database was used to ascertain age and sex. The Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada Permanent Residents Database, current to 2022, was used to classify NORC residents as immigrants, refugees, and others, or nonimmigrants/long‐term residents [ ]. Ontario Drug Benefit claims were used to identify low‐income status, based on eligibility for a drug subsidy [ ]. The Rurality Index of Ontario score was used to identify neighborhood rurality, including urban, small urban or rural [ ]. Building type was classified as apartment, community housing, condo, or seniors housing. Neighborhood‐level information was determined using linkages based on individuals' postal codes from Statistics Canada's Postal CodeOM Conversion File to the Canadian 2021 Census [ ]. This included neighborhood income, as well as area‐level marginalization (ON‐Marg) based on four dimensions: material resources; age and labor force; racialized and newcomer populations; and households and dwellings (Table  ) [ ]. Marginalization was reported by quintiles, with the first and fifth quintile representing the least and most marginalized groups, respectively [ ].
Individual‐level HSU information included open NH applications, number of drugs dispensed, outpatient mental health visits, home care use, unscheduled emergency department (ED) visits, hospitalizations (including those designated alternate levels of care or ambulatory care sensitive conditions), and rostering to primary care (Table  ). All HSU indicators looked back 2 years from index date.

2.4.

## Statistical Analyses
Baseline descriptive statistics of the NORC cohort were compared by multimorbidity status, overall and by sex. Continuous variables were described using mean, standard deviation (SD), median and interquartile range (IQR), and frequencies were presented for categorical variables. Standardized differences (STD) were calculated, with values greater than 0.10 representing meaningful between‐group differences [ ]. The overall prevalence of multimorbidity per 1000 NORC residents was estimated for the complete cohort and disaggregated by sex, age group, income status, immigration status, and rurality.
Clustering of chronic conditions among NORC residents with multimorbidity was assessed using LCA [ ]. LCA is a person‐centered statistical approach that identifies membership in underlying subpopulations within a sample based on a set of measured variables [ ], in this case, 17 chronic conditions. A stepwise and unconstrained approach to model fit was conducted, beginning with a one‐class model and increasing the number of classes until a final model was selected and optimized for parsimony, statistical fit, and clinical interpretability, as per other population‐based latent class analyses on multimorbidity [ ]. Model fit statistics assessed included entropy, G
2 likelihood ratio test, chi‐squared goodness of fit, the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Normalized entropy values > 0.8 indicated accurately defined classes but were not considered in class selection to minimize risk of overfitting [ ]. Lower values in other model fit criteria indicated better fit. BIC was prioritized as it is considered the most reliable LCA fit statistic [ ]. Following the selection of a class solution, where individuals were assigned to classes using maximum posterior probabilities [ ], differences in individual‐ and neighborhood‐level sociodemographic information, and HSU were assessed by class, and STDs were presented relative to a reference class (class 1). Conditions with prevalences above the multimorbid cohort average were characteristic of that class [ ].

2.5.

## Subgroup and Sensitivity Analyses
LCA stratified by sex was conducted to understand potential differential sex‐based clustering patterns. A sensitivity analysis was performed where LCA was conducted which defined multimorbidity with a lifetime lookback for episodic chronic conditions (AMI, cancer, mood disorders, and other mental health).

2.6.

## Nested Observations
To examine the potential of building‐level clustering, we calculated the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) using a random effects model with the number of multi‐morbidities as the outcome. The proportion of variance explained by between‐group (NORC‐buildings) differences was negligible (< 5%), so multilevel analyses were not conducted.
SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) and the R 4.2.0 poLCA package were used for analyses.

3.

## Results
3.1.

## Cohort Description
Of the 200,565 older adults living across 1941 Ontario NORCs (Figure  ), 63% were female, 89% lived in urban settings, and 84% lived with multimorbidity. Chronic condition prevalences can be found in Table  .
 Residents with multimorbidity were, on average, older (mean 78 vs. 74 years; STD 0.58). A larger proportion were nonimmigrants/long‐term residents (86% vs. 80%; STD 0.17) and were more likely to have low‐income (19% vs. 9%; STD 0.27). Furthermore, they were less likely to live in NORC Condo buildings (50% vs. 56%; STD 0.11) and more likely to live in Seniors Housing (19% vs. 15%; STD 0.11) compared to residents without multimorbidity.
Those with multimorbidity had on average 4 conditions (SD 1.67). NORC residents with multimorbidity were dispensed more prescription medications at index date (Polypharmacy (≥ 5 concurrent medications) 43% vs. 5%; STD 0.97) and were more likely to have an open NH application than non‐multimorbid counterparts (3% vs. < 1%; STD 0.19). Multimorbid individuals had greater HSU, including ED visits, hospitalizations, mental health‐related services, home care, and primary care provider visits, compared to those without multimorbidity (Table  ).
After disaggregating by sex, trends remained with some notable differences. First, while low‐income status was higher in multimorbid residents compared to non‐multimorbid residents in both sexes (female: 20% vs. 9%, STD 0.32; male: 16% vs. 10%, STD 0.19), a higher share of female NORC residents experienced low‐income relative to male residents. Second, more females with multimorbidity resided in the lowest income quintile neighborhoods than non‐multimorbid females (51% vs. 46%; STD 0.11), and no difference was observed in male residents. Finally, the prevalence of chronic conditions varied by sex, with an increased prevalence of coronary syndrome and diabetes in males, and increased osteoporosis and mood disorders in females (Table  ).
 
3.2.

## Latent Class Analysis
Entropy remained favorable in ≥ 2 class models, indicating precise class separation, and other fit indicators trended downward with each class addition. BIC was lowest in the 10‐class model (Table  ). A 6‐class solution was selected as it balanced model fit indicators, parsimony, interpretability, and minimized the risk of over‐extraction [ ]. LCA results were based on 16 conditions, as no individual met the criteria for AMI within the 1‐year lookback; prevalence estimates for each condition by class can be found in Figure  , chronic conditions ordered by prevalence within each class in Figure  , and predicted probabilities in Table  .
 While each class was distinct, many of the same conditions were characteristic across classes. Classes were labeled by relative increasing order of multimorbidity. Class 1 (minimally multimorbid), which comprised 13% of the cohort with multimorbidity, was characterized by prevalences above the multimorbid cohort average for conditions including osteoarthritis, osteoporosis, and cancer. Despite being common in those with multimorbidity (≥ 80%), hypertension had a prevalence of 0% in class 1. On the other hand, in class 2 (hypertension/musculoskeletal), the largest class (36%), all members had hypertension co‐occurring with other conditions, including osteoarthritis, osteoporosis, and cancer, among others. All members of Class 3 (diabetes; 23%) had diabetes. This class was also characterized by hypertension. Class 4 (cardiovascular; 10%) was defined by above average prevalences for cardiovascular conditions (CHF, arrhythmia, coronary syndrome, hypertension, stroke), renal failure, and diabetes. Class 5 (respiratory; 8%) was characterized by respiratory conditions, namely asthma and COPD, and mood disorders to a lesser extent. Finally, members of class 6 (very multimorbid; 10%) contained prevalences above the multimorbid cohort average for all 16 conditions.
There were notable differences in sociodemographic, clinical and HSU characteristics across classes (Figure  ). Compared to class 1 (minimally multimorbid, mean age 76 years, 12% low income), all other classes were older (class 2: mean 79, STD 0.41, class 3: mean 77, STD 0.20; class 4: mean 81, STD 0.76; class 5: mean 77, STD 0.23; class 6: mean 81, STD 0.73), and had more low income (class 2: 16%, STD 0.20, class 3: 23%, STD 0.28; class 4: 18%, STD 0.17; class 5: 21%, STD 0.25; class 6: 26%, STD 0.37) (Table  ). Class 4 (cardiovascular) had the lowest proportion of females compared to the reference (46% vs. 75%, STD 0.62). In terms of immigration status, class 3 (diabetes) had a higher proportion of immigrants (16% vs. 9%, STD 0.21) and refugees (3% vs. 2%, STD 0.10) compared to the minimally multimorbid class. In terms of clinical and HSU variables, class 6 (very multimorbid) had a higher average number of chronic conditions (mean 7 vs. 3, STD 3.83), polypharmacy (78% vs. 13%, STD 1.75), and unscheduled ED visits (48% vs. 16%, STD 0.72) compared to the minimally multimorbid class. Other classes also contained significant differences in HSU variables; for example, class 4 (cardiovascular) had less mental health‐related healthcare visits (2% vs. 8%, STD 0.27), and more home care (26% vs. 12%, STD 0.23) than the minimally multimorbid, among other differences (Table  ).
 
3.3.

## 
LCA Stratified by Sex
Subgroup analyses of the LCA by sex revealed 5‐class solutions for both females and males (Figure  ). Classes held constant when stratified by sex, except for two key differences. In females, the 5‐class model deviated from the full model as it lacked the presence of a class that was characterized primarily by above average cardiovascular condition prevalences. On the other hand, in the 5‐class solution for males, a class akin to class 1, characterized by a 0% prevalence of hypertension, was not found.
 
3.4.

## Sensitivity Analysis
Varying the definition of multimorbidity had minimal effect on LCA results (not shown).

4.

## Discussion
Our population‐based analysis of Ontario older adults found a substantial burden of multimorbidity in NORC residents. LCA demonstrated high levels of prevalent condition overlap by class, which remained even when additional classes were added. Given that LCA is a data‐driven approach, this speaks to the burden and complexity of multimorbidity among older adults. This is unlike LCA on patterns of chronic conditions in other populations. In a LCA by Brown et al. (2024) [ ] on multimorbidity in pregnant Ontario women, the class solution demonstrated discrete patterns of multimorbidity. Our 6‐class solution included classes defined by (1) minimal multimorbidity, (2) hypertension/musculoskeletal conditions, (3) diabetes, (4) cardiovascular conditions, (5) respiratory conditions, and (6) above‐average prevalences of all conditions or “very multimorbid.” Members of class 6 had higher HSU including polypharmacy, home care, ED visits, and hospitalizations compared to the multimorbid cohort average. Key differences were observed in sex‐stratified analyses, but further work is needed to characterize multimorbidity among subgroups within this heterogenous cohort.
A 2016 study by Whitson et al. leveraged LCA to identify clustering patterns of chronic illness in American community‐dwelling older adults [ ]. Despite not utilizing all the same conditions, similarities were observed, including the presence of a class where individuals had above average prevalences of all conditions. Like our findings, this class demonstrated higher HSU than other classes. Authors also identified a class with high prevalences of cardiovascular conditions, mostly of male sex, and a class characterized by musculoskeletal conditions, mostly of female sex, both observed in our analysis. Other LCAs on Australian [ ] and Spanish [ ] older adults demonstrated similar class models. An important distinction of our study was the lack of a multimorbidity‐free group, as LCA was restricted to those with multimorbidity. Despite this, Class 1 demonstrated a lower burden of multimorbidity, including fewer chronic conditions and HSU.
4.1.

## Implications
Given extensive multimorbidity among NORC residents, there was considerable overlap in chronic conditions defining each class. While these findings do not point to easily distinguishable clinical groups for targeted services, they underscore the need for SSPs, as the high burden of multimorbidity and co‐location of older adults make NORCs efficient settings for integrated care delivery.
Existing Ontario NORC SSPs have demonstrated success in leveraging NORCs as settings for care delivery [ , ]. Models incorporate staffing resources like Community Coordinators who support NORC residents by acting as a systems navigator (e.g., connecting residents to care, screening, or lifestyle programs) [ ], reducing fragmented care, which is more common in individuals with multimorbidity and contributes to inappropriate prescribing and other downstream harms [ ]. Differences in HSU by class underscores the importance of adapting programs for the local context and the necessity of an integrated approach to care which begins at the community‐level. Applying this approach to NORC residents with a high burden of multimorbidity (class 6, 10% of the NORC population with multimorbidity) could address this important risk group.
Other examples of NORCs as a venue for the management of multimorbidity include the Community Paramedic‐Led Clinic [ ], which provides onsite health assessments and education, and the COPD‐Pathway pilot [ ], which includes outreach, connection to clinical resources, and supports to live independently. Leveraging sex‐based differences in multimorbidity classes could support NORC‐level resource identification (e.g., cardiovascular pathways in male‐dominant NORCs).

4.2.

## Strengths and Limitations
Our study leveraged robust population‐based health administrative data from ICES, which captures almost all Ontario residents and is not subject to recall bias [ ]. Additionally, through the use of validated algorithms at ICES, our study's definition of multimorbidity captured specific conditions with high prevalences, clinical importance and economic burden [ ]. Particularly, our analysis included four conditions: cancer, cardiovascular, diabetes and chronic respiratory, which contribute to over 60% of all deaths in Canada and are of great research and clinical importance [ ].
Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while the definition of NORCs (≥ 30% of the building are aged ≥ 65 years; ≥ 50 older residents) used in this study may have limited our ability to capture other relevant contexts, a resident count of at least 50 older adults within a building ensures a critical mass of older adults for feasibility, sustainability and efficient resource and program delivery. Our threshold of ≥ 30% of older adults in a defined geographic area is consistent with the literature [ , ]. Second, since health administrative data are not designed for research purposes, they do not fully capture important variables to understand multimorbidity risk (e.g., functional status, social isolation, education or social/community contexts). However, we used proxy measures like neighborhood income quintile to better understand the area‐level context [ ].
Third, the LCA methodology presented limitations in this study as it required subject matter expertise to discern a clinically interpretable class solution and presents the risk of reification fallacy, defined as misapplying concrete meaning to abstract concepts [ ]. Despite this, LCA was chosen over other methods of evaluating multimorbidity, like the Charlson Comorbidity Scale or Frailty Index, as it provided latent yet meaningful clusters of co‐occurring conditions at the population‐level, which can be useful for service planning and implementation. Moreover, our class solution aligned with existing literature in other community‐dwelling older adults and provided a broad view of multimorbidity in NORC residents.

5.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, we found high levels of multimorbidity in NORC residents across classes identified by LCA, emphasizing the need for programs that can prevent, delay the onset of, and support the management of co‐occurring chronic conditions in NORCs. Our findings highlight the importance of a systems and place‐based approach to address multimorbidity, spanning health and community care to keep older adults aging in the right place.
