## Abstract

## Background
Positive Play describes non-problematic gambling behaviour. The term avoids the ambiguity of the term ‘Responsible Gambling’, and the negative connotations associated with ‘Problem Gambling’. This purpose of the paper was to identify demographic groups who score consistently low on the positive play in order to determine where interventions are most needed.

## Methods
The study is a secondary analysis of general population data, collected online by AskingCanadians on behalf of Ontario Lottery and Gaming that examined data on positive play and harm reduction related to gambling. The paper included the analysis of survey data from players who reported engaging in one of the four target gambling activities (N = 3701): Lotteries (n = 1832), casinos (n = 1272), online (n = 300), and charity bingo (n = 297).

## Results
People who scored low on all four of the Positive Play scales were more likely to be young, male, single, have a low or middle range income, have at least some university education, and to have been born outside of Canada. In addition, people who scored low on Positive Play scale were more likely to play online games or charity games compared to lotteries. These findings were mostly consistent across game types however due to the small sample sizes for online gamblers and charitable gamblers, some effects did not reach significance.

## Discussion
Awareness efforts for Positive Play should be directed at younger players, males, and recent immigrants. In addition, more effort in encouraging Positive Play needs to be directed at online gamblers and bingo gamblers.

## Introduction
Recreational gamblers typically pay little attention to and report minimal impact from admonitions against problem gambling and exhortations to engage in responsible gambling, despite their gambling status (Jackson et al., ; Lole et al., ). As an alternative, it has been suggested that we shift from a focus on what the player is doing wrong, to encouraging healthy play behaviour or ‘Positive Play’ (Wood & Griffiths, ). The term ‘Positive Play’ describes non-problematic gambling behaviour (Wood & Griffiths, ). The term avoids the ambiguity of the term ‘Responsible Gambling’ and the negative connotations associated with ‘Problem Gambling’ (Gainsbury et al., ; Turner et al., ; Wood et al., ). The purpose of the research was to examine the correlates of Positive Play scale (Wood et al., ) and its subscales, to identify the risk and protective factors, and make recommendations for promoting Positive Play.
Positive play is thought of as engaging in gambling as a recreational activity only (Armstrong et al., ; Dixon et al., ; Wood & Griffiths, , ). It includes emotional regulation, setting limits on gambling behaviours (time and money spent), using harm minimization strategies, having fewer erroneous gambling cognitions, and taking responsibility for one’s gambling (Armstrong et al., ; Connolly et al., ; Delfabbro et al., ; Dixon et al., ; Wood & Griffiths, , ). Tabri et al. ( ) have argued that the focus of prevention intervention should be to encourage healthy positive play among all gamblers rather than focus on what some people with gambling problem players are doing wrong. As such it would provide the players with a guide to healthy behavior, rather simply being a criticism of unhealthy gambling.
Wood et al. ( ) developed a 14-item Positive Play scale (PPS) for which responses use Likert scales. Greater scores on each subscale indicate greater Positive Play. Two items are reverse-scored. In addition, the scale can be broken down into four subscales: two of which are behavioural factors (Honesty and Control, and Pre-Commitment), and two measure belief factors (Personal Responsibility and Gambling Literacy). Wood et al. ( ) developed a large group of potential items, reviewed and tested them with players and experts, and then used factor analysis to reduce the scale to the present 14-item version. All of the Positive Play subscales have been shown to have negative relationships with problem gambling behaviors and harms from gambling, (Delfabbro et al., ; Gray et al., ; Tabri et al., ; Wood et al., ). However, some studies have found that the Positive Play Personal Responsibility subscale was positively correlated with problem gambling severity and harm, and negatively associated with the other Positive Play subscales (Delfabbro et al., ; Gray et al., ). This discrepancy was interpreted by Delfabbro et al. ( ) – a result of discrepancies between what problem gamblers want to do and their behaviour. These mixed results suggest that the relationship between responsibility and harm is complex. Christensen et al., (Christensen et al., ), have argued that traditional responsible gambling policies focusing on recognising the signs of problem gambling are missing people who may be at risk but who are not yet experiencing problems. Of key importance, Christensen et al. ( ) have also shown that setting limits on both frequency of gambling and amount of spend per session are necessary components of harm reduction. A focus on positive play aims to deal with risky thoughts, beliefs, attitudes, and behaviours that could lead to problems; and may be able to reach people at an earlier stage prior to developing problematic gambling habits.
There has been limited research examining Positive Play and how these behaviours might translate to responsible gambling practices. The present study was a secondary analysis of data on responsible gambling and positive play among people who play lotteries, casinos, online gambling, and bingo (GREO, ). The goal of the study was to identify demographic groups who are less likely to use positive play in order to guide future educational and awareness campaigns.

## Methods

## Participants
The sample included a total of 3,701 participants who reported engaging in one of the 4 target gambling activities: Lotteries (n = 1832), casinos (n = 1272), online (n = 300), and bingo (n = 297). Only people who had engaged in one of the four gambling activities in the past year were included in the sample. The sample included roughly equal numbers of males (48.7%) and females (51.1%) as well as 0.2% who reported other or preferred not to say. The sample was distributed across all age groups but were weighted more heavily towards the older participants; 50% of the sample were aged 55 or older compared to the Canadian population 42.3% (Statistics Canada, ). In addition, 77.5% of the sample were born in Canada, and 69.4% reported living in an urban area of greater than 100,000 people, compared to 74.4% born in Canada according to Statistics Canada (Statistics Canada, ).
Education was skewed towards higher qualifications; compared to the general population of Canada with 10.5% reporting high school or less, 30.0% reporting college or technical school, and 50.9% reporting graduating university. According to statistic Canada (Statistics Canada, ), 36.2% of workers in Canada have an undergraduate degree or higher. In terms of income 19.2% of respondents reported a family income of under $50,000, 39.3% a family income of between $50,000 and under $100, 000, and 20% reporting a household income of $150,000 or over. The household income of the participants was a somewhat lower than the Canadian population as a whole. According to statistics Canada 26% of people have a household income $150,000 and over (Statistics Canada, ). The differences between the sample and the general population may reflect the nature of people who gamble in Canada.

## Procedure
This paper is based on a secondary analysis of data collected by AskingCanadians between August 2019 to May 2020 as part of an ongoing nationally representative online panel survey, collected on behalf of Ontario Lottery and Gaming (OLG) (see GREO, ). In Ontario, Canada, legal gambling is managed and controlled by OLG, which licences several corporations to run casinos, bingo halls, lotteries, and online gambling. The proposals were peer reviewed by the GREO to ensure scientific integrity. The secondary analysis was reviewed and approved by the research ethics board of < name removed > as protocol 045/2922.
Survey invitations are pulled from the full panel of 1 million Canadians, in such as way to be representative of the population of Ontario with respect to age and gender. AskingCanadians runs a research panel with access to a research community of 600,000 Canadians who have opted-in to participate in online surveys. Anyone can join the research panel by signing up at the AskingCanadians website. Research participants are recruited to the panel by various methods including social email, and email invitations for specific studies. For the current study, email invitations to participate in the survey were sent to a randomly selected panel members who lived in Ontario Canada. Participants were compensated for their time with credits through the company’s own internal credit system that can be traded for their choice of gift cards or reward points (GREO, ).

## Questionnaire
The survey began with an informed consent form that included assurances of privacy. The participant then completed 14 questions to determine eligibility including age, gender, and questions about their gambling participation. The gambling questions were used to group people into one of four game groups: lottery, casino, online gambling or bingo.
The algorithm for group was conducted to ensure adequate samples for each group of gamblers. Many of the participants reported engaging in multiple gambling activities; and nearly all participants reported buying lottery tickets. Participants were asked questions about positive play and responsible gambling related to only one specific game type. Participants were assigned to groups based on their game participation roughly in proportion in the full sample, but streamed to ensure adequate samples of people in the online and bingo groups. A detailed display of the game participation of the sample is given in the supplementary Table . As shown in Table most participants played lotteries (88%) or casino games (51%), and smaller percentages played online (14%) and bingo (9%).
Questions were asked around the PPS, responsible gambling behaviors, awareness of responsible gambling tools, and attitudes towards the gambling venue. The survey ended with 7 additional demographics questions: income, education, born in Canada, length of time in Canada, ethnic group, size of community, and marital status. This analysis of this paper will include focus on the PPS and its relationship to these 9 demographics questions.
The PPS was the only validated scale used on the survey. According to Tabri et al. ( ), the PPS best fits a four-factor model. Of the four subscales, personal responsibility, α = 0.86, honesty and control, α = 0.87, and pre-commitment, α = 0.87, had very good internal reliability, but gambling literacy, α = 0.68, had a somewhat marginal level of internal reliability. The responses for each item are recorded on a 7-point Likert-scale. For Honesty and Control and Pre-commitment, the questions were scored from never (1) to always (7) and for Personal Responsibility and Gambling literacy, the items were scored from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7). The items for each scale were then averaged for a total score between 1 and 7 for each subscale. Two items on the gambling literacy scale are reverse keyed. Subscale scores were computed for each of the 4 subscales by averaging the Likert scale scores for the items so that the range for each subscale was from 1 to 7.

## Data Analysis
For the variables included in the logistic regression analysis, there were no missing values for positive play, age, marital status, or game group. The largest number of missing values was for income group, and the missing values were handled by entering them into a separate category (prefer not to say). The remaining variables had a small number of missing values (born in Canada 0.7%; city over 100 K 1.3%; education 1.5%; gender 0.2%; and ethnicity 1.8%) for an overall total of 3.1%. The full sample size was 3820, but after deletion of missing values, the sample size was 3701.
The main goal of this project was to identify demographic and player characteristics related to low levels of Positive play (GREO, ). To define the demographic profile for people who score low on positive play, we first computed a subscale score for each of the 4 subscales of the PPS (Tabri et al., ) by averaging the Likert scale scores for the items yielding four subscale scores between 1 and 7 (see Table  ). We then defined a low-score profile using a somewhat arbitrary cut-off of people who scored less than 6.0 out of 7.0 on all 4 subscales. Although this cut-off is arbitrary, it means that people included in the low-scores profile, scored 1 2, 3 or 4 (strongly disagree to neutral) on most of the items on the scale. The average scores on positive play for low-score profiles was 4.58 (SD = 0.78), and the average for the rest of the sample was 6.55 (SD = 0.54). This difference is highly significant, t(3700) = 63.1, p < 0.001, d = 3.5 indicating that this cut off strongly separates people who endorse positive play from those who do not. Using this cut-off score, a total of 9.6% (N = 3,701) of the sample were identified who scored consistently low on all 4 subscales. Previous studies have found that people who endorse one or more on measures of problem gamblers such as the Problem Gambling Severity Index (Ferris & Wynne, ) make up between 6% and 12% of the people who gamble (Brodbeck et al., ; Ferris & Wynne, ; Williams et al., ). Thus, this grouping of low-score profiles is consistent with previous research in terms of the prevalence of one or more symptoms of problem gambling, however a low profile is not synonymous with a gambling problem.
 Note that because this definition of low-scores was somewhat arbitrary, we also computed an alternative definition of low-profile scores using the overall average of the PPS and is presented in the supplementary files and Table . The results of these two methods were very similar.
For the analysis we first examined each individual demographic variable to determine the relationship of each to low-score profiles. The questionnaire included age category, gender (male, female, other), marital status, income range, highest education attainment, if they were born in Canada; and if an immigrant, how long had they been in Canada. In addition, the survey included 17 response options for ethnic group, including an open-ended question for other. People were permitted to endorse multiple items. Due to very small samples for a number of these groups, we consolidated the data from these 17 response options into 7 broad categories: (1) Canadian, (2) Black/Caribbean, (3) European, (4) East Asian/Southeast Asian, (5) South Asian / Middle Eastern, (6) First Nations and Inuit/ Metis, and (7) Other /mixed / prefer not to say. For each of these ethnic variables, responses were scored as 1 for anyone who endorsed that ethnic group, and 0 for all other participants. For the final analysis, we set up a multivariate logistic regression analysis to test each of these variables as predictors of low-scores. Each variable was tested using odds ratios. Based on the preliminary univariate analyses and to reduce the number of small cells sizes, education was collapsed into three groups (high-school or less, college, university), marital status was reduced to single vs. others (married, widowed, or divorced), and born in Canada and length of time in Canada since immigration were combined into a three-category variable (born in Canada, immigrated 10 or more years ago, immigrated less than 10 years ago). In addition, for the ethnic group, First Nations, Inuit and Metis were included in the other group because the group was not significant, and the sample size was very small (n = 18) (see Table for more information).

## Results
The averages and standard deviations for the positive play subscales and total score are given in Table  . Personal Responsibility had the highest average score M = 6.66 (SD = 0.79) out of a possible 7, and Gambling literacy had the lowest average score M = 6.2 (SD = 1.18) out of a possible 7. The majority of the sample endorsed most of the items on the positive play scale and consequently all four scales have a significant negative skew ranging from − 1.3 for gambling literacy to -3.1 for personal responsibility. Nearly 80% of the sample (79.9%; N = 3,701) scored an average of 6 or more on the PPS items, and 29% of the sample scored perfect on all of the PPS. Of particular interest for the current study was defining which participants consistently scored low on these items. This cut-off identified 9.6% of the respondents as having low-scores. People classified as having low-scores had an average scores total score of 4.60 (SD = 0.79) compared to the rest of the sample that had an average total score of 6.56 (SD = 0.53). The average score for people who scored consistently low on the positive play subscales was 2.4 standard deviations below the average for the rest of the sample. Low score profiles were most common among online gamblers (19.6%; n = 300), and charitable gamblers (18.3%; n = 297), followed by casino players (12.6%; n = 1272); and finally, lottery players (4.2%; n = 1832) were the least likely to have low-score profiles, χ2 (3) = 136.8, p < 0.0.001.

## Demographics of Low-Scores
Univariate analyses were conducted for gender, age, education, income, type of gambling, marital status, length of time in Canada (born in Canada, 10 or more years, less than 10 years), size of community, and ethnic group. Significant effects were found for each of these variables with low-score profiles associated positively with gambling online or in charity bingo, with being male, being younger, having been born outside of Canada, being single, being from a community larger than 100 thousand people, and having an income less than 150,000. In addition, people with a college level education and people who endorsed being Canadian or having a European background, were less likely to have low-score profiles. A full description of these analyses is provided in the supplementary files.

## Logistic Regression
We entered all of these variables simultaneously into a multivariate logistic regression as predictors of low-scores. Each variable was entered into the analysis as categorical. The reference category for each variable is indicated in Table  . For ethnic group, Canadian was excluded to avoid multicollinearity. In the multivariate analysis, 6 of these variables had at least one significant odds ratio (OR). Compared to lottery players, Casino had an OR = 3.01, p < 0.001; Online gambling had an OR = 3.70, p < 0.001; and bingo had an OR = 4.72, p < 0.001: all significantly greater odds of scoring in the low range. The rest of the odds ratios for the overall analyses are listed in Table  . The Nagelkerke R-squared was 0.186.
 Most of the effects identified before in the univariate analysis were significant in the combined multivariate model. Compared to the highest income bracket, all income groups had significantly greater odds of low-scores except for those between $20,000 and $35,000 who had an odds ratio of 1.89, which was not significant (p = 0.07). Compared to people 65 and over, all younger age groups had significantly greater odds of low-scores, except for those aged 55–64. Interestingly compared to university educated participants, college educated participants had lesser odds of low-scores, OR = 0.68, p < 0.05.
For ethnic group, two groups had significant effects: people who endorsed European ethnicity had lesser odds of scoring in the low range, OR = 0.66, p < 0.05, and people who endorsed East Asian/Southeast Asian ethnicity had greater odds of scoring in the low range of positive play OR = 1.85, p < 0.01. It is noteworthy that the effect of being a recent immigrant did not drop to non-significance when ethnic group was entered into the analysis indicating that ethnic group and immigrant status has separate contributions to the model.
Two variables identified in the univariate analysis did not reach significance in the multivariate analysis. Marital status (being single) and being from a city of 100,000 or more people were not significant in the multivariate model. Additional analyses found that marital status dropped to non-significant after age or education were entered into the model. Similarly, the effect for a city of 100,000 or more became non-significant after immigrant status was entered into the analysis.

## Analyses for Specific Game Groups
We also conducted logistic regressions for each gambling activity. For lotteries, males were more likely to have low-scores, OR = 2.49, people with the lowest income were more likely to have low-scores, OR = 4.22, p < 0.05, and compared to people born in Canada, recent immigrants OR = 4.95, p < 0.001, were more likely to have low-scores. Finally, people from East Asian / Southeast Asian were more likely to have low-score profiles, OR = 2.88, p < 0.01. The Nagelkerke R-squared was 0.163 indicating that for lotteries, the model accounts for 16.3% of the variance of low-scores.
Among Casino players, for age, most groups were significantly more likely to score low on positive play compared to those over 65: for people aged 18–24 the OR was 3.91, p < 0.05, for those aged 25–34 the OR was 3.54, p < 0.001. for people aged 35 to 44 the OR was 2.40, p < 0.01, and for those of 45 to 54 the OR was 1.91, p < 0.01. In addition, people with family incomes from $35,000 to under $50,000; $50,000 to under $75,000; $75,000 to under $100,000;and $100,000 to under $150,000 all had greater odds of low-scores on positive play than people with family income above $150,000 (OR = 2.53, OR = 2.82, OR = 3.05 and OR = 2.35 respectively). As with lotteries compared to those born in Canada, people who were recent immigrants had an odds ratio of 3.84, and those who immigrated more than 10 years ago had an odds ratio of 3.17. The Nagelkerke R-squared was 0.170 indicating that for casinos gambling, the model accounts for 17.0% of the variance of low-scores.
For online gambling, people with a college education were less likely to have low scores than university educated people, OR = 0.37, p < 0.05. For income, the effects were similar to casino games however the odds ratio was significant for people with incomes from $50,000 to under $75,000, OR = 6.20, p < 0.05, $75,000 to under $100,000, OR = 4.68, p < 0.05, and $100,000 to under $150,000, OR = 5.03, p < 0.05 compared to people with family income of $150,000 and above (OR = 7.13, OR = 6.3 and OR = 4.7 and OR = 5.0 respectively). Interestingly, people who preferred not to say their income also were more likely to score low on positive play, OR = 7.13. The Nagelkerke R-squared was 0.184 indicating that for online gambling, the model accounts for 18.4% of the variance of low-scores.
Finally for bingo charitable gambling, males had greater odds of low-scores, OR = 2.99, p < 0.01, For age, the age group of participants aged18-24 had an OR of 21.18, and for those aged 25–34 had an OR of 11.54, p < 0.001. The only income effect was for people earning between 75,000 and 100,000, OR = 5.28, p < 0.05. People from smaller cities were less likely to score low on positive play, OR = 0.33, p < 0.05, and people who had immigrated 10 or more years ago were more likely to score low on positive play than those born in Canada, OR = 5.87, p < 0.05. The Nagelkerke R-squared was 0.336 indicating that for bingo charitable gambling, the model accounts for 34.8% of the variance of low-scores.

## Discussion
In the current paper, we have identified a number of variables that identify subpopulations that need focused educational and awareness campaigns. Low-score profiles were most common among online gamblers, and charitable gamblers, followed by casino players. Lottery players were the least likely to have low-score profiles on positive play. The small number of low-score profiles among lottery players is consistent with the research literature which has shown that lotteries are generally the least problematic form of gambling (Binde, ; Binde et al., ; Williams et al., ). Even among online gamblers, those who buy lottery tickets had a lower rate of low-score profiles compared to those who play other online games. In addition, the findings regarding online gambling are consistent with other studies that suggest that online gambling is more problematic than other forms of gambling (Binde, ; Binde et al., ; Chóliz et al., ) and have lower levels of positive play compared to other gamblers (Connolly et al., ).
One unexpected finding was the greater number of low-score profile for people who play bingo. Bingo is typically viewed as a soft form of gambling (Moubarac et al., ). However, research has shown it can become problematic (Moubarac et al., ; Boughton & Brewster, ; Mazar et al., ). The high rate of low-profile scores among bingo players may be due to the introduction of electronic forms of gambling at bingo halls that include tablets to play electronic bingo, side games on the tablets, and other electronic gambling machines into bingo halls (Blackwell, ; Harrigan et al., ; Mazar et al., ).
In addition to game type, several demographic variables were significantly associated with low-scores on the PPS: gender, age, immigrant status, education, income, and ethnicity. Males were more likely to have low-scores on Positive Play than females. Low-score profiles decreased with age from 26.7% for people aged 18–24 to 5.5% for people over 65. The observed effects of age and gender are consistent with general population studies of gambling problems (Williams et al., ).
People who had immigrated recently were more likely to have low-score profiles than people born in Canada with the highest rate for people who had immigrated within the past 10 years. In addition to the effect of immigrant status, people from East and Southeast Asia were more likely to have a low-profile score on the PPS while people with a European background were less likely to have a low-profile score on the PPS. Other studies have reported greater rates of problem gambling in Immigrant population (Canale et al., ), but this is not always the case(Wilson et al., ) and greater rates of problems among East Asian populations (King et al., ; Petry et al., ; Sobrun-Maharaj et al., ; Williams et al., ). Some of these variables overlap and in particular people who endorsed East and Southeast Asian and South Asian were more likely to be recent immigrants. However, when the ethnic variables were entered into the model, the effect of immigrant status did not drop to zero suggesting that the effects of ethnic group and the effect of being a recent immigrants are independently related to low-scores on positive play. It is not clear why recent immigrants are less likely to practice positive play, however there is clearly a need to focus positive play information on this population.
For income, compared to the reference category of those with an income above 150,000 all other income groups were more likely to have low-scores except for people in the “prefer not to say” category and people in the $20,000 to under $35,000 income bracket. It is possible that people in the $20,000 to under $35,000 income bracket are more careful with their money than those in income brackets from 35,000 to 150,000, however, it seems more likely that the failure to reach significance for those in the 20,000 to 35,000 group was just a random fluke. The random fluke explanation is backed by supplementary analysis (see supplementary text) where we used a somewhat different definition of a low profile based on the overall score, and people in the 20 to 35 thousand income bracket were also significantly different from the reference group. Though note, that the percentage of low scores in this group was still smaller than most other groups in the sample (8.5%). These results regarding income for low-score profiles are in general consistent with research by Williams et al. ( ). However more research is needed to examine the relationship between income and positive play.
People with a college level education were less likely to have low-scores on Positive Play compared to people with a university level education. This effect is somewhat surprising given that we would have thought that education would be a protective factor. We speculated that the university effect could be due to the greater number of people with a university education among younger participants (age < 44; 70.1%) compared to the older group (age > 44, 54.9%), or the greater involvement in online gambling among younger adults (Emond et al., ; Hollén et al., ; Hubert & Griffiths, ; Montiel et al., ; Powell et al., ; Tran et al., ; Turner et al., ). Nonetheless, this effect of education did not drop to non-significance after age and type of game were entered into the analysis. This finding is quite different from studies of problem gambling which have shown that education is associated with lower rates of problems (Bastiani et al., ; Grönroos et al., ; Williams et al., ). However other studies have shown that university students are susceptible to the gambler’s fallacy (Marmurek et al., ). More research is needed on the link between education and positive play.
Separate examinations of each sub-group of gamblers, found that in general, the results were consistent across the gambling groups. For example, older adults consistently were less likely to have low-scores on positive play for all games except online games (see Louderback et al., for similar results). Males were more likely to have low-scores for all games compared to females. Single people tended to be more likely to have low-scores, but the effect of marital status was not significant for any of the analyses. Families with an income between 35,000 and 150,000 were more likely to have low-scores for casino and online games, but this effect was not found for lotteries or bingo. For all of the groups, people with a college level education were less likely to have low-scores on the positive play score than people with a university level education, but this only reached significance with online gambling. For all gambling sub-groups, immigrants and in particular recent immigrants were more likely to score low on positive play. For most casino, lottery and bingo, people from smaller communities were less likely to have low-scores on positive play, however, this effect was only significant for the bingo analysis.
Tabri et al. ( ) have argued that the focus of prevention intervention should be to encourage healthy positive play rather than focus on what some problem players are doing wrong (Tabri et al., ). One benefit of this approach is that it may reach a larger audience. Most gamblers do not engage in problem play such as chasing loses, but also may not engage in healthy play such as setting limits or viewing gambling as just entertainment. But people who do not have problems may not feel that responsible gambling messages are directed towards them. It is argued that compared to a focus on problem play, a focus on Positive Play will reach a larger audience who may be more receptive to the message than if approached with a criticism of their current behavior. A qualitative study by Gainsbury et al. ( ) reported that different groups of gamblers had different preferences for the type of message. For example, older gamblers preferred messages about limit setting, and younger adults preferred message related to their own play. Suggesting that different messages are needed for different groups. In addition, consistent with the view of Tabri et al. ( ), in Gainsbury et al.’s ( ) study, all groups agreed that using positive non-judgmental language in messaging is important.
A study by Christensen et al. ( ) also found that the majority of Canadian casino patrons are aware of most of the measures for responsible gambling but notes that there is a substantial gap in the literature that has failed to examine how gambler characteristics, and gambling play affect responsible gambling behavior. A study by Hing et al. ( ) demonstrated that self-regulatory strategies can result in reduced Electronic Gambling Machine (EGM) spend and reduced EGM-related harm. The current study identified some demographics of the players and type of game participation that suggest groups to target for positive play messaging. More research is needed to determine what harm reduction strategies work best (e.g., Hing et al., ) and the extent to which different harm reduction strategies are better suited to different groups of people (Gainsbury et al., ). Furthermore, Louderback et al. ( ) reported that the visibility of a Responsible Gambling (RG) program may influence program awareness and engagement in RG.

## Limitations
This study has a number of limitations. The survey data is self-reported which means the scores may not actually translate into positive play attitudes. Invitations to participate in the study were sent out to a random sample of the members of the panel, but membership in the online panel itself if not random: the panel is made up of consumers interested in participating in surveys to money in the form of gift cards or reward points. According to AskingCanadians, the panel is representative of the Canadian population as a whole but only in terms of gender and age; it may not be representative in terms of other variables such as income. As noted above, the sample is somewhat older than the Canadian population as a whole (Statistics Canada, ) and has somewhat fewer people with a household income over $150,000 (Statistics Canada. (2021b). Another limitation is that the survey lacked a good measure of problem gambling to facilitate correlating self-reported PPS scores to previously published demographic propensity for gambling disorders. The effects of demographics for specific groups were largely similar across groups, but the sample sizes for online gamblers and charitable bingo gamblers were rather small. More research is needed specifically on the demographic risks for online gambling which is an emerging issue due to recent changes in legislation in Canada (see Turner et al., ).

## Conclusions
Overall, the results suggest a good uptake of positive play attitudes for the overall surveyed population of people who participate in gambling, with only a small percent of the sample consistently having low-scores on the PPS. The profile of a person who scores low on all four of the Positive Play scales was more likely to be young, male, earning a low or middle range income, of at least some university educational attainment, not born in Canada; and in particular, a recent immigrant. Awareness efforts for Positive Play should be directed towards these groups of people. In addition, more effort on Positive Play needs to be directed at people who participate in online games and charity bingo. These findings were largely consistent across game types, however due to the small sample sizes for online gamblers and charitable gamblers, some effects did not reach significance. Research is needed to determine if encouraging positive play results in any changes in attitudes or behavior, and if encouraging positive play longitudinally predicts lower levels of problematic gambling.
