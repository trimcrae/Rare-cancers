Diabetes Action Canada (DAC) has hosted six in-person workshops over eight years, bringing together over 100 participants from research, healthcare delivery, government, non-profit organizations, and communities with lived experience of diabetes. This paper outlines key lessons and best practices from Diabetes Action Canada’s collaborative approach to workshop co-design with Patient Partners. For the 2024 workshop, a planning committee, largely composed of Patient Partners, played a central role in shaping the agenda. Their contributions ensured active patient participation, addressed power imbalances, fostered inclusivity, and created supportive spaces. Strategies such as co-designed agendas, symbolic markers for patient-led presentations, and facilitated networking sessions effectively enhanced engagement. Evaluations highlighted the importance of equitable participation and multidisciplinary collaboration, emphasizing the scalability of DAC’s co-design principles for diverse research and healthcare contexts. These insights provide a foundation for inclusive, impactful, and patient-centered event planning.

Conferences and meetings are more meaningful when they include the voices of people who are directly affected by the topics being discussed. Diabetes Action Canada (DAC) has been organizing workshops for eight years to bring together researchers, healthcare workers, policymakers, and people living with diabetes. These workshops are designed to ensure everyone, especially those with lived experience, can share their ideas and shape the discussion.
For the 2024 workshop, a planning committee made up largely of Patient Partners (people living with diabetes) helped co-design the event. This included creating activities that were welcoming, easy to participate in, and fair to everyone. Special efforts were made to make the event accessible, like sharing agendas early, highlighting patient-led sessions, and creating encouraging ways for people to connect with one another, regardless of their background or role.
Feedback from the event suggested that these efforts worked. Participants said they felt included and supported, and many new ideas were shared. These lessons can be used to make future events, research, and healthcare planning more inclusive and effective.

## Background
Patient partnership in research has become increasingly prominent in Canada’s research and healthcare landscape. In 2011, the Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR) established the Strategy for Patient-Oriented Research (SPOR) Program, consisting of support units in each province, national networks, and other funded entities with the shared aim of integrating patient perspectives and priorities into all aspects of research to improve healthcare delivery and experiences [ ]. Together, these groups provide a framework that research and healthcare innovators can access to engage with Patient Partners (persons with real-world experience who co-create better health solutions), co-design patient-oriented research (POR), build capacity in patient-engaged research, and ultimately improve outcomes by creating a health system that is more accessible, equitable, and person-centered [ ].
Diabetes Action Canada (DAC), a patient-oriented research network dedicated to improving the health and wellness of people in Canada who live with diabetes or are at risk for diabetes, has developed a process for planning and delivering its in-person events with Patient Partners. First funded in 2016 by the CIHR SPOR program, with matched funds from non-profit organizations, private industry, and philanthropy, DAC has a mission to understand healthcare delivery related to diabetes across the country, identify care gaps, and provide evidence-based strategies to address those gaps [ ]. This mission is achieved through enabling collaboration among Patient Partners, researchers, clinicians, and other health care practitioners, ensuring that diverse expertise and lived experience inform the research process. This multidisciplinary collaboration creates shared understanding of the challenges within the healthcare system and creates opportunities to co-design solutions that improve the experience for all who interact with this system [ ].
To do this work effectively, it is essential to engage with those navigating the health system who encounter systemic barriers, particularly individuals and communities who have been historically overlooked or excluded and who are at higher risk of developing life-altering diabetes-related complications [ ]. Meaningful engagement with these groups carries additional responsibilities, beginning with the need to establish trust and build supportive relationships. Feelings of support are fostered through deliberate efforts to create safe, respectful spaces where individuals feel comfortable sharing their experiences. Healthcare environments often have inherent power imbalances between those seeking care and those providing care [ ]. These imbalances can be unintentionally reinforced, for example, by reminding audiences of speakers’ advanced degrees and formal positions in healthcare delivery and research [ ]. Conversely, power imbalances can be reduced by fostering environments where all perspectives have equal value and where all have open opportunities to learn from each other [ , ]. Adopting this approach not only promotes multidisciplinary collaboration but also ensures that the lived experience of those facing challenges in accessing the healthcare system is central to the development of inclusive and equitable solutions.
Approaches to POR are often subjective. As Patient Partners become increasingly integrated into organizational and governance structures, policies and procedures must evolve to respond to the needs of this important group in the research ecosystem. Merely inviting Patient Partners to attend an in-person event is insufficient; achieving genuine engagement requires deliberate efforts to integrate their perspectives into the broader goals and discussions of the event [ ]. Beginning with a blog post in 2010, the Patients Included™ conference charter provided valuable guidance for including Patient Partners, emphasizing the importance of recognizing them as experts in their own experiences while ensuring they are neither excluded nor exploited [ ]. This approach was applied in several conferences, such as the 17th Congress of the International Society for Peritoneal Dialysis (ISPD) [ ], the 10th International Shared Decision Making (IDSM) Conference [ ], the European Respiratory Society International Congress [ ] and the Copenhagen Diabetes Consensus (CODIAC) 2021 [ ]. These events highlighted the importance of careful planning and tailored support to ensure Patient Partners are comfortable, able to fully participate, and that their contributions are both valued and integrated into the discussions [ – ].
Despite growing evidence that Patient Partner involvement is essential for aligning research priorities among patients, researchers, and health professionals, many organizations continue to face challenges in how best to engage Patient Partners effectively [ ]. This paper presents the lessons learned by DAC over eight years of hosting large academic events that bring together diverse participants, including Patient Partners, researchers, clinicians, policymakers, trainees, and representatives from non-profit organizations. Participation in DAC workshops has always been by invitation, leading to a relatively consistent group of attendees over time. Many original DAC researchers and clinicians continue to participate, alongside a growing number of early- and mid-career professionals interested in POR. Similarly, the Patient Partners who attend include both longstanding contributors who helped establish the Network and newer members whose involvement continues to support DAC’s growth, inclusivity, and mission.
The lessons learned are organized across three key phases: before, during, and after the in-person event, using the most recent DAC in-person Workshop– “Patient Partners as Agents of Change” (May 30th-31st, 2024)– as a case study. By reflecting on these lessons and the iterative improvements made each year, this paper also aims to provide insights into best practices for fostering meaningful patient engagement and multidisciplinary collaboration in the planning and execution of such events.
The approach described in this paper was informed by two key frameworks: Carman et al.’s (2013) Framework for Patient and Family Engagement and the International Association for Public Participation (IAP2) Spectrum of Public Participation [ , ]. Both frameworks conceptualize patient engagement as a continuum that progresses from consultation to involvement and ultimately to partnership or empowerment. These frameworks provide a theoretical lens through which we examine DAC’s evolving approach to patient engagement in planning large in-person academic events. Over eight years, DAC has iteratively refined its methods, shifting from initial consultation strategies toward empowering Patient Partners to assume active leadership roles in event design and delivery.
To ensure that this manuscript authentically represents the experiences and priorities of those most central to the co-design process, we convened a focus group with our Patient Partner co-authors, who were also integral members of the Workshop Planning Committee. This dialogue yielded key “so-what” impact statements highlighting what Patient Partners found most critical and meaningful about the co-design process. These perspectives are explicitly included throughout the manuscript, under the heading “Patient Partner Perspectives”, to amplify their voices and underscore their contributions.Patient Partner Perspectives: “For me, the most meaningful part of the co-design process was being treated as an equal contributor. It wasn’t just about being present—it was about being truly heard and valued in shaping the outcomes.”

## History of Diabetes Action Canada workshops
The DAC in-person workshops have evolved significantly since the first meeting in 2016, which initially engaged seven Patient Partners along with researchers, clinicians, and other knowledge users, to the most recent meeting in 2024, which engaged 39 Patient Partners. Beyond the increase in participation, there has been a notable improvement in the comfort and ease of interaction among attendees from diverse backgrounds. This paper outlines the deliberate steps taken by the DAC team to create environments where Patient Partners are actively supported in their participation and play more central roles in planning and achieving the workshops’ objectives. We present the progression of DAC’s workshops chronologically (see Table  ; Figs.  and ), with the most significant changes occurring in 2024. Figure  illustrates how DAC’s approach aligns with Carman et al.’s Framework for Patient and Family Engagement and the IAP2 Spectrum of Public Participation. Over time, workshop planning evolved from initial consultation (informing and consulting) to deeper collaboration and empowerment. By 2024, the Workshop Planning Committee was composed primarily of Patient Partners, demonstrating a clear shift toward the “empower” end of the engagement spectrum and a strong commitment to genuine patient co-leadership.
Organizational changes were pivotal to the success of the 2024 DAC Workshop. In 2022, DAC received renewed funding from the CIHR Strategy for Patient-Oriented Research (SPOR) program, with a new mandate focused on knowledge mobilization and implementation science through a patient-oriented research (POR) lens. This shift enabled the recruitment of a dedicated Director of Patient Engagement (LM), responsible for strengthening and systematizing patient partnership practices across the Network. For the 2024 workshop, this enhanced leadership was critical for bringing expertise in co-creation and event facilitation, as well as for supporting the well-being of Patient Partners throughout the event.
Administrative roles were also adapted to ensure comprehensive logistical and communication support tailored to the needs of Patient Partners based on continuous learning from past events. Across Canada, other SPOR-funded entities, including both networks and support units, have similarly evolved their approaches to patient engagement, and active Communities of Practice have emerged to foster shared learning and support a national culture shift toward meaningful patient involvement in research [ ].
At DAC, the sustainability of these organizational changes is reinforced through formalized roles, dedicated funding, long-term strategic planning, and the active integration of Patient Partners in governance structures, ensuring that patient engagement remains a core and enduring priority.Patient Partner Perspectives: “Over the years, I’ve seen a shift—from tokenism to real partnership. Each workshop feels more inclusive and collaborative.”

 

 

 

 

## Before the events– co-design with Patient Partners

## Developing the theme
To inspire participation, identifying a unifying theme that is relevant and meaningful to all participants is critical for engagement and success. Historically, workshop themes have emerged based on the evolving maturity of the network’s patient engagement practices, as described in our guiding theoretical frameworks [ , ]. In DAC’s early stages, themes primarily focused on the consultation and involvement of Patient Partners. For instance, the 2017 workshop theme was “Engaging People Living with Diabetes and Their Health Care Teams to Improve Patient Experience and Outcomes” [ ], followed by the 2018 theme, “Enabling Effective Care Pathways for People Living with Diabetes” [ ]. As DAC advanced into deeper partnership and co-design phases, themes reflected these shifts, as seen in the 2022 workshop theme, “Building Strategic Relationships for Impact” [ ]. The 2024 workshop theme was developed explicitly through DAC-led patient engagement activities, drawing on themes identified in the co-developed DAC 2021–2026 Strategic Plan [ ], as well as feedback from all participants in the October 2022 DAC Workshop [ ]. The resulting learning objectives for the 2024 workshop were then refined by DAC’s governance committees, including the Steering Council (comprising 50% Patient Partners), the Patient-Oriented Research Committee, and the Patient Partner Advisory Circles. These are outlined below:

Understanding the role of Patient Partners in knowledge mobilization in research.
Appreciating the role of Patient Partners as agents of change.
Exploring successful case studies of Patient Partner involvement.
Identifying challenges and opportunities in patient engagement.
Developing strategies for sustainable patient-healthcare partnerships.
Identifying roles for industry in patient-oriented research.

The overarching theme, “Patient Partners as Agents of Change,” was chosen to unify these objectives and was used to guide all workshop activities, which were co-designed with Patient Partners to address these objectives.

## Recruiting a Workshop Planning Committee
A Workshop Planning Committee was established to ensure we represented the perspectives of our diverse audiences. We recruited committee members from the DAC Patient Partner community through open calls, peer nominations, and targeted outreach. This committee comprised six Patient Partners, two POR research trainees, five DAC staff, and one patient engagement research leader. Starting in January 2024, the committee met virtually once every month. To capture a broad range of experiences, we carefully recruited members to reflect diversity in age, lived experiences with type 1 and type 2 diabetes, as well as representation from Francophone and Indigenous Patient Partners. The committee also engaged monthly with the DAC Patient-Oriented Research Committee, facilitating collaboration with the broader scientific leadership to ensure that the workshop activities aligned with research goals and delivered value to all target audiences. The committee led seven key tasks:

Adjudicating abstracts to select presenters for workshop activities.
Identifying and selecting plenary speakers.
Meeting with invited speakers to plan and review their presentations, ensuring the content included aspects of patient partnership.
Co-creating a ‘Things to Know’ reference document for Patient Partners and invited research trainees, listing what was included for travel, expenses, compensation, and other logistical information.
Co-designing the workshop agenda to ensure the content was understandable and accessible for all audiences.
Planning the menu to ensure all food and the schedule for food availability aligned with the health and religious needs of Patient Partners and other attendees.
Co-designing the use of symbols on the agenda to indicate presentations with Patient Partner presenters or moderators.

This collaborative effort produced an agenda featuring a diverse range of activities that engaged workshop attendees and emphasized the importance of lived experience in diabetes research and care.Patient Partner Perspectives: “Having the chance to influence the structure of the event, from the menu to the agenda, made me feel included and valued as a team member rather than an afterthought.”

## Incentives for Patient Partner in-person participation
For the DAC 2024 Workshop– “Patient Partners as Agents of Change”, we aimed to ensure strong representation of Patient Partners, fostering community building and creating networking opportunities across all research pillars. In collaboration with both the DAC Patient Partner Advisory Circles and the Workshop Planning Committee, we developed the following approaches to ensure meaningful integration of Patient Partners into workshop activities and comprehensive support for their participation.

Provide full support for attendance: DAC fully covered workshop attendance costs for Patient Partners. A ‘Things to Know’ document, co-designed by our Workshop Planning Committee and aligned with DAC’s compensation policies [ ], was prepared detailing all covered expenses and logistics. This document, written in accessible language, was shared with all Patient Partners along with their invitations to prevent any misunderstandings regarding provided support.
Engagement opportunities to create mutual value: Recognizing that active Patient Partner participation in academic settings can be challenging, DAC co-created a number of engagement opportunities to inspire and facilitate participation and in-person attendance. These included:

 Patient Partner Award: This award, co-developed with the DAC Patient Partner Circles, honors Patient Partners who have made outstanding contributions to advancing patient-oriented diabetes research and care. Nominations were accepted from researchers, peers, or through self-nomination, and were adjudicated by the Workshop Planning Committee. The three awardees, each representing unique experiences living with diabetes, spoke of their work with DAC in a panel discussion.
 Interactive breakout sessions: Researchers were invited to present their work in interactive sessions specifically designed to elicit feedback from Patient Partners. Prior to the workshop, participants selected their top two breakout sessions, and session details were printed on the back of their badges to facilitate navigation. Each session was facilitated by DAC staff or trained facilitators, all of whom received refresher training on best practices in Patient Engagement before the event.
 Patient Partners as co-presenters: Nearly all presentations included a Patient Partner as a co-presenter. This approach provided another opportunity to share information in an accessible format for all audience members. Presentations featuring a Patient Partner co-presenter were denoted by a symbol on the agenda.
 Compensated roles for active participation: Patient Partners were offered various roles throughout the event. These included serving as judges for abstract awards, evaluating posters and oral presentations with a particular focus on the quality of lay communication. Some Patient Partners also acted as compensated social media contributors, sharing their experiences and observations through their personal platforms. Others contributed by writing blogs for DAC’s website [ ] and social media channels. In appreciation for their time and contributions, these roles were compensated as a ‘consultant’ within our DAC compensation policy [ ].

3.Targeted and open invitations: Patient Partners engaged in DAC governance and research program activities, as well as those recommended by DAC research members, were personally invited to attend the workshop. A limited number of open invitations were available to Patient Partners who had not yet engaged with a specific DAC initiative or research project but were eager to participate. We intentionally avoided a formal application process to attend the Workshop, based on feedback that such processes can intimidate and pose barriers for individuals from historically marginalized populations. Instead, we reviewed our records to identify individuals who had participated in DAC-led focus groups and other accessible activities, and who had expressed interest in deeper involvement with DAC through these activities.

Patient Partner Perspectives: This was the first time I wasn’t just invited to attend, I was invited to lead. That shift changed how I saw myself, and how others saw me too.”

## Building inclusive environments
Patient Partners co-designed the call for abstracts and the application information available to all presenters who applied. To support Patient Partners interested in presenting their work but unfamiliar with the abstract submission process, we held an open information session to explain the steps and made a recording of this session available. From this session, we created a Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ) resource to support submissions. We required all presentations to include Patient Partner co-presenters and tagged any abstract submitted by Patient Partners for reviewers. We invited all submitted abstracts to be presented as oral, poster, or artistic presentations, with applicants indicating their preference during the submission process.
Prior to the DAC 2024 Workshop– “Patient Partners as Agents of Change”, we held a facilitator training module for all presenters on best practices developed by DAC, including language tips, facilitation tips, check-in strategies, communication strategies, and different inclusion strategies that Patient Partners repeatedly told us would enable their full participation. This included suggested tactics to start engagement including use of first names and having introductions focus on personal rather than academic achievements. We reviewed and discussed this information with each presenter and facilitator before the workshop and provided it as a resource afterward.
All workshop materials and presentations were made available in both French and English to support attendees’ participation in their preferred language. As a bilingual network, DAC relied on bilingual staff to complete the translations, ensuring that the intended meaning and tone were preserved. We distributed the agenda in both languages to all workshop participants beforehand and made it accessible during the event through QR codes. Further, we translated all presentation materials to ensure that printed materials, including posters, slides, and handouts, were available in both languages. Additionally, one poster was provided in braille for participants with visual impairments. While all presenters at the Workshop delivered their presentations in English, simultaneous French translation was available for the Francophone participants. We accepted audience questions in both French and English, with bilingual moderators ensuring seamless communication.Patient Partner Perspectives: “There was space for my diabetes and my culture. I didn’t have to leave any part of myself behind.”

## Frontloading information to facilitate participation
The Workshop agenda and all meeting materials were pre-circulated to all participants to frontload information and ensure accessibility of the content. The agenda and meeting materials were available as a web link, downloadable PDF, and printed version, allowing participants to interact with the content using their preferred mode. This also allowed all participants to read as little or as much as they wished prior to the event and enabled visually impaired participants to upload materials to readers before the event. With the agenda and meeting materials co-designed with Patient Partners, the content was written in a lay language format.Patient Partner Perspectives: “The inclusion of symbols on the agenda to identify patient-led sessions was a small but impactful detail that demonstrated thoughtfulness and inclusion.”

All Patient Partners were given the food menus in advance of the workshop to facilitate their calculations for planning medications and movement according to how they each best managed their diabetes. Patient Partners co-designed the menus, incorporating their preferences for abundant vegetables, protein, and low-carb options, while also including carbohydrates for those who wanted autonomy and choice. Meal start times and punctuality were strictly respected because Patient Partners highlighted the importance of timing their insulin doses before a meal according to the agenda.

## During the workshop - Establishing spaces where Patient Partners feel confident expressing their views

## Assigning accountable staff to support Patient Partner attendees
Effective patient partnership is central to DAC’s mission. To foster leadership, empathy, and support in patient engagement, DAC has a Director of Patient Engagement (LM) dedicated to guiding activities, facilitating communication, and ensuring a culture of partnership. This role evolves with patient partnership research, providing Patient Partners with consistent access to a familiar, accountable staff member, which is especially crucial during in-person events. In the post-pandemic era, as many with chronic conditions remain cautious about gatherings, this presence fosters a supportive environment, peer mentoring, and informal discussions, easing concerns and strengthening community.

## Networking opportunity prior to the event
Recognizing that Patient Partners may have limited exposure to academic settings, DAC hosts a pre-workshop social dinner exclusively for Patient Partners, key staff, and researcher members on the evening before the workshop—a tradition we have maintained for every in-person workshop. This smaller, more intimate gathering provides an inviting space for Patient Partners to connect, establish relationships, and acclimate to the setting. During the dinner, Patient Partners had the opportunity to begin engaging in discussions about DAC initiatives, network, and start defining their roles within the collaborative environment.

## Establishing comfortable sharing spaces
To further support Patient Partners during the workshop, we implemented twice-daily check-ins—one during breakfast and one at the end of each day. These check-ins were held in-person in a dedicated room and provided a forum for Patient Partners to engage with one another, ask questions, and offer real-time feedback. Led by the Director of Patient Engagement (LM), these sessions helped participants, who were less familiar with DAC or traveling alone, to build connections and navigate the event. Additionally, these check-ins allowed us to monitor participants’ well-being. If any Patient Partner was absent, we initiated follow-up procedures to ensure their safety and well-being.
Each evening, including the night before the workshop, we sent out reminder emails detailing the next day’s check-ins, highlighting changes to the agenda, and emphasizing key presentations. We also spotlighted Patient Partner presentations in the nightly recaps to encourage mutual support among the group.

## Flattening hierarchies
Feedback from previous workshops highlighted that traditional power structures can create intimidating environments that hinder open dialogue. In 2019, we held a plenary session on understanding, navigating, and flattening power dynamics, delivered by Holly Witteman (Scientific Co-Lead for Patient Engagement with DAC). At the DAC 2024 Workshop– “Patient Partners as Agents of Change”, the DAC Executive Director (TM) opened with an introduction focused on sharing personal details to enhance relatability and approachability rather than professional titles or academic credentials. To set an inclusive tone, we encouraged the use of first names throughout the event, and this extended to name tag design, which included only first and last names, thereby minimizing the hierarchical assumptions often associated with roles and titles.Patient Partner Perspectives: “I loved that we didn’t have name tags with affiliations. It let us choose how to introduce ourselves and made it so much easier to start conversations.”

During the workshop, a plenary session was led by invited guests Cheryle Dreaver and Carla Cochrane from the First Nations Health and Social Secretariat (FNHSSM) [ ]. They explored the complex relationship between privilege, power dynamics, and intersectionality within the context of health. Participants took part in interactive discussions, activities, and case studies to explore how power and privilege (or the lack thereof) affect health outcomes, particularly for individuals facing systemic barriers in accessing care for the prevention and management of chronic conditions such as diabetes. These deliberate actions and sessions were intended to diffuse traditional power dynamics, facilitate knowledge exchange, and promote meaningful connections among participants, regardless of their roles within DAC.

## A dedicated respite room
Recognizing the unique needs of Patient Partners, particularly those related to managing their health, we provided a dedicated respite room that was available throughout the workshop. This quiet space offered participants the opportunity to take breaks, reflect on their experiences, or attend to their health in a peaceful setting. Stocked with refreshments and equipped with a telephone for external support, the room could be used privately or by small groups. Time in the room was managed by DAC’s dedicated staff to ensure that all who needed it had access, further supporting the well-being of Patient Partners during the event.

## Program diversity featuring Patient Partner presenters
The development of a balanced and inclusive workshop program was a priority to reflect the broad scope of DAC activities and research initiatives. A notable feature of the agenda was the diversity of presenters, with a strong emphasis on including research trainees and Patient Partners. In fact, over 90% of the sessions included contributions from individuals with lived experience or those who have loved ones affected by diabetes. This emphasis on lived experience was highlighted in the agenda through the use of a handshake symbol, indicating that the presentation was either delivered by or co-presented with someone directly impacted by diabetes. This helped Patient Partners relate more to the content presented and promoted greater participation from the members.Patient Partner Perspectives: A key success noted by Patient Partners was the integration of their lived experiences into every session. One partner commented, “Seeing the handshake symbol on the agenda and knowing that lived experience was a focus made it easier for me to engage.”

## Speed networking for all participants
The Speed Networking session, a key feature of the DAC 2024 Workshop– “Patient Partners as Agents of Change”, was developed in direct response to requests for increased opportunities to foster connections across diverse groups. Designed to minimize power dynamics, it addressed Patient Partners’ and research trainees’ preferences for equitable engagement.
To foster connections across diverse groups, we invited a broad spectrum of participants, including Patient Partner leaders, executive directors, administrative professionals, researchers, and program managers, to participate as ‘mentors’. During the session, participants rotated among tables, spending 10–15 min with each mentor, creating an atmosphere where all attendees—mentors and participants alike—could share knowledge and perspectives with equal standing.
This intentional approach not only disrupted traditional power structures but also promoted the exchange of ideas and facilitated connections between individuals who might not have otherwise interacted.Patient Partner Perspective: “There is truly nothing else like this for the diabetes community. The connection of researchers, students, HCPs, and patients was informative and exciting. Where else would I connect with a man who experienced homelessness, a trucker, and a celebrated researcher? I learned so much.”

## Recognition of contributions
We recognized the contributions of the Workshop Planning Committee and key contributors as integral to the event’s success, naming them in the agenda and formally acknowledging them during the introduction to the event. Special tribute was paid to foundational Patient Partners who had left DAC due to health reasons or in memory of those who had passed away. These individuals were recognized further by sharing follow-up stories on our website (diabetesaction.ca), celebrating their legacy and lasting impact.
All Workshop Planning Committee members, speakers, and steering council members received handwritten thank-you notes from DAC leadership, accompanied by small art prints created by Michael Alexander, an Indigenous artist and DAC Patient Partner. Customized DAC pins were gifted to workshop participants to wear on lanyards or at future events, and all Patient Partners received art prints from Michael Alexander.
These gestures of gratitude reinforced our commitment to an inclusive and supportive environment where all participants feel valued and respected.

## After the workshop– closing the loop
One week after the workshop, the Workshop Planning Committee reconvened to review participant feedback gathered through the evaluation process and collect testimonials from committee members about the event. A post-workshop plan was co-designed to ensure appropriate follow-up with those Patient Partners who presented or were directly involved in the delivery of the Workshop in some capacity, reinforcing their role as valued contributors to the workshop. In addition to the acknowledgements made during the event, all Patient Partners who attended the workshop received thank-you emails containing a link to the evaluation survey to capture their perspectives on the experience.
Patient Partners engaged in enhancing our social media presence by writing blog posts about the event, and had their written pieces featured on the DAC website throughout the summer [ ]. DAC offered to tag them on our social media accounts with their personal accounts if they wanted additional visibility and a platform for their work. This served as a novel approach to workshop evaluation, complementing quantitative surveys with storytelling as an equally important method for assessing and communicating the impact of the Workshop.
A detailed workshop report was written that highlighted the work of the DAC team, the Workshop Planning Committee, and the participants [ ]. The Workshop Planning Committee first edited the report to ensure that it captured all the information accurately and reflected the event’s activities. The report was then made available on the DAC website in both French and English.

## Post-workshop survey
Anecdotal feedback suggested that the 2024 DAC Workshop– “Patient Partners as Agents of Change” was the most successful to date. Survey responses were collected from a diverse range of participants, including researchers, research trainees, Patient Partners, representatives from partner organizations, as well as “Other”, which consisted of community members, government agency representatives, and caregivers who do not identify as Patient Partners.
To assess the impact of our continuous improvement efforts, we analyzed response rates and open-text comments from the post-event evaluation. The survey included questions on the quality of programming and speakers, the relevance of activities to the event’s learning objectives, and participants’ overall experience, with a particular focus on Patient Partners.
Responses were collected using both Likert-scale questions (ranging from “Strongly Agree” to “Strongly Disagree”) and open-ended comments. The evaluation was accessible throughout the workshop via a link and QR code and was later emailed directly to all attendees. The feedback gathered was instrumental in identifying successes, areas for improvement, and recommendations for future workshops. Table  outlines the progression of these responses over time.

 

All participants who completed the survey indicated that the workshop provided an opportunity to express their views and ask questions. Nearly all believed that the environment fostered collaborative and supportive spaces for knowledge sharing (Likert scale ratings: Strongly Agree/Agree-53; Strongly Disagree/Disagree-1). Participants highlighted their appreciation for the diverse range of activities beyond conventional research presentations, such as networking exercises, art exhibitions, and interactive sessions (Likert scale ratings: Strongly Agree/Agree-53; Strongly Disagree/Disagree-1). The opportunity to engage with a wide spectrum of attendees—including Patient Partners, healthcare professionals, researchers, and individuals from various backgrounds—was particularly valued (Likert scale ratings: Strongly Agree/Agree-52; Strongly Disagree/Disagree-2). The omission of designations on name tags helped foster this engagement (Likert scale ratings: Strongly Agree/Agree-51; Strongly Disagree/Disagree-3). Many attendees emphasized the significance of hearing firsthand accounts from Patient Partners, which provided unique insights and challenged existing perspectives (Likert scale ratings: Strongly Agree/Agree-54; Strongly Disagree/Disagree-0).
Overall, attendees praised the creative use of mixed media, such as films, videos, and art, and commended the workshop’s organization, variety, and strong emphasis on patient engagement and interdisciplinary collaboration. The event was widely regarded as an inspiring and educational experience, with many participants expressing enthusiasm for its continuation and expansion in future iterations.

## Suggestions for improvements and changes
Respondents provided the following recommendations for enhancing future workshops:

Logistics: Improved navigation directions to the venue and adjustments to air conditioning were suggested.
Program Pacing: Attendees proposed a slower program pace to allow more time for interaction and reflection.
Patient Partner Involvement: Many participants advocated for increased opportunities for Patient Partners to share their perspectives and engage with one another.
Content Warnings: The inclusion of trigger warnings for sensitive content was recommended.
Poster Sessions: Suggestions included more space around poster displays and structured time for poster presentations.
Networking and Breaks: Attendees recommended longer breaks, a simpler method for networking, and an extended workshop duration to allow for informal interactions and relaxation.
Meals and Facilitation: Plated meals and enhanced facilitation support for session leaders were suggested.
Representation: While efforts to include Indigenous communities and Francophone Patient Partners were acknowledged, participants suggested that future workshops aim for broader representation of these groups.

## Discussion

## Overview of key findings
The Diabetes Action Canada (DAC) workshops have evolved significantly over the past eight years, showcasing continuous learning and adaptation to meet the diverse needs of participants, particularly Patient Partners. This iterative improvement process highlights DAC’s commitment to fostering patient-centered practices and creating inclusive spaces for knowledge exchange and co-construction. By integrating feedback from attendees, including Patient Partners, researchers, and research trainees, each workshop has become progressively more impactful, with the 2024 DAC Workshop– “Patient Partners as Agents of Change”, widely regarded as the most successful to date.
Comparative data demonstrate the growth in Patient Partner participation, from seven in 2016 to 39 in 2024, alongside advancements such as patient-led planning committees, co-designed agendas, and symbols denoting lived experience in presentations. These measures fostered inclusivity, with Patient Partners noting the impact of being treated as equal contributors.Patient Partner Perspectives: “This conference was different—it made me feel like my story was not just data but a catalyst for change.”

Broadly, these findings align with global trends emphasizing the integration of patient perspectives in research and healthcare [ , ]. Similar approaches in other disease communities, such as arthritis, have demonstrated the value of patient-led initiatives in improving research outcomes and fostering multidisciplinary collaboration [ ]. These efforts clearly demonstrate approaches to embed patient leadership into event planning; however, DAC’s approach offers novel contributions by intentionally addressing power hierarchies and embedding inclusivity at all stages of planning. Contextualizing DAC’s approach within Carman et al.’s Framework for Patient and Family Engagement and the IAP2 Spectrum further illustrates DAC’s evolution toward genuine partnership and empowerment. Notable milestones include patient-led agenda setting and equitable compensation policies. Collectively, DAC’s experiences highlight how the systematic application of co-design principles can strengthen patient engagement and foster broader systemic change.

## Challenges and lessons learned
The planning and execution of the 2024 DAC Workshop presented several challenges, primarily stemming from the diversity of participants and the ambition to create an inclusive and accessible event for all. These obstacles provided valuable learning opportunities and underscored the importance of deliberate, empathetic planning.

## Key challenges and strategies

## Balancing diverse needs of Patient Partners
With attendees representing varied health conditions, lived experiences, and preferences, ensuring equitable participation required careful planning. This was addressed through co-designed agendas that incorporated feedback from Patient Partners, emphasizing activities that fostered comfort, collaboration, and inclusivity (e.g., networking, small focus groups, etc.).

## Managing language and cultural differences
To accommodate linguistic diversity, all workshop materials were provided in English and French, with simultaneous translation available during sessions. The symbols in the agenda indicated presentations involving Patient Partners, ensuring lay-language content.

## Addressing accessibility gaps
Accessibility was a core priority. Agendas and workshop materials were available in print and digital form, allowing participants more processing time or the opportunity to add documents to screen readers. Providing information in advance empowered participants to engage with the content at their own pace, while structured agendas ensured clarity and inclusivity for all attendees.

## Advancing inclusive participation
The workshop prioritized equity by creating an inclusive environment for all participants, particularly those from historically underrepresented groups. Despite significant progress, gaps remain in achieving proportional representation from Indigenous and Francophone communities. Future workshops should emphasize targeted outreach, tailored support, and culturally sensitive practices to address these disparities.
The insights gained from these challenges extend beyond planning large academic events. The co-design principles and inclusive strategies applied here can inform research planning, healthcare service design, and public health campaigns. By embedding Patient Partner engagement into broader initiatives, organizations can create meaningful partnerships and achieve more equitable outcomes.

## Sustainability in co-design approaches
Achieving sustainability in co-design practices requires institutional commitment, capacity building, and the development of replicable models. Meaningful engagement requires the commitment of resources in funding, staffing, and time—without which the risk of superficial engagement increases, undermining the effectiveness of the methodology. Ensuring adequate resources allows Patient Partner engagement to become a long-term, embedded component of healthcare research and systems, rather than a one-off initiative.

## Institutional commitment
Sustained co-design practices hinge on organizations formalizing their commitment through dedicated policies and resources. This includes hiring staff specifically tasked with managing patient engagement, establishing funding streams earmarked for in-person gatherings and collaborative activities, and integrating co-design principles into organizational mandates. Without institutionalized support, co-design risks being deprioritized, particularly in resource-constrained environments.

## Capacity building
Successful co-design depends on equipping both Patient Partners and researchers with the necessary skills and knowledge to collaborate effectively. Training programs in patient-oriented research exist, but they largely rely on asynchronous online learning and only tough briefly on the principles of co-design, mutual understanding, or addressing power dynamics to promote equitable participation. For Patient Partners, additional accessible training is needed to support navigation of academic or healthcare settings, while researchers would benefit from formal training on how to center lived experiences in their work. Continuous investment in capacity building strengthens the co-design process and ensures meaningful, reciprocal engagement.

Patient Partner Perspectives: “Moving forward, it’s essential to offer training for all participants on how to navigate co-designed spaces effectively. This will help everyone feel prepared and empowered.”

## Framework development
To support broader adoption of co-design practices, the lessons learned from this work could inform the development of clear and accessible frameworks. Specifically, creating resources such as templates, guidelines, and playbooks would offer practical, step-by-step guidance for other organizations aiming to replicate DAC’s approach. These resources could outline how to engage Patient Partners meaningfully to co-design event agendas, and foster inclusive and supportive environments while allowing flexibility to adapt to different organizational needs and cultural contexts. By offering a structured yet adaptable model, DAC’s experience could help scale patient-centered event planning practices and encourage wider implementation across the research and healthcare community.

## Policy implications
Policymakers should consider how co-design approaches to engage Patient Partners in large academic settings can inform guidelines and policy changes, promote patient-centered care, and foster interdisciplinary collaboration. Embedding these practices into policy would not only enhance sustainability but also drive systemic change toward more inclusive and effective healthcare research and delivery.
These considerations underscore the need for future research to refine and expand the applicability of co-design principles, ensuring they remain relevant across diverse contexts and evolving challenges. Ensuring sustainability also lays the groundwork for exploring broader applications of co-design principles in healthcare and beyond.

## Future directions
The approaches and outcomes of the 2024 DAC Workshop– “Patient Partners as Agents of Change” highlight the potential for co-design principles to evolve and expand in scope. Future research should explore opportunities to apply and adapt these practices across broader populations, diverse contexts, and even sectors beyond healthcare.

## Broader populations
Co-design principles should be evaluated in populations with unique or underserved needs, including individuals with rare diseases, those with complex care requirements, pediatric populations, and caregivers. These groups face distinct challenges that may benefit from inclusive, patient-partnered approaches to healthcare research and service design.

## Cross-Sector implications
While rooted in healthcare, co-design approaches hold promise for other sectors. For example, education systems could leverage co-design to engage students, parents, and educators in shaping curricula or addressing systemic challenges. Similarly, public health campaigns and community planning initiatives could adopt these principles to amplify the voices of those most affected by policy decisions. Exploring these cross-sector implications could unlock innovative applications for co-design, fostering equity and collaboration in varied domains.

## Global and multicultural perspectives
Future research should evaluate the scalability and adaptability of co-design principles in larger-scale initiatives and global forums. This includes assessing whether the current methods for navigating multiculturalism in patient engagement are effective or require adaptation for diverse international contexts. For instance, in collectivist cultures, co-design approaches may need to focus on group or community-level contributions rather than individual perspectives. Similarly, linguistic and systemic differences must be considered to ensure inclusivity and equity in diverse cultural settings.
Such research would establish whether co-design methodologies can maintain their core principles while addressing cultural, linguistic, and systemic variations. Exploring these dimensions globally could further demonstrate the transformative potential of co-design as a universal framework for patient-centered innovation and decision-making.

## Conclusion
The 2024 DAC Workshop– “Patient Partners as Agents of Change” demonstrates the vital role of Patient Partners in shaping large academic events and how co-design approaches to planning can create more inclusive, impactful, and patient-centered engagement. Over eight years, DAC has continuously refined its approach, highlighting the value of meaningful patient involvement in strengthening research and healthcare practices in diabetes. Through the lens of the Carman et al. framework and the IAP2 Spectrum, DAC’s workshop demonstrates how structured and intentional co-design can advance patient engagement from consultative to truly collaborative stages. By embedding Patient Partners in event planning, organizations can foster equitable participation, improve accessibility, and ensure that discussions reflect the priorities of those most affected by research outcomes.
Sustaining and expanding these practices requires a commitment to integrating Patient Partners at every stage of event planning. Future efforts should explore how co-design principles can be adapted across different cultural, linguistic, and systemic contexts to enhance global patient engagement in academic and healthcare settings.
