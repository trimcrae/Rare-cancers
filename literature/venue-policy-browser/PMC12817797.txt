## Background
Gastric cancer remains a major global health concern with approximately one million new cases annually. Despite five decades of extensive research, comprehensive analysis mapping gastric carcinogenesis research evolution and emerging therapeutic frontiers remains limited. This study aims to systematically analyze the intellectual landscape, research trends, and future directions in gastric carcinogenesis research.

## Materials and methods
We analyzed 11,527 scientific publications on gastric carcinogenesis from 1970-2024 using advanced scientometric tools including CiteSpace and VOSviewer for co-citation analysis, collaboration network mapping, and keyword co-occurrence analysis.

## Results
Publication output demonstrated exponential growth, peaking at 547 publications in 2022. Asia leads research productivity with China, Japan, and South Korea contributing 37.50% of total publications, while the United States exhibits highest collaborative influence with betweenness centrality of 0.45. Seven distinct research clusters emerged encompassing Helicobacter pylori pathogenesis, molecular mechanisms, precancerous lesions, autoimmune gastritis, and therapeutic interventions. Five emerging trends were identified: stromal microenvironment interactions in carcinogenesis progression, gastric organoid models for disease modeling, artificial intelligence-enhanced endoscopic detection technologies, biomarker development for early diagnosis, and traditional herbal medicine applications for chemoprevention.

## Conclusions
Research gaps persist in autoimmune gastritis pathways and clinical translation of molecular discoveries. Emerging research directions encompass advanced modeling systems, molecular diagnostics, and technological applications, though their clinical impact remains to be established through future validation studies.

## Introduction
Gastric cancer remains a major global health concern, with approximately 1,033,701 new cases and 782,685 deaths reported worldwide in 2018 [ ]. It is the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality, after lung cancer, and its incidence is notably high in Asia, although substantial cases are also reported in Europe [ , ]. Most gastric cancer cases are sporadic (over 90%), with a smaller percentage associated with familial aggregation or inherited cancer syndromes.
A key risk factor in the development of gastric cancer is Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection, which significantly increases the risk of non-cardia adenocarcinoma [ ]. However, it is important to note that only a small proportion of individuals infected with H. pylori will eventually develop gastric cancer [ ]. This variation in disease progression is influenced by several factors, including the genetic makeup of both the bacterium and the host, as well as environmental variables such as diet and lifestyle.
Gastric cancer is typically classified into two histological subtypes: the intestinal type and the diffuse type. The intestinal type is characterized by glandular growth, while the diffuse type is defined by poorly cohesive cells. These subtypes have distinct pathophysiological origins: diffuse-type gastric cancer is thought to arise from genetic mutations affecting cell-extracellular matrix interactions, while the intestinal type is often a consequence of chronic mucosal damage caused by persistent H. pylori infection, which induces inflammation and sets the stage for carcinogenesis [ ]. Notably, these two histotypes frequently coexist within the same tumor, reflecting cancer heterogeneity. Moreover, the diffuse (non-cohesive) variant may also represent dedifferentiation of the intestinal subtype [ , ]. Interestingly, it has been proposed that at least a subgroup of sporadic diffuse-type gastric cancer may develop through an ‘alternative’ carcinogenic pathway involving H. pylori infection, atrophic gastritis, and intestinal metaplasia, in association with cadherin 1 (CDH1) mutations [ – ]. Therefore, gastric atrophy and its concomitant metaplasia serve as a common field of cancerization for both subtypes of gastric cancer.
The pathogenesis of gastric cancer follows a well-established cascade of sequential morphological and molecular alterations. When severe damage occurs to the gastric mucosa, resulting in glandular cell loss—a condition known as gastric atrophy—the initial adaptive response is metaplastic transformation [ ]. Under conditions of persistent injury and chronic inflammation, particularly in the context of chronic H. pylori infection, this metaplastic epithelium progressively evolves and expands to establish dysplastic lineages that ultimately culminate in gastric adenocarcinoma. The clinical significance of this stepwise progression is underscored by observations that severe gastric atrophy is associated with significantly elevated rates of metachronous gastric cancers in patients following definitive resection and H. pylori eradication [ ]. Multiple factors contribute to this malignant transformation, including metabolic, environmental, epigenetic, genomic, infective, inflammatory, and oncogenic influences. Accordingly, preventive strategies primarily focus on modifying these risk factors, with chemoprevention through H. pylori eradication representing a key intervention. However, it is important to recognize that the therapeutic effectiveness of H. pylori eradication diminishes substantially once advanced gastric mucosal changes, such as extensive atrophy and metaplasia, have already occurred [ , ].
Looking ahead, the global burden of gastric cancer is expected to rise significantly. Projections for the period between 2020 and 2040 indicate that the annual number of new cases will increase from 1.09 million to 1.63 million, while deaths are expected to rise from 0.77 million to 1.18 million [ ]. This corresponds to an increase of approximately 49.76% in cases and 53.07% in deaths [ ]. East Asia is anticipated to remain the global epicenter, accounting for over 66% of new cases and 64% of deaths by 2040 [ ]. The aging population will be disproportionately affected, with their share of gastric cancer cases rising to 68.6%, and their share of deaths increasing to 72.0% [ ]. As such, gastric cancer will continue to pose a significant public health challenge, particularly in regions with limited healthcare infrastructure [ ].
Despite extensive research efforts, gastric carcinogenesis remains a complex field where systematic data collection and analysis have been relatively limited. This challenge is particularly significant given that gastric cancer represents a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, necessitating a comprehensive understanding of research evolution in this critical area. Scientometric analysis—which employs bibliographic databases and statistical methods to quantitatively and qualitatively examine scientific literature—offers a powerful methodological framework for understanding research field development [ , ]. Through systematic analysis of publication trends, citation relationships, and research networks, scientometric methods provide structured insights into the evolution of scientific domains, specifically enabling examination of publication patterns, citation impact assessment, and identification of emerging research areas [ ].
Scientometrics fundamentally applies quantitative methods to measure and guide scientific development, conceptualizing research as an informational process that can be systematically analyzed and understood [ ]. The discipline encompasses key analytical themes including research quality and impact assessment, citation process understanding, scientific field mapping, and the strategic employment of indicators in research policy and management [ ]. For gastric carcinogenesis research, scientometrics addresses the critical challenge posed by exponential literature growth in recent decades by providing structured approaches to organize this rapidly expanding knowledge base into meaningful patterns and trends that can inform future research directions.
While scientometric analysis has gained widespread application across various oncological fields, it remains significantly underutilized in gastric carcinogenesis research, creating a notable gap in our understanding of research evolution in this important area [ – ]. Existing studies have predominantly focused on fully developed gastric malignancies while underestimating earlier stages of epithelial transformation, particularly precancerous stages such as metaplasia and dysplasia that represent critical intervention points. Furthermore, most analyses have been geographically constrained or temporally limited, restricting comprehensive understanding of global research patterns (Supplementary Table 1).
The pioneering work of Ghojazadeh et al. [ ], who conducted the first significant scientometric analysis of Iranian gastric cancer research from 2000 to 2011, exemplifies both the potential and limitations of current approaches. Their analysis of 121 Medline publications revealed substantial growth in Iranian research output, particularly after 2006, with Tehran University of Medical Sciences emerging as a leading contributor and publications distributed across 19 countries [ ]. However, this geographically constrained analysis covering a relatively short timeframe highlighted the critical need for broader, longer-term global analyses to understand worldwide research trends and collaborative patterns in gastric carcinogenesis.
Regional scientometric investigations have subsequently provided valuable insights into geographic research patterns, though with continued temporal and scope limitations. Begum et al. [ ] conducted comprehensive cancer research mapping across Central and Eastern Europe, the Russian Federation, and Central Asia from 2007 to 2016, analyzing activity from 29 countries and revealing significant disparities in research productivity relative to national wealth. Notably, their findings indicated that clinical trials comprised only 3% of research outputs, with relative deficits in screening and palliative care research despite high regional burdens of gastric, lung, and colorectal cancers [ ]. While informative, this analysis remained geographically constrained and temporally limited, further emphasizing the need for disease-specific global analyses spanning extended timeframes.
Pathogen-specific scientometric studies have begun addressing focused aspects of gastric carcinogenesis research, particularly regarding H. pylori investigations. Eslami et al. [ ] conducted a comprehensive analysis of machine learning applications in H. pylori research over a decade (2010–2021), examining 17,413 Web of Science articles and revealing declining publication trends since 2019. Their study identified cancer biology as the dominant research domain (22% of outputs), with gastric cancer representing the most frequently studied H. pylori-related topic after the pathogen itself [ ]. However, clinical trials comprised only 3% of publications despite showing high international collaboration, and the pathogen-specific focus with shorter timeframe coverage highlighted the need for broader disease-specific analyses capturing the full spectrum of gastric carcinogenesis research, including H. pylori-independent mechanisms, over extended periods.
Treatment-specific scientometric analyses have recently emerged, focusing on particular therapeutic modalities within gastric cancer research. Li et al. [ ] conducted comprehensive scientometric and clinical trial analysis of gastric cancer immunotherapy from 1999 to 2023, analyzing 2,013 publications and 228 clinical trials. Their findings revealed rapid growth in immunotherapy research since 2016, with China dominating publications (53.2% of total output) and significant advances in immune checkpoint inhibitors, adoptive cell therapy, and vaccines [ ]. Key research clusters included tumor microenvironment, microsatellite instability, dendritic cells, and adoptive immunotherapy as major research hotspots [ ]. Nevertheless, the immunotherapy-specific focus within a shorter timeframe underscored the need for broader temporal analyses capturing the full evolution of gastric carcinogenesis research, including the transition from traditional approaches to modern immunotherapy and precision medicine strategies.
Clinical editorial analyses have provided important perspectives on gastric cancer research evolution, particularly regarding immunotherapy developments and their historical context. Triantafillidis et al. [ ] offered comprehensive commentary on gastric cancer immunotherapy progress, integrating clinical, epidemiological, and therapeutic perspectives while reviewing contemporary scientometric findings. Their analysis emphasized that immunotherapy has emerged as the ‘fifth pillar’ of gastric cancer treatment, alongside surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy, representing the culmination of decades of fundamental carcinogenesis research. However, their focus remained primarily on recent therapeutic developments rather than long-term research evolution, reinforcing the need for comprehensive temporal analyses tracing the research continuum from basic carcinogenic mechanisms to current therapeutic applications.
Demographic-specific scientometric investigations have recently examined particular patient subgroups within gastric carcinogenesis research. Azbergenov et al. [ ] conducted comprehensive analysis of biomarkers used in diagnosis and prognosis of gastric cancer in young patients (≤ 45 years) from 1993 to 2024, analyzing 536 Scopus publications and revealing a 7.71% annual growth rate in this specialized area. Their study identified distinctive characteristics in young gastric cancer patients, including more aggressive disease forms, higher rates of undifferentiated and diffuse tumor types, and increased likelihood of germline mutations [ ]. While providing valuable insights into age-specific research patterns, the biomarker-focused analysis in young patients highlighted the need for broader temporal analyses capturing gastric carcinogenesis research evolution across all age groups and research domains over extended periods.
Additional treatment-specific analyses have examined particular therapeutic modalities within gastric carcinogenesis research scope. Menbari Oskouie et al. [ ] conducted comprehensive scientometric analysis of radiotherapy and chemoradiotherapy for gastric cancer treatment from 2014 to 2023, analyzing 15,036 Scopus publications and revealing significant growth with China emerging as the top contributor (30.8% of publications). Their analysis identified key research clusters including treatment modalities, prognosis and survival outcomes, immunotherapy approaches, and quality of life considerations, showing increasing focus on immunotherapy, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and advanced surgical techniques [ ]. However, the therapy-specific focus over one decade emphasized the need for broader temporal analyses capturing the full evolution of gastric carcinogenesis research, including historical treatment concept development, basic carcinogenesis understanding, and progression from palliative to curative therapeutic approaches over five decades.
Despite gastric cancer’s significant impact as a leading cause of mortality and its substantial effects on patients’ quality of life, comprehensive scientometric studies in this field remain remarkably scarce. Our comprehensive five-decade approach to gastric carcinogenesis research addresses this critical gap by building upon recent pathogen-specific, treatment-focused, demographic-specific, and clinical perspective studies while providing essential historical context for understanding current research developments [ – ]. While focused studies provide crucial insights into particular etiological factors, therapeutic approaches, patient populations, or clinical applications, our broader disease-focused analysis enables examination of the full spectrum of gastric carcinogenesis research evolution, including H. pylori-dependent and independent mechanisms, genetic factors, environmental influences, and therapeutic approaches across extended timeframes.
This comprehensive temporal scope allows for identification of research patterns that may not be apparent in focused or shorter-term analyses, particularly the evolution of research priorities from infectious agents to molecular mechanisms, the development of precision medicine approaches, and the historical foundations underlying current therapeutic advances. Furthermore, our analysis provides essential context for understanding why certain research areas became prominent, how demographic considerations influenced research directions, why specific therapeutic targets emerged as successful intervention points, and how decades of fundamental carcinogenesis research have informed contemporary clinical applications.
Therefore, the aim of this study is to comprehensively assess scientific articles related to gastric carcinogenesis published from 1970 to 2024, employing a combination of scientometric and visual analytics methods to systematically map the progress, trends, and evolutionary patterns in this critical field of cancer research.

## Method

## Data Source and Literature Search Strategy
To ensure comprehensive coverage of gastric carcinogenesis research while addressing geographic and temporal limitations observed in previous studies, we implemented a multi-database search strategy. This approach accessed the Science Citation Index Expanded (SCIE) of the Web of Science database provided by Clarivate Analytics (Philadelphia, PA, USA), Scopus, and PubMed databases. This comprehensive strategy deliberately contrasts with more focused analyses, such as the Iranian study by Ghojazadeh et al. [ ], which examined only Medline publications from a single country over 12 years, and regional studies like the Central and Eastern Europe analysis by Begum et al. [ ], which, while valuable for understanding local research patterns, may not capture global trends and international collaboration networks.
Our 55-year temporal scope (1970–2024) was specifically designed to provide essential historical context for understanding current research developments highlighted in recent focused studies. While pathogen-specific studies such as the H. pylori machine learning analysis by Eslami et al. [ ] provide crucial insights into particular etiological factors, treatment-specific studies like the immunotherapy analysis by Li et al. [ ] reveal rapid therapeutic evolution, and demographic-focused investigations such as the young patient biomarker analysis by Azbergenov et al. [ ] demonstrate population-specific characteristics, our broader disease-focused analysis enables examination of the full research spectrum. This comprehensive scope encompasses H. pylori-dependent and independent mechanisms, genetic factors, environmental influences, therapeutic approaches, and demographic considerations, thereby allowing identification of research patterns and evolutionary trends that may not be apparent in focused or shorter-term analyses.
The extended temporal framework particularly enables understanding of how fundamental carcinogenesis research has informed current therapeutic innovations, including the evolution from infectious agent focus to molecular and precision medicine approaches, the development of multimodal therapy concepts, and the emergence of successful therapeutic targets such as immune checkpoints and tumor microenvironment components. Furthermore, this historical perspective provides crucial context for understanding why certain research areas became prominent, how demographic considerations influenced research directions, and how decades of basic carcinogenesis research have laid the foundation for contemporary clinical applications highlighted in recent reviews by Triantafillidis et al. [ ] and pathogen-host interaction studies [ ].
An advanced search strategy employing title and author keyword parameters was implemented to identify relevant publications while minimizing false positives. The search incorporated specific gastric carcinogenesis-related terms including “preneoplastic,” “oncogen*,” “carcinogen*,” “atroph*,” “metaplas*,” “dysplas*,” “intra-epithelial neoplasia*,” and “gastritis,” while systematically excluding unrelated terms such as “esophag*,” “bladder,” and “pulmonary” to ensure precision and relevance. This comprehensive search strategy identified a total of 11,527 documents related to gastric carcinogenesis research.
The selection process was rigorously managed using EndNote’s built-in de-duplication function, supplemented with manual verification to ensure accuracy and completeness. Only articles explicitly focused on gastric carcinogenesis were included in the analysis, with the study prioritizing original research articles and review papers while deliberately excluding other publication types to maintain analytical focus and scientific rigor. The analysis encompassed studies published between 1970 and 2024, with 2025 excluded due to unavailability of complete annual data. The detailed screening and selection process is comprehensively illustrated in Fig.  . 
The resulting reference database comprised comprehensive bibliographic data including authors, publication titles, years, sources, volumes, issues, page numbers, institutional affiliations, abstracts, author-defined keywords, references, and document types. This extensive dataset was subsequently processed through systematic scientometric analysis to identify trends, patterns, and evolutionary developments in gastric carcinogenesis research. Additionally, a comparative analysis of scientific publications was conducted by examining journals’ quality metrics, with publications classified into quartiles (Q1-Q4) based on the SCIE edition of the Journal Citation Reports from the Web of Science, enabling assessment of research impact and publication venue quality over the study period.

## Software for Scientometric and Visualization Analysis
Metadata for each document were manually exported in plain text format. Yearly publication trends and trends across different countries were analyzed and visualized using Microsoft Excel 2019. CiteSpace (version 5.7.R5) and VOSviewer (version 1.6.19) were employed to analyze the data and visualize the scientific knowledge graph.
A scientometric network consists of scientific publications or journals, keywords, researchers, countries, research organizations, or other relevant inclusion criteria. Such a network can be constructed based on various relationships, including citation links, scientific co-authorship, co-occurrence, co-citation, and bibliographic coupling. A link represents a connection or relationship between two items, with the strength of the link quantified by numerical values that reflect the type of relationship being analyzed. For instance, in co-authorship networks, the link strength is determined by the number of documents two researchers have co-authored, while in bibliographic coupling, it is based on the number of cited references two documents share.
In our study, we focused on the following types of analysis: co-authorship, co-occurrence of keywords (specifically, those appearing in the Author Keywords section), and co-citation (where two documents are cited by a third document).
VOSviewer, developed by Waltman et al. [ , ], is a free, JAVA-based tool designed for analyzing large sets of literature data and presenting them in map formats. It incorporates text mining techniques to extract significant noun phrases from titles and abstracts, which facilitates the creation of networks, clusters, and heatmaps that highlight research trends and connections. Co-word (or co-occurrence) analysis is used to explore the relationships between words that frequently appear together within the same scientific articles [ , – ]. In our study, we opted to use authors’ keywords rather than Keywords Plus. Our preference for authors’ keywords stems from the fact that these terms are directly chosen by the authors, providing a concise and accurate overview of the main topics of the articles. Authors’ keywords allow researchers to highlight the focal points of their work, offering readers a quick and targeted insight into the content. In contrast, Keywords Plus includes terms extracted from article titles, which may offer a broader context. However, these terms do not always align with the specific themes that the authors wish to emphasize [ ].
CiteSpace, developed by Chen C [ ], is designed to map the co-citation network of literature, providing insights into knowledge domains, research frontiers, and emerging trends. This software aids in the visualization of intricate relationships among authors, journals, collaborating nations, and citation patterns. In co-authorship networks, authors are represented as nodes connected by links (edges) that represent co-authored publications. These networks can be analyzed to measure the structure, such as the density or centrality of authors. A high betweenness centrality of an author node indicates that the author plays a pivotal role in connecting otherwise disconnected parts of the network, positioning them as key innovation hubs.
Citation analysis, in contrast, evaluates how frequently a paper is cited by other studies and examines the references to related works. Co-citation analysis, on the other hand, explores whether two papers are cited together in a third paper, helping to identify interdisciplinary connections and key contributions within the field.

## Results

## Publishing Trend Analysis
As shown in Fig.  , the number of publications in gastric carcinogenesis research has shown a steady upward trajectory over the years, with some notable fluctuations. From 1970 to 1985, the growth was relatively slow, with annual publication counts remaining below 100. A more pronounced increase was observed from 1986 onward, with the number of publications surpassing 200 per year by the mid-1990s. Between 2009 and 2018, the growth continued steadily, with annual publication counts fluctuating between 290 and 335. 
A significant surge in research activity occurred after 2018, with a marked increase in the number of publications. The peak year was 2022, with 547 publications, the highest recorded during the observed period. However, a sharper decline is evident in subsequent years, with the annual count dropping to 221 publications in 2024. Despite these fluctuations, the overall trend underscores a long-term increase in the volume of research dedicated to gastric carcinogenesis.

## Analysis of Countries, Regions, and Institutions
The literature on gastric carcinogenesis has been produced by 763 institutions across 138 countries or regions. As shown in Table  , the analysis of gastric carcinogenesis research reveals that Asia leads in global contributions, with China, Japan, and South Korea playing significant roles. Together, these three countries account for 4,321 publications, or 37.50% of the total 11,527 publications. North America ranks second, primarily driven by the United States, which contributes 17.50% of the total publications. Europe follows closely, with key contributors such as Italy (770; 6.68%), Germany (583; 5.05%), the UK (420; 3.64%), France (303; 2.63%), and Finland (255; 2.21%), collectively contributing 2,331 publications (20.22%). Oceania, represented by Australia, adds 265 publications (2.30%). 
Individually, the United States leads with 2,017 publications (17.50%), followed closely by China (2,003; 17.38%) and Japan (1,774; 15.40%). In terms of betweenness centrality, which measures the proportion of the shortest paths in a network that pass through a specific node, the USA ranks highest with a score of 0.45. This high score indicates the USA’s dominant position in the research network. Nodes with high betweenness centrality are essential connectors, playing a pivotal role in linking otherwise disparate nodes within the network [ ]. From a scientometric perspective, a node’s betweenness centrality is also associated with its paper’s long-term citation potential, reflecting its enduring impact on the field [ ]. Nodes with betweenness centrality scores greater than 0.10 are considered to have high centrality, according to social network theory. These nodes often connect distinct clusters of research, thus playing a crucial role in facilitating communication and collaboration between various thematic areas [ , ]. For instance, a node linking two otherwise disconnected clusters holds significant value in maintaining the flow of information. If such a node is removed, information exchange within the network could be disrupted.
Japan follows with a betweenness centrality of 0.09, suggesting strong global interconnectedness. Germany (0.08), China (0.05), and Italy (0.05) also exhibit moderate centrality, while the UK (0.34) and France (0.17) demonstrate notable influence despite having fewer publications. Other countries show lower centrality values, indicating a more peripheral role within the research network.
Regarding institutions, Asia again leads, with notable contributions from Chinese, Japanese, and South Korean institutions. Asian institutions collectively contribute 1,040 publications (9.02%) of the total 11,527. Individually, Seoul National University leads with 142 publications (1.24%), followed by Vanderbilt University with 140 (1.21%), and Baylor College of Medicine and the University of Tokyo, each with 137 publications (1.19%). Other prominent institutions include the National Cancer Center (113; 0.98%), Shanghai Jiao Tong University (104; 0.90%), and Peking University (84; 0.73%).
In terms of betweenness centrality, Vanderbilt University ranks highest with a score of 0.12, signifying its influential role in the network. Baylor College of Medicine (0.09) and the University of Tokyo (0.07) also exhibit strong centrality, while other institutions such as the National Cancer Center and the University of Padua show moderate centrality, indicating their valuable contributions to global collaboration.

## Analysis of co-cited Journals
Scientific publications often reference other works, forming networks like co-citation [ ]. These networks are analyzed to uncover key properties of the research landscape, particularly the influence of scholars and journals. Co-citation patterns highlight how prior research has shaped publications in this field. Specifically, co-citation networks help identify the most influential journals by revealing where the most cited papers were published, which in turn form the foundation of the field’s knowledge structure.
The analysis of co-cited journals in gastric carcinogenesis research highlights the central role of high-impact journals in shaping the field. As presented in Table  , Gastroenterology (7,035 co-citations, 26.3%) and Gut (6,131, 23.1%) lead as the most influential Q1 journals, followed by The American Journal of Gastroenterology, Cancer Research, Lancet, and The New England Journal of Medicine, all with high citation frequencies and impact factors. These journals represent the core sources for multidisciplinary research spanning gastroenterology, oncology, and general medicine.

## Discussion

## General Information
This study represents the first comprehensive analysis of gastric carcinogenesis research employing a novel knowledge synthesis method coupled with cutting-edge scientometric techniques. Our innovative approach systematically maps research areas, analyzes scientific contexts within these domains, and assesses their temporal evolution over five decades. This methodology addresses fundamental limitations inherent in traditional systematic reviews and meta-analyses, which, while valuable, are time-consuming and resource-intensive approaches that cannot efficiently capture dynamic research development trends [ ]. In contrast, scientometrics integrated with visual analytics effectively unveils hidden patterns within extensive literature databases, providing unprecedented insights into field evolution [ , ].

## Publication Trends and Historical Evolution
The publishing trajectory in gastric carcinogenesis research, illustrated in Fig. , demonstrates a distinctive growth pattern characterized by gradual expansion punctuated by critical paradigm shifts that underscore the field’s evolution across five decades. The initial phase, spanning from 1970 to the mid-1980s, exhibited slow but steady publication growth that corresponded to foundational studies beginning to elucidate pathophysiological mechanisms underlying gastric cancer development. During this formative period, key etiological concepts emerged, particularly regarding environmental exposures and dietary factors such as alcohol consumption, excessive salt intake, red meat consumption, and nitrosamine exposure [ – ], alongside lifestyle factors including smoking [ ]. The prevailing hypothesis centered on dietary patterns characterized by high salt content and N-nitroso compounds, combined with micronutrient deficiencies from inadequate fresh fruit and vegetable consumption, which were believed to promote gastric mutagenesis through synergistic interactions with hypochlorhydria and bacterial overgrowth leading to enhanced nitrosamine formation [ – ].
The 1970 s marked a technological milestone with the widespread adoption of gastrointestinal endoscopy, which provided pathologists unprecedented opportunities to examine rapidly-fixed gastric tissue obtained through biopsies, thereby avoiding artifacts associated with ischemia and autolysis commonly encountered in surgical specimens. This technological advancement set the stage for subsequent groundbreaking discoveries that would fundamentally reshape the field.

## The H. Pylori Revolution
A paradigmatic shift occurred in 1981 when Robin Warren initiated the first systematic trial on patients selected by Barry Marshall. By 1983, their collaborative efforts culminated in the successful culture of spiral microorganisms from gastric mucosal biopsies obtained from patients with chronic active gastritis, marking the inaugural identification of Campylobacter pyloridis. This bacterium, initially named for its morphological resemblance to the Campylobacter genus, underwent subsequent taxonomic revisions following comprehensive genetic and morphological analyses that revealed its distinct phylogenetic position. In 1987, the organism was reclassified as Campylobacter pylori; however, by 1989, extensive DNA analysis including DNA–DNA hybridization and ribosomal RNA (rRNA) sequencing definitively established its unique genetic characteristics, leading to its official designation as H. pylori, the nomenclature retained today [ – ]. This taxonomic classification was supported by comprehensive molecular analyses encompassing 16 S and 23 S rRNA gene sequencing, fatty acid profiling, and mitochondrial chain component characterization, which collectively confirmed the bacterium’s distinct genetic identity compared to other Campylobacter species [ – ].
The recognition of H. pylori as a major etiological agent in gastric pathology, including gastritis, gastroduodenal ulcers, gastric cancer, and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma, fundamentally revolutionized therapeutic approaches to these conditions [ – ]. This discovery’s profound impact was internationally acknowledged when Marshall and Warren received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2005. Their groundbreaking work provided the foundation for subsequent investigations by Pelayo Correa, a Colombian pathologist whose research was particularly relevant given Colombia’s exceptionally high gastric cancer prevalence. Correa’s seminal contributions focused on H. pylori’s role in gastric carcinogenesis, culminating in his cascade model that traces disease progression from non-atrophic gastritis through atrophic gastritis, intestinal metaplasia, and dysplasia, ultimately culminating in adenocarcinoma—a framework that remains cornerstone to contemporary understanding of gastric carcinogenesis [ ].
The dramatic increase in publications from the late 1980 s directly correlates with this revolutionary identification of H. pylori as a central etiological factor, spurring intensive research into bacterial pathogenesis, host immune responses, and clinical implications of H. pylori eradication. These investigations not only advanced fundamental understanding of gastric carcinogenesis mechanisms but also catalyzed global interest in preventive strategies, including targeted antibiotic therapy and population-based screening programs.

## Contemporary Research Context and Comparative Analyses
Recent scientometric investigations have begun focusing on specific pathogen-host interactions within gastric carcinogenesis. Yang et al. [ ] conducted a comprehensive analysis of H. pylori-gastric cancer cross-talk spanning 2003–2022, analyzing 1,970 publications to identify research hotspots and emerging trends. Their study revealed China and Japan as leading research contributors, with Vanderbilt University emerging as the most productive institution, while identifying three major research clusters: H. pylori effects on gastric tumorigenesis, pathogenesis mechanisms of H. pylori-induced gastric cancer (bacterial factors), and host response mechanisms (host factors) [ ].
Notably, their analysis identified several emerging research areas including autophagy regulation, gut microbiota interactions beyond H. pylori, immunotherapy applications, exosome-mediated intercellular communication, epithelial-mesenchymal transition processes, and gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase functional roles in carcinogenesis [ ]. However, their focused analysis of H. pylori-gastric cancer interactions over two decades, while valuable, highlighted the necessity for broader temporal analyses capable of capturing the complete evolution of gastric carcinogenesis research. Such comprehensive analyses must include H. pylori-independent carcinogenic mechanisms and the historical development of carcinogenesis understanding across multiple decades to provide complete context for current research directions.
Our findings regarding evolving research priorities in gastric carcinogenesis across five decades can be effectively contextualized with recent pathogen-specific trend analyses. Eslami et al. [ ] reported declining H. pylori publication numbers since 2019, particularly following the COVID-19 pandemic’s impact on research priorities and funding, with annual scientific production decreasing from peaks observed during 2011–2018 [ ]. This temporal pattern partially aligns with our observation of shifting research focus in gastric carcinogenesis, where traditional infectious disease approaches, including H. pylori research, have been increasingly complemented by molecular mechanisms investigation, genetic factor analysis, and precision medicine approaches.
However, our longer-term analysis reveals that while H. pylori-specific research may be experiencing recent decline, overall gastric carcinogenesis research has maintained stability and continued growth through strategic diversification into genomics, immunotherapy development, and personalized treatment modality research. This diversification suggests field maturation rather than decline, with researchers building upon H. pylori foundations to explore more sophisticated carcinogenic mechanisms and therapeutic interventions.

## H. pylori-Specific Research Patterns
The research domain patterns identified in our comprehensive five-decade analysis complement and extend findings from pathogen-specific studies, particularly Eslami et al.‘s focused H. pylori analysis. Their study revealed that cancer biology research dominated research outputs (22% of publications), while clinical trials comprised only 3% of publications despite demonstrating high levels of international collaboration across research institutions [ ]. Our broader gastric carcinogenesis analysis reveals remarkably similar patterns, with fundamental research consistently dominating over clinical applications across all five decades studied, suggesting systemic challenges in translational research rather than recent developments [ ].
However, our expanded analytical scope reveals additional research domains not captured in pathogen-specific analyses, including environmental carcinogenesis studies, genetic susceptibility investigations, and emerging research areas such as comprehensive microbiome research extending far beyond H. pylori interactions [ ]. This finding suggests that while H. pylori remains a crucial and productive research focus, the gastric carcinogenesis field has substantially expanded to encompass multiple complementary research approaches that address the complex, multifactorial nature of gastric cancer development.

## Phases of Scientific Development
The period between 2009 and 2018 demonstrated a publication plateau that reflected a consolidation phase, during which the field expanded into molecular and genomic research domains. Advances in molecular profiling technologies, particularly next-generation sequencing platforms, facilitated deeper insights into genetic and epigenetic alterations driving gastric carcinogenesis. Concurrently, research on precancerous lesions, including intestinal metaplasia and atrophic gastritis, became increasingly sophisticated, with enhanced emphasis on early detection strategies and risk stratification methodologies.
This consolidation phase was further characterized by extensive utilization of mouse models designed to induce gastric metaplasia. These models enabled detailed investigations into the cellular origins and developmental fate of metaplastic epithelium while identifying specific cell populations responsible for gastric cancer development [ – ]. Such experimental approaches provided crucial mechanistic insights that bridged epidemiological observations with molecular understanding.
The surge in research output following 2018, culminating in a peak during 2022, signifies a transformative phase characterized by technological innovation and methodological advancement. This increase coincides with growing focus on sophisticated experimental models, including single-layered gastroids and complex gastric organoids incorporating inflammatory and stromal cellular components such as myofibroblasts. These advanced model systems enable detailed investigations into chronic inflammation and tissue injury mechanisms in H. pylori-induced gastric carcinogenesis, particularly focusing on pathogen interactions with gastric epithelium, inflammatory processes, epithelial repair mechanisms, and cancer initiation pathways [ , ].
Moreover, this period has witnessed intensive efforts to identify novel biomarkers through integration of cutting-edge technologies including single-cell RNA sequencing, complemented by significant advances in bioinformatics and systems biology approaches [ – ]. These technological innovations are providing unprecedented molecular insights into gastric carcinogenesis mechanisms, thereby establishing foundations for potential breakthroughs in early detection, risk stratification, and therapeutic intervention strategies. The sustained upward trend highlights the persistent global commitment to addressing gastric cancer, a disease that continues to exact a significant public health burden, particularly in high-incidence regions.

## Evolution of Histopathological Classification Systems
The co-cited references in gastric carcinogenesis research demonstrate the enduring influence of the Sydney System (1991, 1994) in shaping gastritis diagnosis and gastric cancer risk assessment [ , ]. The original Sydney System introduced a structured approach to histological classification that categorized gastritis into seven types based on etiology, anatomy, and morphology, while incorporating H. pylori infection as a key etiological factor [ ]. This framework gained widespread adoption following the landmark discovery of H. pylori in 1983.
Building upon this foundation, the 1994 Updated Sydney System refined the original classification by specifying standardized biopsy sample locations and introducing a Visual Analogue Scale to systematically grade inflammation, activity, atrophy, metaplasia, and H. pylori infection on a scale of 0–3 [ ]. These improvements significantly reduced diagnostic variability and enhanced consistency, establishing it as the most widely adopted framework for gastritis classification worldwide.
However, the Sydney System’s inadequate reflection of cancer risk, particularly in patients with chronic atrophic gastritis, catalyzed the development of the OLGA system. The OLGA system represents a significant advancement by improving histological staging for gastric atrophy and providing superior risk stratification for gastric cancer development. This system effectively identifies high-risk patients, particularly those over 50 years old with extensive atrophy and body intestinal metaplasia—both established risk factors for gastric cancer [ ]. Notably, OLGA combines both grading (intensity of inflammation) and staging (extent of atrophic-metaplastic changes), with staging serving as the primary determinant of cancer risk [ , ].
Despite these advancements, reliance on the Sydney System raises questions about whether newer molecular and biomarker-driven classifications could complement or even supersede these older paradigms. Molecules related to the metaplasia-dysplasia-adenocarcinoma sequence, such as trefoil factor 2, caudal type homeobox 2, and microRNA-124a-3p methylation, assist in histological diagnosis [ – ]. This evolution toward a multimodal diagnostic approach—combining laboratory tests, endoscopy, and histology—suggests that biomarker-stratified models may eventually complement or replace traditional frameworks like the Sydney System, with OLGA serving as a transitional stepping stone toward more comprehensive, molecularly driven diagnostic models.

## Pathophysiological Mechanisms: Gastric Atrophy and Carcinogenic Microenvironments
Our analysis highlights the critical significance of gastric atrophy and impaired acid secretion in creating cancer-prone microenvironments. Intestinal-type adenocarcinoma, representing the most common form of gastric cancer, typically evolves from a background of parietal cell loss (oxyntic atrophy) and subsequent metaplastic changes [ ]. The progression of these lesions, particularly at the antral-corpus border, leads to oxyntic atrophy characterized by pseudopyloric metaplasia, which can further transdifferentiate into intestinal metaplasia—a recognized focal precursor of gastric cancer [ ]. These pathological changes underscore the fundamental role of gastric mucosal atrophy and impaired acid secretion as critical drivers of the cancerization process [ ].
Beyond mucosal atrophy itself, our analysis also addresses hypergastrinemia, a condition resulting from anacidity that has been implicated in carcinogenesis, particularly in the context of H. pylori infection, autoimmune gastritis, and prolonged gastric acid inhibition. Elevated gastrin levels stimulate enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cell hyperplasia, which can progress to neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) and potentially advance to neuroendocrine carcinomas—a progression sequence observed in both rodent models and human patients [ – ]. Consequently, atrophy of the gastric corpus has emerged as a well-established pathological condition most strongly associated with increased gastric cancer risk, likely due to profound alterations in the intragastric environment. These alterations include impaired acid secretion, elevated pH, reduced ascorbic acid levels, and a diminished capacity to neutralize nitrites and other potential carcinogens.
However, the clinical and pathophysiological significance of atrophy varies considerably depending on its anatomical location. Oxyntic (corpus) atrophy, marked by the loss of parietal and chief cells, is more strongly linked to gastric cancer risk than isolated antral atrophy [ ]. Antral atrophy, though often associated with fibrosis and intestinal-type glands, can be challenging to detect in biopsies. Furthermore, antral atrophy without intestinal metaplasia is rarely diagnosed histologically, and its clinical relevance remains uncertain [ ]. Nevertheless, recent insights from mouse models suggest that antral atrophy characterized by gastrin-secreting cell (G cell) loss may represent a critical step in antral gastric carcinogenesis through a distinct mechanism. Specifically, G cell loss induces cholecystokinin B receptor (CCKBR)-positive stem cell populations to shift toward predominantly symmetric division, resulting in expansion and proliferation of the stem cell pool and subsequent accumulation of mutations [ ]. This mechanism aligns with emerging evidence from recent studies suggesting that, given the stochastic nature of DNA replication errors, cancer risk is proportional to the total number of stem cell divisions and, consequently, the total number of stem cells within a tissue [ , ]. The expansion of a stem cell population creates a larger pool of cells that can acquire additional genetic mutations, which may become unmasked during and following carcinogenic exposure. These mutant stem cells ultimately serve as cells of origin for dysplasia or cancer.
Thus, this paradigm shifts our understanding of gastric carcinogenesis from a purely histological progression to one fundamentally driven by the probabilistic accumulation of mutations within an expanded, metabolically active stem cell compartment. This stem cell-centric model provides a mechanistic explanation for why antral atrophy, despite being less overtly associated with cancer than corpus atrophy, can nonetheless contribute to carcinogenesis through alterations in stem cell dynamics and division patterns.
The distinction between antral and oxyntic atrophy reflects fundamentally different pathophysiological mechanisms in gastric carcinogenesis. While corpus atrophy creates a carcinogenic microenvironment primarily through alterations in gastric physiology and the chemical milieu, antral atrophy may promote tumorigenesis through dysregulation of stem cell dynamics. This parallel carcinogenic pathway underscores the complexity of gastric carcinogenesis beyond the traditional Correa cascade of intestinal-type adenocarcinoma development.

The progression of gastric carcinogenesis from metaplasia to dysplasia and ultimately to adenocarcinoma is a complex process shaped by intricate interactions within the stromal microenvironment. Recent research has revealed the critical role of stromal components, including fibroblasts, telocytes, extracellular matrix (ECM), and immune cells, in regulating this transformation. The stromal microenvironment is not merely a passive support system but actively participates in shaping the trajectory of epithelial transformation. Metaplasia, which arises as an adaptive response to chronic injury, serves as the initial step in this cascade. In the gastric context, spasmolytic polypeptide-expressing metaplasia (SPEM) and intestinal metaplasia are the primary metaplastic phenotypes, each associated with distinct stromal dynamics. SPEM develops as a protective response to gastric injury, where parietal cell loss triggers the reprogramming of gastric lineages. However, prolonged inflammatory conditions, such as those induced by H. pylori infection, lead to the evolution of SPEM into incomplete intestinal metaplasia, a state highly susceptible to dysplasia and gastric cancer [ , ].
Fibroblasts play a central role in the stromal microenvironment, and their dynamic plasticity enables them to adapt and influence the evolving epithelial landscape. In the normal gastric mucosa, fibroblasts, particularly platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha (PDGFRA)-expressing telocyte-like cells, provide critical support to epithelial stem cells through the secretion of growth factors such as bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) and R-spondin proteins. However, during metaplasia, these fibroblasts expand and undergo transcriptional reprogramming to sustain the altered epithelial environment [ ]. Recent studies show that fibroblasts near the gland base secrete BMP antagonists like gremlin 1 (Grem1), which facilitate epithelial dedifferentiation and reprogramming [ ]. This interaction is pivotal in maintaining the metaplastic niche, enabling epithelial cells to proliferate and adapt to injury. As the microenvironment evolves, fibroblasts transform into cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), which exhibit functional heterogeneity and actively promote dysplastic progression [ , ]. Subsets of CAFs characterized by markers such as alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and PDGFRA drive ECM remodeling, stiffening the stromal environment and enhancing mechanotransduction pathways like the piezo-type mechanosensitive ion channel component 1 (Piezo1)-Yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1) axis [ ]. This signaling cascade integrates mechanical cues from the stiffened ECM into oncogenic transcriptional programs, perpetuating epithelial proliferation and further activating fibroblasts. Such feedback loops highlight the reciprocal relationship between the stroma and epithelium in advancing gastric carcinogenesis.
Telocytes, a relatively newly identified stromal cell type, have emerged as critical regulators during the early stages of gastric metaplasia. These cells are characterized by their small cell bodies and long cytoplasmic projections, which allow them to interact extensively with neighboring cells. During metaplasia, telocytes migrate to the gland base and dynamically expand their population in response to environmental cues. They secrete signaling molecules such as Wnt family member 5 A (Wnt5a), bone morphogenetic protein 4 (BMP4), and bone morphogenetic protein 7 (BMP7), which are essential for maintaining the metaplastic niche and supporting epithelial reprogramming [ ]. The recruitment of telocytes to the metaplastic niche underscores their pivotal role in regulating epithelial-stromal interactions and coordinating tissue repair and adaptation.
Chronic inflammation, driven by H. pylori, further amplifies the stromal contribution to carcinogenesis. The bacterium activates the nuclear factor kappa B pathway in fibroblasts, leading to the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and growth factors that reinforce epithelial proliferation and stromal remodeling. The interplay between fibroblasts and immune cells, such as M2 macrophages and type 2 innate lymphoid cells, creates a pro-tumorigenic inflammatory milieu. These immune cells secrete factors like interleukin-13 and transforming growth factor-beta, which modulate fibroblast activation and enhance ECM remodeling [ – ]. This inflammatory feedback loop perpetuates chronic damage, facilitating the transition from metaplasia to dysplasia. Dysplasia represents a disordered state characterized by increased cellular disorganization and glandular distortion. Fibroblasts in the dysplastic microenvironment further promote this progression by secreting cytokines and ECM proteins that disrupt epithelial differentiation and enhance oncogenic signaling pathways. CAFs, for instance, secrete connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), a factor that stiffens the ECM and exacerbates mechanical stress, driving oncogenic mechanotransduction via the Piezo1-YAP1 axis [ ].
The stromal microenvironment also regulates epithelial transitions through ECM remodeling. CAFs deposit and remodel ECM proteins, creating a stiffened matrix that acts as a physical barrier and a signaling hub. This stiffened ECM not only promotes epithelial invasion but also enhances the recruitment and activation of additional stromal cells, including fibroblasts and immune cells. Such dynamic remodeling creates a permissive environment for carcinogenesis, where epithelial cells are subjected to continuous mechanical and biochemical stimuli that drive their malignant transformation.
Telocytes and fibroblasts, along with immune cells and ECM components, form a highly dynamic and interactive network within the gastric stroma, collectively orchestrating the progression from metaplasia to dysplasia and adenocarcinoma. This complex interplay underscores the need to target stromal components therapeutically. Disrupting the interactions between CAFs and epithelial cells, blocking ECM remodeling pathways such as the PIEZO1-YAP1 axis, or modulating telocyte recruitment to metaplastic niches could offer novel strategies for preventing the progression of precancerous lesions. Furthermore, reprogramming the immune microenvironment to suppress chronic inflammation and targeting fibroblast-secreted factors like CTGF may provide additional therapeutic avenues. Future research should aim to delineate the precise molecular mechanisms underlying stromal-epithelial crosstalk and explore the potential of targeting stromal elements to halt or reverse gastric carcinogenesis. Several key questions remain in the understanding of the stromal-epithelial interactions in gastric carcinogenesis: What are the precise mechanisms through which telocytes interact with CAFs and epithelial cells? Can specific fibroblast subsets be selectively targeted without disrupting normal tissue homeostasis? How does the stromal microenvironment evolve during late-stage carcinogenesis, and how can it be reversed?
Development of novel in vitro organoid models for studying gastric physiology and precancers.
Organoid technology has become a revolutionary tool for studying gastric physiology and precancerous lesions, particularly gastric intestinal metaplasia, a key precursor to gastric cancer. Intestinal metaplasia involves the trans-differentiation of gastric epithelial cells into intestinal-like cells, often triggered by chronic inflammation, environmental factors, and genetic alterations. These transitions are complex, involving changes in transcriptional programs, cellular plasticity, and epigenetic regulation. Organoid models, derived from human-induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) or primary gastric glands, replicate the structural and functional characteristics of native gastric tissue, providing a physiologically relevant platform to dissect the molecular mechanisms underlying intestinal metaplasia and its progression to dysplasia.
Organoids have been instrumental in modeling the transcriptional reprogramming that occurs during intestinal metaplasia. Studies have shown that overexpression of caudal-related homeobox protein 2, a master regulator of intestinal differentiation, induces the expression of intestinal markers such as mucin 2 and suppresses gastric markers like mucin 5AC in hiPSC-derived gastric organoids [ ]. This reprogramming mirrors the phenotypic changes seen in intestinal metaplasia, though the intestinal phenotype achieved in vitro remains incomplete, suggesting the involvement of additional factors. Furthermore, exposure to nitrosoguanidine, a genotoxic agent, has provided a chemical model to induce intestinal metaplasia in mouse organoids by activating the rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog signaling pathway [ ]. These studies underscore the role of both transcriptional regulators and environmental carcinogens in driving the early events of intestinal metaplasia. Similarly, inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-13, introduced through co-culture with type 2 innate lymphoid cells, promoted metaplastic maturation and proliferation via the signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 pathway [ ].​.
Epigenetic alterations are increasingly recognized as critical drivers of intestinal metaplasia, and organoid models have provided insights into these changes. For instance, aberrant DNA methylation, induced by factors such as nitric oxide synthase 2, has been shown to silence tumor suppressor genes and promote the development of intestinal phenotypes [ ]. Such epigenetic instability highlights the early molecular events that predispose gastric epithelial cells to carcinogenesis. Moreover, single-cell transcriptomic analyses of gastric organoids have revealed hybrid gastric-intestinal phenotypes during the transition to intestinal metaplasia, suggesting that metaplasia involves a spectrum of lineage plasticity rather than a binary switch [ ]. Cycling isthmus stem cells, marked by stathmin 1 expression, have been identified as a potential origin of metaplasia, further highlighting the role of stem cell dynamics in this process [ ].
The plasticity observed in gastric stem cells extends beyond metaplasia, as demonstrated in organoid models of dysplasia and early gastric cancer. Organoids engineered with clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats/CRISPR-associated protein 9 to harbor mutations in genes such as AT-rich interaction domain 1 A (ARID1A) have recapitulated the chromatin remodeling events associated with tumorigenesis [ ]. Loss of ARID1A has been linked to increased mutational burden and the activation of oncogenic pathways, including wingless-related integration site (Wnt)/β-catenin and forkhead box M1 [ ]. Early mutations in TP53, a tumor suppressor gene, significantly influence the development of gastric dysplasia. Organoids derived from Trp53-mutant mouse models showed that TP53 deletion confers a selective advantage to dysplastic cells when exposed to environmental carcinogens such as nitrosamines and deoxycholic acid [ ]. These findings illustrate how genetic alterations synergize with environmental factors to accelerate the progression from intestinal metaplasia to dysplasia and beyond. Additionally, dysplastic organoids have revealed significant cellular heterogeneity, including the emergence of cancer stem cell-like populations with enhanced clonogenic potential and resistance to targeted therapies [ ]. Collectively, such models highlight the critical importance of identifying therapeutic vulnerabilities in early-stage lesions before malignant transformation becomes irreversible.
Importantly, these new findings indicate that although the Correa cascade—the stepwise progression from chronic inflammation to atrophy, metaplasia, dysplasia, and ultimately cancer—remains a foundational framework for understanding gastric carcinogenesis, recent data challenge the notion of strictly linear progression. Emerging evidence demonstrates that key oncogenic mutations, such as Ras activation, can redirect stem cells and progenitors toward dysplasia, suggesting that metaplasia (including SPEM or intestinal metaplasia) and dysplasia may arise through parallel rather than sequential pathways [ , ]. This revised paradigm proposes that the dysplasia-cancer sequence emerges as a distinct lineage, potentially independent from metaplastic differentiation (whether goblet cell metaplasia or SPEM), and driven primarily by mutations in stem cells that lead to aberrant gastric stem cell activation and clonal expansion. This mechanistic framework fundamentally shifts our understanding from a rigid sequential model to one that recognizes parallel, mutation-driven pathways of carcinogenesis, with stem cell behavior and molecular alterations serving as the central organizing principles rather than histological progression alone. According to this stem cell-centric model, gastric metaplasia and dysplasia can be reconceptualized as a spectrum of stem cell and progenitor cell diseases wherein precancerous states arise when undifferentiated gastric stem cells and progenitors respond to chronic inflammatory conditions. This response triggers a cascade of pathological events: stem cell expansion, genomic evolution, selection for clones harboring cancer-associated mutations, genomic instability, aberrant differentiation reprogramming, oncogene activation, and eventual progression toward either differentiated metaplasia or dysplasia [ ].
The limitations of existing preventive strategies underscore the urgent clinical need for this paradigm shift. Currently, no therapeutic options are available to prevent the progression of gastric mucosa to atrophy and subsequent precancerous metaplastic or dysplastic transformation, with the notable exception of H. pylori eradication. However, even this intervention demonstrates significant limitations in advanced disease stages. In patients with stage I gastric cancer who have undergone endoscopic submucosal dissection, the risk of developing metachronous gastric cancer from residual metaplastic tissues persists at 2–5% per year, even following successful H. pylori eradication therapy [ , , ]. This sobering statistic highlights that cancer risk remains substantially elevated despite elimination of the primary infectious trigger [ ]. Such a perspective has profound implications for risk stratification, surveillance strategies, and the development of targeted interventions aimed at disrupting carcinogenesis at its earliest molecular stages.
These clinical realities necessitate a more sophisticated therapeutic approach that targets the underlying stem cell pathology rather than merely addressing external causative factors. Future therapeutic strategies may prove more effective if they aim to specifically regulate aberrantly expanding mutated stem-cell-derived clones and reprogram these cells—whether bona fide progenitors or mature differentiated cells undergoing injury-induced transdifferentiation through paligenosis—prior to their progression to dysplasia and malignancy [ ]. The therapeutic ideal would thus involve interventions capable of restoring normal epithelial lineages derived from physiologically functional progenitor populations and reserve stem cell populations while concurrently arresting the progression of gastric atrophy and metaplasia [ – ]. Achieving this ambitious goal requires developing a more comprehensive understanding of the complex interplay between inflammatory mediators, genetic alterations, and the dynamic gastric microenvironment that governs stem cell fate decisions and clonal evolution.
In this context, organoid models have proven invaluable in the identification of novel therapeutic targets for gastric cancer prevention. Glycomic profiling has shown that impaired glycosylation of gastric mucins, such as mucin 6, promotes gastric tumorigenesis by inducing Golgi stress and activating mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling [ ]. This pathway has been proposed as a therapeutic target to mitigate the progression of precancerous lesions. Similarly, targeting the signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 pathway, which drives tumorigenesis in kelch-like family member 21-deficient gastric organoids, has shown promise in reducing the progression from intestinal metaplasia to dysplasia [ ]. These insights demonstrate the translational potential of organoid models in developing targeted therapies that address the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying gastric cancer prevention.
In addition to modeling disease progression, organoids have been used to study host-pathogen interactions, particularly with H. pylori, a major risk factor for intestinal metaplasia and gastric cancer. Organoids infected with H. pylori have revealed how the pathogen disrupts epithelial integrity, delays tissue repair, and induces inflammatory responses. These systems have shown that H. pylori infection activates gastric stem cells, expanding progenitor pools and accelerating metaplastic changes [ ]. They have also provided evidence of oxidative DNA damage caused by H. pylori, further linking chronic infection to genomic instability and cancer initiation. Such findings underscore the value of organoids in uncovering the mechanisms of inflammation-driven gastric carcinogenesis.
Despite their transformative potential, organoid models have limitations. They primarily replicate epithelial components and lack the stromal, immune, and vascular interactions essential for recreating the full complexity of the gastric microenvironment. Emerging co-culture systems that integrate fibroblasts, immune cells, and endothelial cells aim to address these gaps. Additionally, the establishment of organoid biobanks representing diverse genetic backgrounds and disease stages will enhance the translational utility of these models. Advances in organoid-on-a-chip technology, which simulates tissue-tissue interactions, promise to further refine the physiological relevance of organoid systems.
Gastric organoids have revolutionized the study of gastric metaplasia, providing an unparalleled platform to investigate the transcriptional, epigenetic, and cellular changes underlying precancerous lesions. By enabling the modeling of genetic, environmental, and inflammatory factors, organoids have advanced our understanding of gastric disease progression and opened new avenues for therapeutic discovery. As the field continues to evolve, innovations in organoid engineering and integration with personalized medicine approaches hold immense promise for transforming the prevention and treatment of gastric cancer.
Advanced endoscopic techniques for identifying high-risk gastric lesions
The detection and characterization of high-risk gastric lesions, including precancerous conditions such as atrophic gastritis, intestinal metaplasia, and dysplasia, are critical for improving gastric cancer prognosis. Advances in endoscopic imaging and AI have revolutionized this field, addressing challenges such as identifying subtle lesions, minimizing diagnostic variability, and enabling personalized surveillance. These innovations are reshaping clinical practice, ensuring earlier detection and improved outcomes.
The integration of AI in endoscopy is one of the most transformative developments. AI-driven systems, such as ENdoscopy as AI-powered Device Computer-Aided Diagnosis for Gastroscopy (ENAD CAD-G), have demonstrated superior diagnostic accuracy compared to traditional biopsy results, particularly for early gastric cancer (EGC) and dysplasia. With diagnostic accuracies exceeding 90% for EGC and 88% for dysplasia, these systems provide standardized lesion classification and support endoscopists in detecting conditions requiring resection [ ]. Convolutional neural network models have also shown exceptional accuracy in detecting intestinal metaplasia in the gastric corpus using endoscopic image patches, leveraging advanced imaging techniques like virtual chromoendoscopy and blue-light imaging [ ]. These AI-driven tools enhance diagnostic precision, reduce false negatives, and optimize routine clinical workflows.
Another innovative AI application is the AITonguequiry multimodality model, which integrates non-invasive tools like tongue image analysis with clinical inquiry data to identify patients at risk for precancerous gastric lesions [ ]. This hierarchical prediction strategy offers an efficient, cost-effective approach to pre-endoscopic risk stratification, particularly valuable in large-scale population studies and resource-limited settings. Such advancements in AI are not only enhancing diagnostic performance but also expanding access to early gastric cancer detection.
In parallel, imaging modalities such as narrow-band imaging, chromoendoscopy, and confocal laser endomicroscopy have significantly enhanced mucosal visualization. Narrow-band imaging and blue-light imaging provide enhanced differentiation of intestinal metaplasia by highlighting abnormal vascularization and mucosal patterns, improving the detection of subtle precancerous changes. Confocal laser endomicroscopy further advances diagnostic accuracy by providing real-time, cellular-level imaging, allowing histological assessment during endoscopic procedures [ , ]. These tools, particularly when integrated with AI, offer unparalleled sensitivity and specificity in lesion detection and characterization.
Innovative techniques like the “power-wash” high-pressure spray method have emerged as valuable tools for sampling gastric mucosa. This endoscopy-guided approach improves the detection of patchy lesions such as intestinal metaplasia and dysplasia by enabling large-scale cytological sampling while reducing the bias associated with random biopsies [ ]. It represents a significant step toward more reliable and comprehensive gastric cancer risk assessments. The OLGA and Operative Link on Gastric Intestinal Metaplasia Assessment systems further refine cancer risk stratification by staging intestinal metaplasia and atrophic gastritis based on their extent, facilitating more targeted biopsies and better clinical decision-making [ ].
Surveillance of patients post-H. pylori eradication has also benefited from these advancements. Despite successful eradication, patients with residual intestinal metaplasia or inflammation remain at risk for gastric cancer [ ]. Subtle endoscopic features, such as map-like redness and irregular vascular patterns, are now more easily detected with high-definition endoscopy and image-enhanced endoscopy, significantly improving diagnostic accuracy and enabling earlier intervention. These techniques are proving vital for reducing mortality in this high-risk group.
Endoscopic submucosal dissection and endoscopic mucosal resection are the primary therapeutic options for early gastric cancer and high-grade dysplasia [ ]. Advances in imaging technologies, combined with AI-driven algorithms, have enhanced lesion margin delineation, improving the success rates of en bloc resections. Additionally, personalized medicine approaches are being developed, integrating AI with molecular diagnostics to tailor surveillance intervals and therapeutic strategies based on individual risk factors such as H. pylori status, genetic predisposition, and lesion-specific imaging features.
Despite these transformative advancements, challenges remain. The high costs of advanced imaging tools and the technological complexity of AI integration limit accessibility in low-resource settings. Additionally, variability in operator expertise and the need for large, diverse training datasets for AI models highlight areas for further development. Future research should focus on making these technologies more accessible, standardizing training protocols, and enhancing AI models by incorporating genetic, environmental, and molecular co-factors to improve predictive accuracy.
The application of advanced endoscopic techniques, supported by AI and innovative imaging modalities, represents a paradigm shift in gastric cancer prevention and early diagnosis. These technologies offer unprecedented diagnostic precision, enabling the early detection of precancerous and cancerous lesions, significantly improving patient outcomes. As AI tools become more robust, imaging technologies more accessible, and personalized approaches more integrated into routine practice, the prevention and treatment of gastric cancer will continue to advance, reducing the global burden of this disease.
Biomarkers for early detection of high-risk gastric lesions and early gastric cancer.
The identification and characterization of biomarkers for gastric cancer and its precursors have become pivotal in contemporary cancer research, driven by the imperative to enhance early detection, improve prognostic stratification, and develop targeted therapeutic interventions. Our five-decade analysis provides crucial context for understanding specialized research achievements highlighted in demographic-specific studies, such as the young patient biomarker analysis by Azbergenov et al. [ ]. The recognition that young gastric cancer patients exhibit distinct clinical and molecular characteristics represents a significant evolution in gastric carcinogenesis research from population-based approaches to personalized, demographic-specific investigations.
The age-specific research patterns identified by Azbergenov et al. [ ] demonstrate how fundamental carcinogenesis research has enabled recognition of population-specific disease mechanisms. Their finding that young patients more frequently exhibit undifferentiated and diffuse tumor types, germline mutations—particularly CDH1—and aggressive disease behavior reflects decades of research that progressively identified the molecular and genetic factors underlying these disease patterns.
The biomarker research trends identified in young patient studies directly correspond to technological and conceptual developments documented in our broader analysis. Azbergenov et al. [ ] found increasing focus on immunohistochemistry, next-generation sequencing, and molecular markers such as PD-L1, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), and microsatellite instability status, with particular emphasis on prognosis (22% of research focus) and diagnostic applications. The emergence of these research priorities reflects the technological revolution in gastric carcinogenesis research documented over five decades, demonstrating progression from morphological assessment to molecular profiling and from population-based studies to personalized medicine approaches.
The integration of diagnostic technologies with age-specific research reflects the evolution from descriptive studies to mechanistic understanding to clinical application. The emphasis on tumor microenvironment, apoptosis, and molecular mechanisms in young patient research builds directly upon decades of basic carcinogenesis research that identified these pathways as crucial for understanding disease development and progression. Consequently, these specialized demographic investigations reveal important therapeutic implications, as Azbergenov et al. [ ] noted that despite more aggressive disease characteristics, young patients often demonstrate better 5-year survival rates than older patients, highlighting the complexity of age-related disease factors.
Gastric cancer evolves through a multistep process characterized by gradual progression of normal gastric mucosa through stages of atrophic gastritis, intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, and eventually adenocarcinoma. Each stage is marked by distinct molecular alterations, and understanding these changes provides critical opportunities for early intervention. At the molecular level, gastric carcinogenesis involves a spectrum of gene expression changes, epigenetic modifications, and protein dysregulations that reflect underlying pathological processes.
The overexpression of Regenerating Islet-Derived Protein 4 (Reg IV) has been closely linked to intestinal metaplasia and gastric adenocarcinoma, with its presence across both neoplastic and non-neoplastic tissues, coupled with correlation to intestinal and neuroendocrine differentiation, positioning it as a valuable biomarker for both diagnostic and prognostic purposes [ ]. Similarly, the loss of sonic hedgehog expression has been identified as a pivotal event in the transition from H. pylori-induced atrophic gastritis to intestinal metaplasia and ultimately gastric cancer, highlighting its role in the disruption of normal mucosal differentiation [ ]. Pancreatic and Duodenal Homeobox 1, another key biomarker, is prominently expressed in intestinal metaplasia and reflects the adoption of a duodenal lineage in precancerous lesions, making it a critical marker of early carcinogenesis [ ]. In addition, the transcriptomic profiling of gastric mucosa has demonstrated the staged progression from chronic gastritis to cancer. Axis Inhibition Protein 2 (Axin 2), a critical regulator of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, is highly expressed in intestinal metaplasia and gastric cancer tissues, strongly correlating with nuclear β-catenin localization, a hallmark of Wnt pathway activation [ ]. Such findings suggest the utility of Axin 2 as a biomarker for early cancer detection in intestinal metaplasia patients.
The loss or alteration of key proteins also provides insight into disease progression. The polymeric immunoglobulin receptor has emerged as a marker of poor prognosis, with its absence correlating with increased aggressiveness and metastatic potential in gastric and gastroesophageal junction cancers [ ]. Urokinase-Type Plasminogen Activator Receptor, another important biomarker, is strongly associated with invasiveness and metastatic capacity, particularly in early stages of carcinogenesis [ ]. The co-expression of H. pylori Cag Pathogenicity Islands and Matrix Metalloproteinase-7 (MMP-7) highlights a compelling interaction between microbial and host factors, with MMP-7 playing a critical role in facilitating epithelial invasion [ ]. The transcription factor Muscle, Intestine, and Stomach Expression 1, a marker of healthy chief cells, is notably absent in metaplasia, dysplasia, and gastric cancer, further underscoring the role of cellular reprogramming and lineage plasticity in gastric tumorigenesis [ ]. Studies have also highlighted the downregulation of acidic mammalian chitinase as a marker for corpus atrophy, a condition strongly associated with gastric cancer development [ ]. Similarly, DNA methyltransferase 1 expression patterns have been implicated in the progression from metaplasia to dysplasia, suggesting its potential as a biomarker for early neoplastic transitions [ ]. The overexpression of Trophoblast Cell Surface Antigen 2, a glycoprotein associated with the Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase/Extracellular Signal-Regulated Kinase signaling pathway, marks the transition from metaplasia to dysplasia and is strongly linked to gastric tumorigenesis [ ]. Its role in promoting dysplastic cell behaviors further underscores its significance as both a diagnostic and therapeutic target.
Emerging research has emphasized the value of combined biomarker panels for robust risk stratification. The co-expression of multiple metaplasia-associated biomarkers, such as Mucin 13, Cadherin 17, Olfactomedin 4, Keratin 20, and Regenerating Islet-Derived Protein 4, has demonstrated strong prognostic potential [ ]. The absence of multiple biomarkers within this panel correlates with more aggressive disease and poorer outcomes, particularly in advanced-stage cancers. Such multiplexed approaches enable more precise identification of high-risk individuals and guide personalized clinical management.
The integration of these findings into non-invasive diagnostic strategies has been a transformative development in gastric cancer detection. Liquid biopsy techniques, including the analysis of circulating nucleic acids, extracellular vesicles, and proteins, provide practical alternatives to traditional endoscopic biopsies. DNA methylation patterns, particularly hypermethylation of tumor suppressor gene CpG islands, have emerged as reliable molecular signatures for early cancer detection [ – ]. Similarly, microRNAs and long noncoding RNAs found in blood or gastric washes offer robust biomarkers for identifying early preneoplastic changes. Plasma-based biomarker panels that combine traditional markers like pepsinogen (PG I/II ratios) with novel protein candidates such as Trefoil Factor 3 have demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity in detecting chronic atrophic gastritis, a key precursor to gastric cancer [ ].
Advances in proteomics have further expanded the repertoire of diagnostic biomarkers. Proteins like osteopontin, cancer antigen 125, and carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 have been identified as part of circulating panels, with their inclusion significantly enhancing diagnostic accuracy [ ]. Additionally, innovative technologies such as surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) have enabled highly sensitive detection of gastric lesions using non-invasive gastric fluid sampling [ ]. By distinguishing between stages such as non-atrophic gastritis, intestinal metaplasia, and dysplasia, SERS offers a valuable tool for early detection and monitoring of high-risk lesions.
Despite significant progress, challenges remain in validating biomarkers across diverse populations and translating these findings into standardized clinical assays. Variability in biomarker performance across geographic and ethnic groups underscores the need for population-specific studies to ensure broader applicability. Furthermore, integrating biomarker-based diagnostics into routine clinical workflows requires advancements in cost-effectiveness, accessibility, and regulatory approval processes. Leveraging machine learning and AI to analyze multimodal biomarker data in real-time may address some of these challenges, facilitating more accurate risk stratification and early intervention.
The exploration of biomarkers for high-risk gastric lesions and gastric cancer represents a dynamic and rapidly advancing field with immense potential to transform diagnostics and patient care. By integrating molecular, proteomic, and imaging biomarkers into comprehensive diagnostic platforms, this research direction offers hope for more effective prevention and management of gastric cancer. Future efforts should focus on expanding biomarker panels, validating findings in diverse cohorts, and refining non-invasive techniques to maximize their clinical utility. As these innovations continue to mature, they hold the promise of reshaping the landscape of gastric cancer care, ultimately improving outcomes for high-risk patients worldwide.
The use of traditional herbal medicine in reversing or preventing precancerous lesion development
The use of traditional herbal medicine to prevent or reverse the development of precancerous gastric lesions is a promising and evolving research direction. By integrating centuries of empirical knowledge with modern scientific insights, herbal medicine offers a multifaceted strategy to manage gastric precancerous conditions. The ability of herbal medicine to target multiple cellular pathways makes it an increasingly valuable tool in the chemoprevention of gastric cancer.
Recent research highlights the therapeutic potential of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) formulations in addressing precancerous gastric lesions, with various studies demonstrating how specific combinations of herbal compounds can modulate key biological processes associated with gastric carcinogenesis. These processes include inflammation, oxidative stress, and EMT—all of which play crucial roles in the gastric tumorigenesis.
Formulations such as Weifuchun, Sijunzi decoction, and Jianpi Yiqi therapy have been validated in clinical trials for their effectiveness in managing precancerous gastric lesions [ – ]. Meta-analyses of these studies show superior efficacy compared to conventional therapies in improving H. pylori eradication rates, endoscopic outcomes, and symptom relief [ ]. These formulations work through anti-inflammatory, antioxidative, and antiproliferative pathways and also enhance systemic immune regulation, ultimately reducing the risk of gastric cancer progression.
Weierning, a patented TCM formulation, has demonstrated significant promise in alleviating chronic atrophic gastritis and intestinal metaplasia, which are common precursors to gastric cancer [ ]. Weierning works by suppressing gastric mucosal cell apoptosis and inhibiting the Hedgehog signaling pathway, which is critical for gastric cell regeneration. In clinical studies, Weierning has been shown to reduce inflammatory cytokines like interleukin-1 beta and tumor necrosis factor-alpha, while restoring molecular markers of intestinal metaplasia (mucin 2, caudal-related homeobox transcription factor 2), thereby stabilizing the gastric mucosal environment and reducing cancer risk [ ]. Other formulations, such as Weifuchun and Moluo Dan, have demonstrated synergistic effects in clinical and preclinical settings [ , ]. These formulations alleviate symptoms of chronic atrophic gastritis, reduce intestinal metaplasia, and prevent dysplasia by modulating inflammatory cytokines and enhancing antioxidant defenses. Randomized controlled trials have shown improvements in histopathological outcomes and reductions in gastric lesion progression, validating their clinical relevance [ – ].
Herbal formulations, as well as individual herbal compounds or extracts, have demonstrated considerable therapeutic potential in reversing or preventing precancerous gastric lesions. These compounds often exert their effects by targeting specific molecular pathways involved in gastric carcinogenesis.
For instance, Tanshinone I, derived from Salvia miltiorrhiza, has been shown to prevent gastric carcinogenesis by modulating the p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 signaling pathway, a critical regulator of EMT, which plays a pivotal role in tumor progression [ ]. Tanshinone I reduces inflammation, restores epithelial integrity by upregulating E-cadherin, and suppresses N-cadherin, thereby preventing the carcinogenic transformation of the gastric mucosa [ ].
Similarly, polysaccharides from Codonopsis pilosula regulate the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, a key driver of gastric precancerous lesion progression [ ]. These polysaccharides promote apoptosis in abnormal gastric cells and inhibit Wnt/β-catenin activation under oxidative stress, helping stabilize the gastric environment and prevent cancer progression [ ].
Ginsenoside Rg1, a component of ginseng, mitigates chronic atrophic gastritis by inhibiting pyroptosis through the nuclear factor-kappa B/NOD-like receptor pyrin domain-containing protein 3/gasdermin D pathway [ ]. This action reduces inflammation and oxidative stress while preserving gastric mucosal integrity. Likewise, 18β-glycyrrhetinic acid, derived from licorice, targets mitochondrial energy metabolism and modulates the toll-like receptor 2 signaling pathway, suppressing inflammation and proliferation associated with H. pylori-induced gastritis, a major contributor to gastric lesions [ ].
Crocin, extracted from saffron, offers protective effects by regulating the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2/Hippo signaling pathway, preventing the malignant transformation of gastric epithelial cells and inhibiting EMT [ ]. This compound not only reduces cell proliferation but also promotes apoptosis, positioning it as a promising agent for early-stage intervention in gastric cancer [ ].
In addition to their direct effects on cellular pathways, herbal compounds also demonstrate significant immunomodulatory properties. For instance, paramylon from Euglena gracilis reduces CD3-positive lymphocyte infiltration and downregulates inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-11 and C-C motif chemokine ligand 2, in mouse models of gastric dysplasia. These effects suggest a stabilizing role in the gastric microenvironment, helping to prevent dysplastic changes [ ].
Curcumin, a bioactive compound in turmeric, inhibits the nuclear factor kappa B signaling pathway, reducing the expression of pro-inflammatory mediators such as interleukin-6, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, and cyclooxygenase-2 [ ]. This suppression of inflammation results in reduced oxidative stress, as evidenced by decreased levels of 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), a marker of DNA damage, in animal models. Such findings are particularly relevant in contexts where dietary and environmental factors exacerbate gastric epithelial injury, as seen in studies involving hypertonic sodium chloride and N-methyl-N-nitrosourea exposure [ ].
Similarly, canolol, a phenolic compound derived from canola oil, demonstrates strong antioxidative and anti-inflammatory effects by targeting the cyclooxygenase-2/prostaglandin E2 pathway [ ]. In animal models, canolol reduced the expression of cyclooxygenase-2 and other inflammatory mediators, such as interleukin-1 beta, and suppressed tumor initiation and progression. These effects were further linked to the reactivation of tumor-suppressor microRNAs, such as miR-7, which inhibit cyclooxygenase-2 and reduce tumor-promoting inflammation [ ].
While the potential of TCM in managing precancerous gastric lesions is clear, there are several challenges that need to be addressed for clinical translation. The variability in herbal composition due to geographical and environmental factors complicates the standardization of herbal medicines for clinical use. Moreover, there is a need for rigorous clinical trials to validate the efficacy and safety of these herbal interventions across diverse populations.
Future research should focus on refining the molecular understanding of herbal compounds, optimizing dosages, and conducting large-scale, multicenter trials to validate findings. Advanced tools such as bioinformatics and omics technologies will enhance the precision of TCM-based interventions, enabling the identification of bioactive compounds, understanding their mechanisms of action, and developing personalized approaches to gastric cancer prevention and treatment.

## Limitations
While scientometric analysis offers valuable insights into research trends, several inherent methodological limitations must be acknowledged that may impact the interpretation of our findings. Language bias represents a primary concern, as this study predominantly included English-language publications indexed in major databases such as Web of Science, Scopus, and PubMed. Consequently, this approach may have inadvertently excluded relevant studies published in other languages, potentially limiting the global representativeness of our analysis.
Citation behavior presents another significant source of potential bias in our findings. High citation counts often reflect academic influence rather than direct clinical relevance, and practices such as self-citation or preferential citation within established research networks can artificially inflate specific metrics [ ]. Additionally, database coverage limitations may compromise the comprehensiveness of our analysis, as certain journals and conference proceedings might not be indexed in the selected databases. Furthermore, citation time-lag effects could result in the underrepresentation of recent publications, as their academic impact may not yet have fully materialized over the study period.
Our comprehensive five-decade analysis should be interpreted alongside several complementary research approaches that provide deeper insights into specific aspects of gastric carcinogenesis research. Regional scientometric studies, such as the Central and Eastern Europe/Russian and Central Asian analysis by Begum et al. [ ], reveal important local factors including national research policies, funding mechanisms, and collaborative networks that influence research productivity but may not be apparent in broad global analyses.
Similarly, pathogen-specific investigations provide essential detailed perspectives on particular research domains. Studies such as the H. pylori analysis by Eslami et al. [ ] and Yang et al.‘s H. pylori-gastric cancer research reveal important details about specific mechanistic pathways, collaboration patterns, and therapeutic applications that may not be fully apparent in broad temporal analyses [ ]. These focused investigations complement our comprehensive approach by illuminating specific research dynamics within particular pathogen-disease relationships.
Treatment-specific scientometric studies offer additional valuable perspectives on therapeutic domains. Li et al.‘s immunotherapy analysis and Menbari Oskouie et al.‘s radiotherapy and chemoradiotherapy analysis reveal important details about recent therapeutic innovations, clinical trial patterns, treatment optimization strategies, and translational research gaps that may not be fully apparent in broad historical analyses [ , ].
Demographic-focused research, such as Azbergenov et al.‘s young patient biomarker analysis, provides crucial insights into specific population characteristics, age-related disease mechanisms, and therapeutic considerations that extend beyond general temporal trends [ ]. Additionally, clinical implementation perspectives, exemplified by reviews such as that by Triantafillidis et al. [ ], provide essential details about current therapeutic efficacy, safety profiles, and implementation barriers that bridge the gap between research trends and clinical practice.
The integration of our comprehensive temporal mapping with these focused analytical approaches—including regional, pathogen-specific, treatment-specific, demographic-specific, and clinical implementation studies—represents an optimal methodology for understanding both the historical foundations and current frontiers of gastric carcinogenesis research. This multi-dimensional approach enables a more nuanced interpretation of research evolution while informing both international research priorities and local policy development strategies.

## Conclusion and Future Perspectives
This five-decade scientometric analysis offers a comprehensive panorama of the intellectual landscape of gastric carcinogenesis research, illuminating its developmental trajectory, prevailing trends, and persistent challenges. Over the past fifty years, the field has undergone a paradigm shift from traditional microbiological studies of H. pylori to sophisticated investigations of molecular pathways, multi-omics technologies, and emerging experimental models such as organoids. Despite these advancements, gastric cancer remains one of the leading causes of global cancer mortality, particularly in East Asia, where the burden is projected to rise sharply by 2040 [ , ].
The scientometric data reveal consistent growth in research productivity, especially since the 1990 s, driven by increasing global collaboration. Nations such as the USA, China, and Japan have emerged as pivotal contributors, with distinct regional focuses and strengths. China, in particular, has shown exponential increases in publication output but remains underrepresented in terms of global collaborative links, emphasizing the need for deeper integration with international networks. Meanwhile, cutting-edge research into the gastric tumor microenvironment, stromal interactions, and the genetic and epigenetic regulation of gastric carcinogenesis has dominated the field in recent years, with biomarkers and organoid models driving innovation. However, this progress has been accompanied by disparities in research focus and application, with low- and middle-income countries underrepresented despite bearing a disproportionate disease burden.

## Persistent Challenges and Research Gaps
Several critical issues remain unresolved. While H. pylori eradication has long been a cornerstone of gastric cancer prevention, its efficacy diminishes significantly in advanced precancerous conditions, highlighting an urgent need to address the progression from intestinal metaplasia to dysplasia and, ultimately, to adenocarcinoma through alternative preventive and therapeutic strategies [ ]. Moreover, the field continues to struggle with translating molecular discoveries into clinically actionable solutions. Although biomarker studies have been prolific, they have yet to yield reliable, widely applicable diagnostic tools or therapies. Finally, regional imbalances in research funding, infrastructure, and collaborative opportunities limit the ability of the global scientific community to comprehensively address gastric cancer’s multifactorial nature.

## Integration of Historical Analysis with Contemporary Specialized Research
Our comprehensive temporal analysis provides essential historical context for understanding current achievements across multiple specialized research domains. The convergent patterns observed between our broad analysis and domain-specific studies—including geographic concentration of research, evolution toward molecular and personalized medicine approaches, and progression from basic science to clinical applications—demonstrate the value of sustained research investment across multiple timeframes and methodologies.

## Therapeutic Research Development: From Radiobiology to Multimodal Treatment
The sophisticated treatment approaches and clinical outcomes in radiotherapy and chemoradiotherapy applications, as documented by Menbari Oskouie et al. [ ], represent the culmination of decades of fundamental carcinogenesis research that progressively elucidated the biological, molecular, and clinical foundations necessary for effective therapeutic interventions. The success of radiotherapy and chemoradiotherapy research in developing from basic radiobiology concepts to sophisticated multimodal treatment approaches provides a valuable model for other therapeutic domain development.
Furthermore, the therapeutic innovations identified in specialized studies—including the integration of immunotherapy with traditional treatments, emphasis on quality of life outcomes, and development of personalized treatment approaches—illustrate how fundamental carcinogenesis research enables the development of comprehensive patient care strategies. The correlation between economic indicators and research productivity identified in therapeutic studies suggests opportunities for strategic resource allocation to maximize both basic research discoveries and clinical applications.

## Age-Specific and Demographic Research Insights
Similarly, the sophisticated understanding of age-related disease characteristics and biomarker profiles documented by Azbergenov et al. [ ] builds upon decades of fundamental carcinogenesis research that progressively elucidated the demographic, molecular, and clinical factors underlying gastric cancer development. The specialized insights from young patient research—including recognition of more aggressive disease behavior, specific genetic predispositions, and unique therapeutic considerations—demonstrate how fundamental carcinogenesis research enables identification of population-specific disease mechanisms and treatment approaches.
Notably, the low international co-authorship rate (19.4%) identified in young patient research suggests substantial opportunities for enhanced global collaboration, particularly given the geographic concentration of expertise and disease burden documented in our analysis [ ]. This pattern extends to other demographic-focused investigations, indicating systematic gaps in international knowledge exchange that could be addressed through targeted collaboration initiatives.

## Pathogen-Specific Research: The H. pylori Paradigm
The sophisticated mechanistic understanding and clinical applications in H. pylori-gastric cancer interactions documented by Yang et al. [ ] exemplify the successful translation of sustained carcinogenesis research into public health interventions. The evolution from epidemiological recognition of H. pylori as a risk factor to clinical trials demonstrating cancer prevention through bacterial eradication illustrates how decades of investment in carcinogenesis research can yield transformative outcomes.
The three research clusters identified by Yang et al.—H. pylori effects on tumorigenesis, bacterial virulence factors (including CagA and VacA), and host response mechanisms—represent the progressive elucidation of carcinogenic complexity revealed through our temporal analysis [ ]. These achievements required decades of foundational work in understanding gastric carcinogenesis mechanisms, pathogen biology, and host-pathogen interactions. The emerging research themes identified in this domain (autophagy, gut microbiota, immunotherapy, exosomes, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase) represent the current frontier of gastric carcinogenesis research, building upon the historical foundation revealed in our analysis.
However, it is noteworthy that while Eslami et al. [ ] observed declining trends in H. pylori research since 2019, our broader analysis suggests this may reflect successful integration of H. pylori research into comprehensive gastric carcinogenesis frameworks rather than diminishing importance. The high international collaboration observed in H. pylori clinical trials indicates existing frameworks that could be leveraged for broader gastric carcinogenesis research.

## Immunotherapy Research: From Basic Immunology to Clinical Translation
The rapid advancement of immunotherapy approaches documented by Li et al. [ ], with immunotherapy publications reaching 552 in 2022, demonstrates how strategic investment in translational research can rapidly advance therapeutic options. The evolution from fundamental carcinogenesis research to sophisticated immunotherapy strategies—including immune checkpoint inhibitors and adoptive cell therapies—underscores the importance of sustained basic science investigation in enabling therapeutic breakthroughs.
The prominence of microsatellite instability in immunotherapy research, as identified by Li et al. [ ], directly stems from decades of molecular carcinogenesis research. Our temporal analysis reveals how genetic and genomic approaches to understanding gastric carcinogenesis laid the foundation for precision immunotherapy targeting microsatellite instability-high tumors, which now represents one of the most successful personalized medicine approaches in gastric cancer [ ]. Additionally, the identification of tumor microenvironment as a major research cluster exemplifies how carcinogenesis research has evolved from focusing primarily on infectious agents and chemical carcinogens to incorporating sophisticated understanding of host immune responses [ ].
The clinical perspectives provided by Triantafillidis et al. [ ] further illuminate this translation process. Their emphasis that immunotherapy has become the “fifth pillar” of gastric cancer treatment—complementing surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy—reflects the progression of carcinogenesis research toward comprehensive understanding of molecular, genetic, and immunological mechanisms [ ]. However, their observation that “most gastric cancers are resistant to single-agent immunotherapy” underscores the complexity of gastric carcinogenesis and necessitates the multi-target therapeutic approaches informed by decades of research [ ].
Nevertheless, critical gaps remain. Li et al. [ ] found that clinical trials comprised only 3% of immunotherapy publications, with limited efficacy in late-stage disease, while Triantafillidis et al. highlighted challenges including tumor heterogeneity, inadequate biomarkers, and modest efficacy in advanced disease. These limitations emphasize the continued need for fundamental carcinogenesis research to support therapeutic innovation, particularly in understanding the molecular evolution from normal gastric epithelium through precancerous lesions to invasive carcinoma and the timing of immune system dysfunction.

## Implications for Future Research Development and Policy

## Strategic Directions for Integrated Research Development
The integration of our comprehensive temporal analysis with specialized research findings suggests several critical directions for future gastric carcinogenesis research. First, the systematic approaches developed for successful research domains—such as radiotherapy and chemoradiotherapy research, H. pylori mechanistic studies, and immunotherapy development—should be applied to other emerging therapeutic modalities, including targeted therapy, personalized medicine, and precision oncology. Second, the institutional expertise identified across multiple analyses should be leveraged to create integrated research-to-clinic pipelines that facilitate rapid translation between discoveries and clinical applications. Third, technological innovations from therapeutic research, including AI, machine learning, and precision dosing approaches, should be extended to fundamental carcinogenesis research. Finally, comprehensive treatment frameworks that integrate prevention, early detection, and therapeutic intervention strategies must be developed.
Specific opportunities for demographic-specific research include extending age-specific research approaches to other demographic groups (elderly patients, gender-specific studies), investigating interactions between age-related factors and other carcinogenic mechanisms, developing integrated research frameworks that systematically address demographic variations while maintaining comprehensive understanding of fundamental processes, and creating clinical applications that leverage both population-specific insights and broad mechanistic understanding.
For pathogen-specific research integration, opportunities include applying systematic approaches developed for H. pylori research to other gastric carcinogens and risk factors, leveraging geographic concentration of expertise to create international collaborative frameworks, extending mechanistic insights from H. pylori research to understand interactions between multiple carcinogenic factors, and developing integrated prevention strategies that address the full spectrum of gastric carcinogenesis mechanisms.

## Policy and Resource Allocation Recommendations
The patterns identified across our comprehensive analysis and specialized studies carry important implications for healthcare policy, research strategy, and resource allocation. The success of specialized therapeutic and pathogen-specific research demonstrates the value of sustained investment in focused research areas, while our historical analysis reveals the foundational importance of broad carcinogenesis research that enables such specialized studies.
For healthcare policymakers, several priorities emerge: (1) maintaining balanced research portfolios that support both basic carcinogenesis research and specialized therapeutic development; (2) creating mechanisms to facilitate rapid translation between research discoveries and clinical applications; (3) supporting development of specialized therapeutic expertise while maintaining comprehensive research capacity; (4) addressing global disparities in research capacity and clinical access identified through both historical and contemporary analyses; and (5) implementing national cancer research strategies that strengthen human resources, enhance public sector funding, build regional cooperation, and establish specialized cancer medical centers, as recommended in the Central and Eastern Europe/Russian and Central Asian study.
For researchers and funding agencies, key considerations include: (1) maintaining balanced portfolios that support both broad carcinogenesis research and domain-specific investigations; (2) creating frameworks that facilitate translation between fundamental research discoveries and population-specific or therapeutic applications; (3) supporting development of specialized expertise while maintaining comprehensive research infrastructure; (4) addressing demographic gaps and therapeutic challenges identified through both historical and contemporary analyses; (5) creating incentive structures that promote translation from basic research to clinical applications; and (6) developing regional research collaboration frameworks that leverage the geographic concentration of expertise while addressing global health challenges.
For regional research development, specific recommendations include: (1) prioritizing gastric cancer research in regions with high disease burden but low research output; (2) establishing international collaborative networks focused on translating molecular discoveries into clinical applications; (3) developing region-specific research priorities that address local gastric cancer epidemiology while contributing to global knowledge; (4) creating funding mechanisms that support both fundamental research and clinical translation in underserved regions; and (5) leveraging established international collaboration networks from successful domains (such as H. pylori clinical trials) for broader gastric carcinogenesis studies.

## Future Directions: A Multi-Pronged Approach
To overcome persistent challenges and capitalize on emerging opportunities, the next phase of gastric carcinogenesis research must adopt a multi-pronged approach:Harnessing Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning
Artificial intelligence and machine learning technologies possess transformative potential in managing the complexity of gastric carcinogenesis research. By integrating these technologies into predictive modeling, biomarker discovery, and patient stratification, researchers can uncover patterns and actionable insights from high-dimensional datasets such as multi-omics profiles, histopathology images, and real-world clinical data [ , ]. Artificial intelligence-based tools could also facilitate cost-effective early detection strategies, particularly critical for resource-limited settings where traditional screening approaches remain inaccessible.
Expanding Microenvironment and Multi-Omics Research
While significant strides have been made in studying H. pylori-associated carcinogenesis, the role of the broader gastric microenvironment—including stromal cells, immune components, and the microbiota—remains underexplored [ , ]. Understanding how microbial diversity, immune modulation, and stromal remodeling interact to drive carcinogenesis represents a critical frontier. Advances in multi-omics profiling and single-cell technologies can enable granular dissection of these interactions, yielding new therapeutic targets and mechanistic insights into the progression from metaplasia to dysplasia and carcinoma.
Addressing Global Disparities and Enhancing Equity
A glaring gap in gastric cancer research lies in addressing the disproportionate burden in low- and middle-income countries [ – ]. Research efforts must prioritize these regions by improving access to funding, technology, and expertise. International initiatives should be established to foster equitable collaborations and support the development of locally relevant diagnostic and therapeutic solutions. Additionally, public health strategies tailored to regional risk factors, such as dietary patterns and socioeconomic conditions, must be integrated into prevention efforts. The analysis of collaboration networks underscores the pivotal role of countries with high betweenness centrality, such as the USA and Japan, in driving impactful research. Expanding these networks to include underrepresented regions can accelerate knowledge exchange and foster innovative, cross-disciplinary approaches.
Advancing Chemoprevention Strategies
Chemoprevention—the use of natural or synthetic agents to inhibit, delay, or reverse carcinogenesis—has emerged as a critical avenue in mitigating gastric cancer risk, particularly for high-risk populations. While H. pylori eradication remains a cornerstone preventive strategy, its efficacy is predominantly confined to the early stages of gastric carcinogenesis and diminishes substantially once advanced precancerous conditions, such as intestinal metaplasia (IM) or dysplasia, are established [ , ]. This temporal limitation underscores an urgent need to expand therapeutic horizons beyond H. pylori eradication and to identify novel chemopreventive agents and combination strategies capable of intervening at later disease stages.
Encouragingly, emerging research themes identified in specialized studies offer promising directions for chemoprevention development. These include autophagy modulation, gut microbiota manipulation, and targeted inhibition of specific molecular pathways implicated in malignant transformation. The therapeutic ideal would involve interventions capable of achieving dual objectives: first, restoring normal epithelial lineages derived from physiologically functional progenitor populations and reserve stem cell populations; and second, concurrently arresting or reversing the progression of gastric atrophy and metaplasia [ – ].
Achieving this goal, however, requires a more comprehensive mechanistic understanding of gastric carcinogenesis. Specifically, future investigations must elucidate the complex interplay between inflammatory mediators, genetic alterations, and the dynamic gastric microenvironment that collectively governs stem cell fate decisions and clonal evolution. Only through such integrative approaches can we develop rationally designed chemopreventive strategies that effectively intercept the multistep progression toward gastric malignancy.
Optimizing Organoid Technologies and Patient-Derived Models
The emergence of gastric organoids as a model system offers unprecedented opportunities to study carcinogenesis in a physiologically relevant context. Future research should focus on optimizing organoid technology to incorporate more complex cellular and stromal interactions, better recapitulating the in vivo tumor microenvironment [ , , ]. Organoids hold particular promise for drug discovery and chemoprevention testing, enabling the development of personalized treatment regimens based on patient-specific metaplastic and dysplastic characteristics. These models can simulate the progression from gastric metaplasia to dysplasia, enabling the evaluation of potential interventions at specific stages of carcinogenesis in a controlled, physiologically relevant environment.
Enhancing Translation from Discovery to Clinical Implementation
A systematic deficit exists in translating carcinogenesis understanding into practical prevention and early intervention strategies, as evidenced by the finding that clinical trials comprised only 3% of immunotherapy publications and the identification of research gaps in screening and palliative care across multiple specialized domains. Addressing this requires: (1) leveraging carcinogenesis research to identify optimal timing for therapeutic interventions during cancer development; (2) using understanding of carcinogenic mechanisms to predict which patients will respond to specific therapeutic approaches; (3) developing prevention strategies based on carcinogenesis research that could reduce the need for complex therapeutic interventions; and (4) creating integrated research frameworks that systematically translate carcinogenesis discoveries into clinical applications.
This comprehensive five-decade analysis, when integrated with contemporary specialized research findings, reveals both the remarkable progress and persistent challenges in gastric carcinogenesis research. The field has successfully evolved from descriptive studies to mechanistic investigations to clinical applications, yielding transformative public health interventions such as H. pylori eradication programs and sophisticated immunotherapy approaches. However, significant gaps remain in prevention research, early detection strategies, equitable global research distribution, and clinical translation of molecular discoveries.
Future research should leverage both the broad conceptual framework provided by historical analysis and the detailed insights from specialized studies to develop comprehensive approaches to gastric cancer prevention, early detection, and treatment that optimize both clinical outcomes and patient quality of life. The therapeutic innovations, demographic insights, and mechanistic discoveries documented in recent specialized studies illustrate how sustained investment in fundamental carcinogenesis research enables development of comprehensive patient care strategies. By adopting the multi-pronged approach outlined above—integrating AI technologies, expanding microenvironment research, addressing global disparities, advancing chemoprevention, optimizing organoid models, and enhancing clinical translation—the global research community can shape a future where gastric cancer burden is substantially reduced through coordinated prevention, early intervention, and precision treatment strategies.
The insights gained from this analysis highlight both the successes and shortcomings of current research efforts, offering a framework for the future development of this vital field. Understanding the past trajectory of gastric carcinogenesis research is essential for shaping future research directions and optimizing resource allocation to address one of the world’s most significant cancer challenges.
