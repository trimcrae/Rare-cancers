## Background
Conferences are valuable platforms for academic discourse. However, trainees, individuals with lower income backgrounds, and attendees from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) often face significant financial barriers that may limit accessibility to conferences. This study evaluates fees for attending international cardiac surgery conferences to better understand potential financial barriers.

## Methods
Registration fees for 2022-2023 international cardiac surgery conferences were analyzed. Fees were categorized on the basis of the career stage and society member status of the attendee. Other data collected included the meeting’s subspecialty, location, availability of discounts, and virtual components.

## Results
Seventeen conferences were identified. Discounts are widely available for students, residents, and fellows attending cardiac surgery conferences in 2022-2023. Society member students’ fees ranged from US $0 to $390 (median, US $15; interquartile range [IQR], $0-$198). Society member residents' and fellows’ fees ranged from US $0 to $795 (median, US $200; IQR, $75-$390). Society member staff’s fees ranged from US $200 to $802 (median, US $545; IQR, $441-$608). Four conferences (23.5%) explicitly mentioned discounts for LMIC attendees, whereas 5 conferences (29.4%) mentioned virtual components.

## Conclusions
Conference fees remain substantial for trainees and LMIC participants, who are respectively burdened with tuition costs and lower incomes and purchasing powers. Conferences should explore avenues to reduce financial barriers to provide more equitable opportunities for current and future generations of cardiac surgeons.

## In Short

▪Cardiac surgery conference registration fees vary widely but may be prohibitive to trainees, individuals with lower socioeconomic status, and individuals from low- and middle-income countries.
▪Efforts are needed to increase conference accessibility in person and remotely to ensure a more equitable playing field.

Cardiac surgery conferences provide crucial platforms for academic discourse, enabling learning, skill acquisition, expert interaction, and collaborations. However, financial and visa barriers often limit access, particularly for trainees and attendees from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). The emergence of virtual conferences during the COVID-19 pandemic opened up numerous avenues for learning and knowledge sharing, providing a more level playing field and access to a broader and more inclusive audience. As conferences return to mostly in-person formats, financial barriers continue to hinder accessibility for some, skewing participation toward those with more baseline opportunities or resources. Policies aiming to reduce barriers to conference participation are crucial to addressing inequity as they act to democratize access to information and education for all, promoting inclusivity and diversity and strengthening the academic community.
This paper evaluates fees for international cardiac surgery conferences to better understand financial barriers faced by attendees. This may help identify opportunities to bridge gaps, promoting equitable opportunities to access, participate in, and benefit from conferences.

## Methods
Cardiac surgery conferences by international societies or representing a world region were included. Institutional (ie, local) and national conferences were excluded because of informal sharing or language limitations and thus inability to be systematically identified. Registration fees were collected from conference websites for 2023 conferences; if unavailable, 2022 conferences were identified. If different registration fees were reported on the basis of timing, “early-bird” fees were chosen. Although this may underestimate true financial barriers, we assumed that trainees and participants from LMICs seek the lowest fees to minimize financial barriers. Registration fees were categorized by attendees’ career stage (students, residents, fellows, staff). Fees by membership status (society member or nonmember) were identified when different fees existed. All fees were converted to 2023 US dollars according to the currency exchange rate reported by the International Monetary Fund on March 20, 2023, and rounded to the nearest US dollar. Other data collected included subspecialty focus, meeting location, availability of discounts, scholarships or waivers for registration fees, and availability of a virtual component.
Data were tabulated and descriptively analyzed with Microsoft Excel version 16.70 (Microsoft Corporation). Cost data were assumed to be nonnormally distributed and expressed as medians (interquartile range [IQR]). Categorical variables are expressed as counts, number (frequency, %). Outlier data were identified using the 1.5 IQR rule. Analyses were conducted after removal of outliers.

## Results
A total of 19 international cardiac conferences were identified. Conference fees were analyzed for 17 conferences as fees for 2 meetings were not available online. The 17 conferences were hosted by 13 societies across 11 countries. Seven conferences were held in the United States, 2 in Italy, and 1 each in Australia, Brazil, Canada, Germany, Greece, Japan, the United Kingdom, and South Korea ( ). Seven (41%) conferences were categorized as cardiothoracic surgery or cardiac surgery at large. Subspecialty conferences included 3 (18%) congenital, 2 (12%) coronary, and 1 (6%) each on aortic valve, aortic, mechanical circulatory support, mitral valve, and transplant/heart failure. 
Of the 17 conferences, 5 (29%) explicitly mentioned a virtual component. Among these, 2 (40%) offered live-stream access, with 1 limited to international attendees. Two (40%) provided on-demand access, whereas 1 (20%) offered an unspecified "limited" virtual option ( ). 
Most conferences offered different fees based on level of training (n = 15 [88%]). All 15 offered student discounts, although 1 did not offer the discount to international students. The same 15 conferences offered discounts to residents and fellows. Five (38%) societies granted further reductions in fees to students with society membership at 7 (41%) conferences. Six (46%) societies, accounting for 8 (47%) conferences, offered further discounts to resident and fellow society members. Among staff, 12 (92%) societies offered discounts for membership at 15 (88%) conferences.
Student fees, when including those with society member discounts, were US $0 to $390 (median, US $15; IQR, $0-$198). Student fees for non–society members were US $0 to $560 (median, US $74; IQR, $0-$200).
Fees for residents and fellows, including the discount for society membership where available, ranged from US $0 to $795 (median, US $200 (IQR $75-$390). Nonmember fees for residents and fellows ranged from US $23 to $795 (median, US $225; IQR, $100-$560).
Staff fees for society members ranged from US $200 to $802 (US $545; IQR, $441-$608). Nonmember fees for those with staff level of training ranged from US $200 to $1270 (median, US $718; IQR, $595-$995; ). 
Four (24%) conferences explicitly offered discounts or waivers for attendees from LMICs. One offered the equivalent of resident/fellow fees to attendees exclusively from low-income countries and not middle-income countries. One offered student fees to all attendees from LMICs, whereas another offered a 20% discount to individuals from LMICs. Last, 1 offered a 40% discount to upper-middle-income country attendees, with further nondisclosed discounts and subsidies available to LMIC attendees. One additional conference did not offer waivers for attendees from LMICs but did offer discounted rates for those originating from the host country ( ). 

## Comment
This study analyzed conference fees for 17 international cardiac surgery conferences held in 2022-2023, whereby fees varied widely. Although reduced registration fees for students and trainees were common across conferences, only 4 provided explicit information about waivers for attendees from LMICs. Few conferences offered scholarships based on financial need, which combined with the limited availability of virtual components represents a barrier to equitable access to research and education.
Conferences are recognized as a valuable opportunity for early-career researchers and trainees to enhance their careers and to expand their professional networks. However, attendance costs pose a significant barrier, particularly for attendees from LMICs, who have historically been underrepresented at international conferences. Velin and coworkers examined 112 global health conferences with >250,000 attendees during the last 3 decades and identified challenges hindering LMIC representation, including the high cost of registration, travel, accommodation, and food.
Attending conferences is also challenging for trainees from high-income countries because of rising training costs. In the United States, the inflation-adjusted cost of obtaining a medical degree rose by 220% between 1992 and 2017. Moreover, the Association of American Medical Colleges revealed a median debt of US $200,000 for graduates in 2019. In the United Kingdom, cardiac surgery trainees must attend and present at numerous conferences, often bearing such expenses. Meanwhile, physician and specialist remuneration has not kept pace with these escalating costs. When combined with inflation and rising interest rates, overwhelming debt likely dissuades some, especially those from lower income households or lesser funded institutions, from attending conferences. This includes those hoping to attend conferences in or near their own cities or in places where staying with family or friends is a possibility and accommodation costs need not be considered.
The impact of socioeconomic status (SES) on educational opportunities has long been recognized. Students from low-SES backgrounds face various obstacles, such as financial constraints, societal biases, and few role models. , Consequently, high-income households are overrepresented among student bodies, leading to fewer networking and mentorship opportunities for lower SES students. , Furthermore, some conference scholarships require preexisting connections to the organization, often requiring a letter of recommendation from a society member, effectively excluding students with fewer social resources or those affiliated with institutions lacking a cardiothoracic surgery department. Ironically, it is these students, with fewer baseline resources, who stand to benefit most from conferences.
The challenges faced by individuals from lower SES backgrounds attending conferences are exacerbated for those from LMICs. Conferences are typically held in high-income countries, and international attendees often must acquire a visa, which can be expensive, take several months, or be denied. This tedious process results in an underrepresentation of researchers from many countries as some may refrain from submitting their work because of anticipated attendance difficulties. Without a confirmed visa, securing affordable flights and conference registration becomes difficult, forcing attendees to pay higher prices. In addition, conference fees often do not cover food and lodging, and attendees from LMICs often face the added burden of diminished purchasing power because of their currencies. Thus, registration costs alone may not be the primary impediment for individuals from LMICs when it comes to attending conferences; nevertheless, they add to the already high financial and administrative burdens.
The call for critical examination of historical and systemic inequities has highlighted unequal power dynamics in medical and scientific research communities. Despite efforts to increase diversity and inclusivity, socioeconomic diversity remains a significant challenge. Barriers, including financial constraints and travel restrictions, disproportionately affect individuals from lower SES backgrounds and LMICs, perpetuating gaps in representation in the workforce and exacerbating systemic inequities. Creating more opportunities for diverse individuals to attend and contribute to conferences is crucial for promoting a more equitable and representative community.
The pandemic-driven shift toward virtual and hybrid conferences increased accessibility by offering on-demand viewing and recorded presentations. Features like Computer Aided Real-Time Transcription and American Sign Language interpretations dismantled linguistic barriers. This shift particularly benefited individuals facing financial or logistical barriers preventing them from attending conferences, enhancing diversity in presenters and perspectives and allowing previously underrepresented individuals to share their valuable insights with a broader audience. As most conferences return in person, some organizers are adopting a hybrid model, offering in-person and virtual options. Whereas this benefits those who may not otherwise be able to attend, it also creates a 2-tier system whereby only in-person attendees reap the full benefits. Those who cannot afford to attend may miss out on valuable networking, mentorship, and education opportunities. Furthermore, to date, only a minority of conferences provide virtual options.
It is irrefutable that hosting an in-person conference incurs expense, necessitating attendance fees. The venue is 1 of the most substantial costs, along with staffing, audiovisual equipment, and refreshments and catering. For conferences operating at a profit, funds may be used to support other endeavors, such as journals, scholarships, and webinars. Societies may also leverage conferences to raise awareness for their affiliated charitable organizations. However, to forge a more equitable future and to prioritize access for diverse attendees, conferences should take specific steps. First, conference fees should be stratified by training level and SES and offer needs-based scholarships. The Thoracic Surgery Foundation, established by The Society of Thoracic Surgeons, serves as an example by offering grants for research and scholarships for trainees and by supporting the attendance of health care workers from LMICs at Society of Thoracic Surgeons events. Second, conferences should offer more accessible virtual options, including small group networking sessions to encourage connections among attendees, replicating the in-person experience as closely as possible.
This study has limitations. Society membership costs, which may affect discounted fees for members, were not accounted for in the analysis. Some members may pursue membership only for reduced conference registration costs; as such, the membership costs could be considered “conference costs.” Late registration fees and expenses like accommodation were not considered, potentially underestimating the true financial barriers. However, besides conference location, nonregistration costs are typically beyond conference organizers’ control, whereas trainees commonly seek low-cost or free accommodation options if needing to travel. Moreover, societies were not contacted to inquire about discounts or waivers, assuming that the provided information accurately reflected costs and waivers. Furthermore, we excluded national and institutional societies because of difficulty of systematic identification and instead focused on international meetings, which provide larger stages and participation numbers for participants to benefit from. Finally, the absence of qualitative data precludes a comprehensive understanding of whether conference registration costs serve as the primary barrier for trainees and individuals from LMICs to attend conferences.
In conclusion, cardiac surgery conferences present considerable financial barriers to many. Knowledge pursuit should be unrestricted by geography, nationality, or finances. Surgical innovation necessitates global collaboration and idea exchange. Reforms in policies, attitudes, and funding are necessary to acknowledge the professional privilege associated with conference attendance.
