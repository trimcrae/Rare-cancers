## Abstract
The Respond pillar of the Ending the HIV Epidemic in the U.S. initiative, which consists of activities also known as cluster and outbreak detection and response, offers a framework to guide tailored implementation of proven HIV prevention strategies where transmission is occurring most rapidly. Cluster and outbreak response involves understanding the networks in which rapid transmission is occurring; linking people in the network to essential services; and identifying and addressing gaps in programs and services such as testing, HIV and other medical care, pre-exposure prophylaxis, and syringe services programs. This article reviews the experience gained through 30 HIV cluster and outbreak responses in North America during 2000–2020 to describe approaches for implementing these core response strategies. Numerous jurisdictions that have implemented these response strategies have demonstrated success in improving outcomes related to HIV care and viral suppression, testing, use of prevention services, and reductions in transmission or new diagnoses. Efforts to address important gaps in service delivery revealed by cluster and outbreak detection and response can strengthen prevention efforts broadly through multidisciplinary, multisector collaboration. In this way, the Respond pillar embodies the collaborative, data-guided approach that is critical to the overall success of the Ending the HIV Epidemic in the U.S. initiative.

## INTRODUCTION
The Ending the HIV Epidemic in the U.S. initiative, which aims to reduce new HIV infections in the U.S. by 90% by 2030, includes 4 pillars: Diagnose, Treat, Prevent, and Respond. The Respond pillar includes activities also known as cluster and outbreak detection and response (i.e., cluster detection and response [CDR]) and offers a framework to guide focused implementation of proven HIV prevention strategies from all pillars where transmission is occurring most rapidly.
CDR, a longstanding public health approach to reducing illness and death from infectious diseases, – involves the following:

identifying rapid transmission or changing transmission patterns;
reviewing available information and gathering additional information to understand the factors associated with increased transmission and opportunities for improved prevention; and
swiftly responding to these changes to decrease transmission by augmenting services and resources for the affected population.
The presence of an HIV cluster is a signal of potential increased HIV transmission within a geographic area, subpopulation, or social network and indicates gaps in prevention or care. Tailoring and focusing services to the relevant transmission network and addressing these gaps helps to improve the health of people with HIV and stop transmission to others.
Before the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) implemented prospective, systematic analysis to detect clusters in 2016–2017, few health departments (HDs) conducted routine HIV cluster detection, and HIV cluster response occurred sporadically. – Beginning in 2018, CDC required all CDC-funded HDs to detect and respond to HIV clusters. Although HDs are in varying phases of implementing this work, capacity for CDR has increased substantially in the U.S. The Ending the HIV Epidemic initiative provides additional funding to HDs to strategically advance HIV prevention, including, for the Respond pillar, to implement innovative approaches to rapidly detect and respond to clusters and outbreaks. 

## HOW ARE CLUSTERS AND OUTBREAKS OF HIV DETECTED?
Cluster detection employs multiple methods. Sometimes, HIV clusters and outbreaks are identified when an increase in diagnoses or a change in the characteristics of those affected is noticed by healthcare providers, community members, or HD staff conducting partner services who identify, locate, and notify sex and needle-sharing partners of people with HIV and facilitate evaluation and care. , – HDs and CDC also analyze surveillance data to systematically identify HIV clusters of increased diagnoses in a geographic area, sometimes called time−space clusters. , – Finally, analysis of molecular data reported to HDs can identify clusters of highly similar HIV sequences, indicating rapid transmission. – State and local HDs detect molecular clusters using tools such as Secure HIV-TRACE (Secure HIV TRAnsmission Cluster Engine) and identify time−space clusters using CDC-provided or locally developed analysis programs.
These approaches have varying strengths and weaknesses. Time−space analysis can provide an early signal in geographic areas or populations with relatively few HIV diagnoses at baseline, but an increase in diagnoses does not always represent increased transmission. Molecular analysis better identifies geographically dispersed clusters and, in areas with higher baseline HIV rates, can identify clusters before the number of infections is large enough to be identified in other ways but is dependent on linkage to care and sequence reporting. Partner services identifies social networks but is limited by clients’ ability or willingness to name partners. All approaches rely on diagnosis (ideally timely diagnosis) of at least some portion of the cluster, and no approach will capture the full extent of the transmission network. Using multiple approaches maximizes early detection of clusters and outbreaks and the identification of the larger network of people affected by rapid transmission. – , , , – 

## WHY IS RESPONDING TO CLUSTERS AND OUTBREAKS IMPORTANT?
HIV is transmitted through networks. CDR offers opportunities to identify and reach people in networks affected by rapid transmission, including those with undiagnosed HIV, those with HIV who might not be accessing HIV care and other services, and those who do not have HIV but might be at risk. Effective prevention and care strategies can be tailored to reach all who are affected.
Clusters and outbreaks have shown rapid and sometimes explosive transmission. Individual clusters and outbreaks (detected using either molecular or other approaches) have had transmission rates exceeding 30 times the national rate, and transmission rates in molecular clusters identified through routine analysis of surveillance data average 8–11 times the national rate. , , – Rapidly growing clusters contribute disproportionately to new infections, and responding to these clusters may slow HIV evolution toward variants with higher transmissibility. 
Response efforts should be scaled to the size and characteristics of the cluster or outbreak and the needs of the transmission network. Although large outbreak responses are often highly visible, CDR also includes intervening in the many clusters detected while still small, which can most effectively limit transmission. , Therefore, CDR spans routine, day-to-day efforts to identify and prioritize clusters; initial response to small clusters; and expanded or escalated response to more concerning clusters and outbreaks ( ).
 

## WHAT POPULATIONS ARE AFFECTED BY CLUSTERS AND OUTBREAKS?
Because transmission is not uniform even within priority populations, CDR goes beyond identifying subpopulations with the highest number of HIV diagnoses. Analysis by race/ethnicity and transmission category shows that groups experiencing rapid transmission do not necessarily mirror those with higher numbers of diagnoses and that these groups change over time and show substantial geographic variation. Therefore, ongoing, routine analysis by HDs is necessary to identify the networks experiencing rapid transmission. By identifying these networks that are not being effectively reached by existing HIV prevention services, such ongoing analysis presents a vital opportunity to address health equity.
In recent years, the largest responses have been to outbreaks among people who inject drugs (PWID). , Most were detected through nonmolecular approaches, such as time−space analysis, partner services, or identification by providers or community members, because these approaches are particularly effective at detecting clusters when the baseline number of cases in a population is relatively low.
However, sexual transmission is the predominant mode of HIV transmission in the U.S. and in molecular clusters of rapid transmission. , , Although responses to clusters and outbreaks related to sexual transmission have occurred, , , , , , , , , , , , , these responses often do not garner as much attention or resources as do outbreaks among PWID and have not frequently been described in the literature. Using diverse methods to identify clusters in a variety of populations is important to build support for addressing gaps in services affecting networks experiencing rapid transmission regardless of the mode of transmission.
This manuscript reviews the experience gained through 30 HIV cluster and outbreak responses during 2000–2020 ( , available online) and describes approaches for implementing the 3 core CDR strategies : (1) establishing partnerships, processes, data systems, and policies to facilitate robust, real-time CDR; (2) investigating and intervening in transmission networks; and (3) identifying and addressing gaps in programs and services revealed by CDR. Although outbreak responses have occurred in several other countries, this review highlights CDR experiences in North America disseminated through manuscripts, conference presentations, or public communications (e.g., health alerts).

## APPROACHES FOR STRATEGY 1: PARTNERSHIPS, PROCESSES, DATA SYSTEMS, AND POLICIES TO FACILITATE ROBUST, REAL-TIME CLUSTER DETECTION AND RESPONSE

## Internal Health Department Partnerships
Given the multidisciplinary and multisectoral nature of CDR and the need for rapid decision making for a response, strong partnerships and active engagement of staff with diverse areas of expertise and leaders with authority to implement change across program areas are essential. For most jurisdictions, this involves establishing a standing committee or other processes to routinely review data to inform detection and response. For example, Maryland established a cluster response team, including leadership and staff in HIV surveillance, sexually transmitted disease (STD) prevention, and the integrated HIV prevention and care services program. Maryland, Virginia, and the District of Columbia, which often have clusters that cross their boundaries, have implemented data sharing agreements to facilitate cross-jurisdiction cluster detection and also hold cross-jurisdiction standing meetings, promoting stronger collaboration during cluster response. Philadelphia, through its Demonstrating Expanded Interventional Surveillance (DExIS) program, engages staff from multiple units in case reviews to determine service gaps and develop immediate and long-term action plans for response activities. At the federal level, an HIV Outbreak Coordination Unit has promoted proactive cross-unit collaboration. 
During escalated responses, enhanced coordination is useful. West Virginia; Scott County, Indiana; and Seattle/King County have implemented Incident Command Structures during escalated responses. , , A local response team established during a Philadelphia outbreak was strengthened by including non-HIV partners, such as viral hepatitis, STD, opioid, immunization, and public health preparedness programs. Enhanced collaboration and communication between local and state HDs and among HDs in bordering states have also been useful in addressing an outbreak among PWID in Northern Kentucky and Ohio, , and when requested, assistance from CDC and other federal agencies has facilitated escalated response. , , , , , 

## External Partnerships
Involving local community members and organizations in planning, implementing, and evaluating response activities can strengthen the delivered interventions. Community members, including people with or at risk of HIV, provide critical input and perspective on challenges and barriers and can help HDs design messaging and materials to avoid further stigmatizing affected communities and deliver services effectively. , , , , , Most HIV clusters occur in populations that are marginalized. Therefore, working with organizations trusted by affected communities has been particularly important in responses to clusters in diverse communities. , , , – 
Local organizations, including HIV services providers, community-based organizations, and syringe services programs (SSPs), may first recognize clusters , and can serve as implementation partners well positioned to carry out response activities. Partnerships with community organizations during responses have led to expanded HIV testing in homeless shelters and encampments, , emergency departments, , , , jails, , and community-based organizations. , Partnerships with Ryan White HIV/AIDS Program providers and case managers have helped to meet the needs of populations experiencing social and structural barriers to care. , , , HDs have collaborated with SSPs and mental-health providers to expand services for PWID. , , , , Partnerships with academic institutions have helped to strengthen analytic capacity for CDR. , , , , , , In response to a molecular cluster in San Antonio, providers, community members, and HD staff established the End Stigma, End HIV Alliance, whose efforts led to a quadrupling of HIV testing, accelerated achievement of the goal of 7-day linkage to HIV care, and activities to reduce stigma. 
Outbreak response planning and training exercises have identified gaps and needs in these partnerships and have facilitated community outreach before an outbreak occurred. , , Engaging community organizations before a cluster or outbreak occurs speeds the ability to respond, should one arise, and can lead to interventions that prevent future outbreaks. , , , 

## Expediting Availability of High-Quality and Timely Surveillance Data for Real-Time Decision Making
The success of CDR depends on the availability of high-quality and timely surveillance data. In the U.S., commercial, public, and private laboratories report the results of tests performed for HIV diagnosis and care, including HIV sequences generated through drug resistance testing, to HDs. Increasing the rapidity of laboratory reporting and automation of HD data processing practices can improve the availability of timely data and speed detection of and response to clusters, increasing opportunities to avert additional cases. , , , 

## Analyzing, Integrating, Visualizing, and Sharing Data From Diverse Sources for Cluster Detection and Response
Successful CDR requires analyzing and integrating data from multiple sources, including HIV, STD, and viral hepatitis surveillance; partner services; and care systems, while maintaining appropriate data protections. Because these data often exist in siloed systems, data integration can be a challenge for response. , , Some HDs have developed innovative approaches to data integration and used new tools, such as MicrobeTrace, for visualization. , , , , HDs have incorporated data sources beyond case surveillance and partner services data; a Philadelphia outbreak response included HIV behavioral surveillance, medical examiner, shelter and homeless outreach, and SSP data, providing information about social and environmental determinants. Regional data sharing through the use of data sharing agreements and collaboration through the engagement of staff and leadership in regular meetings has improved services, care engagement, and viral suppression in cluster and outbreak responses in the District of Columbia and Cincinnati metropolitan areas. , 

## Creating and Maintaining Flexible Funding Mechanisms Capable of Supporting Response Efforts
Successful CDR requires flexible approaches to allocating funding and resources. HDs have had success in leveraging diverse funding resources, including HIV surveillance and prevention funding; opioid, overdose, and injury prevention funding; Ryan White HIV/AIDS Program funding; public health preparedness funding; and state funds. , , To improve the ability of programs to pivot and adjust priorities in response to evolving situations, CDC is supporting HDs to (1) proactively design funding agreements to allow their funded entities to shift resources to address clusters and outbreaks and (2) identify resources from diverse sources that can be made available in case of a cluster or outbreak. , 

## APPROACHES FOR STRATEGY 2: INVESTIGATING AND INTERVENING IN NETWORKS

## Understanding Networks
Understanding HIV transmission networks is essential for identifying intervention opportunities. A detected cluster indicates a larger underlying network, which includes people with diagnosed and undiagnosed HIV and people who do not have HIV. , , A variety of approaches, including review of existing HD data, HIV partner services expansion, peer-recruitment strategies, , and qualitative and quantitative data collection, can elucidate the extent and characteristics of a network, social or structural barriers, challenges to accessing services, and opportunities for public health interventions. provides examples of several approaches to understanding networks. , , – 
 

## Providing Support for Linkage to and Retention in Critical Services
As factors facilitating HIV transmission, including challenges affecting people in HIV transmission networks, are revealed, this information should be used to strengthen linkage to critical services, including testing and subsequent retesting for HIV, hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and STDs; pre-exposure prophylaxis; SSPs; HIV medical care; and other essential support services (e.g., housing, social services). Incident infections in rapidly growing clusters originate equally from diagnosed but unsuppressed cases and undiagnosed infections, highlighting the importance of promoting retention in care, medication adherence, and viral suppression in addition to partner services and other case-finding activities. Prioritizing people in clusters for linkage to care and treatment interventions can reduce onward HIV transmission, and reaching all members of a cluster with these services is more effective than prioritizing certain people within a cluster. 
Multiple HIV responses have successfully linked people to needed services. After enhanced public health follow-up for a molecular cluster in British Columbia, the median viral load of cluster members decreased from 38,109 copies/mL to undetectable. During a response, West Virginia implemented same-day HIV care appointments, enhanced data to care, and care coordination, improving viral suppression rates. Viral suppression also improved after a response in North Carolina that included linkage to care and a response in Miami, which included wraparound services, intensive linkage, and patient navigation. Initiation of a new HIV care site in Indiana led to a viral suppression rate of 75%. These responses also linked people to services such as hepatitis A vaccination, hepatitis B and C treatment, and medication for opioid use disorder.

## APPROACHES FOR STRATEGY 3: IDENTIFYING AND ADDRESSING GAPS IN PROGRAMS AND SERVICES REVEALED BY CLUSTER DETECTION AND RESPONSE
Amid a response, gaps in programs and services must be identified and addressed swiftly to limit transmission. Outbreaks have occurred even in areas that were believed to have strong prevention programs, , , , underscoring the opportunity for cluster response to inform changes to prevention programming and support efforts to pursue continuous quality improvement.
Gaps might be identified in the delivery of or access to testing, HIV and other medical care, pre-exposure prophylaxis, SSPs, or other services. Programmatic response efforts may include strengthening these core HIV services and redoubling efforts to address social and structural gaps relating to homelessness, poverty, or stigma, as exemplified by the responses discussed in this article. Addressing gaps in ways that are lasting is important, and such improvements, whether made during or after cluster response, are important for preventing future clusters and outbreaks. shows key examples of various interventions to address gaps in programs and services. – 
 Identifying and addressing gaps in services is not only relevant for large outbreaks. The DExIS program in Philadelphia provides a model for continually and prospectively assessing barriers faced by sentinel cases, including people in small clusters, and using that information to modify long-term policy strategies and funding aims. 
In addition to improving services, focused public health messaging is an important component of response. Numerous responses have also included important communications such as clinical advisories, dear colleague letters, health alerts, social media campaigns, and print materials intended for community members and providers that addressed important topics in HIV prevention, testing, and care and dispelled misconceptions or myths. , , , – , – , , , , , 

## CLUSTER AND OUTBREAK RESPONSE OUTCOMES
Response interventions occur at individual, network, and programmatic levels. Given the complex, multilayered nature of response interventions and limitations in determining what outcomes would have been observed if no focused response activities had occurred, assessing the impacts of response interventions can be difficult. However, multiple responses have seen changes in diagnoses, transmission, care outcomes, and other key outcomes after implementing response efforts.
For example, in the Indiana outbreak, after the state public health emergency was declared, transmission fell below the epidemic threshold, suggesting that improving awareness of the outbreak, establishing an SSP, and deploying additional care services reduced transmission. Quantifiable benefits were also seen in opioid use disorder treatment, hepatitis A virus vaccination, hepatitis C virus treatment, and endocarditis prevention. Likewise, response efforts in Northeastern Massachusetts, Seattle, and West Virginia were followed by substantial decreases in new HIV diagnoses, , , and the lack of new linked cases after a response in North Carolina suggested that response efforts ultimately prevented further transmission. Numerous responses have also documented improvements in important intermediate outcomes, including HIV testing, HIV diagnosis, pre-exposure prophylaxis uptake, SSP utilization, linkage to care, retention in care, and viral suppression. , , , , , , , , , , , 

## ADDITIONAL CONSIDERATIONS
Although CDR efforts have shown real, proven public health benefits, it is important to also consider any possible unintended consequences. Some community members and HIV advocacy organizations have expressed concerns about the possible use of molecular data as evidence in legal cases against people with HIV. Many states have HIV criminalization statutes or use general statutes (e.g., reckless endangerment) to prosecute people for HIV exposure or transmission. These statutes and actions are not based on science and run counter to public health efforts. Most states have strong protections and policies against releasing public health data as evidence in legal proceedings, but specific protections vary widely. CDC does not support the use of public health data for nonpublic health purposes.

## RESEARCH GAPS
Further research is needed to strengthen the strategies for CDR; research is particularly important to expand the understanding of effective response strategies and tools for implementing these strategies ( ). Implementation science research can elucidate how best to implement testing, treatment, and prevention interventions during a response and how to evaluate outcomes. Social science research can inform best practices for engaging community members, organizations, and other stakeholders in cluster and outbreak preparedness, planning, and response, including addressing HIV stigma, structural barriers, and social determinants of health. Refining methods for predicting and detecting clusters early can aid early response efforts. Across all of these domains, research can help to bolster the understanding of how to optimize CDR for clusters related to sexual transmission.
 

## CONCLUSIONS
The use of CDR can identify rapid transmission so that proven, effective strategies can be implemented in affected populations or networks. Efforts to address important gaps in service delivery revealed by CDR can strengthen prevention efforts broadly through multidisciplinary, multisector collaboration. In this way, the Respond pillar embodies the collaborative, data-guided approach critical to the overall success of the Ending the HIV Epidemic in the U.S. initiative.
