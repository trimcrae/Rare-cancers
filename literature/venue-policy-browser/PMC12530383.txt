## Abstract

## Objectives
To describe the implementation of a multidisciplinary, ethically grounded hackathon as a model to develop and evaluate generative AI (GenAI) solutions for real-world clinical challenges within a hospital setting.

## Methods
The GenAI Health Hackathon (GAHH) organised at Hospital Clínic de Barcelona included 13 challenges were selected via an internal call based on clinical impact, feasibility and data availability. Participants accessed anonymised real-world data through a secure cloud environment. Teams employed large language models and retrieval-augmented generation to build prototypes addressing tasks such as clinical text structuring, decision support and workflow automation. Human-in-the-loop validation, explainability and regulatory safeguards were emphasised.

## Results
The hackathon yielded multiple AI prototypes tested on real data. Results varied: entity recognition reached 90.5% accuracy, summarisation >90% clinician concordance and nutritional models achieved F1 scores of 0.75–0.93. Lower scores (F1<0.52, Jaccard Index <0.4) were seen in complex reasoning or multilingual tasks. Bias was explored in 10 projects, with mitigations such as stratified sampling, prompt tuning, disclaimers and expert oversight. A transferable framework was proposed to replicate responsible GenAI hackathons in clinical contexts.

## Discussion
Interdisciplinary collaboration and real-world testing proved essential for aligning GenAI with clinical needs. The hackathon revealed challenges in bias, evaluation and integration but offered a transferable framework for responsible innovation under General Data Protection Regulation and the European Union Artificial Intelligence Act.

## Conclusions
The GAHH demonstrated that GenAI can be safely and effectively applied in healthcare with rigorous governance and interdisciplinary collaboration, offering a scalable model for responsible AI innovation.

## WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC
While generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) has shown promise in medical summarisation and decision support, most applications remain at the pilot stage, with limited integration into real clinical workflows. No prior studies have documented a hospital-led GenAI initiative using real-world health data under General Data Protection Regulation and European Union Artificial Intelligence Act compliance.

## WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
This study demonstrates that generative AI can be ethically and effectively deployed in a hospital setting through a multidisciplinary hackathon model and provides the first structured evaluation of multiple GenAI prototypes applied to real clinical tasks, including quantitative performance, bias analysis and regulatory alignment.

## HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY
The proposed framework offers a replicable path for institutions to explore GenAI responsibly, informing future efforts in clinical AI development, ethical oversight and cross-sector collaboration. It may accelerate institutional readiness for GenAI adoption while guiding policy on safe, compliant innovation.

## Background
 Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) has the potential to transform multiple sectors, including the health sciences, through its advanced capabilities in natural language processing powered by large language models (LLMs). In clinical practice, GenAI offers promising applications such as reducing documentation workload, supporting clinical decision-making, improving the availability of data for research purposes, automating administrative processes and enhancing education for both patients and healthcare providers. , For instance, LLMs such as Generative Pre-trained Transformer-4 (GPT-4) have been used to generate discharge summaries and progress notes, helping to alleviate the documentation burden and reduce physician burnout ; generative models have demonstrated near-passing performance on clinical decision-making tasks, including differential diagnosis and treatment planning in complex patient scenarios ; and GAN-based tools have enabled the creation of realistic synthetic health records that support research while protecting patient privacy. 
Despite these potential benefits, the adoption of GenAI in clinical settings remains limited. Several factors contribute to this slow integration. First, there is a lack of robust and consistent, evidence-based data demonstrating a favourable impact of GenAI tools on key clinical outcomes. Second, these systems present significant technical challenges, particularly regarding explainability, transparency and the risks of generating plausible but factually incorrect or fabricated information (hallucinations) and biased outputs. Third, the deployment of GenAI solutions is resource-intensive, creating obstacles related to their integration within the existing healthcare information systems. Fourth, in Europe, those tools need to comply with important regulatory frameworks such as General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and, more recently, with the new European Union Artificial Intelligence Act (EU AI Act). A sign of the prevailing caution surrounding generative AI in healthcare settings is that, as many recent surveys in the US and UK show, most efforts are either in the piloting or early deployment phase, with very few full-scale institutional adoptions. Furthermore, there is evidence of low adoption of GenAI due to clinician scepticism. 
The pathway from identifying a clinical problem suitable for a GenAI solution to its actual deployment will need the participation of multiple professional roles. , Hackathons are competitive events aimed at proposing functioning engineering solutions on specific topics mainly related to data science and programming. Hackathons gather professionals from various sectors to work collaboratively and intensively in specific challenges during a predefined time frame, having the potential to unravel innovative solutions for specific challenges. To date, the only published experience involving generative AI in a healthcare setting has been reported by Small et al, who designed an initiative aimed at training and engaging New York University (NYU) Langone Health staff in the responsible use of GenAI. While participants interacted with real clinical data to simulate tasks such as summarisation, extraction and transformation, the activity was conducted in an educational setting, without direct application to real-time clinical workflows or patient care. As such, no prior study has reported the deployment of generative AI to perform clinically relevant tasks on authentic patient data under a production or operational framework—particularly one compliant with the GDPR and aligned with the EU AI Act’s risk evaluation framework. To our knowledge, the present work constitutes the first documented implementation that meets these criteria. , 

## Methods

## Study design
This manuscript adopts an applied descriptive research approach, aligned with the principles of open innovation and participatory digital health methodologies. The design and execution of the GenAI Health Hack (GAHH) draw inspiration from the datathon model proposed by Aboab et al. which emphasises interdisciplinary collaboration, real-world data and the cocreation of reproducible knowledge in clinical settings.

## Selection of challenges, participants and mentors
The selection of challenges followed a structured, participatory process to ensure relevance and feasibility. An internal call invited hospital staff to propose real-world problems addressable using GenAI. Submissions followed a standardised template detailing objectives, data needs and evaluation metrics. A review committee assessed proposals, clarifying scope or feasibility issues with promoters and prioritised them via structured voting based on clinical impact, feasibility and data accessibility.
Registration for participants was open, free and required no specific background. The organisers ensured the formation of balanced teams always including a combination of participants with technical knowledge (eg, statisticians, computer scientists, data scientists), clinical domain experts and ethics or legal professionals as well as one or two challenge promoters. The latter were responsible for defining the clinical problem, supporting dataset identification and interpretation, and ensuring the alignment of proposed solutions with real-world clinical needs throughout the hackathon process. Each team also included one of the GAHH mentors, who provided guidance and support during the competition.
Seventeen mentors, affiliated with or associated with the Critical Data Group at Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT)—mostly postdocs and doctoral candidates— joined from Europe, Asia and the USA, enhancing the teams’ diversity. Each mentor was assigned to a team to help address technical challenges. Some also offered brief training sessions on model evaluation metrics, bias and policy considerations.

## Pre-hackathon phase: data preparation and anonymisation
The Clinical Informatics department prepared real-world data for the selected challenges by identifying and extracting medical documents and structured datasets in close collaboration with challenge promoters, who defined cohort selection criteria. Although GAHH was not a datathon that involved training predictive models, real data remained essential for prompt tuning, validation and bias detection. Annotated datasets are particularly useful for benchmarking performance, and having structured variables enables teams to identify disparities across patient subgroups.
 summarises the data processing workflow. To comply with privacy regulations and reduce reidentification risks, a multilayered deidentification process was applied. Metadata was first removed to eliminate disclosure risks. SHA-256 cryptographic hashing with random seeding anonymised patient identifiers while preserving data integrity. This approach enabled consistent linkage between unstructured documents and their associated structured sociodemographic data, which were stored under the same hashed identifier. The aim was to allow analysis of model behaviour across patient subpopulations—such as by age, gender or social vulnerability—without exposing direct identifiers, as the textual variables had been further obfuscated using a hide-in-plain-sight strategy. For textual data, a custom-developed pipeline—validated internally and under publication—detected and masked 28 types of identifiers per Medical Document Anonymization (MEDDOCAN) guidelines, using LLMs via Amazon Web Services (AWS) Bedrock in a secure environment. Structured demographic datasets were anonymised using k-anonymity with ARX-based techniques. All data were stored in AWS S3 with strict access controls. Each challenge had a separate, read-only folder, ensuring participants accessed only designated datasets under strong governance.
 

## Hackathon phase: data access
Participants interacted with anonymised clinical datasets exclusively via the hospital’s secure AWS infrastructure. Strict access policies allowed analysis but blocked downloads or modifications, ensuring data integrity and regulatory compliance. Teams applied AI and analytical methods to develop prototypes aligned with their goals. AWS and MIT mentors supported platform use and resolved technical issues.

## Post-hackathon phase: data disposal and extended projects
At the event’s end, all datasets—including intermediate artefacts—were irreversibly deleted to meet privacy regulations. Teams wishing to continue submitted amendment proposals to the hospital’s IRB. Upon approval, access was reauthorised under stricter governance, preserving institutional compliance.

## Ethical considerations and IRB approval of challenges
The initiative strictly adhered to ethical frameworks, including the Declaration of Helsinki (2013), Spain’s Law 14/2007 on Biomedical Research and the GDPR. It leveraged Article 9 (1)(j) of the GDPR, which permits the use of anonymised patient data without explicit consent for research purposes. This exemption was justified by the retrospective, large-scale nature of the data, which made recontacting patients impractical while ensuring rigorous deidentification. To mitigate algorithmic bias, proactive evaluations identified and addressed risks, aligning with principles of non-maleficence and justice. Transparency was ensured through a layered GDPR-compliant privacy notice system.
A structured summary of how key provisions of the GDPR and the EU AI Act were operationalised throughout the hackathon is provided in , including aspects such as lawful basis for data processing, human oversight, transparency requirements and AI system risk classification.
 Ethical oversight was structured around an overarching framework document approved by the hospital’s IRB (approval number HCB/2024/0967), ensuring consistent ethical standards across the hackathon. Seven challenges received individual approvals (HCB/2024/1069, HCB/2024/1071, HCB/2024/1075, HCB/2024/1078, HCB/2024/1081, HCB/2024/1107, HCB/2024/1148) while six were exempted as they did not involve human data. Additionally, a Data Protection Impact Assessment and a risk assessment were developed and validated by the hospital’s data protection officer.

## Technological infrastructure
AWS provided a cloud-based infrastructure with free accounts for participants. Permissions were updated dynamically to enable access to services such as Amazon Connect. Data were stored securely in Amazon S3; development occurred mainly in Amazon SageMaker. Graphics Processing Unit (GPU)-enabled instances were available for high-demand tasks, though most workloads ran on modest configurations due to inference being offloaded to Amazon Bedrock. For retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), teams used OpenSearch and Kendra. Available LLMs included LLaMa 3 (8B, 70B, 405B Instruct), Claude 3 (Haiku, Opus, 3.5 Sonnet), Mistral Large 24.11 and Amazon Titan Text.

## Challenges overview
After the evaluation process, 13 challenges were selected, designed to address real-world problems in healthcare, ranging from primary use of data in patient care to secondary uses for research, medical education and administrative optimisation. shows a brief description of the selected challenges and summarises the task types, performance evaluation approaches, bias identification and mitigation strategies, and regulatory considerations addressed across the projects, while presents the challenges according to the landscape of applications.
 Participants followed a structured but adaptive approach across all challenges. After accessing the anonymised datasets, teams began with data exploration and preprocessing, addressing issues like missing values or inconsistent formats. Prompt engineering was a common method to tailor LLM behaviour to clinical needs, sometimes combined with RAG. Iterative refinement cycles were used, with human-in-the-loop evaluation ensuring alignment with clinical relevance. Finally, teams presented their prototypes with evaluations of feasibility, accuracy and clinical relevance. Supplemental File 1 describes the evaluation framework employed.
Despite the heterogeneity of tasks, several recurring themes emerged, reflecting pressing clinical needs:
Structuring unstructured data: many teams focused on transforming free -text into structured information. Human validation was key, often through manually annotated benchmarks.
Clinical decision support: most challenges aimed to assist clinicians while requiring human oversight, especially in high-risk domains.
Workflow automation: several teams sought to reduce administrative burden (eg, colonoscopy screening, research protocol review), though full automation was not achieved.
Bias evaluation: while many teams lacked time to develop full benchmarks, several explored bias proactively, either by assessing model output disparities across clinical contexts and demographics, or applying mitigation strategies like stratified sampling or manual adjudication.
Performance evaluation: common metrics used across challenges included accuracy, AUC, precision, sensitivity, specificity, F1 Score and response time.
Regulatory alignment was a guiding principle across all challenges. Projects adhered to GDPR through strict deidentification protocols, data governance and transparency measures. The EU AI Act and Medical Device Regulation (MDR) were key references for determining risk level. High-risk applications—such as those involving clinical recommendations—were treated as decision-support tools requiring explainability, disclaimers and traceability mechanisms. Limited risk systems, such as administrative chatbots, focused on user awareness and consent. Many teams implemented safeguards proactively, including privacy notices, clinician feedback loops and early dialogue with compliance officers to anticipate future regulatory demands.

## Discussion

## Lessons learned and institutional reflections
The GAHH represents more than a technical milestone—it represents a cultural shift towards AI-driven healthcare. Through collaboration, education and experimentation, the event catalysed institutional change, encouraging hospitals to explore AI adoption. A hallmark of the hackathon was its strong pluridisciplinary collaboration, with teams combining clinicians, data scientists, ethicists and regulatory experts. Bridging these disciplines is vital to align AI with clinical workflows and patient care standards. Analysis across the 13 challenges showed that interdisciplinary engagement led to more actionable and resilient solutions. Clinical insight shaped use-case relevance, while technical skills ensured performance and feasibility. Beyond enabling robust and ethically sound AI development, the hackathon also served as a catalyst for peer-to-peer learning across disciplines. The diverse profile of participants proved essential in navigating this technological landscape, allowing them to rapidly exchange knowledge, challenge assumptions and cocreate solutions in real time—an increasingly necessary dynamic in fields driven by generative AI.
The GAHH revealed several challenges, especially in data availability and management. Identifying relevant data within EHRs required close collaboration with different teams, and extracting datasets while ensuring metadata removal, anonymisation and validation to prevent data loss was highly complex. Operating under ethical and regulatory constraints also required sufficient time for IRB coordination, and compliance with privacy laws and ethical standards. Another key lesson was the importance of defining challenges based on data quality, availability and feasibility, therefore ensuring adequate data to support AI development.

## Proposed transferable framework for responsible GenAI hackathons
Based on our experience in organising the GAHH, we propose a transferable framework for conducting responsible GenAI hackathons in healthcare institutions. The model is structured around three phases:
Pre-hackathon phase: this includes an internal call and structured selection of challenges, IRB approvals, secure data extraction and anonymisation, cohort definition with clinical leads and technical preparation of cloud infrastructure. Clinical, technical and ethical mentors are also recruited at this stage.
Hackathon phase: multidisciplinary teams work on selected challenges using real-world anonymised datasets within a secure cloud-based environment. Technical development is supported by mentors, and human-in-the-loop strategies are adopted to ensure explainability, safety and alignment with clinical needs.
Post-hackathon phase: datasets are deleted unless interested teams submit IRB amendments to continue their work under reinforced governance. Projects are evaluated based on feasibility, clinical relevance and ethical soundness.
Core features of this model include GDPR-compliant and EU AI Act-compliant data governance, early stakeholder engagement, structured mentorship and built-in evaluation mechanisms. While tailored to our institutional context, we believe this framework can inform similar initiatives seeking to explore GenAI innovation safely and ethically in clinical environments.

## Comparison with previous health hackathons
The outcomes of the GAHH align with the growing literature on health-focused hackathons. Prior studies show that hackathons can produce lasting prototypes, collaborations and even startups. The CAMTech survey reported that about one-third of projects advanced postevent, with many securing funding or institutional backing. Likewise, a Berlin hospital hackathon led to functional app prototypes and strategic recommendations for clinical innovation. Furthermore, our approach resonates with the datathon model proposed by Aboab et al, which emphasised structured challenge selection, pre-event training, interdisciplinary team composition and continuous collaboration. Their international critical care datathons used the MIMIC database and laid the groundwork for translating real-world clinical questions into reproducible research through short-term, team-based data analysis events. 
What distinguishes the GAHH is its novel use of real clinical data under strict compliance (GDPR and EU AI Act) and its emphasis on evaluating generative AI through the lens of explainability, bias and regulatory risk. Unlike earlier events, this hackathon integrated cloud-native GenAI tools, advanced anonymisation and ethical oversight from start to finish.

## Limitations and implementation challenges
Despite promising results, moving GenAI prototypes from hackathon to clinical practice presents hurdles. These include the need for ongoing validation, integration into hospital systems and long-term institutional support. Concerns around liability, transparency and trust must be addressed with rigorous evaluation and clear governance. Nonetheless, the hackathon revealed strong opportunities. The structured approach to challenge selection and evaluation offers a replicable model. It also highlighted GenAI’s potential to streamline workflows, support decisions and enhance data accessibility for clinicians.
Regulatory frameworks such as the EU AI Act and the MDR introduce additional hurdles for clinical deployment, especially for high-risk AI systems. These frameworks require rigorous validation, postmarket monitoring and traceability of AI outputs—barriers that are particularly challenging for early-stage teams or academic groups. Moreover, the absence of reimbursement mechanisms for AI-powered tools, particularly those developed by startups, creates structural limitations to their sustainability and market adoption. Without clear financial pathways, even clinically valuable innovations may struggle to reach real-world implementation.
While several challenges have continued development posthackathon—particularly when driven by the sustained interest of challenge promoters and supported by the Clinical Informatics teams providing dedicated resources for continued work—other projects have faced significant resource constraints. These include balancing clinical duties with innovation tasks, limited availability of technical resources (especially for activities beyond routine operations) and the time required to configure environments or acquire equipment for local model deployment. This underscores the practical barriers that can hinder the translation of hackathon innovations into sustained clinical practice.

## Conclusions
The GAHH at HCB demonstrated that AI-driven innovation can be effectively applied to real-world healthcare challenges through interdisciplinary collaboration and structured evaluation. Overcoming barriers to AI adoption—such as data access, explainability and regulatory compliance—requires ongoing effort, but the hackathon serves as a key milestone toward integrating AI into clinical practice.
Future directions include implementing and scaling the most promising AI solutions from the hackathon. Efforts will focus on securing institutional support for validation, piloting selected projects within hospital workflows and fostering long-term collaborations between researchers, clinicians and AI developers. Plans are also underway to host future editions of the hackathon, expanding participation and refining the framework based on lessons from this first edition. The pioneering nature of the initiative highlights its potential for broader scalability, setting a precedent for AI-focused innovation events in healthcare worldwide. The transferable framework developed for organising responsible GenAI hackathons in healthcare, combining secure data governance, structured mentorship and built-in evaluation across all phases, serves as a blueprint for institutions aiming to explore generative AI safely and ethically in clinical settings.
