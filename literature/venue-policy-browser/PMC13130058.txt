## Abstract
An invisible wall separates the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) from the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (PTO). This wall blocks inter-agency communication, depriving the PTO of information relevant to patent applications and depriving the FDA of information relevant to drug-approval applications. Consequently, it is too easy for a drug company to tell the PTO ‘our drug is new’ (in hopes of speeding a patent grant) while telling the FDA ‘our drug is not new’ (in hopes of speeding drug approval). Better communication between the FDA and PTO would increase agency accuracy by preventing inconsistent representations, and would increase agency efficiency by avoiding informational asymmetry and duplication of effort. Presidents Obama and Trump created enhanced mechanisms that allow the PTO to receive information from counterparts in foreign countries and from industry about what is truly innovative. It would also be helpful for the PTO to enjoy the expertise of its own sister agency, located down the road. Despite scholarly and governmental proposals to mandate cooperation between the FDA and the PTO, such coordination remains limited in practice. This article proposes to break through the invisible wall between the FDA and the PTO, and to open pathways for communication between the two agencies.
Keywords: federal agencies, food and drug administration (FDA), inter-agency communication, patent and trademark office (PTO), pharmaceutical drugs

I.

## INTRODUCTION
Good fences make good neighbors. But they make bad federal agencies.
The fences here mentioned are the invisible walls that block the flow of information from one federal agency to another. The origins of any such invisible wall—turf battles, divisions of labor, vertically oriented agency hierarchies, the siloing tendencies of agency information—can be debated. Regardless of the origins, the consequences of such a wall are of special concern when the wall stands between the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (PTO). It has long been clear that each agency possesses information that could be crucially helpful to the other agency’s mission, yet the barrier remains.
The issue of information-sharing between agencies implicates governmental efficiency as much as accurate decision-making. In this age of slashed budgets, agencies must accomplish more tasks with fewer resources. But the problem is not simply that agencies have smaller staffs to handle bigger burdens. At bottom, the problem is that, while an agency’s efficiency can be increased by relying on the expertise within a sister agency, it can be a daunting challenge to identify who within the sister agency has that expertise, and then to have the sister agency share the expertise. But indisputably an agency that can meet the challenge can avoid wasted effort while enhancing the quality of its own decision-making.
The need for better inter-agency communication is clearest in the case of inconsistent statements made by a drug company to the FDA and the PTO. It is, unfortunately, far too easy, and perhaps human nature, for a drug company to tell the PTO ‘our drug is new’ (in hopes of speeding a patent grant) while telling the FDA ‘our drug is not new’ (in hopes of speeding drug approval). Applicants are speaking to each agency’s interests. But society has larger interests: ensuring that each agency gets to the truth of the matter and enabling all agencies to do better work with tighter budgets.
The potential for increasing accuracy and efficiency through interagency cooperation, however, reaches beyond the case of inconsistent representations. Both the FDA and the PTO bring different expertise to approval of related materials. If handled with an eye towards efficiency, rather than an accumulation of useless paperwork, communication between the two agencies could avoid informational asymmetry and consequent duplication of effort, and could enhance the work of both.
Removing the wall between the agencies would follow a general trend since the turn of the millennium to make a broader range of information and expertise available to the PTO. The wall between the FDA and the PTO is particularly odd, given that efforts instituted under President Obama and President Trump have created enhanced mechanisms that allow the PTO to receive advice and information from counterparts in foreign countries and from industry about what is truly innovative. It would also be helpful for the PTO to enjoy the expertise of its own sister agency, located down the road.
Although there is increasing scholarly and governmental discussion proposing steps to mandate FDA-PTO cooperation, such cooperation has, to date, been sorely lacking. This article proposes to break through the invisible wall between the FDA and the PTO, and to open pathways for cooperation so that drug companies can no longer make inconsistent statements to the two agencies.
Lest the problem appear limited to inconsistent statements, poor inter-agency communication permits many problems beyond the forked tongue. If the FDA says that a drug approval applicant has not proved that the drug can address a particular ailment or accomplish a particular result, the lack of cooperation between agencies could still permit the PTO to grant a patent claiming that the drug can address that ailment or accomplish that result. If the only patentable dose of a drug is known by the FDA to be excessive and toxic, the invisible wall could still permit the PTO to grant a patent for that excessive and toxic—and therefore harmful and socially non-useful—dose of the drug. If the FDA directs a drug applicant to take a particular action that results in a particular drug or drug modification, the invisible wall could still permit the applicant to patent that drug or drug modification. And because FDA expertise could be critically helpful to patent examiners, the invisible wall could still permit patent examiners to grant a patent for what the FDA knows is essentially a routine, and therefore non-patentable, optimization of a drug.
Change is not easy, especially for agencies where the work is voluminous, the demands ranging from congressional inquiries to disgruntled applicants are never-ending, and the funding is limited. Moreover, embedded processes are always difficult to change. Even if new processes may be more efficient and effective, the transition is jolting. As the study of switching costs has shown, moving away from a system that you, and others, understand costs time and mental energy, as well as creating frustration. Beyond simple switching costs, making a decision alone is certainly easier. If the decision is ill-advised, however, the follow-on costs can be substantial. 
This article proceeds as follows. Part II addresses how poor inter-agency communication diminishes the quality of each agency’s decision-making, causing the FDA and the PTO each to operate in the dark. Part III presents four case studies showing how poor inter-agency communication has harmed the public interest. Part III also gathers the evidence—within and beyond case law—of inconsistent statements made to the FDA and PTO. Part IV traces the history of recent efforts to improve FDA-PTO communication, beginning with President Biden’s Executive Order on Promoting Competition in the American Economy, issued July 9, 2021 (Executive Order) and culminating in the joint FDA-PTO listening session of January 19, 2023 (Listening Session). Part V examines the effectiveness of existing disclosure regimes, the significance of the risk of inconsistent statements made to the FDA and the PTO, and the pharma industry’s misguided effort to minimize the problem. Part V also explores two issues of timing—namely, the government’s authority to mitigate a risk before the risk materializes into actual harm, and when during a drug’s life cycle FDA-PTO cooperation should occur. Finally, Part VI proposes practical suggestions for removing the invisible wall between the FDA and the PTO and improving the flow of information between the two agencies.

II.

## HOW POOR INTER-AGENCY COMMUNICATION DIMINISHES THE QUALITY OF EACH AGENCY’S DECISION-MAKING

 

Understanding the need for interagency cooperation requires a basic understanding of the doctrinal context of the relevant decisions before each agency. This section briefly reviews the relevant doctrines and describes how poor inter-agency communication compromises the efficiency and efficacy of each.
II.A.

## Brief Tour of Relevant Patent Doctrines
Although most people associate patents with the agency that administers them, the PTO, patents are a constitutional creature. Tucking the Patent Clause within the remarkably few words of the U.S. Constitution, and with great foresight, the founders focused on enhancing innovation in the nation by granting Congress the power to ‘promote the progress of ... [the] useful arts’. The constitution also specified the mechanism for promoting national innovation, describing it as ‘securing for limited Times to Authors and Inventors the exclusive Right to their respective Writings and Discoveries’. From those origins, the patent system emerged.
As developed over the subsequent two centuries and currently codified in the Patent Act of 1952, an inventor must satisfy five elements of patentability to obtain a patent grant. The invention must be (i) within the proper subject matter; (ii) novel; (iii) non-obvious; (iv) useful; and (v) appropriately disclosed. 
The two elements most relevant to inter-agency cooperation are novelty and non-obviousness. Briefly, the Patent Act, as amended in 2011 by the America Invents Act, describes the novelty requirements in the negative, that is, ‘a person shall be entitled to a patent unless’ the invention claimed in the patent application was already patented, described in a printed publication, in public use, on sale, or otherwise available to the public before the inventor filed the patent application. In addition, the patent application must have been filed before anyone else filed a patent application claiming the invention. These exception categories are known as ‘prior art’.
As prescribed by the courts and the PTO, for a patent to fail the novelty requirement, there must be prior art that explicitly or inherently contains, and precisely matches, every element of the claimed invention. When a patent fails the novelty test, patent lingo describes it as ‘anticipated by the prior art’.
Obviousness is closely related to the novelty requirement. Novelty guards against patent claims that match the prior art with precision. In contrast, obviousness guards against patenting slight differences over, and minor tinkering with, known devices and techniques. 
Obviousness hinges on what a person of ordinary skill in the art—not a great expert—would understand and would be able to do without too much experimentation. For example, if physicians are already using a method to treat patients, or would understand that method from the prior art, a patent claiming that method of use would be invalid. 
In addition to the five elements of patentability, one should note that pharmaceutical patents are generally described as either primary, secondary, or tertiary. Primary patents claim rights to the chemical compound or other type of molecule that constitutes the active ingredient in a medication. Secondary patents claim anything else, such as a new method of using the molecule to treat a different disease state, a different formulation of the drug (capsule, time-release, or cream, rather than a tablet), a different dosage regimen, or a different manufacturing technique. Tertiary patents relate to the device for delivering a drug, like auto-injectors, such as Epi-Pen, and mechanisms for injecting certain GLP-1 weight-loss drugs or delivering insulin.
Scholarship examining patent litigation involving the five elements of patentability has suggested that obviousness and ‘anticipation by the prior art’ (part of novelty challenges) are among the most commonly litigated issues. Looking at types of patents, scholarship suggests that secondary patents are weaker, meaning that they are more likely to be invalid or invalidly applied to a drug. For example, one study determined that in the pharmaceutical industry, secondary patents are more frequently overturned in litigation than primary patents, if the litigation is pursued to completion rather than settled. The patent-holder won 92 per cent of the time when a primary patent was challenged but only 32 per cent of the time when a secondary patent was challenged. Other scholars have argued that the patent system suffers from an overabundance of secondary patents on drugs, which has the effect of extending the length or expanding the breadth of protection for a drug. This phenomenon maintains higher drug prices beyond the original monopoly granted, by creating new barriers for less-expensive versions of the drug. 
Unsurprisingly, each of these issues—anticipation by the prior art, obviousness, and secondary patenting—could be improved through interagency cooperation. As described in the following subsection, such cooperation could help the PTO restrict the flow of invalid patents, reducing the burden on the judiciary and on the PTO itself.

II.B.

## Lack of Cooperation Causes the PTO to Operate in the Dark
Lack of communication between the agencies causes the PTO to end up in the dark and thus to grant an invalid patent. One scenario involves inconsistent assertions as to whether the drug at issue is new or not new. A company applying to the FDA for drug approval should not be able to tell the FDA that the drug is not new (an assertion assuring the FDA that the drug has already been deemed safe and thus should get quickly approved) when the same company, in its application to the PTO for a patent on the same drug, tells the PTO that the drug is indeed new (an assertion assuring the PTO that the drug is non-obvious). 
How is it that the PTO might not be aware of assertions to the FDA that the drug at issue is not new? The failure of the assertion to reach the PTO’s attention could occur for several reasons. First and foremost, the company might have withheld from the PTO the fact of the assertion. If the assertion was publicly available, the problem could be that the PTO could not find the assertion, or (if it found the assertion) did not realize the significance of the assertion without explanatory guidance from the FDA. If the assertion was not publicly available—for example, it was maintained by the company assertedly as confidential or as a trade secret—then the question is whether the assertion of trade secrecy or confidentiality was working, in design or in effect, to thwart information-sharing from the FDA to the PTO. Suppose, for instance, that a company’s interpretation of clinical trial results—an interpretation saying that the results were expected and consistent with past results—were submitted to the FDA under a claim of trade secrecy and confidentiality. That claim would enable the company to submit a discrepant interpretation to the PTO—an interpretation saying that the results were unexpected and therefore novel—because the claim would bar the FDA from disclosing to PTO the different interpretation submitted to the FDA.
Besides assertions to the FDA of non-novelty, the PTO might also be kept in the dark about assertions to the FDA that, if disclosed to the PTO, could raise the on-sale bar to a patent grant. The on-sale bar provides that no patent may be granted if the matter for which a patent is sought was on sale more than a year before the patent application was filed. More specifically, the provision setting forth the on-sale bar states that a patent may not be granted if ‘the claimed invention was patented, described in a printed publication, or in public use, on sale, or otherwise available to the public [more than one year] before the effective filing date of the claimed invention’. Where a drug is the claimed invention, clinical testing of the drug can sometimes involve or coincide with auxiliary agreements—for example, ‘technology licenses, joint research agreements, supply agreements, material transfer agreements, and even ubiquitous nondisclosure agreements’—that in turn involve a transfer of title to the drug. This was the fact pattern of a recent Supreme Court case. Where a drug application’s description of clinical testing includes details of such an auxiliary agreement, the FDA may end up possessing information relevant to the on-sale bar, if the company, more than one year after the auxiliary agreement, applies to the PTO for a drug patent. (Such a patent application might occur when, after a drug receives its initial patent, the company changes the manufacturing process, applies to the FDA for approval of the new manufacturing process and/or the resultant drug, and then applies for a patent on the new process or resultant drug.) The information in the FDA’s possession about the date of the auxiliary agreement—information that is highly relevant to the on-sale bar—should also be in the possession of the PTO prior to the PTO’s consideration of the applicant to patent the new process or resultant drug. But there is no reliable mechanism to ensure that the company submits the information to the PTO, nor is there a reliable mechanism to ensure that the FDA on its own will provide the information to the PTO. If the information does not make it to the PTO, the PTO is likely to grant a patent that should have been barred by the on-sale bar.
A third area where the PTO might be kept in the dark, and hence might improperly grant a patent, involves so-called ‘510(k)’ medical device submissions. As described below in Part II.C.2., a company applying to the FDA for a 510(k) approval for a medical device must establish ‘substantial equivalence’ between its device and a ‘predicate’ device. But if the company has also applied to patent its device, the company’s representation to the FDA of substantial equivalence can, under some circumstances, be an admission not just of obviousness but also of infringement, and could lead to rejection of the patent application. But the company cannot necessarily be relied upon to self-report to the PTO the representation to the FDA of substantial equivalence. And upon receiving a representation of substantial equivalence, the FDA does not, as a matter of practice, share that representation with the PTO. Were it otherwise, the PTO would routinely receive that information, which could help the patent examiner decide whether the patent application should be denied as obvious or infringing. If the company did not itself share the information with the PTO, the company’s patent could also be subject to a defense of inequitable conduct.
Most important, the problem goes beyond the potential for directly inconsistent statements. Patent examiners normally are not clinicians, that is, physicians or pharmacists. Nor are they necessarily pharmaceutical scientists. Input from the FDA can fill that knowledge gap, helping patent examiners to determine whether a pharmaceutical patent application represents a true innovation, rather than something that is obvious to physicians or the FDA itself.
As noted in the introduction, both President Trump in his first administration and President Obama enhanced mechanisms that allow the PTO to receive advice and information from counterparts in foreign countries and from industry about what is truly innovative. For example, an executive action from President Obama led to an expanded program at the PTO ‘to make scientists and engineers available to examiners for purposes of educating them on the latest state-of-the-art technology, advancements, and emerging trends in their technical fields’. Similarly, during President Trump’s first administration, PTO Director Iancu reported to the Senate Judiciary Subcommittee on Intellectual Property in 2019 that the PTO was piloting projects to help examiners better identify prior art by collaboration between multiple PTO examiners as well as examiners from foreign patent offices. It would also be helpful for the PTO to enjoy the expertise of the FDA, another U.S. government agency with significant expertise in the field, in general, and possibly experience with information in the specific patent at issue either in the past or in the future.
A timing issue here bears elaboration. Typically, a company’s filing of a patent application with the PTO long precedes the company’s filing of a drug approval application with the FDA. From that precedence, one might conclude that the PTO’s decision on the patent application will be made long before the FDA could possibly learn of information relevant to patentability, let alone transmit that information to the PTO before the PTO’s decision on the patent application. But that timing disconnect, even if it might limit the value of the FDA’s information-sharing as to the company’s primary patent (ie, the patent on the drug’s active ingredient), does not limit the value of the FDA’s information-sharing when it comes to the company’s application for a secondary patent. (A secondary patent claims a modification of some aspect of the drug other than the active ingredient and may be applied for long after after the filing of the application for a primary patent. ) In the case of an application for a secondary patent, it is quite conceivable that a representation made to the FDA, or information in the FDA’s possession, that is relevant to patentability could be transmitted to the PTO prior to the PTO’s decision on the application for the secondary patent. Note that where a company seeks to obtain a secondary patent for its drug, the drug product may already be on the market, or the company has already had significant communication with the FDA about the drug. Thus, the FDA may well have information that is relevant to the PTO’s assessment of any application for a secondary patent, including information that may contradict assertions made by the company to the PTO. Many applications for secondary and tertiary patents are filed as the drug is moving through FDA processes and even after approval of the drug. 
Even if a drug company has not yet made any submission to the FDA, but is pursuing a patent application with the PTO, the FDA may still have information that could be helpful to the patent examiner. For example, the FDA, by virtue of its expertise, could be in a position to state that the product for which a patent is sought is already well-known or would be obvious to a skilled artisan. 
There are yet other areas where FDA input could be helpful to the PTO, and hence where lack of cooperation and information-sharing could lead the PTO to grant applications that should be rejected. Consider these examples. If the FDA rejects a claim that a drug helps headaches, then the company should not be able to get a patent based on the claim that the drug helps headaches; the PTO must, somehow, be informed of the FDA’s rejection of the claim. If the FDA requests that a company take action or make a modification, the company should not be able to obtain a patent on the action or modification; if the FDA requires it, then it by definition is not novel, and the PTO must, somehow, be informed that the genesis of the action or modification was an FDA request. If a company engages in a routine optimization of a drug regimen, the company should not get a patent for that routine optimization; the PTO must, somehow, be informed that the optimization was routine. In all of these cases, the FDA can help PTO patent examiners determine whether an invention for which a patent application has been filed is a true innovation or not. 

II.C.

## Brief Tour of Relevant Laws and Regulations for the FDA
II.C.1.

## Relevant FDA laws and regulations related to drugs
The Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938 (FDCA) and its numerous amendments aim to protect consumers from product dangers. Although the FDA was established roughly two decades before passage of the FDCA, the 1938 legislation and amendments gave the agency an enhanced role in regulating pharmaceuticals. Today, manufacturers must ‘submit evidence of drug safety and effectiveness, [maintain] specific strength and purity standards, [and ensure] that drug labels list active ingredients and contain truthful statements’. All of these are administered by the FDA.
Since passage of the 1984 Hatch-Waxman Act, the FDA has played a key role in administering the Act’s expedited pathway for ensuring that generic drugs can enter the market as soon as a drug’s protections expire. The same is true for the counterpart legislation related to expediting the pathway for approval of biosimilar drugs, the Biologics Price Competition and Innovation Act (Biosimilars Act), which was part of the 2010 Affordable Care Act.

II.C.2.

## Relevant FDA laws and regulations related to medical devices
In the world of medical devices, there are three ways a company can get FDA approval: by seeking pre-market approval, which is required for high-risk devices for which there’s no ‘predicate’ device (ie, a similar device already approved and on the market); by seeking de novo approval, which is required for low-to-moderate-risk devices for which there is no predicate device; and by seeking approval pursuant to a 510(k) submission, which is required for devices for which there is a ‘predicate’ device. Of these, the 510(k) submission directly implicates the issue of FDA-PTO cooperation. To get 510(k) approval, the applicant company must establish ‘substantial equivalence’ between its device and the predicate device. In that circumstance, both the FDA and the PTO likely are making similar decisions on a device without any communication between the two agencies.

II.D.

## Lack of Cooperation Causes the FDA to Operate in the Dark
The flow of potentially helpful information is not always from the FDA to the PTO. The PTO, too, might have information and expertise from which the FDA could benefit. For example, the FDA’s role in administering the Hatch-Waxman Act and the Biosimilars Act —expedited pathways for approval of generics and biosimilars—includes numerous administrative decisions related to details of numerous issues. The PTO may have information that can be helpful in these decisions. Consider Paragraph IV assertions under the Hatch-Waxman Act. When a company receives FDA approval for a drug, the company provides a list of patents relevant to the drug, updating that list with any later-acquired patents. The FDA publicizes the list in what is known as the Orange Book. A generic company may assert that the patents are invalid and may therefore file for approval of a drug before the patents expire, through what is known as a Paragraph IV certification. Hatch-Waxman specifies that, if the patentee company then sues the generic for patent infringement, the FDA should not approve a generic through a Paragraph IV certification for a period of 30 months, unless a court rules before expiration of the 30 months that the patent is invalid or non-infringed. In some cases, however, pharmaceutical companies engage in strategic behavior to delay patenting by improperly listing patents in the Orange Book. For example, patents on devices such as inhaler caps, dose counters, or injector pens rather than on a drug are not permitted in the Hatch-Waxman Orange-Book scheme, and any such listing would be improper. In fact, the FTC recently took action against numerous companies for improperly listing patents in the Orange Book. The FDA feels that it lacks the expertise to determine if a patent is improperly listed in the Orange Book and must wait for a court to rule so or for the 30-month stay to expire. In such cases, the PTO could assist the FDA by confirming that a particular patent asserts only device claims and, therefore, should be removed.
Another area in which the FDA could benefit from the PTO’s expertise lies with the FDA’s decision on a 510(k) application, related to approval of a medical device. The FDA has its own disclosure requirement called the ‘Application Integrity Policy’ (AIP). In the 510(k) submission, the company must establish that its new device has the ‘same technological characteristics as the predicate device or different technological characteristics that do not raise different questions of safety and effectiveness than the predicate device’. Technological characteristics include materials, design, and energy source. If the company, when it is prosecuting its patent application, asserts that the device ‘is patently different in terms of materials, design, [or] energy source’, the company qua patent applicant may be casting doubt on the ‘integrity’ of its representations in its 510(k) submission, which stated that the characteristics are the same. That discrepancy could prompt the FDA to ask whether the AIP was violated; if it was violated, then the 510(k) approval could be rejected or withdrawn, products could be ordered withdrawn from the market, and a civil or even criminal investigation could be started. In short, the patent applicant, in seeking to distinguish prior art that is the predicate device, could be setting itself up for an AIP investigation and potentially an adverse finding with the attendant consequences. Regular communication from the PTO to the FDA would enhance the FDA’s ability to determine if the AIP was violated.
Even putting the AIP aside, better communication from the PTO to the FDA could give the FDA information on which to conclude that the 510(k) pathway is unavailable and that an alternative pathway, such as pre-market approval, must be used. For example, if a patent specification states that the device improved safety and efficacy beyond what is known in the art, then a ‘different question[]’ has been presented by the new device. That, in turn, means that the 510(k) pathway is unavailable, and, most likely, the new device must use the pre-market approval pathway.
More generally, the PTO could inform the FDA whether an applicant for a drug patent has taken the position that the drug at issue is new and meaningfully different from what has come before. That position might contradict a position taken before the FDA and thus lead the FDA to deny an application for drug approval.

II.E.

## Brief Tour of Relevant Laws and Regulations Authorizing Information Disclosure
As noted below in Part V.A., there are three main regulatory mechanisms that authorize information disclosure. First is the duty imposed by the PTO on patent applicants to disclose information material to the determination of whether a claimed invention is patentable. Second is 21 U.S.C. § 372(d)’s requirement that the FDA give the PTO, upon request, information concerning ‘such questions relating to drugs as the [PTO] may submit concerning any patent application’. Third is 37 C.F.R. § 1.105, which permits a patent examiner to ask a patent applicant to provide additional information. As Part V.A. demonstrates, each of these mechanisms is problematic. Additional mechanisms—that are limited yet effective, without being abstract or burdensome—are proposed below in Part VI.

III.

## FOUR CASE STUDIES REVEAL THE PROBLEMS ARISING FROM POOR INTER-AGENCY COMMUNICATION
Following are four case studies reflecting the problems arising from insufficient communication between the FDA and the PTO.
III.A.

## Ibrutinib
Ibrutinib (imbruvica) is a cancer drug made originally by Pharmacyclics, which is now owned by AbbVie. Pharmacyclics’ early studies showed that, for treatment of B cell cancers, ibrutinib had efficacy at low doses; that result was reported by the company in 2009 in a conference submission and in 2010 in a press release. In 2013, the FDA, while giving the drug accelerated approval for treatment of mantle cell lymphoma at a daily dose of 560 mg, noted that the drug showed a maximum response at a daily dosage well below 560 mg. That is, the 560 mg dose was excessive in the FDA’s view. The FDA accordingly told the company, ‘We recommend you evaluate lower doses of ibrutinib in future clinical development ...’. Then, in 2014, the FDA, while giving the drug accelerated approval for previously treated chronic lymphocytic leukemia at a daily dose of 420 mg, noted that the 420 mg dose was higher than needed and suggested that the company ‘consider exploring lower doses in future development programs’. Again, the FDA informed the company that the approved dose was excessive. Notwithstanding the FDA’s recommendation and suggestion, subsequent trials of ibrutinib used the 420 and 560 mg daily doses. 
The problem for patients is that ibrutinib is cardiotoxic, causing a higher incidence of atrial fibrillation. But lowering the daily dose to 140 mg, following the FDA’s 2013 recommendation, ‘would be expected to ... eliminat[e] or reduc[e] the risks of’ atrial fibrillation without reducing efficacy. 
While cardiotoxicity was the problem for patients, unpatentability was the problem for the company. More specifically, only higher doses—420 mg or higher—were able to be, and were, patented. Lower doses were found by the PTO’s patent examiner to be obvious in view of the 2009 conference submission and the 2010 press release. Thus, patent law encouraged the company to pursue the excessive, riskier dose rather than the lower, less risky dose. 
Whatever may be said of the FDA’s approval of an excessive, riskier dosage, the issue for present purposes is that the company sought, and the PTO granted, a patent for that excessive, riskier dosage. Had the FDA’s view that the higher dose was excessive been communicated to the PTO, the PTO might not have granted the patent. This history militates in favor of creating a mechanism by which the PTO could obtain from the FDA information that might help prevent issuance of patents on harmful, or even non-beneficial, inventions.

III.B.

## Tenofovir
Gilead Sciences (Gilead) recently agreed to pay $40 million to settle a federal lawsuit concerning the HIV drug tenofovir. The suit charged Gilead with intentionally shelving its less-toxic version of the drug until expiration of the patent on the more-toxic version of the drug. Key allegations follow.
Gilead was originally developing two versions of tenofovir: the more-toxic version, tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF); and the less-toxic version, tenofovir alafenamide fumarate (TAF). In 1999, Gilead’s first applications to patent TDF were granted by the PTO. In 2000, Gilead submitted to the PTO a provisional patent application for TAF indicating that TAF was less toxic than TDF but no less efficacious. , In 2001, the FDA granted approval of TDF. By 2004, Gilead had applied to patent TAF. , But in 2004, Gilead suddenly announced that it was stopping research on TAF, explaining vaguely that TAF was ‘unlikely’ to be ‘highly differentiated’ from TDF. , Yet, even after that announcement, Gilead kept seeking and obtaining patent protection for TAF. , In 2010, contradicting the 2004 explanation, Gilead announced that it was now pursuing TAF, an assertedly ‘new’ drug. , In 2014, Gilead applied to the FDA for approval of TAF. In 2015, the FDA granted approval. 
TDF’s last patent expired in 2018; TAF’s last patent does not expire until 2032. , Although the complaint does not say so directly, the implication is that by delaying its development of TAF, Gilead was able to delay creation of the final version of TAF, and thus delay the patenting for that final version. Gilead thereby extended its patent monopoly from 2018 (when TAF’s last patent would have expired had Gilead developed TAF instead of TDF) to 2032 (when TAF’s last patent actually expires).
In the years between 2001 (when TDF got FDA approval) and 2015 (when TAF got FDA approval), thousands of HIV patients taking TDF suffered agonizing damage to their kidneys and bones because of TDF’s toxicity. Had those patients been on TAF instead, the number suffering kidney and bone damage would have been vastly lower.
It turns out that Gilead shelved TAF in 2004 for financial reasons, rather than because of any claimed lack of differentiation from TDF. By withholding TAF from the market, and marketing only TDF until TDF’s patent expiration in 2018, Gilead made billions from TDF; subsequently, by further developing and further patenting TAF, and by going to market just before expiration of the TDF patent, Gilead stands to make many more billions from TAF, until TAF’s patent expiration in 2032. That two-step approach vastly increased the company’s profit. By 2015, TDF was generating $11 billion in sales for Gilead. Having milked as much gain as possible from TDF—at the expense of patient health—Gilead then began marketing the safer TAF and restarted the clock on its ability to make yet more billions. By contrast, had Gilead developed and marketed TAF instead of TDF, it would have avoided thousands of toxicity cases, but would also have faced two economic problems: First, it would have made almost no profit from TDF once TAF went on sale, given TAF’s superiority (ie, having lower toxicity but no less efficacy); and TAF would have reached the ‘patent cliff’ 14 years sooner (ie, in 2018 instead of 2032).
What does all of this have to do with communication between the FDA and the PTO? Let us take as axiomatic that whenever one of the two agencies has information that a particular drug is more toxic than another equally efficacious drug, that agency should share the information with the other agency. So if the PTO was on notice of TAF’s lower toxicity by, say, 2000 (when Gilead filed its provisional patent application for TAF) or even by 2004 (when Gilead filed its patent application for TAF), the PTO should have shared that information with the FDA; had it done so, it is possible that the FDA might have been more inclined to deny, withdraw, or even delay approval of TDF pending the development and approval of TAF, TDF’s less toxic but no less efficacious alternative. Similarly, once the FDA became aware of TAF’s lower toxicity, the FDA should have shared that information with the PTO; had it done so, the PTO would certainly have been armed against any secondary patenting of TDF, and, insofar as statutory law permitted, the PTO might have been prepared to entertain a proceeding to reexamine the TDF patent(s).

III.C.

## Sotorasib
Sotorasib (Lumakras) is a cancer drug made by Amgen. Between June 2019 and March 2022, Amgen filed a series of applications to patent daily sotorasib doses of 180, 240, 360, 720, and 960 mg. , , , , , , Of particular note, Amgen filed patent applications in March 2021 and March 2022 for a daily sotorasib dose of 240 mg, explaining in each application that drug exposure at a daily dose of 240 mg was expected to be similar to drug exposure at a daily dose of 960 mg. , , , In other words, the applications indicated that the optimal dose was 240 mg as compared with 960 mg. But neither application resulted in a patent grant.
Despite the conceded optimality of the 240 mg daily dose, Amgen in December 2020 submitted to the FDA a new drug application for a 960 mg daily dose. In May 2021, the FDA granted accelerated approval for the 960 mg daily dose, and Lumakras went on the market at a monthly cost of $17,900. The FDA’s accelerated approval, however, was conditioned on the company’s performance of a post-marketing study concerning whether the daily dose could be lowered from 960 to 240 mg. 
In August 2022, the PTO granted a patent for the 960 mg daily dose of sotorasib. That was apparently the only dose—of all the above-mentioned doses that Amgen applied to patent—for which a patent was actually granted.
In October 2023, Amgen announced the result of the post-marketing study required by the FDA’s accelerated approval. The result was that, as compared with the 960 mg daily dose, the 240 mg daily dose had lower toxicity but the same efficacy (in view of the progression-free survival data). , , Nonetheless, the FDA’s advisory committee voted 10-2 in favor of the 960 mg daily dose, explaining that the result of the post-marketing study was (as one medical journalist stated) ‘uninterpretable because of the loss of equipoise’—the ‘loss of equipoise’ referring to when doctors and patients believe that a study’s ‘experimental arm offers a better therapy than the control arm’, and that belief therefore permits bias to enter the study. , The FDA ordered another post-marketing study, without granting full approval. In December 2023, Amgen announced that the FDA had completed its review of Amgen’s application for full approval, that the FDA acknowledged Amgen’s completion of the post-marketing study, and that patients would continue taking the 960 mg daily dose. 
Senator Richard Durbin was skeptical of this outcome given that the post-marketing study showed the 240 mg dose to be as efficacious as, but less toxic than, the 960 mg dose. By letter to Amgen dated May 10, 2024, Durbin asked whether Amgen believes that the 960 mg dose is optimal compared with the 240 mg dose, whether Amgen will withdraw its drug dosage formulations if the 240 mg dose gets regulatory approval, and whether Amgen will agree not to raise its unit cost if the recommended dose drops to 240 mg. Sensing the possibility of patent-driven dosing—as opposed to patient-driven dosing—Durbin noted in his letter that in December 2023 Amgen obtained an additional Lumakras patent that would extend Amgen’s monopoly to 2040 and that Amgen applied for yet another patent on Lumakras’s dosing. 
When asked by a trade publication to respond to Senator Durbin’s questions, Amgen claimed that, according to the post-marketing study result, the 960 mg daily dose shows a ‘more favorable benefit–risk profile than’ the 240 mg daily dose. Amgen added that the 960 mg daily dose was accordingly ‘affirmed by the FDA as the most effective option of the two doses’. 
From the foregoing history, a few points emerge. First, even while asserting to the PTO that the optimal dose was 240 mg (in March 2020 and March 2021), Amgen sought FDA approval for the 960 mg dose (in December 2020). Second, the 960 mg dose generates four times as much sales value for Amgen as the 240 mg dose. Third, it was strangely convenient and fortunate for Amgen that only the 960 mg dose—out of the several doses for which Amgen sought a patent—actually received a patent. Fourth, some questions are unanswerable: Did Amgen abandon pursuit of a patent on the 240 mg dose because the 240 mg was obvious in view of prior art, with Amgen’s consolation prize being a patent on the 960 mg dose? Or did Amgen end up pursuing a patent only for the lucrative 960 mg dose because Amgen was determined from early on to obtain FDA approval of that lucrative dose? What is clear is that all the pieces fell into financial place for Amgen: It got accelerated approval from the FDA for the most lucrative dose, 960 mg; it got a patent for that same dose; and, notwithstanding that the 240 mg dose was shown to be optimal both in Amgen’s patent applications and in Amgen’s post-market study, the FDA still ended up backing (at least provisionally) the 960 mg dose.
How might better communication between the FDA and the PTO have altered this history, if not changed the outcome? Had the PTO informed the FDA in March 2020 of Amgen’s view that the 240 mg dose was optimal, the FDA might have been primed from earlier on to demand a study of the 240 mg dose. And if the FDA had had study-based evidence of the 240 mg dose’s optimality earlier on, the FDA might have embarked on a course less accepting of the 960 mg dose and (as Prof. Mark Ratain suggests) might have encouraged Amgen to seek accelerated approval of the 240 mg dose. Conversely, had the FDA immediately disclosed to the PTO the results of the post-market study showing the 240 mg dose to be optimal, the PTO would have been armed against secondary patenting of non-optimal doses. And the PTO might have been prepared to entertain a proceeding, statutory law permitting, to reexamine the grant of a patent for what appears to have been a demonstrably non-optimal dose.

III.D.

## Extended-Release Oxycodone
Among the many charges leveled against Purdue Pharma, one includes obtaining a patent from the PTO that Sarpatwari and co-authors assert ‘would have been obvious to any pharmaceutical chemist’. As Purdue’s protection cliff for extended-release morphine neared, the company filed for a patent applying the method used to create extended-release morphine to create an extended-release version of oxycodone. The PTO granted the patent. 
Sarpatwari et al. argue that substituting morphine for oxycodone using the existing method could have been easily handled by a person having ordinary skill in the art of drug chemistry. The juxtaposition of a weak secondary patent with conclusion of protection on a prior drug could indicate that Purdue Pharma attempted to use the patent system improperly to extend its protection on morphine by shifting the morphine technology to oxycodone. If so, the patent system may have played its own small role in the opioid-dependence epidemic.
Across all of these examples, one should note that patent examiners face enormous constraints if they wish to reject a patent application, particularly for obviousness. First, a patent examiner’s decision that the invention is obvious to those skilled in the art does not end the matter. In patent applications and in court, an assertion that those skilled in the art would simply combine information in the prior art to reach the invention can be overcome by, among other things, a showing that the combination led to unexpected results. When patent examiners reject an application for obviousness, an applicant can respond with a simple declaration from the applicant’s expert asserting that the results were unexpected. In practice, that declaration is uncontested with no opportunity at the patent-granting stage for rebuttal. Examiners are backed into a corner, forced to accept the declaration at face value, when such a declaration is self-serving, at best, and of questionable value, at worst. As a result, the patent examiner’s own judgment can be overcome with uncontested declarations from a pharmaceutical company’s experts. 
Second, the Federal Circuit, which decides all patent appeals, recently ruled that if a patent examiner rejects an application for obviousness, the examiner must explain why and explain it with specificity. Thus, an overburdened patent examiner must take the time to justify the decision in detail, a deterrent to even the most dedicated examiner.
As discussed in the solutions section below, both of these handicaps for patent examiners could be eased by the ability to hear, learn from, and reference the FDA, an agency full of persons with ordinary skill in the art.

III.E.

## Evidence of Inconsistent Statements in Case Law
As noted above, inconsistent statements to the FDA and PTO need not exist for informational asymmetry to burden agency decision-making—for example, when the FDA uniquely possesses information relevant to patentability, yet the PTO labors in the absence of such information. For now, however, we will focus more particularly on the problem of inconsistent statements and on litigated examples.
The problem of inconsistent FDA-PTO statements, not to mention obvious ones, extends beyond the theoretical and the case studies above. First are the two cases—Bruno and Belcher—cited by the PTO in its own public pronouncement on the problem (see infra Part IV.E.). Bruno Indep. Living Aids, Inc. v. Acorn Mobility Servs. upheld a finding of inequitable conduct because Bruno, the holder of a patent on a stairlift, did not disclose to the PTO information on invalidating prior art that was submitted to the FDA in a 510(k) application. , The 510(k) application was submitted to show that Bruno’s device was ‘substantially equivalent’ to a predicate device and thus that FDA should permit marketing. ,  Belcher Pharms., LLC v. Hospira upheld a finding of inequitable conduct because the patent owner failed to give the PTO the information previously given to the FDA about prior art that was inconsistent with the position taken before the PTO.
But there are cases besides these two. Merck & Co. v. Danbury Pharmacal, Inc. upheld a finding of inequitable conduct because the patent applicant gave prior art material to the FDA but not to the PTO, and also misled the PTO by providing the PTO with a statement about side-effects that was different from its statement to the FDA. Ranbaxy Labs. Ltd v. Abbott Labs. found, in partially denying Abbott’s motion to preliminarily enjoin marketing of a generic drug, that Abbott’s disclosure of two studies to the FDA but not to the PTO constituted inequitable conduct, rendering two patents-in-suit unenforceable. Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Ben Venue Laboratories, in which defendant charged that plaintiff’s patents were unenforceable because of plaintiff’s inequitable conduct, denied plaintiff’s motion for summary judgment, holding that plaintiff’s characterizations of a drug study ‘were profoundly inconsistent’ and that plaintiff ‘took one position before the PTO, and another before the FDA’. A trio of cases—Baxter Int’l, Inc. v. CareFusion Corp. ; Baxter Int’l, Inc. v. CareFusion Corp. ; and Exergen Corp v. KAZ USA —involved statements that were arguably inconsistent, but, because there was insufficient proof of deceptive intent, the court found no inequitable conduct under the heightened standard adopted by the Federal Circuit in its Therasense decision (see infra Part V.A.).
Putting aside claims of inequitable conduct, the Federal Circuit has repeatedly held that the decision of whether to grant (or invalidate) a patent may be based on statements made to the FDA, including statements that undermine positions taken before the PTO. In In re Copaxone Consol. Cases, the Federal Circuit, in upholding the invalidation of patent claims, endorsed the district court’s reliance on the patentee’s ‘statement to FDA that “one may certainly expect”’ a claimed effect based on the prior art—a statement that, while not ‘invalidating prior art’, was ‘evidence of a [skilled artisan]’s motivations and expectations when reading the prior art at the time of the invention’. Similarly, in Pfizer, Inc. v. Apotex, Inc., the Federal Circuit, in invalidating patent claims and rejecting the patent owner’s argument that a drug’s effects were unexpected, relied on ‘a suggestion in [the patent owner]’s supplemental filing with the FDA that it was known that the [drug] would work for its intended purpose ...’. As discussed more fully below (see Part V.B.-C.), the foregoing list of cases belies the pharma industry’s assertion that inconsistent FDA-PTO statements can be found in only two cases.

III.F.

## Beyond Case Law
Patents have a life well beyond a judicial opinion on the merits. Since 95–97 per cent of patent cases settle, , , and since most patent cases settle before a merits decision can issue, using case law as the exclusive search universe for locating inconsistent FDA-PTO statements (see infra Part V.B.-C.) will vastly underreport such inconsistency. Limiting the search to decisional law excludes most of the actual search universe. Relatedly, decisional law underreports the incidence of inconsistency because detection of inconsistency is so difficult, especially for private parties, which lack the resources that government offices have. By their nature, inconsistent assertions do not readily come to light for several reasons. The maker of the inconsistent statement (typically, the plaintiff patentee) has no incentive to disclose its own wrongdoing. The opposing party (typically, the defendant alleged infringer) lacks the legal and financial tools to discover the inconsistency. And the procedures followed by the relevant agencies—the FDA and the PTO—are not designed to reveal inconsistencies. The Public Interest Patent Law Institute (PIPLI) has succinctly articulated the problems: ‘Given the public’s limited ability to investigate false statements to the USPTO—particularly those that conflict with nonpublic FDA submissions—the examples that are known likely represent the tip of the iceberg’. And in a letter to Sen. Tillis and PTO Director Vidal, PIPLI similarly stated, ‘Unfortunately, conflicting submissions often go unnoticed. In most cases, the FDA and USPTO do not know that they are both considering statements about the same product from the same company’. 

IV.

## CURRENT COOPERATION OVERTURES
Recent discussion about FDA-PTO cooperation began four years ago, on July 9, 2021, as part of a Biden Administration Executive Order related to many different competition issues. Subsequent discussion among Senators, the FDA, and the PTO indicated that part and parcel of that need was the necessity of exposing inconsistent statements being made to the FDA and the PTO, and of information-sharing between the FDA and the PTO. The following section describes the sequence of events and the lack of progress.
IV.A.

## Executive Order
The 2021 Executive Order focused on a range of competition-related issues. Regarding pharmaceuticals, the Order noted that the price of prescription drugs in the U.S. is far higher than in other countries and that ‘patent and other laws have been misused to inhibit or delay—for years and even decades—competition ... denying Americans access to lower-cost drugs’. 
As an initial step to ‘help ensure that the patent system, while incentivizing innovation, does not also unjustifiably delay generic drug and biosimilar competition’, the Order directed that the FDA write a letter to the PTO ‘enumerating and describing any relevant concerns of the FDA’. Finally, the Order directed that agencies with overlapping jurisdiction over the ‘policing of anticompetitive conduct’ ‘are encouraged to coordinate their efforts ... with respect to ... sharing relevant information and industry data ...’. 

IV.B.

## Leahy-Tillis Letter
Two months later, the legislative branch joined the fray, focusing on the risk of inconsistent statements and on actions that the PTO could take alone—that is, without the FDA. In a letter to the director of the PTO dated September 9, 2021, Senators Leahy (Dem. Vermont) and Tillis (Rep. North Carolina) expressed their concern over inconsistent statements and asked the PTO to consider requiring applicants to disclose additional information during patent examination, including relevant statements to other agencies regarding the invention or prior art. The letter noted that inconsistencies are discovered too late because ‘[t]hese conflicting statements too often are submitted confidentially or go unnoticed until after a patent issues, and only then do third parties have the incentive or ability to review the patent owner’s statements before both agencies’. Accordingly, the letter stated that at the time of patent examination, the PTO must require patent applicants to disclose relevant statements made to other agencies about the invention or prior art. As noted, this approach would require action by a single agency alone, which is considerably less complex than interagency cooperation, and would put the burden on patent applicants, which relieves the burden on the government. On the other hand, the timing might take place long before statements are made to the FDA.

IV.C.

## FDA Letter to PTO
On September 10, 2021, the next day after the Leahy-Tillis letter, the FDA carried out the President’s instructions regarding sending a letter to the director of the PTO concerning anti-competitive practices that impede market entry of generics and biosimilars. Among other suggestions, the FDA’s letter offered to train patent examiners about the FDA’s publicly available information and databases, thereby helping examiners identify prior art. The letter further asked whether examiners should receive additional resources in order to better resolve all the complexities of applications for pharmaceutical patents. 

IV.D.

## PTO Response
After this initial flurry, the PTO responded roughly a year later, on July 6, 2022. Without mentioning the Leahy-Tillis letter directly, the PTO’s response demonstrated consideration of the letter’s substance. Among the many issues addressed in its response, the PTO discussed the risk of inconsistent statements and, more generally, the problem of requiring patent applicants to submit information to the PTO that the applicants have submitted to other federal agencies. As part of the PTO’s own actions, the agency noted that it is exploring initiatives to require that patent applicants provide relevant information to the PTO that has been submitted to other agencies and remind patent applicants of their disclosure obligations and the ramifications of failing to disclose required information at the PTO. 
Regarding inter-agency cooperation as one of the ways to address the problem of patent-related barriers to generic and biosimilar entry, the letter stated in express (albeit non-committal) terms that the PTO will ‘work with the FDA to evaluate consistency in representations to the USPTO ... and the FDA’ and ‘will explore with the FDA whether other avenues exist to determine whether patent applicants have submitted inconsistent statements between the agencies’. 
The PTO also stated that it would continue discussion with the FDA, collaborate with FDA to train examiners regarding publicly available FDA resources relevant to prior art searches and the state of the art in pharmaceuticals, and would ‘[r]emain open’ to discussing anti-competitive practices, like patent thicketing, evergreening, and product hopping. These steps represented considerable progress, although the tentative nature of the language, with words such as ‘will explore’, ‘remain open’, and ‘is ... exploring’, demonstrates the challenges of altering an agency’s internal practices, let alone initiating any form of content cooperation. Altering an agency’s internal practices is like changing the course of an aircraft carrier. It is not easy and takes time.

IV.E.

## Disclosure Notice
True to its word, the PTO published a notice (Disclosure Notice) in the federal register three weeks later, on July 29, 2022. The Disclosure Notice detailed every patent applicant’s ‘duty to disclose to the USPTO information material to the patentability of a claimed invention’. That disclosure duty was described as a component of the more general duty of candor and good faith, and applies in both patent prosecution and in PTAB proceedings. The Disclosure Notice was particularly noteworthy because the key document cited as the motivation for the Notice—other than the Executive Order—was the Leahy-Tillis letter concerning inconsistent statements being made to the FDA and the PTO. Clearly, the Disclosure Notice regarded the risk of inconsistent statements as being a primary area of concern.
According to the Disclosure Notice, information is material to patentability if it ‘refutes, or is inconsistent with, a position the applicant takes in’ arguing for patentability or in opposing a PTO argument against patentability. Thus, the Disclosure Notice stated that every patent applicant’s duty of candor includes a duty to disclose ‘statements made to the USPTO that are inconsistent with statements submitted to the FDA ...’. The duty also includes a duty to correct the record: ‘If a party to a USPTO proceeding discovers that an earlier position taken in a submission to the USPTO or another Government agency was incorrect or inconsistent with other statements made by the party, the party must promptly correct the record’, including by submitting the inconsistent information. Moreover, the patent examiner, for purposes of obtaining a complete record, may request information that is ‘not necessarily material to patentability’, and such information may include ‘statements made or information submitted to other Government agencies such as the FDA’. Anyone submitting a paper to the PTO also has a duty of reasonable inquiry:
[E]ach party presenting a paper to the USPTO…has a duty to perform an inquiry that is reasonable under the circumstances. This reasonable inquiry may comprise reviewing documents that are submitted to or received from other Government agencies, including the FDA. 
Those who have a duty of disclosure or reasonable inquiry must
ensure that the statements made to the USPTO and other Government agencies, or any statements made on their behalf to other Government agencies regarding the claimed subject matter, are consistent. ... [P]roviding material information to other Government agencies, including the FDA, while simultaneously withholding the same information from the USPTO undermines both the intent and spirit of the duty of disclosure and violates those duties. 
One should note that the duties described above extend as well to information received from other Government agencies:
[E]ach individual with a duty to disclose, or party with a duty of reasonable inquiry, should review documents it receives from other Government agencies to determine whether the information should be submitted to the USPTO. 
Nor can a corporate applicant sidestep the duty of candor by walling off the part of the company that applies to the FDA for drug approval from the part of the company that prosecutes patents before the PTO. Finally, the Disclosure Notice warned that an applicant’s failure to comply with these duties can constitute inequitable conduct that can render a patent unenforceable. 

IV.F.

## Listening Session
On January 19, 2023, the PTO and the FDA together conducted a ‘Listening Session’ on Joint USPTO-FDA Collaboration Initiatives. In addition to those invited to present at the session, 80 comments were submitted, by academicians, public interest groups, representatives of the pharma industry and patent bar, and others. 
Although the comments collectively described dozens of proposals for FDA-PTO collaboration, one of the most notable sets of comments concerned the risk of inconsistent statements made to the PTO and the FDA. These consistency-focused comments fell into two groups. First were those (made primarily by critics of the current system) which stressed that the FDA’s failure to share information with the PTO—especially drug applicants’ statements—allowed the PTO to grant drug patents that should never have been granted because the FDA’s information would have shown the inventions to be obvious or violative of the ‘on-sale bar’, see supra Part II.B.). These comments stressed that the consequent proliferation of weak or invalid drug patents created a barrier to competition and generic entry.
The second set of comments consisted of those made primarily by representatives of the pharma industry and the patent bar which questioned the very notion of an inconsistency problem and asserted that evidence of such a problem was lacking. These comments asserted (often in conspicuously similar language) that major changes in agency policy and practice should be ‘evidence-based’, that evidence establishing inconsistent statements was sparse, and thus, that no change in agency policy or practice was necessary. For the sake of brevity, we will call these two groups ‘insiders’ (for industry and patent bar) and ‘outsiders’ (for critics of the current system).
The contrast between the two sets of comments displays a different framing of the problem. Insiders take a more narrow view of the problem as primarily one of inconsistent statements. Critics take a broader view that includes both inconsistent statements and information that would help examiners identify and address weaknesses in patent applications. The two viewpoints also differ on the extent of the problem and whether more limited changes to existing regimes are sufficient. Finally, the two viewpoints echo somewhat, although not precisely, the divide between responses by the FDA and responses by the PTO. Both agencies agree that patent examiners would benefit from informal education sessions about the state of the art in medicine and medical treatments, as well as from training in the FDA’s public data system for prior art searches. These are significant improvements in the flow of information between the two agencies. The agencies differ, however, on the extent to which more direct and formalized processes that would provide communication on specific drug or patent applications would be beneficial.

V.

## EXAMINING THE DIVIDE
As described in the prior section, patent system insiders and outsiders divide on certain perspectives, some of which overlap with the differing perspectives of the FDA and the PTO. Together, the dividing issues center on whether the problem goes beyond inconsistent statements, whether the problem is rare, what timing is relevant for any inter-agency interaction, and whether smaller, more informal changes to the existing regimes are sufficient. The following section examines each of these divides.
V.A.

## Are Existing Regimes Effective?
As noted above, patent system insiders and outsiders, as well as the PTO and the FDA to some extent, differ on the extent to which existing regimes are effective and whether smaller, more informal additions are sufficient or whether formalized and more extensive pathways are needed. The existing disclosure regimes lie in three places: the duty of disclosure required by the PTO; 21 U.S.C. § 372(d); and 37 C.F.R. § 1.105.
Although patent applicants have a duty to ‘disclose to the USPTO information material to the patentability of a claimed invention’, the primary means of enforcing that duty—the inequitable conduct doctrine—has prerequisites that are exceedingly difficult to satisfy. As the Federal Circuit held in its Therasense decision in 2011, the ‘accused infringer must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the applicant knew of the reference, knew that it was material, and made a deliberate decision to withhold it’. And the disclosure violation must be ‘but for’ material: ‘When an applicant fails to disclose prior art to the PTO, that prior art is but-for material if the PTO would not have allowed a claim had it been aware of the undisclosed prior art’.  Therasense’s heightened intent requirement has made it especially difficult to establish inequitable conduct: Post-Therasense, 87 per cent of the failures to prove inequitable conduct are due to failure to satisfy that intent requirement. Aside from the difficulty of proving inequitable conduct, it should also be noted that inequitable conduct is a defense to patent infringement—and thus presupposes an infringement litigation in which to bring the defense. But since many breaches of the disclosure duty can happen without litigation, the litigation defense of inequitable conduct is at best a haphazard means of enforcing the duty. Astonishingly, the PTO has no mechanism for investigating applicants’ compliance with the duty of disclosure, and patent examiners are barred from addressing applicants’ compliance. 
Nor has FDA-PTO communication been fostered by 21 U.S.C. § 372(d), which requires the FDA, upon request of the PTO, to ‘furnish full and complete information with respect to such questions relating to drugs as the [PTO] may submit concerning any patent application’. The PTO rarely uses this provision, , , even though the FDA often has information regarding whether the invention sought to be patented is non-novel and/or runs afoul of the ‘on-sale bar’. 
Under 37 C.F.R. § 1.105, a patent examiner may request a patent applicant to provide additional information, but, again, the provision is not often invoked. The impediments to the use of this provision are numerous: A supervisor’s approval is often necessary before a request may be made; requests cannot be made unless ‘the benefit from the information exceeds the burden in obtaining information’; and examiners are not permitted to base a final rejection on information received pursuant to a request. 
Thus, existing regimes are insufficient, with some of the insufficiency resting on regulatory barriers that incentivize inertia. Regardless of the causes, the existing pathways for sanctions are rarely used. One could argue that despite the lack of use, the mere threat of a sanction is enough to keep people in line. With the value of patents in pharmaceuticals, however, the siren call of revenue could easily overcome the deterrence effect of an unlikely sanction. In other words, a duty without a realistic risk of consequences is a paper tiger.

V.B.

## Is the Risk of Inconsistent Statements Significant?
In opposing recent efforts to prevent inconsistent FDA-PTO statements, patent industry insiders suggest that the inconsistency problem is too ‘rare’ to warrant new measures, arguing that only two cases over the last 34 years reflect inconsistent statements about biopharmaceuticals. Unfortunately, the assertion is based on an artificially limited universe of information. Specifically, the underlying analysis is restricted to cases of ‘inequitable conduct’. As explained above (supra Part III.E.) and in the next section (infra Part V.C.), the analysis grossly understates the applicable body of caselaw.
Echoing the two-case suggestion, other insiders point to the fact that the PTO’s Disclosure Notice cites only two cases. , One could imagine the latter as a reasonable point, except that the Disclosure Notice never purported to perform an exhaustive search.

V.C.

## The Two-Case Argument’s Limited Universe
The two-case argument rests on an unduly limited body of case law. First, the argument discounts (i) cases that predate the Federal Circuit’s Therasense decision, which made it much harder to establish the deceptive intent necessary for a finding of inequitable conduct (see supra Part V.A.); (ii) district court cases (as opposed to Federal Circuit cases); and (iii) FDA device cases (as opposed to FDA drug cases). 
First, inconsistent statements happen regardless of deceptive intent. Concerns raised over inconsistent statements are not necessarily aimed at nefarious actions or those that rise to the level of intentional deceptions. Society’s interest in economic efficiency and promoting innovation reaches to innocent inconsistencies, for example, when the company’s patent prosecution department does not interact sufficiently with the company’s division applying for FDA approval, or when the FDA and the PTO communications are separated in time. Second, district court cases show inconsistent statements just as readily as Federal Circuit cases do. Third, the ease with which patent applicants can tell the PTO one thing and the FDA another is shown as fully by medical device cases as by drug cases. In short, anyone assessing the incidence of inconsistency should fully review pre-Therasense, district court, and medical device cases, in addition to post-Therasense, Federal Circuit, and drug cases. 
Another exclusion resides in limiting the search to cases upholding a charge of inequitable conduct. Cases rejecting a charge of inequitable conduct can also reveal inconsistent FDA-PTO statements. Recall that a charge of inequitable conduct can be rejected purely for lack of deceptive intent. For that reason, the search should expand to include cases that, while rejecting the inequitable-conduct charge for lack of deceptive intent, nonetheless involve inconsistent FDA-PTO statements. Expanding the search in this way makes sense. The problematic conduct here includes all inconsistent FDA-PTO statements, regardless of mens rea. Where a statement made to the FDA was withheld from the PTO, what matters for present purposes is not whether withholding was with deceptive intent but whether there was withholding at all. As noted in one submission to the Listening Session, decrying inconsistent FDA-PTO statements is not to impute nefarious motives to pharmaceutical companies. It is hardly surprising when companies emphasize for the FDA that little is new, so there is nothing to worry about, and no extra data are needed, and yet emphasize for the PTO that the drug product is wonderfully different and innovative. Applicants are speaking to each agency’s interests. But society’s larger interest is to ensure that each agency gets to the truth of the matter and understands the big picture. 
Even ignoring the difficulty of proving intent post-Therasense, the larger picture is that intentionally deceptive inconsistency is only one sliver of the much broader problem of inconsistency generally. And the crux of that broader problem is the undue power it gives to patent applicants over patent examiners.
Absent new rules, and despite the steps described in Part III above, applicants can still unilaterally blind examiners to FDA statements that militate against a patent grant, whether the applicants are acting with deceptive intent or not. Emphatically, it is for the examiner and no one else—least of all the applicant—to resolve questions about what information should be before the examiner. As the Federal Circuit itself has held, ‘Close cases should be resolved by disclosure, not unilaterally by the applicant’. Case law pre- , and post-Therasense applies that holding. The holding is also consistent with long-standing PTO policy: ‘Presumably, applicants will continue to submit information for consideration by the Office in applications rather than making and relying on their own determinations of materiality. An incentive remains to submit the information to the Office because it will result in a strengthened patent and will avoid later questions of materiality and intent to deceive’. Perfectly summarizing the problem in a recently filed brief, the State of California stated that ‘by withholding or misrepresenting material information relating to patentability’, drug companies ‘tak[e] advantage of the ex parte nature of patent proceedings’. 
One should also note that patent system insiders and the PTO itself have engaged in an additional form of narrowing, wholly apart from the exclusion of inconsistent statements that never get to the courthouse steps (supra Part III.F.). By limiting the problem to directly inconsistent statements and excluding patents improperly granted because the invention fails the elements of novelty or non-obviousness, one further limits the universe of the problem and the potential impact of any adjustments—both small and large.

V.D.

## The Timing Conundrum
Two timing perspectives play a role in the division between patent industry insiders and outsiders along with the divide between the PTO and the FDA. First, should government mitigate a risk before the risk fully materializes into harm? Second, when should any cooperation between the PTO and the FDA take place in the life cycle of patenting and drug approval?
V.D.1.

## Should government mitigate a risk before the risk materializes fully into actual harm?
The patent industry insiders’ argument concerning the limited number of findings of inequitable conduct based on inconsistent statements contains an implicit assumption: Government should not act if a risk has not fully materialized into actual harm; it should wait until sufficient harm comes to pass. That assumption is remarkably odd. The standard by which the government decides to mitigate a risk has never been whether the risk has eventuated in actual harm. No government has ever been so hobbled, nor should it be.
Instead, government must remain able to mitigate a risk before the risk materializes into actual harm. As one scholar has stated, ‘Risk is distinct from, and antecedent to, harm[;] the existence of risk may permit or require a state to act before harm has occurred’. 
Indeed, any other view leads to absurd results. Taken to its logical conclusion, such doctrine would dictate that a gaping hole in America’s air defenses warrants no mitigation unless and until the Russians successfully bomb the American homeland. Such an approach cannot possibly ground a theory of governmental action. Thus, even if the examples of problematic statements to one agency or the other were far more limited, government can and should act before more extensive harm occurs.

V.D.2.

## When in a drug’s life cycle should FDA-PTO cooperation occur?
Although the PTO’s response (supra Part IV.D.) suggests general training for patent examiners without any timing limitation, the PTO implicitly limits its duty approaches to the time period during which a patent application is pending. For example, the PTO’s Disclosure Notice explains that the duty of candor for a party submitting written information to the PTO includes disclosing ‘statements made to the USPTO that are inconsistent with statements submitted to the FDA’ and an obligation to correct the record if the party ‘discovers that an earlier position taken in a submission to the USPTO or another Government agency was incorrect or inconsistent with other statements made by the party’. However, the wording of the Disclosure Notice suggests that those duties occur only within the patent examination process or some other active process at the PTO. For example, the Disclosure Notice describes the duty to correct the record as occurring ‘[i]f a party to a USPTO proceeding discovers’ an inaccuracy or inconsistency. This and other language could imply that such a duty does not exist outside the timing of a PTO proceeding.
As described above (supra Parts II.B., IV.B.), timing can be a challenge in controlling problematic statements. FDA approvals can occur long after patents have been granted, meaning that the patent examination process has ended and potentially all post-grant challenges at the PTO have ended. In addition, applicants for approval of biologic drugs are not required to give the FDA a list of patents, let alone to update that list. As a result, the FDA’s materials would not even link the drug to any particular patent rights. Thus, limiting duties to specific moments in the life cycle of a drug risks missing problematic statements that occur asynchronously.

VI.

## PROPOSED SOLUTIONS TO DISMANTLE THE WALL
The recent congressional and administrative activities related to inter-agency cooperation have brought tremendous progress. The PTO’s commitment to having FDA staff present information to PTO staff on the state of the art in various subfields of drugs and devices constitutes an enormous advance and provides the potential for an enormous flow of informal information exchanges. Similarly, training patent examiners to use the FDA’s extensive, public materials for purposes of locating prior art could open significant, new horizons for examiners. In addition, simply highlighting the problem of inconsistent and problematic statements creates, at least for the moment, heightened sensitivity to the issue among staff at both agencies. One should not underestimate the significance of these activities. The goal is to use these initial steps as a scaffolding upon which to construct a truly effective and efficient solution to the information asymmetries plaguing the system.
VI.A.

## Challenges
Crafting a way to move beyond these initial steps faces challenges that cannot be taken lightly. The following sections consider the major obstacles one must navigate in designing effective changes to agency process.
VI.A.1.

## Agency burdens: endless paperwork, limited results, and switching costs
A major concern of changing agency processes is the risk of creating endless paperwork and meetings that waste everyone’s time and energy. Scholars and commentators have described, for example, how various efforts have simply ended up hamstringing agency personnel from making any useful decisions. 
A classic example of this problem is the Paperwork Reduction Act. In an effort to reduce inefficiency at agencies, the federal government undertook an initiative to ensure the reduction of useless paperwork. The effort resulted in the laborious creation of a policy that dictated a form that agency staff must fill out asserting that each project conforms with the Paperwork Reduction Act. In other words, the Paperwork Reduction Act simply created reams of useless paperwork. 
An offshoot of the useless paperwork problem is the problem of giving over-burdened agency staff the equivalent of a crushing workload that breaks the agency’s budget—all of that for limited, if any, results. In fact, when informing the Congressional Budget Office of the anticipated cost of a bill to improve FDA-PTO communication, the FDA estimated that the proposal would cost it $90 million, while the PTO estimated that the proposal would cost it $7 million. The bill was reported favorably out of the Senate Judiciary Committee, but never made it any further in the congressional process. 
Some of the problem rests with efforts that fail to account for the constraints already in place, a problem that agency staff understand intimately from experience. In addition, one cannot ignore the burden of switching costs, in which the costs of making any changes includes the burden on employees who must learn and implement new ways of doing things. Agency staff, in particular, may resist and object to process changes from the understandable perspective of the exhausting effort required. Consider how much fun it is when one’s information technology department changes to a new online, collaborative workspace system, or a new cloud storage method, or even just makes ‘simple’ changes like providing a new laptop or pushing through a system update. Oh joy.
However, an outsider’s perspective can be useful in breaking the confinement of habitual viewpoints. Where an insider might say, ‘this is the only way to do it’, an outsider might point out that the emperor is naked, nevertheless.

VI.A.2.

## Burden on patent applicants
Another serious risk concerns unnecessary burdens on various parties and systems beyond the agencies. For drug companies, expanding duties and penalties could result in an endless stream of useless litigation. Overall efficiency would not be enhanced if, as a result of ensuring that agencies tasked with approving applications have full and consistent information, anyone challenging the validity of a patent in court could now routinely add a new charge. What would the new charge be? That the patent holder violated a broad and vaguely worded obligation because of a statement in some document given to another agency a decade ago, when circumstances were different. The result would be an enormous burden on patent holders and the judiciary, dwarfing any efficiency gains.
Similarly, adding a process that will require agencies and applicants to provide endless folders and boxes (or the electronic equivalent) to both agencies will not improve knowledge flow or efficiency. As modern data scientists will tell you, the key is knowing where to look. 

VI.A.3.

## Timing troubles
Timing provides an additional serious obstacle. As described briefly in Parts II.B. and IV.B. above, basic drug patents normally are obtained long before a company begins working with the FDA on the various phases of animal and human trials and, later, on a request for approval. Any inconsistent statement made to the FDA would take place long after the patent examination process has ended. Thus, by requiring that obligations attach only during the period of patent examination, one ensures that the useful information won’t exist then.
In addition, patents often do not list a particular drug or even a particular formulation. And particularly with biologic drugs, drug companies have no obligation to clarify which patents relate to each product. Even patent information for non-biologic drugs is limited in some ways. Thus, information provided by the PTO to the FDA could be too old and could suffer from either agency’s inability to know what information would be useful.
In short, each of the strands of difficulties above wind together to create a Gordian knot—an image from an ancient legend in which a king tied an extraordinarily intricate knot and prophesized that whoever untied the knot would rule Asia. Untangling the knot appears to be an impossible task.

VI.B.

## Slicing Through the Gordian Knot
In referencing the Gordian knot, one would be remiss in failing to note that, according to legend, Alexander the Great broke the knot by cutting through it with a sword. In other words, even with intractable problems, it is possible to develop bold and creative solutions that slice through the heart of the problem.
Here, the heart of the problem lies with insufficient information for either agency at a critical point when the agency must take action or make a decision. As described in the challenges subsection above (supra Part VI.A.), any interaction must impose a limited burden on both agencies and on those who must apply to the agencies for pharmaceutical patents or approvals. Moreover, it may not be possible to solve all angles of the problem. Moving to the heart of the issue in a thoughtful and targeted manner, however, could provide enormous benefit.
Along these lines, Senator Durbin, along with co-sponsors Senators Grassley, Tillis, Leahy, Cornyn, and Coons, introduced a bill to enhance PTO-FDA coordination titled the Interagency Patent Coordination and Improvement Act of 2022. The bill contained important but general language, essentially leaving the design of cooperation to an interagency task force, although noting that the task force would operate towards specific ends. 
Working from that base, a pathway can be designed that meets the challenges set out above. For a successful approach, one would need to: (i) pick the topic; (ii) pick the timing; (iii) limit the burdens; and thereby (iv) lubricate the wheels of agency decision-making. The goal is forward motion, not meddling. The approach we describe below does not solve all problems, but is a reasonable, efficient, and effective step.
VI.B.1.

## Picking the topic
To streamline the pathway we will describe, the pathway should begin by asking patent applicants to include a short, ‘plain language’ description of their invention along with the patent application. The plain language description need not be considered a binding legal statement of the reach of a particular invention. In fact, it is better for the statement not to be legally binding in relation to the metes and bounds of the rights, to avoid the type of careful and coded wordsmithing that occupies enormous time for patent and other attorneys. The purpose is to signal non-patent experts at the FDA that they might wish to look further at a particular patent application. And although the statement is aimed at FDA staff, it may also help the public search for patents. In any event, the plain language statement should remain non-binding as described. 
For picking the topic, the agencies might focus on secondary patent claims, at least as a starting point. Secondary patent claims—those related to pharmaceuticals other than claims to a single chemical or biologic molecule—have incurred much of the criticism regarding non-novel and obvious patents, as well as regarding inconsistent statements.
In addition, many modern secondary patents are filed close to, or even after, a drug has been approved, making it more likely the FDA is familiar with the scientific landscape surrounding the drug. To limit the pool even further, the agencies could begin by focusing on secondary patents filed after a drug receives FDA approval. In the alternative, the agencies could limit the pool to claims related to particular types of secondary claims, claims to new indications for the molecule, new formulations of the molecule, doses, dosing schedules, or drug combinations.

VI.B.2.

## Picking the timing
As for picking the time, the period in which a patent examiner is reviewing the responses an applicant submits after the examiner has filed a final rejection of the application would provide an ideal juncture. One should note that in common parlance, a ‘rejection’ may mean that any hope of going to the prom with your crush is lost. Not so in patent parlance. A rejection, even one designated a ‘final rejection’, is not the end of the road. An applicant can file a response or submit a request for continued examination, or RCE. 
In particular, patent applicants can respond to a so-called final rejection for non-obviousness with an affidavit from the applicant’s chosen scientific expert. The affidavit would attest to clinical or pharmacological findings created with the invention that would be unexpected to those skilled in the art, rendering the invention non-obvious. In practice, the patent examiner must simply accept the affidavit as accurate. 
To combat such declarations—which are unexamined, unopposed, and potentially self-serving—as well as other responses applicants submit to rejections, patent examiners would benefit from the ability to receive input from an expert at the FDA. Focusing on this time window could provide an opportune period, when examiners have already engaged in rounds of narrowing and rejecting, and when the applicant’s responses are particularly difficult to combat.

VI.B.3.

## Limiting the burdens
Limiting burdens will be critical. Rather than asking the FDA to consult on every final rejection including secondary patent claims, the pathway could involve periodically giving FDA staff a list of the short, plain-language statements of patents in which the PTO has received responses to a final rejection. This would allow FDA staff the opportunity to skim the list and look further at (or reach out to examiners for) inventions that appear potentially suspect from an expert’s perspective. And, of course, patent examiners should have an easy method of reaching out to the FDA, if an examiner could use a consultation at any time. This type of process would be far less cumbersome and burdensome than requiring that the two agencies must consult on even a limited group of patents, no matter what.

VI.B.4.

## Lubricating the wheels of agency decision-making
Easing any rigid requirement of consultation on everything within a class of patents could ease the sense that the agency cooperation constitutes meddling. The idea is to help make the PTO’s processes smoother and more effective, giving examiners a simple and practical way to identify problematic patent claims and strengthen the examiner’s responses. The ultimate goal would be having the endless, back and forth examination process focus more quickly on what, if anything, is innovative in a patent application.
Of course, patent applicants may complain that in life, quicker often means less accurate. In this case, however, it is difficult to imagine that the ability to consult with scientists at the nation’s expert drug-approval agency would render decisions less accurate.
Nor need there be concern that input from the FDA to the PTO might compromise the independence of patent examiners. The FDA’s input would be expert assistance, not ex parte advocacy. Federal law—in the form of 21 U.S.C. § 372(d) —has long contemplated FDA input in PTO decisions, without any concern that examiner independence might be eroded. The Executive Order, the FDA Letter and PTO Response, the Listening Session, and other efforts at improving inter-agency communication have raised no concern of such erosion. Any residual concern can be alleviated by a requirement that the patent examiner disclose to the patent applicant any input received from the FDA.
Both agencies could face the problem that the FDA may have information that cannot be publicly disclosed because it is proprietary. How could they share perspectives that flow from such confidential information? If the information the FDA wishes to share with the PTO is confidential, the PTO could provide for the confidentiality to continue as long as appropriate. Although this may run counter to the ethos in which all written exchanges during patent examination must be made public in the record, in practice, many exchanges between the applicant and the examiner are not captured in writing. Similarly, if confidentiality raises hackles, the agencies could limit their communication to oral, rather than written—although that might make the exchanges more cumbersome. A quick email with an attachment may be far more efficient for staff than trying to arrange an interaction in real time. The agencies also should be mindful that assertions of confidentiality are not necessarily accurate. For example, trade secrecy cannot trump the Patent Act, and trade secret law does not displace federal patent law. 
The information exchange process should be designed to benefit the FDA, as well. The same principles outlined above could be applied to allow the PTO to comment on or respond to requests when the FDA receives a request for a 510(k) equivalence approval, where the inventor has filed for a new patent. The language of the Senate Judiciary Committee’s high-level approach also suggests that the PTO could help the FDA identify relevant patents that apply to a particular drug application. Once again, the same principles can be applied.
In short, although invisible, the wall between the FDA and the PTO is real. The foregoing provides a roadmap for detecting and addressing it, despite the challenges. One can only hope that, with this roadmap, legislators and regulators will dismantle the wall rather than sitting on the fence.
