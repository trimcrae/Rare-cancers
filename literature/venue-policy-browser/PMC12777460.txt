ZhangYilin – ]. Talent transfer (TT) initiatives have long served as strategic tools to optimize athlete utilization [ – ]. TT usually refers to the practice model of transferring athletes who are already successful in one sport to another sport for development by selecting them [ ]. For example, the “Fast Track” program jointly promoted by UK Sport and British Skeleton aimed to identify promising athletes from other sports and retrain them specifically for Winter Olympic disciplines such as skeleton [ ]. Similarly, Australia secured the gold medal by effectively implementing TT pathways in their women’s Rugby 7 s team, targeting experienced team sport athletes to overcome the lack of established pathways and experience among athletes in Rugby 7 s [ ].
China’s breakthrough achievement at the 2022 Beijing Winter Olympics (BWO): securing third place in the medal tally with nine gold medals, marking its best historical performance—serves as a compelling case study of this trend [ ]. Notably, this success is reportedly linked to China’s distinctively contextualized TT strategy. In the Chinese context, this strategy is known as “cross-discipline cross-border selection (跨界跨项选材)” [ ]. Data indicate that approximately one-fifth of athletes in the Chinese delegation at the BWO were recruited into national winter sports teams through this mechanism, with cross-sport athletes constituting nearly half of participants in snow events [ ].A more specific hypothesis is that TT provides an additional pathway to identify and develop athletes in disciplines where China traditionally lacked depth, thereby making a distinct contribution to team composition and performance beyond the effects of general state-funded Olympic preparation.
When Beijing secured the 2022 Winter Olympics bid in 2015, nearly one-third of each Winter Olympic event (e.g., bobsleigh, luge, and Nordic combined) had not yet been formally established in China [ , ]. To achieve full participation and address competitive weaknesses [ ], 23 Chinese national departments, including the National Development and Reform Commission (NDRC), General Administration of Sport of China (GASC, the highest administrative body for national sports), Ministry of Education, and National Tourism Administration, jointly issued policies promoting TT to overcome foundational and resource limitations [ ].
Consequently, China launched the “Roller-to-Ice” project in 2015 as a state policy-led pilot for TT [ ]. The project demonstrated the model’s feasibility, generated localized practice, and laid the foundation for a development system suited to national conditions. Several athletes emerged from this initiative. Geng Wenqiang, a former track and field athlete, joined the national skeleton team through the 2015 TT program [ ] and won gold at the 2018 IBSF World Cup in Innsbruck [ ]. Shao Qi, previously a gymnast [ ], transitioned to freestyle skiing in 2016 and earned silver at the 2018 FIS Junior World Championships in Belarus, setting a national record at the time [ ]. By the BWO, China achieved full participation in bobsleigh, luge, and skeleton for the first time by recruiting athletes from sports such as track and field, weightlifting, and wrestling [ ]. Transferred athletes, including Yan Wengang and Peng Junyue, adapted through intensive training despite having no prior winter sports experience [ ]. Yan Wengang went on to win a bronze medal in skeleton, China’s first Olympic medal in the event [ ].
Current research on TT predominantly focuses on sport-specific investigations [ – ]. For instance, studies have examined physiological adaptations (e.g., maximal oxygen uptake), technical competencies, and performance outcomes of endurance athletes transitioning from disciplines such as kayaking, rowing, and running to cross-country skiing [ ]; individual response variability in cross-country skiing TT [ ]; skill transferability and divergence between canoeing and kayaking [ ]; epidemiological patterns of injuries among transferred snowboarders [ ]; and biomechanical analyses of transitions from taekwondo to other sports [ ]. A limited number of studies explore underlying mechanisms, proposing that TT efficacy stems primarily from inter-sport similarities in demand profiles—including strength, speed, endurance, and psychological adaptability—between origin and target disciplines [ , ]. Furthermore, scholars emphasize the paucity of empirical evidence validating TT effectiveness, with critical influencing factors remaining underexplored [ , ]. Despite these efforts, no study has evaluated a state-scale program in a late-developing winter-sport nation.
Against this backdrop, this study advances the following hypotheses: Through a systematic TT strategy, China is expected to overcome key challenges in the development of winter sports during the BWO cycle, expand its athlete pool, improve competitive performance, and enhance winter sports outcomes across multiple dimensions (such as economic benefits and social benefits, etc.), thereby laying a foundation for the long-term development of winter sports. This study uses the BWO as a pivotal case to achieve four research objectives:a comprehensive summary of the snow and ice sports development dilemmas facing China ahead of the BWO;
systematically investigate policies and measures implemented during the BWO cycle;
summarize multifaceted outcomes of TT in enhancing China’s Olympic performance; and
distill a China-specific pathway for winter sports TT.

This research aims to provide novel empirical insights into athlete selection strategies for global winter sports and establish a strategic framework to help international policymakers optimize athlete development. With the upcoming 2026 Milan-Cortina Winter Olympics, the findings will directly assist late-developing nations in addressing chronic shortages in athlete pools and slow progress in competitive performance, thereby providing methodological guidance to promote equitable global advancement in winter sports.

## Methods

## Study design
To achieve the four core research objectives proposed in this study, the research team adopted a comprehensive mixed-methods research design.
At the policy level, a systematic policy retrieval strategy was implemented, focusing on authoritative documents issued by central government bodies since China’s successful bid for the 2022 Winter Olympics in 2015. These policy texts were rigorously filtered using the keyword “跨项选材” (“cross-discipline talent selection”) to ensure the study remained focused on state-led talent transfer (TT) development strategies.
To evaluate the practical implementation of TT policies, the research team designed and conducted a quantitative questionnaire survey. The survey was collaboratively developed by the research team, the coaching staff of the Winter Sports Management Center (WSMC), and relevant experts. It was distributed via the mobile-based “Wenjuanxing” platform and targeted TT administrators, head coaches, and sports science researchers, with the aim of gathering authentic feedback from frontline policy implementers. The collected data were statistically analyzed using SPSS version 26.0.
Recognizing that Olympic success extends beyond medal counts, the study introduced a multidimensional outcome evaluation framework to comprehensively assess the direct and indirect impacts of TT. This framework includes seven dimensions, such as athlete participation, competitive performance, program coverage, and socio-economic benefits.
Lastly, to comprehensively evaluate the contribution of TT to China’s winter sports competitiveness, the study analyzed the number of national-level scientific research projects, academic publications related to TT, and Olympic medal data across different time periods—focusing in particular on the critical phase from the Olympic bid to the official hosting of the Games.

## Data collection and analysis
All TT data referenced in this study were obtained from authoritative Chinese governmental bodies and official platforms. Primary sources included the General Office of the CPC Central Committee (China’s top policymaking authority), the GASC, and WSMC. Supplementary data were gathered from credible state-affiliated outlets such as the official website of the Chinese Olympic Committee, The Beijing News, CCTV.com, and People.cn. All cited data are publicly accessible and verifiable. For improved readability, references are presented in English. Data collection was carried out by ZYL, DBW, WJJ, and QL in 15 June 2024, while the analysis and processing were completed by ZYL, DBW, and YYH. All data visualizations were created using the “ggplot2” package in R 4.3.3.

## Policy system retrieval process
A systematic retrieval of relevant policies was conducted. The search was carried out on July 20, 2025. The initial screening was conducted by two team members (ZYL, QL), who were responsible for reviewing and compiling documents from various databases (including the policy databases released by the national and provincial sports bureaus). A third member then determined the final set of included policies based on the predefined inclusion criteria (DBW).
Policy documents were systematically retrieved using “TT” as the primary search term, focusing on materials released since 2015 via official Chinese government portals, including the GASC, provincial sports bureaus, the State Council.
The selection of documents adhered to three core principles:prioritization of policy documents jointly issued by central authorities (e.g., the CPC Central Committee, State Council, or GASC);
strict adherence to the keyword “跨项选材” (“cross-discipline talent selection”), “跨界跨项选材” (cross-discipline cross-border selection) and 跨季跨界跨项选材 (Cross-season, cross-discipline, and cross-sport talent selection) during information extraction;
inclusion of official policy documents such as guidelines, notices, decisions, development plans, and strategic outlines. Exclude non-textual materials, conferences, news reports, tables, and other documents that do not pertain to the strategic guidance of the TT policy.

Based on the above principles, the study firstly selected policy texts through team discussion and expert evaluation (some of the instructors of this topic are Chinese experts in this field), and finally identified a sample of policies that are closely related to the " talent transfer” through interactive verification, collation and selection by multiple parties.

## Questionnaire survey
A questionnaire was used to investigate the basic characteristics of China’s TT strategy during the Winter Olympic cycle. The questionnaire was directly distributed to the Winter Sports Management Center of China (WSMC), as this center is the direct administrative body responsible for all winter sports programs nationwide. The personnel involved in this department are directly engaged in the management and implementation of the TT strategy and have in-depth knowledge of its execution and specific processes.
This study presents selected questionnaire data, while the full version of the questionnaire is available in the appendix. The questionnaire was initially drafted by the research team based on relevant reports from Chinese media and institutions, as well as academic literature. It was subsequently refined through interviews with domain experts. These experts included officials from the WSMC, national team coaches in ice and snow sports, strength and conditioning coaches, research assistants, and specialists in TT (Appendix).
An exploratory factor analysis was conducted using principal component analysis, with varimax orthogonal rotation applied to extract factors. Internal consistency reliability was tested using cronbach’s α coefficient.
A total of 28 questionnaires were distributed and all were returned, yielding a response rate of 100%. All 28 respondents were affiliated with the WSMC. Among them, 10 were engaged in ice sports and 18 in snow sports. Although 68% of the respondents had only 1–3 years of experience, this is because they transitioned from other fields. Nevertheless, there were still 4 respondents with 4–10 years of experience and 5 respondents with more than 10 years. The sample included 4 TT administrators, 10 head coaches of ice and snow sports, and 14 sports science researchers (Table  ).

 

## Evaluation of multidimensional winter olympics outcomes
This study, through discussions with an expert team, categorizes the outcomes into direct and indirect impacts to provide a comprehensive evaluation of China’s achievements. Direct effects included: (1) participant numbers, (2) competitive performance, (3) TT success rates, and (4) proportions of transferred athletes. Indirect effects included: (1) winter sports promotion, 2) venue infrastructure development, 3) economic benefits and social impacts, (4) public engagement, (5) managerial capacity enhancement, (6) scientific research advancement and technological competitiveness, 7) impacts on pre-transfer sports and coaching recruitment capabilities.
Considering that athletes should not be defined as successful solely based on winning Olympic medals, every significant achievement in an athlete’s career should be regarded as a success. In China, an athlete’s progression typically follows a path from city-level sports teams to provincial teams, then to the national team, with athletes being selected from the national team to participate in the Olympics and strive for Olympic medals. Therefore, China currently employs a four-tier evaluation framework to assess TT success [ ]. Tier 1: Provincial-level selection into national team training camps with attainment of National First-Class Athlete certification; Tier 2: Formal national team membership through training assessments, awarded the National Elite Athlete title; Tier 3: Olympic qualification; Tier 4: Olympic medal acquisition. Consequently, evaluating TT success requires multidimensional analysis of phase-specific conversion rates across disciplines and developmental stages.
However, systemic data gaps persist in tracking full-cycle athlete progression (from initial selection to Olympic participation) across winter sports. In particular, the selection data for emerging programs such as snowmobiles and steel-framed snowmobiles have not yet been established as a public database, and the information on echelon construction for collective programs such as curling has not been fully disclosed. To mitigate sampling bias risks from selective reporting (e.g., overrepresenting high-performance disciplines), this study excluded TT success rates from the quantitative analysis framework to ensure methodological robustness. Similar data limitations affect TT proportion metrics, which were likewise omitted from systematic evaluation for the same methodological reasons.

## Results

## Challenges in china’s winter sports prior to the BWO

## Ice and snow sports programs underachieve and imbalance development
For a long time, China’s development in winter sports has lagged behind that of world powers such as Norway, the United States, and Germany (Fig.  ). Prior to the Beijing Winter Olympics, China had won a total of 62 Winter Olympic medals (13 gold, 28 silver, and 21 bronze), far fewer than traditional Winter Olympic powerhouses such as Norway (368), the United States (305), Germany (240), Canada (199), and Austria (232). Its total of 13 gold medals ranked only 14th, behind regional neighbors South Korea (31) and Japan (14). In addition, the number of silver and bronze medals was also markedly lower.

 

Before successfully bidding to host the Beijing Winter Olympics, roughly one-third of China’s 109 Winter Olympic events had no established domestic program, and another third were still in their early stages of development. Medal achievements were largely concentrated in a few disciplines such as short track speed skating, freestyle skiing aerials, speed skating, and figure skating, while snow events lagged behind, yielding very few medals (Table  ).

 

## Monolithic athlete development model in winter sports
Prior to its Olympic bid, China’s approach to cultivating winter sports athletes followed a rigid three-tiered system comprising traditional sports academies, sports colleges, and subsequently, provincial or national teams [ ]. This model was further constrained by widespread parental reluctance to enroll their children in training programs, largely due to concerns regarding employment insecurity following athletic retirement.

## Geographical imbalance in winter sports
Winter sports in China have traditionally been concentrated in the northeastern provinces of Heilongjiang, Jilin, and Liaoning, as well as the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region. These areas offer harsh winters and consistent snow cover, which support participation. However, seasonal and infrastructural limitations have restricted national expansion, resulting in a persistently limited athlete pool [ ]. In addition, winter sports resources and facilities are predominantly located in northern regions such as Northeast and North China. Southern regions, constrained by climate, have experienced slow development. In speed skating, the largest discipline by participant numbers, athletes are heavily concentrated in Heilongjiang and Jilin, with a substantial gap in representation across other provinces (Fig.  ).

 

## Talent shortages in BWO
China’s winter sports system continues to face substantial shortfalls in both the number and expertise of specialized personnel, including athletes, coaches, and referees, when compared to leading nations. In 2014, the year before China’s successful Olympic bid, only 4,339 winter sports athletes were registered nationwide, supported by 348 certified coaches [ ]. By 2015, the number of registered athletes increased slightly to 5,111, but this growth was modest and insufficient to close the broader talent gap [ ]. Nearly 4,000 athletes were engaged in ice sports such as speed skating and figure skating, while snow disciplines accounted for only about 1,000.
Between 2012 and 2014, China’s track and field programs recruited 10.75, 14.88, and 17.80 times more athletes per year than winter sports, respectively (Table  ) [ – ].

 

## Weak infrastructure for ice and snow sports
Insufficient infrastructure has long hindered China’s winter sports development. The country’s first comprehensive skating venue was built in Harbin in 1983, yet by 2015, China had only ~ 200 skating venues, over half of which occupied less than 1,000 m² (the smallest being 200 m²) [ ]. Similarly, ski resort development began with Yabuli Windmill Village (China’s first ski resort, constructed for the 1996 Asian Winter Games), reaching ~ 500 ski resorts by 2015, with total ski trails spanning ~ 1,000 km and covering ~ 30 million m² [ ].
As of 2020, China had approximately 770 ski resorts, with a per capita ski area of just 0.0001 km² [ ]. This figure is substantially lower than that of established winter sports nations such as Switzerland (0.016 km²) and Austria (0.011 km²) [ ]. Moreover, by 2015, China operated only 198 aerial cable cars nationwide [ ], whereas Switzerland had over 2,000 ski lifts and cable systems in operation [ ].

## Policies and regulations for ice and snow sports need improvement
Winter sports in China have historically faced significant policy gaps. Prior to 2014, the GASC had issued no policy documents explicitly titled “TT” and only 197 that referenced “winter sports.” Between 2014 and March 1, 2025, these numbers rose to 1,087 and 958, respectively [ ]. During this period, institutional systems for venue management [ ], medical support [ ], and athlete ranking [ ] were substantially restructured. Before the Olympic bid, many disciplines lacked formal evaluation standards. By 2024, GASC had introduced comprehensive ranking criteria for all winter sports [ ]. Athletes entering national training camps through TT are now classified under a four-tier certification system: Second-Class Athlete, First-Class Athlete, National Elite Athlete, and International Elite Athlete [ ].

## Insufficient funding and scientific research support for winter sports
Between 2013 and 2022, only 567 academic papers in China took “winter sports” as their core research theme, with fewer than ten published annually before 2015. Following the successful bid to host the Winter Olympics in 2015, publication output rose sharply, with particularly notable growth during 2020–2022 (Fig. ).
It is also evident that winter sports research accounted for only a very small proportion of all sports-related projects in China. As shown in Fig. , among more than 1,300 sports science projects funded by the National Social Science Fund of China, only 47 were related to winter sports, and there were no such projects prior to 2015 [ ]. After the successful bid for the Winter Olympics, there was a modest increase, reaching nine projects in 2019.

 

## China’s initiatives during the winter olympics preparation period

## Policy guidance
The policy retrieval process covered documents issued by the GASC and all provincial-level sports bureaus across the country (Fig.  ). A total of 6,008 documents were identified. After removing 5,707 duplicate entries, largely due to the common practice of provincial agencies forwarding and implementing GASC-issued policies, a refined set of unique documents was retained for analysis.

 

Exclusion of conference, news, etc., during the Winter Olympics preparation period, China issued a total of 50 policies (Appendix), including 17 [ , , , , – ] at the national level (Table  ) from authorities such as the General Office of the CPC Central Committee, General Office of the State Council, NDRC, GASC, Ministry of Education, and National Tourism Administration, along with 33 at provincial/municipal levels. Most policies focused on TT, while a few used winter sports as the main theme but explicitly addressed TT matters. The characteristics and main points of national policies are presented chronologically in Table  .

 

An unexpected finding emerged during the peer review process: when we updated our search timeline, we discovered a surge of newly published TT-related strategies from last year and this year, developed in preparation for the upcoming Milan Winter Olympics.

## Local implementation
The questionnaire demonstrates acceptable reliability and structural validity. The standardized Cronbach’s α coefficient is 0.760, indicating good internal consistency. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure is 0.508, slightly above the minimum acceptable threshold of 0.50. Bartlett’s test of sphericity yielded χ² (78) = 190.395, p <.001, suggesting the data are suitable for factor analysis. The eigenvalues of the extracted factors are 4.48, 2.04, and 1.38, with rotated variance contributions of 31.92%, 15.44%, and 13.43%, respectively, accounting for a cumulative variance of 60.79%(Appendix).
In terms of TT implementation, 93% of respondents reported that their organizations had established formal TT plans, and 66% indicated that TT was carried out annually, with more than half conducting it twice a year. Regarding talent sources, 71% of athletes came from lower-tier teams, 64% were identified through peer recommendations, and 7% were recruited from amateur sports schools.As for the time from selection to final confirmation, 50% of respondents stated that confirmation could be completed within one month. In terms of selection criteria, almost all respondents (97%) considered physical fitness the primary indicator, while technical ability was also emphasized. Recognition of psychological, adaptive, and behavioral performance indicators ranged from 64% to 86% (Fig.  ).

 

## China’s achievements at the BWO
According to data from the International Olympic Committee, China ranked third in the medal table at the BWO, marking a significant leap from 11th place in 2014 and 16th place in 2018, and successfully entering the global top three [ ]. In 2014, the year before the Olympic bid, China won a total of nine medals (three gold, four silver, and two bronze). Compared with 2010 and 2018, the differences in the numbers of gold, silver, and bronze medals were no greater than two in any category, indicating overall stability in performance but a lack of gold medal dominance. In 2022, China’s total medal count rose to 15, including nine gold medals, representing a 67% increase in total medals and a 200% increase in gold medals compared with 2014. Furthermore, for the first time in Winter Olympic history, China achieved full participation across all 15 disciplines, whereas in previous editions it had never fielded athletes in every discipline (Figs.  and ).

 

 

From 1980 to 2022, the number of Chinese Winter Olympic athletes increased from 28 to 176—an expansion of more than sixfold (Fig.  ). The sharpest growth occurred after 2014 (after the bid for the BWO), with the number rising from 66 athletes in 2014 to 176 in 2022, a 167% increase over two Games. Although the per-athlete medal yield (measured as medals per 100 athletes) declined from 13.6 in 2014 to 8.5 in 2022, the total medal count reached a historic high.

## Concurrent developments in Chinese winter sports
It is important to acknowledge that the indirect outcomes cannot be fully attributed to the implementation of the TT strategy. However, TT has clearly contributed to expanding the athlete pool. This expansion has been accompanied by growth in training centers, sports facilities, competition frequency, audience engagement, research investment, public participation, and measurable economic and social returns. To enhance athlete performance, China has also prioritized technological innovation and improvements in management capacity. Collectively, these factors have supported the broader development of winter sports in China.

## Popularization and promotion of BWO
The number of China’s athlete was increasing during the TT period, and a total of 58 high-level international and domestic events, including the Asian Winter Games, were successfully hosted [ ]. Participation expanded beyond traditional centers in the northeast and north to include broader regions, with notable growth in provinces such as Guangdong and Sichuan [ ]. Activities now extend beyond skiing and skating to include disciplines such as curling, bobsleigh, and ice hockey. For example, the Qingdao Winter Festival introduced curling experience sessions that attracted over 200 participants [ ]. At the same time, winter tourism has become a major contributor to the sector’s growth. In the 2023–2024 season, China recorded 385 million winter leisure tourism visits, with projections surpassing 500 million in 2024–2025 [ ]. Moreover, now 2,062 schools are designated as winter sports specialty institutions, with plans to expand to 5,000 by 2025 [ ]. Winter sports curricula have been implemented in 16 provinces, supported by programs such as “Millions of Youths on Ice and Snow” [ ] and the “Campus Winter Sports Program” [ ]. In Beijing alone, over 2.1 million primary and secondary students have participated in winter sports activities [ ].

## Winter sports venue development [ ]
Before Beijing’s successful bid for the BWO in 2015, China had fewer than 500 winter sports venues. The total ski trail area was approximately 30 million square meters, spanning 1,000 km. About 80% of ski resorts had trails under 50,000 square meters, and most facilities were concentrated in the northeastern and northern regions. Southern areas lacked professional winter sports infrastructure.
From 2016 to 2020, winter sports facilities expanded rapidly. The number of standard ice rinks grew from 157 to 388, and ski resorts increased from 568 to 770. According to the Venue Development Plan, by 2022 China aimed to build at least 650 ice rinks, including 500 new ones, and 800 ski resorts. The plan also targeted 100 million square meters of ski trail area and 3,500 km of total trail length, including 240 new resorts contributing 70 million square meters and 2,500 km.
As of 2021, the 770 ski resorts were distributed across 28 provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities. Heilongjiang, Shandong, Xinjiang, Hebei, and Shanxi had the highest numbers, with Hubei and Xinjiang leading in new openings in 2019. By 2025, China is expected to reach 2,678 winter sports venues, including 1,764 ice rinks and 914 ski resorts—an increase of over five times compared to 2015. In addition, 417 new facilities have been completed. A notable example is the development of China’s first urban cross-country ski trail in Hulunbuir, which allows residents to access skiing as easily as urban jogging [ ].

## Economic and social benefits
China’s winter sports industry has experienced robust economic growth. The market expanded from 270 billion yuan in 2015 to 890 billion yuan in 2023 and is projected to exceed 1 trillion yuan by 2025 [ ]. The sector shows strong consumption momentum, with nationwide ski resort spending surpassing 35 billion yuan [ ]. During the 2024–2025 winter season, over 500 million winter tourism visits are expected, generating more than 630 billion yuan in revenue. Harbin Ice-Snow World alone is projected to contribute 16.4 billion yuan [ ].
China’s winter sports industry has produced notable social benefits. Ski resorts recorded over 190 million visits, marking a 22.8% year-on-year increase. Five northern provinces—Heilongjiang, Liaoning, Jilin, Inner Mongolia, and Xinjiang—accounted for more than 76 million visits [ ]. Guangdong and Sichuan, despite warmer climates, ranked among the highest in per-resort attendance, with 440,000 and 380,000 visits, respectively [ ]. Although pandemic restrictions reduced in-person attendance, the BWO reached a record global audience through advanced broadcasting technologies. The Games attracted 2.01 billion viewers across television and digital platforms, representing a 5% increase from the 2018 PyeongChang Olympics [ ].
Olympic venues such as the Shougang Big Air and the National Speed Skating Oval have been opened to the public, supporting a sustainable model that integrates legacy use with commercial operations. This approach has encouraged mass participation and contributed to regional tourism [ ]. The Games also advanced the development of the Beijing-Zhangjiakou Sports Culture Tourism Belt, which includes more than 20 high-quality winter tourism routes. These routes have attracted 385 million visits, with forecasts exceeding 500 million for the 2024–2025 season [ ].
The BWO also set new environmental standards. All venues operated entirely on renewable electricity and adopted CO₂-based refrigeration systems, offering a replicable model for carbon-neutral global events [ ].

## Public participation
Since the BWO, China has recorded a 30.2% year-on-year increase in foreign ski visitors [ ]. According to surveys by the Public Opinion Research Center of the National Bureau of Statistics, 24.56% of residents now participate in winter sports [ ]. The survey, launched in October 2021, shows consistent growth in participation since the Olympic bid in 2015, reaching 346 million participants by October 2021. This includes 300 million adults, with a participation rate of 26.95%, and 46 million minors, with a rate of 15.62% [ ]. China has thus achieved its target of engaging 300 million people in winter sports, a milestone recognized by national leadership as a key legacy of the Games [ ].
Participation has expanded across the country, following a pattern of “South Expansion, West Extension, and East Advancement.” Twelve provinces and municipalities now report participation rates above 30%. Regionally, eastern China accounts for 143 million participants, central China for 68 million, western China for 84 million, and northeast China for 51 million [ ]. While the northeast maintains the highest participation rate due to favorable climate conditions, other regions show comparable engagement with limited variation [ ].

## Management system enhancement
Before 2015, winter sports in China were governed mainly by general regulations such as the National Fitness Regulations (2009) [ ] and the Sports Law [ ], without dedicated policy frameworks. Venue safety was regulated by the Administrative Measures for Licensing High-Risk Sports Operations (2013) [ ], but limited enforcement reduced its effectiveness. Furthermore, the absence of clear policies for equipment manufacturing and tourism hindered broader industry development.
The BWO accelerated the standardization of winter sports management. By 2024, the State Administration for Market Regulation had issued 42 national standards across four key areas [ , ]: Public Training(12 standards) [ ], Event Management (Standards for snow and ice sports events) [ , ], Venue Safety (6 standards) [ ] and Equipment Manufacturing (22 standards) [ ].

## Advancements in scientific research and technological competitiveness level
Analysis of academic publications reveals significant growth in TT research in China. A search of the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) database using the keywords “跨项选材” (cross-discipline talent selection) shows only 3 relevant publications prior to 2015, compared to 169 publications between 2015 and 2025 - representing a 56-fold increase. The research focus has evolved substantially from early-stage (pre-2015) basic studies on phenomenon description [ , ] and case analysis [ ] to systematic investigations across multiple dimensions in recent years (2015–2025), including: TID methodologies [ , – ], analysis of key influencing factors [ – ], exploration of success patterns [ , ], application of genetic selection technologies [ , ], and development of TT evaluation standards [ – ].
In terms of research funding, TT studies have gained substantial support from national-level research programs. While no related projects were funded by the National Social Science Fund before 2015, three major research projects were approved between 2020 and 2022 [ ]. These projects focus on critical areas including practical implementation pathways, policy framework development, and effectiveness evaluation of TT [ ]. As shown in Fig.  , China’s winter sports research projects have grown significantly since 2014. Although growth slowed in 2018 due to the Summer Olympics and in 2021 due to the pandemic, the overall trend remains upward.
On September 5, 2018, the GASC released the Science and Technology Support Plan for the BWO, identifying technological innovation as a central strategy for enhancing athletic performance [ ]. Over the course of five years, China achieved the domestic production of snowmaking machines, snowplows, and related equipment, accompanied by the development of an efficient snow storage system that significantly reduced snow loss rates [ ]. In bobsleigh, China independently developed its first carbon fiber sled, which, through wind tunnel testing and structural optimization, reduced drag by 8% and maintained stable performance at speeds of up to 160 km/h [ ]. Winter sportswear also advanced. Nanoscale bionic materials and electric heating ensured insulation in extreme cold [ ]. The “Ice Sharkskin” suit, co-developed by Donghua University and Anta, reduced wind resistance by 5 to 10%, supporting performance in events like short track speed skating [ ].

## Impact on Pre-transfer sports and coaching recruitment capabilities
The implementation of TT has created new development pathways for athletes in traditional sports such as martial arts, gymnastics, and track and field. It has helped address the challenge of short athletic careers caused by intense competition or age limitations. For example, Ye Kangjia, a student from Henan Tagou Martial Arts School, faced intense competition in martial arts and found it difficult to win awards. Through TT, he switched to skiing and won a gold medal at the National Winter Games [ ]. Athletes who successfully switched sports attracted public attention, and media coverage of snowboarder Liu Jiayu [ , ], who has a martial arts background, increased the focus on traditional martial arts [ ].
In addition, the implementation of TT has raised demands on coaches’ talent identification (TID) capabilities, requiring deeper understanding of both shared traits and specific demands across sports. In martial arts, for instance, coaches identified flexibility and aerial posture control as common with freestyle skiing. Training programs were then adjusted to emphasize these attributes, leading to the development of athletes such as Zhao Qinglin [ ].

## Discussion: china’s key experience in TT
As the first systematic empirical research globally to explore the application and effectiveness of the TT strategy in competitive sports, this research aims to investigate the development status of winter sports in China before the implementation of the TT strategy, the implementation of the TT strategy under the host nation context, and the preliminary outcomes after its implementation.
Before the implementation of the TT strategy, China faced long-standing structural challenges, including uneven development of disciplines (characterized by relative strength in ice events and weakness in snow events), insufficient reserves of athletes and coaches, significant regional disparities, weak infrastructure, and inadequate family and policy support.
Through extensive national policy support, increased attention from the research community, and a top-down systemic implementation framework, existing evidence preliminarily suggests that the strategy has, to some extent, facilitated the transformation and development of the winter sports system. This is reflected in the successful expansion of the athlete pools as well as progress in competitive performance, research achievements, mass participation, and social and economic benefits. Additionally, the TT strategy has provided a valuable practical example of shifting competitive sports from an elite-oriented model to a broader mass participation approach. The strategy also contributed to the achievement of the national strategic goal of engaging 300 million people in winter sports.
Given the interim results of China’s TT strategy under its specific policy environment and resource system, the following section will provide an in-depth analysis from the perspective of practical experience.

## The whole-national system facilitates TT
The “whole-national system” is a state-led governance model unique to China. It refers to the centralized mobilization and coordination of national resources across government agencies, public institutions, and increasingly, private actors in order to accomplish major strategic objectives with maximum efficiency [ ]. Unlike the more decentralized or market-driven models common in many Western countries, this system emphasizes unified leadership, task-oriented resource integration, and coordinated execution. It has been one of the key drivers behind China’s rapid development over the past few decades and has been widely applied in critical areas such as space exploration, infrastructure development, and competitive sports.
In the context of sports, the whole-national system has played a central role in the systematic development of China’s competitive sports. Since the 1984 Los Angeles Olympics, Chinese sports authorities have deliberately concentrated resources on strategically selected disciplines to enhance international competitiveness [ ]. This focused investment and efficient organizational model have enabled China to achieve rapid progress across multiple sports, consistently delivering strong performances on the global stage and solidifying its position as a leading sporting nation. With the ongoing reform of China’s economic system, the traditional whole-national system has undergone institutional evolution. In coordinating resources, the state no longer relies solely on administrative directives but instead adopts a combination of policy incentives, fiscal guidance, and social mobilization to stimulate the active participation of local governments (as illustrated by the TT incentive policies presented in Table  ), research institutions, and enterprises [ ]. This institutional transformation has significantly enhanced the flexibility and sustainability of sports governance and has created a more open and systematic environment for advancing the TT strategy.
During preparations for the BWO, China’s national system demonstrated strong organizational and mobilization capacity. Through top-down planning and policy guidance, the execution of the TT strategy was accelerated, enabling simultaneous advances in talent development and competitive performance. First, the GASC, in collaboration with multiple departments, introduced strategic initiatives such as “Northern Ice, Southern Expansion, Western Outreach, and Eastern Advancement,” which provided institutional safeguards and policy support for TT and athlete development [ ].
At the provincial level, TT criteria were adapted to local contexts, leveraging the national system’s resource allocation advantages. Scientific evaluations were conducted to identify transferable skills across disciplines. As a result, athletes from summer sports such as athletics, gymnastics, and martial arts were recruited into winter sports and provided with targeted training support and international competition opportunities [ ].
In terms of specific implementation and transition, top-down policy issuance directly guided the execution details of each team. First, in China, local sports teams, including coaches, athletes, and managers, are directly managed by local administrative units. These teams typically receive support in terms of funding, medical care, and other resources from these administrative units. Each sports team must carefully review the TT execution policies issued by the local administrative units and carry out internal selection processes accordingly. Afterward, provincial teams assess the athletes selected through internal screening. Following a certain period of follow-up (usually 1–3 months, Fig.  ), they decide whether the athletes will be included in the provincial team. Similarly, athletes from provincial teams undergo a similar process to transition to the national team, eventually competing in the Olympics. During this process, each local team will strictly follow a selection plan and use the evaluation criteria for sports projects published by the GASC (Fig.  ). In addition, previous studies have highlighted the challenges faced by athletes and managers during the transition process, such as external pressures and regulatory constraints [ ]. In China, similar issues exist in the TT process. For example, coaches in various sports are often reluctant to transfer athletes, whom they have trained extensively, to higher-level units. Therefore, national departments have directly issued corresponding incentive policies for both coaches and athletes to encourage them to actively recommend outstanding athletes from their teams or themselves. (Sect.  and Appendix).
In summary, China’s TT implementation process established a “central–local” coordination mechanism. The central government led the cross-regional allocation of resources, while local governments developed training bases and talent pipelines tailored to their own conditions. In southern provinces in particular, where geographic and climatic conditions were less favorable, purpose-built artificial snow venues and simulated training environments were constructed to effectively offset the limitations imposed by natural conditions [ ].

## National policies ensuring TT implementation
National policies have played a central role in supporting the implementation of TT. Between China’s successful bid for the Winter Olympics in 2015 and the Games’ delivery, the government issued 50 policy documents related to TT, ranging from national-level strategies to local execution (Appendix).
At the national level, the NDRC, in coordination with the GASC and other agencies, promoted talent mobility and TT from summer to winter sports. It encouraged collaboration between northern and southern regions through shared resources, including athlete and coach exchanges, to strengthen national capacity in winter sports [ , , ]. This policy orientation influenced national team selection across disciplines [ , , , ]. For example, China’s first freestyle skiing aerials team was formed by recruiting gymnastics and trampoline athletes through TT. In collaboration with domestic sports universities, the 16-member team received targeted physical training and began intensive preparation in Hebei Province. Although the athletes had no prior snow experience, their transition was facilitated through a progressive “pool–land–snowfield” training model designed specifically for the BWO [ ].
In addition, the Chinese government introduced a series of safeguard policies and incentive mechanisms to support TT implementation. The Department of Competitive Sports stipulated that athletes selected for national training teams via TT would be granted the National First-Class Athlete title. Those already holding this status could apply for the Master of Sports title. Athletes who qualified for the BWO or were selected for the national delegation could receive the International Master of Sports title in their new discipline [ ]. The GASC also established rewards for coaches and support staff, including the Outstanding Contribution to Sports Award and opportunities for professional title promotion [ ]. In 2019, the national gymnastics program procured winter sports physical training and medical support services through public contracts to enhance Olympic preparation.
At the local level, provinces actively implemented TT policies and issued 33 supporting documents (Appendix). Regions such as Ningxia [ ], Inner Mongolia [ ], Guangxi [ ], and Jiangsu [ ] launched targeted TT programs. In Ningxia, 61 candidates from middle schools and sports academies were recruited into six winter sports disciplines as early as March 2017 [ ]. Other provinces developed specific selection criteria based on winter sports demands. For example, Chongqing outlined eligibility requirements including age (13–18, with flexibility for exceptional talent), gender (open to all, with preference for females), and physique (projected height and weight, prioritizing lean body composition; male ≤ 1.75 m, female ≤ 1.65 m) [ ].
This vertical policy framework has produced notable results. According to a 2017 evaluation by the GASC, TT policies led to an increase of 2,248 registered winter sports athletes within one year [ ]. The integrated talent development model established through TT has since been extended to new Olympic disciplines such as breaking and sport climbing, reflecting strong institutional spillover effects [ , ]. The framework is now evolving for the post–Winter Olympics era. The revised Athlete Technical Level Management Measures (2023) formally included TT achievements in athlete evaluation standards for the first time, signaling a shift toward coordinated multi-sport talent development [ ]. This policy innovation not only consolidates progress in elite sports but also reshapes the broader structure of China’s athletic talent system.

## Dynamic adjustment throughout the TT process
The successful implementation of China’s TT strategy for the BWO relied fundamentally on a policy of “dynamic adjustment.” Both the transfer frequency (mean 1.50 ± 0.73 times per year) and the transfer cycle (mean 1.33 ± 0.64 years) reflected a flexible approach tailored to the specific needs of each discipline. Transfers were largely based on recommendations from lower-tier and same-tier teams (96% and 86% respectively), and the time from initial selection to final confirmation ranged from one week to three months (mean 3.28 ± 1.10 months). This pattern indicates that TT implementation was not a linear process but rather one characterized by phased optimization and rolling adjustments in response to athletes’ adaptation status.
At the same time, a scientific, multidimensional evaluation system was established, covering quantitative indicators such as physical fitness, technical skills, psychological qualities, and competitive performance. Athletes underwent regular phased assessments (e.g., every three months) and annual comprehensive evaluations (Fig.  ) to ensure that training progress met expectations. For athletes who struggled to adapt to winter sports or whose performance plateaued, a “survival of the fittest” mechanism was applied, with some returning to their original sports and others transitioning into coaching roles. New candidates were continually drawn from summer sports talent pools to replenish the roster.
In terms of strategic adjustment, China deliberately avoided direct competition with traditional winter sports powerhouses in Europe and North America, instead prioritizing the development of emerging disciplines such as skeleton racing and freestyle skiing aerials. The freestyle skiing team, for instance, recruited gymnasts and martial artists for their aerial control prowess, securing 2 gold and 1 silver medals at the Beijing Olympics—exemplified by the successful transitions of Xu Mengtao and Jia Zongyang [ ].
In terms of technological adjustment, take skeleton racing as an example: among the 20 athletes selected from track and field and weightlifting within three years, five broke into the world’s top 30 through wind tunnel simulations, virtual reality (VR) training [ ], and the use of low-drag equipment [ ]. This group included Geng Wenqiang [ ] and Yan Wengang [ ], who repeatedly set Asian records. It is worth noting that similar advanced scientific technologies applied in sports science have been proven to not only facilitate talent identification but also gradually become one of the core technologies for injury prevention and optimizing athletic performance [ ]. Therefore, in the field of sports science, the application of advanced technologies such as artificial intelligence, wearable devices, and genetic testing seems to have become a leading trend [ ].

## Adhering to the fundamental logic of talent identification
TID systems are designed to identify young athletes with potential for success in senior elite sports [ ], typically influenced by three key factors: the performer (birthdate, genetics, physiology), the environment (birthplace, family, coaches), and training (early specialization, training volume) [ ]. Regarding birthplace, China’s 1.4 billion population creates a unique “pyramid base effect,” providing the world’s largest potential athlete pool for TID systems. The 2018 “TT Program” implemented standardized physical tests (e.g., 30-meter sprint, vertical jump) and athletic potential assessments, screening over 20,000 participants to select thousands for national training teams [ ]. Ultimately, approximately 20% of these athletes officially joined winter sports national teams through TT, with cross-sport athletes comprising nearly 50% of snow event teams [ ]. The combination of the scale effect and the host nation effect contributed to the significant increase in China’s delegation size, which reached a record-breaking 176 athletes in 2022, a 47% increase from the 2018 Pyeongchang Winter Olympics.
Genetic identification technology was integrated into the TT process to enhance athlete selection. Prior to the Olympics, national policy advocated combining genetic testing with traditional assessments to improve precision [ ]. Post-Games evaluations confirmed its contribution to performance outcomes [ ]. Professor Hu Yang’s team at Beijing Sport University developed molecular markers for endurance, speed, and power [ ]. These were used to assess transfer potential, event suitability, and injury risk.By linking genetic data with performance metrics, the team offered personalized guidance on training, recovery, and nutrition. In freestyle skiing aerials, this approach enabled timely interventions, supporting athlete consistency and contributing to Olympic success [ ]. Although there is currently no data that explicitly demonstrates the specific contribution of this technology, it is certain that the application of genetic testing provides a revolutionary approach to identifying athletic talent. Not only can it improve athlete success rates, but it also allows for large-scale, low-cost athlete selection, facilitating the discovery of potential sports talents and providing new momentum for the development of global competitive sports [ ].

## Early diversified development paving the way for TT
Chinese scholars began questioning early specialization as early as the 1980s. A 1982 study on track and field showed that early specialization did not significantly enhance performance [ ]. Follow-up research advocated diversified development in youth to build a broad motor skill base [ , ].
The TT practices during Winter Olympics preparations led to the systematic formulation of China’s dual theoretical paradigm for athlete development in 2018 [ ]: The first derives from Ericsson et al.‘s (1993) deliberate practice theory, advocating a linear development path of “early identification - early specialization - early achievement” [ ]; the second is based on Côté et al.‘s (2007) Developmental Model of Sport Participation (DMSP), establishing a dual-track framework that combines “traditional specialization” with “early diversification (ages 6–12) and late specialization (ages 13–15)” [ ].
Since the Olympic bid, China has developed a distinctive three-tiered athlete development system: (1) Synergistic development of similar sports: Youth receive composite training in sports with analogous competitive characteristics (e.g., tennis/volleyball/badminton/table tennis), enhancing specialized abilities through skill transfer while building talent reserves for TT. This maintains training systematicity while expanding career options [ ]. (2) Complementary development of dissimilar sports: Cross-training in fundamentally different disciplines (e.g., soccer/swimming) fosters balanced athletic development through multidimensional capacity building, creating possibilities for heterogeneous sport transfers. This “bucket effect” mitigation strategy significantly improves adaptive capacity [ ]. (3) Cross-seasonal collaborative development: Breaking temporal barriers between summer/winter sports enables skill transfer (e.g., physical attribute conversion, movement pattern adaptation) through shared training resources [ , , ]. This “counter-season” model optimizes resource allocation while establishing TT pipelines.
Early diversified development serves as the prerequisite for TT, which requires multi-sport training backgrounds during youth, accumulated diverse experiences, and varied motor skill acquisition to enable successful transitions. Studies similarly demonstrate that successful TT typically possess training experience in three or more disciplines [ ]. Therefore, TT should promote the early multidimensional development of athletes, focus on multidimensional potential of athletes, and improve the overall performance of athletes.

## Scientific evaluation of inter-sport similarities
China has established efficient TT selection criteria through scientific analysis of sport similarities. In the early 1990 s, Chinese scholars proposed that some athletes who underperformed in their original sports might demonstrate greater potential in other disciplines due to transferable athletic abilities - this “latent potential” forms the foundation of TT [ ]. Lin Minji’s research on case studies of athletes like Pei Fang and Wang Lei further validated that scientific evaluation of sport similarities could effectively help mediocre athletes achieve better performance in new disciplines [ ].
This concept received definitive verification during the BWO. For sports like bobsleigh and skeleton that require explosive power in starting phases, selections primarily targeted track and field sprinters and hurdlers, with particular emphasis on power output tests [ ]. Statistics show that over 70% of China’s Olympic bobsleigh and skeleton team members originated from track and field [ ]. The technical similarities between sprint starts (including push-off angles and acceleration rhythm) and bobsleigh launch mechanics, along with directly transferable muscle explosiveness and neural response speed, enabled successful transitions. For instance, former sprinter Du Jiani became a main brake woman for China’s bobsleigh team within six months of systematic training, ranking top-10 in World Championships and qualifying for Beijing Olympics [ ]. Another example, Liu Rongsheng - a former long-distance runner selected for cross-country skiing in December 2018 - demonstrated competitive performance at the Olympics (38th in men’s skiathlon) after less than four years of specialized training, setting China’s best Olympic record in this event [ ].
It is worth noting that these cases also highlight the importance of cross-sport coaching experience and emphasize the crucial role that coaches play in the successful implementation of TT [ ]. In practice, coaches integrate the technical strengths from athletes’ original disciplines into winter sports training systems, thereby enhancing adaptation efficiency. For example, Coach Yuan Xiaoyi utilized his track and field background to improve the training of China’s skeleton and bobsleigh teams. By organizing targeted training and competitions abroad, he helped athletes quickly acquire new skills.
Furthermore, gymnasts and martial artists with exceptional aerial body control were frequently recruited for freestyle skiing aerials. Olympic champion Xu Mengtao’s background in gymnastics directly contributed to her precise flip control after event transfer [ ].

## International perspectives and practice models
Although China’s TT practice offers valuable experience, its effectiveness and applicability require further verification and exploration across different countries and contexts. In Australia, one significant shift has been the expansion of the talent identification scope from athletes alone to the wider community, accompanied by the establishment of a dedicated agency. This agency formed a full-time team of 14 staff members with diverse academic, technical, and experiential backgrounds, set up five branch offices nationwide, and partnered with 29 universities and local sports academies to create talent testing centers [ ]. Similarly, in the United Kingdom, TT is jointly implemented by UK Sport, the English Institute of Sport (EIS), and a range of national governing bodies, targeting not only registered athletes but also any individual who believes they have the potential to become a champion [ ]. China’s TT process likewise involves extensive interdepartmental collaboration (Table  ). However, with a population base of 1.4 billion, expanding the transfer pool beyond the athlete population would result in considerable consumption of manpower and resources. Furthermore, China already has a large number of well-trained athletes. An important takeaway, therefore, is that to avoid wasting resources or failing to identify high-quality athletes, it is essential to conduct a thorough assessment of a country’s specific context and resource allocation before implementation. Canada, similar to China, has established TT programs and incentive policies, offering educational subsidies to athletes to support their pursuit of academic degrees or vocational skills training. Likewise, it has put in place rigorous training and monitoring programs to dynamically adjust athletes’ developmental pathways [ ].
In 2007, UK Sport launched its first TT project, Sporting Giants, screening nearly 4,000 athletes by age (16–25 years), height (male ≥ 1.91 m, female ≥ 1.80 m), and sporting background. Candidates were assessed for potential via anthropometric measurements, strength, speed, endurance, and skill tests. Ultimately, 58 advanced to the “talent confirmation stage,” where they were assigned to handball, rowing, volleyball, or canoeing, and then underwent psychological evaluation, personality profiling, and specialized guidance [ ]. By contrast, as reflected in the survey results (Fig.  ), China’s TT evaluation considers physical and technical tests, psychological assessments, learning and adaptability in the target sport, as well as competition and training performance post-transfer, forming a comprehensive appraisal system.
Compared with China’s formal, large-scale, organized, and systematic TT strategy, the above-mentioned countries have implemented TT primarily through small-sample experiments and case studies. Using successful participation in the Olympics through the TT strategy as the benchmark for success, in 2012 Summer Olympics, the United States (6%) and Canada (5%), both without systematic TT programs, have achieved transfer success rates similar to those of Australia (5.1%) and the United Kingdom (7%), where planned programs are in place [ ]. However, in Vancouver 2010 Winter Olympics, the success rates in the United Kingdom (23.1%) and Australia (26.5%) are significantly higher than those in Canada (10.9%) and the United States (8.9%) [ ]. This further supports the notion that systematic TT implementation plays a crucial role in the Winter Olympics—remaining effective even in a large-sample context such as China.
Additionally, previous research has emphasized the mutual influence between international mega-events and host countries, which is directly relevant to the implementation of TT during China’s BWO [ ]. After securing the bid for the Winter Olympics, China set a clear national goal of participating in all events. This national strategy directly guided the comprehensive implementation of TT to select as many athletes as possible to meet Olympic qualification standards. The increase in participating athletes undoubtedly brought multifaceted benefits, such as boosting spectator engagement and local economies. For example, a prior study examining host country experiences, such as the Qatar World Cup, found that institutional support combined with a clear strategic vision can act as a catalyst for social transformation [ ]. Thus, the progress China achieved through TT in terms of economic and social benefits, among other indirect accomplishments, can be explained by this key insight. Therefore, when drawing lessons from China’s experience to promote the development of winter sports, institutional support and clear goal setting remain particularly crucial.
That said, transferring China’s centrally coordinated model into more decentralized sporting environments remains a practical challenge. Large-scale TT implementation, as seen in China, fundamentally depends on strong governmental policy backing and administrative support. In addition, through the number of medals and participants, it can be observed that China’s TT strategy reflects the trade-off between scaling up and maximizing efficiency. In the 109 Winter Olympic events [ ], Chinese athletes competed in 35 events for the first time. Although the total number of medals reached a historic high in 2022, the number of medals per capita decreased. This suggests that China’s TT strategy prioritized scale expansion over maximizing efficiency, but the specific success rate of the TT strategy remains unknown. In interviews with field experts, one expert stated, “Although we cannot currently determine the true success rate of TT, it has undoubtedly expanded the athlete base. With more athletes, the chances of winning medals naturally increase.” A coach noted, “At the time, some athletes on our team met the standards, but with so many transfers, the criteria kept getting stricter.” Another expert remarked, “I believe talent transfer should continue for the next Winter Olympics, but on a smaller scale, since most teams have now been established.” Therefore, for nations in the early stages of winter sports development, it is necessary to adapt TT experience to local conditions and administrative structures and consider the issue of output and returns, with endorsement from relevant government departments or official bodies likely being a key prerequisite. Multi-departmental or multi-institutional cooperation also appears indispensable. In addition, adhering to the fundamental logic of athlete identification and promoting early diversified development can lay a solid foundation for subsequent TT work. When TT is deemed feasible, priority should be given to evaluating the similarities between the original and target sports, establishing a systematic set of transfer evaluation criteria, and making dynamic adjustments during the TT process to maximize effectiveness.
Additionally, athletes and coaches adapting to a new sport should pay particular attention to injury prevention [ ]. In a prospective cohort study involving 39,880 athletes, Gao et al. identified the epidemiology of injuries among skiers involved in China’s TT program. They found that snowboarders participating in the TT program were at a higher risk of injury during their initial snow training, with younger female athletes being especially vulnerable. The study emphasized the importance of specially designed land-based training programs to prevent collisions with equipment or falls, as well as to mitigate contactless injuries resulting from loss of control [ ]. Therefore, a holistic and systematic approach rather than isolated intensified training methods is crucial, especially for athletes transitioning to a new sport [ ].
Finally, given that qualification for most Olympic events is determined by points accumulated in the one to two years preceding the Games, athlete identification should ideally commence immediately after the conclusion of the previous Olympics. This ensures that athletes with TT potential can undergo systematic training and competition at the optimal time, allowing for continuous, data-driven assessment of their prospects for Olympic qualification.

## Limitations
The transfer success rate of athletes between teams of different levels is the most compelling evidence of the fundamental role TT strategies have played in the development of ice and snow sports in China. A higher success rate means that more athletes in a particular event come from the TT strategy, which directly reflects the effectiveness of the TT strategy in China. Furthermore, this study argues that defining the success of TT solely by winning Olympic medals is unreasonable. Instead, the achievements of athletes at various stages after their transition should all be considered successes, as these often represent the result of many years of effort. However, due to insufficient public data and the large volume of information, we were unable to successfully compile and analyze the relevant data to verify the causal relationship between the numerous achievements China has made and the TT strategy. Therefore, while the TT strategy has highlighted many benefits at the BWO, the true output and returns remain unknown.
In addition, although WSMC is a representative department in the TT strategy and the low experience level of the primary respondents is due to the fact that many of them, like the athletes, transitioned from other sports, the small sample size (n = 28) and limited experience (68% with 1–3 years of experience) restricted a broader generalization of the TT process and a deeper understanding of winter sports. The KMO value of 0.508 further indicates that the reliability of the questionnaire results may be relatively low and should be interpreted with caution. Therefore, the survey should be considered an initial exploratory result, and more detailed implementation processes need further investigation.
Finally, external factors such as home-field advantage, infrastructure investment, geopolitical influence, and potential subjective reporting biases from government departments, particularly the host-nation effect, may have impacted both the direct and indirect outcomes of China’s performance at the BWO. The influence of these factors is difficult to fully isolate.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, prior to bidding for the Beijing Winter Olympics, China’s winter sports development faced numerous challenges. However, as the host country, preliminary evidence such as increased participation, improved athletic performance, and potential economic and social benefits support the effectiveness of the TT strategy in the Winter Olympics.
The core experience of China’s TT can be distilled as: “State-led framework, multi-tiered policy incentives, and systematic implementation”, encompassing key elements such as state institutional and policy support, dynamic adjustment throughout the TT process, adherence to the fundamental logic of talent identification, early multidimensional athlete development, and assessment of inter-sport similarities. China’s practice demonstrates that the TT strategy is likely to be an effective pathway for late-developing nations to enhance competitive sports performance and promote the growth of winter sports.
It is worth noting that limitations in fully assessing the actual effectiveness of China’s TT strategy arise from factors such as missing data, insufficient validity of the questionnaires, small sample sizes, and external environmental factors like the host-nation effect.

## Future research
Our statistical analysis unexpectedly revealed that incomplete data suggests most successful TT cases appear to align with the Event-group Training theory [ ] - indicating higher success rates when transferring between similar event groups. Future research could strengthen this perspective through comprehensive data collection.
Furthermore, as winter sports continue developing globally, future studies could incorporate more national cases to enhance the generalizability of China’s TT experience.
