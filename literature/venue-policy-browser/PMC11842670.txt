## Introduction
The study of integration of migrant groups into host societies has been the focus of sustained research activity over the years and has received further impetus through the experience of strong refugee inflows into a number of European countries in 2014–2016, which has become a matter of great political and social concern. Traditionally, research on refugees had concentrated on the classic large immigration countries—the USA, Canada, and Australia—but the recent refugee waves (and the prospects of future waves) into Europe have generated an increased focus in Europe as well. The present research studies refugee integration using a special dataset compiled from a detailed survey of refugees coming from Syria, Iraq, Afghanistan, and Iran to Austria over the period 2010–2018.
In the analysis, we focus on two domains of integration: labour market integration (LMI) and social integration (SI). The focus on LMI is well established in the literature (Battisti et al., ; De Vroome & Van Tubergen, ; Delaporte & Matloob, ), which in the main reflects that it leads to financial independence; that, in the particular context of current Western European economies, it alleviates demographic and labour shortage pressures and allows migrants/refugees to contribute to the social security system; and that, finally, it is itself a means by which SI can be achieved. SI takes place through many channels, such as through cultural interaction, neighbourhood relations, participation in leisure activities, and the involvement of children in the educational system. In the context of our study, furthermore, SI can itself contribute to LMI. While LMI is captured in terms of being in (paid) employment, as compared to being unemployed or inactive, SI distinguishes between social networks (and their ethnic composition) and social capital. For both domains of integration, we identify the key determinants and shed light on their causal interrelationships.
We build on previous research on refugees’ LMI and SI, but also address additional aspects that have received little attention so far. We contribute to the existing literature in several ways. First, we simultaneously study the causal relationship between LMI and SI of refugees. In many of the studies, both domains of integration are analysed separately (Fasani et al., ; Ibrahim et al., ; Lamba & Krahn, ; Martinovic et al., ; Robila, ). There are, of course, studies that look at the impact of one domain on the other (Franzen & Hangartner, ; Kanas et al., , ; Lancee, ; Martén et al., ; Martinovic et al., ), but so far we have found none that tests for causality running both ways. Prevailing interdependencies between both domains of integration are important from a policy perspective as, for instance, LMI can be accelerated by means of both LMI and SI policies and measures.
Second, we use different SI measures to account for the complexity and multidimensionality of SI. Following other studies (e.g. Cheung & Phillimore, ), we distinguish the concept of social network from that of social capital. Social networks refer to the interactions that take place with persons either from the host society, or from the place a migrant/refugee comes from (co-ethnics), or with migrants/refugees that come from other countries. In quantitative research such as ours, a network is defined by how wide and how intensive the interaction with members of a particular network is. In the context of integration, this is important as social networks can assist integration processes as they provide information and also other types of support for individuals in a foreign, unfamiliar place. In our study, we take into account the ethnic composition of social networks and differentiate between co-ethnic networks with people from the same country of birth and non-coethnic networks, mainly with Austrians. The differentiation by ethnic composition allows a more nuanced and differentiated examination of the role of various networks in integration. Social capital, a concept developed by Putnam ( , ), refers to the direct mobilisation of resources that a social context can potentially provide to a person. It refers to whether a person has actual access—through the social capital he/she has built up—to support in particular circumstances, such as information on job vacancies, interaction with public authorities, social contacts, etc. Social capital is closely linked to social networks, but both the potential to provide support and the nature of this support depend very much on the type of social network in which an individual places him/herself, as well as the individual’s personality characteristics and the particular position that he/she occupies in it.
Third, the study draws on a specific dataset of refugees in Austria, which received one of the highest refugee inflows per capita in Europe, particularly in 2014–2016, during the escalation of the war in Syria. Along with Germany and Sweden, Austria absorbed the largest number of asylum seekers per capita in the EU. Sweden received 17 asylum applications per 1000 inhabitants in 2015, Austria 10 and Germany six. Previous post-WWII refugee flows into Austria were predominantly from other European countries, and this influx was the first significant refugee inflow from outside Europe—Afghans, Syrians, Iraqis, and Iranians lodged the most applications for asylum, together accounting for 76% of all applications in Austria in 2015 (BMI, ). The share of Middle Eastern migrants (defined by the four groups specified above) amounted in 2013 to 2.99% of the total migrant population in Austria (0.67% of the total Austrian population) and rose to 7.73% in 2016 (1.88% of Austria’s total population). The contributions of the four groups in the overall increase of Middle Eastern migrants over the period 2013–2016 are as follows: Afghans 34%, Syrians 44%, Iraqis 14%, Iranians 9%. In view of this, the study of this group of refugees is of particular importance, given that ‘South-North’ migration and refugee flows into Europe can be expected to become more dominant in the future. , Furthermore, the study of recent refugee inflows in this context is particularly interesting as it can analyse the roles that social networks and social capital play in the very early stages of integration, when a historical legacy of social networks from the refugees’ region of origin in the host country hardly exists, or when such network supports become established with the arrival of the refugees.
The employment rate of migrants from the Middle East is low compared with other large non-EU migrant groups in Austria, amounting to 22% for Afghans and Syrians, 20% for Iraqis and 21% for Iranians in 2013, i.e. before the big refugee inflow from the Middle East, compared with 41% for Turks and 27% for Bosnians. Hence, in addition to the lack of established networks, this group of refugees also faces difficulties entering the Austrian labour market. Importantly, ‘refugees’ in our analysis refer to those who have already received a positive decision on asylum or subsidiary protection and thus exclude those still in an asylum application status. They therefore have formal full access to the Austrian labour market as well as to social insurance in the same way as the resident (‘home’) population, as well as receiving further institutional support (both through general labour market policy institutions and also through specific integration programmes designed for refugees; for the latter, see, for example, Ortlieb et al., ; Bešić et al., ). Another specificity—discussed in more detail in Sect.  on the choice of an appropriate instrument for the instrumental variable (IV) analysis—is the importance of club membership in the Austrian context, whereby people with a migration background form a significant share of club members. Clubs—especially sports clubs—are an important venue for refugees (sports clubs particularly for male refugees and migrants), where they can develop social networks with both natives and other refugees and migrants. This is particularly encouraged within the Austrian policy-making framework for refugee and migrant integration (for details, see the Federal Sports Promotion Act). 
In a first-stage analysis, our results show that there is a two-way relationship in which better SI (using social network and social capital indicators) is associated with better labour market outcomes and vice versa, i.e. refugees in paid employment are socially more integrated than unemployed (or inactive) refugees. In both directions, the relevant variable is SI with Austrians; in most instances, SI with co-ethnics was not statistically significant. Nonetheless, in a test for complementarity of the two types of social networks (Austrians and co-ethnics), we find them to be complementary. In a second-stage analysis, we test for endogeneity and obtain robust results in an IV approach for SI being causally relevant for LMI, although we could not prove (owing to weak instruments) a causal effect of LMI on SI. In the former case, it is again SI with Austrians that causally benefit LMI of refugees in terms of a higher probability of being employed (by 89 percentage points) and a lower probability of being unemployed (by 80 percentage points). Such strong effects have also been found in other causal studies (Kanas et al., ; Lancee, ).

## Literature Review and Main Hypotheses
The literature on migrants’ integration into host societies and their economies has become extensive, and studies on refugees’ integration have also developed quite strongly. Overall, one can distinguish between the literature that focuses more on economic issues, in particular LMI, and the literature that examines social, cultural, and political integration. It is clear that different dimensions of integration can and do interact. Research has to acknowledge that, in many instances, interaction can be so strong that integration in one domain cannot be understood without examining progress and blockages in the other domains.

## Social Networks, Social Capital, and Labour Market Integration
The literature generally recognises that social networks play a major role in the economic integration of immigrants and refugees (Beaman, ; Cheung & Phillimore, ; De Vroome & Van Tubergen, ; Delaporte & Matloob, ; Franzen & Hangartner, ; Kanas et al., ; Kazemipur, ; Lamba & Krahn, ; Martinovic et al., , ; Robila, ). It particularly emphasises the difference of the impact of social networks with co-ethnics and with persons of the host society (Damm, ; Edin et al., ; Kanas et al., ; Martén et al., ): they provide different access to information and to contacts and have different implications for the direction and the provision of resources for integration (into the labour market and otherwise). Putnam ( ) refers to the associated build-up of ‘social capital’ as ‘bonding’ if it results from contacts with one’s own ethnic community and as ‘bridging’ if it emerges from contacts with natives.
Contacts with members of the host population in and outside the workplace have been shown in many studies as important for gaining access to important information, affecting job placements and earnings once in employment (Drever & Hoffmeister, ; Kazemipur, ; Tammaru et al., ; Delaporte and Matloop, ; Cheung & Phillimore, ; Verhaeghe et al., ; Lancee, , ; Mouw, ). However, contacts with one’s own community also provide social support and may improve labour market access (Ibrahim et al., ). Immigrant ethnic enclaves may provide labour market information and access to jobs (Kanas et al., ; Levanon, ; Martén et al., ). The benefit for new migrants, and specifically for refugees, of network contacts with co-ethnics depends on how well established these co-ethnic groups are in the host society to provide relevant information and support. Contributions that study specifically the role of ‘ethnic enclaves’ as support networks for refugees (Damm, ; Edin et al., ; Martén et al., ) find that positive impacts of ethnic networks on labour market outcomes and their role can be important, especially in the early phases of LMI (Battisti et al., ).
However, there is also evidence of some negative aspects of a strong anchorage in co-ethnic networks (Battisti et al., ; Finney & Simpson, ), such as if there is a high linguistic concentration in the areas where immigrants or refugees live (Borjas, ; Chiswick & Miller, ; Cutler et al., ; Damm, ; Edin et al., ). There is also a large literature on the enforcement effects that anchorage in co-ethnic or more generally migrant networks can have on labour market segmentation (Colic-Peisker & Tilbury, ; Leontaridi, ; Lusis & Bauder, ).
Although the above literature emphasises the importance of social networks and social capital for LMI, the interaction is likely to go the other way as well, and this might especially be important in relation to developing relationships with the host population. Interaction at the workplace often provides an important sphere for social interaction with co-workers, which could then extend beyond the workplace itself. By contrast, unemployed refugees tend to have fewer opportunities to develop interethnic relationships (Martinovic et al., ).
In our study, we use social networks—also further differentiated by their ethnic composition—and social capital as measures for SI and first establish the relationship between SI and LMI. Similar to the evidence in related studies, we expect that SI and LMI are related and hypothesise that socially more integrated refugees are also more likely to be in paid employment (H1a), and vice versa (H1b). However, the type of social network matters greatly as co-ethnic social networks provide different information, support, and resources than social networks with natives. Particularly in the context of the recent wave of refugees that could not rely on established resident co-ethnic communities from the same region of origin, the development of contacts with members of the host society could be considered to be of much greater importance in obtaining information about the unfamiliar conditions regarding labour market access and employment. Hence, for our sample of recent refugees, we expect that refugees with stronger social networks with Austrians are more likely to be in paid employment than those with stronger co-ethnic networks (H1c).
Our analysis also looks into the relationship between refugees’ co-ethnic networks and their networks with Austrians. It determines whether the two networks are complements, such that refugees with stronger (weaker) co-ethnic networks also have stronger (weaker) networks with Austrians, or substitutes, such that refugees with stronger co-ethnic networks have weaker networks with Austrians (or vice versa). More so than other categories of migrants, refugees often come with few pre-existing networks of family and friends. This was particularly the case for the recent wave of refugees in Austria, which was the first significant refugee inflow from the Middle East. Hence, in the early phase after arrival, refugees have a particular incentive to build up networks with both co-ethnics and natives in parallel, thereby building on the—initially—limited contact linkages between the co-ethnic community and the host community. Therefore, in this particular context, we expect that stronger social networks with co-ethnics are complementary to social networks with Austrians (H2).

## Causal Relationships Between Social and Labour Market Integration
We also address the causal relationships between both domains of integration. The theoretical and empirical literature cited above generally points to a positive effect of SI on LMI. Hence, we expect that SI supports LMI and also—given the particular situation of the recent Middle Eastern wave of refugees—that social networks with Austrians are generally of particular importance to facilitate LMI (H3). Conversely, LMI is also found to have a positive effect on SI, as jobs provide an important sphere for interethnic relationships. We therefore test the hypothesis that LMI leads to better SI (H4). However, depending on the type of LMI—given the possibility of labour market segmentation—it might enforce stronger co-ethnic SI (H5a) or stronger SI with Austrians (H5b).
Overall, we consider the main contribution of this study to focus on the test of causal interrelationships between SI and LMI and the relevance of different ethnic compositions thereby (H3, 4, 5a–b) as well as the analysis of complementarity between co-ethnic and Austrian social networks (H2). H1a-c represents preliminary stages in the analysis prior to testing for causal relationships.

## Data
The analysis uses data from a large-scale survey of recognised refugees and persons with subsidiary protection status from Syria, Afghanistan, Iraq, and Iran, between 15 and 64 years of age, and resident in Austria. It is a unique longitudinal dataset, with a one-year re-interview interval. For this paper, we use data from the second and third waves. 
The second wave was conducted between December 2017 and April 2018 in five Austrian provinces (Vienna, Upper Austria, Styria, Salzburg, and Tyrol). The third wave was conducted between March and mid-May 2019, in all nine Austrian provinces.
Three different methods were used to recruit participants. First, the majority of the interviewees were reached by a random sampling of asylum seekers and beneficiaries of subsidiary protection who either are or were previously registered with the Austrian Public Employment Service (AMS), which was based on AMS client data. By law, recognised refugees are obliged to register with the AMS if they want access to support services (such as initial accommodation support, financial support, support through labour market policies, etc.) and hence the dataset captures the bulk of refugees who have obtained asylum status in Austria. Interviewees were selected based on random sampling, stratified by province, gender, and citizenship. Depending on available contact information, participants were invited via text message, email, or regular mail, all of which contained a personalised link to the online questionnaire (CAWI). The response rates were 11% in the second wave and 6.6% in the third wave.
Second, various offices of the AMS and other institutions concerned with refugee integration were visited in the five above-mentioned Austrian provinces. Trained bilingual interviewers approached refugee clients in person and invited them to participate in the interview. The selection criteria were the same as mentioned above. Interviews were conducted on-site either face-to-face (CAPI) or through self-administered questionnaires (CASI) on tablets. Face-to-face interviews were conducted by the trained interviewers in German, Arabic, or Farsi. The questionnaire was the same as administered online.
Third, respondents from the previous survey waves, including the first wave (August 2016 to May 2017) who had agreed to be re-interviewed, were contacted again. They form the panel part of the survey.
The questionnaire is largely based on the questionnaire from the German IAB-BAMF-SOEP refugee survey 2016 (TNS Infratest Sozialforschung, ) and covers topics such as employment, social integration, health, education, and family and living conditions. It was available in German, Arabic, and Farsi. Participants received a shopping voucher of EUR 5–10, depending on whether they were participating for the first time or had participated in previous surveys.
The data were cleaned to remove partial interviews and interviews that were completed too quickly, as well as contradictory answers. The quality of responses hardly differed across survey methods. After cleaning, the survey sample comprised around 4030 refugees (1630 refugees from the second wave and 2400 from the third wave). Panellists numbered only around 100 in the second wave and just over 300 in the third wave. They were included only when they first appeared in a survey wave. 
Our analysis uses a sub-sample that is restricted to individuals from Syria, Afghanistan, Iraq, and Iran who had come to Austria after 2010, were older than 18 years at the time of the interview, had a resident status that granted them access to the labour market and for whom complete information was available for all model variables. We have also excluded the small group of females, who represent less than 30% of the restricted sample and for whom tests have shown heterogeneity of behaviour when they were included in the estimations and for whom the instrument for SI used in our IV strategy—sports club density—is less relevant. Although the sample is small and the number of females is unevenly represented in the second and third waves (i.e. females were oversampled and therefore overrepresented in the third wave), which is a concern for a rigorous comparison across genders, we have nonetheless attempted to identify the interrelationships between SI and LMI among women. The results are discussed in Sect.  . Summary statistics as well as two-sample hypotheses tests of key model variables point to important differences between men and women in the restricted sample with respect to LMI and SI, and previous work experience, among other factors (see Table ).
After exclusion of these cases, the final sample used in our analyses comprises 1020 respondents. As concerns sampling mode, 81% of respondents had been contacted based on the AMS random sample, 14% were interviewed on site, and 5% had participated in previous survey waves.

LMI is captured in terms of people’s labour market status at the time of the interview, namely as (i) unemployed (and registered as searching); (ii) inactive due to, for instance, parental leave, studying/training, ill-health/physical disabilities, household responsibilities, or other reasons (unspecified); and (iii) employed, only in paid employment (any type of unpaid, voluntary work is excluded). The composition of the group of inactive persons in the sample is dominated by (young) refugees in education or training (71%).
As concerns SI, we construct different indices from various survey questions. To account for the complexity and multidimensionality of SI, we distinguish the concept of social network from that of social capital. Social networks are measured by their size as well as by how frequently contacts are made with people in the network, which together capture two of their key defining characteristics. Generally, network size captures the physical extent of a person’s network and is a proxy for the potential information and resources that can be obtained. The literature emphasises that larger social networks are better than small ones, also in the context of LMI (Edin et al., ; Munshi, ). However, rather than the availability of potential information and resources embedded in larger social networks, it is their access (and subsequent actual mobilisation) that should matter more for (labour market) integration outcomes. In particular, this potential can be tapped into and exploited only if people access and actively participate in their social networks, which requires social interactions and more intense contacts with those who constitute these networks. The size of the social network is captured by the question ‘How many people do you know in Austria who are important to you and who you feel close to (excluding parents, partner, children)?’ Answers are coded on a five-point scale, ranging from (1) none to (2) one, (3) two, (4) three, and (5) more than three (as an open answer option). We further distinguish social networks by their ethnic composition and differentiate between co-ethnic networks (only with people from the same country of birth) and networks with non-coethnics. The size of co-ethnic networks comprising people who come from the same country of origin/are of the same nationality, is captured by the question ‘How many of the aforementioned persons are countrymen?’ As there was no similar question that captures the size of networks with non-coethnics, we approximated it through the difference between the size of the total network and the size of co-ethnic networks. The ethnic composition of the group of non-coethnics is captured only from the third wave onwards, which shows that non-coethnic networks are dominated by Austrians, and we therefore refer to these networks as networks with Austrians. The frequency with which contacts are made with the people from the network is again differentiated by the ethnic composition of the social network: ‘How often do you spend time with persons from your country of origin who are not related to you?’ captures the contact intensity with the co-ethnic network, while ‘How often do you spend time with Austrians?’ captures the contact intensity with Austrians. Both questions are measured on a six-point scale, ranging from (1) never to (6) daily.
Social capital is measured by means of six survey questions that capture whether respondents ‘know someone in Austria who … ‘ (1) ‘would help you move or renovate your apartment’, (2) ‘would draw your attention to a job vacancy’, (3) ‘you could discuss personal problems with’, (4) ‘would assist you in completing a form from public authorities’, (5) ‘lend you 200 Euro’ or (6) ‘you could have a pleasant time with over coffee or tea’. By construction, this set of questions refers to the total social network and does not differentiate between social capital either related to people from the same country of origin or Austrians (or migrants from a third country). All social capital variables are dummy variables and equal to one in the case of an affirmative answer.
Based on these questions, three indices were constructed:Austrian network index (NETW-AT) captures the size of a person’s network predominantly with Austrians and the contact intensity with Austrians only;
Co-ethnic network index (NETW-COB) captures the size of a person’s network with other persons from the same country of origin and the intensity of contact with them;
Total social capital index (SC-TOT) refers to a person’s overall social capital.

To construct the three SI indices, the first principal component from a polychoric principal component analysis (PCA) is extracted from each set of underlying questionnaire items described above. The polychoric correlation matrices point to fairly strong correlations between the different variables (ranging between 0.6 and 0.9), which renders the PCA a viable approach. Generally, the first principal components of the three SI indices account for 60–70% of the variance in the data. All underlying variables show sufficient positive correlation with the first principal components and all underlying variables vary together. To ease their interpretability, the first principal components are rescaled to have a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one.

Conversely, a standard ordinary least squares (OLS) approach is used for the different social integration indices with dummies for respondents’ labour market status as additional exogenous variables.

Second, the residuals are retained and used as additional regressors in a multinomial logit model, together with the endogenous variable and all exogenous variables . As the usual standard errors are incorrect, we use a two-stage bootstrap and, to guarantee a certain degree of precision, use 1,000 bootstrap replications to calculate correct standard errors.
A relevant and exogenous instrument for the first-stage regression requires that it is sufficiently correlated with the endogenous variable , but uncorrelated with the outcome variable , conditional on other control variables. In addition, the exclusion restriction requires that there should not be any theoretical reason why the instrument should be related to the outcome variable, but only via the instrumented variable (Wooldridge, ). We use as instrument the density of sports clubs (per 10,000 inhabitants) by province (NUTS-2 region) two years before arrival in Austria. Hence, the identifying variation comes from the variation across regions and time of arrival in Austria. Sports club density lends itself well as a relevant instrument for SI as sports clubs can be a focal point for refugees to meet new people and develop their social networks (Doidge et al., ; Makarova & Herzog, ), not only with natives but also with other refugees and migrants. In Austria, people with a migration background form a non-negligible share of club members (The Expert Council for Integration, ) which can facilitate integration with other migrants and refugees. Furthermore, Austrian policy makers attribute an important role for SI to clubs—particularly sports clubs—which is stipulated in the Federal Sports Promotion Act (§§1 and 2) and reflected in the financial support and awards given each year to clubs for innovative and successful integration initiatives for migrants, such as the ‘Annual Integration Prize for Sports’. Sports clubs are of particular importance for the sample of male refugees in our analysis, as male refugees participate more strongly in sports clubs than their female counterparts (Stura, ). In principle, membership of clubs could support members to increase their employment chances through, for instance, the provision of assistance with CV writing and interview techniques as in different ‘Sport Plus’ initiatives (for an overview, see European Commission, ). However, in the Austrian case, this is not declared as an explicit aim of the clubs we included in our analysis. Any informal support in this direction would be channelled via social networks and social capital (McDonald et al., ), which justifies our approach. Hence, the suggested instruments seem appropriate ones for SI.
For Eq. ( ), we follow Wooldridge and Imbens ( ) and use a two-step IV procedure: First, the endogenous variable is regressed on a set of exogenous control variables, and three instruments and fitted values are derived for both labour market outcomes. Second, Eq. ( ) is estimated by IV, with the fitted values and all other exogenous variables as instruments. The usual standard errors are (asymptotically) valid.
For instruments, we use two variables that capture a person’s past labour market experience prior to arriving in Austria, in terms of whether a person has ever had a job for more than two months before arriving in Austria and whether a person has worked during their flight. Moreover, we use as a third instrument the economic situation of the household relative to other households in the home country before migration (dummies for ‘above’ and ‘below’ average, respectively, and average as reference). All three instruments are considered to be relevant as past labour market experience is found to be a good predictor of current labour market status, owing to acquired experience and human capital in previous jobs (Buddelmeyer et al., ). Moreover, wealth in the form of financial savings reduces the urgency of job search and the probability of transiting into employment (Algan et al., ; Bloemen, ; Bloemen & Stancanelli, ). The instruments’ relevance will be established during our empirical analysis. Both of the first two instruments, related to a person’s past labour market experience, are exogenous because, unless a refugee’s current social network in Austria consists of previous employers or co-workers, they exclusively affect SI only through LMI. In fact, most Middle Eastern refugees are reported to travel with family members or alone (REACH, ), with little evidence that they migrated with former employers or co-workers who could later constitute their social networks. For a similar reason, the third instrument, related to the household’s economic situation before migration, is also exogenous, as we argue that—apart from family, which is excluded from our definition of SI—there is no evidence that refugees’ pre-flight and current social networks in Austria overlap, and therefore it would affect SI only through LMI. 

## Results

## Social Networks, Social Capital, and Labour Market Integration
In line with Hypothesis 1a, our results show that refugees in paid employment are socially more integrated than unemployed (or inactive) refugees (Table  ). This, however, only holds for SI with Austrians (p < 0.05); there are no statistically significant effects for SI with co-ethnic networks. Moreover, refugees in paid employment also have more social capital (p < 0.05). For the small sample of women, it is only the coefficient on social capital that is significant (p < 0.05). We have taken into account that the three SI indices we have constructed from different response items may be distorted. For example, the two network indices also include contact intensity, which is generally higher among employed persons (whose co-workers tend to be Austrian). Moreover, social capital includes six items, but only one is directly related to LMI ((2) ‘would draw your attention to a job vacancy’). Hence, in a robustness check, we have excluded contact intensity and looked only at network size—measured by the number of (co-ethnic and Austrian) network members—as an indicator of social networks. Similarly, we have looked at the six support items as separate indicators of social capital to see what role job referrals play relative to the other items. Our results remain robust and consistent with those reported in Table  : unemployed refugees have smaller networks with Austrians; refugees in paid employment are more likely to know someone who would draw their attention to a job vacancy (the remaining social capital items all turn out to be insignificant). 
Our results also show—in line with Hypothesis 1b—that socially more integrated refugees are also more likely to be in paid employment than to be inactive (p < 0.01) (Table  ). However, the employment effects of SI tend to be stronger for social networks than for social capital. Furthermore, in line with Hypothesis 1c, we find that the ethnic composition of social networks matters strongly and that stronger social networks with Austrians are of particular importance for the probability of being in paid employment. Conversely, co-ethnic social networks have no statistically significant relationship with the probability of being in paid employment. We find similar patterns for the small sample of women, although they benefit less than men from social networks with Austrians, but benefit more from social capital. 
In contrast, we find that refugees with stronger social networks with Austrians and more social capital are less likely to be unemployed (p < 0.01 for both). Our robustness analysis, looking only at network size as an indicator of social networks and the six support items as separate indicators of social capital, shows similarly consistent results to those presented in Table  : larger networks with Austrians are associated with a lower (higher) probability of being unemployed (employed); there are no significant results for the size of co-ethnic social networks. Moreover, ‘job referrals’ are positively (negatively) associated with the probability of being employed (unemployed). The coefficients for the few other items of relevance are much smaller. 

## Complementarity Between Social Networks
Consistent with Hypothesis 2, our results show that both networks are complements (Table  , Column (1)), but the relationship is rather weak ( =0.13, p < 0.01). Further analysis shows that the complementarity between both networks differs across ethnic groups and only holds for Syrian refugees (p < 0.05) (Table  , Column (2)). 

## Additional Factors relevant for SI
Table  points to differences by religious affiliation and country of birth: Christians are socially more integrated with Austrians (p < 0.05) and have more social capital (p < 0.01), whereas Muslims are socially more integrated with co-ethnic networks (p < 0.01). Moreover, compared with refugees from Afghanistan, refugees from Iran and Syria are socially less integrated with Austrians (p < 0.01 and p < 0.05, respectively). Refugees from Syria are socially more integrated with co-ethnic networks (p < 0.01).

## Additional Factors relevant for LMI
Consistent with previous studies, Table  shows that the probability of being in paid employment increases with the length of stay in Austria and is higher among refugees who are better educated (p < 0.01) (have at least a high-school leaving examination, i.e. Matura), indicating that better education facilitates the transition into employment.
We also find that the probability of being in paid employment (compared with being inactive) is lower among recognised refugees than among refugees who have been granted subsidiary protection (p < 0.1). This suggests that recognised refugees have a more secure time horizon regarding their stay in Austria and hence are more inclined to undergo further education and training than those who have been granted subsidiary protection. Similar to SI, we find that religious affiliation also matters for LMI, with Muslims less likely to be in paid employment (p < 0.05).

## Accounting for Endogeneity
As discussed in Sect.  , we applied IV procedures to account for the endogeneity to establish causality between the two domains of integration. Here, we discuss only the results for the causal effect of SI on LMI. Because our instruments proved to be weak, we could not robustly establish the causality from LMI to SI, and so we have moved the discussion of the results to the Annex.

## Causal Effect of SI on LMI
We first determined the relevance of the instrument and ran the first-stage regression, where we regressed the three SI measures (plus the full set of control variables) on the instrument that measures sports club density at the provincial level. Table  shows that sports club density and SI are statistically significantly related: a higher sports club density by one is associated with significantly higher SI with Austrians by 0.022 standard deviations (p < 0.01), but is not significantly related to co-ethnic networks or social capital. 
Furthermore, the residuals from the second-stage regression are significant for all three measures of social integration (p < 0.01), indicating that they are all endogenous. However, as the instruments for SI with co-ethnic networks and for social capital are insignificant, the second-stage results cannot be used to infer endogeneity. Hence, the results from the second-stage regression allow only for a causal interpretation of the relationship between social networks with Austrians and LMI. In line with Hypothesis 3, we find that better SI with Austrians leads to a higher probability of being employed by 89 percentage points (p < 0.01) and a lower probability of being unemployed by 80 percentage points (p < 0.01). Such a strong employment effect is also found in studies of immigrant men in Germany, whose likelihood of employment increases by 106% (Kanas et al., ) or by 51% (Lancee, ) as a result of having contacts with Germans. Hence, an important role is attributable to sports clubs, which facilitate refugees’ SI—particularly with Austrians—and are indirectly also beneficial to their LMI.
Sports club density is also relevant (i.e. significantly related to SI) for the small sample of women, but only for SI with Austrians. Furthermore, the residuals from the second-stage regression are significant (p < 0.01), but only meaningful for SI with Austrians. In line with Hypothesis 3, but in contrast to men, the causal effect of SI on being in paid employment is much weaker, estimated at 31 percentage points (p < 0.001), highlighting that, for women, the LMI integration benefit from stronger SI (given the instrument—i.e. sports club density—that we used) with Austrians is lower than for men.
Importantly, both coefficients are higher than in the exogenous setting (see Table  above), suggesting that not accounting for endogeneity leads to biased (underestimated) estimates potentially attributable to measurement errors in the endogenous variable or to the effect of the instrument on the subset of individuals who were affected by the instrument (‘local average treatment effect’) (Card, ).
Moreover, in line with the literature on the differential effect of country of origin on refugees’ LMI that shows that culturally ‘closer’ refugees integrate quicker than more distant ones (see, for example, Fasani et al., ; Jestl et al., ; Lundborg, ), we also determine differences by country of origin in the causal effect of SI on LMI. 

## Conclusions
We analysed LMI and SI of recent male refugees in Austria and studied the direction and size of prevailing interrelationships between employment, unemployment, social networks (differentiated by their ethnic composition), and social capital. We used a unique survey dataset of about 4000 refugees from Syria, Iraq, Afghanistan, and Iran who have come to Austria since 2010, thereby covering the recent strong refugee wave of 2014–2016.
Consistent with previous studies, our analysis shows that LMI and SI are interrelated such that socially more integrated refugees are also more likely to be in paid employment, and vice versa. It also shows that refugees in paid employment are socially better integrated with Austrians.
Beyond that, the analysis also addresses the inherent endogeneity between LMI and SI and investigates the causal relationship between both domains of integration. It finds an important causal link and shows that social networks with Austrians facilitate LMI, in terms of improved chances of finding paid employment. In contrast to the strong relevance of social networks with Austrians, our results show no specific support from co-ethnic networks for finding employment. This deviates from some studies, which acknowledge the importance of ‘ethnic enclaves’ in this respect (Damm, ; Edin et al., ; Martén et al., ). This result could be a consequence of the limitations of the instrument that we were able to use in our analysis, or it could reflect the actual weakness of co-ethnic networks in furthering employment prospects for this particular group of refugees. Despite this, the robust result with respect to the importance of Austrian networks for LMI should support policies that enhance social network development with Austrians as key for refugees’ transition into employment.
The analysis also addressed the relationship between refugees’ co-ethnic networks and their networks with Austrians and shows that the two networks are complementary, rather than substitutes. This is important, because stronger linkages with the co-ethnic community do not hinder, but are complementary to, contacts with non-coethnics, predominantly Austrians. This result may reflect the specific situation of the group of recent refugees we study who, during the early phase of integration, build their networks and socially integrate with both groups in parallel. The recognition of this complementarity should be important for refugee integration policy.
In many respects, our analysis points to the particular circumstances and challenges faced by the group of recent refugees during the early phases of integration. Hence, future longitudinal research will study over the next survey waves whether the relative importance of different social networks (as well as their relationships to each other) and labour market prospects change over time as the same group of refugees moves on to the next stages of integration. Furthermore, future research—building on an extended dataset—can deepen the analysis as to whether aspects of social and labour market integration and their interaction operate differentially for different groups (by age, qualifications, gender, etc.) and for different types of jobs.

## Results for the Causal Effect of LMI on SI
A particular two-step IV approach was applied to account for the endogeneity and establish the causal effect of LMI on SI. We use two instruments that capture whether a person has ever had a job before arriving in Austria and has worked during flight, and another instrument that captures the economic situation of the household before flight (relative to others in the home country). Table presents the results, which highlight that previous work experience outside Austria is unable to predict the probability of being in paid employment in Austria. Furthermore, under-identification tests suggest that the instruments are jointly highly relevant (p < 0.01). Cragg-Donald Wald F statistics suggest that the instruments are not too strong (the maximum relative bias due to weak instruments is slightly higher than 10%). Because weak instruments not only lead to biased point estimators but also affect the endogeneity test, which cannot be rejected at conventional levels of statistical significance, no inferences can be drawn as to the potential causality running from LMI to SI, and Hypotheses 4, 5a, and 5b can neither be confirmed nor rejected. This also applies to the small sample of women.
