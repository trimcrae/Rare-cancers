## Background:
As technological advancements grow, so does its impact. Humans are seeing more and more computerization, a lot of it is being used for surgery. Their merger results in various legal problems relating to regulation, patient safety, and governance.

## Objectives:
The main objectives of this article are to study and join together legal rules and systems as it pertains to the use of artificial intelligence in medical situations for all main countries.

## Methods:
A narrative legal review was conducted to see what’s been going on since 2010. Their sources were official documents like drug administration and legislation along articles written by numerous professors. Researchers in government are finding new ways to monitor themselves.

## Results:
Most AI health diagnosis had a regulated path if having to be approved, though one diagnostic system challenged that. The high-risk method of using robotic surgery leaves room for error between the overseeing surgeon and the manufacturer of the robot to be liable. Cross-cutting subject consist of the GDPR, HIPAA, the post-market and the surveillance, cybersecurity, and ethical considerations, which consist of the legal and privacy issue we have to take and apply into our lives every day. The progress in regulations has been noticed, but remains unclear for its implementation across different parts of the world.

## Conclusion:
A cohesive framework is required to balance innovative advancements with safety precautions for patients. Future governance must combine technology standards, medical testing, and safety rules to improve how artificial intelligence and robots are used in medical centers.

## BACKGROUND
AI-assisted tools are now extensively used in diagnosis pathways to help with image sorting, risk prediction, and even independent assessment in specific cases (ex: diabetic eye screening) ( ). Operating rooms have witnessed the evolution of robotic platforms from merely telemanipulation systems to complex computer-assisted surgical ecosystems with levels of autonomy. True telesurgery across borders is technically now feasible but legally rarely done ( ). These technologies facilitate earlier detection, fewer complications and greater access ( ). However, they also raise challenges for the existing legal notions of device classification, informed consent, change control of learning algorithms, data protection, and damage allocation for harm ( ).
Two regulatory “layers” are central. Sector-specific rules for medical devices describe when software or robots are medical devices, set premarket evidentiary requirements and impose post-market vigilance (EU MDR/IVDR; FDA device law; MHRA guidance) ( ). Next, the horizontal AI or human rights laws such as the EU AI Act; the Council of Europe AI Convention impose additional governing duties such as risk management, transparency, and human oversight that may apply in parallel ( , ). Both of them are privacy and security legislation (GDPR; HIPAA) regulating training data, deployment data flows, and patients’ algorithmic rights ( ). In conclusion, in practice, organization standards and guidance (e.g., IMDRF, ISO, IEC, NIST, NICE) implement risk management as well as software lifecycle control and evidence standards ( , ).

## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
4.1.

## When AI is a ‘medical device’: software and robots in law

Software as a Medical Device (SaMD)

Regulators typically classify standalone software as a medical device if it has a medical purpose and is intended to its functions assist or inform clinical decisions ( ). EU software regulations refer to improvements made to distinctive software, which are designated with the “EU class” by the developer and are used as a medical device for the body or for human health ( ). The software is considered medical device software (MDSW) if it has a medical purpose. Its classification is based on the intended purpose and risk ( ). The MDCG (Medical Device Coordination Group) has released guidance to help with the clinical and performance evaluation of MDSW. The guidance reinforces expectations around analytical validity, clinical association and clinical performance ( ).
The International Medical Device Regulators Forum (IMDRF) has a classification system for SaMD (Software as a Medical Device) with risk categories I-IV based on importance of information and severity of clinical condition. The FDA and the EU have adopted the above IMDRF principles to help jurisdictions and manufacturers use a common taxonomy ( ).

Robotic and computer-assisted surgical systems

RAS devices are considered medical devices under the federal law. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) deals with computer-assisted surgical systems—robots for surgery (RAS)—through its device authorities and has a topic page explaining such systems’ uses (planning, navigation, and surgical assistance) ( ). Many RAS devices were cleared via the 510(k) pathway as Class II devices with special controls showing substantial equivalence to predicates instead of via de novo or PMA pathway ( ). The submissions to the FDA related to image-guided navigation systems highlight how non-clinical testing and comparison to predicates support clearance ( ).

4.2.

## Horizontal AI frameworks and their interaction with health-device law

The EU AI Act and medical AI

In 2024, the EU AI Act (phased application) would become the first comprehensive AI statute, incorporating a risk-based approach with high-risk requirements for AI designed as a safety component of products governed by sectoral law, including devices regulated by law, including medical devices ( ). When software qualifies as a medical device under the MDR/IVDR, it will usually be considered as high-risk AI. As such, it will have various obligations covering risk management, data quality, technical documentation, transparency, human oversight, and post-market monitoring. The high-risk AI obligations therefore complement the existing obligations of the MDR/IVDR, but as a general rule of thumb, help the manufacturer in its conformity assessment under the MDR/IVDR ( ). For SaMD (software as a medical device) diagnostic AI, this means two types of compliance: rules governing the device (for safety and performance) plus AI Act governance controls (for example, data-set representativeness and bias mitigation) ( ).

Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI

The first global treaty regulating artificial intelligence opened for signature in September 2024 at the Council of Europe, and aims to ensure the enjoyment of human rights, democracy, and the rule of law throughout the entire AI lifecycle. Although the Convention does not regulate medical devices, it will establish binding principles on its signatories (US, UK, EU, etc.). These principles can have a cascading effect on sectoral rules on risk assessment, transparency, accountability, and avenues to contest decisions impacted by AI. Health organizations ought to expect that the governance of clinical AI will include these human-rights-oriented duties (e.g. the rights to explanation and remedy) ( ).

4.3.

## United States: FDA pathways and adaptive AI

FDA pathways for AI/ML devices and autonomy in surgery

FDA has allowed many AI/ML-enabled devices through existing device pathways. A major milestone in the realm of evaluation systems was the granting of the 2018 De Novo authorization of the IDx-DR (now LumineticsCore). This was the first ever autonomous diagnostic system for diabetic retinopathy that makes an independent clinical decision for use in a primary care setting ( ). Most of the FDA-cleared surgical robots systems are categorized Class II that is cleared through 510k. Moreover, existing systems are clustered at a low level of autonomy (level 1) and human-in-the-loop control. These regulatory decisions impact the burden of proof and after-market regulations governing robot platforms ( ).

Learning algorithms and the Predetermined Change Control Plan (PCCP)

AI models often require updates after they are deployed, such as drift correction and/or performance improvements. The FDA along with partners from outside the U.S. articulated 10 Good Machine Learning Practice (GMLP) principles and has also developed PCCPs for “machine learning-enabled medical devices” so that developers can prospectively specify the “what/why/how” of future model changes without having to re-submit each update ( ). The PCCP Design is based on a lifecycle approach: definition of the algorithm change protocol, data management and monitoring plans, and guardrails. As patient-centered care paradigms (PCCPs) go from concept to practice, makers of diagnostic AI and adaptive assistance in surgery will need strong MDSAP/ISO13485 QMS links to data governance, verification, and real-world performance monitoring ( ).

Cybersecurity as a device quality and safety issue

The FDA’s cybersecurity guidance for 2023-2024 was released in draft form in May 2022. It has five key cybersecurity aspects–secure-by-design engineering, SBOMs, threat modelling, and coordinated disclosure. It directly ties these aspects to premarket submissions that have cybersecurity risk. In this regard, it is highly pertinent to networked diagnostics and connected RAS consoles. Since both RAS and SaMD are dependent on data pipelines and hospital networks, cybersecurity obligations have become integral to the safety and compliance of the device ( , ).

## European Union and United Kingdom: MDR/IVDR, NICE, MHRA

EU classification, clinical evidence, and performance evaluation

A medical device is basically a software designed to perform diagnosis or curing of a human. If a software is made to perform such a task, it is termed a medical device under EU law. The law classifies a medical device by its risk and it depends on where it is used and what can happen if it fails ( - , ). The MDCG guidance on MDSW includes the expectations for clinical evaluation (MDR) and performance evaluation (IVDR) for software including analytical validation, clinical association and clinical performance that particular attention to the quality and generalizability of datasets 10. Robots and surgery navigation equipment are subject to MDR rules regarding safety, performance, and clinical proof. This means that, depending on class, notified bodies will perform a safety performance and clinical proof-assessment of devices ( , ).

United Kingdom: MHRA and the NICE Evidence Standards Framework

Even after BREXIT, the UK continues to regulate software/AI as a medical device by way of MHRA guidance and proposed renewed UKCA regime. The MHRA’s guidance on “Software and AI as a Medical Device” describes requirements, evidence, and post-market expectations for AI-enabled tools ( ). Moreover, 2022’s Evidence Standards Framework (ESF) update to NICE to include AI or adaptive algorithms sets out clinical and economic evidence standards for digital technologies considered for NHS commissioning ( ). The ESF shapes adoption and reimbursement but mainly sets out how to align with regulation based upon its risk-based principles ( ).

4.5.

## Data protection, privacy, and algorithmic rights in diagnostics and surgery

EU GDPR: special-category data and automated decisions

Information relating to health is classified as a ‘special category’ under the GDPR. Therefore, they are subject to strict conditions on processing (Art. 9) and further safeguards ( ). Right at the automated decision making of individuals (art. 22) give them rights when they are subject to decisions that have legal or similarly significant effects and that result from automated decisions which are considered “solely” by automated means. In general, explicit consent is required or else there must be a legal basis and meaningful human oversight and contestability ( , ). When AI works on diagnosis, they should have humans watching. They must tell us how they design these projects. They must also guide us on challenging their designs when something goes wrong. This is more the case if systems do screening or triage without humans in the picture ( ).

HIPAA in the US: de-identification and data governance

In the United States, the Privacy Rule of HIPAA regulates the PHI held by the covered entities and BA. Organizations frequently utilize HIPAA-de-identified data through either “Safe Harbor” removal of key identifiers or an “Expert Determination” that ensures very low risk of re-identification for AI development and post-market surveillance ( ). HIPAA is more limited in its use as compared to the GDPR. Not all consumer health data is covered by HIPAA. However, its guidance on de-identification can serve as a key resource. This is important for lawful use of data for training devices and monitoring. To create this report, you only have to find the right person. After that, you get the report in some hours ( ).

Clinical evidence, safety management, and standards

Risk management and software lifecycle

ISO 14971:2019 is the cornerstone international standard for medical-device risk management, and requires an orderly process that identifies hazards, estimates and evaluates risk, controls risk and monitors their effectiveness over the life-cycle of the device ( , ). IEC 62304 specifies lifecycle processes for software (development planning, software risk management, verification/validation, maintenance) and is closely contained within ISO 14971( ). These guidelines ‘translate’ accountability for legal requirements into engineerable diagnostics AI and surgical system activities ( ).

NIST AI RMF: a horizontal risk and governance scaffold

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0) is a non-binding framework that can nevertheless be influential. It addresses characteristics of trustworthy AI (validity, reliability, safety, transparency, privacy enhancement, fairness, accountability) as well as organizational functions (MAP-MEASURE-MANAGE-GOVERN) ( ). Health-sector organizations are using the AI RMF more and more to add enterprise AI governance to their device-specific responsibilities such as bias monitoring, incident response, documentation, etc., that aligns with the MDR/FDA and post-market surveillance ( ).

Clinical performance and value

Payers and health systems want proof of clinical effectiveness and economic value in addition to safety and performance. In the UK, appropriate evidence levels and deployment standards—including those for adaptive algorithms—are specified in the NICE ESF, to guide commissioning decisions. Governments and Policy-Makers AI-based diagnostic tools are expected to undergo robust clinical evaluation, monitoring of real world performance as well as equity evaluation for clinical utility across the population ( , , ).

## Liability and accountability in AI diagnostics and robotic surgery

Product liability and failure to warn: lessons from robotic surgery

Law suits on RAS have examined what responsibility manufacturers (the makers of robotic-assisted surgical systems) have to warn representatives of known or able known risks with the adoption of such systems and their efforts to support safe adoption through training and credentialing ( ). In the case of Taylor v. Intuitive Surgical, Inc. (Washington Supreme Court, 2017), a court ruled that the maker of the da Vinci system has a duty to warn the hospital which purchased the system not just the surgeon, based on the state product liability act. This shows that an institutional duty may be relevant to complex surgical platforms ( ). Though case law is specific to one jurisdiction, the lessons from Taylor are broadly applicable. If products require training, proctoring, and hospital credentialing, warnings and implementation support must go to the purchaser-institution ( ).

Professional negligence and standard of care

Clinicians remain accountable for upholding the standard of care while using AI tools or RAS. Courts will probably examine if a reasonably careful doctor would take, ignore, or situate an AI output; personality documentation of human oversight becomes a defensive necessity. As clinical decision making rises, the share of responsibility between clinician and device will be tested. Also, ever-more under PCCP systems. In order to defend care, transparent instructions for use, clear indications/contraindications, and audit trails of model recommendations and human decisions will be paramount ( ).

Cross-border tele-surgery and jurisdictional complexity

Real cross-border telesurgery does not happen yet, a result of technological, licensing and legal hurdles, although is more and more studied and piloted ( ). There are some legal challenges regarding licensing credentials across jurisdictions where the applicable law is. Forum is, for example, where is the “procedure’ legally located? Liability allocation remote surgeon local surgeon hospital or hospital device. Moreover, there is a manufacturer data protection cybersecurity across borders. Scholars and policy proposals call for malpractice insurance arrangements and international frameworks that allocate accountability and provide patients with redress for procedures conducted remotely ( , ). While uniform rules wait to emerge, institutions should implement contractual architectures to specify governing-law venue, indemnities, and incident response; and ensure compliance with local medical-device and data protection regimes.

5.

## CONCLUSION
The use of artificial intelligence (AI) in diagnostics and robotic surgery is a complex one, laying at the junction of device law, AI governance, data protection and clinical liability. While the legal landscape is complicated, it is becoming easier to navigate, according to experts. According to experts, device regimes, including the FDA, EU MDR/IVDR, and MHRA, will provide a backbone for safety and performance as a result of horizontal instruments. An executive instrument is the EU AI Act. A single governance framework that treats models as regulated devices, anticipates adaptation through PCCPs, incorporates human oversight and cyber-security, and aligns clinical evidence with real-world monitoring offers developers and healthcare institutions a practical, lawful and trustworthy pathway to deployment.
These frameworks can be supportive of innovation if they are implemented diligently while protecting patients in diagnosing and operating.
