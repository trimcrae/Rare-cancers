## Abstract
Introduction: Social capital is a common target of intervention used by policymakers to address the health challenges of older adults. However, evidence for the effectiveness of social capital interventions remain still clearly lacking. Methods: This study used a longitudinal study design. We used three waves of data (2016, 2017, and 2019) from 6025 older adults in Japan. We assessed ten social capital indicators in 2019 as outcomes. The exposure was participation in community gathering places in 2017. Pre-exposure characteristics and prior outcome levels in 2016 were adjusted. Linear regression analysis and modified Poisson regression analysis were employed according to the nature of the outcome. Results: Community gathering places participation was positively associated with nine social capital indicators; frequency of meeting friends (B: 0.55), number of friends seen within a month (B: 0.49), number of community organizations (B: 0.28–0.55), norms of reciprocity (B: 0.18), and attachment (B: 0.15); all results showed p1

## Introduction
In recent years, disciplines such as public health, medicine, epidemiology, and gerontology have increasingly emphasized the significance of social capital in shaping health outcomes ( ). The concept of social capital has generated considerable scholarly debate, particularly regarding its definition and operationalization. Various disciplines—including sociology, economics, political science, and public health—have proposed distinct yet overlapping conceptualizations of social capital ( ). For instance, Lin's theory focuses on individual access to instrumental resources through social ties ( ). In contrast, Coleman and Putnam emphasize the collective benefits derived from network structures and generalized trust ( ; ). Despite these differing perspectives, a growing academic consensus has emerged around a multidimensional understanding of social capital. While the precise definition remains debated, many scholars broadly describe it as resources embedded in social relationships, such as mutual aid, enforcement of group norms, stocks of trust, and shared expectations ( ; ; ). These resources may benefit individuals within a group or the group as a whole. More recent frameworks distinguish between cognitive dimensions (such as trust and reciprocity) and structural dimensions (such as social networks and participation), acknowledging that social capital can function both at the individual level and at the community level ( ). This multidimensional nature highlights the importance of clearly specifying how social capital is conceptualized and measured in empirical research. As this consensus evolves, public health practice increasingly acknowledges social capital as both a determinant of health and an actionable intervention target (E. ). Importantly, social capital is also conceptualized as a key intermediate mechanism through which social participation and community-based interventions exert their influence on health outcomes ( ; ). Given these circumstances, policymakers are seeing the benefits of implementing social capital as an intervention for improving health outcomes (E. ).
One of the countries where policymakers are enthusiastic about community interventions utilizing social capital is Japan, which has the world's oldest population. As of 2024, government estimates indicate that adults aged 65 and older account for 29.3 % of Japan's population, and this proportion is projected to increase to 34.8 % by 2040. Over the years, the Japanese government has reformed its policies to promote healthy ageing ( ). In light of the limited effectiveness of the high-risk strategy launched in 2006, Japan has, since 2015, adopted a Healthy Ageing strategy centered on a population approach ( ; ; J. ). The focus of this approach is the use of community gathering places called “Kayoi-no-ba.” Community gathering places serve as important local hubs where older adults can engage in health-promoting activities such as physical exercise, recreational hobbies, and other forms of social participation ( ; ; ). Policymakers expect the potential of these spaces to cultivate social capital by encouraging interpersonal interaction, mutual support, and community cohesion among residents ( ; ; J. ). According to the forms and dimensions of social capital noted in “The measurement of social capital (Ester ),” community gathering places can foster cognitive and structural social capital and contribute to participants' health ( ; ; ).
Community gathering places in Japan have two notable features. First, many municipalities have developed community gathering places, and the number of people participating has increased. Community gathering place participants increased by about 1.05 million, from 1.318 million (3.9 % of the population 65 years and older) to 2.374 million (6.7 %) in, between FY2015 (in the year when it was fully implemented) and FY2019 before the covid-19 pandemic ( ). Community gathering places had been developed in 1670 of 1741 municipalities (95.9 %) by FY2019 ( ). To achieve Healthy Ageing, the Japanese government has set a target participation rate of 8.0 % among those aged 65+ years in community gathering places by 2025, and it intends to further expand the number of community gathering places ( ). Second, similar initiatives led by health professionals and other professionals ( ; ; ; ; ), the community gathering place in Japan is also unique in being led by citizen volunteers. As Japan's aging population cannot be supported by professional services alone,' the Community-based Integrated Care System' is being developed ( ). As part of this effort, the community gathering place is operated by citizen volunteers. This approach in Japan, a super-aging country, can be a reference for other countries with aging populations.
Participation in community gathering places is associated with various health-related outcomes, including physical function ( ), physical activity ( ), social participation ( ), self-rated health ( ), higher-level functional capacity ( ), frailty ( ; ), functional disability ( ; ; ), dementia ( ), care costs ( ) and medical costs ( ). An outcome-wide approach further supports the broad health and well-being benefits associated with participation in community gathering places ( ). In this study ( ), participants were more likely than non-participants to engage in hobby groups, volunteer organizations, senior citizens’ clubs, and cultural or educational groups. They also reported more frequent contact with friends and exhibited better higher-level functional capacity, highlighting the multidimensional advantages of such community involvement. One of the reasons this population approach using community gathering places has become a major strategy is that evidence has been reported that it is effective for critical outcomes, functional disability ( ; ; ; ; ). Previous studies ( , ; ) infer that social capital functions as a key intermediate mechanism of protective association with functional disability and dementia is the cultivation of social capital generated by participation in community gathering places.
Although systematic and scoping reviews have shown that social capital is associated with better health outcomes, they also highlight a lack of robust evidence on whether interventions can effectively enhance social capital among older adults ( ; ; ; ). This limitation applies not only to the context of community gathering places but also to a variety of other settings ( ; ; E. ). For instance, previous reviews have reported mixed results for group-based activities, volunteer programs, and community engagement initiatives ( ; ; E. ). These interventions often improved structural aspects of social capital such as social participation, while effects on cognitive elements like trust and reciprocity were less consistent ( ; ; E. ). Only a small number of studies have specifically examined the association between participation in community gathering places and subsequent changes in social capital ( ; ). Moreover, these studies have generally assessed only a subset of the multidimensional resources encompassed by the concept of social capital ( ; ; Ester ). In the present study, we focus on structural social capital, operationalized as the frequency and number of social interactions with friends and participation in community organizations.
There is a pressing need for longitudinal research to more rigorously assess the dynamic relationship between community participation and social capital development. For example, a previous study conducted in Japan ( ) employed a longitudinal design with only two survey waves, which limited its ability to adequately address issues of reverse causality. To mitigate such concerns and reduce bias from unmeasured confounding, it is recommended that longitudinal studies adjust for pre-exposure outcome values (i.e., social capital indicators measured prior to participation in community gathering places) ( ). However, such rigorous adjustment typically requires data collected at a minimum of three time points—one for baseline covariates, one for exposure, and one for outcome assessment—an approach that has rarely been implemented in existing studies.
Based on theoretical frameworks and prior empirical research, we hypothesized that participation in community gathering places contributes to the development of subsequent individual-level social capital. In this study, we focused specifically on individual social capital due to limitations in data availability. We evaluated social capital as an intermediate mechanism that may lead to improved health outcomes. This perspective is consistent with prior studies identifying social capital as a mediating factor between community engagement and health benefits ( ; ). To test this hypothesis, we investigated the association between participation in community gathering places and subsequent individual-level social capital. We utilized data from a three-wave longitudinal cohort study of older adults in Japan. To minimize potential bias from confounding and reverse causation, we adjusted for a comprehensive set of pre-exposure covariates, including baseline measures of the outcome variable. While direct health indicators were not included, our findings provide foundational evidence for mechanisms from intervention to health impacts.

## Methods
2.1.

## Data
We used data on older adults aged 65 and older in Fukuoka City from two sources. With a population of approximately 1.62 million, of which 25.6 % of the population is 65 years old and older, Fukuoka City has the fifth largest city population in Japan, not including the 23 wards of Tokyo, after the cities of Yokohama, Osaka, Nagoya, and Sapporo. It is the largest city and the administrative, economic, and transportation center of the Kyushu region. The first data were taken from the 2016 and 2019 surveys in Fukuoka City of the Japan Gerontological Evaluation Study (JAGES) ( ; ), a nationwide survey of older adults aged 65 years and over in Japan. The participants for this study in Fukuoka City were randomly selected from functionally independent older adults. The second data was a list of participants in “Fureai Salon” (Fukuoka City Social Welfare Council., 2018) for 2017, one of the community gathering places in Fukuoka City. The Japanese word Fureai refers to the formation of emotionally connected relationships among people of different age groups and occupations within a community. The Fureai Salon is an initiative of the Council of Social Welfare intend to create a welfare community where everyone can live in peacefully ( ). It is held once to four times a month (about 2 h each time). At Fureai Salons, each venue offers exercises, songs, recreation, tea ceremonies, handicrafts, health checks, grand golf, and seasonal events. are photos taken at the time of the Fureai Salon. We had participant lists listed at all Fureai Salons in Fukuoka City and used an electronic version of the list.

These figures clarify how the exposure and outcome data were constructed and linked across three time points. In 2016, JAGES randomly mailed self-administered questionnaires to older adults aged 65 years and over (n = 15,099; response rate: 62.9 %). We excluded those certified as requiring assistance, those who responded with an incorrect age, and incomplete responses (n = 822). In 2019, JAGES conducted a follow-up survey (n = 6,214, follow-up rate: 43.5 %). We combined the data on these 6214 individuals with those in the 2017 Fureai Salon participant list (n = 6255) to create our analysis sample. Of the 6214 respondents to the follow-up survey in 2019, 173 overlapped with the 2017 Fureai Salon all participants list. We excluded respondents whose age and gender were not consistent across survey waves, as well as those with incorrect height and weight (n = 9). Ultimately, we included 6205 individuals, 173 (2.7 %) were identified as participants in the Fureai Salons based on official attendance records from 2017.
 This study was performed following the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee. In the 2016 surveys, all participants were informed that participating was voluntary and that completing and returning the questionnaire by mail indicated that they consented. We also stated in our explanatory statement that we would analyze the responses in conjunction with administrative data and use them to inform policies for older adults. For the 2019 survey, a consent checkbox was added to the questionnaire due to revised ethical guidelines.

2.2.

## Outcome variables
We examined eight outcomes based on previous studies ( ; ; ) on social capital indicators. Eight outcomes were obtained from the survey conducted in 2019 (two years after the exposure assessment). This included cognitive social capital, structural social capital, and horizontal social capital (Ester ): social networks, social participation, community trust, norms of reciprocity, attachment, and social support.
2.2.1.

## Social networks
We used social networks as one of the structural social capitals (Ester ). Social networks were measured by the frequency of meeting with friends and acquaintances and the number of friends and acquaintances who met in a month ( ; ; ; ; ). The frequency of meeting with friends and acquaintances was measured using the following question: “How often do you see your friends?” Participants were given the following answer choices: “4 or more times a week,” 2–3 times a week,” “Once a week,” “1–3 times a month,” A few times a year,” and “Never.” The number of friends and acquaintances who met in a month was measured using the following question: “How many friends/acquaintances have you seen over the past month? Count the same person as one, no matter how many times you have seen him/her.” Participants were given the following answer choices: “None,” 1–2,” “3–5,” “6–9,” and “10 or more.” Based on previous studies ( ; ) using these indicators as outcome variables, these indicators were coded such that the larger the number, the greater the frequency of meeting with friends (0–5) and the greater the number of friends met (0–4) and those were used as continuous values.
We performed additional analyses because the choices of these indicators were categorical. Based on previous studies ( ; ; ) using these indicators as outcome variables, we used these indicators as binary outcomes in our additional analysis. The frequency of meeting with friends and acquaintance was categorized as “yes” if individuals selected any of the six choices from “4 or more times a week” to “Once a week”, and “no” if they selected “1–3 times a month,” “a few times a year” or “never” ( ; ; ). The number of friends and acquaintances was categorized as “yes” if individuals selected any of the five choices from ““10 or more”, and “no” if they selected “None” to “6–9” ( ).

2.2.2.

## Social participation
We used social participation as one of the structural social capitals (Ester ). Social participation was measured through participation in community organizations ( ; ). In this study, we used the number of community organizations in which they participate as an indicator of social participation ( ; ). We used the community organizations included in the Survey of Needs, for which the Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare provided a model ( ). We numbered the number the following seven types of community organizations: volunteer groups, sports groups or clubs, hobbies groups, senior citizen clubs, neighborhood associations, learning or cultural group, and activities to teach skills or pass on experiences to others. The participation in each community organization was measured using the following question: “How often do you attend activities for the following groups?” Participants were given the following response choices: “4 or more times a week,” 2–3 times a week,” “Once a week,” “1–3 times a month,” A few times a year,” and “Never.” We defined participation as “at least once a week” or more and used the total number of community organizations that they were participating in as a continuous value (0–7).

2.2.3.

## Community trust, norms of reciprocity, and attachment
We used community trust, norms of reciprocity, and attachment as the cognitive social capitals (Ester ). These three indicators constitute social cohesion, one of the social capital indicators developed in a previous study ( ). The community trust, norms of reciprocity, and attachment were measured using the following question: “The following questions are about the area where you live.”” Do you think people living in your area can be trusted in general?”” Do you think people living in your area try to help others in the most of situations?” “How attached are you to the area you live?” Participants were given the following answer choices: “Very,” “Moderately,” “Neutral,” “Not really,” and “Not at all.” Based on previous studies ( ) using these indicators as outcome variables, All three indicators were coded such that the larger the number, the higher the degree of community trust, norms of reciprocity, and attachment; they were used as continuous values (0–4).
We performed additional analyses because the choices of these indicators were categorical. Based on previous studies ( ; ) using these indicators, we used these indicators as binary outcomes in our additional analysis. These indicators was categorized as “yes” if individuals selected any of the five choices from “Very” to “Moderately”, and “no” if they selected “Neutral” or “Not at all” ( ; ; ).

2.2.4.

## Social support
We used social support as the cognitive social capitals (Ester ).We used the availability of support with friends/neighbors as social support ( ; ). The availability of support with friends/neighbors was measured using the following question: “Do you have someone who listens to your concerns and complaints?” “Do you listen to someone's concerns or complaints?” (For emotional support) “Do you have someone who looks after you when you are sick and confined to a bed for a few days?” “Do you look after someone when he/she is sick and confined to a bed for a few days?” (For instrumental support). The respondents were asked whether they received/provided emotional support and received/provided instrumental support to/from friends/neighbors. We defined emotional and instrumental support as available when either received or provided. Those who received/provided support to/from their friends/neighbors were assessed as yes in a binary variable.

2.3.

## Exposure variables
Our exposure variable was participation in community gathering places, taken from the 2017 Fureai Salon participant list. Those who participated at least once in the Fureai Salon in FY 2017 were defined as participants ( ). We also performed additional analyses using the number of times participated in community gathering places.

2.4.

## Covariates
We used all covariates from the 2016 survey, one year before the point was assessed exposure, 2017. Following previous studies on community gathering places ( ; ; , we included the following pre-exposure characteristics: age (constant value), gender, years of education (≤9 or ≥ 10 years), household equivalized income (continuous value), employment (never/retired or current), marital status (married or not married), living alone, activities of daily living (dependent or independent), and disease/sequelae under treatment. These characteristics were potential confounding factors that could correlate with participation in community gathering places and social capital outcomes.
To reduce the possibility of reverse causation (i.e., pre-baseline social capital leads to participation in community gathering places), we also adjusted for the initial values of each outcome ( ). For example, if the outcome was the frequency of meeting with friends and acquaintances in 2019, then the frequency of meeting with friends and acquaintances in 2016 was adjusted.

2.5.

## Statistical analysis
We expressed all continuous values as mean (standard deviations), and categorical variables as numbers (percentages). To assess potential selection bias from sample attrition, we showed the sample linked to the 2016 survey and the 2017 Fureai Salon participant list with the sample linked to the 2019 survey (n = 6,205, ), and the sample linked to the 2016 survey and the 2017 Fureai Salon participant list (n = 14,263, ).
 We applied separate regression models to examine the association between participation in community gathering places in 2017 and eight outcomes from the 2019 survey, adjusting for pre-exposure covariates obtained from 2016 surveys (confounders before each outcome value) ( ). Depending on the nature of the outcome, we used different models: (1) linear regression for continuous outcomes (the frequency of meeting with friends and acquaintances, the number of friends and acquaintances met in a month, the number of participations in community organizations, community trust, norms of reciprocity, attachment); (2) Poisson regression for non-rare binary outcomes with a prevalence ≥10 % (emotional social support and instrumental social support). Modified Poisson regression models for non-rare binary outcomes estimate cumulative incidence rate ratios ( ). Standard errors were clustered at the school district (n = 143) level to determine the potential correlation of the participants within the same district.
We used Bonferroni correction to address potential issues in multiple testing and increased risk of false positives resulting from simultaneously assessing associations between participation in community gathering places and the eight social capital indicators ( ). We divided the nominal significance level of the test (α = 0.05) by the number of tests and defined a more conservative p-value cutoff for the Bonferroni correction, at p = 0.00625 (0.05/8).
Missing values were imputed by averaging many unpruned classification or regression trees using random forest imputation, an iterative method based on random forests that provides multiple imputation schemes ( ).The missing variables used ranged from 0.2 to 12.3 %. We used R, version 4.2.1, and the missForest packages for this analysis.
Moreover, we conducted several additional analyses. First, because the selection of certain questions was not linear, the following indicators used binary variables as outcome variables to support the results: the frequency of meeting with friends and acquaintances, the number of friends and acquaintances met in a month, community trust, norms of reciprocity, and attachment. Second, to examine whether social capital fostered as participation increased in community gathering places, we conducted analyses where the exposure variable was the number of times of participation. Third, we used the augmented inverse probability weighting method ( ; ), which combines the weighted inverse probability method and a predictive model, to estimate the average treatment effect (ATE). This doubly robust method has been shown to perform well in observational studies with small and imbalanced treatment groups. Prior studies ( ; ) demonstrated that this approach can yield valid estimates even when the proportion of treated individuals is low, as in our sample with only 2.7 % participating in community gathering places. This analysis allows us to correct for bias between community gathering place participants and non-participants as much as possible. Fourth, to evaluate the robustness of the estimated associations to unmeasured confounding, we calculated E-values for each exposure–outcome association ( ). Finally, we examined antecedents for participation in community gathering places by conducting a logistic regression analysis to estimate odds ratios for the associations between pre-exposure characteristics and participation in community gathering places. All statistical analyses other than the imputation of missing values used Stata 17/IC (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

4.

## Discussion
In this longitudinal study, we conducted a two-year follow-up to test the hypothesis that participation in community gathering places, the primary strategy for Healthy Ageing in Japan, fosters subsequent social capital. There were three main findings. First, participation in community gathering places was positively associated with several social capital indicators, such as more frequent meeting friends, a greater number of friends seen within a month, the number of community organizations, norms of reciprocity, attachment and more instrumental social support. Second, there was modest evidence that participation in community gathering places was associated with greater community trust. Third, there was no strong evidence that participation in community gathering places was associated with emotional social support. As hypothesized, participation in community gathering places fostered subsequent structural social capital. Although partially at variance with the hypothesis, participation in community gathering places fostered cognitive social capital, excluding subsequent emotional support. We also found that the greater the frequency of participation in community gathering places, the more social capital was subsequently fostered. While our study did not include direct health outcomes, the results provide theoretical and empirical groundwork for future intervention to health impacts through social capital pathways.
We used three waves of data from older adults who responded to two surveys in 2016 and 2019 and a list of community gathering places participants in 2017. Loss-to-follow-up between pre-baseline and follow-up waves could have contributed to selection bias. Due to selective attrition, our analytic sample was younger, more educated, more likely to be married, and more likely to report higher incomes and better individual social capital than the original sample. The target population for the 2016 survey was randomly selected from functionally independent older adults in Fukuoka City. According to the 2016 annual report, the number of functionally independent older adults aged 65 and older in Fukuoka City was 253,072, the actual number of participants on community gathering places participant list in 2017 was 6255. The percentage of participation in community gathering places calculated from this information was 2.5 %. Although there was selective attrition, this was similar to the 2.7 % participation rate in the community gathering place in our analytic sample.
Our findings support the mechanisms described in previous studies ( , ; ) regarding the health-preserving effects of community gathering places. Previous studies have shown that participation in community gathering places maintains physical and cognitive function. Social interaction gained from participation reduces the incidence of functional disability ( ; ) and dementia ( ). A key point in designing community-based interventions is to focus on access and ensure that interventions can be integrated into the universal health network ( ). The Healthy Ageing strategy in Japan recommends that community gathering places be made available within walking distance of participants’ homes ( ). This is because the distance from home is a strong predictor of participation in a community gathering place ( ) and because community gathering places are intended to foster social capital within communities, which roughly corresponds to the daily living area ( ; J. ). Community gathering places (Fureai salon) in Fukuoka City ( ) were also developed on a school district basis, which may have promoted interaction and mutual assistance among neighborhood residents.
We found solid evidence for participation in community gathering places and the subsequent fostering of structural social capital. First, participation in community gathering places could itself be an opportunity for meeting friends and acquaintances in the participant's neighborhood. Second, participation in community gathering places lead to making new friendships and increased opportunities of joining new community organizations by being invited by other participants ( ).
However, the evidence for the thesis that participation in community gathering places leads to subsequent cognitive social capital was not solid. Community trust, norms of reciprocity, and attachment may have been easier to foster through engaging with neighbors in local venues maintained by school districts. Unlike evidence that participation in community organizations promotes social support ( ), only instrumental support was found in participation in community gathering places in this study. Emotional support was originally higher and less varied among the participants in community gathering places, and emotional support increased among the non-participants. Differences between participants and nonparticipants in community gathering places may be easier to find with respect to instrumental support, which is more advanced than emotional support.
These community gathering places in Japan are unique in that they are run primarily by citizen volunteers ( ; ). Unlike other efforts led by public health nurses and other professionals ( ; ; ; ; ), residents living in the community run the community gathering places, which may be a factor that fostered social capital. The operation of the community gathering places may become an exchange between residents, and informal connections among residents may be easily formed through participation. As Japan's population continues to age, professional services alone will be unable to meet the needs of older adults, such that the promotion of this kind of informal support will be ever more valuable.
This study focused on indicators of social capital at the individual level, but studies on social capital and their contextual effects at the local level have been accumulating ( ; ; ; ; ). In the future, policymakers may expect contextual effects on residents if community interventions through local meeting places lead to the development of social capital at the community level.
Our findings may provide useful information for combating social isolation ( ; ; ). This is a priority public health issue and policy challenge for older adults ( ). During the UN Decade of Healthy Ageing 2021–2030, social isolation is a cross-cutting theme across the four main action areas of the decade ( ). Social isolation has also been reported to negatively impact the broader health and well-being of older adults ( ). In particular, social interaction and social participation with friends are considered important for broader health and well-being ( ). Systematic reviews have shown many interventions to address social isolation in older adults focusing on social capital ( ; ; ). However, as with social capital ( ; ; ), there does not yet appear to be conclusive evidence of its effects on social isolation and other health-related outcomes ( ; ; ). In the context of social isolation, intervention in family relationships, such as spouses and children, is often difficult. However, the social capital indicators used in this study include those with a high potential for intervention, such as friends and neighbors. The fostering of structural social capital through community gathering places may help prevent social isolation and promote the health and well-being of older adults.
Community gathering places, such as Fureai Salons, are already widespread across Japan and represent a core component of its Healthy Ageing policy with 95.9 % of municipalities implementing them and 2.374 million older adults (6.7 %) participating as of FY2019 (Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, 2020). These citizen-led initiatives have the potential to serve as a vital strategy for addressing social isolation and promoting well-being in a super-aged society. Our findings support their effectiveness in fostering individual-level social capital, providing important empirical backing to expand participation in these programs. To fully harness their potential, it will be essential to ensure broader accessibility, promote participation among underserved populations, and strengthen multisectoral coordination and ongoing evaluation.
We note some limitations of this study. First, we must recognize the influence of unmeasured confounding factors. Specifically, the participation in community gathering places in 2016 is unknown. Additionally, the E-value analyses provide robust evidence for some outcomes. For example, an unmeasured confounder must be associated with both community gathering place participation and higher community trust by a risk ratio of 1.27-fold to shift the CI to include the null value. The basic attributes with the largest estimates in the attribute analysis were female (3.15), unemployed (1.98), number of friends seen within a month (1.31) and number of community organization (1.35). Second, although only 2.7 % of participants engaged in community gathering places, the small size of the exposed group may have reduced statistical power and limited our ability to conduct additional analysis, such as program-specific analyses. This also raises concerns about the generalizability of the findings. Third, the percentage of participation in community gathering places, 2.7 %, may be insufficient for a population approach. It is necessary to evaluate the program's effectiveness, including other community gathering places other than the Fureai Salon developed in Fukuoka City. Fourth, this study assessed social capital indicators at the individual level, but it is yet to be known whether indicators at the community level have improved. Considering the definition of social capital, evaluation at the community level will be needed in the future. Finally, the generalizability and transportability of the findings are limited because the sample was taken from older people in Fukuoka City. Social and cultural factors, along with socio-environmental factors, may change some of the findings of this study. Fureai Salon is a nationwide project, and there will be a need for data collection in the future to verify the effectiveness of the program on a national scale.

5.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, we found that participation in community gathering places may have a positive association with social capital among older adults. In particular, interaction with friends and neighbors, helping each other, and participation in community organizations are more valuable than family relationships because of their potential for intervention. The findings of this study provide valuable insights for promoting interventions against social isolation, which is a severe challenge for achieving Healthy Ageing. In the future, more community intervention studies will be needed, particularly those designed to foster social capital or to explore alternative mechanisms linking participation to health outcomes.
