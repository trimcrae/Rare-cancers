## ABSTRACT
The aim of this study was to explore and conceptually visualize risk perception in sufferers of food allergies (FA) and propose a suitable definition for practice, research, and policy. This concept analysis followed Rodgers’ method. Data sources included PubMed, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature, APA PsycINFO, and APA PsycARTICLES. Initial searches were conducted in 2023 and updated in 2025. The search terms were “Food hypersensitivit*,” “Food anaphyla*,” “Food allerg*,” “risk perception,” “perceived risk,” “perceived severity,” “social risk,” and “experience.” Studies were systematically selected based on predefined inclusion criteria, such as presenting at least one attribute, antecedent, or consequence of risk perception and containing descriptions of sociocultural contexts and psychosocial aspects. Only English‐language peer‐reviewed articles were included, and study quality was appraised using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT); in total, 34 articles met these criteria. Attributes of FA sufferers’ risk perception comprised four content areas, namely, situational uncertainty, probability of coping with allergies, prospects for social participation, and perceived threat and emotional responses. Antecedents included FA perceptions and self‐concept, support for FA received from others, skills and readiness for managing FA, and perceived social context of FA. Consequences included behavioral choices, allergic reactions, and perceived social limitations. This study presents a comprehensive definition of risk perception encompassing both physical and psychosocial dimensions. Understanding FA sufferers by considering the identified attributes of risk perception is essential for healthcare providers and family members when providing support.

## Practical Applications
This study provides a conceptual framework for future research in risk perception of FA sufferers, potentially leading to more individualized support and informing strategies for caregivers and healthcare professionals. Furthermore, it highlights that understanding these attributes is crucial not only for those involved in the care and support of individuals with FA, including family members, but also when it comes to improving food product design, allergen labeling, and public education about food allergens, which are essential for creating safer and more inclusive food environments.

## Quality Appraisal
The selected papers were read several times to identify and understand the words, phrases, and sentences representing the concept of risk perception in FA sufferers. Methodological quality was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) (Hong et al. ), which is designed for the appraisal of qualitative, quantitative, and mixed‐methods studies. Two authors independently evaluated the methodological quality of the included studies. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus with a third author. In accordance with MMAT guidelines, no overall score was calculated. No studies were excluded based on this assessment. The detailed results of the quality appraisal are presented in Table .

## Data Abstraction and Synthesis
At this stage, based on Rodgers’ approach, specific questions were developed to clarify the characteristics of the concept. These included surrogate terms and related concepts, attributes, antecedents, examples, and consequences (Tofthagen and Fagerstrøm ). The extracted raw data were recorded in a data extraction sheet. The sheet was structured into the following categories: surrogate terms, related concepts, antecedents, attributes, and consequences. Each category was analyzed independently. Appropriate expressions that summarized the raw data's underlying meaning were selected and assigned codes. During the coding process, particular attention was paid to preserving the original meaning of the text.
In addition to physical aspects such as onset, psychological and social aspects perceived by sufferers were also included as components of the concept during the data abstraction process. The questionnaire used for data extraction is presented in Table  .
 The codes were inductively organized based on similarities and differences. Subsequently, categories were developed through increasing levels of abstraction and were integrated into conceptual elements. To minimize potential researcher interest bias, the decision was made to deliberately refrain from moving to broad interpretive analysis until data collection was nearly complete; focus was instead on systematic extraction and organization. When questions arose or new insights emerged during the analytical process, the original text was revisited to verify consistency with the source material and to clarify interpretations. If discrepancies in interpretation occurred among researchers during coding and categorization, discussions were conducted among all authors. Consensus was achieved after confirming consistency with the original text.

## Overview of the Concept
Table  summarizes the literature with Rodgers’ framework, presenting the antecedents, consequences, related concepts, surrogate terms, and attributes of risk perception in FA sufferers.
 3.1

## Surrogate Terms
Surrogate terms are alternative ways of expressing the concept other than the word or phrase selected by the researcher (Rodgers ). In this study, the phrase risk perception was selected. The surrogate terms were identified as perceived risk (Ciaccio et al. ; Cook ; Harrington et al. ; LeBovidge et al. ; Lu et al. ; Monks et al. ; Sampson et al. ; Sansweet et al. ) and perceived severity (DunnGalvin et al. ).

3.2

## Related Concepts
Related concepts bear some relationship to the concept of interest but do not appear to share the same set of attributes (Rodgers ). The related terms identified in this study included threat (Abdelwadoud et al. ; Brown et al. ; Dean et al. ; DunnGalvin et al. ; Egmose et al. ; Fenton et al. ; Gallagher et al. ; Harrington et al. ; Herbert and Dahlquist ; Herbert et al. ; Lieberman et al. ; Lu et al. ; Marklund et al. ; Monks et al. ; Noimark et al. ; Shemesh et al. ; Stensgaard et al. ; Stjerna ; Stjerna and Brady ), health risk (Lu et al. ; Stjerna ), and social risk (Stjerna ; Stjerna and Brady ). In this study, threat was conceptualized as a potential harmful event that exists prior to being subjectively recognized as risk; that is, it is positioned as the object of cognition. Contrastingly, health risk and social risk were understood as content domains of the risk addressed in this study. Specifically, they constitute components of physical risk and psychosocial risk, respectively. Although these concepts are each related to risk perception to some extent, they do not share the same set of defining attributes that characterize risk perception. Therefore, they were distinguished and treated as related concepts.

3.3

## Attributes
Attributes comprised four content areas, which were further broken down into 10 components.
3.3.1

## Situational Uncertainty
This dimension refers to the perception of uncertainty embedded in the external context surrounding the individual.
3.3.1.1

## Presence or Absence of Allergens
This refers to situations in which FA sufferers worry about the possible existence of allergens in their food, others’ food, or their surrounding environment, such as on a table or other surfaces after someone has eaten (Cook ; Fenton et al. ; Macadam et al. ; Stjerna ). Furthermore, in certain situations, they remained vigilant about the risk of reacting to airborne allergens, which can trigger allergic reactions (Marklund et al. ; Stjerna ).

3.3.1.2

## Extent of Familiarity With Eating Places
This refers to the degree to which FA sufferers are aware of allergen‐related measures—both human and physical—at the places where they eat. Specifically, key factors included whether the environment is familiar (Macadam et al. ; Sommer et al. ), whether facilities such as schools and restaurants and the food preparers appropriately accommodated the needs of individuals with FA (Marklund et al. ; Peniamina et al. ), and the presence of reliable allergen labeling (DunnGalvin et al. ). Additionally, the availability of an environment where treatment can be received in case of an emergency was also considered (Marklund et al. ).

3.3.1.3

## Effects of Allergy Disclosure on Relationships
The studies revealed sufferers’ concerns regarding the effects of allergy disclosure on relationships; they worried that disclosing their condition could lead to feelings of isolation (Fenton et al. ) or being bullied (Monks et al. ). They also expressed concerns about being criticized by others for receiving special treatment (Stensgaard et al. ) or receiving more attention than necessary (Cook ; Dean et al. ). Additionally, they were concerned about how their approach to interacting with their parents, particularly when they perceived their parents’ involvement as overprotective, could impact their relationship (Shemesh et al. ; Stensgaard et al. ).

3.3.2

## Prospects of Social Participation
This area refers to the perceived prospects of being able to participate in social activities while receiving support from others.
3.3.2.1

## Likelihood of Seeking Help
This refers to the extent to which sufferers feel they are able to request support related to their condition when participating in activities. They considered whether those around them knew about FA (Abdelwadoud et al. ; Lu et al. ; MacKenzie et al. ) and whether they would be capable of responding in the event of anaphylaxis (Abdelwadoud et al. ; Stjerna ). Additionally, they considered the balance between dependence on others and their autonomy (Fenton et al. ; Sommer et al. ; Stjerna ), which also included the degree of dependence on their parents (Fenton et al. ; Sommer et al. ).

3.3.2.2

## Evaluating the Possibilities of Participating in Activities
This refers to sufferers considering how they can safely participate in activities, including dining out. For example, in social gatherings such as parties, they were concerned about losing control if they drank alcohol (Stjerna ) and the attention drawn by carrying an epinephrine auto‐injector (Macadam et al. ). They constantly explored ways to stay away from allergens (Fenton et al. ) and perceived airplanes as particularly dangerous owing to the inability to physically escape allergens (Stjerna ). Thus, they either chose to accept the risks and participate (Peniamina et al. ) or restrict dining out and certain activities for greater safety (Ciaccio et al. ; Dean et al. ).

3.3.3

## Probability of Coping With Allergies
This area reflects the individual's perceived capacity to cope with allergen‐related risks.
3.3.3.1

## Probability of Identifying and Coping With Allergens
This reflects sufferers’ awareness of their ability to check for the presence and quantity of allergens in food and the environment and to respond appropriately to avoid exposure. They considered the likelihood of checking allergens in food by looking at ingredient labels and restaurant menus (Marklund et al. ) or directly asking restaurant staff (Gallagher et al. ; Marklund et al. ). Being able to judge how much they could safely eat provided reassurance (MacKenzie et al. ). When they believed surfaces might be contaminated with allergens, they recognized the need to wash their hands and clean the table or other surroundings. Additionally, they were concerned about whether they could effectively carry out these measures (Fenton et al. ; Stjerna ).

3.3.3.2

## Allergic Reaction Preparation
This refers to the extent to which sufferers perceive themselves as being prepared in the event of an allergic reaction. In everyday life, FA sufferers routinely check whether they have their medications or an epinephrine auto‐injector with them when participating in meals or activities (Monks et al. ; Sampson et al. ). Sufferers who had experienced anaphylaxis recalled the feeling of losing control during an episode and anticipated the need to ask others to administer the epinephrine auto‐injector, if necessary (Macadam et al. ). They also perceived their limited understanding of their condition as a concern, particularly feeling anxious about the correct application and timing of the epinephrine auto‐injector, which further affected their willingness to participate in everyday activities (Akeson et al. ).

3.3.3.3

## Evaluating the Consequence and Alternatives of Eating
This refers to when sufferers assess whether to eat based on their understanding of allergens in food, considering the threat of an allergic reaction and the potential reactions of those around them. Sufferers checked for the presence and quantity of allergens in food, and if they judged the risk of an allergic reaction to be high, they avoided eating the item (Fenton et al. ; Peniamina et al. ). Additionally, in social settings, such as school meals, after‐school activities, or group events, sufferers considered ways to enjoy meals by bringing alternative foods or exploring options they could share with others (Cook ; MacKenzie et al. ).

3.3.4

## Perceived Threat and Emotional Responses
This area reflects emotional responses in daily life and the subjective threat evaluations of each risk factor.
3.3.4.1

## Overall Emotions
This refers to the various emotions sufferers experience while living with FA. Sufferers experienced the hassle and embarrassment of checking for allergens (Gallagher et al. ) and having an allergic reaction in front of others (Sampson et al. ). Additionally, disclosing their condition and being treated as special and not equal led to negative emotions, such as sadness, frustration, disgust, and feelings of isolation (Egmose et al. ; Sansweet et al. ). When seeking understanding responses or help from others, they sometimes felt they might be viewed as a burden (Fenton et al. ; Peniamina et al. ), and when they failed to obtain understanding or supportive responses, they felt disempowered (DunnGalvin et al. ; Fenton et al. ). Among adolescents and young adults, sufferers expressed gratitude for their parents’ concern yet sometimes felt overprotected (Stensgaard et al. ). Moreover, owing to their dependence on their parents and others for managing their illness, they experienced frustration with themselves (Herbert et al. ; Stensgaard et al. ). In contrast, when others were understanding of their allergies and showed consideration—such as bringing allergen‐free snacks—they felt joy and relief (Stensgaard et al. ; Stjerna ), which positively impacted their lives.

3.3.4.2

## Perceived Threat Level
This refers to the extent to which FA sufferers feel threatened by various physical and psychosocial risk factors. One study reported that, when sufferers felt too embarrassed to disclose their allergy, they knowingly consumed food that could trigger a reaction (Marklund et al. ). This finding indicates that the perceived level of threat associated with each risk factor substantially influences behavioral choices.

3.4

## Antecedents
The antecedents of FA sufferers’ risk perception are based on their recognition of living with FA in their cultural background. The following antecedents were identified in this study.
3.4.1

## FA Perceptions and Self‐Concept
The first antecedent of risk perception was how sufferers perceived both their illness and themselves as individuals living with the illness. Perceptions of illness were understood to have been shaped by past experiences, including fear of allergic reactions (Akeson et al. ; DunnGalvin et al. ), restrictions in daily life when dining out or traveling (DunnGalvin et al. ; Egmose et al. ), and feelings of alienation from being treated as special (Fenton et al. ; Stjerna ).
Self‐perceptions varied: Although some viewed both their illness and themselves negatively (DunnGalvin et al. ; Peniamina et al. ), others reported gaining social confidence (Herbert and Dahlquist ), adopting healthier eating habits (MacKenzie et al. ), or experiencing personal growth through allergen avoidance (Abdelwadoud et al. ).
Over time, living with FA came to be accepted as part of daily life (Dean et al. ), and eventually, this was integrated into their understanding of themselves as individuals with the condition (Herbert et al. ). This shift in how sufferers perceived themselves in relation to their illness was considered an antecedent that influenced how they perceived risks in their daily lives.

3.4.2

## Support for FA Received From Others
In childhood, parents play a crucial role in disease management. How parents perceive the illness affects the development of the child's anxiety and depression (LeBovidge et al. ). Moreover, their attitude toward managing the illness influences the child's awareness and behavior regarding their condition (Acaster et al. ; Rouf et al. ).
For example, parental efforts to help the child live a normal life despite the illness (Rouf et al. ; Stjerna and Brady ) and viewing FA as opportunities for growth (Akeson et al. ) were experiences that shaped the foundation of sufferers’ risk perception.
In addition, experiences of receiving considerate behavior from those with whom meals are shared (Egmose et al. ; Marklund et al. ) and having someone to talk to—such as others with FA (Monks et al. ) or healthcare professionals (Marklund et al. ; Peniamina et al. )—also influenced sufferers’ risk perception.

3.4.3

## Skills and Readiness for Managing FA
Possessing knowledge and judgment skills regarding FA (Hamshaw et al. ) enables sufferers to understand the severity of their condition (Macadam et al. ), choose safe foods (MacKenzie et al. ), and effectively ask staff about the presence of allergens (Marklund et al. ). Additionally, carrying an epinephrine auto‐injector at all times and having the skill to use it appropriately (Gallagher et al. ; Macadam et al. ; Monks et al. ), along with the ability to assess situations and flexibly choose to take risks when needed—such as checking labels only for new foods—contributes to reducing the psychological burden related to risk perception (Fenton et al. ; MacKenzie et al. ; Marklund et al. ; Monks et al. ).

3.4.4

## Perceived Social Context of FA
Policies such as food labeling and allergen‐free zones influence sufferers’ living environments and form the basis of risk perception (Brown et al. ; Greenhawt et al. ; Harrington et al. ). These policies also raise public awareness and promote understanding from others (Dean et al. ). Avoidant behaviors and lifestyles vary depending on national policies and cultural backgrounds (Greenhawt et al. ). Low‐income sufferers, in particular, face difficulties in accessing safe food and emergency care (Abdelwadoud et al. ; Sansweet et al. ), which lays the groundwork for how they perceive risks.

3.5

## Consequences
The consequences of FA sufferers’ risk perception include the frequency and severity of FA and the extent of perceived social limitations.
3.5.1

## Behavioral Choices
Some sufferers chose coping strategies such as avoiding dangerous foods, educating others, and removing themselves from dangerous places (Fenton et al. ). When people around them did not understand their allergies, or when being attentive to food choices felt troublesome, they chose not to attend parties (MacKenzie et al. ).

3.5.2

## Allergic Reactions
Those who ask others to be considerate or carry an epinephrine auto‐injector tend to experience fewer reactions (Greenhawt et al. ), whereas adolescents who neglect caution, such as by failing to check food labels, have experienced life‐threatening reactions (Cook ).

3.5.3

## Perceived Social Limitations
To avoid allergic reactions, some sufferers experience limitations in relationships (Dean et al. ; Stensgaard et al. ) and struggle to engage in social activities (Stensgaard et al. ). However, sufferers who accept, understand, and manage their illness feel that “it doesn't stop me from doing what I want to do” (MacKenzie et al. , 599).

3.6

## Proposed Conceptual Definition
FA sufferers’ risk perception is defined as interpreting situations in everyday life involving meals, which involves the consideration of situational uncertainty, prospects for social participation, probability of coping with allergies, and perceived threat and emotional responses. ## Discussion
This study clarified the attributes of risk perception among individuals with FA. The principal finding is the conceptualization of risk perception as a multidimensional construct comprising four attributes: (1) situational uncertainty, (2) prospects for social participation, (3) probability of coping with allergies, and (4) perceived threat and emotional responses. Notably, the concept was structured to include not only the risk of symptom onset but also perceptions related to social impact and coping capacity, thereby incorporating psychosocial dimensions.
Comparing these findings with Ferrer et al. ( ) TRIRISK model further clarifies the position of the present study. TRIRISK conceptualizes risk perception as comprising three types: experiential, deliberative, and affective (Ferrer and Klein ; Ferrer et al. ). Among the attributes identified in this study, situational uncertainty, perceived coping ability, and expectations regarding social consequences correspond to the deliberative component, whereas threat appraisal and affective responses relate to the affective component. However, a distinctive contribution of the present study lies in its inclusion of psychosocial risks, extending beyond estimations of future symptom onset.
Another important characteristic identified in this study is the transformation of risk perception through lived experience. Experiential risk perception refers to cognition formed through the integration of deliberative and affective information, resulting in rapid and intuitive responses (Ferrer and Klein ). As the ability to manage the condition increases, coping in daily life improves and, for some individuals, allergies become “a way of life” that “doesn't really bother” them (MacKenzie et al. ). Patients may at times accept certain risks (Fenton et al. ) and develop skills to adjust restrictions according to context. The accumulation of such lived experiences may contribute to the formation and modification of experiential risk perception through the integration of deliberative and affective dimensions. As Paterson ( ) noted, understanding bodily responses, developing negotiation skills, and establishing a collaborative environment for disease management help objectify the illness and prevent feelings of loss of control. In the present study, these lived experiences were conceptualized as antecedents of risk perception among individuals with FA.
In addition, the four attributes identified in this study suggest that individuals with FA interpret situations while considering multiple factors that may at times be difficult to reconcile when making behavioral choices in everyday life.
Nguyen ( ) identified attributes of patients’ risk perception of developing diabetes as perceived likelihood; personal, general, cognitive, emotional, and comparative risk; and unrealistic optimism. In contrast, regarding FA, risk perception focuses on the possibility of symptom onset, prospects of social participation and relationships, and coping ability. This suggests that sufferers need to seek understanding and support from others to prevent allergic reactions. However, while obtaining such support may contribute to ensuring safety, disclosing the condition may also lead to psychological burdens and feelings of social isolation. In Japan, diabetes affects approximately one in ten people (Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare .), whereas FAs are relatively less recognized. Although both conditions require dietary management, FA sufferers are more likely to feel psychological burdens when sharing information about their condition with others, partly due to the lower level of awareness (Fenton et al. ; Peniamina et al. ). These differences in the attributes of risk perception between the two conditions are believed to reflect differences in their social recognition.
Furthermore, the consequences of risk perception in FA sufferers include the behavioral choices, allergic reactions, and perceived social limitations. Conversely, the consequences of risk perception in patients at risk of developing diabetes involve intentions for health‐promoting behaviors, adaptation, and the prevention or delay of Type 2 diabetes mellitus. This difference reflects the nature of the diseases: FA poses acute, immediate risks such as anaphylaxis, whereas diabetes manifests as gradual, long‐term effects of dietary behavior. A distinctive feature of risk perception among FA sufferers is that avoiding certain foods to prevent allergic reactions often limits social participation, whereas the risk of immediate severe reactions further restricts choices. This requires a balance between onset risk, fear of “special” treatment, and social limits.
Health behavior theories can be enhanced and their predictive validity improved by distinguishing between deliberative, affective, and experiential risk perceptions (Ferrer et al. ). This study contributes to the refinement of health behavior theory in the FA context by conceptualizing risk perception in such a way that it includes psychosocial dimensions. However, the findings were derived from a concept analysis of the literature, and Rodgers ( ) indicated that the researcher's interests can introduce bias in selecting examples that reflect their influence. Thus, further empirical investigation is needed to examine the practical applicability of the proposed conceptual framework not only in clinical settings but also across diverse populations and cultural contexts. Beyond healthcare practice, these insights may contribute to food science and public health, particularly by improving food product design, allergen labeling, and public education about food allergens with the goal of creating safer and more inclusive food environments.

5

## Conclusion
This study clarified the concept of risk perception among individuals with FA using Rodgers’ evolutionary concept analysis method. On the basis of the findings, a framework for understanding risk perception in this population was proposed. The study highlights the importance pf healthcare providers, family members, and others involved in patient support understanding individuals with FA.
The analysis further suggested that risk perception is formed through experience‐based antecedents and that its attributes are interrelated. Moreover, the perceived degree of each attribute may be associated with outcomes such as behavioral choices, allergic reactions, and the extent of perceived social limitations. Future quantitative research is needed to measure the components of risk perception, visualize the relationships among them, and elucidate the structural model, including relevant background factors.
