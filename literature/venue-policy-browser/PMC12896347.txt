The unfair conditions experienced by PWDs across all aspects of life, including within the Primary Health Care (PHC) system, are responsible for inadequate access to primary health services, which could result in adverse health outcomes. There is a need to improve access to PHC services for Persons with Disabilities in Zambia’s communities to foster the achievement of universal health coverage by 2030. The COVID-19 pandemic has further widened these inequalities, particularly impacting PWDs. Our research aimed to examine the determinants of access to primary health care among persons with disabilities in Zambia and explore their experiences during the COVID-19 pandemic. The study used a mixed-methods concurrent approach, with a qualitative component involving focus group discussions and a quantitative component consisting of a cross-sectional study that used structured questionnaires conducted in the three districts of Zambia (Lusaka, Mazabuka, and Monze). A total of 985 participants were interviewed, and Seven Focus Group Discussions were conducted. The WHO access to health dimension was used. Qualitative data was analysed thematically while quantitative data was analysed using descriptive statistics and log-binomial model analysis. StataCorp version 17 software was used for the analysis. The study established that the persisting challenges were the unavailability of instruction in reading braille, the lack of PHC sign language interpreters, and non-subscription to the national health insurance. Results from log-binomial model on access to PHC among participants were that individuals with minor disability (aPR: 7.32, 95% CI: 4.14–12.94), moderate disability (aPRs: 1.27, 95% CI: 1.07–1.51), primary education (aPR: 1.68, 1.11–2.53) secondary education (1.67: 1.11–2.51), tertiary education (aPR: 2.28,95% CI: 1.46–3.56) were more likely to access primary health care. Mazabuka District (aPR, 0.78, 95% CI: 0.63–0.97) spinal cord impairment (aPR: 0.76, 95% CI: 0.61–0.95), vision disability (aPR, 0.38 95% CI: 0.27–0.55), cognitive disability (aPR, 0.50 95% CI: 0.32–0.79) hearing disability (aPR 0.45, 95% CI: 0.21–0.97) were less likely to access primary health care. The study revealed that access to primary health care among persons with disabilities in Zambia and experiences during the COVID-19 pandemic were significantly constrained across all five WHO access dimensions. There is a need to ensure that access to PHC among Persons with Disabilities is improved to achieve universal health coverage.

## Introduction
The global COVID-19 pandemic hindered the ongoing progress towards the Sustainable Development Goals on health for all, as well as the provision and accessibility of Primary Healthcare (PHC) [ ]. As of June 15, 2025, the number of confirmed COVID-19 cases in Africa has been 9,225,568, with 182,417 mortalities [ ]. However, there has been a decrease in the number of cases reported with a slight increase in the number of deaths as of January and February, 2025 [ ].
However, the pandemic has also provided an opportunity to reform the health systems with a focus on PHC to address the challenges in the areas of prevention, preparedness, response, and recovery during and after pandemics [ ].
Since the Alma-Ata declaration of 1978, which aimed to reduce discrimination and inequitable access to health care, countries have increasingly prioritised primary health care as a means to achieve universal health coverage and address social determinants of health [ ]. PHC challenges have persisted, leading to significant inequalities, especially in low and middle-income countries, such as poor resource allocation, infrastructure, leadership, patient and community engagement [ – ].
Efforts have been made to address the challenges of accessing PHC before the COVID-19 pandemic [ ]. However, the problem has been exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic, whilst facing global challenges such as economic recession, spiralling inflation rates, public and private debt, and cost-of-living challenges [ ]. These factors have resulted in decreased utilisation of available PHC services, directly impacting the health and quality of life of Persons with Disabilities (PWDs) [ ].
Globally, it is estimated that around 1.3 billion people, or 16% of the world’s population, experience severe disabilities [ ]. Studies conducted among PWDs have indicated that they face the highest level of health inequalities [ , ]. The health systems have inadequacies that still act as barriers to Persons with disabilities, such as lack of knowledge, negative attitudes, and discriminatory practices among healthcare workers [ ]. Other challenges that contribute to health inequalities among PWDs include lack of transportation, limited availability of services, inadequate drugs or equipment, costs, inaccessible health facilities, and health information in formats that cannot be understood [ , ]. These barriers highlight the inadequacies of the developing health system and, to some extent, individual factors among PWDs [ ].
Since Primary health care is a whole-of-society approach aimed at effectively organizing and strengthening national health systems to bring services for health and well-being closer to communities, its implementation can potentially reduce health inequalities among PWDs [ ].
Due to the widening inequalities for PWDs, it is important to understand their unmet healthcare needs to explain the existing inequalities, which are influenced by social and economic factors in different settings, such as developing countries [ , ]. Furthermore, studies that could help identify inequalities among PWDs do not include them, despite this group experiencing the greatest health inequalities [ ]. Therefore, it is recommended that PWDs should effectively participate in research studies and policy development to improve their welfare [ ].
Some studies have established determinants of access to PHC to be higher among those living in high wealth index households, availability of health insurance and lower levels of access among those divorced, due to transport, stigma, low levels of employment and barriers faced in health care systems [ , ].
Policies that are inclusive of (PWDs) are even more important in LMIC nations, especially in Africa, where health systems are overburdened and underfunded [ ]. Research that looks holistically at the reactions of African governments’ health systems to the inclusion of PWDs is still lacking despite the observed inequalities [ ].
The prevalence of PWDs was estimated to be 10.9 per cent among adults (18 + years) and was higher in urban than in rural areas, and higher among females than among males in Zambia [ ]. Many Zambians with disabilities face discrimination and a lack of opportunity, resulting in their systematic exclusion from services, legal aid, healthcare, and other essential resources [ ].
Since 2000, PHC service coverage has steadily improved in Zambia from the index score of 28 to 56 by 2024, however, there is still need to link incremental changes with key performance indicators to establish monitoring mechanisms that enhance the effectiveness of free PHC policy implementation [ ].
Although in Zambia, PWDs have access to Social Cash Transfer as a means to help them sustain their livelihood, there have been concerns that health systems are non-responsive to their needs [ , ]. Studies in similar settings, such as Africa, that have explored the prevalence and determinants of disability, the issue of healthcare access specifically for PWDs remains relatively understudied, especially in Zambia [ ]. Addressing access to PHC among this vulnerable group of PWDs will help to achieve the Universal Health Coverage (UHC) by 2030 [ ]. This is a crucial test to determine the success of Sustainable Development Goal 3 [ ]. Therefore, this study, aimed to examine the determinants of access to primary health care among persons with disabilities in Zambia and explore their experiences during the COVID-19 pandemic in the three Districts of Zambia.

## Method

## Study outcomes
The determinants of access to PHC among PWDs in three selected districts of Zambia.
The lived experiences of PWDs in accessing PHC during the COVID-19 pandemic in three selected Districts of Zambia.

## Study design, setting, and population
Part of this study follows the methodology of the study previously published by Mwiinde et al., [ ]. A cross-sectional mixed-method study design was conducted among PWDs in Lusaka, Mazabuka, and Monze Districts of Zambia between June and August 2023. The study used a convergent parallel mixed-methods study design to gather different yet complementary data on the same subject in order to enhance understanding of the determinants of access to PHC and the experiences encountered during COVID-19 among PWDs [ ]. The mixed method collected quantitative and qualitative data concurrently and analysed them simultaneously and independently, and later integrated for interpretation. The results of the analysis were combined to draw conclusions. The study targeted PWDs aged 18 years and above in the selected Districts of Zambia. The participants were identified from the approved register maintained by the District Social Welfare. Those not registered on the list were selected if they had a health record confirming their disability status from a Government Health Facility. In this study PWDs were defined according to the Persons with Disabilities Act No. 6 of 2012 [ ] which is also used in the Social Welfare Register. The act defines PWDs as individuals with a permanent physical, mental, intellectual, or sensory impairment that alone, or in combination with social or environmental barriers, hinders the ability of a person to participate in society fully or effectively on an equal basis with others. The Social welfare registers further categorise disability into three categories: Severe, Moderate, and Mild disability using the Zambia National Disability Survey, 2015 [ ].

## Inclusion and exclusion criteria
Only PWDs from the three Districts (Lusaka, Mazabuka, and Monze) and 18 years and above were eligible to participate in the study. The PWDs selected for the study were eligible only if they had a medical record of disability from the government or a government facility. Those who were already approved as PWDs registered on SCT (Social Cash Transfer) were eligible to participate. Furthermore, participants with severe disability who could not respond on their own questionnaire were represented by their caregivers or parents. PWDs with no hospital record or approved medical record and who were not on SCT were not eligible to be enrolled in the study. Those who did not give consent to participate and had stayed in Lusaka, Mazabuka and Monze Districts for less than one year were not eligible for the study.

## Sampling procedure
For quantitative data, a simple random selection process was used to select the participants for the study. This was achieved through stratification for each district Lusaka, Mazabuka and Monze to ensure that each subgroup is adequately represented in the sample, thereby reducing sampling variability and bias. The participants were randomly selected from the register obtained from the Social Welfare Department using the random number generator. Once selected for the study through random selection, those with phone numbers were called directly to identify their households for an interview or to determine the nearest venue used for community meetings among PWDs.
For participants who could not be contacted or whose households could not be identified, the next available name on the sampling list was selected. From each household, only one participant was selected in cases where more than two people were living with disabilities. Community Welfare Assistance Committees (CWACs) were fully involved at all stages in the community to help introduce the data collectors to the households and assist in identifying the households of PWDs. Participants who were not on the social welfare list but had confirmed hospital records of disability were all combined and randomly selected using the random number generator to ensure that there was no bias with the PWDs on the register in the district of study. The households of these participants were identified with the help of the CWACs in the communities. Parents or guardians guided all participants with psychological and cognitive disabilities, completing the questionnaire and signing the informed consent on their behalf.
For qualitative data, purposive sampling was used to select the participants among PWDs meeting the inclusion criteria such as physically impaired, hearing, and visually impaired. To capture diverse perspectives, the study included persons with various types of disabilities, in line with the WHO’s recognition of the need for inclusive research that reflects the heterogeneity of disability experiences [ ]. The urban and rural participants conducted FGDs independently from each other. Focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted with identified PWDs to obtain in-depth information on the challenges they face in accessing PHC and their experiences during the COVID-19 pandemic. In this study, each focus group discussion had between 8 and 12 participants and they were distributed across disability types [ , ]. We aimed to continue collecting qualitative data until saturation was reached with seven FGDs conducted (Monze three focus group discussions with a total of 26 participants, Mazabuka two focus group discussions with a total of 20 participants, and Lusaka, two focus group discussions with a total of 24 participants [ , ].
During the focus group discussions, all PWDs were included in one focus group discussion, similarly with males and females. This was to ensure that there is diversity among participants to ensure generalizability to the broader population of PWDs in Zambia.
Professional sign language interpreters, approved by the District Social Welfare Departments and affiliated with the Community Welfare Assistance Committees (CWACs), were available to assist participants with speech and hearing impairments. Participants with visual impairments were accompanied by caregivers, who then maintained a distance during the meeting to allow full, independent participation without interfering with the discussions.

## Data collection
This study used the quantitative data set as outlined by the study methodology by Mwiinde et al., (21). Quantitative data was collected through the developed structured questionnaires among PWDs (Supplementary Information File 1). Upon reviewing the literature in the Zambian context, it was found that no local validated tool was utilised to ascertain access to PHC among PWDs in Zambia. Consequently, professionals with expertise in the area were consulted and validated the developed tool. Literature review regarding the existence of data collection tools for assessing access to PHC among PWDs was thoroughly conducted. As a result, specialists in PHC took part in brainstorming sessions to assess the effectiveness of the questions developed in the structured questionnaire in reflecting the determinants that influence access to PHC for PWDs (Supplementary Information File 1).
The developed structured questionnaire was pre-tested to assess the clarity, strengths, and weaknesses of the questionnaire and to evaluate whether it would obtain the intended responses. The consistency of a questionnaire was assessed using test-retest reliability by administering it to the sampled participants (n = 30) at two different times in Chikankata and Chilanga Districts. This approach helped assess whether the questionnaire produced comparable results when completed by the same individuals under identical circumstances at different times. When the results were consistent, this indicated that the questionnaire was considered reliable for administration and use [ ]. The questionnaire contained questions on the state of Disability from the medical report or register, the social demographic factors of the participants, the available health care scheme, and challenges faced in reaching the PHC facilities. Furthermore, information was collected on the income gained from SCT and other sources of income, such as employment. Experiences at the PHC facilities and in the COVID-19 vaccination centres were included in this study. The questionnaire was used on an ODK application to collect the data. In order to guarantee that the data collected with ODK (Open Data Kit) was reliable, measures such as skip logic, range checks, daily synchronization, and backup procedures were implemented [ ].
Qualitative data collection: Focus group discussions (FGDs) with identified PWDs were conducted to obtain in-depth information on the persistent challenges they face in accessing PHC and their experiences during the COVID-19 pandemic. The process began with a review of existing literature on PHC access among PWDs globally and in Zambia, as well as tools from similar studies. Insights from this review informed the identification of broad thematic areas for discussion. Open-ended questions were designed to elicit participants’ lived experiences, challenges, and coping mechanisms during the COVID-19 pandemic. Probes were added to encourage deeper reflection on specific aspects, such as physical accessibility of facilities, costs of services, availability of assistive devices, attitudes of health care providers, and perceived quality of care. FGD data collection continued until saturation was reached [ , ]. A total of seven focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted in this study: three in Monze, two in Mazabuka, and two in Lusaka.

## Operation definition
Disability was defined in a local context as a permanent physical, mental, intellectual, or sensory impairment that alone, or in combination with social or environmental barriers, hinders the ability of a person to fully or effectively participate in society on an equal basis with others (22).
Access to Primary Health Care Services in this study, access is defined as the opportunity to reach and obtain appropriate healthcare services in situations of perceived need for care [ ].
Data saturation refers to the point at which no new data or themes are emerging from the data set, which indicates that the data have been fully explored to their potential and used widely [ ].

## Data analysis
The data was analysed using the statistical software STATA Corp version 17 [ ]. Frequencies and Percentages were computed as descriptive statistics. The chi-square test assessed the association between categorical variables, such as access to PHC. This was used to determine whether observed differences in proportions were statistically significant before inclusion in the model for analysis. The outcome variable of interest was “access to PHC ”, which means the respondent was able to access PHC services in full. Independent variables included sex, age, marital status, place of residence (rural/urban), state of disability (Minor, moderate, severe), access to health Insurance, denomination, etc. Descriptive analyses were conducted on the proportions of disability, SCT earned income from other sources, transport cost to PHC facilities and health insurance cover. The income generated from other sources among PWDs was contrasted with the national average individual income of K4,215 in the year 2023 according to the Jesuit Centre for Theological Reflection (JCTR) in Zambia [ ]. Comparing PWDs’ income with the national average provides a benchmark for identifying economic disparities that may influence their ability to afford and access primary health care services.
A log binomial model was used to assess the predictors of access to PHC among PWDS in this study. We used a log-binomial regression model to estimate prevalence ratios (PRs) for determinants of access to primary health care. This model was chosen over logistic regression because the outcome of interest was common (> 10% prevalence), making odds ratios likely to overestimate the association. The log-binomial approach allows for direct estimation of PRs, which are more interpretable in cross-sectional studies. We defined access as a dichotomous outcome. A stepwise backwards elimination approach was then applied, sequentially removing non-significant variables while retaining those with p < 0.05 in the final model. Access to PHC was dichotomised into two outcomes: a yes or no response for the respondents. Participants were asked whether they were able to access primary health care independently, and whether such care was affordable, available, accommodating to their needs, and acceptable in terms of both the attitudes of PHC workers and the nature of the services provided. “Yes” or “No”. The results were reported as prevalence ratios with their 95% Confidence Interval (CI) at a statistically significant level of 0.05. STATA version 17 Software was used for the analysis. The variables used in the model were statistically significant in both the chi-square model and the bivariate model analysis. Other variables, which were not statistically significant in the Chi-square or bivariate analyses, were included based on evidence from the literature.
The demographic variables used include sex, disability status, employment, marital status, education and family members. The variables for disability were mobility, spinal cord, vision, cognitive, psychological, head injury, hearing, and speech disability.

## Analysis of qualitative data
The audio recordings of the interviews conducted in the three districts were transcribed and entered into an Excel spreadsheet for data analysis. Furthermore, the field notes were obtained from the focus group discussions. The transcribed and field note data were compared to ensure no mismatches in the key points. Thematic analysis was used to analyze the qualitative data. The data was reviewed and coded by groupings. This process involved having at least two researchers independently code the same data to assess the consistency and accuracy of the coding process. Disagreements among coders were then discussed and resolved to refine the coding scheme and ensure a more robust analysis was conducted [ ]. The structured focus group discussion guide was analysed using the inductive approach [ ]. The code book was developed and themes were further examined to identify the most relevant, consistency and richness in the experiences of PWDs during the COVID-19 pandemic regarding access to PHC. Themes such as the persisting challenges in accessing PHC services, the challenges faced during the COVID-19 pandemic in accessing Primary Health Care Services, and the accommodation of PWDs by PHCF were considered. The major themes were used to draw conclusions.
This study adapted the World Health Organization’s (WHO) five core dimensions of access to health care availability, accessibility, affordability, acceptability, and accommodation to reflect the specific needs and barriers experienced by persons with disabilities in the Zambian context [ ]. The framework was used to guide the design of data collection tools and the organization of both qualitative and quantitative findings. Modifications included emphasising disability-related factors such as facility accessibility, support needs, and communication barriers [ ].

## Results

## Characteristics of the study participants
In this study, the proportion of those who reported mobility impairment was 42% in Lusaka, 12% in Mazabuka, and 20% in Monze. The proportion of individuals with spinal cord disability was 14% in Lusaka 3% in Mazabuka and 4% in Monze. Those who reported head injuries accounted for 7% in Lusaka, 5% in Mazabuka, and 3% in Monze. The proportion of those with vision disability was 13% in Lusaka, 6% in Mazabuka, and 6% in Monze. Hearing disability accounted for 10% in Lusaka, in both Mazabuka and Monze 3%. Speech disability accounted for 12% in Lusaka and 3% in Mazabuka and Monze. Cognitive disability accounted for 9% in Lusaka, 5% in Mazabuka and 4% in Monze. Psychological disorders accounted for 7% in Lusaka, 5% in Mazabuka, and 4% in Monze (Fig.  ). 

## Availability of primary health care services among persons with disabilities
About 99%, (n = 985) of the participants in the survey indicated the availability of the PHC services within the catchment area or ward. Around 35.93% (n = 351) indicated that they are staying within the proximate of one kilometer, 60.90% (n = 595) less than 5 km, 1.9% (n-19) less than 10 km and 1.23% (n = 12) within 15 km. When asked about the COVID-19 Vaccine availability. The quantitative study indicated that approximately 99.6% (N = 986) of the participants indicated that the COVID-19 vaccine, which was available during the COVID-19 pandemic, was available for free. However, the Participants indicated during the focus group discussion that the costs related to PHC for the purchase of special medication, which is available as a long-term treatment and rehabilitation service, have been there and persisting. However, others indicated that in the event of stockouts in the PHC facilities, they are prescribed to purchase the drugs; these incidents are not that common. “To make matters worse some long-term medications for PWDs are not covered by insurance, such as NHIMA, like the drug my son is taking. Sometimes you are also told to buy certain tests for my son to be attended to, such are challenges that cannot be seen in the general population and make us with PWDs to spend more money when accessing health services” (FGD 6).

## Accessibility of PHC through outreach services among PWDs
About 31.98% (n = 315) indicated that the PHC facility are within reach in less than 30 min, 44.37% (n = 437) indicated that the facilities are reachable in less than an hour, about 19.39 (n = 193), less than 3 h 3.15% (n = 31) and less than 4 h were less than 1%. The outreach services were indicated to be one of the key areas that enhanced approachability and the ability to perceive the PHC system of PWDs. During the COVID-19 vaccination campaign around 54% (n = 362) of the PWDs were vaccinated from the outreach services or community vaccination centers. Around 35.14% (n = 237), were vaccinated at the health facilities and 10% (n = 70) were vaccinated at the hospital.
During the focus group discussions, participants stated that “the outreach program was helpful during the COVID-19 pandemic as it helped the number of PWDs to be vaccinated at the nearest vaccination centre avoiding travelling”.
Among the respondents indicated that the available way of reaching the PHCF among PWDS was by walking on foot, 53.91% (n = 531), followed by 31.68% (n = 312) who needed to use a public transport system, such as a motor vehicle. While 7.11% (n = 70) indicated that the most reliable mode of reaching the health facility was using a wheelchair. Those who depended on being lifted by a caregiver were 1.3% (n = 13). 5.89% (n = 58) were dependent on using a bicycle to transport to reach the health care facility. Only 0.10 (n = 1) indicated that they would use an oxy-cut as an available means of transport to reach the health facility (Fig.  ). 
Among the participants (N = 985), 57% (n = 561) did not need to pay for transportation costs to the health facilities, and 47% (n = 463) were from urban areas, while 10% (n = 98) were from rural areas. In the urban area, those who needed transport but could not afford the cost of k500 were 314, and in the rural areas, there were 16. Those who incurred transportation costs less than 1000 were all from the urban areas. Around 32.15% (n = 316) indicated that the transportation cost was not affordable for their livelihood, 67.85% (n = 667) indicated that the transportation system was affordable. Regarding the accessibility of the roads, 33.54% (n = 330) indicated that it is not accessible because they struggle to reach the health care facilities, while 66.46% (n = 654) indicated that the road is accessible. However, when requested to identify barriers to accessing PHC in health facilities 37% indicated that the state of the road was one of the challenges in accessing the PHC services. About 4.3% indicated challenges to accessing health care facilities due to high traffic levels in the town’s urban areas, such as Lusaka. Those in rural areas indicated that physical barriers such as streams and hills are major obstacles (1.4%). Furthermore, the inadequacy of pedestrian infrastructure is one of the barriers identified, accounting for 1.8% (Table  ). 
During the focus group discussions, the participants in rural areas indicated that “Long distance challenges and communication barriers limit the ability to access PHC because the transport system is not readily available. The focus is the public transportation business, of which the routes used are not close to the health centres, making transport to reach the health facilities difficult unless one hires, which is expensive” (FGD 1 and 2).
“The use of wheelchairs in our rural places comes with a challenge that they are difficult to push in sand, hills, streams and muddy areas in the rainy season due to poor road networks. What works well is a bicycle, and this means that we will depend on the available well-wisher or a caregiver to take you to the health facility” (FGD,2).
Those in the urban area indicated that the wheelchair is useful to help reach the health facility or get on the bus. “However, the challenges are that drivers sometimes do not regard those in wheelchairs, and at times it becomes dangerous to be alone you will always need an accompanying person to have a safe journey. However, the situation of the person with disabilities will determine the kind of transport you will need to use. There are those in critical situations where you need to hire a taxi to reach the PHCF”(FGD 3).

## Accessibility of PHC services enhanced by social cash transfer and income earned by PWDs
From the SCT, approximately 51.4% (506) of participants earned money, while 48.6% (n = 479) were not registered under the SCT. Among those who earned money from the SCT and were considered to have severe disabilities, 5% (n = 25) earned $40 (ZMW 800). Additionally, 68% (N = 341) earned $20 (ZMW 400), while those with minor disabilities comprised 27.24% (n = 137) and earned $10 (ZMW 200).
Regarding employment status and other income generated, around 92% (915) of participants did not earn extra income. Approximately 4% (n = 42) generated income less than k499, while 1.8% less than k999 (n = 18) and 0.1% earned around 1500 (n = 1) and 1% 4000 and 6000, respectively. Overall, only 0.1% of participants earned above the country’s average income of k4250, comparing the findings with those of the Jesuit Centre for Theological Research.

## Affordability: the use of the National health insurance among PWDs
The study included a total of 985 participants. Of these, 13.85% (n = 136) had insurance coverage. Within this group, approximately 13% (n = 129) resided in urban areas, while 0.7% (n = 7) lived in rural areas. Additionally, 12.5% (n = 124) were employed in informal sectors, 1% (n = 10) were unemployed, and 0.2% (n = 2) held formal employment. It is worth noting that approximately 86% of the participants did not have health insurance. During the focus group discussions, participants with health insurance expressed concerns about the lack of coverage for essential long-term medications for individuals with disabilities, both before and during the COVID-19 pandemic. This issue has had a particularly adverse impact on households where the guardians or parents are unemployed.

## Acceptability of PHC services among PWDs
When asked about the attitude of the primary health care staff towards PWDs on a likelihood scale of highly accommodative, accommodative, slightly accommodative, and not accommodative over 44.32% (n = 433) indicated that they were highly accommodative, and 43.09 (n = 421) indicated that they were accommodative. Those who indicated that they were slightly accommodative were 10.44% (n = 102), and those who indicated that they were not accommodative were 2.15% (n = 21). Regarding the skill of the health care workers and the desire for care among PWDs, 98.20% (n = 925) indicated that they were satisfied with the skills exhibited by the health care workers in the health facilities.
Furthermore, 36.44 (n = 168) indicated that they often visit PHC facilities, while 42.73 (n = 197) indicated that they often visit primary healthcare facilities. Those who did not visit the PHCF over a long period were 20.82% (n = 96).
In other health facilities, during focus group discussions, the participants indicated that “In the days of old (20 years and beyond), it was mandatory to attend to the disabled clients upon arriving at the health facilities. They provided us with cards for entry and never allowed us to queue, but this time around, there is no difference between the disabled and the able patients. We are not given special care in some health facilities” (FGD 5).

## Accommodativeness of the PHC services for PWDs
Though the respondents 87% (n = 854) indicated that they were happy with the health care services at the health facilities 12% (n = 123) indicated that they were dissatisfied with how some health care workers were not providing them the treatment they deserve.
In a focus group discussion, the participants indicated that the PWDs are treated equally with people without disabilities. This problem has been observed in some health facilities. “The other challenge is that due to long queues during the COVID-19 pandemic, seeking health care services or vaccination among those with mental disability was a problem because sometimes they become impatient to wait until they are attended to and they might not be able to accept being on the queues. Furthermore, they refuse to be vaccinated or take medication because they do not understand the need”(FGD 6).

## General accommodativeness of PHC workers to PWDs
On a likelihood scale, the participants indicated that 31.78% (n = 313) were highly satisfied with the work of the healthcare workers, and 29.44% (n = 290) were satisfied with the quality of treatment provided by the healthcare workers. 29.34% (n = 289) were neutral or did not know anything about the satisfaction with the healthcare services. 8.32% (n = 8) and 1.02% (n = 10) indicated that they were not satisfied with the services of the healthcare workers.

## Accommodation of PHC services in reading braille and sign language interpreters
It was identified that 0.8% indicated that there was no availability of reading braille in the health facilities that could guide them on where to go and what to do when they reach the health facilities.
“It is always a challenge to want to ask members of the public who might not be interested to speak to you. The experiences during the early days of the COVID-19 pandemic were not good, especially for the deaf, who could not fully comprehend what was happening, as they woke up early in the morning to find that many people were wearing masks. The fortunate part is that during the news on the Zambia National Broadcast, there is a sign language interpreter. Therefore, those who could understand the sign language could know what was happening and inform others. There have been emergencies that require an interpreter at the health facilities however, it is always a challenge”(FGD, 4).
The participants also indicated that failure by the health workers to be able to use sign language was always a barrier to whether if one goes to the health facility alone, he/she will be attended to. It was always indicated that those who needed a sign language interpreter needed to go with a caregiver to help the interpreter. “During the COVID-19 pandemic, due to the inadequacy of the healthcare workers at times, you are already in a queue waiting to be attended. When your name is called, if people around do not know that you are deaf, you are always likely to be overlooked”. (FGD 4).

## Sociodemographic characteristics and their association with PHC seeking (N = 985)
From the total sample size of the participants (N = 985), most of the respondents 37.9% (n = 373) were within the age group of 18–35 years of age and the lowest were above the age of 55 years. The study had a balanced sex distribution (50% male, 50% female). Age was associated with the ability to seek PHC (χ = 16.61, p < 0.001). The highest proportion were those who finished primary education 45% (n = 441) and the lowest were those who completed secondary education 2.44% (n = 24), education was associated with access to PHC (χ = 21.72, p < 0.001). The highest proportion 44.16% (n = 435) were the singles and the lowest were the windowed 7.9 (n = 78), Marital status was also associated with access to PHC services (χ = 17.75, p < 0.001). Those who were employed were 94.31% (n = 929), and the employment status of the participants was associated with access to PHC services (χ = 7.83, p < 0.020). The highest proportion of the participants had severe disability, 57% (n = 560), and the lowest was minor disability, with 5% (n = 45); the level of disability was associated with the ability to access to PHC services (χ = 50, p < 0.001). In terms of the state of disability, spinal cord disability 21% (n = 205) was associated with access to PHC services (χ = 7.87, p = 0.005). Head injury disability 15% (n = 146) was associated with the ability to seek PHC services (χ = 14.15, p = 0.042). Of those with vision disability, 25.1% (n = 247) of the participants were associated with access to PHC services (χ = 37.56, p < 0.001). The proportion of those with cognitive disability was 18% (n = 174) and was associated with the access to PHC services. Those with psychological disability were 15.46% (n = 152) and associated with access to PHC services (χ = 12.66, p < 0.001) (Table  ).

## Determinants of access to PHC among PWDs
The results of the binary log-binomial model provided the unadjusted prevalence ratio (aPR) and the adjusted prevalence ratio (aPR) for the log-binomial regression model of access to PHC among PWDs (Table  ). Respondents with moderate disabilities had 1.27 higher prevalence of access to PHC than those who had severe disability (aPR = 1.27; 95% CI, 1.07, 1.51), holding all factors constant. Respondents with minor disabilities had a 7.32 times higher prevalence of access to PHC than those who had a severe disability (aPR = 7.32; 95% CI, 4.14, 12.94). Respondents with primary education had 1.68 higher prevalence of access to PHC than those who had severe disability (aPR = 1.68; 95% CI, 1.11, 2.53). Respondents with secondary education had 1.67 higher prevalence of access to PHC than those who had severe disability (aPR = 1.68; 95% CI, 1.11, 2.51). Respondents with tertiary education had 2.28 higher prevalence of access to PHC services than those who had severe disability (aPR = 2.28; 95% CI, 1.46, 3.56). Respondents who were from Mazabuka had 22% less prevalence of access to PHC than those who were single (aPR: 0.78, 95% CI: 0.63–0.97). Respondents who had spinal cord disability were 24% less likely to access PHC services than those without the disability (aPR = 0.76; 95% CI, 0.61–0.95). Respondents who had vision disability were 62% less likely to access PHC services than those without vision disability (aPR = 0.38; 95% CI, 0.27–0.55). Respondents who had a cognitive disability were 50% less likely to access PHC services than those without cognitive disability (aPR = 0.50; 95% CI, 0.32–0.79). Respondents who had hearing disability were 55% less likely to access PHC services than those without hearing disability (aPR = 0.45; 95% CI, 0.21–0.97) (Table  ). 

## Discussion
The findings indicate that access to primary health care (PHC) among people with disabilities (PWD) in Zambia during the COVID-19 pandemic was significantly constrained across all five WHO access dimensions (availability, accessibility, affordability, acceptability, and appropriateness/quality) [ ]. In the study areas of Zambia, income and employment disparities place many persons with disabilities at a socioeconomic disadvantage, limiting their ability to access primary health care. These constraints increase vulnerability to unmet health needs, underscoring the importance of targeted policies, such as subsidised health services, disability-sensitive employment initiatives, and transportation support, to reduce inequities in access.
Our findings reveal that the majority of PWDs are those with severe disability, not in employment, no primary education background, and have poor or limited access to PHC. Similar to the findings of WHO [ ], in 2023 the rates of disability are increasing globally as the result of an increase in chronic diseases and the aging population in LMIC the PWDs have less access to healthcare services and therefore experience unmet healthcare needs. Addressing these inequities requires disability-inclusive health services, targeted social protection, and integration of PWDs into national health insurance to advance universal health coverage.
In this study, the majority of the PWDs are dependent on family members who are their caregivers for support to access PHC due to the high levels of severity, similar to what is indicated in the National Disability Policy [ ] that the disabled person of PWDs usually depend on the family for help and support. Furthermore, our results indicate that those with spouses can access PHC services and reach the PHC facilities, indicating the difference in needs with those who are single, divorced, and widowed needing more support to access PHC services. Similar to the findings of Hunt et al., [ ] who indicated that improving financial capital and social support has the potential to change the livelihood of PWDs. Policies should strengthen community-based and family caregiver support systems to ensure persons with severe disabilities can access PHC without relying solely on informal family assistance.
Their main dependence to earn income is through the SCT which supports them for their livelihood to those who are registered, this supports the affordability of PHC services, including critical medications needed by the PWDs. Those who are not registered have challenges in managing their livelihood and may limit accessibility and affordability. Our results are a true indication of the findings in the National Disability Policy [ ], which indicates that it is hard for persons with disabilities to get an education or skills training due to the inadequacy of financial support. In our study accessibility and affordability have also been driven by the social cash transfer programmme among PWDs. According to the Ministry of Community Development and Social Welfare [ ], individuals receiving SCT greatly improve their social lives and the education system for children attending schools. In terms of employment, 92% of the PWDs indicated that they are not in any form of employment, causing high dependence on income from the SCT systems to improve their livelihood or manage the social-economic challenges. Similar to the findings of Shahidi et al. (32), there have always been lower reports of employment levels among PWDs than those who are not disabled.
The study findings indicate that 54.6%, are on social support, compared to 2020 at 41%, in 2022, with an increase to 59%.This improvement in income among PWDs has also indirectly improved access to PHC services. Comparing these findings with those of Durán-Valverde et al., [ ], who indicated that 20% PWDs have access to social support in LMIC.
Our study findings further indicate the alarming situation of the majority 86.15% of the PWDs are not on any health care insurance in the country. Despite having access to free PHC services at the community level, there is a great necessity for this minority group to be put on health insurance to have meaningful social support in their daily needs of medication and other necessary health costs, which are usually more than in the able population as they seek health care. Similar to the findings of van Hess et al., [ ] Those with health insurance among PWDS had an increased chance of accessing or utilising healthcare compared to those without. Furthermore, this calls for unique research to understand how health insurance can affect PWDs amidst the opportunities of the free PHC services available in Zambia, similar to the recommendations of Banks et al. [ ] who indicated that there is a need for further research to understand how social health protection affects PWDs and how the programs can be designed towards the achievement of SDG 3. This will also increase the responsiveness of the PHC system in increasing access among PWDs [ ].This underscores the urgent need for policies that promote inclusive health insurance schemes to enhance financial protection and improve access to essential health services for PWDs. Without targeted interventions, the majority remain vulnerable to high out-of-pocket costs, limiting their access to necessary care.
This study has also revealed that mobility or transportation cost is one of the persistent challenges among PWDs making them have reduced access to healthcare facilities. This problem has been reported not only in LMIC but also in high-income countries such as the USA [ – ]. The findings underscore the urgent need for disability-inclusive health policies in Zambia that specifically address transportation barriers. Incorporating transport subsidies into the National Health Insurance scheme and expanding community-based health outreach could substantially reduce access inequities. Enforcing disability-friendly transport standards under the Persons with Disabilities Act and decentralizing health services would also align with Zambia’s commitments to equitable health care. Cross-sector collaboration and strengthened advocacy are vital to ensuring these policies translate into tangible improvements for PWDs’ access to primary health care.
The study established that disability severity had an impact when accessing PHC. This could be attributed to distance and long waiting queues in PHC facilities. Similar to what was reported in Canada, PWDs had challenges accessing health care services due to transport and long waiting times to see the physician [ ]. This requires that these individuals are well supported in reaching the PHC facilities in times of need. PWDs who had minor and moderate disabilities were more likely to access PHC compared to those with severe disabilities. It is essential to understand the level of severity of disability to provide the most appropriate PHC services for PWDS. Therefore, tailored support mechanisms are essential to facilitate access for those with severe disabilities, while PHC services must be adapted according to the level of disability severity to ensure equitable and effective care.
There is a need to support PWDs in education as it is also a determinant of accessing PHC services as revealed by the study that those who are educated have more access to PHC than those who are not educated, similarly in other studies have indicated that adults with higher educational attainment have better health and lifespans compared to those who are less educated [ ].
Employment was one of the determinants of access to PHC, indicating that those in employment had access to PHC and could visit the PHC facilities without any major support because they are on national health insurance cover, they can acquire any transport system available to help them reach the facilities and are able to seek PHC services anywhere.
Furthermore, our findings indicate that those in the town that has the rural population were less likely to access PHC compared to those in a cosmopolitan city such as Lusaka similar to the findings Gizaw et al., [ ] who indicated that in developing nations, rural residents lack access to nearby healthcare facilities and are less likely to receive medical attention from physicians, health officers, nurses, or midwives.
Our findings have demonstrated the high need for health workers who can provide sign language services to PWDs as a way of reducing inequalities in PHC facilities, this will reduce the chances of being miss diagnosed [ ] similar to the findings of Bown et al., [ ] who indicated that some deaf patients would not see the general practitioner without a sign language interpreter. Furthermore, Lee et al., [ ] indicate that disability should be included in the health education curriculum to help PWDs benefit from the services of the healthcare workers. In order to enhance accessibility to primary health care, it is essential to broaden the provision of education and training concerning physical disability issues to include both clinical and nonclinical health workers. This will contribute to a more client-centered PHC system [ ].
Accommodativeness has been identified as a challenge due to the persistent challenges of unavailability reading brails before and during the COVID-19 pandemic, increasing the inequalities among PWDs while exposing them to higher risks. The reading brails should be available to improve communication among the disabled with or without epidemics for the whole primary healthcare continuum if UHC is to be achieved (21).
The health information systems targeting the masses should be provided in instruction reading braille in areas accessible to PWDs furthermore, it will be very important to include community health workers among health carders to be trained in sign language to ensure that there is adequate health-related information reaching the population, avoiding infodemic and misinformation [ ] for the attainment of Sustainable Development Goal 3 (21). This will enhance the accommodation of PHC systems, which is inclusive.
With the current communication barriers among PHCW and PWDs due to missing brails and non-availability of sign language interpreters in most health facilities the PWDs are subjected to infodemic on matters such as COVID-19 vaccination which is difficult to manage for reasons that the PWDs are not provided with adequate information to help understand the situation at hand. Similar to the findings of Brady et al., [ ] there is a need for effective communication among PWDs to foster information dissemination on policy practice, implementation, and research. Implement mandatory provision of accessible communication tools such as Braille materials and sign language interpreters in all primary health care facilities. This will help address communication barriers, reduce misinformation, and promote inclusive health service delivery.
Outreach services have been instrumental in enhancing the accessibility, availability, affordability, and acceptability of COVID-19 vaccination as a primary health care intervention within study districts. These services contribute to improving the appropriateness of care by enabling PHC workers to effectively reach community members and achieve vaccination targets. Continued investment and expansion of outreach programs are essential to sustaining equitable health service delivery and maximizing public health impact [ ]. Outreach services are critical in enhancing access to primary health care by addressing barriers related to geographic distance, affordability, and acceptability, thereby improving health outcomes in underserved populations [ ].
Acceptability remains one of the key requirements to keep on orienting PHC workers to effectively manage PWDs whenever they are seeking PHC services similar to the findings of Hashemi et al., individuals with disabilities, regardless of whether they are in high-income or low- and middle-income countries, are more prone to experiencing poorer overall health acceptability compared to those without disabilities [ ]. Reducing personal attitudes among PHCW to help the PWDs effectively is one of the need areas of our findings, similar to the findings of Magnusson et al., [ ] in Serra Leone to reduce negative attitudes towards PWDs through continuous education. Continuous education and awareness programs for PHC workers are critical to ensuring equitable and effective PHC delivery to PWDs across all settings.
The overall findings further indicate that persistent inequalities and emerging health inequalities exist in PHC, such as inadequate social support at PHCF, lack of support, poor financial support, and poor health-seeking behaviour due to not being included in the social health scheme. Cautious care must be emphasised when attending to PWDs in the PHC facilities, as it was one of the best practices some years back. However, it is emerging as an inequality.
The ability to reach the required UHC with reduced major inequalities can only be achieved if the PWDs are adequately taken care of in their seeking of PHC services and ensure that indicators for disability inclusion are integrated into the monitoring and evaluation frameworks of national health systems globally [ ].
The study has demonstrated that access to PHC among PWDs stretches beyond the health care system to include the state of disability, severity of disability, transportation, communication system, income, availability of caregivers, etc. Similar to the findings of Vergunst [ ] Among PWDs concerns about access go well beyond issues with the healthcare system in its narrowest sense; issues with transportation, poverty, and mental obstacles all require consideration. There is need to ensure that the PHC systems are well funded to ensure that it brings substantial health benefits and build human capital to serve the underprivileged, like PWDs [ ].

## Study limitations
The cross-sectional study design used in this research could not determine causal relationships between independent variables and the outcome. This design depended on respondents’ recollections of their experiences accessing PHC, including the last time they used PHC services in their area, making it susceptible to recall bias. Additionally, participants’ answers may have been affected by their wish for solutions to the difficulties they encounter as PWDs. The use of FGD findings which are not generalizable to the broader population since the study captured in-depth, contextual insights of the participants. Some of the themes could be better explored with in-depth interviews rather than the FGDs which participants may have hesitated to open up.

## Conclusion
The study revealed that access to primary health care among persons with disabilities in Zambia during the COVID-19 pandemic was significantly constrained across all five WHO access dimensions. Accessibility and affordability were some of the challenges, due to the severity of disability among PWDs, non-inclusiveness of the national health insurance for PWDs, while appropriateness remained limited due to non-availability of reading braille, and challenges, such as non-prioritising of PWDs when seeking PHC services, which need to be addressed to help achieve the Universal Health Coverage 2030 agenda. Nonetheless, targeted outreach services moderately improved availability and acceptability among PWDs. Transport is one of key areas requiring attention among PWDs to ensure that Currently, the provision of reading braille and sign language interpreters in the PHC facilities as well as during health promotion programs is a key need among PWDs. Incorporating special education (sign language and reading braille) in the Primary Health Care training for healthcare and non-healthcare staff would help to bridge the gap of poor communication between healthcare workers and PWDs even in times of pandemics such as the COVID-19. There is a need to ensure that PWDs are provided an opportunity to be in the national health insurance without any stressful contribution, which can exacerbate their livelihood. We recommend that the LMIC conduct studies exploring access to health insurance amongst PWDS and its role in improving access to healthcare for PWDS in rural and urban areas. Furthermore, LMIC should engage in disability-disaggregated monitoring, and the integration of disability indicators into PHC systems. There is need to ensure that PWDs are included in the national health planning and UHC monitoring frameworks. Furthermore, needs assessments should be conducted among PWDs to ensure that those who need support (severe disability) to reach out to the PHC facilities are assisted with better methods of reaching the PHC facilities.
