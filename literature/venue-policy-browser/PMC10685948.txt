## Abstract

## Introduction
Child mortality rates remain high in sub-Saharan Africa, including Ethiopia. We are conducting a cluster randomised control trial in the Gondar zone of the Amhara region to determine the impact of pairing Orthodox priests with community health workers, known locally as the Health Development Army (HDA), on newborns’ nutritional status, early illness identification and treatment, and vaccination completeness.
Ensuring intervention efficacy with scientific rigour is essential, but there are often delays in adopting evidence into policy and programmes. Here, we present a protocol for conducting parallel implementation research alongside an efficacy study to understand intervention implementability and scalability. This will help develop a scale-up strategy for effective elements of the intervention to ensure rapid implementation at scale.

## Methods and analysis
We will conduct a stakeholder analysis of key implementation stakeholders and readiness surveys to assess their readiness to scale up the intervention. We will conduct semistructured interviews and focus group discussions with stakeholders, including HDA members, health workers, Orthodox priests, and caregivers, to determine the core intervention elements that need to be scaled, barriers and facilitators to scaling up the intervention in diverse sociocultural settings, as well as the human and technical requirements for national and regional implementation. Finally, to determine the financial resources necessary for sustaining and scaling the intervention, we will conduct activity-based costing to estimate implementation costs from the provider’s perspective.

## Ethics and dissemination
The study received approval from the University of Gondar Institutional Review Board (approval no: VP/RTT/05/1030/2022) and the University of Washington Human Subjects Division (approval no: STUDY00015369). Participants will consent to participate. Results will be disseminated through workshops with stakeholders, local community meetings, presentations at local and international conferences, and journal publications. The study will provide evidence for factors to consider in developing a scale-up strategy to integrate the intervention into routine health system practices.

Keywords: community child health, decision making, health education, health services accessibility, organisation of health services

## STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY
This implementation study will generate evidence of effective strategies for involving non-clinical local leaders, who are community influencers, in health programmes to address health-related challenges in their communities.
A trial that incorporates implementation science methods offers the opportunity to increase the efficiency and speed of scaling up interventions to improve newborn health outcomes.
The use of a multimethod approach for data collection and analysis will provide comprehensive information on the factors to consider when implementing and scaling up interventions in communities with diverse sociocultural norms, even if the trial outcomes are modest or absent.
Limitations of the study include conducting activity-based costing solely from the provider’s perspective (excluding household costs relevant to malnutrition and vaccine-preventable diseases), and potential disruptions in the study region affecting scalability perceptions.

## Introduction
In Ethiopia, one in 17 children dies before their first birthday due to common causes such as prematurity-related conditions, respiratory infections, diarrhoeal diseases, and poor nutrition. Surviving children face ongoing challenges to their development. Thirty-seven percent of children under five years are stunted, 7% are wasted and 21% are underweight. Additionally, only 59% of children are exclusively breastfed for the first six months of life. Behavioural interventions like exclusive breastfeeding, hygiene practices, proper nutrition, and early infection identification can reduce child mortality, improve development, and be delivered effectively within communities. 
Community health workers (CHWs) are essential for promoting newborn care behaviours in Ethiopia. Non-health community influencers like faith leaders can also play a vital role due to their social capital and influence. Involving them as intervention champions can improve the effectiveness of behaviour change interventions. For example, women exposed to family planning messages from religious leaders were 1.7 times more likely to use contraceptives in Nigeria. A study in Ethiopia’s Amhara region found that pairing Ethiopian Orthodox priests with local CHWs increased first antenatal care visits by 14%, fourth antenatal care visits by 26%, and facility deliveries by 10%. In the Leading Advancements in the Uptake of Newborn Community Health (LAUNCH) trial (NCT05111899), we aim to expand on this model and investigate whether pairing CHWs with faith leaders can improve the health and survival of newborns during their first six months of life. The current study is an ancillary study to LAUNCH, aimed at conducting a process evaluation of the LAUNCH study, with the primary goal of comprehending the context for scalability.
The LAUNCH Study is a community-randomised trial in the Gondar region of Ethiopia that trains and pairs Ethiopian Orthodox priests and CHWs (known locally as the Health Development Army, HDA) to provide newborn health outreach and education in their community. If the LAUNCH intervention is effective in improving targeted child health outcomes, it can be scaled across Ethiopia. However, delays in moving from efficacy research to scaled implementation are common due to intervention complexity, limited leadership engagement, resource scarcity, and poor scale-up of intervention core components. Conducting parallel implementation research alongside efficacy studies may speed the transition from efficacy research to public health impact. 
To identify the contextual determinants influencing the scalability of the LAUNCH intervention, we are conducting complementary implementation research. We aim to identify relevant health system, cultural, and political barriers as well as facilitators to scaling the intervention so that necessary adaptations can be made to accommodate varying implementation contexts in the future. Specifically, the study involves: (1) identifying critical stakeholders who should be involved in scale-up, (2) determining the organisational readiness to scale the programme in regions with diverse sociocultural norms, (3) understanding human and technical requirements necessary for the Federal Ministry of Health (FMOH) to adapt and adopt the LAUNCH intervention into routine government activities, and (4) modeling the cost of implementing the LAUNCH intervention when owned by the government. This research will increase the likelihood that trial findings can be translated into timely and meaningful programmatic decision-making.
In addition to examining the contextual determinants of scalability, we will calculate the unit cost of reaching a mother–baby dyad with optimal health outcomes (exclusive breastfeeding, no malnutrition or recovered from malnutrition, all vaccines up to date by 6 months). Finally, we will conduct sensitivity analyses and examine politically relevant scale-up scenarios that the FMOH might consider.

## Methods and analysis

## Study setting
Ethiopia is the second most populous country in Africa, with an estimated population of 120 million. In 2019, infant and neonatal mortality rates in Ethiopia were 47 and 33 deaths per 1000 live births, respectively, which are higher than the overall neonatal mortality rate of 27 deaths per 1000 live births in sub-Saharan Africa. Additionally, only 44% of children under two years receive all basic vaccinations, while 37%, 21%, and 7% of Ethiopian under-5 children were stunted, underweight, and wasted, respectively. 

## Summary of the LAUNCH trial
The LAUNCH trial is a cluster randomised trial conducted within the kebeles of Minziro Health Center, located in the Gondar zone of the Amhara region, Ethiopia. Over 80% of the population follows the Ethiopian Orthodox faith, and a previous study in the region demonstrated that pairing priests with CHWs, known as the HDA led to a 14% increase in first antenatal care visits, a 26% increase in fourth visits, and a 10% increase in facility deliveries. 
The LAUNCH trial intervention pairs Orthodox priests with HDA members and trains them to conduct health outreach for pregnant and postpartum mothers. Their activities encompass identifying pregnant women, delivering health education to families, facilitating access to child health services, and providing health education at religious and community gatherings. Control clusters receive standard-of-care facility-based prenatal and postnatal care.
Outcomes of the LAUNCH trial include rates of early initiation of breastfeeding, exclusive breastfeeding for up to 6 months, vaccination coverage at six months, and the child's nutritional status. Infants enrolled in the study undergo baseline evaluations within ten days of birth, and the primary outcomes are measured during follow-up visits between five and six months after birth.

## Study design
This implementation research is nested within LAUNCH, a cluster randomised trial in Gondar, Ethiopia. This is a multimethod study that includes stakeholder analysis using social network analysis, surveys, and activity-based costing, along with qualitative approaches, which comprise Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) and in-depth interviews. The purpose of this research is to identify key stakeholders engaged in scaling an intervention like LAUNCH, the readiness of the Ethiopian health system to scale the LAUNCH intervention, core elements of the LAUNCH intervention that should be maintained and scaled up, and the potential incremental costs of integrating the LAUNCH intervention into the Ethiopian health system. The summary of the study components is presented in .
 

## Objective 1: stakeholder analysis to identify key stakeholders who will influence or be affected by the scale-up of the LAUNCH intervention in Ethiopia
Stakeholder analysis involves identifying individuals or groups who may have an impact on, or be impacted by, a decision or action. By assessing stakeholder interests, it may be possible to proactively identify opportunities for collaboration and engagement with key decision-makers. For example, stakeholder analysis was used in Uganda to understand opinions about the success and sustainability of a maternal and newborn health initiative, revealing stakeholder concerns and strengthening long-term viability. Likewise, stakeholder analyses were conducted in five countries participating in a programme to enhance mental healthcare; findings were used to identify the most influential and supportive actors for scaling up care, and to prioritise key stakeholders to engage. The purpose of stakeholder analysis in our study is to identify individuals who will influence or be affected by the scale-up of the LAUNCH intervention.

## Sampling

## Identification of stakeholders
We will engage two FMOH representatives to identify key stakeholders involved in Maternal and Child Health (MCH). They will generate a list containing the name, organisation, type of organisation, position, and contact information of each identified stakeholder. They will also record the administrative level at which the stakeholder works, including central level (federal ministries, regional bureaus), zonal departments, district (woreda) offices, and community level (kebele, which is the lowest administration unit in Ethiopia). 

## Data collection procedures

## Stakeholder relationship assessment
The research team and FMOH representatives will develop a sociogram depicting relationships between identified stakeholders. Relationships between stakeholders will be classified and colour-coded as supervisory or formal authority relationships, financial support relationships, technical assistance partners, and relationships characterised by formal communication channels only. The directionality of relationships on the sociogram will be represented by arrows; we will use a straight line with no arrow for relationships with no clear direction of influence between them.

## Validation of the stakeholder map
The stakeholder map will be reviewed and validated during a consultative workshop with stakeholders from national and regional levels. Workshop participants will be selected purposively as key informants based on their involvement in MCH activities, including representatives from the national MCH Directorate, CHW Department, Finance Department, Ministry of Women and Social Affairs, Ministry of Local Government, and Gondar Regional Health Bureau. The workshop will also include MCH-oriented global health initiatives and bilateral organisations, including personnel from the World Health Organisation, US Agency for International Development, Global Alliance for Vaccines and Immunization, Global Fund, local non-governmental organisations (NGOs), and civil society representatives such as the Inter-Religious Council of Ethiopia.
During the workshop, the team will present the preliminary stakeholders’ list and sociogram and facilitate a discussion on suggested modifications to the sociogram. A trained facilitator from the University of Gondar will lead the meeting, while two additional facilitators will take notes and update the stakeholders’ identification sheet by incorporating newly identified stakeholders and relationships during the meeting.

## Data collection tool
We will use a stakeholder identification worksheet to list all stakeholders involved in MCH in Ethiopia. This worksheet will encompass stakeholder names, affiliations, organisation types (public, NGO, private), positions, and levels (central, regional, woreda, and community).

## Stakeholder analysis outcomes
We will identify the most influential stakeholders by calculating the degree centrality of each stakeholder, defined as the number of relationships a stakeholder has in the network. To identify the stakeholders who influence the flow in the system, we will calculate betweenness centrality, defined as the number of times a stakeholder serves as a connection between two other stakeholders. 

## Data management and analysis
We will conduct a social network analysis. To maintain anonymity during the analysis, the team will assign a unique identification number to each stakeholder, and the research team will maintain a password-protected link log. The data analysis will be conducted using the Social Network Analysis package in R. We will present separate graphs for each measure, accompanied by tables displaying the degree-centrality and betweenness-centrality scores for each stakeholder in the network.

## Objective 2: to determine the readiness of the Ethiopian health system to scale up the LAUNCH intervention in other areas
Health system readiness refers to the capacity of a health system to effectively implement new policies or programmes and shared commitment to successfully carry out these changes. Readiness assessments typically involve determining if health system members understand the tasks required to implement new policies or programmes, assessing availability of required resources, and identifying favourable conditions for implementation. High levels of readiness are associated with increased acceptability and intent to adopt new policies or programmes, resulting in more successful implementation. In our study, a readiness survey will identify the Ethiopian health system’s readiness and capacity to scale up the LAUNCH intervention and identify points of lower readiness, where additional support or change management might be needed prior to implementation of a scaled up LAUNCH intervention or other community-based programmes for newborn health.

## Sampling for the readiness survey
The stakeholder list described previously will serve as the sampling frame for administering the readiness surveys. We will conduct stratified random sampling of individuals by administrative level (national, regional, zonal departments, district/woreda, and community level). Individuals will be eligible to participate in the readiness survey if they have worked in their organisation for at least 1 year. We will randomly select 10% of individuals from each administrative level to participate. For administrative levels with five or fewer participants, all the individuals will be invited to participate. In case a participant declines or is unable to participate in the survey, they will be replaced with an individual from the same administrative level.

## Readiness survey data collection
We will contact selected participants via email (if available) or telephone to invite them to participate in the study. Upon contact, the data collector will read the consent form in the language preferred by the participant (English or Amharic) by phone, and the participant will provide verbal consent to participate in the survey. Participants can then opt for survey administration by phone, in-person administration or self-administration online.
Participants opting for self-administration will receive a survey link via email or text message with study details. Before taking the survey, participants must reconfirm their consent by checking the ‘I Consent’ checkbox. For telephone or in-person administration, the data collector will schedule a private one-on-one meeting in a private venue. Each participant will be given a token of $2 in airtime to compensate for their time participating in the study.

## Readiness data collection tool
The readiness questionnaire will be adapted from a structural readiness assessment developed for use in low-income and middle-income countries. The survey includes a total of 50 questions, which vary slightly depending on the participant’s administrative level. Responses are recorded on a Likert scale of 1–5, 1 being ‘strongly disagree’ and 5 being ‘strongly agree’, with an option of responding ‘don’t know’. The questionnaire will be piloted with three stakeholders outside of the sampling frame, and changes will be made according to the stakeholders’ recommendations to improve questions’ wording and enhance interpretability. The survey will be translated into Amharic, and two separate people fluent in English and Amharic will validate the translation. The questionnaire will be administered using the Kobo Toolbox application, a cloud-based tool for data collection and management that can be used offline on any platform, such as computers, tablets, and smartphones. 

## Readiness outcomes
The structural readiness outcomes to be measured are presented in . In summary, we will capture two main readiness components: readiness for change and capacity for change. These components will be measured across seven implementation domains: (1) policy environment, (2) leadership structure, (3) financial resources, (4) material resources, (5) human resources, (6) technical capacity, and (7) community-delivery infrastructure.
 

## Data management and analysis for readiness survey
All survey data will be maintained in a password-protected OneDrive. Survey data will be cleaned and analysed using Microsoft Excel and R.
We will calculate descriptive statistics for each structural readiness component (readiness for change and capacity for change), subconstruct, and implementation domain. We will report medians and interquartile ranges, as we anticipate a small sample size at some administrative levels. Non-parametric tests will be conducted to compare differences in readiness scores between administrative levels. We will also assess concordance between domain scores using an analysis of variance models to determine variation in scores among and between stakeholders at different administrative levels.

## Objective 3: conduct qualitative research to identify implementation facilitators and barriers, delineate core LAUNCH intervention components, and understand stakeholder perspectives on scaling the LAUNCH intervention
Qualitative research can be used to understand implementation context, identify barriers and facilitators, explore the perspectives and experiences of individuals or groups, and evaluate implementation outcomes. This comprehensive understanding is essential for the successful development, implementation, and scale-up of interventions that are appropriate, effective, and sustainable in the long term. We will conduct qualitative research with multilevel stakeholders to identify barriers and facilitators to implementing the LAUNCH intervention, understand stakeholders’ perspectives on scaling up the intervention and identify the core elements of the LAUNCH intervention that should be sustained and scaled up.

## Sampling and sample size
We will select participants from each administrative level identified during stakeholder mapping (described above). We aim to select a minimum of 20 key stakeholders and engage 64 participants in eight FGDs, with each FGD accommodating a maximum of eight participants, with a mix of males and females. Details about the participants who will be included in the interviews and FGDs can be found in . If necessary, up to 10 additional key stakeholders and 40 participants for five FGDs may be included to reach data saturation. 
 We will use purposive sampling for administrative levels with few stakeholders (mainly at the national level) to select participants for an interview, considering their position and role in MCH activities. We will ensure that those identified in the stakeholder analysis as influential are included in the interviews. For administrative levels with many stakeholders (the community level), we will randomly select participants for the FGDs from logs of health workers, priests, and LAUNCH trial participants. All participants will be at least 18 years old.

## Qualitative data collection procedures
We will collect qualitative data through semistructured key informant interviews and FGDs. The data collectors will contact the selected participants via email (if available) or telephone to invite them to participate in the interview. FGD participants will be contacted via telephone or in person. Those who accept will be informed of the FGD schedule and will confirm their availability.
The data collectors will read information about the study and solicit verbal consent to participate. Both interviews and FGDs will be audio-recorded with participant consent. If the interviewee declines to be audio-recorded, the data collector will take detailed notes during the interview process. The data collector will transcribe the notes within 24 hours after the interview, with as much detail as possible. If a participant declines to be audio-recorded for the FGD, he/she will not participate in the study. Interviews and FGDs will be conducted in the language preferred by the participant. During the data collection, the data collectors will also have the flexibility to explore additional questions based on participants’ responses. Interviews will last up to one hour, and FGDs will last approximately two hours. Each participant will be given a token of $2 to compensate for their time participating in the study.

## Qualitative data collection tools
We will use semistructured interview guides, tailored to each stakeholder group. The guides contain questions about participants’ attitudes towards the LAUNCH intervention, barriers, facilitators and opportunities for scale-up and are informed by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR). The CFIR is a widely used framework that helps researchers systematically identify and explore contextual factors that may affect implementation success. It encompasses five key domains: innovation, outer setting, inner setting, individuals, and implementation process. Other questions will be developed based on literature about potential implementation barriers and facilitators and research team experiences. Question guides will be piloted to improve question formulation, add/modify probes, and determine the estimated time needed for data collection.

## Data management and analysis
After each interview or FGD, the data collectors will upload the recordings to OneDrive. The audio files will be transcribed, and any identifiable information will be removed. A study team member will validate the transcription by conducting two one-minute random audio and transcript spot checks. Transcripts will be translated into English by professional translators and validated by study team members fluent in both languages.
All qualitative data will be analysed using Dedoose. We will use thematic coding to analyse the data, with two coders based in Seattle and Ethiopia. A mix of inductive and deductive coding will be used. An initial codebook informed by the CFIR will be developed deductively. After reading 10 transcripts and exploring key topics, the codebook will be further modified deductively. Then, each coder will code one transcript with the draft codebook. The codebook will be refined, and any new codes will be proposed as necessary. The coders will use the final codebook to analyse the remaining transcripts. Consensus meetings will be held weekly to discuss coded transcripts and update the codebook as necessary. Where any discrepancies in coding cannot be resolved, a third coder (tiebreaker) will be consulted. Themes will be supported by participant quotes and through comparison of stakeholder responses across administrative levels.

## Objective 4: quantify the costs of the LAUNCH intervention and estimate the financial resources needed for scaling the intervention across different regions of Ethiopia
When planning the scale-up and sustainability of an intervention, considering implementation costs is essential as it helps understand the potential affordability of implementing the intervention at a larger scale. This information not only improves budget predictions but also enhances future calculations of cost-effectiveness. We will use activity-based costing to estimate the cost of implementing the LAUNCH intervention in a real-world setting, from the provider’s perspective, to help the FMOH in planning whether and how to incorporate the LAUNCH intervention into their national health agenda.

## Data sources and collection of cost information

## Expenses from LAUNCH intervention
We will primarily use an expenditure-based approach, drawing from study expenses (itemised receipts) and FMOH reports when possible. We will categorise itemised receipts as either research related (e.g., research staff salaries, research travel expenses, and data collector training) or programmatic costs (e.g., CHW/priest training costs, transportation for monthly meetings, educational materials, and field supervision). We will exclude all research-related costs from the final analysis. Each programmatic cost will be classified as a fixed or variable cost. Fixed costs include meeting and training venue costs, programme manager communication fees, and vehicle costs for field supervision. Examples of variable costs include meals during meetings, transport and per diem fees, educational materials, and salaries. 

## Data from readiness survey
During readiness surveys, we will also capture the average amount of time in hours (opportunity costs) spent by the HDA, health extension workers (HEWs), priests, and the programme manager implementing the intervention each week. This information will be used to calculate the equivalent costs if they were to be paid for their participation in LAUNCH activities.

## Results from the LAUNCH intervention trial
We will use LAUNCH trial data to capture the following health outcomes necessary for costing: (1) the proportion of mothers exclusively breastfeeding through 6 months, (2) the immunisation status (complete vaccination and individual vaccines) of a child at six months, and (3) the nutrition status (defined as a child who is not malnourished at six months considering height/weight and mid-upper arm circumference measurements). This information will be used to calculate the unit cost in US dollars for a mother–baby dyad to achieve all targeted health outcomes ( ).
 

## Other sources
To estimate the costs of new activities not originally part of the LAUNCH intervention but now recommended for scaling, or for costs not included in the LAUNCH expenses, such as the salaries for HDA and priests (given that they are unpaid volunteers), we will request information from the FMOH or other relevant stakeholders based on similar activities in other programmes. In cases where data are unavailable, we will use estimates from existing literature as needed. For instance, HDA potential wages will draw from the latest estimated monthly income for unskilled professionals reported by the Central Statistical Agency of Ethiopia, while the programme manager’s salary will be based on the gross salary published by the Ethiopia Civil Service Commission. For the priests, we will use average payments over the last 6 months, as reported by the Ethiopian Orthodox Church in Gondar.

## Data collection tools
We will employ a Microsoft Excel data extraction sheet to input LAUNCH expenses, capturing essential details including document numbers for future reference, cost descriptions, month and year of expenditure, unit cost descriptions, unit prices, quantities (or number of people if applicable), and total costs. This sheet will also include cost categories (research or programmatic), types (start-up or ongoing costs), and phases such as planning and implementation. For accuracy, predefined categories will be implemented, limiting data entry to dropdown options. To validate the process, we will pilot the data extraction sheet with 10 transactions and make adjustments as necessary to ensure comprehensive cost capture.

## Outcomes: unit costs
The unit costs, along with their definitions and calculations, are presented in . We will calculate the unit cost of reaching a mother–baby dyad with optimal health outcomes, including exclusive breastfeeding, no malnutrition, recovery from malnutrition and up-to-date vaccinations by 6 months. Additionally, we will calculate subunit costs, including the cost per mother–baby dyad where the mother breastfeeds exclusively and the baby is fully immunised; the cost per mother–baby dyad where the mother breastfeeds exclusively; the cost per mother–baby dyad where the child is fully immunised, and the cost per child who is not malnourished at 6 months.

## Data management and analyses
Data will be stored in Microsoft Excel templates and analysed using R.
We will calculate total costs, including total financial costs (fixed and variable costs) and opportunity costs of volunteer labour time (HDA and priests). Financial costs will be computed based on LAUNCH expenses for each activity, including planning, training, implementation, monitoring and evaluation, and salaries. To estimate wages for HDA members and priests, an assumption of 8-hour workdays and 22 workdays per month will be made. Monthly gross wages will be divided by 22 days to determine daily wages, then further divided by eight to estimate hourly wages. These will be multiplied by the time they dedicated to participating in the intervention. We will then sum all the costs to obtain the total costs of implementing the LAUNCH intervention in one kebele for a year.
As described in the outcomes section, we will calculate the unit cost in US dollars for a mother–baby dyad to achieve all targeted outcomes ( ). We will divide total costs by the number of mother–baby dyads that successfully achieved all targeted outcomes. Additionally, we will calculate the proportion of each sub-cost and also conduct a series of scenario analyses across different scale-up scenarios ( ). Additionally, to examine any uncertainty in the prices, we will conduct sensitivity analyses in which we vary main cost drivers by 10%. We will use a tornado diagram to display sensitivity analyses. 
 

## Scale-up scenarios
To inform the FMOH about possible scale-up pathways, we will use findings from the qualitative analysis to identify the main activities and subactivities of the intervention that, according to participants, should be scaled up as they are, those that should be adapted, those that should not be scaled up and new activities that should be added if the intervention is scaled up ( ). We will use these findings combined with the costing results to propose possible scale-up scenarios and costs for each scenario and its potential impact on improving immunisation coverage, reducing malnutrition, and increasing the proportion of women who exclusively breastfeed as the primary outcomes.

## Ethics and dissemination
The study has been reviewed and approved by the University of Washington Human Subjects Division (approval no: STUDY00015369) and Gondar’s Institutional Review Board (approval no: VP/RTT/05/1030/2022). All participants will provide verbal consent to participate in the study. The study will not collect any identifiable information. Data will be stored on OneDrive, accessible by the University of Washington and the University of Gondar.
We will employ various dissemination strategies. First, we will conduct a results dissemination workshop with key stakeholders in the Ethiopian health system. The workshop will validate the study’s findings and generate further reporting recommendations. We will incorporate the feedback from the dissemination workshop into a final report submitted to the FMOH. In addition, we will present findings to the local community, which includes HDA, HEWs, and Orthodox priests involved in implementing the LAUNCH intervention.

## Discussion
This implementation study will guide the development of a strategy for scaling up the LAUNCH intervention, and other models for engaging community influencers and CHWs in advancing newborn health in Ethiopia.
If the intervention is scaled, implementers will need to systematically identify key influencers who can partner with health workers, including but not exclusive to faith leaders, and provide linkages to healthcare. The specific cadre of influencers will necessarily vary from setting to setting based on sociodemographic, cultural, and other contextual factors.
The results of this study will elucidate the role of local influencers in improving community programmes and opportunities to engage them in health programmes, which is essential to sustain programmes. This study will also provide evidence about how to proactively engage regional or national leaders in scale-up activities by understanding their unique influence on scale-up and their readiness to do so. This may reveal potential areas where pre-emptive change management is needed to overcome foreseen barriers to effective scale-up. 
This study has several limitations. We will use activity-based costing from the provider’s perspective to understand potential affordability of the intervention for the FMOH. However, we would not evaluate household costs related to malnutrition or vaccine-preventable diseases, which limits our ability to conduct a societal cost-effectiveness analysis. Furthermore, the Amhara region is at risk of political and military disruption, which could adversely affect perceptions about the scalability of the intervention. Finally, it is possible that the LAUNCH trial demonstrates minimal or no effect on health outcomes, in which case the FMOH may not choose to scale the intervention. However, findings from this scope of work may still be useful in understanding opportunities, challenges, and best practices for scaling other MCH programmes with strong community-based elements.
In summary, this implementation research is designed to support the rapid translation of the LAUNCH trial into timely programmatic delivery to benefit newborn health at scale. Insights gathered during this project will allow for an in-depth understanding of human, material, and financial resources necessary to scale interventions that leverage community influencers to improve health in other MCH domains.
