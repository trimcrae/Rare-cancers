## Background
Despite strong evidence supporting breastfeeding’s benefits for maternal and child health, breastfeeding rates (e.g. any breastfeeding, exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months etc.) in China are still suboptimal. The pervasive marketing of breast-milk substitutes (BMS) plays a significant role. Yet research on BMS marketing—especially in China—is scarce, and a clear characterization of the channels and underlying themes through which pregnant women and new mothers encounter such advertising is critical for informing effective regulation of BMS promotions, advancing breastfeeding practices and ultimately improving maternal and child health.

## Methods
A marketing diary approach was employed in a one-week pilot study involving 20 participants in Beijing and Jinan, China. Each participant was required to document all BMS marketing-related messages encountered—capturing screenshots or photos and annotating channel and thematic content. Quantitative exposure proportions by channel were calculated, and qualitative data underwent thematic content analysis by two independent coders. This study aimed to preliminarily map the online marketing ecology of BMS and to compare these findings against existing regulatory frameworks, thereby informing actionable recommendations to strengthen monitoring of compliance of BMS marketing.

## Results
A total of 234 BMS marketing records were documented, 90.2% of which occurred via digital platforms. Among online channels, social media (45.3%), e-commerce websites (15.4%), video streaming platforms (15.0%) and general web browsing (14.5%) predominated; offline channels accounted for the remaining 9.8%. Four principal promotional strategies were identified—competitive promotions, nutritional claims, corporate identity building and word-of-mouth endorsements. Eight thematic categories emerged, led by nutrition claims, sales promotions and infant health benefits. Several participants critically appraised ambiguous or scientifically unsubstantiated assertions.

## Conclusions
Chinese pregnant women and mothers of infants aged 0–18 months encounter frequent, multifaceted BMS marketing (activities, or strategies, or tactics) predominantly through digital channels. China’s current regulatory framework governs food labelling but affords limited monitoring of BMS advertising across all channels—particularly digital platforms—underscoring the need for comprehensive regulation that explicitly includes online marketing tactics. A multisectoral strategy—combining policy reform, targeted health education and rigorous enforcement of the International Code of Marketing of Breast-milk Substitutes (the International Code)—is required to protect and promote breastfeeding.

## Introduction
Food marketing has expanded rapidly alongside industrial and socioeconomic development [ ]. Under the World Health Organization’s (WHO) International Code of Marketing of Breast-milk Substitutes and subsequent relevant World Health Assembly resolutions (the Code), BMS are defined as “any food being marketed or otherwise represented as a partial or total replacement for breast-milk, whether or not suitable for that purpose”; the scope also covers feeding bottles and teats, infant formula, follow-up formula, and toddler and growing-up milks [ ]. Unlike other foods, the promotion of BMS poses distinct public-health challenges given the essential and long-lasting benefits of breastfeeding for mothers and infants [ , ]. Globally, suboptimal breastfeeding contributes to an estimated 595,379 child deaths each year from diarrhea and pneumonia, while breastfeeding could avert approximately 98,243 maternal deaths from breast and ovarian cancers and type 2 diabetes annually [ ]. The economic loss attributable to not breastfeeding is about US$341.3 billion annually (≈ 0.70% of world gross national income) [ ]. Despite these advantages, formula manufacturers often prioritize commercial interests over public health [ , ]. At the same time, the extent to which health is protected ultimately depends on the priorities set by governments, as their policy decisions shape whether regulatory action effectively safeguards mothers, infants, and populations more broadly. Although BMS marketing is ubiquitous, systematic research characterizing its channels, strategies and thematic content remains limited [ ].
World Health Organization has put forward relevant guidelines recommending exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months of an infant’s life [ ] and has set a global target of 50% exclusive breastfeeding by 2025 [ ]. By 2030, this target is expected to increase to at least 60% [ ]. According to the Global Breastfeeding Scorecard 2023 released by the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) and WHO, global exclusive breastfeeding prevalence has increased by 10% points over the past decade, reaching 48% [ ]. However, exclusive breastfeeding prevalence in China remains low. The Report on Nutrition and Chronic Disease Status of Chinese Residents (2020) estimated that 34.1% of infants aged < 6 months were exclusively breastfed [ ].
China has the largest and fastest-growing BMS market [ ]. According to Euromonitor data, infant and toddler formula and other baby food products in China accounted for approximately 65% of the baby food retail value in Asia Pacific in 2024 [ ]. Content analysis of 26 parenting apps in China identified formula-milk banner ads on 20 apps, some of which report up to 20 million monthly active users [ ]. Although the Code prohibits advertising and other forms of BMS promotion to the general public [ , ] China’s original Rules Governing the Administration of Marketing of Breastmilk Substitutes was repealed in 2017 without replacement, creating a regulatory gap in online and offline advertising monitoring of compliance [ , ]. Despite the Advertising Law of the People’s Republic of China prohibiting any mass-media or public‐venue advertisements for infant dairy products, beverages or other foods that purport to wholly or partially replace breastmilk, the broader regulatory framework specifically addressing BMS marketing remains sparse [ ]. Public-health experts have called for bans on BMS promotion to protect infant health [ ] and for relevant regulations—especially for digital channels—to be strengthened [ ].
Marketing influences consumer attitudes and behaviour by altering implicit [ , ] and explicit memory processes [ ] shaping food preferences and consumption patterns [ ]. Qualitative diary methods, which enable real-time, individual-level documentation of exposures, are well suited to capturing the complexity of digital marketing techniques [ ]. Mothers are the primary decision-makers in infant feeding [ – ]. Although BMS marketing is widespread, systematic, participant-level evidence on real-world exposure—especially in digital environments—remains limited; recent multi-country work led by UNICEF and WHO has begun to address these gaps [ ].
This China fieldwork formed part of the UNICEF/WHO multi-country study on early infant feeding decisions; the present paper reports the China diary sub-study, which used a qualitative, screenshot-verified diary approach in two urban settings (Beijing and Jinan) to document how pregnant women and new mothers encountered BMS online. This pilot study employed a structured marketing-diary protocol to map where and how Chinese pregnant women and new mothers encounter BMS marketing online. It was hypothesized that participants are exposed to a diverse repertoire of digital promotional strategies. The objectives were to: (1) explore the channels through which BMS marketing is encountered; (2) delineate the marketing tactics employed; and (3) characterize the thematic content of BMS messages. These findings were then contextualized within the remit of current Chinese regulations to identify policy gaps and to develop actionable, evidence‐based recommendations for strengthening regulatory monitoring, promoting breastfeeding uptake, and improving maternal and child health.

## Methods and materials

## Participants
The United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) and the WHO collaborated with eight countries —Bangladesh, China, Mexico, Morocco, Nigeria, South Africa, the United Kingdom and Vietnam—to initiate a cross-national study to assess the impact of BMS marketing on infant feeding choices [ ]. Our fieldwork contributed data on China to that initiative; here we provide a detailed analysis of the diary (screenshot-verified) component. To attain a deep understanding of the BMS marketing to which mothers are continuously exposed, we conducted an analysis of a diary study involving mothers who captured screenshots or photographs of BMS marketing messages over the course of one week. Twenty participants, including 5 pregnant women and 15 mothers with babies under 18 months old, were sampled from Beijing and Jinan in China. The inclusion criteria for pregnant women were as follows: women over 18 years of age, pregnant for more than three months. We recorded at enrolment whether pregnant women intended to breastfeed, intended to formula feed or were undecided about infant feeding. Women with pregnancy complications, such as gestational diabetes, gestational hypertension, or gestational anemia were excluded from the study. The inclusion criteria for mothers was as follows: women over 18 years old with no health problems that could affect infant feeding and with children less than 18 months old. Informed consent, anonymity, and data confidentiality notices were given to all subjects. Research ethics approvals were granted by the Capital Institute of Pediatrics, Beijing, China (No. SHERLL2020002). Before the investigation, the research subjects signed an informed consent form indicating that participation in the survey was voluntary and that participants had the right to withdraw at any time. The data were anonymized and used only for research purposes.

## Procedure
Diary studies are commonly used in nutrition, health, and psychology [ ]. After a public-health expert panel finalized the study protocol, a specialized marketing consulting company carried out specific training work. During training, participants were shown example screenshots/photos and were instructed on what to record (platform/channel, date and time, message type and content, brand/product, and brief context). Over the one-week diary period, participants recorded every BMS advertisement they encountered by photographing or screen-capturing the material and appending a brief annotation. In addition, they were required to attach a short text description of the photos. Included in the text was the brand, channel, strategy, topic and anything identified as a BMS marketing message. Diaries were collected and completed by mobile phone. Pen and paper were used when necessary. Research staff issued daily reminders to minimize omissions and ensure timely, comprehensive data collection.

## Materials
This study was designed based on the standard smartphone functions—photography, screenshots and note-taking—enabling participants to record entries at any time and in any location using their preferred format. Prior to the diary period, study staff contacted each participant to confirm mastery of the recording procedures. Participants were instructed to document any exposure to BMS marketing—whether prompted by friends’ recommendations, active searches or incidental encounters. Baseline information on city of residence, maternal status and infant feeding pattern was collected before the diary began, and engagement and completion were assessed by the completion of the whole week diary. The diary recording template is provided in Appendix . All content involved was translated by an appointed translator and checked by the research team.

## Data analysis
All marketing messages captured in the diary entries were subjected to thematic content analysis [ ]. Two independent coders, both public health graduate students, applied a coding schema derived from established marketing literature [ ]. Discrepancies in code assignment were resolved through a review of the original entries and consensus discussions among the project team. Upon completion of the initial coding, the draft report was peer-reviewed by two nutrition and health-communication experts not involved in the primary analysis.
A general inductive approach [ ] guided the analytic workflow: (1) repeated close reading of all diary entries to attain deep familiarity; (2) development of a preliminary thematic framework aligned with the study’s objectives of identifying where and how mothers encounter BMS promotions; (3) systematic sorting of coded data into thematic categories; (4) synthesis of findings via tables and exemplar quotations; and (5) critical reflection and discussion of emergent patterns. To ensure rigor, qualitative content-analysis procedures [ , ]—including iterative refinement of codes and maintenance of an audit trail—were applied throughout. Finally, identified marketing strategies were compared against current Chinese laws and regulations governing BMS promotion. Study reporting conforms to established qualitative reporting standards [ , ].

## Results

## Demographic characteristics
Twenty women from Beijing (10) and Jinan (10), China, took part in the diary study. Table  presents the baseline demographic characteristics of the study participants. Participants included 5 pregnant women and 15 postnatal women, of whom 6 had children younger than 6 months of age, 7 had children between 7 and 12 months of age, and 2 had children older than one year. Each participant finished the record for one week, so the completion rate of the study was 100%.

 

## Overall exposure to BMS marketing
Every participant surveyed reported seeing BMS marketing in the consecutive week. In addition, all participants came across marketing messages about BMS at least once a day. The study finally obtained 234 records from 20 participants. One entry was incomplete because the message disappeared before it could be fully captured; this record was retained for descriptive counts but excluded from theme analyses that required an image and a main-theme description. The number of recorded exposures to BMS marketing varied widely among mothers (from 7 times to 33 times). Detailed exposure profiles for each participant are provided in Appendix Table .

## Exposure channels of BMS marketing
The marketing of BMS was extensive. The advertisements covered multiple channels, not only offline but also online (Table  ). The results indicate that all participants were mainly exposed to online information. While traditional offline channels were rarely mentioned, 90.17% of the exposure was online, and only 9.83% of the exposure was offline. Among all online exposure channels, social media, E-commerce, video platforms, and web browsing accounted for the most significant proportion in descending order. Advertising, seminars, elevators, and stores were mentioned in offline channels.

 

## Key strategies in BMS marketing
Across all entries, four primary promotional strategies were coded: (1) competition (comparative positioning on price, ingredients, or functions); (2) health claims; (3) brand-building/endorsements (formerly labelled “public praise”; e.g., celebrity promotion, brand heritage); and (4) word-of-mouth (personal networks—family, friends, neighbours—and online mother groups). Overall, competitive positioning accounted for 33.91% of exposures, followed by health claims (27.47%), brand-building/endorsements (24.46%), and word-of-mouth (14.16%). Table  reports the distribution by maternal status and by city.

 

## Key themes in BMS marketing
Theme coding identified eight recurrent themes across BMS marketing exposures (Appendix Table ). Participants were exposed to BMS-related information covering the following themes: sales promotions; nutrition claims; purported infant-health benefits; suitability for the Chinese population; quality-control measures; breastfeeding-related information; appeals to maternal love; and messaging around transitioning between formulas. Marketing messages frequently conveyed more than one theme within a single exposure. Nutrition claims—especially statements about the ingredients and stated effects of milk powder—were the most frequently mentioned. Promotions, including free samples and price discounts, were also often observed. Claims about health benefits for babies were common and included good digestion, improved immunity, growth and development, “imagination and development” (advertising phrasing suggesting cognitive/creative development), “comprehensive” development, and “no inflammation.” Some participants reported receiving product recommendations—some explicitly based on product features, and others without a clear rationale.

## Additional observations: mothers’ critical appraisal of marketing and one-click purchasing.
Beyond the main analyses above, diary entries also revealed two recurring observations: (i) mothers’ critical appraisal of scientific-sounding marketing claims, and (ii) one-click transitions from advertisements to purchase pages on digital platforms.

## Perceived misleading or incomplete “scientific” claims
Some BMS advertisements used scientific-sounding language to highlight “innovative nutrition technology” or to assert that “research shows…” without specifying study methods or results. Such phrasing was perceived by several participants as incomplete or potentially misleading. Perceptions of algorithmic targeting were also common; many mothers noted that “big-data” recommendations appeared to shape what they saw, and they did not fully trust all advertising information. Some participants reported receiving product recommendations—some explicitly based on product features, and others without a clear rationale.

## From seeing to buying: one-click transitions
Consistent with prior results indicating the dominance of online channels, diary entries described shortened pathways from exposure to purchase. In multiple instances, an advertisement image or post linked directly to an in-app product page, offering one-click ordering. This contrasted with earlier patterns that required separate steps (seeing an ad → locating a store or website → placing an order). Current Chinese measures focus primarily on labelling and specific settings; explicit provisions governing e-commerce purchase flows and embedded links are limited.

## Discussion
Diary-based methods have rarely been applied to BMS marketing. By capturing exposures in real time, marketing diaries provide quantitative counts together with contextual qualitative detail, offering better timeliness and completeness than recall-based approaches. Using this approach, the present study generated evidence on maternal exposure to BMS marketing in two Chinese cities.
This study described where, what, and how participants encountered BMS marketing. By using data from two cities, we were able to show that exposure to BMS marketing persists despite the Code’s prohibition on promotion to the general public. This finding is consistent with previous research [ , , ], yet provides more nuanced and detailed evidence.
Specifically, exposure to BMS marketing was multifaceted, arising from both online and offline channels and encompassing a diverse array of promotional tactics and thematic messages [ , ]. Marketing appeared to be deeply embedded in women’s daily digital routines, underscoring the need for continued attention to BMS promotion in contemporary media ecosystems. Prior studies have also highlighted the rapid expansion of online BMS marketing as a social phenomenon [ ]. In the present study, online channels were predominant across cities, pregnancy status, and feeding pattern—likely reflecting both technological change and the centrality of smartphones in daily life. With respect to the regulatory environment, China’s Advertising Law prohibits mass-media or public-venue advertisements that claim infant formula wholly or partially replaces breastmilk, restricts paid endorsements in specified categories, and—together with product-registration rules—has strengthened labelling requirements. However, many digital promotional practices fall outside label-specific provisions, and broad monitoring of compliance with the Code—particularly online—remains limited in scope within the measures reviewed [ ]. Channel-inclusive approaches that address webpage layouts, embedded e-commerce links, live-streaming/push formats, and other direct-to-consumer interfaces may therefore be warranted to complement labelling-focused rules.
Findings also indicated frequent use of celebrity/brand-building appeals and peer-to-peer diffusion (i.e., friends, family, neighbours, and mothers’ groups). While current measures emphasize restrictions within healthcare settings, community and social-media channels are less directly addressed. China’s Breastfeeding Promotion Action Plan (2021–2025) reinforces the importance of health education for women and families across the perinatal period [ ]. On the other hand, the guidelines provided by the Food and Drug Administration, the State Health and Family Planning Commission, and the State Administration for Industry and Commerce, strictly prohibit any promotional activities related to BMS in medical institutions [ ]. The present diary evidence suggests that non-clinical channels—for example, family networks and mothers’ clubs—played a notable role and merit consideration in future breastfeeding-support and communication strategies. The channels observed in this study were not assessed for legal compliance; rather, they illustrate how marketing can circulate in spaces not specifically targeted by existing provisions.
Moreover, the findings indicate that BMS marketing frequently emphasizes price promotions, nutrition/ingredient claims, purported infant-health benefits (e.g., easy digestion/absorption, immune support, growth and development, cognitive/“comprehensive” development, and reduced inflammation), suitability for the Chinese population, quality-control/technology credentials, breastfeeding-related information, appeals to maternal love, and messaging around formula switching. Consistent with prior research [ ]our study found that BMS advertising commonly uses emotive appeals to mothers, such as invoking maternal love. The 2023 Lancet Breastfeeding Series also highlighted how the commercial milk formula industry frequently exploits parental concerns about unsettled infant behaviours and perceived milk insufficiency through unfounded product claims and advertising messages, thereby influencing feeding decisions [ , , ]. The present study adds more detailed, participant-level evidence from Beijing and Jinan, China, regarding the substantive content of such marketing. To effectively protect the health of infants and young children and further regulate the promotions and sales behaviour of BMS, the State Food and Drug Administration, the National Health and Family Planning Commission, and the State Administration for Industry and Commerce issued the “Notice of the State Food and Drug Administration, National Health and Family Planning Commission, and State Administration for Industry and Commerce on Further Regulating the Propaganda and Sales of Breast Milk Substitutes” in 201343. This document stipulates that infant formula labels should prominently display messages on the superiority of breastfeeding, must not include pictures of infants, and must avoid terms such as “human milk-like” or “breast milk-like.” In this study, we found that while such content is restricted from appearing on labels, companies often promote it in advertising outside the label, which may fall outside label-focused provisions. The now-abolished “Administrative Measures for the Sale of Breast Milk Substitutes” stipulated that the sale of BMS cannot use marketing methods such as giving away products or reducing prices [ , ]. At present, these methods are still the main marketing methods for BMS. The 2023 update to the “Measures for the Administration of Formula Registration of Infant Formula Milk Powder Products” by the State Administration for Market Regulation places new restrictions on labels and instructions [ ]. It prohibits claims related to disease prevention, immunity enhancement, and vague terms like ‘imported milk source’. This comprehensive regulation focuses on labels and instructions but does not address other promotional content. The existence of these instruments indicates awareness of deceptive marketing tactics; however, important regulatory loopholes remain. Current provisions focus on labels and instructions and do not explicitly cover digital advertising formats, influencer- or group-based promotion, price promotions or free samples, or one-click purchase links on social media. Closing these loopholes would require extending Code-aligned rules to online environments, defining permissible and prohibited claims irrespective of medium, and assigning clear responsibilities for compliance monitoring by platforms.
Furthermore, as digital marketing has evolved, one-click purchase interfaces have become increasingly common. Although this study delineated exposure patterns by feeding status and first encounter, it did not capture conversion metrics (e.g., click‐through or purchase rates). Live‐streaming platforms and social media frequently embed direct purchase links within BMS advertisements [ ], which likely amplify consumer uptake [ ]. In offline settings, consumers rely primarily on product labels; by contrast, online decision‐making is influenced by a wider array of page‐level content. Future regulatory frameworks for BMS promotion should therefore extend beyond labelling to encompass webpage layouts, embedded e-commerce functionalities and direct‐to‐consumer interfaces.
Interestingly, some participants demonstrated the ability to critically evaluate BMS marketing messages rather than accepting them unreservedly. Programs that provide professional guidance and access to rigorously reviewed information from public, academic, and clinical sources, with transparent conflict-of-interest statements, may help women evaluate claims and make informed decisions.
Based on the BMS marketing diaries of 20 pregnant women from Beijing and Jinan, this paper attempted to evaluate the specific exposure of this population to BMS. Although the sample size was small, this study collected as complete information as possible on 20 women exposed to BMS marketing. To our knowledge, this is the first study using the diary method to explore BMS marketing to mothers. We can use this information to respond to the marketing of BMS with regulation and policy action to limit what is not scientific and ethical and promote breastfeeding in society as a whole. Compared to the Code, it appears that China has established some degree of standardization in areas such as the labelling of BMS, monitoring of compliance of healthcare institutions, and food safety norms for manufacturers and distributors. However, there might be a need for further enhancement in the promotion of BMS, distribution of samples, and particularly in the regulation of advertisements through online channels that are beyond food labelling [ ].
As an exploratory pilot study, several limitations should be noted. First, the self-report diary method may have led to under-capture of fleeting or subconscious exposures to BMS marketing. This limitation also signals a research gap with direct relevance to marketing regulation; methods capable of detecting transient and targeted content are needed to inform enforceable standards. Nevertheless, the entries are likely to reflect the most salient and memorable promotional messages, which are of primary relevance to maternal decision-making. Second, the sample was limited to 20 women in two urban centers (Beijing and Jinan), constraining the generalizability of findings to other regions and demographic groups within China. Despite these constraints, this study offers novel empirical evidence on the channels, tactics and thematic content of BMS marketing as encountered by Chinese pregnant women and new mothers. Given China’s position as the largest global market for BMS, these insights provide a valuable foundation for future large-scale, mixed-methods research and for the development of more effective regulatory and educational interventions.
Future work should integrate these marketing characteristics into strengthened BMS regulations, validate and refine the thematic framework through large-scale quantitative or mixed‐methods studies, assess the credibility and monitoring of compliance of digital BMS content, test the impact of regulatory and media‐literacy interventions, and adopt a coordinated multisectoral approach to bolster breastfeeding promotion and curb online BMS marketing [ , ].

## Conclusion
Despite policy guidelines discouraging casual promotion of BMS, marketing—particularly via digital channels and beyond traditional food-labelling regulations—remains pervasive in China. Current laws and regulations do not adequately address online or broader promotional practices, leaving significant gaps in monitoring of compliance. A concerted, multisectoral response is required: government agencies to extend regulatory frameworks to encompass all forms of BMS marketing; health professionals to strengthen mothers’ critical appraisal skills; and civil society and communities to actively support breastfeeding-friendly environments.
