## Abstract

## Abstract

## Introduction
Clinical learning is the crucial element of undergraduate nursing education, with preceptors playing a pivotal role in bridging theoretical knowledge and practical skills. Their clinical teaching behaviours (CTBs) directly influence students’ confidence, clinical competence and transition into professional practice. While global research on CTBs exists, evidence from resource-constrained settings like Pakistan remains scarce, where preceptors face unique challenges. Existing studies often focus solely on either quantitative assessment of preceptors’ competencies or qualitative narratives, failing to provide comprehensive, data-driven insights. To bridge this gap, this study employs a mixed-methods approach, integrating quantitative and qualitative data to capture the perspectives of both undergraduate nursing students and preceptors, providing a comprehensive understanding of CTBs in this context.

## Methods and analysis
The study will employ a convergent mixed-methods design, where quantitative and qualitative data are collected and analysed independently but concurrently, with integration occurring during the interpretation phase. In the quantitative phase, all undergraduate nursing students engaged in clinical practicum (n=50) and their respective preceptors (n=14) at the study hospital will be included. The Clinical Teaching Behavior Inventory, a validated tool, will be used to assess students’ perceptions of preceptors’ CTBs across six domains. In the qualitative phase, all 14 preceptors will be invited to participate in semistructured face-to-face interviews to explore their perspectives and experiences related to CTBs. The qualitative data will be analysed using Creswell’s five-step manual content analysis, while quantitative data will be analysed through descriptive and inferential statistics using SPSS V.25. Integration of findings will be achieved through the Pillar Integration Process, a systematic four-stage method, employing joint-display tables to provide a comprehensive understanding of CTBs.

## Ethics and dissemination
Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee (IRB&EC# 618-24) of Shifa Tameer-e-Millat University, Islamabad, Pakistan. Written permission for data collection will be secured from the institutional authorities. Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants. Confidentiality will be maintained by anonymisation of all identifying information and secure data storage. The results will be published in a scientific journal following the Mixed-Methods Article Reporting Standards and presented at a national conference.

## STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY
This study protocol outlines a convergent mixed-methods design that combines quantitative and qualitative approaches to provide a comprehensive understanding of preceptors’ clinical teaching behaviours in undergraduate nursing education.
The key strength of this study lies in the use of the Pillar Integration Process as the integration method, which provides a structured framework for synthesising quantitative and qualitative data into integrative themes, enhancing the depth and comprehensiveness of findings.
The study’s findings may be limited by its focus on a single institution which could affect the generalisability of the results.

## Background
 Clinical learning is a fundamental component of undergraduate nursing education, where students integrate theoretical knowledge and develop practical skills under the supervision of a clinical teacher or preceptor. The preceptorship model, adopted in nursing education worldwide, provides a structured framework where senior registered nurses with clinical expertise serve as preceptors, supervising students in clinical settings to support their professional growth. By engaging students in direct patient care, preceptorship enhances their psychomotor skills, critical thinking and decision-making, thereby preparing them for independent nursing practice. 
Preceptors play a dual role as both supervisors and mentors, with clinical teaching being a fundamental responsibility in facilitating students’ learning and professional development. Their effectiveness in this role is assessed through clinical teaching behaviours (CTBs), which encompass verbal and non-verbal actions that directly influence students’ clinical learning experiences. Effective CTBs such as promoting active learning, providing timely and constructive feedback, facilitating interprofessional communication, demonstrating professionalism and fostering a supportive environment enhance student engagement, confidence and overall clinical performance. Conversely, ineffective CTBs, such as inadequate supervision, inconsistent feedback or a lack of support, can result in negative learning experiences, diminished professional growth and potential compromises in patient safety. 
While there is extensive global literature on the preceptorship model and preceptors’ CTBs, much of the research has been conducted in high-resource settings, where structured preceptor training and institutional support mechanisms are in place. , In contrast, preceptorship in the Pakistani nursing education system presents a unique context, with preceptors facing substantial challenges, including workloads, time constraints and limited institutional support, all of which may hinder effective clinical teaching. The lack of formal training further exacerbates these issues, resulting in inconsistencies in feedback and communication barriers, which may compromise student learning and satisfaction, ultimately affecting nurses’ retention in the workforce. 
Although previous studies have explored preceptors’ roles, existing research is largely fragmented. Many studies are either quantitative, focusing on preceptors’ knowledge, competencies and student satisfaction, , or qualitative, capturing subjective experiences without providing comprehensive data-driven insights. This methodological gap limits the ability to generate a holistic understanding of CTBs, particularly in resource-constrained settings like Pakistan, where institutional support and training opportunities for preceptors are limited. To bridge this gap, this study will employ a mixed-methods approach, integrating quantitative and qualitative data to capture the perspectives of both undergraduate nursing students and preceptors. By providing a context-specific understanding of CTBs, this study aims to generate actionable insights to strengthen preceptorship, enhance students’ clinical learning experiences and support professional development of future nurses, ensuring they achieve competence to meet the evolving demands of healthcare.

## Objectives
The objectives of the study are to:
Assess the CTBs of preceptors as perceived by undergraduate nursing students using the Clinical Teaching Behavior Inventory (CTBI) (quantitative phase).
Explore preceptors’ perceptions of their own CTBs through face-to-face semistructured interviews (qualitative phase).
Synthesise quantitative and qualitative findings to identify areas of alignment and divergence, providing a comprehensive understanding of CTBs (integration phase).

## Methods and analysis

## Study design
This study will employ a convergent mixed-methods design to understand CTBs of preceptors from the perspectives of undergraduate nursing students and preceptors. Quantitative and qualitative data will be collected and analysed independently but concurrently, with integration occurring during the interpretation phase. illustrates the study design, featuring distinct quantitative and qualitative components with parallel yet separate processes of data collection and analysis, followed by an integration phase where findings from both components are merged.
 

## Setting and duration
Pakistan’s nursing education system consists of both public and private nursing institutions that offer a 4-year Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BSN) programme as the entry-level qualification for professional nursing practice. These institutions are affiliated with teaching hospitals, where students receive theoretical education in classrooms and practical training in hospital settings.
This study will take place at a private nursing institution and its affiliated teaching hospital in Islamabad, the capital city of Pakistan. The institution serves as a major hub for nursing education, drawing students from various regions across the country, while its affiliated teaching hospital provides comprehensive healthcare services to a diverse patient population. The institution follows an outcome-based education approach, integrating theoretical coursework with hands-on clinical training to develop students’ critical thinking, clinical decision-making and professional competencies. The institution offers a range of nursing education programmes at undergraduate, graduate and postgraduate levels, including a 4-year BSN, a 2-year Post-Registered Nurse (PRN) for diploma-holding nurses pursuing degree completion, a Master of Science in Nursing and a Doctor of Philosophy in Nursing. The BSN programme admits 50 students per cohort, with a total of 100 students enrolled annually. Clinical placements in the BSN programme are structured across different semesters, with early-year students primarily supervised by clinical instructors from the institution, while final-year BSN students undergo intensive clinical practicum under the preceptorship model. In this model, students are paired with senior registered nurses in a one-to-one ratio, allowing for individualised mentorship and skill development in the clinical setting.
The study will span over a duration of 4 months, from the third week of January to the third week of May 2025. This duration aligns with the institution’s semester system policy, ensuring data collection during the final semester when fourth-year BSN students complete their clinical practicum under the preceptorship model. Therefore, this timeframe will ensure data collection while students are actively engaged in their clinical practicum.

## Quantitative phase

## Population and sample
The quantitative phase will focus on two distinct population groups who are involved in the clinical teaching and learning process in the study setting: preceptors and nursing students.
The preceptors in this study are senior registered nurses with a minimum BSN or PRN qualification and at least 1 year of clinical experience. They play a critical role in supervising and mentoring students during their clinical practicum under the preceptorship model.
Nursing students in this study refer to those enrolled in the BSN programme who are currently engaged in clinical practicum under the preceptorship model. In the study setting, BSN students in their fourth professional year undergo clinical practicum during the eighth semester, where they are paired with the preceptors at hospital.
In the study setting, a total of 14 preceptors and 50 BSN students are engaged in clinical practicum. To ensure comprehensive representation, this study will target the entire population of both preceptors and students. This approach is chosen to provide an unbiased, holistic representation of the study population while minimising selection bias and strengthening the study’s validity. 

## Recruitment and data collection
The preceptors will be invited to participate in the study through formal communication, including an invitation letter via email and an informed consent form. The preceptors will be provided with detailed information about the study’s purpose, procedures and their rights. For recruitment of students, an informational session will be arranged to explain the study’s purpose, procedures and duration. The students will be informed about confidentiality measures, the voluntary nature of their participation and the right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Following the informational session, the students will be provided with a written informed consent form to confirm their participation.
For data collection, CTBI, a validated tool developed by Lee-Hsieh et al , will be employed to measure preceptors’ CTBs as perceived by the students. The CTBI consists of 23 items categorised into six domains: commitment to teaching, creating a learning environment, use of appropriate clinical teaching strategies, facilitating students for interprofessional communication, providing feedback and evaluations, and showing concern and demonstrating supportive behaviour. Responses to each item are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating more effective CTBs. The tool has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, with a high internal consistency reliability of 0.96 measured by Cronbach’s α and construct validity confirmed through factor analysis yielding a good model fit. Although the CTBI has not been previously localised for the Pakistani context, it will be pilot-tested in a similar setting to assess its reliability and ensure contextual relevance prior to full-scale data collection.
The quantitative data will be collected through the observation method. Nursing students, after confirming their participation, will attend an orientation session to understand their role as observers before the data collection begins. This session will be conducted by AJ and SR and will include detailed instructions on using the CTBI tool, clarifying each domain and providing guidance on accurately rating observed behaviours. Emphasis will be placed on the importance of completing the CTBI form immediately after each encounter to reduce recall bias. CTBI forms will be provided in a digital format (Google Form), enabling students to conveniently and efficiently complete them using their mobile devices immediately after each clinical encounter with the preceptor. This approach will not only facilitate ease of data collection but would also help maintain objectivity by preventing students from discussing their observations or sharing completed forms with peers. SR will oversee data collection from students, ensuring the regular monitoring and retrieval of submitted forms to maintain accuracy and completeness of the data.

## Data analysis
Quantitative data will be screened for inconsistencies or any missing information that, if identified, will be clarified by contacting participant students. The data will then be entered into SPSS V.25.0 for statistical analysis. Initially, the average response for each item on the CTBI will be calculated across all participants. Response averages will be categorised as follows: strongly agree (4.1–5.0), agree (3.1–4.0), neutral (2.1–3.0), disagree (1.1–2.0) and strongly disagree (0–1.0). Subsequently, the mean and SD will be calculated for each of the six CTBI domains. Then item-level and domain-level analysis will be performed to provide detailed insights into areas where preceptors excel and where improvements are needed, offering actionable evidence for enhancing CTBs.

## Qualitative phase

## Population and sample
The qualitative phase will focus on the preceptors involved in mentoring BSN students under the preceptorship model. All 14 preceptors in the study setting will be invited to participate in the data collection for the qualitative phase.

## Data collection
The qualitative data collection will involve semistructured face-to-face interviews with preceptors to explore their perspectives and experiences related to CTBs. A predefined interview guide with open-ended questions will be used to conduct interviews. The key areas covered in the interview guide are detailed in .
 After receiving a signed consent form indicating preceptors’ participation, AJ will coordinate with them to schedule a mutually convenient date and time for a one-to-one interview. The interview will be conducted in a setting chosen to ensure privacy, comfort and minimal interruptions. Based on the preceptor’s preference, the venue will either be their office or a separate room within the study hospital specifically booked for this purpose. Each interview will last approximately 30–45 min, providing sufficient time to thoroughly explore the research question while encouraging preceptors to share detailed and in-depth responses. All the interviews will be conducted by SY, a trained researcher with expertise in qualitative interviews, ensuring consistency and the appropriate use of probing to elicit meaningful and comprehensive data. The interviews will be conducted in English or Urdu, depending on the preceptor’s comfort and preference, to facilitate clear communication and ensure that participants can express themselves freely. The interviews will be audio-recorded using a digital recorder to ensure an accurate and reliable data collection. Field notes will be taken during the interviews to capture non-verbal cues that add additional context to the verbal responses, along with the interview settings or any significant observations that might enhance data interpretation. Additionally, a reflexive journal will be maintained throughout the study to record the researcher’s reflections, thoughts and emotions to identify and address potential biases, while enhancing the credibility of findings. 

## Data analysis
Qualitative data analysis will begin with the meticulous transcription of interview recordings. The transcriptions will be carefully reviewed for accuracy to ensure they faithfully reflect the participants’ responses. Special attention will be given to maintaining the integrity of the content while ensuring that identifiable information is removed to preserve the confidentiality and anonymity of the participants. For interviews conducted in Urdu, a skilled bilingual expert fluent in both Urdu and English will be engaged to provide an accurate and contextually appropriate translation, ensuring that nuances, idiomatic expressions and cultural context are appropriately conveyed during the translation process. Once the translation is completed, bilingual expert and research team members (KA, SY) will verify the English-translated transcripts by cross-checking them against the original Urdu responses to ensure linguistic accuracy, contextual fidelity and preservation of participants’ intended meanings.
Creswell’s five-step manual content analysis method will be employed to analyse qualitative data. The first step involves an immersive reading of the interview transcripts to gain a thorough understanding. Next, meaningful units of data, such as phrases or sentences reflecting preceptors’ perceptions of their CTBs, will be identified and labelled with descriptive codes. Direct quotes will be used as codes to preserve the original context and meaning. Moreover, to minimise researcher bias in the coding process, KA and SY will independently read the transcripts and identify meaningful units of data, assigning initial codes without prior consultation. Discrepancies in coding will be resolved through consensus. In the third step, similar codes will be grouped together to form broader categories representing emergent themes or concepts that reflect patterns in the data. Subsequently, these categories will undergo iterative review and refinement to accurately reflect the data. This process may involve merging, splitting or reorganising codes into subcategories to accurately represent the depth and richness of data, ensuring the credibility of the study. All steps of the data analysis process will be meticulously documented to maintain an audit trail, contributing to the study’s dependability and confirmability.

## Integration phase
In the integration phase, the Pillar Integration Process (PIP) will be employed to systematically combine the quantitative and qualitative findings in a way that aligns with the third objective of this study. The PIP is well suited to this objective as it is designed to bring together the strengths of both qualitative and quantitative data, providing a richer and more nuanced understanding of the CTBs of preceptors. This process includes four stages: listing, matching, checking and integrated theme development.
To facilitate integration, the quantitative data from the CTBI will be transformed into qualitative text, where statistical trends, mean scores and response distributions for each domain will be analysed and interpreted narratively. This process will allow meaningful comparison with qualitative findings, ensuring that numerical patterns are contextualised within preceptors’ experiences and practices for a more comprehensive understanding of their CTBs. The first stage of PIP, listing, will involve organising the key findings from both the qualitative and quantitative data sets into a comprehensive list, establishing a clear overview of the data points that reflect preceptors’ CTBs as perceived by students and preceptors themselves. In the second stage, matching, the qualitative and quantitative findings will be compared with identifying areas of alignment, where both data sets provide similar insights, as well as divergence, where the data sets offer differing perspectives. The third stage, checking, will involve verifying the consistency and accuracy of the identified matches, ensuring that the connections between the data sets are reliable and valid. The final stage, integrated theme development, will synthesise the matched and checked data into cohesive integrated themes based on the merging of the quantitative and qualitative findings. These themes will represent a comprehensive understanding of CTBs, reflecting both the perceptions of the nursing students and the preceptors. Throughout this process, joint display tables will be used to visually represent the integration of the data, helping facilitate the comparison and synthesis of qualitative and quantitative findings (see ).
 To ensure the rigour of the integration process, the Mixed-Methods Integration Quality Framework (MMIQF) will guide the study. The MMIQF comprises 44 criteria across four domains: planning, data collection and analysis; interpretation; general reporting and joint displays. These criteria address integration as both a process and an outcome, ensuring coherence between qualitative and quantitative components. Joint displays, a key element of MMIQF, will visually synthesise data from both strands, facilitating clarity and transparency.

## Ethics and dissemination
Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee (IRB&EC# 618-24) of Shifa Tameer-e-Millat University, Islamabad. Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants, including preceptors and students, prior to their involvement in the study. Participants will be provided with a comprehensive briefing on the study’s purpose, data collection procedures, voluntary nature of their participation and right to withdraw at any time without consequences. Consent for audio-recording of the interviews will also be obtained. Confidentiality will be maintained by removing all identifying information from the data and assigning unique codes to the participants. Anonymised quotes will be used in reporting the findings of the study to preserve participants’ privacy. All raw data will be stored securely on a password-protected personal computer accessible only to the research team. The participants will be informed that no monetary rewards or incentives will be provided for their participation in this study. However, to ensure transparency and promote reciprocity, the summary of research findings will be shared with them on completion of the study.
The findings of the study will be shared with the key stakeholders, including the study hospital, educational institution and policymakers, to guide strategies for improving preceptors’ CTBs. Additional dissemination efforts will include presentation at a national or international conference and publication in a peer-reviewed scientific journal in accordance with the Mixed-Methods Article Reporting Standards established by the American Psychological Association. 

## Patient and public involvement
Patients or members of the public are not involved in the design, conduct or reporting plans of this study. However, the study focuses on preceptors’ CTBs, which directly impact the learning experience of nursing students and in turn the quality of patient care. Hence, the findings of this study are intended to indirectly benefit patients by enhancing clinical teaching practices, contributing to the preparation of competent and patient-centred nursing professionals.
