## Introduction
Artificial intelligence (AI) technologies are becoming increasingly prominent in our everyday life and are expected to become ever more interwoven in social, health, and care systems in the near future. Within the domain of disability care, the potential for AI to enhance service accessibility and efficiency is receiving growing attention from service providers and researchers ( ; ). In parallel, a range of assistive AI technologies such as smart mobility devices and wheelchairs ( ), conversational agents for cognitive support ( ), and robotics for physical assistance ( ) are increasingly being piloted to support daily living, mobility, and rehabilitation for people with disabilities. This expanding discourse surrounding AI positions these technologies as tools with the potential to enhance autonomy, participation, and care access ( ). However, it simultaneously raises ethical and social questions concerning the future of care work, relational and affective dimensions of care, and the risks of reproducing or amplifying existing inequities in disability service delivery ( ). Within this context, care service providers such as disability support workers, occupational therapists, nurses, and care managers have been identified as early adopters of AI in care practices ( ) and as those expressing concern about potential threats to job security, financial stability, and the relational foundations of care ( ; ).
Media representations of AI, circulated through mainstream news, entertainment media, and online platforms, play a significant role in shaping public perceptions ( , ; ; ; ). , using a national representative survey of US adults, found that media use and interpersonal communications influence how people perceive and evaluate AI. Frequent exposure to AI-related media correlates with heightened emotional reactions, where sensational or negative portrayals tend to amplify fear and skepticism, while positive portrayals foster optimism, at times generating unrealistic expectations. Building on this, and demonstrate that exposure to specific media framing, whether celebratory or cautionary, influences public perceptions of AI’s benefits and risks, support for regulation, and trust in science. Importantly, however, such media narratives are not uniformly interpreted across populations. Individuals’ responses to AI-related media are mediated by their social locations and lived experience within intersecting systems of power which ultimately affect how they perceive and evaluate AI technologies. While research has begun to explore how factors such as education and political affiliation influence responses to AI media narratives ( ), the role of intersecting social identities including gender, race, age, and migration histories is underexplored.
Intersectionality, coined by Kimberlé , highlights how individuals experience and conceive the world differently within the overlapping and interlocking systems of power and oppression. Media representations of AI, therefore, are interpreted and consumed through the lens of their lived experiences within such systems ( ). As contends, intersectionality offers a critical frame for understanding how public discourses including media narratives are engaged with in ways that reflect individuals’ differential positioning within structures of domination. For example, marginalized individuals often read dominant media narratives through resistant or critical perspectives informed by their histories of exclusion and misrepresentation ( ). In the case of AI, narratives of technological progress and innovation may be met with skepticism or concern by communities disproportionately harmed by state surveillance, automation, and algorithmic discrimination ( ; ).
In disability care, care providers bring diverse social identities and lived experiences to their encounters with emerging AI technologies. Given the care sector itself is often characterized by a high concentration of women, migrants, and racialized individuals ( ), feminized, racialized care providers and migrant workers may approach AI with particular sensitivities rooted in experiences of sexism, racism, and institutional forms of control such as immigration surveillance ( ; ). further demonstrates how algorithmic systems reproduce intersecting biases, leading to outcomes that disproportionately disadvantage marginalized populations. These historical and structural inequities structure the attitudinal landscape surrounding AI adoption in care settings among care service providers. Therefore, the intersecting identities of these care service providers may influence their perceptions of technological changes including the promises and risks associated with AI, particularly in relation to trust, perceived threats to job security, and concerns about the erosion of the relational and affective foundations of care ( ; ).
Despite these dynamics, little research to date has explored how care service providers, as frontline workers, perceive and respond to AI in their service provision. This gap is particularly concerning in the context of care service providers assisting culturally and linguistically diverse (CaLD)
 
 migrants with disabilities given that these clients often experience intersecting forms of marginalization including racialization, linguistic exclusion, precarious migration status, and disabling environments which contribute to their systemic exclusion from mainstream disability services ( ; ). While AI-based interventions hold the potential to address communication barriers or service navigations, they also carry risks of further exacerbating inequities through biased algorithms ( ), cultural insensitivity ( ), and loss of relational care, which is especially critical for trust-building with marginalized communities such as CaLD migrants with disabilities ( ; ; ). Care service providers thus occupy a critical position as their perceptions of, trust in, and engagement with AI technologies may profoundly shape whether such tools foster greater inclusion or, conversely, reproduce existing patterns of exclusion in care service delivery.
In this study, we use an arts-based research (ABR) methodology, a one-act play, to examine care providers’ perceptions of AI as relational, affectively grounded, and socially embedded. We aim to understand what care providers think about AI and how these perceptions are constructed, shared, and negotiated in collective dialogue. Situated at the complex intersection of technological innovation, media narratives, and care service delivery, this study aims to address the following research questions (RQs):

RQ1: How do care providers perceive and make sense of AI within the context of providing care for CaLD migrants with disabilities?

RQ2: How do media narratives contribute to care providers’ fears, hopes, and ambivalence toward AI?

RQ3: How are care providers’ perceptions of AI shaped by intersecting social identities such as gender, race, age, migration histories, and professional roles?

## Methodological Design: Intersectionality-Informed Arts-Based Research
This study is a part of the four-year project, funded by the Australia Research Council (grant number LP: 190900099). The project employs an intersectionality-informed, participatory approach to examine how AI technologies are used, adapted, or resisted in everyday life of CaLD migrants with disabilities and in the context of care service provision and how they might be reimagined to support inclusion, autonomy, and equitable access. Situated within this larger initiative, this present study draws on an ABR methodology to explore the ways in which media narratives influence care service providers’ perceptions of AI in the context of providing care to CaLD migrants with disabilities.
An intersectionality-informed lens guides the study theoretically and conceptually. Intersectionality illuminates how multiple, overlapping social identities along with systemic structures of power, privilege, and oppression intersect to influence the lived experiences of multiply marginalized groups ( ). It further acknowledges that systems of oppression are not experienced in isolation but interact to shape individuals’ realities in compounding ways. Accordingly, we examine how care service providers’ intersecting social identities and positionalities inform their interpretation of and engagement with AI technologies and related narratives.
In keeping with the participatory characteristic of the project, this study integrates an arts-based mode of inquiry to collaboratively and expressively represent participants’ perspectives. Specifically, a one-act play was developed, grounded in the collected data, to illustrate the diversity of experiences and meanings that care service providers bring to the topic of AI in disability care. Arts-based approaches surface narrative complexity, embodied knowledge, and the subtle interplay of identity, power, and affect in shaping how technologies are understood and engaged with ( ; ). As such, the arts-based component of this study serves to complement the intersectionality-informed analysis and illustrates how AI-related media narratives are filtered through the lived experiences of care providers operating within complex and often inequitable care systems. In this sense, the holistic weaving of data into a non-fiction creative composition enables a deeper understanding of these otherwise little-known narratives concerning care service providers’ attitudes toward and expectations of AI ( ).

## Data Collection: Participatory Workshops
The data collection phase involved two participatory workshops with a group of 15 care service providers including disability support workers, occupational therapists, physiotherapists, speech pathologists, rehabilitation counsellors, and care managers assisting CaLD migrants with disabilities. Participants were recruited through partnerships with two community organizations (Two organizations: Western Sydney Migrant Resource Centre and YourSide) and represented a diverse group of care service providers across professional roles, gender, race, age, migration histories, and cultural backgrounds. The workshops were conducted over two days and organized into small groups to foster collaborative discussion and critical reflection. The research team involved four academic investigators, two partner investigators, and three Masters of Social Work Students. The thematic focus of each session is presented as follows:- Session 1: Discussion on technology, equity, and inclusion through brainstorming activities. Researchers provided a general overview of technology (e.g., iPhone, laptops, and desktop computers) and highlighted the growing integration of AI into everyday devices, platforms, and search engines (e.g., Google);
- Session 2: Presentation on AI usage in health, care, and social services contexts (successful and unsuccessful cases in care service provision in Australia);
- Sessions 3 and 4: Analysis of case studies related to AI usage in disability and healthcare;
- Sessions 5, 6, and 7: Reflections and discussion on the actual and future usage of AI for the delivery of care services to CaLD migrants with disabilities.

The workshops combined frontal sessions with participatory and collaborative activities, fostering participants’ knowledge while promoting their active involvement. Throughout sessions 5, 6, and 7, researchers audio-recorded participants’ discussions and took ethnographic field notes to ensure transparency in the analysis ( ). The integration of interactive experiences allowed researchers to elicit dynamic conversations, producing nuanced insights and unanticipated directions ( ). The workshops were designed with attention to participants’ social identities, drawing on diverse cultural, linguistic, and experiential knowledge to support inclusive participation. To further enhance accessibility and multiple forms of engagement, all materials were developed using Universal Design for Learning principles ( ; ), incorporating multiple communication formats and prioritizing on cultural relevance and content accessibility. Additionally, workshops included breaks and unstructured moments (e.g., coffee and lunch breaks), to support informal interactions, relationship-building, and side discussions among participants and researchers.

## Data Analysis: Thematic Analysis
To start the data analysis phase, audio recordings of sessions 5 to 7 from both workshops were transcribed using a paid transcription service with the same transcriber completing all workshop transcripts. All participants were de-identified in the transcripts using pseudonyms. Transcripts were analyzed collectively to identify patterns and recurring themes among the participant groups across the two service provider focus groups which involved service professionals (as described above) from different fields who worked directly with CaLD migrants with disabilities. This method was chosen because of its explorative and interpretative nature ( ), enabling the research team to develop core themes shared among the different participants, while grounding the analysis in the concrete data ( ). First, research team members conducted a thorough reading of the transcripts and field notes, focusing on grouping raw data under common conceptual themes ( ). After organizing themes into categories (axial coding), the relationships between concepts and categories were analyzed to distill continuities and divergences among the participant members (selective coding). To avoid the risks of biases in the analysis and ensure transparency in the process, codes and categories were revised by all team members until consensus was shared across the research team in relation to descriptive thematic groupings ( ; ).

## Data Representation: ABR Approach and One-Act Play
The research team decided to utilize ABR data representation of the findings as a means to capture the affective dynamics of the participants within the focus group discussions. Our discussion to undertake this representational process was determined after writing the qualitative descriptive findings as outlined below. The research team agreed that the use of ABR for data representation offered an innovative and inclusive channel for promoting readership engagement with the emotional and relational dimensions of the participants’ experiences ( ; ).
While traditional thematic reporting risks abstracting these experiences from the relational, conversational, and affective contexts in which they arise ( ), ABR offers a mode of representation attuned to the dialogic and relational nature of care work itself where meaning is continually negotiated in embodied interaction ( ). This is important in the context of disability care, where embodied presence, affective labor, and relational trust are critical to professional practice ( ; ; ). Moreover, ABR enables a poietic process of collaborative meaning-making ( ) and generates narrative forms that honor participants’ diverse voices while challenging dominant representations of AI and care work. Theatre-making was thus employed as an inclusive research practice, providing multiple means for narration ( ) and promoting reciprocity between aesthetics, research, and pedagogical impact ( ). In relation to intersectionality-informed research, an ABR approach can amplify voices and ways of knowing that are often marginalized within academic and policy discourses including embodied, affective, and relational knowledges that dominant systems of power have historically devalued ( ; ). Over the past five years, ABR approaches have been employed in research in a variety of areas, including the social sciences ( ), aged care ( ), health ( ), women and sports in the Global South ( ), and refugees ( ). ABR has also been used in studies on sensitive topics such as homelessness and domestic violence ( ; ), migrant sex workers ( ), young people’s lived experiences of anxiety and pain ( ), and disability within multicultural communities ( ; ).
It should be noted that although the use of ABR approach was guided by a commitment to arts-based, performative epistemologies that foreground emotion, relationality, multiplicity in meaning-making, and pedagogical impact, the decision to represent the data through a one-act play was made by the research team. While this choice was rooted in our intention to center relational and affective dimensions of care work, we recognize that, from an intersectionality-informed and power-conscious perspective, research processes including decisions around data representation should aim to decenter researcher authority and amplify the agency of participants, particularly those from marginalized communities ( ; ). We therefore acknowledge this as a limitation of our methodological approach.

## One-Act Play: Character Description and Scene Development
The one-act play draws on data from across the workshops and represents a synthesis of recurring themes, affective tones, and interactional dynamics. Characters were crafted as composite figures while preserving the multiplicity of experiences of the research participants. The aim is to ensure that the representation of our research participants was flattened, but that they maintained their affective dynamism within the focus groups. Care was taken to reflect the diversity of the research participants across their identities of gender, race, age, cultural and migration backgrounds, and professional roles. There are six characters whose voices are reflected in this play: Ulyssa (a woman of Middle Eastern background, mid-40s), Renee (a woman of a multicultural background, mid-40s), Shell (a young Chinese woman, late-20s), Haruki (a Japanese man, early-60s), Platon (a South American man, mid-40s), and Ruth (a Caucasian woman, late-30s). While not all 15 participants were represented as individual characters, the inclusion of six voices enabled narrative coherence while maintaining fidelity to their contributions within the workshop.
The fictional setting of the train platform and commute was chosen as an interpretive device to represent relational and dialogical dynamics observed during the workshops. This choice also allowed the narrative to prioritize the ways in which participants’ conversations about AI unfolded in informal, peer-to-peer interactions where meanings and attitudes toward AI were negotiated. The characters are amalgams of real participants’ voices and perspectives but do not represent any one individual in full. As such, the play should be read as an interpretive representation grounded in the data, designed to evoke the embodied, affective, and relational dimensions of the participants’ engagement with AI narratives ( ; . Importantly, the play is not intended as universal or definitive, rather it invites critical engagement with how media narratives about AI are negotiated through the intersectional positionalities of those delivering care to CaLD migrants with disabilities, who are themselves migrants or members of diaspora communities themselves.

## Discussion
In this study, we used an intersectionality-informed ABR approach to highlight care service providers’ interpretations of AI as expressed and negotiated through relational, embodied dialogue. We argue that perceptions of AI are intersectional accomplishments shaped by social locations (e.g., gender, race, age, migration histories, and professional role) and by the service context of working with CaLD migrants with disabilities ( ) and are understood through embodied, relational, and socially situated interactions that reflect the ethics and affective dynamics of care ( ; ).
Characters’ narratives in the one-act play demonstrate that affective response to AI ranging from fear to cautious optimism is made not simply by media exposure but by how such media narratives are interpreted through the lens of individuals’ social locations and care practice contexts. This aligns with prior work showing that media framing of AI can amplify or mitigate public fears ( ; ), but that such framing is interpreted differently across intersections of race, gender, age, migration status, and professional role ( ). For providers supporting clients who face linguistic barriers, culturally patterned help-seeking, and unequal digital access, dystopian imaginaries (e.g., Ulyssa’s reaction) resonated with everyday experiences of surveillance, misrecognition, and administrative harm, echoing histories of algorithmic injustice and racialized securitization ( ; ). Others, especially those with clinical or data governance experience (e.g., Shell), interpreted AI through the lens of professional regulation and digital fluency rather than generational difference. Overall, emotional responses toward AI were structured by the intersection of media narratives with embodied identities, professional experiences, and the structural conditions of care work, illustrating that sense-making about AI is relational, intersectional, and context-dependent.

## Dialogic Production of AI Meaning: Relational Spaces of Care Providers’ Sense-Making
Characters’ dialogues in the one-act play demonstrate that understandings of AI were co-constructed and reshaped through relational interaction grounded in care ethics emphasizing empathy, attentiveness, and reciprocity and through cultural attunement to the diverse linguistic and social worlds of CaLD migrants with disabilities. The play’s conversational structure, unfolding through informal dialogue among individuals of diverse gender, race, age, migration histories, cultural backgrounds, and professional roles, reveals that AI perceptions are negotiated within affective, embodied, and dialogic spaces. These findings echo feminist and sociocultural perspectives on knowledge as situated and relationally co-constructed ( ; ) and dialogic theories of meaning-making that emphasize how understanding emerges through interaction ( ). They also align with work in critical technology studies that highlights how perceptions of new technologies are shaped through socially situated, relational processes of everyday sense-making ( ). Similarly, conceptualize trust in AI as a fluid process built through clinicians’ situated interactions and evolving needs, demonstrating that AI meaning and trust are negotiated through practice. In our one-act play, care service providers working with CaLD migrants with disabilities showed relational dynamics mirrored their everyday practices of cross-cultural communication, empathy, and negotiation central to their work. The collaborative and emotionally responsive exchanges, such as moments when one character validated another’s fear or reframed a technological concern, echoed the relational sensitivities required when mediating between clients’ cultural expectations, linguistic barriers, and institutional demands.
In policy and government discourse, AI is often framed as a “technology solution” to improve productivity, efficiency, and service uptake. However, the affective dynamics within the play showed that case service providers working with CaLD migrants with disabilities experience such narratives through a lens of relational ethics and structural awareness, anticipating risks of exclusion, discrimination, and bias for both themselves and their clients. Reflecting this, it is clear that policies positioning AI as a neutral or efficiency-oriented innovation risk overlooking the affective and relational dimensions through which frontline care providers interpret and enact technology. Therefore, equitable AI implementation that respects care service providers’ expertise, attends to the cultural and linguistic contexts of clients, and avoids reproducing systemic inequities within care is essential.
By foregrounding these dialogic processes, the play reveals that care service providers’ sense-making about AI is an ethical and cultural practice as much as a technical one that is embodied, relational, and dialogic and emerges through intersectional positionalities and collective interactions. In practice, if care service providers’ understanding of AI is informed by relational dialogue and affective responsiveness, fostering critical and inclusive dialogic spaces within care organizations may be key to supporting more reflective and equitable engagement with AI technologies ( ). This approach aligns with feminist care ethics which emphasizes the importance of relational sensitivity, empathy, and attentiveness to others’ needs ( ; ). Top-down technical training alone may fail to address the relational, affective, and positional dimensions of care providers’ responses to AI as these dimensions are essential for promoting just and ethical adoption of such technologies in the care of CaLD migrants with disabilities.

## Researcher Learning and Methodological Reflexivity
Engaging an ABR approach enabled the research team to represent participants’ affective and relational knowledge in ways that conventional thematic reporting could not fully capture. The process of transforming qualitative data into a one-act play while attending to intersectionality required sustained attention to participants’ intersecting social locations which informed their perspectives on AI and interactions during the workshops, as well as the rhythms of speech, pauses, humor, and emotional resonance that characterized the discussions. This representational choice made visible how participants’ sense-making about AI was embedded in individuals’ social locations and care practice contexts, as well as in tone, gesture, and collective energy rather than isolated statements. Through this process, the research team recognized that ABR can be a methodological stance that foregrounds embodiment, emotion, and relationality as sources of knowledge ( ; ). It also underscored the ethical and affective responsibilities that accompany representing participants’ voice through creative means ( ). Working with care providers who support CaLD migrants with disabilities heightened our awareness of issues of translation, cultural nuance, and representation. Furthermore, it prompted the research team to critically reflect on researcher positionalities and their impact on interpretive decision and the portrayal of participants voices ( ), leading to the iterative discussions about character development and dialogue to ensure they remained grounded in participants’ lived experiences without appropriating or simplifying their perspectives. Reflecting on our methodological choices, the use of ABR in our study showed how creative representation can serve as an analytic and ethical practice that captures the embodied and relational complexity of participants’ experiences while challenging technocratic and disembodied ways of knowing about AI and care ( ). Therefore, we argue that integrating ABR with intersectional lens provides a methodological pathway for future studies seeking to illuminate the affective and ethical dimensions of AI in care practice.

## Conclusion: AI—The Unfamiliar Terrain
By illuminating care service providers’ perceptions of AI through an intersectionality-informed arts-based one-act play, this study highlighted AI not as a simple set of technical tools but as a socially and relationally constructed phenomenon. The one-act play preserved the embodied language, affective responses, and dialogic negotiations through which participants made sense of AI in the context of their care work for CaLD migrants with disabilities ( ).
The findings demonstrate that AI remains unfamiliar and contested terrain for many care service providers. However, this unfamiliarity was not a matter of individual knowledge gaps or generational differences. Rather, participants’ unfamiliarity, hopes, fears, and ambivalences toward AI were structured through relational dialogue grounded in care ethics and mediated by intersecting social identities and structural systems. While media narratives provided influential framings, participants interpreted these narratives through the lens of their lived experiences of migration, racialization, gendered care work, and professional precarity. As such, our findings highlight that AI sense-making is a fundamentally relational, embodied, and ethically charged process.
Care ethics was particularly salient in how participants engaged with one another’s fears and hopes about AI. Affective resonance, empathy, and relational attunement structured these dialogues which reflect the values that underpin ethical care itself ( ; ). This indicates that for care providers, the introduction of AI into care services is experienced with profound implications for relational care, trust, and client well-being.
Participants’ fear about job security and AI-driven automation were rooted in their unique social positions and the structural precarity of feminized, racialized care work ( ; ) and in the realities of providing care within an inequitable system. This may help avoid framing participants’ fears as “misperceptions” and situate them within histories of marginalization and labor exploitation, thereby resisting the depoliticization of their concerns.
The study has practical implications for the ethical implementation of AI in care services. First, fostering inclusive and relational spaces for dialogue among care service providers is needed to support reflective engagement with AI, honor care values, and surface ethical concerns that might otherwise remain silenced. These spaces would enable care service providers to collaboratively explore how AI may impact relational care, trust-building, and equity in service provision. Second, intersectionality must be integral to the design and governance of AI implementation processes in care. AI design, deployment, and training processes should not treat care service providers as a homogenous user group but must recognize the diverse migration histories, racialization, gendered experiences, and labor precarity that influence their engagement with AI, and their different social locations within the care system.
