## Abstract

## Background
Pregnant women are susceptible to iodine deficiency. Most iodine nutritional studies in pregnant women have involved cross-sectional evaluations of iodine status. This prospective observational study was designed to longitudinally follow the iodine status of pregnant women in different trimesters of pregnancy and to assess the demographic characteristics and dietary influences on iodine status.

## Methods
A prospective cohort study was conducted at Taipei Veterans General Hospital from March 2019 to July 2022. A total of 211 women were enrolled and each subject was asked to provide a spot urine sample at each trimester. Urinary iodine concentration (UIC) was measured using inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry. Dietary habit was assessed via food frequency questionnaires (FFQs) at each trimester.

## Results
The median UICs for the first, second and third trimesters were 156.3 µg/L (interquartile range [IQR]: 100.7-241.1), 146.2 µg/L (84.6-224.4), and 169.9 µg/L (94.4-283.5), respectively. Compared to the first trimester, participants consumed seaweed (p = 0.004 and p < 0.001, respectively), fish (p < 0.001 for both), and prenatal multivitamins (p < 0.001 for both) more frequently during the second and third trimesters. Additionally, an increasing number of women raised their intake of dairy products as their pregnancies progressed. Multivariate analysis indicated a positive association between nulliparity and UIC < 150 µg/L (adjusted odds ratio [OR]: 2.258, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.167–4.367, p = 0.02). Additionally, less prenatal iodine-containing multivitamin intake increased the odds of UIC < 150 µg/L across all trimesters (adjusted OR: 2.562, 95% CI: 1.234–5.319; p = 0.01 for the first trimester; adjusted OR: 2.264, 95% CI: 1.167–4.395; p = 0.02 for the second trimester; and adjusted OR: 2.022, 95% CI: 1.031–3.967; p = 0.04 for the third trimester).

## Conclusions
Mild iodine deficiency was observed in the second trimester despite adequate iodine status in the first and third trimesters. Nulliparous women and those not ingesting iodine-containing multivitamins are at greater risk of iodine deficiency, highlighting the need for targeted dietary education and interventions.

## Background
Iodine plays a crucial role in thyroid hormone synthesis, a process vital for fetal growth and neural development during pregnancy [ ]. Thyroid hormones contribute significantly to the growth of axons and dendrites, synapse formation, myelination, and neuronal migration [ ]. Severe iodine deficiency during pregnancy has been linked to increased risks of miscarriage, stillbirth, cretinism, neonatal hypothyroidism, and neuropsychomotor retardation [ ]. On the other hand, iodine intake exceeding 500 µg/day is also not recommended due to the possibility of thyroid function derangement among pregnant women and patients with autoimmune thyroid diseases or deficient iodine intake combined with multinodular goiter [ ]. Infantile adaptive and fine motor developmental delays were also discovered among children who were delivered by mothers with excessive iodine intake during pregnancy [ , ].
 As pregnancy progresses, there is a 30–50% increase in glomerular iodine clearance, elevated conversion of thyroid hormone to an inactive form at the placenta by type III deiodinase, and heightened production of maternal and fetal thyroid hormones, leading to an increased demand for iodine throughout pregnancy [ ]. Pederson et al. demonstrated that daily dietary iodine intake exceeding 200 µg can prevent thyroid gland enlargement and fluctuations in thyroid function tests [ ]. Another study suggested that iodine ingestion of approximately 130 µg/day may be insufficient to prevent an increase in thyroid volume [ ]. In light of these findings, the U.S. Institute of Medicine established the estimated average requirement (EAR) for iodine at 160 µg/day for pregnant women aged at least 14 years. The recommended dietary allowance (RDA) was set at 140% of the EAR, approximately 220 µg/day [ ]. The Endocrine Society and the World Health Organization (WHO) recommend an iodine intake of at least 250 µg/day during pregnancy, representing a 10% increase above the RDA and 100 µg above the recommended level for average nonpregnant women [ , ]. Although some obstetricians recommend iodine supplementation starting from the second trimester and continuing through breastfeeding to meet the increased iodine demand during pregnancy, there are currently no formal guidelines or recommendations from the Taiwanese government or obstetric and endocrinology societies.
 Universal salt iodization has been recommended as the primary strategy for eliminating iodine deficiency disorders [ ]. However, fewer than 10 brands of salt available in Taiwanese markets were iodized [ ]. Population surveys revealed that for people older than 19 years, the median urinary iodine concentration (UIC) was 100 µg/L in 2005–2008 and 104 µg/L in 2017–2020 [ , ]. These studies indicated a borderline adequate iodine status under the voluntary salt iodization policy. Nevertheless, pregnant women are still at risk for iodine deficiency. A national survey in 2017 to 2019 revealed a median UIC of 148 µg/L during pregnancy despite having 77.0–87.8% of the households using iodized salt [ ]. In contrast, a single-center survey conducted in 2018 among 257 pregnant women in an urban area of northern Taiwan indicated an adequate median UIC of 225.3 µg/L (interquartile range [IQR]: 109.1–514.2 µg/L) [ ]. These two studies were cross-sectional surveys which included different women in different trimesters. None of the studies performed in Taiwan has evaluated differences in iodine status of the same group of women across trimesters.
 Studies have shown that the median UIC varies in different trimesters, with some studies showing higher UIC levels in the early trimesters than in the late trimesters [ – ], whereas others showing the opposite trend [ – ]. Nevertheless, dietary changes in iodine intake in different trimesters also play important roles, but not all studies have evaluated dietary habits along with UICs [ ]. The trend of gestational UIC may also differ in iodine-sufficient or iodine-deficient regions. It would be of particular interest to prospectively assess UICs in all three trimesters in Taiwan to determine the time window of vulnerability. This study endeavors to bridge these gaps by tracking and evaluating the iodine status and changes in dietary habits in the same cohort throughout the three trimesters of pregnancy and determining the relationships between diet, demographic variables, and iodine status.

## Methods

## Study design and data collection
This prospective cohort study was conducted at Taipei Veterans General Hospital and received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 2016-03-013 A). The participants were recruited at the Obstetrics and Gynecology Outpatient Department on a voluntary basis when they first came for a routine pregnancy checkup. Pregnant women in Taiwan generally visit local clinics to confirm pregnancy and come to the hospital later when they have decided the venue for delivery. Therefore, there were limited women in their first trimesters at this site and nearly all women in their first trimesters at gestational ages of 7–13 weeks were invited. During the COVID pandemic period, recruitment was temporarily paused in May to July, 2021. Individuals who were taking anti-thyroid drugs, who were receiving thyroid hormone replacement therapy, who had undergone thyroid surgery or head and neck radiotherapy, or who were scheduled for abortion were excluded.
 All participants provided written informed consent before study entry. Each woman filled out a food frequency questionnaire and provided a random spot urine sample at the outpatient area while they were waiting for inspection [ ]. The questionnaires were completed through one interviewer on one participant interview to ensure that all questions were fully understood and properly answered. Sample collection was performed once in each trimester at the following period: gestational ages of 7–13 weeks in the first trimester, 19–27 weeks in the second trimester, and 28–37 weeks in the third trimester. The timing of urine sample collection varied, as samples could be taken either in the morning or afternoon, in a fasting or non-fasting state, depending on the scheduled appointment time. Demographic and obstetrical data including woman’s age, sex, parity, gestational age and information regarding newborns were obtained from the medical record of the hospital.

## Urinary iodine concentration
To evaluate iodine nutrition, UIC is commonly utilized as a measure of recent iodine intake, given that more than 90% of iodine from diets is excreted in urine. For population-based studies, UIC can be measured in spot urine samples from a representative target cohort and is expressed as the median, which correlates well with 24-hour samples [ , ]. The median UIC was used to determine recent iodine status, following the United Nations International Children’s Emergency Fund recommendation [ ]. Iodine deficiency during pregnancy is defined by a median UIC below 150 µg/L, as adequate dietary iodine intake theoretically corresponds to UIC levels between 150 and 249 µg/L [ ].
 The urine samples were stored at -20 °C after collection and thawed at room temperature before analysing with an Agilent 7700 Series inductively coupled with a plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) system [ ]. The ICP-MS alkali dilution method utilized ammonia solution and offers high sensitivity, fast turnaround times, and low limits of quantitation and detection for iodine measurements [ , ]. Its analytical performance has been validated in Taipei Veterans General Hospital [ ] and is one of the recommended methods for measuring UIC by the WHO [ ]. The lower limit of iodine detection was 1.0 µg/L. Quality control was performed by measuring urine reference material (REF 8847, REF 8849 from RECIPE Co., Munich, Germany) and human urine samples from the Ensuring the Quality of Urinary Iodine Procedures program (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Washington DC, USA) [ ].

## Food frequency questionnaire
The non-validated FFQ used in this study has been adopted in several peer-reviewed articles [ , ]. The FFQ was divided into two sections. Following basic information, including residential area, education, smoking and pre-pregnancy body height and body weight, the first section comprised questions regarding the frequency of consumption of iodine-containing foods, with response options including 1, 3, 5, and 7 days per week or never. The iodine-containing foods used were (1) seaweed, (2) fish, (3) seafood other than fish, (4) dairy products, and (5) prenatal multivitamins. The participants were able to identify the brands of prenatal multivitamins by referring to an illustrated chart (Supplementary Fig.  ). The second section aimed to determine whether iodized salt was utilized in the participants’ households. Participants need to identify the brands of iodized salt on the questionnaire and mark them. In addition, the frequency of eating outside the household was obtained.

## Statistical analysis
The UIC and intake frequencies, which were not normally distributed, are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), while other continuous variables are expressed as the means ± standard deviations (SDs). Each set of dietary habits was categorized into two subgroups, indicating individuals who “seldomly” consumed a particular type of food (≤ 1 day) versus those who “often” consumed it (≥ 3 days per week). This categorization was chosen to balance the number of participants in each group to ensure sufficient statistical power for testing. Average daily iodine intake from iodine-containing multivitamins was calculated by multiplying the intake frequency and the iodine content labelled for each tablet or capsule (Supplementary Fig.  ). Statistical tests included Mann‒Whitney U and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, Kruskal‒Wallis tests for comparisons of categorical variables, and Pearson’s chi‒square test for associations. Friedman’s test with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to assess differences in dietary habits across trimesters. Multivariate logistic regression, incorporating age, body mass index (BMI) and variables with a p value less than 0.2 in univariate analysis, identified factors associated with UIC < 150 µg/L. Analysis was conducted using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software, version 25.0. A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered significant.

## Results

## Patient characteristics
A total of 211 women were recruited. Two women terminated the study early due to stillbirth and were excluded from analysis. After excluding urine samples collected outside the correct time window (n = 11 in the first trimester and n = 5 in the second trimester), missing data (n = 2, 16, and 4 in the first, second, and third trimesters, respectively), and participants lost to follow-up (n = 13 in the second trimester and n = 21 in the third trimester), the final number of urine samples eligible for analysis was 196 in the first trimester, 175 in the second trimester, and 171 in the third trimester. The samples were collected on average at gestational age 10.2 ± 1.6 weeks of pregnancy during the first trimester; at gestational age 22.8 ± 2.2 weeks of pregnancy during the second trimester; and at gestational age 32.2 ± 1.7 weeks of pregnancy during the third trimester. Among the analysed 209 women, the mean age was 32.9 ± 4.4 years, with 32.5% of the participants being or being more than 35 years old. Approximately 12.3% of the women had a prepregnancy BMI in the range of obesity, defined as a BMI ≥ 27 kg/m2. Nearly 58.4% of the women were nulliparous, while 33% had a previous history of miscarriage. The mean weight of the newborns was 3079.6 ± 360.6 g, and 7.7% of the newborns were born at a low birth weight, defined as a birth weight lower than 2500 g. The demographic characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table  .
 

## Food frequency questionnaire
The changes in the intake frequencies of iodine-containing foods during each trimester are summarized in Table  . Among the various iodine-rich food sources, participants more commonly consumed dairy products and prenatal multivitamins. The number of women who consumed seaweed (p = 0.004 and p < 0.001), fish (p < 0.001 and p < 0.001, respectively), and multivitamins (p < 0.001 and p < 0.001, respectively) was significantly higher in the second and third trimesters compared to the first trimester. Additionally, there was a significant increase in the percentage of women who consumed dairy products more frequently in the second trimester (p < 0.001) and third trimester (p < 0.001) compared to the first trimester, as well as in the third trimester compared to the second trimester (p = 0.004).
 A total of 31 brands of prenatal multivitamins were consumed by the study participants, with 11 brands (35.5%) not containing iodine. The iodine content per tablet or capsule of multivitamin ranged from 50 µg to 150 µg for 18 brands, with only two brands (6.5%) containing an amount equivalent to or greater than 250 µg per tablet or capsule. The percentages of women who ingested high-iodine (≥ 250 µg) multivitamin supplements were 1.4%, 1.4%, and 1.9% in the first, second, and third trimesters, respectively. A total of 40.3%, 70.6%, and 65.9% of the women regularly took multivitamins in the first, second, and third trimesters, respectively, and 28.0%, 50.2%, and 46.0% of the women took iodine-containing multivitamins in the first, second, and third trimesters, respectively. For individuals who frequently took iodine-containing multivitamins, the mean daily iodine intake from these supplements was 122.0 µg (SD: 46.8 µg), 124.1 µg (SD: 37.4 µg), and 126.7 µg (SD: 43.3 µg) during the first, second, and third trimesters, respectively. In contrast, for those who seldom consumed iodine-containing multivitamins, the mean daily iodine intake from these supplements was 0.2 µg (SD: 2.1 µg), 0.6 µg (SD: 3.4 µg), and 0.4 µg (SD: 2.6 µg) across the same trimesters.
 Nearly half of the women used iodized salt at home (58.3%, 49.8%, and 48.3% in the first, second, and third trimesters, respectively). A significant difference in household use of iodized salt existed between nulliparous and multiparous women in the second trimester (43.1% versus 60.5%, p = 0.013). However, dining outside the home was frequent during pregnancy (median 7, 5, and 5 days in the first, second, and third trimesters, respectively), and the type of salt used in meals eaten could not be determined.

## Urinary iodine concentration
The median UICs were 156.3 µg/L (IQR: 100.7–241.1 µg/L), 146.2 µg/L (IQR: 84.6–224.4 µg/L), and 169.9 µg/L (IQR: 94.4–283.5 µg/L) in the first, second and third trimesters, respectively (Fig.  ). These findings indicate that the iodine statuses were adequate in the first and third trimesters but inadequate in the second trimester.
 

## Variables associated with urinary iodine concentration
Table  summarizes the univariate analysis of the association between each variable and UIC, along with the median UIC in subgroups classified based on demographic factors and dietary habits.
 For nulliparous women in the second trimester, the median UIC was lower (117.2 µg/L vs. 176.2 µg/L, p = 0.003), and the percentage of women with UICs < 150 µg/L (62.4% vs. 41.9%, p = 0.007) was greater compared to the multiparous women. For nulliparous women who did not use iodized salt, the median UIC was lower than that for those who did use iodized salt (116.0 µg/L, IQR: 77.1–186.6 µg/L vs. 171.5 µg/L, IQR: 101.7–291.6 µg/L, p = 0.046).
 For women who seldom took iodine-containing multivitamins, the median UICs were lower in both the first (144.5 µg/L vs. 195.1 µg/L, p = 0.02) and second trimesters (121.7 µg/L vs. 161.8 µg/L, p = 0.03). Additionally, the percentage of women with UICs below 150 µg/L was higher among those who seldom took iodine-containing multivitamins in both the first (52.1% vs. 30.8%, p = 0.008) and second trimesters (64.5% vs. 45.5%, p = 0.008). In the third trimester, the median UICs were also lower (133.6 µg/L vs. 211.7 µg/L, p = 0.03) among those who seldom took iodine-containing multivitamins. The other variables were not significantly associated with UICs < 150 µg/L.
 In the univariate analysis of the first trimester, the factors with p-values less than 0.2 were education, parity, seaweed, and iodine-containing multivitamins, with p-values of 0.073, 0.056, 0.186, and 0.164, respectively. For the second trimester, the factors were history of miscarriage, parity, and iodine-containing multivitamins, with p-values of 0.139, 0.008, and 0.106, respectively. In the third trimester, parity and iodine-containing multivitamins were the factors with p-values of 0.129 and 0.011, respectively. These factors were incorporated into multivariate analysis along with age and BMI in each trimester. Multivariate analysis revealed that nulliparous women had significantly higher odds of having a UIC < 150 µg/L in the second trimester (adjusted odds ratio [OR]: 2.258, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.167–4.367, p = 0.02). Additionally, women who seldom took iodine-containing multivitamins were more likely to have a UIC below 150 µg/L across all trimesters: the first (adjusted OR: 2.562, 95% CI: 1.234–5.319, p = 0.01), the second (adjusted OR: 2.264, 95% CI: 1.167–4.395, p = 0.02), and the third trimester (adjusted OR: 2.022, 95% CI: 1.031–3.967, p = 0.04) (Table  ).
 

## Discussion
This study comprehensively investigated the changes in iodine status, intake frequencies of iodine-containing foods and supplement, and factors associated with iodine nutrition throughout pregnancy. The median UICs were 156.3, 146.2, and 169.9 µg/L in the first, second, and third trimesters, respectively. These results indicate adequate iodine status in the first and third trimesters but mildly deficient iodine status in the second trimester. Recent studies evaluating UIC across three trimesters are summarized in Supplementary material 1 [ – , – , – ]. Some studies have shown that iodine deficiency can occur throughout all three trimesters [ , ], while others have found it may develop in the second and/or third trimesters [ , , , , , , , ]. Even in countries where the general population is iodine-sufficient, pregnant women may still experience iodine deficiency during pregnancy. Consequently, evaluation of the association between iodine status and diet across trimesters is important. Torres et al. reported that increased milk consumption in the first trimester and increased intake of iodized salt and iodine supplements across all three trimesters contributed to a greater percentage of women with UICs ≥ 150 µg/L [ ]. Hynes et al. reported that women who consistently took iodine-containing supplements before and during pregnancy had greater odds of achieving adequate iodine levels [ ]. Andersson et al. and Zhang et al. both indicated that reliance on iodized salt might not lead to adequate iodine nutrition during pregnancy [ , ]. Nonetheless, the majority of the studies did not examine the relationship between iodine-containing food intake and UIC values [ , – , , , – ]. In the current study, intake frequencies of iodine-containing foods and supplements varied significantly between trimesters, with increased consumption of seaweed, fish, dairy products, and multivitamins observed in the second and third trimesters compared to the first trimester. Nulliparous women and those not ingesting iodine-containing multivitamins had greater odds of having UICs < 150 µg/L.
 Although the iodine status is generally adequate in Taiwan, mild iodine deficiency has been noticed in 2013 when the median UIC was 96 µg/L for people aged over 6 years [ ]. As a result, a regulation requiring the iodine content of salt to be specified on the packaging was implemented in 2015. Furthermore, in June 2017, the Taiwan Food and Drug Administration increased the potassium iodide or potassium iodate content in iodized salt from 12 to 20 mg/kg to 20–33 mg/kg [ ]. Despite efforts to increase public awareness and improve iodine nutrition, iodine intake from salt is still volatile and unreliable under the current voluntary salt iodization policy. In this study conducted in metropolitan Taipei, most of the women dined outside of the home for more than half a week. Although half of our participants used iodized salt at home, salt does not serve as a stable source of iodine nutrition. Consequently, an emphasis on increasing portions and servings of iodine-containing food is warranted.
 The intake frequency of fish, dairy products and multivitamins increased during the second and third trimesters in the studied women. However, the iodine status in the second trimester is still mildly deficient. A possible explanation is that although the frequency of iodine-containing food or supplement consumption among the participants increased, the amount consumed might still be insufficient. This indicates a need for more research in Taiwan on the iodine content in foods to better inform the public about the appropriate portions of iodine-rich foods to consume. Although iodine contents have been measured in three kinds of seaweeds and milk or milk alternatives in Taiwan [ , ], they are largely unknown in the majority of food types. Additionally, fluctuations in urinary iodine concentration (UIC) may be influenced by physiological changes during pregnancy, such as increased maternal urinary iodine clearance due to a higher glomerular filtration rate, which can elevate UIC in the first trimester [ ]. This effect may mask underlying mild iodine deficiency. On the other hand, greater placental iodine transfer to the fetus and increased-GFR-related iodine losses could contribute to lower UIC in the second trimester [ ].
 In this study, women who frequently consumed iodine-containing multivitamins maintained adequate iodine levels throughout all trimesters, unlike those with lower median UICs who seldom took them. The American Thyroid Association recommends a daily intake of 150 µg of iodine, in the form of potassium iodide, for pregnant women [ ]. Although Taiwan’s dietary reference intake (DRI) for iodine is higher for pregnant women, there is no specific guidance on increasing iodine intake [ ]. Research in other countries has shown that iodine-containing multivitamin supplementation raises median UICs in pregnant women [ ]. A study in South Australia found that women taking iodine-containing multivitamins had adequate and increasing median UICs throughout pregnancy [ ]. Similarly, a nationwide study in Sweden revealed inadequate iodine status in women not taking supplements with more than 150 µg of iodine daily [ ]. Based on the results of this study, iodine-containing multivitamins serve as a major source of iodine nutrition and can be recommended for pregnant women in Taiwan. Continuous attention and education regarding iodine status and dietary intake are also necessary for the optimal care of these women.
 Previous studies have rarely explored the link between nulliparity and iodine deficiency. However, Adalsteinsdottir et al. reported a lower UIC in nulliparous women than in multiparous women [ ]. McMullan et al. reported insufficient knowledge among nulliparous women regarding iodine-rich food sources and how to calculate the iodine content in a meal [ ]. This study revealed less use of iodized salt in nulliparous women, which we postulate to contribute to lower UICs. As a result, appropriate dietary education should be provided to nulliparous women in time to prevent iodine insufficiency.
 The limitations of this study are as follows. First, the iodine content in most foods was not measured in Taiwan, and the precise amount of iodine-containing foods ingested could not be calculated in our FFQ. Second, this was a single-center survey, which cannot be generalized to the entire population of Taiwan. Third, this study did not investigate the influence of maternal iodine status on children’s outcomes. Despite these limitations, the study has notable strengths, including a large number of participants and a complete follow-up throughout pregnancy. To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive investigation to explore the associations among dietary habits, demographic variables, and iodine nutrition across all three trimesters.

## Conclusions
Iodine nutrition was sufficient during the first and third trimesters but deficient in the second trimester of pregnancy. Intake frequencies of iodine-containing foods and supplement changed significantly between trimesters. Nulliparous women and those who did not use iodine-containing multivitamins were found to be at risk for lower UICs during pregnancy. The results of this study highlight the importance of providing comprehensive iodine nutritional education, especially for first-time mothers. Additionally, iodine-containing multivitamins serve as a major source of iodine in pregnancy and are suggested to ensure adequate iodine nutrition.
