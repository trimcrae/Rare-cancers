## Abstract
Sport holds profound global social influence, yet its systemic environmental footprint is often overlooked in sustainable development governance. Although environmental initiatives have proliferated in professional sport, many remain limited to public relations—fragmented, symbolic, and inadequate to tackle structural challenges. To facilitate a transition, a scientific framework is needed that offers coherent, verifiable pathways, bridging the gap between isolated sport initiatives and the broader systems of global environmental governance.

Sport is both a potential driver of environmental degradation and an increasingly direct victim of climate and ecological change , . However, current sustainability practices within the sport sector often prioritize visibility in front of the camera while neglecting key issues related to planetary ecological thresholds. For instance, cross-border and long-distance travel by spectators at major sporting events typically constitutes the dominant source of greenhouse gas emissions , far exceeding the mitigation gains achieved through the installation of solar panels or energy-efficient lighting . Likewise, the release of organic pollutants and heavy metals from synthetic turf, as well as microplastic pollution generated from short-lifespan functional textiles , , are rarely addressed in organizers’ environmental reports or public communications. This gap between visibility and substance reinforces greenwashing behaviors , , undermines public trust, and constrains the accumulation of methodological, empirical, and theoretical knowledge within academia. Meanwhile, sport not only affects the environment but is increasingly affected by it. Climate change exerts a pronounced impact on competitive sport . Rising greenhouse gas concentrations and increasing global temperatures have been shown to impair athletes’ performance and physiological capacity under extreme heat and humidity , , thereby threatening both fairness and safety in competition and exposing the structural vulnerability of the sport system to the climate crisis. Moreover, global warming poses a direct threat to winter sports : higher temperatures and altered snowfall patterns have resulted in insufficient snow cover, deteriorating tracks, and elevated event risks . In some regions, energy-intensive artificial snowmaking has become essential to sustain operations, creating an adaptation paradox that further intensifies environmental stress. This vulnerability demonstrates that sport is no longer a peripheral concern in the climate crisis but a critical domain that demands systematic scientific intervention (Fig.  ).
 Continued reliance on symbolic gestures rather than structural reform is likely to not only impede substantive sustainability progress but also erode public trust in the sector’s green transition. To overcome this fragmentation and performative approach, the sport community is encouraged to develop a coherent and verifiable scientific framework to guide systemic transformation. To this end, we propose a three-dimensional roadmap structured around three interrelated pillars: Diagnostics (Pillar I), Intervention (Pillar II), and Governance (Pillar III). Together, these pillars provide an integrated framework for moving the sport sector from symbolic greening toward systemic accountability.

## Pillar I: Diagnostics. Establishing a scientific evaluation framework beyond greenwashing
A major obstacle to the sustainable transformation of the sport industry lies in the absence of standardized and systematic assessment methodologies . This quantitative vacuum acts as a ‘sustainability smokescreen’ , where the lack of standardized metrics enables selective disclosure of marginal gains while systemic impacts remain obscured. Standardizing these methodologies is therefore a prerequisite for institutional accountability, ensuring that data serves as a transparent evidence base rather than a shield for performative greenwashing. In this regard, Pillar I advances a diagnostic agenda through three specific pathways: life cycle assessment (LCA), multi-dimensional indexing, and data transparency.
The first pathway within Pillar I focuses on promoting the application of Life Cycle Assessment (LCA). While widely adopted in the energy and manufacturing sectors, Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) remains underutilized in sport . Systematically applying LCA to sporting goods, apparel, venues, and temporary infrastructure uncovers environmental impacts, including emissions, energy use, and pollutants, across the entire value chain, providing a quantitative baseline for evidence-informed decision-making. Developing sport-specific LCA guidelines and databases is essential to curtail selective disclosure, enhance methodological transparency, and establish the robust data foundation required for effective sectoral governance.
A second pathway within Pillar I involves establishing a multi-dimensional event-footprint approach. Systematic assessment should ideally transcend narrow carbon accounting to encompass a multi-dimensional ‘event footprint’ index. While carbon metrics are essential, an exclusive focus on emissions risks ‘problem shifting’—where mitigation gains are achieved at the expense of increased water consumption, biodiversity loss, or socioeconomic strain on host communities. Anchored in the planetary boundaries framework , assessment should move toward the implementation of a broader set of indicators, including water footprint, land-use change, material circularity, and socioeconomic impact. Such a multi-dimensional approach would help policymakers and organizers optimize resource allocation and enhance environmental performance throughout the planning and operational phases.
A third pathway within Pillar I is the development of an open data platform. Scientific credibility and verifiability depend on a transparent data infrastructure. Currently, sustainability data, from emissions to waste, remain largely under organizer control, lacking independent auditing and academic accessibility. To ensure scientific integrity, a global open-access database should be established and governed by an independent oversight mechanism, such as a consortium of academic and non-governmental organizations. Decoupling data management from sport governing bodies facilitates longitudinal comparisons, strengthens public accountability, and mitigates greenwashing through decentralized verification.

## Pillar II: Intervention. Catalyzing systemic technological and behavioral change
Building on Pillar I, Pillar II recognizes that a scientific diagnostic framework alone cannot propel systemic transformation. At present, sustainable technologies and practices remain niche and experimental, failing to disrupt the resource-intensive production models that dominate the industry. Simultaneously, sport’s immense capacity to reshape social norms and consumer behavior remains largely latent, leaving its potential to catalyze broad-scale sustainability transitions significantly underutilized. To catalyze this systemic transformation, Pillar II advances three pathways aimed at technological scaling, behavioral design, and social advocacy.
The first pathway within Pillar II focuses on scaling circular economy models and low-carbon innovations. The sport industry’s demand for high-performance gear often necessitates reliance on petrochemical-based composites and short-lived textiles, incurring heavy environmental costs. Transitioning to circularity requires cross-disciplinary innovation in bio-based or fully recyclable materials , alongside a shift toward ‘product-as-a-service’ models, such as leasing and remanufacturing, to extend product lifespans . However, these innovations face significant trade-offs between ecological benefits and performance rigor , necessitating the systematic LCAs proposed in Pillar I. While initiatives like Adidas’s recyclable footwear and Li-Ning’s waterless dyeing show potential, scaling these models remains a radical challenge to the industry’s entrenched, petrochemical-dependent growth paradigm. Meaningful change is frequently stymied by supply chain inertia and the ‘adaptation paradox,’ where energy-intensive technologies, such as artificial snowmaking, sustain traditional operations rather than catalyzing systemic reform. Overcoming these barriers requires institutionalized incentives and a collaborative governance ecosystem where suppliers, manufacturers, and consumers act as co-enforcers of shared sustainability standards.
A second pathway within Pillar II lies in designing behaviorally informed interventions. Sporting events can serve as natural laboratories for studying and shaping pro-environmental behaviors. Leveraging behavioral science, including nudging and social norms , , organizers can conduct randomized controlled trials to assess interventions such as incentivizing low-carbon travel, optimizing waste-sorting, and utilizing real-time data to reinforce collective environmental responsibility. However, to avoid what Jules Boykoff et al. term a ‘sustainability smokescreen ,’ these behavioral interventions should be decoupled from the sole control of event organizers and subjected to independent, third-party verification. Finally, the expansion of digitalization and online broadcasting presents critical pathways to transform consumption patterns, offering practical avenues to help mitigate the carbon footprint of long-distance spectator travel.
A third pathway within Pillar II involves empowering athletes and clubs as advocates of change. Athletes and sport clubs command exceptional public trust and symbolic influence, often surpassing traditional communication channels . Establishing incentive mechanisms, such as sustainability awards, specialized training, and collaborative networks, can empower these actors to champion environmental values, thereby amplifying the social reach of the green transition. While empirical evidence suggests that advocacy by elite athletes significantly reshapes public attitudes, this transformative potential remains largely untapped. Sport possesses a unique capacity for social diffusion; through coordinated campaigns and community initiatives, it serves as a potent medium for fostering environmental awareness. Transforming sport’s vast social capital into an active driver of environmental action represents a promising, yet underrecognized, pathway toward systemic sustainable transformation.

## Pillar III: Governance. Integrating sport into global environmental policy
Pillar III anchors the diagnostic and intervention agendas in a robust governance architecture that bridges the divide between the sport sector and formal environmental policy frameworks. Currently, the industry remains largely disconnected from binding accountability, frequently benefiting from ‘sporting exceptionalism’, a policy status where entrenched political and economic interests override environmental urgency. Under the banner of ‘public interest,’ major sporting events often prioritize capital accumulation and urban politics over long-term ecological health . This is facilitated by an ‘accountability gap’ , where the lack of local oversight allows transnational sport organizations and host cities to bypass sustainability commitments. Navigating this fragmented landscape of diverse stakeholders remains a formidable challenge. To dismantle these structural barriers, Pillar III advances a governance framework structured around three pathways that embed sport into global climate and ecological policy architectures.
The first pathway within Pillar III focuses on incorporating major sporting events into Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs). Sporting events entail extensive transportation, construction, and consumption activities whose environmental impacts are far from localized. As the primary signatories to the Paris Agreement, it is arguably essential for national governments to assume ultimate accountability for event-related emissions and resource use within their jurisdictions. We propose that governments mandate the inclusion of major sporting events in their Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) updates, positioning national environmental authorities as the central bodies for auditing and enforcement. This aims to ensure that event-related emissions and resource consumption are strictly quantified, reported, and mitigated under the same regulatory oversight as other high-impact industries, effectively moving beyond voluntary, non-binding commitments. Such measures would not only strengthen event-level oversight but also foster closer cooperation between sport authorities and national climate policymakers.
A second pathway within Pillar III involves enforcing binding sustainability standards. Organizations like the International Olympic Committee (IOC) and FIFA possess immense regulatory power, yet their sustainability mandates often falter due to information asymmetry and the ‘principal-agent problem,’ where local political interests supersede environmental commitments . To overcome this, sport governance is encouraged to shift from voluntarism to a coercive model centered on legally binding pre-bidding requirements . These criteria are designed to encourage host cities to integrate green design, low-carbon transport, and circular supply chains from the earliest planning stages. Crucially, implementation should ideally balance equity and feasibility by accounting for economic disparities between developed and developing nations to help ensure a just transition. Moreover, differentiated standards should be established according to event typologies, for example, periodic mega-events should emphasize full life-cycle venue management, while non-periodic events should prioritize optimizing transport systems and spectator participation models. In addition, sport facilities should be designed for multifunctional use and spatial-temporal integration, allowing large venues to serve as sites for cultural performances, exhibitions, and community activities after events. Such strategies enhance spatial efficiency and social return on investment. For new or temporary facilities, low-impact, modular, and demountable designs should be encouraged, promoting the integration of sport spaces with local communities and natural landscapes to improve land-use efficiency and ecological adaptability. Host cities should also avoid the burden of “venue legacies” by leveraging existing educational or public infrastructure through shared-use models that support sustainable post-event operations .
A third pathway within Pillar III connects sport with multilateral environmental agreements. The environmental footprint of sport extends well beyond climate change, encompassing issues such as plastic and water pollution, soil degradation, noise disturbance, heavy metal contamination, and biodiversity loss . Aligning sport governance with other multilateral environmental agreements, such as the Convention on Biological Diversity and the Minamata Convention on Mercury , can promote cross-sectoral policy integration. Furthermore, the UN’s Sport for Climate Action Framework provides a vital platform for aligning the sector with the Paris Agreement goals . However, moving beyond their currently voluntary nature depends on integrating such initiatives with the binding accountability and transparent reporting mechanisms discussed above, thereby helping to bridge the gap between signatory status and material emission reductions. For instance, restrictions on the use of mercury- or lead-containing materials in sport equipment and infrastructure, or the implementation of ecological mitigation measures for events held in biodiversity-sensitive regions, would help position sport as an active contributor to global environmental governance.
Taken together, Pillar I (Diagnostics), Pillar II (Intervention), and Pillar III (Governance) provide a coherent framework for repositioning sport from symbolic sustainability claims to systemic accountability. In summary, sport’s role in the global climate and ecological crisis is evolving from symbolic advocacy to systemic participation. This transformation necessitates coordinated action among governments, industry, sport organizations, and civil society to build the institutionalized alliances required for sustainable governance. Future research and policy efforts are encouraged to transcend technical optimization, instead confronting the underlying power dynamics and political barriers that have historically impeded reform. Realizing substantive change requires a fundamental shift in governance: moving away from the ‘special status’ of voluntary initiatives toward the legally binding accountability of global climate architectures, such as the NDC framework. Ultimately, sport’s ecological transition is a complex, long-term endeavor contingent on continuous innovation and cross-sectoral synergy. Through the integration of scientific evidence, policy instruments, and collective action, sport is better positioned to transcend symbolic gestures and become a substantive catalyst for global sustainable development.
