## Abstract

## Rationale & Objective
The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic disproportionately affected vulnerable individuals, including people with kidney disease. We elucidated longitudinal trends in hospitalization and mortality among individuals receiving maintenance dialysis before and during the pandemic and explored how universal vaccine availability affected COVID-19 outcomes by race/ethnicity.

## Study Design
A retrospective cohort study.

## Settings & Participants
Adult maintenance dialysis patients between 2018-2023 at a national not-for-profit US provider.

## Predictors
COVID-19 era (2020-2023), race/ethnicity.

## Outcomes
COVID-19 vaccination status; hospitalization and death (COVID-related, all-cause).

## Analytical Approach
Zero-inflated Poisson models and logistic regression models were used to calculate hospitalization and mortality rates, respectively, adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, dialysis vintage, and number of comorbid conditions.

## Results
Among 41,257 patients receiving maintenance dialysis, all-cause hospitalization dropped abruptly in March/April 2020 and increased thereafter, albeit remaining below prepandemic rates. All-cause mortality exhibited typical seasonal variability during 2018-2019, subsequently increasing with onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in March 2020. Mortality peaked in January 2021 at 228 deaths per 1,000 person-years before declining to 151 deaths per 1,000 person-years in March 2021. Subsequently, mortality transiently increased during the Delta and Omicron variant periods, peaking at 188 and 189 deaths per 1,000 person-years, respectively. Thereafter, all-cause mortality remained below prepandemic levels. After widespread SARS-CoV-2 vaccine availability in 2021 with vaccine provision in dialysis facilities, the COVID-19 mortality rate among all race/ethnicity groups declined significantly (b = −5.3; P < 0.001). There were no statistically significant differences by race/ethnicity in the vaccination status at each year’s end.

## Limitations
Potential residual confounders and underreporting of COVID-19–related outcomes.

## Conclusions
All-cause mortality increased sharply in 2020 and early 2021, reflecting COVID-19-related deaths, whereas non–COVID-19 mortality declined during the early phase of the pandemic and subsequently remained below prepandemic levels. After introduction of SARS-CoV-2 vaccines, all-cause mortality declined to below prepandemic levels, likely reflecting the impact of widespread vaccination in the dialysis population.

Index Words: Dialysis, COVID-19, vaccination, mortality, hospitalization

## Plain-Language Summary
This study evaluated trends for mortality, hospitalization, and vaccination status, before and during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic in a national US dialysis cohort. Among 41,257 patients, all-cause hospitalization rates abruptly dropped in March 2020 despite an increase in all-cause mortality, coinciding with the national lockdown when the pandemic began. COVID-19–related mortality dropped significantly among all races/ethnicities after the availability of COVID-19 vaccines in 2021, with similar vaccination rates for dialysis patients attained across racial/ethnic groups. The continued vulnerability of patients on maintenance dialysis to severe COVID-19 outcomes highlights the need to maintain universal access to vaccines at their most convenient venue while promoting continuous updated vaccine status to limit COVID-19–related hospitalization and death in this high-risk population.

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic severely affected vulnerable individuals, including profound effects on people dependent on being treated with dialysis. Mortality among hospitalized patients with COVID-19 and chronic kidney diseases (CKDs), including those receiving dialysis, greatly exceeded mortality in the non-CKD population. , During the pandemic, certain characteristics of the dialysis population predisposed them to worse outcomes as compared with the general population, including a predominantly older population with multiple serious comorbid conditions, high rates of multifactorial immunocompromise, and limited ability to physically distance because of health care needs or socioeconomic challenges, particularly among individuals dependent on being treated with in-center hemodialysis. , , Notably, early in the pandemic, excess deaths were reported among African American and Hispanic patients receiving dialysis relative to White patients receiving dialysis. 
Although there have been several previous descriptions of clinical outcomes among dialysis patients during the early COVID-19 pandemic, , , , there is a dearth of information on the pandemic’s overall impact on hospitalization and mortality outcomes, how vaccination may have modified these outcomes, and how outcomes in the late-pandemic period compared with prepandemic outcomes. This study describes longitudinal trends in hospitalization and mortality rates among patients receiving maintenance dialysis at a mid-size national provider during the COVID-19 pandemic period (2020-2023) within the context of the 2 previous years (2018-2019), incorporating data on race and ethnicity and vaccination status.

## Methods

## Study Population
Dialysis Clinic, Inc (DCI) is the largest not-for-profit dialysis provider in the United States, operating more than 240 outpatient dialysis clinics in 28 states. Adults aged 18 years and older treated at DCI any time between January 1, 2018 and December 31, 2023 with maintenance dialysis were included in this retrospective cohort study.

## Demographic and Clinical Characteristics
Patient characteristics were obtained from the DCI electronic health record (EHR) and included age, sex, race/ethnicity classified in mutually exclusive groups as non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic African American, Hispanic, and Other (Asian, Pacific Islander, American Indian, and multiracial), and dialysis vintage. Baseline comorbid conditions (immunodeficiency disorder, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, stroke/cerebrovascular disorder, thyroid disease, and cancer) were extracted from Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services form 2728 and updated with new diagnoses from the EHR. Hospitalization and mortality rates were stratified by dialysis modality (in-center hemodialysis versus home dialysis) and race/ethnicity. International Classification of Diseases-10 codes and medication names used to define variables are listed in .

## SARS-CoV-2 Vaccination
We summarized vaccination administration by administration location and vaccination status based initially on the number of doses (as the initial mRNA vaccine series consisted of 2 to 3 doses), then on subsequent vaccine receipt. A person was considered vaccinated with one dose beginning 14 days after receiving the first vaccine dose because this is when initial protection against severe infection likely begins. , , An initial single dose of adenovirus vaccine (Ad26.COV2.S/Janssen) was counted as equivalent to 2 mRNA vaccine doses. With each additional dose, patients were considered to have reached full immunity for that dose on the 14th day after vaccine administration. A booster dose was authorized starting in September 2021, and a bivalent booster was recommended by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in September 2022. Similarly, updated monovalent vaccines were approved in September 2023. Each vaccine category is mutually exclusive.

## Hospitalization and Mortality Outcomes
Outcomes were derived from the DCI EHR. Outcomes of interest included hospitalization and mortality. Hospitalization events were entered by dialysis staff. Per policy, patients were queried about hospitalization before every treatment and rigorously ascertained after any missed treatment. We applied the 2-midnight rule to define a hospitalization. Adjacent hospitalization records within 3 days were considered as a single hospitalization episode. COVID-19–related hospitalization was defined as a hospitalization episode within 30 days of COVID-19 diagnosis in which COVID-19 was documented as the primary diagnosis. Non–COVID-19-related hospitalizations were further categorized into mutually exclusive subgroups of infection, cardiovascular, or other cause, with similar methodology as used by the United States Renal Data System (USRDS). The primary cause of death was identified from Medicare form 2,746, which was reviewed and approved by the treating nephrologist. Deaths occurring within 30 days after withdrawal from dialysis were included in the mortality outcome. A COVID-19-related death was defined as a death occurring within 90 days of COVID-19 diagnosis with COVID-19 documented as the primary cause of death.

## Statistical Analyses
Descriptive statistics summarized categorical variables with numbers and proportions and continuous variables with means and standard deviations, by year. Crude event rates for hospitalization and mortality were calculated as the total number of events in each month or year divided by patient-time at risk. Time spent in the hospital was deducted from the hospitalization time at risk. Time-at-risk for mortality was defined by the cumulative total length of time that each patient was receiving dialysis. Time-at-risk begins January 1st of each year or at entry, whichever is earlier. Crude rates were number of events divided by time at risk, whereas unadjusted rates were derived from univariable models. Adjusted rates included age, sex, race/ethnicity, dialysis vintage, and number of comorbid conditions as variables in zero-inflated Poisson models for hospitalization (because of excessive zeros) and logistic regression models for mortality. Linear regression weighted for total patient count was used to examine the distribution of vaccine status among race/ethnicity groups and to examine trends in hospitalization and mortality over time. This retrospective study was reviewed and deemed exempt by the WIRB-Copernicus Group (formerly Western Institutional Review Board) Institutional Review Board work order 1-1456342-1. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4.

## Results
From 2018-2023, a total of 41,257 prevalent patients received maintenance dialysis at DCI. Demographic and clinical characteristics for prevalent patients during the first week of each calendar year are shown in . Despite the increasing mean age of the patient cohorts, the mean number of comorbid conditions decreased over the 6-year study period. Overall, 57% of patients were men, 42% were non-Hispanic White, and 87% of patients were treated with in-center hemodialysis.
 

## Hospitalization Over Time
Crude monthly all-cause hospitalization rates decreased between 2018 and 2023 ( ; ). There was an abrupt drop in all-cause hospitalization rates in March and April 2020, and, although hospitalization rates rose thereafter, they remained consistently below prepandemic levels, albeit with seasonal peaks. COVID-19–associated hospitalization dropped considerably in 2022 and 2023. Hospitalization rates declined among both in-center and home dialysis patients in 2020 and generally remained below prepandemic levels from 2021-2023 ( ).
 For both cardiovascular and non-COVID infection-related hospitalizations, rates followed a similar pattern to the overall hospitalization rates ( ). There was a sharp decline in the adjusted all-cause hospitalization rate in 2020 (0.98 per person-year) and a slight increase in 2022 (1.02 per person-year). These changes occurred alongside a steady decline in COVID-19 hospitalization rates from 2020-2023 ( ). For context, all-cause hospitalization rates among DCI-treated patients for each cohort year from 2018-2021 were lower than the corresponding overall rates available from the USRDS for the same period ( ). 

## Mortality Over Time
The crude monthly all-cause mortality rate among prevalent dialysis patients decreased by 17.4%, from 202 per 1,000 person-years in January 2018 to 172 per 1,000 person-years in December 2023, with a notable spike in March 2020 when the pandemic began ( ). Mortality peaked during the winter of 2020-2021, declining thereafter, concurrent with initial vaccine access and seasonal changes. COVID-19 associated mortality increased again at the onset of the Delta variant-dominant period (summer 2021) and at the onset of the Omicron variant-dominant period (winter 2021-2022). Adjusted annual non–COVID-19 mortality rates slowly decreased throughout the study period (173 per 1,000 person-years in 2018 to 148 per 1,000 person-years in 2023) ( ). All-cause mortality rates were higher among in-center patients than among home dialysis patients throughout the study period ( ).
 

## SARS-CoV-2 Vaccination Status Over Time
The distribution of SARS-CoV-2 vaccination status over time is shown in . SARS-CoV-2 vaccines were first introduced in December 2020. By March 2021, 53% of DCI patients were vaccinated with at least one dose. By the end of 2023, <20% of patients were unvaccinated, whereas 23% of patients had received the updated monovalent vaccine. There were no statistically significant differences across race/ethnicity in vaccination status at years’ end ( ). For 2021, 83%-86% had 2 or more doses across race/ethnicity groups; for 2022-2023, the combined percentages of 3 or more doses or receipt of the latest vaccine ranged from 60%-68% across race/ethnicity groups.
 Compared with White patients, non-White patients tended to receive vaccines in dialysis clinics rather than in the community. In 2022, among vaccinated patients, 57% of White patients as compared with 65%-72% of non-White patients were vaccinated in a dialysis facility, whereas, among vaccinated patients in 2023, 68% of White patients as compared with 81%-86% of non-White patients were vaccinated in a dialysis facility ( ).

## Outcomes by Race/Ethnicity
All-cause and COVID-19-related hospitalization and death by race/ethnicity are shown in and and in and . In 2020, individuals identifying as White had the lowest COVID-19 hospitalization rates (0.03 per person-year) and the second lowest COVID-19-mortality (15.1 per 1,000 person-years), while those identifying as Hispanic or Other race had much higher rates. In 2021, COVID-19 associated hospitalization rates were lower than in 2020 among all race/ethnicity groups except for those identifying as White, in which hospitalization rates were similar to those observed in 2020. In 2023, hospitalization rates were much lower than in previous years across all race/ethnicity groups ( ). COVID-19–associated death rates peaked for all race/ethnicity groups in 2020, dropping substantially in 2021 for those identifying as Hispanic and Other. COVID-19–associated mortality was markedly lower among all groups in 2022 and 2023, although COVID-19–associated mortality was 2-fold higher in patients identifying as White in 2022 when compared with other race/ethnicity groups ( ). Of note, annual all-cause mortality rates and all-cause hospitalization rates were higher in 2019 than in 2022 or 2023 for all race/ethnicity subgroups ( and ).
 

## Discussion
The COVID-19 pandemic had a substantial impact on hospitalization and mortality among maintenance dialysis patients, with all-cause hospitalization rates abruptly dropping in March 2020 despite an increase in all-cause mortality, coinciding with the national lockdown when the pandemic began. Since then, hospitalization rates increased but generally remained well below prepandemic rates, whereas mortality rates, after a peak that coincided with the 2021-2022 winter season and the Omicron variant surge in late 2021, also remained below prepandemic levels. Multiple factors likely explain these patterns, including widespread availability and uptake of vaccines against SARS-CoV-2 in dialysis facilities, seasonal patterns of infectious diseases, and rigorous masking and infection prevention policies in dialysis facilities that may have also affected non–COVID-19-related hospitalization and mortality rates. In addition, COVID-19 disproportionately resulted in the deaths of the most vulnerable patients receiving dialysis and the most vulnerable patients with advanced CKD who could be anticipated to initiate dialysis in the coming years. , , This likely resulted in selecting for a healthier dialysis population in later years, as suggested by the mean number of comorbid conditions decreased over the 6-year study period.
Of importance, chief medical officers from major dialysis organizations, providing care to >97% of dialysis patients in the United States, collaborated to share best practices to manage the COVID-19 pandemic. Improvements in clinical practice initiated during the pandemic may have persisted, such as greater avoidance of hospitalizations and stricter compliance with infection prevention policies and procedures. In addition, a consortium of providers and advocates worked with the CDC and White House to enable vaccine delivery in dialysis clinics, resulting in high vaccination rates, as evident in our patient cohort, with a subsequent lower risk of severe COVID-19–related outcomes. , , , The White House vaccine allocation allowed universal access to SARS-CoV-2 vaccines across dialysis facilities and likely affected hospitalization and mortality rates as early as the spring of 2021. This allocation for dialysis providers ended in 2022, when the bivalent formula was introduced, although dialysis facilities remain able to order vaccine from commercial suppliers.
At DCI dialysis facilities, ongoing universal access to updated vaccines resulted in >60% of dialysis patients being vaccinated with 3 or more vaccine doses or the latest vaccine through the end of 2023. The proportion of DCI patients who were up to date with the latest SARS-CoV-2 vaccination far exceeded those across the US dialysis population as reported to the CDC in both December 2022 (bivalent booster uptake of 22.6% at DCI compared with 10.3% across all dialysis providers) and in December 2023 (2023-2024 monovalent booster uptake of 22.7% at DCI and 6.4% across all dialysis providers) ( ). 
Data from the National Center for Health Statistics show high COVID-19 associated mortality rates in the general population among African American (rate ratio [RR]: 1.53 vs White individuals) and Hispanic (RR: 1.56 vs White individuals) individuals, with this mortality disparity in the general population most notable during COVID-19 surges. , Among dialysis patients, this pattern is different. White dialysis patients and African American dialysis patients had similar COVID-19–related mortality rates in 2020 and 2021, whereas individuals identifying as Other race and as Hispanic ethnicity had higher mortality rates in 2020 before falling to rates lower than White and African American patients in 2021. These data are consistent with racial disparities of excess death reported during early stages of the pandemic among Medicare beneficiaries with kidney failure. 
A report evaluating Medicare patients with kidney failure from early in the pandemic speculated that the disparity in excess deaths was substantially reduced and initially reversed by the introduction of vaccines as early as by the end of January 2021 (excess deaths among Hispanic: 17.4%, African American: 19.4%, and White: 27.6%). Our study supports this conclusion, providing additional context from the prepandemic period in 2018 and 2019 and extending follow-up through 2023. Of note, whereas mortality among all racial/ethnic subgroups dropped significantly after the widespread availability of COVID-19 vaccines in 2021, there was a marked initial drop in all-cause mortality from 2020 to year-end 2021 among individuals identifying as Hispanic and as other race at least in part because of lower excess COVID-19 associated deaths.
Among the DCI maintenance dialysis population, higher proportions of non-White patients received COVID-19 vaccines in a dialysis clinic compared with their White counterparts, a finding that is consistent with previous research, suggesting that universal accessibility of care and equitable access to COVID-19 vaccines through the dialysis clinics may have improved COVID-19 protection for disproportionately affected minorities. This contrasts with the general population, where vaccine coverage was the lowest among African American and highest among Asian individuals in 2021-2022. The lower vaccination coverage among individuals identifying as African American in the general population may reflect worse access to health care and greater vaccine hesitancy, factors that in other studies are associated with unvaccinated status. In addition to providing universal access to SARS-CoV-2 vaccines, dialysis clinics likely served as a point of trusted care from an established relationship, potentially helping address vaccine hesitancy by providing education on side effects as well as framing the risks associated with COVID-19 versus the risk associated with vaccination. 
Higher all-cause hospitalization and mortality rates were observed among patients receiving in-center dialysis compared with patients receiving home dialysis throughout the study period. These differences could arise from less potential SARS-CoV-2 exposure for patients dialyzing at home relative to those traveling thrice weekly to a clinic for dialysis treatments. Of note, home dialysis patients also tended to be younger with fewer comorbid conditions, potentially improving vaccine responsiveness and therefore providing greater protection against severe COVID-19 outcomes. , 
Our study has several strengths. First, the study examined the overall impact of the pandemic, extending beyond the early pandemic era to explore the evolution of COVID-19’s impact across the vaccine era and during periods of SARS-COV-2 variant and subvariant dominance. Second, this study comprises a large, nationally distributed dialysis population with detailed follow-up, including key longitudinal outcomes. Finally, given detailed demographic and comorbid condition data, we were able to evaluate both unadjusted and adjusted hospitalization and mortality rates, including by race/ethnicity subgroups.
There are some limitations of the study. First, comorbid conditions captured at DCI may be subject to misclassification or underreporting. Nevertheless, comorbid conditions were captured from multiple sources, including the end-stage renal disease Medical Evidence form (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services 2728) and subsequent episodes of care documented in the EHR. Second, there could be potential misclassification of outcomes. However, hospitalization data capture is highly reliable among dialysis patients, particularly the ∼85% of patients receiving in-center hemodialysis, reflecting required documentation of all missed hemodialysis sessions. Furthermore, communication with home dialysis patients was supported by telemedicine and the natural tendency to maintain contact with the clinic staff, particularly in dealing with uncertainties of the pandemic era. Third, socioeconomic status was not available. However, age, sex, and race/ethnicity were included in the adjusted models. Fourth, we did not have data on the specific SARS-CoV-2 variant causing the infection and were unable to account for differences in virulence. Finally, time trends and temporal associations do not necessarily imply causation. Similar to all observational studies, the data herein provide valuable context and knowledge to inform hypothesis generation.
In conclusion, the COVID-19 pandemic impacted hospitalization and mortality rates among patients treated with maintenance dialysis, with a sudden drop in all-cause hospitalization but a marked increase in all-cause mortality at the pandemic start. Introduction of SARS-CoV-2 vaccines changed the course of hospitalization and mortality by early 2021. Although unadjusted all-cause hospitalization and mortality rates from 2018-2022 in our cohort are lower than the corresponding national rates reported by the USRDS, the trends are similar; therefore, subsequent trends in rates observed for 2023 are likely generalizable to the national dialysis population. In addition, although the hospitalization rate gradually increased after the early pandemic period, it remains at or below prepandemic levels by 2023. Persistently lower non–COVID-related mortality rates may be influenced by a combination of survival of healthier patients with CKD with fewer comorbid conditions initiating maintenance dialysis, a healthier overall dialysis population with fewer comorbid conditions after the initial pandemic period, and maintenance of industry-wide best practices that were shared and implemented during the pandemic. Availability of vaccination in the dialysis clinics likely reduced racial disparity in COVID-19 mortality. The continued vulnerability of patients on maintenance dialysis to severe COVID-19 outcomes highlights the need to maintain universal access to vaccines at their most convenient venue while promoting continuous updated vaccine status to limit COVID-19–related hospitalization and death in this high-risk population.
