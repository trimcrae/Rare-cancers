## Highlights
•This study offers insights to enable Radiation Therapists’ research and sustainability.
•The total publication output among the three centres was 445 over the 10 years.
•Multidisciplinary collaboration was common in all three centres.
•Radiation Therapists published most often in profession-specific journals.
•Capacity-building strategies improved Radiation Therapists’ research engagement.
Keywords: Radiation therapists, Radiotherapy, Research, Evidence-based practice, Education

## Abstract

## Purpose
Radiation Therapist (RTT) research culture is essential for driving innovation and informing evidence-based practice. This study aimed to assess RTT research output and institutional capacity-building initiatives across international clinical academic cancer centres.

## Methods
This was a retrospective analysis of RTT research activities and capacity-building initiatives from 2013 to 2022 at three centres located in Canada (CA), the Netherlands (NL) and the United Kingdom (UK). Data was collected on research output by identifying all RTT author publications (first, second, or senior author). Institutional capacity-building initiatives were captured from each centre and described using full-time equivalents (FTEs). A qualitative analysis was conducted on all RTT publications to identify common research topics.

## Results
Over the 10 years, the total number of RTT-authored publications was 445 across the centres (CA:291; UK:79; NL:75). RTTs as first authors ranged from 14.7 % to 44.3 % and RTTs as senior authors ranged from 0 % to 27.8 % of publications. Centres with increasing FTEs demonstrated increasing research productivity, with publications changing from 21 to 34 in CA and from 3 to 15 in the UK centre. Multidisciplinary collaboration was common among all centres. Prominent RTT research themes included technological applications, RTT professional development and quality assurance, clinical outcomes, dosimetry, and patient care. Common strategies to build research capacity included educational initiatives, the creation of dedicated research roles, and promoting research dissemination.

## Conclusion
This study highlighted the contributions of RTTs to radiation oncology research and how a comprehensive approach to building research capacity results in high RTT research output and collaboration.

## Introduction
Research is essential for informing evidence-based radiation therapy practice and drives innovation [ ]. Historically, Radiation Therapists (RTTs) have often been perceived as lacking a strong research culture compared to other healthcare professionals, but RTTs worldwide are increasingly willing to participate in and lead research [ , , , , ]. RTTs have a unique perspective in multidisciplinary radiation therapy research, making significant contributions to the overall quality of care [ , ]. Many recognize the potential of participating in research to improve job satisfaction and career growth [ ]. Moreover, some RTTs are branching out from traditional clinical roles to pursue advanced practice or clinical academic career paths [ , ].
As a result, this shift from primarily clinical responsibilities to engagement in research highlights the need for effective research engagement strategies, which are not a one-size-fits-all model [ ]. A diverse approach is needed to engage RTTs in radiation therapy research studies [ ]. RTTs also have evolving research interests, with technological advancements being a prominent RTT research topic in recent years [ , ]. Therefore, to enhance RTT research capacity and interest, radiation therapy centres should implement targeted strategies. For example, a single-centre case study at a Canadian radiotherapy department documented multiple programmatic changes, including educational initiatives, infrastructure support, and research dissemination activities that successfully increased the quantity and quality of RTT research output [ ].
While individual institutions have reported success, how initiatives impact RTT research capacity and output internationally remains unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate RTT research output alongside institutional capacity-building strategies across international clinical academic cancer centres.

## Methods
A retrospective analysis of RTT research activities and capacity-building initiatives was conducted across three clinical academic cancer centres located in Canada (CA), the Netherlands (NL), and the United Kingdom (UK) from 2013 to 2022. This time period was chosen to capture a decade of RTT publications and to align with the availability of institutional records across all three centres. This study used a mixed-methods design, combining quantitative analysis of research outputs with qualitative assessment of research themes and institutional initiatives. This study was limited to the collection and analysis of data from publicly available, peer-reviewed publications and did not involve the use of identifiable or direct human participant data. Following the Tri-Council Policy Statement (TCPS 2), no formal research ethics board approval was required, and Radiation Therapy department leadership was informed, or institutional approval was obtained at all three participating cancer centres [ ].

## Data collection

## Research output Metrics
Data were collected on research output using a structured PubMed search from January 1, 2013, to December 31, 2022, to identify all RTT author publications at each of the three participating clinical academic cancer centres. The search strategy used institution-specific terms within the RTT profession to ensure all relevant publications were identified. RTTs were identified by professional designation (i.e., MRT(T), RTT or institutional role) as listed in the publication or institutional staff database. Additionally, an internal publication record database was searched to identify any publications not indicated in PubMed. For each RTT author publication, data were collected on publication title, publication year, journal name, authorship positions held by an RTT (first, second, senior or co-author), presence of multidisciplinary collaboration (Radiation Oncologists (ROs), Medical/Research Physicists (M/RPs), and multicentre collaboration (authors from other centres)).

## Institutional capacity-building initiatives
Institutional capacity-building initiatives designed to enhance RTT capacity and output were collected from internal reviews conducted by the RTT research leads or senior research RTTs of each of the three clinical academic cancer centres. Contributing programmatic features were described using RTT research full-time equivalents (FTEs) and the presence of institutional strategies to enhance RTT research capacity (such as access to training, continued education, research roles and development opportunities) [ ]. The information was validated through senior leadership at each centre to ensure accuracy and comprehensiveness.

## Data analysis
Quantitative data analysis of RTT research output was performed for the ten-year period. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the total number of publications with RTTs on the author list, the RTT authorship roles, and collaborative authorship. Trends were plotted to visualize changes in RTT research output over time and across the three centres. Appropriate inferential statistics (i.e., Spearman’s correlation) were used to examine associations between the number of RTT research FTEs and the total number of RTT publications. A chi-square test was conducted to assess all RTT-authored publications across the three centres and differences in research journal subject categories between the periods of 2013–2017 and 2018–2022. P-values were reported, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05 using R Studio (Version 2024.12.1 + 563).
Institutional capacity-building initiatives were analyzed retrospectively through descriptions provided by each department’s RTT research leaders or senior leaders using internal reports, which are not publicly available. They were analyzed thematically to identify key strategies, categorizing them according to their primary purpose, as described in Rosewall et al.'s previously used framework [ ].
A qualitative analysis was conducted on each RTT publication to identify common RTT research topics and research journal subject categories. NVivo (Version 15) was used to identify the most prevalent RTT research themes. Inductive open coding was used to identify emerging RTT research topics without applying predefined categories. Followed by axial coding, a process that connected the initial codes to overarching core RTT research themes. One researcher independently conducted the coding, and after coding the first set of publications, the emerging codes were reviewed with a second researcher experienced in qualitative methods. The remaining publications were then coded, and final themes were reviewed to ensure consistency, validity and reproducibility.

## Results

## Research output metrics
Across the three centres, the total RTT publication output was 445 over the 10‐year period. The total number of publications with RTT(s) in the author list for the CA centre was 291, the UK centre was 79, and the NL centre was 75 (See ). An analysis of authorship roles revealed that RTTs were first authors on 41.6 % of publications in CA, 44.3 % in the UK and 14.7 % in the NL. Senior authors (last authors) varied widely across the three centres, with RTTs holding this position in 17.9 % of publications in CA, 27.8 % in the UK and 0 % in the NL. While the percentage of RTTs listed as second authors was 30.6 % of publications in CA, 19.0 % in the UK and 29.3 % in the NL. Multidisciplinary collaboration was common, with all centres having an RO, M/RPs or RTT as collaborators. Multicentre authorship was prevalent in all centres, accounting for approximately a quarter of RTT publications from each centre, 27.1 % in CA, 24.1 % in the UK and 28.0 % in the NL (See ).
 Across the three centres, RTTs listed as first, second or senior authors published most often in RTT profession-specific journals (See ). Examples of RTT profession-specific journals included the Journal of Medical Imaging and Radiation Sciences (2.0 Impact Factor), Technical Innovations & Patient Support in Radiation Oncology (2.8 Impact Factor), and Radiography (2.8 Impact Factor). RTTs were most frequently listed as co-authors in publications appearing in radiation oncology journals (i.e., International Journal of Radiation Oncology, Biology, Physics 6.5 Impact Factor), general oncology journals (i.e., Frontiers in Oncology 3.3 Impact Factor), general medical research journals (BMJ Open 2.3 Impact Factor), and physics journals (i.e., International Journal of Medical Physics Research and Practice 3.2 Impact Factor). There was a statistically significant difference in the distribution of research journal subject categories between the periods 2013–2017 and 2018–2022 (p = 0.0016). RTT-authored publications increased from 41 to 68 in profession-specific journals, 6 to 23 in physics journals, 75 to 84 in radiation oncology journals, and 37 to 52 in oncology journals. Publications decreased from 37 to 22 in general medical research journals over the same time period.
 Over the 10-year period, the change in research RTT FTEs was 3 to 5.4 in the CA centre, 1 to 2.5 in the UK centre, and 2 to 1.6 in the NL centre. The annual average number of publications per research RTT FTE was 4, 4, and 2 for the CA, the UK, and the NL centres, respectively. There was a strong positive association between research RTT FTE and the annual number of publications ( rho = 0.74; p = 0.014). Centres with increasing FTEs had increased research output, with the number of RTT publications increasing from 21 in 2013 to 34 in 2022 in the CA centre and from 3 to 15 in the UK centre.
The thematic analysis of the RTT publication titles identified patterns and common RTT research themes. The analysis revealed five common themes: (1) technological applications, (2) RTT professional development and quality standards, (3) clinical outcomes and toxicity, (4) treatment planning and dosimetry and (5) patient care (See ). The most prevalent research theme within each centre was identified based on the proportion of total coded segments. Clinical outcomes and toxicity were the most common RTT research themes at the CA centre, representing 33.4 % of the total codes. This theme consisted of the subcodes survival analysis and treatment-related toxicity profiles. Technological applications were the most common theme at the NL and the UK centres, accounting for 44.4 % and 44.8 % of their coded publications, respectively. Magnetic resonance (MR)-guided radiation therapy, adaptive radiation therapy, image guidance systems, cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), and intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) applications and implementations comprised the technological applications theme. Treatment planning and dosimetry were the fourth prevalent theme, and patient care was the least represented theme across all three centres (see and ).
 

## Institutional capacity-building initiatives
Research institutional capacity-building initiatives were identified retrospectively by RTT research leaders at the three centres and classified using a previously described framework [ ].
1.Initiatives to “Provide Structure and Infrastructure to Sustain Research” included establishing dedicated roles and funding for research RTTs, clinician-scientist RTTs, advanced-practice RTTs positions. Journal clubs were used to increase RTT research awareness. Additionally, attendance at research conferences, workshops, and seminars was encouraged to build research knowledge and engagement in research.
2.Initiatives to “Build Research Knowledge and Skills” included implementing an RTT research mentorship program where RTTs with different experience levels would pair with an experienced RTT researcher to support and gather the knowledge and skills necessary for independent research. Centres also supported RTTs pursuing post-certification degrees (Master's and Doctoral degrees) by offering study time and tuition fee support. Additionally, centres developed a collaborative network with multidisciplinary healthcare professionals to share radiation oncology research and offer RTTs opportunities to participate in various research projects.
3.Initiatives to “Promote Dissemination of Research Findings and Develop a Network Between Researchers” included implementing internal research days featuring oral presentations by faculty or trainees conducting research in radiation oncology and keynote lectures on relevant research topics. Collaborative initiatives to support RTTs included writing groups where RTTs would come together to improve their writing, provide feedback, and support colleagues' work. Additionally, the three centres provided financial support to RTTs to ensure sustainable assistance. Financial support was through peer-reviewed research grants or departmental funding and was used to backfill for RTT research time, software or conference fees. Additionally, various methods were used to promote researcher activities, including department-wide emails congratulating RTT presentations, abstracts, or publications. The centres promoted research achievements by offering RTTs the opportunity to present their work to other RTTs in the departments and embedding research responsibilities into specific RTT job roles.

## Discussion
This retrospective analysis of RTT research activities and capacity-building initiatives from 2013 to 2022 across three clinical academic cancer centres identified common factors such as authorship role, research themes, and institutional capacity-building initiatives that were associated with RTT research output.
An important finding of this study was the various roles RTTs have in research, including first, second, and senior authorship positions, which are comparable to those in other RTT studies [ , , ]. Additionally, the analysis revealed that profession-specific journals were the primary venue for RTTs. In all three centres, RTTs were more likely to be first or senior authors in an RTT profession-specific journal, in contrast to their more collaborative or supporting authorship roles in broader oncology, physics, or general medical research journals. This may also reflect the supportive environment of RTT profession-specific journals, which prioritize RTT-led research and facilitate the inclusion of RTT colleagues as authors. These results align with previous reports in allied health research, highlighting the significance of dedicated platforms and leadership roles in enhancing capacity and research culture within the profession [ ].
The NL and UK centres had an M/RP collaborator on 100 % and 67.1 % of their RTT research publications, respectively. The technical role of the M/RP aligns with the themes of MR-guided adaptive radiation therapy and image guidance systems, which were the most prevalent research topics in the two centres. This was also reflected in the overall thematic analysis across the three centres, where the collaboration of M/RPs was highest in the publication themes of “Technological Applications” and “Treatment Planning & Dosimetry”. In contrast, the CA centre had a lower percentage of M/RP collaborators, which corresponded to a lower focus on these themes. Instead, RTT research publications at the CA centre focused on various themes such as survival analysis, advanced RTT roles, and quality improvement.
The study also highlighted the growing integration of RTTs across various centres through multicentre collaborations, a finding consistent with other studies [ ]. Similarly, Halkett et al., found that 14 % of RTTs in Australia have been involved in international research studies and 28 % in national research studies, underlining the growing number of multicentre publications for RTTs [ ]. This integration is likely facilitated by national and international collaborative initiatives, such as the European Society for Radiotherapy and Oncology (ESTRO) international RTT committee, which enhances collaboration and knowledge exchange among RTT researchers worldwide [ ].
Furthermore, the thematic analysis revealed that patient care was the least common RTT research theme across the three centres. While published literature has reported RTT-led research in patient care, our findings suggest that differences in centre priorities and research infrastructure may influence the research focus in this study [ , ]. Additionally, this may reflect that, although the RTT role is rooted in patient care, the clinical setting and competency profile often emphasize the application of technology [ ]. RTTs were frequently first or senior authors on research focused on the themes of “RTT Professional Development & Quality Standards” and “Technological Applications.” This pattern of authorship could suggest that RTTs are driving research in areas closely aligned with their professional development and technological expertise, representing their expanding role [ ]. This trend is further reflected in the research journal subject categories data, where publications authored by RTTs significantly increased in physics and profession-specific journals when comparing the periods 2013–2017 and 2018–2022. The comparatively low RTT authorship leadership in “Patient Care” research may indicate a potential gap between research focus and clinical practice priorities. Understanding how to bridge technology with patient care and RTT research is essential, as highlighted by an ESTRO group of international RTTs [ ].
The distribution of ROs and M/RPs collaborators across research themes highlights their discipline-specific expertise. They collaborated with RTTs significantly with “Technological Applications” and “Clinical Outcomes & Toxicity” research. In contrast, “RTT Professional Development & Quality Standards” involved fewer ROs and M/RPs as collaborators, which suggests that RTT authors are less likely to seek out collaborators from other professions when evaluating those topics.
Contributing to the research roles of RTTs were capacity-building initiatives across the centres, demonstrated by increased multidisciplinary collaborations. ROs were frequent collaborators in RTT publications among the three centres, emphasizing their role in supporting RTT-driven research. Furthermore, this may speak to the role of an RTT clinician-investigator research lead at these centres, who actively involves the RTTs as multidisciplinary team members. Supporting this conclusion about the contributions of an RTT research lead is a study by Hanlon et al., who highlighted the impact of RTT research leadership to achieve a higher level of research activity [ ].
Historically, RTTs have had limited involvement in research, with some indicating that they feel their access to research opportunities and role is more of a collaborator than an initiator [ , ]. An Italian study on RTT’s involvement in research showed that 38.6 % of RTTs did not feel supported by other healthcare professionals in their research teams [ ]. A UK survey on radiographer research roles found that some individuals perceived inequities in access to research opportunities compared to other healthcare professionals, emphasizing the importance of targeted capacity-building initiatives [ ]. To enable RTTs as researchers, the centres in this study implemented multiple initiatives to encourage and support RTTs as researchers, such as journal clubs, dedicated research time, and mentorship programs [ , , ]. Collectively, they contributed to an RTT research culture conducive to increased research output and sustainability [ ].
The primary limitation of this study is its retrospective design. Due to the nature and timeline of publishing a peer-reviewed manuscript, it is hard to draw a direct cause-and-effect relationship between a specific initiative and an increase in publication output. Furthermore, research by RTTs may only be presented at conferences, seminars, or lectures and might not be published. This study measured RTT output solely through peer-reviewed manuscript publications, which could have led to an underestimation of the total research output. In addition, institutional databases and PubMed were used to identify RTT publications. However, they were not cross-checked with each other, which may have led to a risk of some publications being missed. Institutional capacity-building initiatives were also grouped together into categories to avoid contrasting single-centre characteristics, which limits the ability to draw direct conclusions on how the initiatives were successful. Moreover, because initiatives were implemented concurrently, this might introduce confounding variables, making it challenging to identify which initiatives had the greatest impact on research output. Future studies should investigate the longitudinal effects of specific institutional capacity-building strategies to more fully understand which initiatives result in the greatest academic benefits.
Despite these limitations, this study's strength is its multicentre collaboration. Many centres develop and implement capacity-building strategies, but because these are often not shared, the translation of these initiatives into other centres is slowed [ ]. The findings from this study showcase collaboration among three international clinical academic cancer centres to quantify RTT research and describe the initiatives undertaken over a decade to encourage RTTs as researchers.

## Conclusions
This study highlighted international RTT contributions to the growing body of evidence-based research in radiation oncology. RTTs are increasingly participating in research with common factors driving RTT research output, including RTT research leadership, mentorship, dedicated research time, multidisciplinary and multicentre collaboration. These three high-performing centres demonstrate how a comprehensive approach to building research capacity results in high RTT research output, productivity, and collaboration.
