## Background
In Miami-Dade County Florida (Miami), the rate of newly diagnosed HIV per 100,000 population is four times the national rate with a large majority of transmission due to male-to-male sex. Latinx men are prescribed HIV preventative medication (PrEP) at a lower rate than White individuals; as such, efforts are being made by community health organizations to engage Latinx men who have sex with men (LMSM) in PrEP promotion programs. The objective of this study is to characterize the features, strengths, and weaknesses of the implementation of a PrEP promotion program (DiversiPrEP) at three community based organization (CBO) locations across Miami-Dade and Broward Counties.

## Methods
All 30 staff members were invited via e-mail to participate; 11 staff participated in semi-structured interviews were conducted by two authors to assess implementation of the DiversiPrEP program with questions mapped to the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR). We used directed content analysis to analyze qualitative data.

## Results
All staff members self-identified as male and 82% (9 of the 11) identified as Hispanic/Latino. Facilitators to providing PrEP services included: 1) individualized counseling for the PrEP process; 2) providing inviting community-focused spaces with staff who reflect the demographics of the priority population; and 3) hiring non-medical staff that bridge the gap as a translator of both medical and administrative issues. Negative influences on implementation included: 1) federal funding restrictions on lab and testing costs that hinder the ability of the CBO to provide no-cost testing to clients; and 2) finding ways to advertise services to LMSM that may not already be familiar with the CBO.

## Conclusions
CBO administrative staff provide actionable policy and implementation recommendations for offering culturally tailored PrEP services in other LMSM communities. CBO can reduce PrEP service barriers by acting as intermediaries for navigation of the healthcare system and deferring costs to the clients through federal and state funding programs.

## Background
In the most recent nationwide estimates provided by the Centers for Disease Control in 2022, gay, bisexual, and other men who reported male-to-male sexual contact represented 67% of all new estimated HIV infections and accounted for 83% of infections among all men [ ]. In 2019, The US Department of Health and Human Services developed the Ending the HIV Epidemic in the US (EHE) initiative with an aim to reduce new HIV infections in the US by 75% by 2025, with a particular emphasis on the counties and states that have the highest estimated rates of HIV transmission — one of the top counties for HIV incidence in adults is Miami-Dade, Florida [ ]. To contextualize Miami-Dade County’s HIV epidemic issue, the rate of new HIV diagnoses per 100,000 people nationally in 2021 (the most recent national statistics reported) was 13; in Miami-Dade County in 2023, the rate was 37.6 [ , ].
To reduce the rate of HIV diagnoses, Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is a drug that can be taken daily in oral form or provided via an intramuscular first injection, a second injection one month later, then injections every two months thereafter provided by a physician [ ]. When taken as prescribed, PrEP reduces HIV transmission from sex by up to 99% [ ]. Despite the U.S Food and Drug Administration’s (FDA) approval of oral PrEP over a decade ago, gaps in access persist for those at highest risk of HIV, especially Latinx/Hispanic subgroups [ , ]. From 2012 to 2021, PrEP use increased among Americans, but PrEP equity for Black and Hispanic Americans decreased [ ]. Although this issue is widely known among infectious disease clinicians and researchers, implementation science research is needed to understand the reasons for these gaps in PrEP access, initiation, and persistence among LMSM in counties similar to Miami-Dade in demographics, where 69% of the population identify as Hispanic/Latinx [ ].
Prior work highlights significant unmet needs of PrEP programs for Latino men who have sex with men (LMSM) including limited PrEP awareness, inadequate healthcare access, financial constraints, and stigma. To address these barriers specific to LMSM, targeted strategies such as DiversiPrEP, a culturally tailored intervention to promote PrEP initiation, are essential to reduce disparities in HIV prevention within this population [ ]. In the US South, lack of Medicaid coverage, PrEP stigma, low health literacy, low access to available PrEP providers, and low perceived HIV risk have been barriers to implementation of PrEP programs that meet the needs of the most vulnerable populations to HIV infection [ ]. In Miami, a PrEP demo project that provided anticipatory counseling and resources for PrEP still had a significant portion of participants report that they were unable to access PrEP from community sources, and were more likely to be non-White and/or Latino [ ]. More specifically for LMSM, access to Spanish-speaking and/or Latino providers, stigma discussing sex with providers, and navigating cost-sharing and insurance have been implementation barriers that influenced injectable and oral PrEP uptake programs [ – ].
Given the increased prioritization of the use of implementation science in the field of HIV, several determinant, process, and evaluative frameworks have been employed to better understand and evaluate factors related to the success of programming and innovation implementation [ , ]. One commonly cited framework is the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), a determinants framework that incorporates 39 theory-based constructs organized across five general domains of Innovation, Outer Setting, Inner Setting, Individuals, and Implementation Process [ , ]. However, few studies have focused on the evaluation of PrEP-related prevention programs and implementation strategies in practice [ , ].
In a 2022 systematic review, fewer than 10% of studies that assessed determinants of PrEP implementation measured structural and logistical domains within CFIR [ ]; thus, expansion within the nascent field of implementation science could be to study the effect of new contexts such as pharmacies and community health centers (i.e., structural and logistical domains) on improving PrEP initiation. Since the publication of the systematic review, one study noted that LMSM identified structural barriers of public transportation, locations of community clinics that provide PrEP, and the resources of time and money needed for regular appointments, testing, and medication [ ]. However, understanding the logistical domain would likely require the PrEP providers to be interviewed to understand the barriers that LMSM may not be aware of that exist before and after their appointment at a community clinic.
In addition to assessing the determinants of PrEP implementation, researchers must identify and operationalize specific strategies to overcome context-specific barriers and leverage facilitators of implementation. The Expert Recommendations for Implementation Change (ERIC) project was developed from an interdisciplinary need to update and systematically organize implementation strategy terms, definitions, and categories [ ]. An expert panel consisting of implementation scientists and mental health clinical managers reached a final consensus of 73 implementation strategies [ ]. Through a mapping process, ERIC strategies can be linked to specific CFIR determinants [ ]. Furthermore, the development of ERIC strategies and definitions provides a consistent agreed-upon benchmark so that other researchers can expand on existing implementation studies; an issue that complicates future replication of behavior change interventions [ ]. Thus, using CFIR combined with ERIC strategies allows for the possibility of tailoring or expanding on PrEP initiation studies with LMSM communities that may exist outside of the Miami-Dade County.
To address the disparity of PrEP uptake in Miami-Dade County among LMSM, the senior author and a CBO developed the DiversiPrEP program, a culturally tailored PrEP program that represents five ERIC strategies: increase demand, promote adaptability, alter client fees, intervene with clients to enhance uptake and adherence, and tailor strategies [ ]. Figure  shows the process of how a client at the CBO begins the program with an HIV test (yellow box), and their positive or negative status determines the treatment or services they will receive to the right and left, respectively. DiversiSafe, an intervention nested within the DiversiPrEP program, has been offered at all the community partner’s health clinic sites since 2020 and provides an explanation of choices for participants to either stay HIV-negative or to live a healthier and safer life with HIV. Those with a nonreactive HIV test result would be enrolled in the DiversiSafe intervention to introduce risk reduction strategies that they can choose from to prevent the acquisition of HIV, including PrEP, Treatment as Prevention, Negotiated Safety, condom use, and emergency nonoccupational postexposure prophylaxis (nPEP). Bilingual counselors that work at a community health clinic, most of whom are immigrants to the US or who are born to immigrant parents, provide a brief overview of each strategy. If a client decides that PrEP is a viable strategy, they will then receive PrEP services, which include personalized PrEP navigation services, no-cost clinical and laboratory testing visits, adherence coaching provided via telemedicine, and tailored PrEP delivery. Our evaluation of the DiversiPrEP program was performed concurrently with its implementation and is the focus of this manuscript. 
The purpose of this study was to conduct qualitative semi-structured interviews with staff members of a CBO that implemented a culturally tailored HIV prevention program (DiversiPrEP) to characterize the strengths and weaknesses. The semi-structured interview guide was created using the CFIR implementation framework linked with ERIC strategies to understand the barriers and facilitators to implementing this program in a community of LMSM. The following manuscript was written to achieve the objectives of: 1) highlighting the lived experiences of CBO employees and their views on the implementation of health outreach; 2) evaluating the effectiveness of culturally tailored aspects of this HIV prevention program (DiversiPrEP) and the nested intervention (DiversiSafe) in South Florida for the LMSM community; and 3) providing future directions and evidence for other CBOs that are interested in the development and implementation of HIV prevention programs.

## Methods
Eleven qualitative interviews were completed with community clinic staff at various levels of a CBO employed across three locations in Miami-Dade and Broward Counties whose focus was on improving sexual health in the community, especially for LMSM. Invitations to participate were sent via email in June 2021 within the CBO to 30 employees at all levels of management who had implemented the DiversiSafe intervention — this included Outreach Coordinators, Case Managers and PrEP Navigators from each location as well as the Executive Director of the organization. Those who were interested and willing to participate in the study were included and completed one interview with study staff with no repeat interviews conducted (n = 11); thus, convenience sampling was used. The remaining 19 employees did not respond to the emails from study staff. All staff members self-identified as cisgender male and 82% (9 of the 11) identified as Hispanic/Latino.
All participants provided written consent electronically prior to study entry. The semi-structured interview guide was created through an iterative process that began with the CFIRguide.org Interview Guide Tool [ ]. The study team worked in collaboration with the San Diego Center for AIDS Research Implementation Science Hub (SD CFAR IS Hub) to identify the most relevant constructs for the DiversiPrEP project over the course of three months, with regularly scheduled meetings. The SD IS Hub was assigned to our team by the ISCI consortium as part of the Ending the HIV Epidemic (EHE) supplement process. Experts from the SD CFAR IS Hub provided critical feedback on 1) the inclusion of constructs to appropriately respond to project aims and 2) the mapping of the constructs to the questions included in the semi-structured interview guide.

## Interview guide development
The interview guide was produced from the CFIR Guide website and was further adapted to the DiversiPrEP project by first adding open-ended questions to help establish rapport and build background information about the interviewees. Then, the study authors altered the language of these questions to provide clarity for interviewees on the constructs and ensure that the questions were tailored to inquire about specific aspects of the HIV prevention intervention. The interview guide was designed to investigate the influence of covers general contextual information about the interviewee, knowledge of the landscape of HIV prevention in the geographical region, characteristics of the intervention that could be adapted or had been adapted, perspectives on the population receiving the intervention, the environment within the CBO, and external policy implications related to the CBO’s ability to implement the intervention. These adaptations were decided upon in a series of monthly meetings with the study team and SD CFAR IS Hub consultants. Once a final, semi-structured interview guide was produced, all questions were remapped to the CFIR constructs, ensuring that the language changes and adaptations made had not changed the understanding or meaning. We then used the CFIR-ERIC matching tool to match the ERIC strategies to the CFIR constructs included in the semi-structured interview guide in line with previous approaches to matching CFIR and ERIC strategies in qualitative implementation research methods [ ]. The final semi-structured interview guide can be found as Supplement 2.

## Recruitment, enrollment, and interview process
All staff members were sent a recruitment e-mail (Supplement 1) on April 2, 2021 which marks the beginning of the recruitment period. Interested staff members e-mailed our study and were asked if they had worked at the community organization for at least 6 months. Qualitative interviews lasting 45–90 min were conducted by either the first and second authors of this manuscript and were audio recorded via Zoom videoconference between June 2021 and October 2021. Interviews were conducted in a password protected Zoom room and only the interviewer and the CBO employee were present during the interview. CBO employees were asked if they were in a private room prior to beginning the interview; additionally, employees were given the option to have their video cameras turned off during the session. Participants were not paid for their participation but were given up to two hours of their existing paid work time to dedicate to this interview if they opted into the study voluntarily. The Zoom platform managed by [Institution] adheres to the highest level of HIPAA compliance and all data collected were password protected. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the [Blinded University].
The study team for this project had an existing relationship with the CBO that had been established by the senior author, who has worked with this community partner consistently for the past seven years. Both interviewers, one male and one female, were graduate students pursuing doctoral education at the time of the interviews and had previous formal education and training in quantitative and qualitative methods, including conducting semi-structured interviews. All interviews, except one, were conducted in English. One interview conducted in Spanish was translated by a native Spanish speaker on the university research team who did not participate in the interviews.
The CBO staff and administration were aware of the professional goals of both researchers and their interest in being involved with this DiversiPrEP project as the DiversiSafe intervention was developed by the Executive Director of the CBO and the senior author. As part of ensuring anonymity, the authors of this manuscript have attributed each quote in this manuscript to a participant number to distinguish them. However, given the unique titles for each position and the limited number of managerial roles, we did not feel comfortable identifying the interviewees with each of the quotes with regards to their position title or location within the CBO. The roles of the interviewees varied from outreach coordinators that managed public events to advertise CBO services and provide HIV prevention information, patient navigators that act as a point of contact from the initial visit with community members from testing to insurance coverage to providing clarification or information regarding HIV prevention methods, and programs coordinators that oversee each CBO branch and coordinate staffing of outreach and medical appointments, communication between executive staff and branch staff, and gauging patient needs specific to the demographic served at each location.
Interviews were automatically transcribed by Zoom software and then cleaned by the respective interviewer conducting the interview while listening back to the audio recording. The research team utilized a triangulation approach to coding for content analysis. Both interviewers reviewed the 11 transcripts and coded them independently using Microsoft Excel to assign each CFIR construct a score within a -2 to + 2 scale that measures the negative or positive influence of the construct on how the DiversiPrEP program was implemented, as developed by Damschroder et al. [ ]. Damschroder et al.’s article provides a table with criteria for each number on the scale that assesses based on valence and strength of influence. Valence refers to the influence that the subconstruct has on the implementation process of the intervention being studied. Strength of rating refers to the use of concrete examples and strength of language in quotes provided by participants. For example, a score of -2 means that the construct acts as an impediment to work processes and most interviewees (at least two individuals) describe explicit examples of how the construct affects the intervention negatively. A score of -1 is assigned when interviewees make general statements about the construct impeding the implementation of the intervention but do not provide concrete examples. A score of 0 is assigned when the construct has a neutral effect on the intervention or there are both positive and negative influences cited by interviewees at different levels of the organization that balance each other out. The + 1 and + 2 scores have the same criteria as the negative scores but with positive influence that facilitates the implementation of the intervention. Scores for each interview’s constructs were compared between the two coders, resulting in 79% agreement. Any scores that differed were then discussed by both coders, and a final score was agreed upon by consensus.
This study completed data validation similar to that of Damschroder et al. for coding [ ]. Once consensus was achieved for all individual construct scores for each interview, one of the authors acted as an auditor (i.e., a qualitative expert not integrally involved in data collection and did not have prior relationships with any of the CBO staff) and reviewed all codes to provide a neutral check that both scores given for each subconstruct accurately reflected the data presented and that quotes were in support of the scores. The auditor received both sets of scores and quotes chosen by each coder and provided written feedback for scores that differed. Any discrepancy between the auditor and the coders was then discussed between all three members and ratings were adapted by consensus.

## Results
We created a matrix that listed the ratings for each of the pre-mapped CFIR constructs of intervention characteristics, outer setting, and inner setting (Supplement 2). Within these CFIR constructs, we assessed the sub-constructs of Relative Advantage, Adaptability, Patient Needs and Resources, Structural Characteristics, Implementation Climate, and External Policies and Incentives. Overall, Relative Advantage, Patient Needs and Resources, and Implementation Climate were seen as positive influences on DiversiPrEP program implementation. Adaptability, Structural Characteristics were seen as negative influences that hindered implementation. Networks and Communication provided mixed responses and was decided as a neutral influence based on rating between the two coders and the outside auditor. We did not include our rating for External Policies and Incentives given that the CBO Executive Director was the only managerial staff that participated and therefore we did not have sufficient evidence to decide on the trend among the three branches of the CBO, but we provide our qualitative findings below. Ratings for each location on the + 2/-2 scale are provided in Supplement 3.

## Relative Advantage
The construct of Intervention Characteristics aims to assess aspects of an intervention that impact implementation success and includes two sub-constructs: relative advantage and adaptability. The sub-construct of Relative Advantage is the stakeholders’ perception of the advantage of implementing the intervention versus an alternative solution. Participants identified that the CBO’s main advantage over state health outreach programs and other programs available in the community was the no-cost HIV/STI testing offered to clients. This was a main priority for the CBO because it reduced the initial barrier that most clients voiced about being underinsured or uninsured and worrying about costs for testing, thus preventing them from seeking testing altogether. The CBO Executive Director described the situation in the quote below:Many of them [other CBOs] started out saying that they wouldn't charge anything and that they just wanted to help the community and get as many people enrolled in PrEP as possible, and then they would notice a drop off in clients we were sending to those clinics [when] they quietly started instituting fees. So the first thing that was instituted was lab fees and that's typically, I would say the median is $70, some of them are $90 per lab visit and, of course, you have to do these every three months so that adds up. And then, some of the [other organizations] that were saying that they were going to do the most free services, then start charging office visit fees and that's typically $90. So that's easily $160 for somebody every three months [and] that's a pretty significant expense for many people. We don’t charge for anything.

Additionally, other participants identified that their clients complained of cost and backlog to receive test results at other CBOs, further clarifying how difficult it could be for LMSM in Miami to enroll in a community-based PrEP program and adhere to the CDC clinical practice guidelines for continued PrEP care (i.e., regular HIV testing) [ ].
Another aspect of Relative Advantage identified by multiple participants was that the CBO served as a one-stop shop for testing, medication trial packs, and navigating the insurance process after the initial visit. In the DiversiPrEP model, an individual care coordinator is assigned to each client and is their contact point if they have any questions or issues with setting up testing appointments, getting a trial bottle of PrEP, coordinating pharmacy follow-up to begin PrEP prescription, or resolving insurance copays to continue with PrEP refills as needed. As one interviewee (identified in this manuscript as Participant 1) stated,We [care coordinators] are trying to get you on PrEP, and it’s not necessarily just a nurse or a nurse assistant that has a high volume of clients, and they have other needs to attend to. So, the fact that PrEP is a priority for the organization is kind of a good differentiator because the counselors are looking to navigate you into successfully filling out your prescription.

By assigning an individual care coordinator whose focus is specifically on helping the client navigate their individual healthcare situation, the client can expect a familiar face to answer any questions and coordinate with other providers that the client may not be familiar with. Another interviewee, Participant 2, explained this process for the client:I mean, I believe, like we have like a more friendly approach, as our motto is like to welcome everybody for a safe space, so it's not just like going to a doctor's visit. So, people feel comfortable just coming in and it's more quick than other places, [there's] less protocol than other places. For example, at [some other organizations], you would have to go to registration and then to another floor to wait your turn in their clinic and then the provider sees the client and then the lab assistant sees the client and then the PrEP Navigator sees the client and then the cashier. So for us over here, the same counselor is the one that will do the counseling, the testing, and, you know, the enrollment for the client so [the client] is not passed around for a lot of different aspects of the service.

## Adaptability
The sub-construct of adaptability is the degree to which an intervention can be adapted, tailored, refined, or reinvented to meet the client’s needs in the context of their community. For this study, participants identified the expansion of integrated technology as an area that would make PrEP service more effective, such as automating text reminders to be sent for clients’ clinical appointments and pharmacy refill requirements. Many participants generally stated that the client’s ability to pick up prescriptions and attend clinic appointments were two of the major areas where PrEP implementation was difficult, and that clinic staff often needed to spend extra time re-engaging with clients to establish care. This sub-construct was rated as a negative influence on the program’s implementation. As Participant 5 stated, navigating the technology and being attentive to communication can be difficult and hinder clients at various stages in between getting tested at the CBO and receiving their PrEP prescription:Like, for example, their pharmacy sends an automated text notifying the client that their medications are ready. But some pharmacies are like 'if they don't answer the phone we don't we don't dispense them their medication' so that also affects [the process] because sometimes the clients say ‘oh, my medication isn't ready, my pharmacy never has it ready on time, or I run out of medication, I don't want to be out of medication’.

Participant 5 stated that ideally there would be automated pharmacy communication through website for PrEP ordering instead of requiring clients to answer phone calls. He also underlined the differences in coordinating with pharmacy chain communication protocols compared to the CBO (i.e., the ability to text in addition to calling for following up on medication refils, ordering, etc.), especially for medication home delivery requirements.
Participant 1 noted that the process may be difficult for the client due to the amount of communication needed for the client to triage any issues that arise in the transition between HIV testing and obtaining the PrEP prescription and to account for time needed to complete the process:Filling up a prescription is dealing with insurances and prior authorizations. Those are kind of like the number one barrier, so if I think about it in the level that it affects our organization, we need to kind of train our staff to more effectively and quickly get the information required to fill out these authorizations. I think it would be highly important in order to not necessarily raise our level of success but speed up the process because it does take time with pharmacies.

Many interviewees mentioned that clients received automated texts for prescriptions from pharmacies and that the clients could also benefit from an automated text system at the community health center to send reminders for appointments or to follow up with their care coordinator instead of the care coordinator attempting to reach them by phone.

## Patient Needs and Resources
The Outer Setting construct assesses the external influences on intervention implementation and the organization involved in the program being studied. The sub-construct of Patient Needs and Resources assesses the extent to which patient needs and the barriers and facilitators to meet those needs are accurately known and prioritized by the organization. According to our interviews, the Patient Needs and Resources sub-construct had a positive influence on the DiversiSafe program; this organization primarily serves LMSM and tailor services and outreach events to make this population feel comfortable engaging in HIV prevention measures. As Participant 3 noted, the LMSM population includes clients who have recently immigrated from Latin American countries and who have little or no experience engaging with the US healthcare system:And another example for sure is clients who are uninsured, naturally they think ‘if I don't have insurance,’ you know, they just hear that PrEP is expensive, so they can't afford it, so why even bother getting on it? So, we definitely try to let them know that there are options we can enroll them in, you know, different programs whether they're insured or not, and if their situation changes, we make sure to follow up with them. So, I think they definitely value that, and that is something they prefer because you know they just like to know that there are options, so I think [that] while giving them the services that we provide, we make sure to really educate them as well because that's something that they could relate to maybe a friend or someone they know.

Participant 3 also described how LMSM who may be undocumented have discussed misinformation as a barrier to accessing PrEP and how his role in dispelling that information as a part of DiversiPrEP was important:I mean a lot of the times [patients] think that oh I don't have my, you know, I don't have a social security like I can't, I can't get on PrEP, I can't do this or whatever, or they're going to send me back to my country. I mean it's unfortunate that they think that way, but you know it's also good that they even come to us expressing those concerns, because, like I say, we just really try to educate and let them know that that's not the case.

Participant 2 supported the concept of identifying specific patient needs to regions of Miami and particularly Miami Beach, an area heavily trafficked by tourists who engage in the nightlife and party scene:Our clients are very targeted, like most of our clientele are gay men and mostly Hispanic, at least in this office. So, [the staff] pretty much know what they need and want and usually they mostly live in this area. [The staff are] very much aware [of the needs of LMSM] and then we do like the outreach in this area, whether it's by internet or by the [mobile HIV testing] van. We have good connections with the, let's say, gay venues around the area and, actually, those venues also mention us when there's new people that just want to get tested or something. Because in the beach area there's a lot of new traffic of new clients, or not only clients, but people from Latin America, either for vacation, for work, for a better future, or just because they just like this Miami sunny living, party place.

The interviewees also highlighted that the CBO had developed relationships with insurance representatives and local pharmacies to triage any issues that clients had in the process of obtaining a PrEP prescription after receiving a trial pack of PrEP from the community patient care coordinator. Additionally, many interviewees noted that the organization was dedicated to creating a comfortable atmosphere for clients instead of a more clinically focused setting found in a traditional medical system.

## External Policies & Incentives
Another Outer Setting sub-construct that was identified was External Policies and Incentives. This includes external strategies to spread interventions, including policy and regulations (governmental or other central entities), external mandates, recommendations and guidelines, pay-for-performance, collaboration, and public or benchmark reporting. This sub-construct was specifically addressed in the interview with the Executive Director of the CBO, who had the most visibility of how external state and federal regulations affected funding restrictions and relationships with other organizations. Although we initially coded this sub-construct, no other managerial staff participated in the study besides the CBO Executive Director. Therefore, we decided to present the qualitative findings but not score this as a CFIR barrier or facilitator since an overall trend could not be established among the three branches and their managers. However, the Executive Director did provide some insight on how federal funding restrictions limited the percentage of the budget that could be paid for PrEP-related lab costs. The Executive Director explained further here:Really the only way state, federal, and even county get involved is with regards to funding. Now none of them pay for our health care staff salaries but there's a myriad of different rules of which ones will allow you to spend a portion of a grant that you competed for and you've got a set amount of money. They had all sorts of rules that said, you could not spend it on STD testing those rules have finally started going away. But now they're still almost all of them say no, you can't put any of that money into the lab specimen analysis that's necessary for PrEP. So it becomes a huge obstacle, if I right I'm thinking, particularly with [grant name] which I finally got them to change your mind on this, but for a year since the RFP came out it's specifically under the Ending [the] HIV Epidemic funding portfolio it's supposed to focus on PrEP per CDC and HRSA, and they said you can't spend any money on your PrEP [lab expenses]… we got the permission to do the STD labs as long as we don't exceed 5% of our grant award under our CDC federal grants.

The Executive Director further explained that even though HIV testing is a requirement to provide PrEP under CDC guidelines, the CBO often must seek out smaller non-federal foundation grants and funding to provide supplementary assistance for testing costs as a means of no-cost PrEP service being provided to the LMSM clients. Additionally, he highlighted that the CBO becoming a covered entity under the 340B federal program has also contributed funding towards providing free HIV testing needed for PrEP initiation, explaining:Under 340B, [The CBO] would automatically be entitled to a portion of the difference between what the wholesale prices is [of PrEP at a pharmacy], what it costs, and what the retail reimbursement was from the insurance companies, as long as you take that money and use it to increase access [to PrEP].

## Structural Characteristics
The CFIR construct of the Inner Setting represents the capacity for an organization to change and provide the shared receptivity of involved individuals to an intervention and the extent to which the intervention being studied will be rewarded, supported, and expected within the organization. Within Inner Setting, the sub-constructs of Structural Characteristics and Networks and Communications were assessed for this study. Structural Characteristics refer to physical infrastructure (e.g., layout of spaces, tangible materials that support functioning within the program), IT infrastructure, and organizational or hierarchical structure i.e., work infrastructure that affect implementation within an organization. Networks and Communications refers to the formal and informal relationships needed for information sharing across Inner Setting boundaries, such as structural barriers or professional or cultural differences.
In our interviews, the sub-construct of Structural Characteristics was highlighted as having a negative influence on the implementation of the DiversiPrEP project. The interviewees described that physical signage and service promotion in the community (unrelated to venue-based outreach) were lacking to attract potential patients who did not have a direct referral but may want to know more about PrEP or HIV testing. Participant 4 shared that "our offices, we don't have any like signs or posters outside of the office, so that means to kind of like discrete when people are just coming in, for testing and they don't want to you know someone that's coming into the office next door, knowing that they're coming in." Another potential explanation for this perceived lack of promotion was due to municipal restrictions on exterior signage, especially when the CBO office was located in a building that they were tenants or shared with other businesses, as was identified by multiple participants. To address this barrier and increase the reach of the organization, staff at each location engaged in promotion through social media, hosting community social events, and purchasing monthly advertisements in local LGBTQ publications.

## Networks and Communication
The sub-construct of Networks and Communication had mixed responses, providing a neutral influence on DiversiPrEP implementation. This sub-construct represents the nature and quality of social working networks and how the individuals in these networks communicate formally and informally within an organization. Interviewees often provided examples of how they were able to coordinate and troubleshoot tasks within their respective branch locations. Some interviewees also explained that the organization was attempting to develop interbranch relationships by having bimonthly organization-wide meetings to foster relationships between branches and discuss broader issues that occurred at all sites that implemented DiversiPrEP. Participant 5 stated:We have great communication, and in that I will say that all with the upper management arm we basically have like a kind of like meetings like to check in every day. every other every Tuesday, you know, in [that] we get involved with the other offices as well, so we not only check how things are going in our [branch] office but week every Tuesday we have a meeting to make sure that everything is going well and the other offices [with] all the managers and coordinators.

## Implementation Climate
The final sub-construct that we queried about for Inner Structure was Implementation Climate, which refers to the fit between meaning and values attached to the intervention by involved individuals; how those values align with individuals’ own norms, values, and perceived risks and needs; and how the intervention fits with existing workflows and systems. The interviewees all found this sub-construct to have had a positive influence on DiversiPrEP and often indicated that the staff was culturally competent and prioritized to ensure that each office was a safe space for the community. Participant 6 explained that promoting and maintaining staff who were reflective of the community being served was a core mission of the organization:“I think our staff have completely bought into the values of the organization, which I think is so important. Our vision is a community with decreased barriers to healthcare and with increased equity, and our mission is to serve primarily the Latinx and other communities of color in the gay, bisexual population or transgender [population]. The staff are entirely reflective of that; our staff hail from 13 different countries and territories; about half of them are themselves immigrants to the US, and nearly all the other half are first generation here…about half are living with HIV themselves. So for the staff, it’s more than just a paycheck."

Additionally, cultural awareness came up in interviews, as the staff explained that they had opportunities to dispel misinformation among immigrant clients about receiving services (e.g., clients without a social security number could still be eligible for PrEP prescriptions). The cultural competence and ambiance of safety provided the opportunity to engage in open communication about PrEP and integrated services (i.e., STI and HIV testing and prevention).

## Discussion
The aim of this study was to identify the determinants of the implementation of the DiversiPrEP program at a CBO at three locations throughout Miami-Dade and Broward Counties in Florida by interviewing staff at various levels of administration. In other studies that have investigated PrEP access and initiation programs in the US, sexual and gender minority (SGM) adult patients, which included MSM, identified that not having someone advocate for them within a healthcare system to access PrEP, costs of pursuing PrEP, were barriers to access [ ]. Among PrEP providers, one PrEP implementation barrier for patients was navigating various funding programs provided by pharmaceutical companies (i.e., patient assistance programs for PrEP) and insurance, requiring medical staff to dedicate significant time to ensure that patients were able to initiate and then sustain PrEP prescriptions [ ].
The subconstructs of Relative Advantage, Patient Needs and Resources, and Implementation Climate were seen as positive influences on DiversiPrEP program implementation, while Adaptability and Structural Characteristics were seen as negative influences that hindered implementation. Networks and Communication was decided as a neutral influence due to mixed ratings. The key implementation facilitators across positive influenced subconstructs that were identified included incorporating a “one-stop-shop” approach to PrEP service delivery, assigning coordinated navigation services for each client, establishing connections with local pharmacies and insurance agents, hiring and training culturally competent and knowledgeable community organization staff, developing an organizational climate that promotes positive client experience, and aligning the implementation and program goals of DiversiPrEP with the goals and mission of the community organization. Staff also identified some barriers to delivering DiversiSafe intervention. Overall, the interviewees also identified the need for automatic text reminders for clinic appointments and PrEP refills, which have since been implemented by the CBO after completing the interviews. Expanding services to include automatic reminders was thought to address retention in care and reduce staff effort in re-engaging clients in care, which can be a time-intensive process. In line with the abovementioned barrier, conceptualizing and proposing costs for technology development during proposal development and implementation could address this issue in future efforts.
The findings of this study pertinent to LMSM clients involve having CBO staff and PrEP navigators that reflect the demographics of the clients being served, incorporating and modifying health education to dispel rumors regarding PrEP and insurance or payment assistance, and providing a safe space that encourages discussion of stigma related to initiating PrEP in a welcoming community setting. These factors of providing culturally-tailored PrEP services align with other evidence in Miami with LMSM that identified stigma, knowledge about PrEP, and financial stressors as barriers to engaging with HIV prevention and behavioral health [ ]. As mentioned by Participant 3, LMSM in Miami that are recent immigrants and were fearful of being deported when engaging with the healthcare system to initiate PrEP needed to have a discussion with the PrEP Navigator to discuss this perceived barrier. This fear of immigration policies and engagement with the healthcare system is another area that should be tailored to LMSM for PrEP programs and has been identified by another study [ ].
We also found that a key facilitator of the implementation was the perceived advantage among staff of offering no-cost HIV/STI testing services. Reducing the cost of testing and laboratory services was thought to alleviate a major barrier for Latinx individuals seeking HIV prevention services in South Florida. However, funding restrictions that limit the percentage of a community organization’s operating budget that can be allotted to cover laboratory and testing costs for clients may negatively impact the ability to offer those services at no cost. Although the CBO in this study was able to offer such services to reduce barriers to PrEP for its clients, navigating available funding sources to cover these costs is a complex process. We would encourage leadership at CBOs who are interested in providing low/no-cost PrEP services to engage with their state, county, and local health departments and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention for additional information regarding funding for PrEP assistance and ancillary services.
Some funding avenues already exist for CBOs to decrease barriers to the PrEP care and HIV care continuum but utilizing them requires combining funding mechanisms that do not have overlapping restrictions on the way funding can be spent. The funding avenues for these CBOs include the 340B Drug Pricing Program; the Manufacturer Assistance Program (e.g., Gillead Advancing Access, ViiV Patient Assistance Program); state and federal HRSA funding; local fundraising efforts and community partnerships, specifically for clients living with HIV; and the Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA) Ryan White HIV/AIDS Program (RWHAP). CBOs providing PrEP care and the HIV care continuum should be acutely aware of the barriers to care for the populations that they serve and may need to be creative regarding stitching various funding sources to provide the services they have at minimal or no cost to the client.
One potential avenue outlined in the CBO Executive Director’s interview to overcome this barrier is the federal 340B Drug Pricing Program. This program allows qualifying community health clinics and hospitals that treat low-income and uninsured patients so that they can buy outpatient prescription medications at a discounted rate, including PrEP and ART [ ]. Manufacturer assistance programs, such as Gilead’s Advancing Access, provide brand-name drugs (e.g., Descovy and Truvada) to uninsured patients [ ]. Another recent Manufacturer Assistance Program available comes from ViiV Healthcare in the form of a Patient Assistance Program, specifically for uninsured and underinsured patients living in poverty [ ].
Policymakers can also play a role in expanding the reach of PrEP programming. The passing of legislation that increases the availability of PrEP (both name-brand and generic forms) through a national PrEP program may be a key step in expanding access to PrEP in community-based settings [ , ]. Killelea and colleagues (2022) proposed a national PrEP financing and delivery system to improve access and uptake of PrEP medications by offering expanded access to medications and lab services for individuals who are on Medicaid or are uninsured, creating a network of community-based PrEP providers. This proposal by Killelea et al. was conceptualized as a program that supplements rather than replaces existing programs or activities that have been successful [ ].
Our findings highlight the critical role that PrEP navigators play in implementing culturally tailored care. Nonmedical staff are important for improving the client experience of care because they bridge the gap as translators of both medical and administrative issues that can occur between a walk-in appointment and consistently taking PrEP. The PrEP counselor acts to shepherd the client through issues with testing and understanding lab results, dispelling rumors about PrEP or other risk prevention methods, staying in contact with clients to remind them about monthly follow-ups, and helping them navigate the difficulties of scheduling prescriptions with their pharmacy as well as working with insurance to ensure coverage of their prescription. This reduces the mental burden on clients to not only engage with care for testing and information about HIV risk prevention but also decreases the possibility that the client will disengage with care at any of the steps to obtain and take PrEP consistently to manage their risk. As a result of having nonmedical staff solely dedicated to the client experience, these staff members can also be a source of referrals to external services that the client may feel comfortable discussing during their check-ins (e.g., mental health treatment, substance use treatment, housing, food banks, GED or ESOL certificate programs, and immigration services). Culturally tailored PrEP counseling and navigation programs have been found to be effective in improving uptake and persistence in PrEP services [ – ]. The relationships of the PrEP navigators in our study with local insurance agents, healthcare organizations, and pharmacies could be another way to alleviate barriers to persisting in HIV prevention for clients who may be underinsured or uninsured.
Our findings and recommendations should be reviewed while considering this study’s strengths and limitations. The strengths of the current study include the following: the collaboration with San Diego CFAR IS Hub investigators to develop and refine our qualitative interview guide, which is grounded in a widely used implementation science framework; the use of a rigorous approach to assigning valence to the identified implementation determinants that impact how DiversiPrEP intervention was delivered and maintained in a community-based setting; the involvement of an independent auditor to verify our scoring approach and findings; and the embeddedness of our community partner in the Latinx community in South Florida, an EHE priority jurisdiction.
Our study also has several limitations. First, the CBO that the study team partnered with is an organization that had an established relationship with the senior author based on previous outreach and research efforts. We recognize that this increases the potential for bias, as participant staff members may have only wanted to share favorable recommendations to the study team. The study team largely does not engage with the CBO clinical staff for research purposes and did not have prior working relationships with any of the participants at the time of the interviews. The study team also reinforced to all potential participants that anonymity would be prioritized and that results would be aggregated in the paper with no specific names of participants being shared with anyone other than the two interviewers on the study team.
Additionally, this CBO aims to serve the Latinx population in Miami and prioritizes hiring community members as staff; thus, the staff largely reflects the population and is largely homogeneous in gender and ethnicity (i.e., Latino, male, cisgender). The eleven respondents that volunteered to be interviewed for this study do not differ significantly from those that did not participate but the authors recognize that this was a small sample of a small CBO and may not reflect other CBOs in Miami-Dade or Broward county that provide PrEP services generally. Efforts were made by the two interviewers to collect data from multiple levels of management at each of the three branches, but only the Executive Director opted to participate from upper management. This is a limitation of the findings in demonstrating how each level perceives the CFIR subconstructs as positive or negative influences to PrEP program implementation.
Given that only those that volunteered to be interviewed were included in the qualitative data collection for this manuscript, sampling bias may have occurred due to the nature of convenience sampling within the organization, and it is possible that staff members that felt that they had something positive to contribute were self-selecting for participation. With regards to the lack of managerial participation, we recognize that data collected from the Executive Director may be biased in that he was involved in the creation of the DiversiSafe intervention prior to incorporation into the DiversiPrEP project. We were unable to collect further data about the subconstruct of Executive Policies and Incentives as a result and thus did not score this as a positive or negative influence. Furthermore, the qualitative results in this manuscript provided generalized viewpoints from CBO staff that meld experience working with clients over time and only provide insight into some CFIR constructs. To The study team also published qualitative data from both MSM and LMSM in Miami-Dade and Broward Counties who had visited the CBO for sexual health services to investigate other CFIR constructs that were not addressed [ ].
Additionally, South Florida is a unique context in that it is one of the few communities in which the majority of the population identify as Latinx [ ]. Given the heterogeneity of the Latinx community in South Florida, the interviewees’ experiences regarding some of the regional barriers to implementation at this CBO may not be generalizable to other HIV service providers in other areas of the US with non-Latinx populations. Second, in this study, we highlight the experience of one CBO with three (as of publication, now four) locations across South Florida, which may have some specific CFIR constructs that are not generalizable to other organizations in South Florida or other geographical contexts. However, these findings provide commentary on how both the inner and outer setting sub-constructs need to be critically evaluated and tailored to the specific needs of the community being served.

## Conclusions
The lessons learned from implementation of a PrEP program for LMSM that are could be useful for other CBOs involved in HIV prevention are: 1) offering low- or no-cost lab work/testing by exploring a combination of state and federal funds to cover costs can reduce a major barrier for LMSM to access PrEP; 2) assigning PrEP navigators to clients to provide culturally tailored care and triage issues with pharmacies and insurance companies and; 3) having representation of the community within the organization at various levels of administration to help identify specific patient needs and integrate cultural values into the planning and implementation of tailored PrEP programs. CBOs should also consider the channels that they promote PrEP programs and how information is shared within their organization between branches as well as levels of management.
