## ABSTRACT

## Aims
To examine the role and impact of carer involvement in rehabilitation for community‐dwelling individuals with dementia, focusing on cognitive stimulation therapy, cognitive rehabilitation, cognitive training, cognitive behavioural therapy, and exercise.

## Design
A systematic review and synthesis without meta‐analysis.

## Data Sources
Five electronic databases, reference lists, and citations were searched (2017–2024), targeting primary research that reported results concerning one or more of those five focused rehabilitation interventions for people with dementia and their carers.

## Methods
Results were synthesised using narrative approaches. The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool and the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool were used to appraise the quality of included studies.

## Results
Forty‐one studies (12 main trials, 22 pilot studies, and 7 sub‐studies) were included. While the patterns between carer involvement level and types of rehabilitation were observed, their relationship to intervention effectiveness was unclear. High carer involvement in cognitive stimulation therapy and exercise was associated with improved cognition and quality of life for people with dementia and better health‐related quality of life for carers. Pilot studies showed mixed but generally positive trends, with increased depressive symptoms in carers needing further investigation. Qualitative findings highlighted social interaction and improved caregiving knowledge as key enablers to positive experiences, whereas lack of motivation was the main barrier to rehabilitation engagement.

## Conclusion
This review identified several patterns between the level of carer involvement and intervention types. However, the mechanism underlying different involvement levels and rehabilitation success remains unclear. More rigorous research is needed to determine the relationship between carer involvement and the effects of rehabilitation interventions on supporting the independence of people with dementia.

## Impact
This review enhanced the understanding of carers' roles and impacts in supporting dementia rehabilitation and possible links to optimal health outcomes.

## Reporting Method
Synthesis Without Meta‐analysis (SWiM) reporting guideline.

## Patient or Public Contribution
No patient/public contribution.

Keywords: caregivers, cognitive rehabilitation, cognitive stimulation therapy, dementia, exercise, rehabilitation

## Summary
What is already known
○Informal carers play a vital role in supporting people with dementia. Working in partnership with carers in rehabilitation is recommended but has not been fully explored.
○A gap in the literature remains regarding the roles and impacts of carer involvement in dementia‐specific rehabilitation interventions.

What this paper adds
○Cognitive rehabilitation, individual cognitive stimulation therapy and most exercise interventions require high carer involvement, whereas group cognitive stimulation therapy and exercise combined multicomponent intervention consistently have low carer involvement.
○High carer involvement in cognitive stimulation therapy is associated with improved cognition for people with dementia and health‐related quality of life for carers; high carer involvement in exercise is associated with improved quality of life for clients.

Implications for practice/policy
○The development of future dementia‐specific rehabilitation should consider the roles and needs of carers.
○Future research is needed to explore how different levels of carer involvement may impact rehabilitation outcomes across different intervention types.

1.

## Introduction
Dementia is a global health priority. In 2019, it was estimated that approximately 55 million individuals worldwide are living with dementia, with 50% of the global cost of dementia attributed to informal care (Prince et al.  ; World Health Organisation [WHO]  ). Dementia is also considered one of the leading contributors to disability and dependency among older populations, affecting the quality of life of people with dementia and their carers (Martyr et al.  ).
Rehabilitation is increasingly recognised as an essential part of dementia care and working in partnership with family carers is commonly recommended in dementia rehabilitation (Gitlin and Bruneau Jr  ). According to WHO, rehabilitation is defined as “a set of interventions designed to optimise functioning and reduce disability in individuals with health conditions in interaction with their environment” (World Health Organization  , 104). Based on the systematic review of quality dementia clinical guidelines (Jeon et al.  ), the WHO Mental Health Gap Action Programme guideline (WHO  ), and a focused review of dementia rehabilitation (Sikkes et al.  ), the following five interventions were deemed to have the strongest evidence for dementia rehabilitation: physical exercise, cognitive stimulation therapy (CST), cognitive rehabilitation (CR), cognitive training (CT), and cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT). CST is a mental‐stimulation‐focused therapy aimed at improving a person's cognitive function and quality of life through themed group activities (Spector et al.  ); CR is an individualised, goal‐oriented and problem‐solving approach aimed at optimising function in everyday life concerning the person's needs, wishes, and preferences (Clare et al.  ); CT is a structured, guided practice on standardised tasks to improve or maintain ability in particular cognitive domains (Bahar‐Fuchs et al.  ); CBT encompasses a collaborative psychological approach to change people's thinking and behavioural patterns (Fenn and Byrne  ); and exercise refers to planned, structured and repetitive physical movement to maintain or improve physical fitness (Chodzko‐Zajko et al.  ).
While a recent systematic review identified that carer involvement in cognitive‐based interventions improved carer quality of life and depression (Leung et al.  ), greater carer involvement in some of the interventions could also increase stress levels (Amieva et al.  ). However, little attention has been paid to addressing potential negative outcomes concerning carers and their needs. To our knowledge, no systematic review has examined the roles and impacts of carer involvement on health and well‐being‐related outcomes in dementia‐specific rehabilitation.

2.

## The Review
2.1.

## Aims
This systematic review aims to provide a synthesis without meta‐analysis of the current evidence on carer involvement in selected rehabilitation interventions for people with dementia.

2.2.

## Design
This integrative systematic review was guided by the Synthesis without meta‐analysis (SWiM) guidelines to report results (Campbell et al.  ). The protocol was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (Lee et al.  ).
The primary review question was: Are levels of carer involvement associated with health and well‐being outcomes of the rehabilitation interventions? The following two questions were also explored to provide context for the carer's role in the interventions:

What are the roles and experiences of carer involvement in rehabilitation for people with dementia?
What are the scope and types of carer involvement in these interventions?

2.3.

## Information Sources and Search Strategy
Searches from five electronic databases (PubMed, MEDLINE, EMBASE, CINAHL, and PsycINFO) were conducted between 1 July and 16 November 2022 and updated to 20 February 2024. The search strategy was informed by the “Population, Phenomenon of Interest and Context” framework and developed drawing on previous systematic reviews on similar topics and consultations with an experienced librarian in health disciplines. See Appendix  for key search terms. Reference lists and citations of identified studies were hand‐searched. Only papers published in English and within the last 5 years were included to ensure the recency of the evidence.

2.4.

## Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria
All studies examining the impacts and experiences of informal carers' direct and indirect involvement in five chosen interventions—CT, CST, CR, CBT, and exercise (alone, combined or adjunctive of other interventions) for people with dementia and their carers were included. Outcome measures were related to the health and well‐being of people with dementia and their carers. Studies were excluded if:

Other population groups (e.g., mild cognitive impairment) were combined with the targeted population without separate results;
No carer outcomes were reported; or
Publication of opinion papers, discussion papers, systematic reviews, editorials, conference abstracts, literature reviews and guidelines.

2.5.

## Search Outcome
A total of 4440 papers identified from searches were imported to Endnote 20 bibliographic software (Clarivate Analytics  ), and duplicates were removed. Title and abstract screening of 2446 articles was performed independently by C‐YL and verified by Y‐HJ and KW after removing duplicates. Articles with ambiguity to eligibility were retained for full‐text screening. The selection of 145 full‐text papers for review was conducted independently (C‐YL) and cross‐checked by the other two authors (Y‐HJ and KW) to ensure the eligibility of included papers. Any conflicts were resolved through discussion. Key reasons for the exclusion of full‐text articles included (1) no carer involvement, (2) no client and carer outcomes, and (3) no carer outcomes. Following screening, 41 articles were selected for inclusion (see Figure  ).
 
2.6.

## Data Extraction
Data were extracted by (C‐YL) and cross‐checked by the second (Y‐HJ) and third (KW) authors. Extracted data included study characteristics (country, sample size and study design), participant characteristics (sex, age, the relationship of carers and care recipients, stages of dementia), intervention characteristics, outcome measurement time points, and health and well‐being related outcomes for people with dementia and their carers. Descriptive data related to the roles and experiences regarding how carer involvement supports people with dementia participating in rehabilitation were also extracted.

2.7.

## Quality Assessment
One author (C‐YL) independently assessed the risk of bias and the quality of included articles based on the Cochrane risk‐of‐bias Tool version 1 (RoB) (Higgins and Green  ) and Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) (Hong et al.  ), and consulted with the remaining authors (Y‐HJ and KW) for any disagreements. RoB was used to appraise the effectiveness of the interventions from full‐scale trials (e.g., randomised controlled trials or pre‐post studies), and MMAT was applied for the remaining study designs.

2.8.

## Synthesis
A synthesis without meta‐analysis was conducted by grouping studies in textual descriptions and tabulations based on intervention types and study designs to examine the characteristics and variability of outcomes of interests between studies. Relevant information on carer involvement was extracted from various sources, including original paper, protocol, trial registration, intervention development paper, and Materials , to develop items to capture the details of carer involvement. Criteria to guide the level of carer involvement (low, medium and high) were then established, building on those items and discussion among all authors: (1) carers were part of the intervention delivery or were actively involved from the beginning to the end of the intervention/programme (High level); (2) carers were partly involved in intervention sessions and/or had some active involvement in the intervention (Medium level); and (3) carers had passive roles in the intervention (e.g., assisting in transportation and monitoring of outcomes) (Low level). Results were synthesised using descriptive and narrative approaches.
Due to high variability in outcome measures, Fisher's exact test and Z‐score were used to examine the relationship between the level of carer involvement and individual intervention outcomes, as well as overall intervention types, by comparing the differences in the proportion of statistically significant results (p < 0.05) between groups. As more than half of the interventions were graded high carer involvement, we combined low‐ and medium‐level carer involvement into one variable and compared it to high‐level carer involvement.

3.

## Results
3.1.

## Study Characteristics and Quality
Of the 41 included articles (34 studies and seven sub‐studies), 12 were full‐scale trials (10 randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and two pre‐post studies), four of which were conducted in the UK, and the remaining eight were in the US (n = 2), Europe (n = 2), Asia (n = 2), Canada (n = 1) and Australia (n = 1). Three of the RCTs (Clare et al.  ; Nyman et al.  ; van Santen et al.  ) accompanied four qualitative studies (Barrado‐Martin et al.  , ; Morgan‐Trimmer et al.  ; Warmoth et al.  ) and one mixed‐method study (van Santen et al.  ) to complement the main results. The remaining 22 studies were pilot designs, including seven RCTs, 14 pre‐post studies and one mixed‐method study. A case report (Jeon et al.  ) and a qualitative study (Naylor et al.  ) were published alongside their respective pilot trials (Jeon et al.  ; Marinho et al.  ). Studies were mainly carried out in the communities or participants' homes. See Table  for details on the study characteristics.
Twenty‐five of 34 studies targeted people with mild‐to‐moderate dementia. The remaining nine studies focused on mild‐stage only (n = 5) or did not specify the stage (n = 4) (see Table  ). Carers were mostly spouses or adult children. The most commonly measured outcomes for people with dementia were cognition, independence, mobility (including physical performance), health‐related quality of life (HRQoL), quality of life (QoL), mood (e.g., anxiety and depression) and behaviours (e.g., neuropsychiatric symptoms [NPS]). For carers, key outcomes reported were HRQoL, QoL, burden, mood, and distress associated with their care recipients' NPS.
Thirteen studies were multicomponent interventions, eight were exercise studies, eight were CST, and the remaining five were CR. No studies on CBT or CT were found to have carer involvement, and therefore, this review focused on the abovementioned four interventions. The intervention length was between 6 weeks and 2 years. The most common session frequency was weekly (10/34) and fortnightly (7/34), with an average total session of 22.7 (ranging from 5 to 75 sessions). The total direct intervention hours (total number of sessions × session duration) ranged from 10.5 to 72.5 h. See Table  for detailed intervention characteristics.
Most of the 12 main trials (n = 9) were assessed as low to moderate risk of bias. Appropriate randomisation and allocation concealment were done in 10 studies. Blinding of participants and therapists was not feasible in all studies, but seven studies blinded assessors to minimise detection bias. Three studies had unclear risk due to no assessor details, and two had high risk because of unblinded assessors. Ten studies reported missing data and used appropriate imputation methods, suggesting low attrition bias. Two studies reported high dropout rates. See Table  for further details. Pilot randomised trials were generally moderate to high quality, with only one study lacking proper randomisation (Table  ). The study quality for pilot non‐randomised studies was moderate (Tables  ). Qualitative and mixed‐method studies were high quality, with consistent data collection, analysis, and interpretation (Tables  and ).

3.2.

## The Types and Details of Carer Involvement
As shown in Table  , of the 34 studies, 22 studies had high‐level carer involvement, four had medium, and eight had low.
 3.2.1.

## Exercise (n = 8)
Most exercise studies (n = 5) were graded as a high level of carer involvement, including two technology‐based interventions (Menengic et al.  ; Taylor et al.  ). The exercise types included Tai chi, aerobic and strength training, balance training, chair‐based exercise, muscle memory building for daily function and individualised exercise plans. The roles of carers in the exercise vary across studies. Carers were more likely to be involved in “doing homework based on agreed goals” than in the other three interventions. Other common tasks involved (1) attending pre‐assessments or orientations, (2) attending sessions and/or home visits, and (3) monitoring and assisting in practice. One balance‐training‐focused intervention only required low carer involvement for monitoring client situations with no specific roles in supporting the interventions (Taylor et al.  ).

3.2.2.

## Cognitive Stimulation Therapy (n = 8)
Four studies used group CST and four individual CST. Five studies were graded as high carer involvement. For example, carers were trained to deliver individual CST at home (Kor et al.  , ; Orrell et al.  ; Rai et al.  ). They received demonstrations from the therapists and were given support materials (e.g., manuals and kits) to stimulate the client's cognitive and social functioning. Carers were involved in the development phase, intervention transition, and home practice (Paddick et al.  ).
Three group‐based CST had low carer involvement (Bhowmik et al.  ; Kelly et al.  ; Marinho et al.  ), indirectly involving carers in the interventions, such as (1) driving the client to the venues, (2) monitoring client's progress and working in partnership with therapists in general throughout the intervention.

3.2.3.

## Cognitive Rehabilitation (n = 5)
All CR interventions involved high carer involvement. Carers played an important role from the beginning to the end of the interventions. Carers actively identified goals and developed care plans across all different CR studies. They were partially or fully required to present during sessions, transferred the knowledge and skills to daily practice, and supervised and practised strategies at home.

3.2.4.

## Multicomponent Interventions (n = 13)
Over half of the multicomponent interventions (n = 7) had high‐level carer involvement. Two studies with medium‐level carer involvement incorporated CR elements and delivered in groups (Bartels et al.  ; D'Cunha et al.  ). Exercise combined with group‐based CST consistently had a low level of carer involvement, where carers were encouraged but optional to join sessions or monitor and report client concerns (Giebel et al.  ; Neudorf et al.  ; Regan et al.  ; van Santen et al.  ). Most carers who participated in multicomponent interventions received psychoeducation, differentiating from the above three interventions. Other main roles of carers under this group were (1) attending sessions and/or home visits, if possible, and (2) receiving education to transfer learning into daily practice.

3.3.

## Effectiveness of Interventions—Key Outcomes for People With Dementia
The effectiveness of interventions was synthesised from 12 main trials in Table  , focusing on key domains: cognition, independence and mobility, health‐related quality of life (HRQoL), quality of life (QoL), neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS) and others.
3.3.1.

## Multicomponent Interventions (n = 5)
A CST and cycling combined intervention showed statistically significant improvements in cognition, memory and social functioning for people with mild‐to‐moderate dementia (van Santen et al.  ). Evidence from pre‐post studies also supported enhanced HRQoL and short‐term QoL, but these have yet to be captured quantitatively in larger trials (Neudorf et al.  ; Teri et al.  ). No adverse effects were noted in other outcomes.

3.3.2.

## Exercise (n = 3)
Tai Chi significantly reduced falls and improved client QoL (Nyman et al.  ). While two studies found non‐significant cognitive effects, a statistically significantly greater cognitive impairment was observed in moderate‐to‐high intensity aerobic and strength training in the long term (12 months) with unclear clinical relevance (Lamb et al.  ). No improvements in independence and mobility were found.

3.3.3.

## Cognitive Stimulation Therapy (n = 3)
Significant cognitive benefits were found in both virtually delivered group CST (Bhowmik et al.  ) and carer‐delivered individual CST (iCST) (Kor et al.  ). Kor et al. ( ) also reported statistically significant short‐term improvements in QoL and NPS. Another carer‐led iCST significantly improved the carer‐client relationship from clients' perspectives (Orrell et al.  ). No significant effects on independence, HRQoL, or depression were found.

3.3.4.

## Cognitive Rehabilitation (n = 1)
Apart from clients' goal attainment and satisfaction, there was a positive but non‐significant effect in other domains for CR (Clare et al.  ).

3.4.

## Effectiveness of Interventions—Key Outcomes for Carers
The following key carer outcomes were identified and reported from 12 main trials: HRQoL, QoL, carer burden, mood (anxiety, depression, stress), distress related to neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS‐related distress), and other outcomes. See Table  for details.
3.4.1.

## Multicomponent Interventions (n = 5)
Two pre‐post multicomponent programmes incorporating exercise components showed a statistically significant improvement in carers' mental health (Neudorf et al.  ) and a reduction in short‐term depression (Teri et al.  ).

3.4.2.

## Exercise (n = 3)
Tai Chi exercise was found to have a statistically significant negative impact on carers' lower extremity function, although the reason was unclear (Nyman et al.  ). No statistically significant effects on HRQoL, QoL and carer burden were identified.

3.4.3.

## Cognitive Stimulation Therapy (n = 3)
Individual CST significantly enhanced carer HRQoL (Orrell et al.  ), carers' attitudes toward caregiving experiences and the person with dementia (Kor et al.  ) but not for the caregiving relationship (Orrell et al.  ). A virtually delivered group CST found a statistically significant reduction in NPS‐related distress (Bhowmik et al.  ).

3.4.4.

## Cognitive Rehabilitation (n = 1)
CR significantly improved the management of functional disability (goal attainment). No other statistically significant effects in HRQoL and stress were reported (Clare et al. ).

3.5.

## The Overall Trend of Outcomes for Pilot Studies
Among the 22 pilot studies, eight were multicomponent interventions, and the remaining studies were exercise (5/14), CST (5/14), and CR (4/14). As the primary goal of pilot studies was not to establish evidence of effectiveness, we report overall findings below.
3.5.1.

## Key Outcomes for People With Dementia
As per Table  , we identified short‐term cognitive benefits in people with dementia participating in exercise (Mehling et al.  ; Menengic et al.  ), individual and group CST (Kelly et al.  ; Kor et al.  ; Paddick et al.  ; Rai et al.  ) and multicomponent interventions (D'Cunha et al.  ; Olakehinde et al.  ), but not in CR. Cognitive stimulation therapy, alone or combined, was the only rehabilitation that consistently improved client functional ability (D'Cunha et al.  ; Marinho et al. ; Olakehinde et al.  ; Rai et al.  ; Regan et al.  ). Balance training exercises reduced concerns about falling and fall risk for clients (Taylor et al.  , ). A mixed but generally positive trend was observed across all interventions in improving depressive symptoms (7/13 studies), QoL (9/14 studies) and NPS (6/8 studies).

3.5.2.

## Key Outcomes for Carers
As per Table  , improved carer burden was found from carers participating in CST (alone or combined) (Kor et al.  ; Olakehinde et al.  ; Paddick et al.  ) and most exercise interventions (Mehling et al.  ; Menengic et al.  ). Mixed results were observed in multicomponent programmes (2/3 studies) and CR (3/4 studies). Reduction in anxiety or stress was seen in three CST studies (Paddick et al.  ; Kor et al.  ; Rai et al.  ) and NPS‐related distress across four intervention types. However, no interventions improved carer depression in the short term (Jeon et al.  ; Kor et al.  ; Mehling et al.  ; Paddick et al.  ; Rai et al.  ), with mixed trends in HRQoL and QoL.

3.6.

## The Relationships Between Carer Involvement Level and Intervention Outcomes
As shown in Table  , we compared outcomes of all types of rehabilitation from 12 main trials using the level of carer involvement (Low/Medium vs. High). For clients, 24.0% of outcomes from the Low/Medium carer involvement group showed significant benefits of interventions compared to 16.7% of outcomes from the High carer involvement group. For carers, 21.4% of outcomes from the Low/Medium carer involvement group were significantly improved, whereas 15.9% of the outcomes from the High carer involvement group showed significant improvement.
 By examining the association between carer involvement level and effective outcomes, we identified that two CST studies and one multicomponent intervention having CST components, with mixed levels of carer involvement, were associated with improved client cognition (Bhowmik et al.  ; Kor et al.  ; van Santen et al.  ). Two CST studies with different levels of carer involvement were associated with enhanced carer HRQoL (Orrell et al.  ; Neudorf et al.  ). Two multicomponent studies, CST and exercise combined with low carer involvement, showed enhanced client HRQoL (Neudorf et al. ; van Santen et al.  ). Two exercise studies and one individual CST study with high carer involvement demonstrated improved client QoL (Kor et al.  ; Nyman et al.  ; Teri et al.  ). See Table  for more details of other outcomes.
 Similar patterns were observed in 22 pilot studies that showed positive effects (difference of 16.7%, 95% CI: 0.8, 32.6, p = 0.053 for client outcomes; difference of 11.4%, 95% CI: −12.4, 35.1, p = 0.367 for carer outcomes). Notably, carers' depressive symptoms were consistently worsened across the six studies with high carer involvement across all types of interventions, although this trend was not observed in the main trials.

3.7.

## Carer Involvement Experiences in Dementia Rehabilitation (n = 13 Studies)
Carers valued “socialising opportunities” with clients, therapists, and other dyads (n = 7 studies). They appreciated the time spent together, building new relationships, and sharing experiences, which connected them with individuals and groups. Carers reported reduced stress and improved client well‐being, attributing it to the enjoyment of sessions (Kelly et al.  ; Mehling et al.  ). Social interaction was also feasible in virtual environments, although internet stability needed improvement (Neudorf et al.  ; Ptomey et al.  ). Social benefits may further motivate client and carer commitment to session participation (Barrado‐Martin et al.  ; Naylor et al.  ; Neudorf et al.  ).
“Lack of motivation” among clients and carers was seen as a notable barrier to intervention participation (n = 8 studies), often due to distance, travel issues (Kelly et al.  ; Mehling et al.  ; Neudorf et al.  ) and time commitment (Barrado‐Martin et al.  , ; Kor et al.  ; Mehling et al.  ). Carers found it challenging to engage the person with dementia due to low motivation. Therapists made additional efforts to engage carers as their support in reinforcing strategies could influence client motivation (Morgan‐Trimmer et al.  ). Maintaining logs and establishing home practice routines were also used to improve motivation and practice continuity (Barrado‐Martin et al.  ).

Carers gained a better insight into caregiving skills and knowledge of dementia‐related health issues through participation in rehabilitation (n = 5 studies), which enhanced communication and caregiving relationships with the clients (Kor et al.  ; Morgan‐Trimmer et al.  ). Establishing a good therapeutic relationship further assisted therapists in supporting the dyads with personalised goal setting and strategy development (Jeon et al.  ; Morgan‐Trimmer et al.  ). Improved confidence for dyads was developed through positive changes from the interventions, such as better exercise performance (Barrado‐Martin et al.  ; Ptomey et al.  ). However, the client's unexpected health issues, such as shoulder pain, presented challenges to further class and home practice engagement (Barrado‐Martin et al.  , ).

4.

## Discussion
To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review examining the role of carer involvement in rehabilitation for people with dementia on various health outcomes. Carer roles in these interventions varied from transporting clients, attending sessions, monitoring practice, and receiving training to deliver the intervention. Due to high variability in study outcomes, no statistical differences were found between the carer involvement level and outcomes for the main or pilot trials. Several patterns of the level of carer involvement across the rehabilitation were identified: (1) Cognitive rehabilitation was the only intervention with high carer involvement across studies, (2) individual cognitive stimulation therapy often required high carer involvement, whereas group cognitive stimulation therapy was the opposite, (3) cognitive stimulation therapy and exercised combined multicomponent interventions consistently to had low carer involvement and (4) most exercise and technology‐based rehabilitation required medium or high carer involvement.
We have found that the impact of carer involvement in dementia‐specific rehabilitation was poorly articulated in the reviewed studies. High carer involvement in individual cognitive stimulation therapy and multicomponent exercise was especially associated with improved client QoL. On the other hand, despite low carer involvement, multicomponent interventions (exercise combined with group cognitive stimulation therapy) still showed improved client HRQoL. Improved client cognition and carer HRQoL were observed in interventions with cognitive stimulation therapy components (alone or combined), with the level of carer involvement depending on the format (individual vs. group). However, the interplay between carer involvement level, intervention types and intervention effectiveness could not be fully explored. There may be no direct relationship between low carer involvement and positive outcomes since interventions can be effective or ineffective regardless of carer involvement. A recent Cochrane review conducted by Woods et al. ( ), which included five cognitive stimulation studies with informal carer involvement (Bottino et al.  ; Cove et al.  ; Maci et al.  ; Onder et al.  ; Orgeta et al.  ), suggested short‐term cognitive benefits for people with mild‐to‐moderate dementia but no significant differences in carer‐related health outcomes. Neely et al. ( ) further compared cognitive training with and without carer involvement in an RCT and found improved client memory from the intervention group with carer support.
Types of carer involvement vary between intervention types, with some patterns emerging: “Practising homework” was more common in exercise interventions, goal identification and/or strategy development in cognitive rehabilitation, carer training in individual cognitive stimulation, and psychoeducation in the multicomponent interventions. “Intervention transition” (receiving education, information or training for home practice) was found to be an important type of carer involvement across rehabilitation interventions, indicating carers may be key to intervention sustainability. A systematic review and meta‐analysis by Leung et al. ( ) identified three types of carer involvement in cognitive‐based interventions, including cognitive training, cognitive stimulation therapy and cognitive rehabilitation, which aligned with some of our categories—(1) carer‐delivered interventions (2) partly attending some sessions; (3) repeating some tasks at home. Previous RCTs also engaged carers in cognitive training regarding supporting homework exercises (Giuli et al.  ) and being trained to facilitate techniques with the clients (Quayhagen et al.  ). This was not found in our review, possibly because the review was limited to published studies in the last 5 years, suggesting that carer involvement in cognitive training may not be a common feature of cognitive training any more. Similarly, cognitive behavioural therapy may not necessarily involve carers, although previous studies showed that carers attended sessions (Spector et al.  ; Walker  ). Cognitive behavioural therapy studies were excluded from this review as they were designed for carers only.
From qualitative exploratory findings, the benefits of social connection and improved understanding of caregiving knowledge and skills were identified as key factors to positive experiences and outcomes of rehabilitation. The main challenges include poor motivation due to time commitment and travel issues. This phenomenon may be explained by an interplay between individual, social and community levels where encouragement from carers and social engagement with others in similar conditions may reinforce motivation to exercise (Hobson et al.  ).
4.1.

## Strengths and Limitations
This review contributes to the role and impacts of carers in supporting rehabilitation for people with dementia. We specifically examined the role of carer involvement in the health and well‐being of people with dementia and their carers and added understanding to the level, types, details and experiences of carer involvement and their relationship with the intervention outcomes to support future partnerships with carers in rehabilitation. Our review was comprehensive, and all authors reviewed the data extraction and synthesis process to ensure the rigour of this review.
However, caution must be taken when interpreting the intervention effects due to the small sample sizes, varied intervention characteristics, a combination of different interventions, and diverse roles of carer involvement. Also, as the same intervention could be modified to increase acceptability, concluding the relationship between the outcomes and the impacts of carer involvement on rehabilitation was challenging. The exclusion of papers without carer involvement limits examining effect differences between similar rehabilitation with and without carer involvement. However, the number of studies excluded due to this reason was small (n = 44). As all included studies do not directly evaluate the role of carers in rehabilitation, we can only synthesise details of carer involvement based on possible time commitments and demands of the interventions from the original text descriptions. Furthermore, as our search terms were limited to English and most identified articles were conducted in Western countries, cultural differences and expectations on carer involvement in rehabilitation may not be fully explored globally.

5.

## Conclusion
The effectiveness of rehabilitation has been largely explored in dementia care literature, but little focus has been placed on the role of carers. Our findings identified patterns between carer involvement levels and types of rehabilitation interventions. However, due to high study heterogeneity and imbalance in the numbers of different rehabilitation intervention studies, there was insufficient evidence to suggest which interventions will yield the best health benefits in terms of intervention length, intensity, delivery methods, and having carers as enablers or implementors. More research is needed to examine the effect differences between rehabilitation concerning carer involvement.

6.

## Differences Between Protocol and Review
The initial research question for this review was: Does carer involvement in dementia‐specific non‐pharmacological interventions improve the health and well‐being related outcomes of people living with dementia and carers? Upon reviewing the initial search results, it was deemed appropriate to revise the question to enable more specific aspects of carer involvement to be answered.
