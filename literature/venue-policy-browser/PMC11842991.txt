## Abstract

## Objectives
This study aims to analyse the disparity between the rules and procedures at social venues in Uganda and the current regulatory requirements concerning venue worker safety.

## Methods
Between 2021 and 2023, we analysed national laws/policies impacting the safety and health of venue workers in Uganda. This analysis was complemented by data collected from 66 in-depth interviews with venue workers. Additionally, we organised two subnational/national dialogues with key stakeholders in Uganda.

## Results
Our findings revealed minimal adherence to national regulations concerning workplace safety and health at venues. Many rules/procedures implemented at these venues directly contradicted the principles outlined in national regulations. There were barely any laws explicit to the safety and health of workers at social venues with the exception of the Hotels Act, 2000, which only applies to venues offering accommodation services. The identified significant regulatory compliance gaps in social venues include inconsistent and low wages, discriminatory recruitment practices such as a preference for young and beautiful females and the employment of underage workers.

## Conclusion
Employment in social venues continues to lack regulations that address the unique occupational hazards, many of which are linked with increased risk of HIV acquisition. Furthermore, existing regulations relevant to the sector are not complied with by social venues, and consequently safe workplace in these environments remains a significant challenge.

## WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC
The risky work environment at social venues is influenced by specific rules and procedures in these venues. However, it is unclear whether these rules and procedures are in alignment with national regulations.

## WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
This study is the first to examine the regulatory compliance gaps between existing national legislation and the unwritten rules governing social venues in Uganda.
It reveals minimal adherence to national regulations on workplace safety and health by these venues. Moreover, there was barely any law explicit to the safety and health of workers at social venues, except for the Uganda Hotels Act, 2000, whose scope was limited to only venues offering accommodation services.
The study underscores opportunities for policy improvement such as enacting venue-specific policies and broadening of venue licensing requirements to include HIV prevention measures which should be embraced by those working in HIV prevention among key and priority populations.

## HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY
This study contributes to the growing evidence base that underscores the need for structural interventions targeting key and priority populations for HIV, such as social venue workers, whose work environment contributes to their vulnerability to HIV. The regulatory compliance gaps identified in this study can be used to inform better planning and prioritisation of workers’ safety and health matters at social venues, through streamlined implementation mechanisms and stakeholder coordination. Additionally, the findings can guide the training of venue managers in the existing and new workers’ safety and health standards.

## Introduction
 Social venues (commercial places for entertainment or socialisation such as nightclubs, bars, hotels, restaurants, karaoke parlours, video halls, massage parlours and dance halls) are venues where individuals can go to relax, have fun, drink alcohol or find a sexual partner. These venues are recognised as significant employers and entry points into the labour market for young people. However, employment in social venues is associated with challenges such as bullying, disruptive and aggressive behaviours, excessive drinking, as well as sexual harassment and overt sexual behaviours all of which pose safety and health risks, including HIV. The social context of these work settings further increases workers exposure to various risk behaviours such as alcohol consumption, sexual harassment, condomless sex and sexual activities, including sex work. 
Additionally, the physical characteristics of social venues such as dim lighting, dark corners, room service and unisex bathrooms can create environments conducive to on-site sexual activity. Social venues are generally recognised in the literature as high-risk working environments linked to the incidence of HIV or sexually transmitted diseases (STIs) and associated risky behaviours. These risks can have devastating impacts on not only the workers, but also on the enterprises, surrounding communities and economies.
As highlighted by prior studies, the safety and health of workers at social venues remain a significant public health challenge due to the persistent work-related risks in these settings. According to the International Labor Organization, safety and health at work relate to “all aspects of working life, from the quality and safety of the physical environment, to how workers feel about their work, their working environment, the climate at work and work organization”. Occupational safety and health are fundamentally the responsibility of the government, which must collaborate with its agencies, the private sector, development partners and employers to ensure a safe working environment.
In Uganda, all matters related to occupational safety and health fall under the jurisdiction of the Occupational Safety and Health Department within the Ministry of Gender, Labor, and Social Development. Uganda has ratified several international labour conventions, which include rights such as freedom to form trade unions, freedom from forced labour, non-discrimination in employment, social security and acceptable conditions of work. In alignment with the above international standards, Uganda has made significant strides in developing laws and policies that uphold these standards. Key frameworks include the Occupational Safety and Health Policy, the National Child Labor Policy, the National Policy on HIV-AIDS and the World of Work, the Labor Unions Act among others. These frameworks provide the mechanisms for implementing occupational safety and health standards in Uganda, requiring employers such as venue managers to comply with the set safety and health standards and regulations.
To our knowledge, there is no comprehensive information available on the compliance with the regulatory requirements concerning occupational safety and health in employment linked to social venues in Uganda. However, research indicates that the high risks associated with social venues stem from weak regulation, poor enforcement and limited labour organisation. This article compares the current national legislation on worker safety and health and the unwritten rules (informal institutions) governing social venues in Uganda. Additionally, we explore stakeholders’ perspectives on opportunities for reducing workers’ vulnerability to HIV in socialising environments.

## Methods

## Study design
The study used a qualitative research design. It used document analysis, in-depth interviews (IDI) and dialogue meetings conducted between October 2021 and April 2023 in Uganda. A qualitative approach provides necessary in-depth and exploratory tools to achieve a clear picture of the phenomenon (in this case, the current regulations at a national level and the unwritten rules at social venues). In addition, some scholars have documented deliberative methods, such as the dialogue meetings used in this study, to facilitate free and open discussion and debate within and between citizens, policy actors and researchers. The dialogue meetings used in this study also facilitated the validation of the results of the document search and IDI. Furthermore, document analysis has also been documented as an effective tool in healthcare policy-making and research. 

## Document analysis
This method involved a review of regulatory documents relating to workers’ safety and health in Uganda. The review aimed to elucidate a contemporary worker safety and health policy context to enable an analysis of the degree to which social venues complied with the regulatory requirements that apply to them. It also identified gaps between internally established systems and the regulatory requirements at a national level. We extracted clauses or specific texts that implied occupation safety and health in Uganda, including texts from the parent legislative framework, the Constitution of the Republic of Uganda (1995), the labour laws, health, gender and other related laws and policies.
Document analysis drew from Walter and Gilson’s Health Policy Triangle (HPT) framework for analysing public health policies. The framework posits that health policy research should not only focus on the content of the policy, but also the actors, context and processes. Whereby content includes policy objectives, operational policies, legislation, regulations, guidelines, and so on; actors refer to influential individuals, groups and organisations; context refers to systemic factors: social, economic, political, cultural and other environmental conditions; and process refers to the way in which policies are initiated, developed or formulated, negotiated, communicated, implemented and evaluated. In particular, the constructs highlighted by the HPT framework guided the document review and data extraction. Furthermore, the document analysis process was guided by the Ready materials, Extract data, Analyze data, and Distil (READ) guidelines (25), which provides a step-by-step guide to conducting document analysis for qualitative policy research. ## Step 1: ready your materials
The regulatory documents were systematically identified using the following strategies:
Searching grey literature databases relevant to the subject of the review.
Conducting Google searches for documents published on the internet by the first author. Other documents were also identified and obtained through a pragmatic and purposive desktop internet search of the Uganda Ministry of Health, Ministry of Labor Gender and Social Development, Uganda AIDS Commission, Ministry of local government, the Parliament of Uganda, Makerere University archive and websites of other government entities.
The search yielded a total of 36 regulatory documents. Of the 36 documents retrieved, 24 were considered relevant for this study. shows the documents that were included in this study: one constitution, 12 legislative Acts, 10 policy documents and one national guideline.
 

## Inclusion criteria
Consistent with earlier approaches, we defined ‘a law’ as a body or a system of rules that are created and enforced through social or governmental institutions to regulate and oversee the behaviour of the individuals living in society. A ‘policy document’ was defined as any national policy, strategy, or action plan. Two reviewers (the first author SB and a research assistant of graduate level of education) assessed the eligibility of the regulatory documents against the following inclusion criteria, with disagreement resolved by a consensus meeting:
Regulations described rights, entitlements, obligations and national approaches to workplace safety, health and well-being.
Policies were the most contemporary for the country (ie, superseded policies were not included). In order to judge whether a policy was superseded, reviewers examined whether the contents (eg, vision, goals, target areas and scope) of older policies were reasonably reflected in an updated version. Where the reviewers agreed that the scope and foci of an older policy were reflected in a new version, the older version was excluded. Where an older version contained information (eg, targets, foci and strategies) that differed substantially from a newer policy, both documents were included.

## Step 2: extract data
Two reviewers (the first author and the research assistant) extracted data from the regulatory documents. To ensure consistency during document review and data extraction, we adapted and used a standard data extraction template developed previously. The template guided extraction on the vision and scope of the regulatory document; aspects of worker safety, health and well-being including what objectives/aims were proposed; and how they would be achieved through specific strategies/actions. Basic data such as the document title, author and date were also extracted. The parameters that guided the literature review and analysis are policy content for worker safety, health and well-being in Uganda (ie, policy objectives, operational policies, regulations/guidelines such as rights, entitlements, obligations and institutional arrangements), actors and processes ( ). The extracted content revealed the rights, entitlements, obligations and institutional arrangements for worker safety, health and well-being in Uganda.
 

## Step 3: analyse data
The data extracted from the various policy documents were collated. We conducted a qualitative content analysis to examine the data. Content analysis describes a family of approaches for systematic examination of texts. It is the close, comprehensive and organised reading of a set of texts to identify themes, intent or patterns. We applied a conventional qualitative content analysis approach (‘open coding’ or inductive)—no predetermined codes or themes. The first author and a research assistant reviewed the summary extracts (text from the regulations) and jointly generated codes. They read all the texts line by line for comprehension and manually selected relevant lines of text to be ‘coded’ and sorted into themes. Our objective was to capture and describe the most complete spectrum of policy activities related to worker safety and health. The first author and a research assistant discussed the codes and categorised them. The team identified consistent and overarching themes and supporting subthemes by analysing and sorting codes. The sorted themes were reviewed and refined by all the authors. Although presented as a linear, step-by-step procedure, the analysis was an iterative and reflexive process. 

## Step 4: distil the findings
Following the analysis of the extracted data, the first author took lead on distilling the findings. The findings were distilled into a series of tables and quotes woven into a narrative presenting a general description of regulatory compliance gaps relating to work safety and health at social venues in Uganda. The brief illustrative quotes interspersed within selected paragraphs subtly demonstrated the richness of data.

## Data from the interviews with venue workers and managers
We used secondary data from an earlier qualitative study that explored the rules and procedures at social venues in Uganda and how the rules influenced HIV vulnerability for the venue workers. For this paper, we specifically used data from 22 venue managers and 44 young workers, describing the rules and procedures at social venues. The primary study was conducted between October and December 2021 in the greater Rakai district in the central region of Uganda. The detailed methodology of the primary study is elaborated elsewhere. 

## Dialogue meeting with key stakeholders
Forty-nine national and subnational stakeholders were engaged in two dialogue meetings held at subnational (Rakai) and national levels. Each meeting constituted about 20–25 participants. At the beginning of every meeting, participants were informed about the purpose of the meeting, including intentions to analyse and publish excerpts from the meeting deliberations. The stakeholders engaged through these dialogues included officials responsible for worker safety, health, and well-being, representatives from social venues, local government decision-makers, policy-makers from relevant ministries and government agencies. The meeting deliberations focused on practices, guiding laws, rules and policies affecting venue workers’ safety and health. The guiding questions included: Why does the current situation persist at the venue despite known risks? What needs to be done to close the gaps and get closer to full compliance with the identified regulatory requirements? These broad questions were used to structure and guide the discussions in the meeting and participants’ contributions. Participants were divided into small groups of discussions and plenary sessions held to share group summaries and agree on divergent views.
The meetings also validated the findings from the review of regulation documents relating to workers’ safety and health as well as IDI data. These findings were shared in the dialogue meetings before the deliberation questions were discussed. The national-level meeting was conducted in English, an official language in Uganda. The participants of the national-level meeting ably dialogued competently in English without translating into the local dialects. On the other hand, the subnational meeting was conducted in a local dialect used in Rakai.

## Data analysis
Analysis of the IDIs and dialogue deliberations: the IDIs and dialogue meetings deliberations were analysed using thematic content analysis. A coding structure consisting of themes and constructs (both a priori and emergent) was developed in an iterative process. The first author reviewed the IDI transcripts and the reports on the deliberative meetings without the coding structure and inductively identified concepts and themes raised from the individual interviews and meeting deliberations. The coding structure evolved to include specific topics outlined earlier and novel concepts or subtopics that allowed a greater appreciation of the depth and breadth of the data presented. The first author and the research assistant coded the transcripts using Atlas.ti (V.22). The team reviewed the coded text to generate themes and identify illustrative quotations for each theme. The analysis was discussed, and discrepancies in coding, and other issues that required clarity were settled by discussion. Quotes that best described the various categories and expressed what was aired frequently in several groups were chosen.

## Gap analysis
The rights, entitlements, obligations and institutional arrangements for worker safety, health and well-being in Uganda extracted from the national regulations were compared with the existing rules and procedures at the social venues in Uganda (identified from the secondary data—IDIs with venue workers and managers). The rights, entitlements, obligations and institutional arrangements relating to the safety and health of workers at social venues provided a benchmark to compare the workers safety and health standards or rules and procedures at the venues in Uganda, in order to establish compliance by the venues. A summary of the gap analysis is presented in . The rights, entitlements, obligations and institutional arrangements relating to work safety and health were categorised into four areas:
Standards related to recruitment practices, remuneration of workers and compensation.
Standards related to discrimination and inequalities at workplace.
Standards related to sexual harassment and other unfriendly working conditions.
Standards related to HIV/AIDS prevention and treatment at workplace.
Standards related to operational guidelines and collective bargaining.
Relatedly, the stakeholder dialogue meetings held at subnational and national level validated the findings from the document analysis, IDIs as well as the gap analysis results. This engagement facilitated the discussion of recommendations for improvement of the regulatory frameworks regarding venue worker safety and health.

## Patient and public involvement
In this study, local research teams were used to collect data. In addition, research uptake engagements were conducted at district and national levels. Several HIV/AIDS stakeholders both at subnational and national levels were engaged through the research uptake engagements

## Results
 compares the current national legislation relating to worker safety and health and the unwritten rules at social venues in Uganda. The findings on the regulatory compliance of social venues are organised into six overarching themes: (1) standards related to recruitment, remuneration and compensation; (2) standards related to discrimination and inequalities; (3) standards related to unfriendly working conditions; (4) standards related to HIV/AIDS prevention and care; (5) operating procedures and collection action and (6) policy recommendations. These themes reflect the most repeated topics observed, comparing the excerpts from the current national legislation relating to worker safety and health and the rules and procedures at social venues.
Overall, there was low adherence to the national workplace safety and health regulations at social venues. Most unwritten rules at social venues contradicted the principles of workplace safety and health. There were barely any laws explicit to the safety and health of workers at social venues except for the Hotel Act. The Hotel Act was, however, limited to only venues that offered accommodation and broadly emphasised customers’ safety.

## Theme 1: standards related to recruitment, remuneration and compensation
Several rights, entitlements, obligations and institutional provisions from the national regulations highlighted standards associated to recruitment practices, remuneration of workers and compensation in case of injuries or disease. Some standards emphasised included proper contracting of all workers, equal pay for equal work done, timely payment, overtime and leaves of absence, among others. Except for a few big venues, most social venues had casual workers, who were verbally contracted. The contract of service was neither written nor detailed as stipulated by the law. There were also significant variations in wages across venues, and in general, the wages were very low (ranging from less than US$ 1 to US$2 per day). There were no payments for day-offs, sick leave and no compensation in case of injury or disease obtained while at work. Non-compliance with social security remittance was another regulatory compliance gap relating to the recruitment and remuneration of workers at the venues.
I get UGX 4000 ($ 1.1) per day, but sometimes we don’t get paid, or we are paid the next day because some days are bad with very few customers, Participant 1—Female in early 20s.
Similarly, employment of workers under 18 was common, especially at small, rural venues. This contradicted the national legislation which prohibits contracting minors under 18 years. The young workers at the venues were also not protected from sexual and economic exploitation at the venues. During the subnational deliberative meeting, one venue representative confirmed that employment of minors was still a common vice at social venues in the study area.
The reality is that many venues employ children under 18 years old. Though some children disguise their age when they come to us in search of jobs. But still, some venue managers prefer young workers because they are cheap labor and not very exposed, Venue representative 2, Sub-national dialogue meeting.

## Theme 2: standards related to discrimination and inequalities at work
Several regulations in Uganda emphasised non-discrimination. For example, the equal opportunities commission Act, 2007 states, “Elimination of discrimination and inequalities against any individual or group of persons on the ground of sex, age, race, color, ethnic origin, tribe, birth, creed or religion, health status, social or economic standing, political opinion or disability, and take affirmative action in favor of groups marginalized based on gender, age, disability or any other reason created by history, tradition or custom for redressing imbalances which exist against them; and to provide for other related matters.”
Despite the emphasis, our findings highlighted several discriminatory recruitment practices and inequalities at the venues, notably, the beauty bias in hiring, hiring preference for female, unmarried young workers and providing accommodation to mainly female workers. There were also inequalities in the payments of venue workers. Regarding discriminatory hiring practices, workers remarked:
Here [at the social venue], they prefer female workers except for specific roles that require physical strength, like security [laughs]. With age, they [the employers] always like young girls in their twenties or even below 20 years, and they must be physically attractive. If a worker is old, they should outstand in appearance to be considered for the job here [at the social venue]. Participant 2—Male in early 20s.

## Theme 3: standards related to sexual harassment and other unfriendly working conditions
Sexual harassment was the most prominent unfriendly working condition at social venues. Sexual harassment may include sexual intercourse, sexual contact or any sexual activity that is on the promise of preferential treatment, detrimental treatment or based on threats to future employment. This behaviour may be through language, visual materials and physical or sexual acts. Despite the law prohibiting such in employment relations in Uganda, this study reported several forms of sexual harassment at the venues. They included uninvited sexual or suggestive comments or jokes about the workers’ physical looks, sexual gestures through hand or body movements, and requests for sexual favours. Other forms of sexual harassment at the venues included inappropriate physical touching of the workers’ body parts, including fondling, hugging, or kissing, sexual intercourse and discussions about sexual relations/fantasies at work, among others. One venue worker remarked:
The rules here (venue) favor the customers; a customer can come and call you to serve him, but before he makes his order, sometimes he can start asking for other things like relationship, sex, or ever touches you. You may choose to move away, but he can complain to your boss that you do not have a good customer care, Participant 6—Female in early 20s.
Other unfriendly working conditions at the venues included mistreatment of workers, non-provision of food and other essentials, that is, medical care, unfriendly working hours and shifts, unjust disciplinary codes, unfavourable power relations between employer and worker, and customer and worker. Power relations exhibit rude bosses and customers.

## Theme 4: standards related to HIV/AIDS prevention and treatment
Promotion of the principle of non-discrimination and protection of the rights and dignity of persons affected and infected by HIV/AIDS was emphasised across most national regulations. Relatedly, the provision of HIV services was also alluded to in some regulations, such as the Uganda National HIV/AIDS Policy, the National policy on HIV/AIDS and The World of Work, among others. At venue level, however, the study noted a gap in the availability of HIV-related services. Where services were reported, they were not as compressive as alluded to by the national policies relating to HIV service delivery.

## Theme 5: operational guidelines and collective bargaining
According to the national regulations, employers were expected to have workplace policies on the safety and health of workers. These were barely present at social venues. The national-level deliberative meeting revealed some workers’ unions relating to social venues, such as the Uganda Hotels Food, Tourism & Allied Workers Union and the Uganda Hospitality, Leisure, and Allied Workers Union. However, the interviews and deliberations at a subnational level were silent on existence and workers’ membership to those or related unions.

## Theme 6: policy recommendations
Stakeholder deliberations highlighted some policy recommendations. These included broadening of venue licensing requirements to include HIV prevention measures; and enacting of regulations that are explicit to the issues affecting venue workers.
Development of venue-specific regulations: this subtheme reflects discussions about developing laws and policies that are explicit to the social venue work setting. Only the Hotel Act, 2000, was specific to venues offering accommodation services. This implied that the current regulations did not explicitly address other types of venues, such as bars, restaurants, massage parlours and discotheques, among others. Given the uniqueness, severity and predominately health-related risks at social venues, stakeholders proposed developing a comprehensive venue-specific law and policy. Relatedly, they proposed developing venue-workplace policies promoting HIV prevention among workers. The stakeholders further mentioned that these workplace policies should be made mandatory and part of the requirements for venue licensing. In the same vein, the deliberations also highlighted enforcement of national laws at the venues, a case in point employment of minors. One participated remarked:
Members [meeting participants] we should agree and take seriously the issue of employment of minors in socializing venues. These are our future generation and require to be protected. The laws prohibit employment of minors… We should work together in our individual capacities to eliminate this practice. Venue managers present, I appeal to you not to recruit girls below 18 years…, the gender and probation offices conduct unexpected inspections at these venues to assess the situation on the ground. District representative 1, Sub-national dialogue meeting.
Revision of licensing requirements to accommodate HIV prevention: subnational—town councils licensed venues in urban centres, while the subcountries licensed those in rural settings. Only two licensing requirements related to worker safety and health at the venues: the condition of the premises and the presence of sanitation facilities. This highlights the missed opportunity to add HIV prevention measures as requirements for licensing. The opportunity to revise the current venue licensing requirements to include the availability of HIV prevention services such as condoms, pre-exposure prophylaxis and Health education for workers was proposed. One participant remarked:
When licensing the social venues, we inspect the venue for the safety or condition of the premises, and presence of sanitary facilities. True, if more conditions related to workers safety such as, presence condom dispensers or other measures were added onto the list of licensing requirements, we would still put them look out for them when licensing venues, District representative 4, Sub-national dialogue meeting.

## Discussion
This study analysed the regulatory compliance gap between the existing relevant legislation and the unwritten rules at social venues in Uganda. Findings from this study show minimal adherence to the national regulations relating to worker safety and health by social venues. A comparable adherence (poor) was reported by similar study, though from a developed setting. The study reported a substantial lack of compliance with the national regulations relating to workers’ safety and health regulations by restaurants. The minimal adherence to national regulations reported in the current study is indicative of the implementation realities at the venues that impede adherence or implementation of these regulations. Examples of implementation realities at the venues include the nature of employment and the worker interactions at the venues, the grey zones of the venue work setting such as workplace relationships, power dynamics between venue actors, bullying and normalised sexual harassment practices. Relatedly, the current study showed that most unwritten rules at the venues contradicted the principles of workplace safety and health set out in the national regulations. A systematic review of occupational health and safety legislation and regulatory implementation showed that regulation relating to workers’ safety and health might be present but difficult to implement because of implementation realities such as malcoordination of authorities, under-resourced inspectorates and workplace conditions that limit worker representation. Furthermore, there were barely any laws explicit to the safety and health of workers at the venues except for the Hotels Act, 2000, whose scope was limited to only venues offering accommodation services. Having laws specific to the social venue industry could tailor regulations to the unique implementation realities at the venues. A policy study on workplace safety and health laws and policies in the same context (Uganda) highlighted challenges such as limited human capacity, poor planning, limited transparency and accountability that could explain the status quo. 
This study also documented several compliance gaps with the existing national regulations regarding worker safety and health at the venues. These included employment of minors, low wage, hour rules violation, lack of on-job training, no labour laws notifications at the venues and unfriendly working conditions such as sexual harassment, non-compensation for injuries or diseases incurred on the job, among others. The compliance gaps reported in this study have also been documented elsewhere. For example, a survey of 433 Chinatown restaurant workers reported that 95% of workers earned less than a living wage, 50% earned less than minimum wage, 42% never received paid sick days, 31% never received their full wages owed and only 3% received health insurance from their employers. From a sub-Saharan setting, a review of the literature showed that conditions such as low wages, long working hours and part-time or temporary employment arrangements still dominated the hospitality industry in South Africa, hence highlighting the need for more programmes and policies addressing these unfriendly conditions at social venues.
Furthermore, inadequate safety and health standards and environmental hazards are particularly evident in the informal sector. Social venues in Uganda are mainly private enterprises under the informal sector. Venue workers in Uganda have a limited ability to exercise their rights or bargain for improved work conditions because of the informal nature of their work. The informal sector is characterised by employment situations and labour relations which not only differ from those in the formal sector but also often infringe established rules and laws. Notably, this paper highlighted the absence of explicit laws on the safety and health of workers at social venues. In the absence of legal protection, experiences of violence, abuse or exploitation from clients, employers or other parties may not be registered; in addition, workers may avoid reporting for fear of losing their employment. Relatedly, the criminalisation, regulation and policing of sex work (for example in Uganda) are documented to lead to human rights abuses, such as workplace raids, harassment, arrests, rape, confiscation of condoms and extortion of bribe by police. A study from Cameroon showed that individuals who undertake sex work are deeply affected by verbal, physical and sexual violence perpetrated by clients, police, intimate partners and others throughout their lifetime. Our earlier study reported that venue workers practised transactional sex and therefore may be equally affected. The protection of the health and welfare of informal workers is a challenge that should be addressed with an integrated approach to health promotion, social protection and quality employment creation.
Lastly, this study documented policy recommendations, including revision of venue licensing guidelines to include HIV prevention services at the venues and enacting of explicit laws for the hospitality industry. Employment linked to social venues (hospitality industry) is one of the largest employers in Uganda and elsewhere. In the recent times, such venues have provided employment to about 144 million workers worldwide (4.6% of total global employment). Relatedly, the sector records a high proportion of unskilled part-time workers and often has a young workforce with up to 50% of employees under 25 years. It is also observed that child labour is common in this sector. These coupled with the gendered nature of employment in this sector, with women relegated to lower paid employment and exposure to sexual risks highlights the need for explicit laws that address the challenges that are unique to this sector.
In the current situation, the opportunity exists to broaden venue licensing requirements to include HIV prevention measures, that is, periodic provision of HIV/AIDS education to venue workers, developing workplace HIV notices, and availability of condom dispensers and condoms, among others.

## Strengths and limitations
To our knowledge, this research is the first study to explore the regulatory compliance gaps at social venues in Uganda and opportunities for reducing HIV vulnerability at the venue level. This paper contributes to the body of literature on HIV in the workplace by focusing on a subpopulation of workers with risks like the traditionally defined key populations of HIV. This paper highlights many regulatory compliance gaps at the venue level, which could be addressed by having protective measures included in the venue licensing requirements, expanding preventive services to venues and enforcing the existing and new regulations relating to the safety and health of venue workers. We applied rigorous, best practice and established methods to each part of the research. For example, we used several data sources, that is, document review, IDIs and deliberative meetings, to develop a comprehensive understanding of the phenomena hence data source triangulation.
Important to note are the limitations of this study. Document analysis, like any research method, can be subject to concerns regarding validity, reliability, authenticity, motivated authorship, lack of representativity and so on. However, we mitigated these by using standard techniques to enhance qualitative rigour, such as the READ approach. Our study was also modest for IDIs and policies in one country, and thus, we cannot speculate whether our results are transferable to other similar settings. Nonetheless, we believe the study presents essential insights into the contemporary safety and health issues of workers at employment linked to social venues, hence can aid labour officers, health managers, licensing and compliance officers, venue owners/managers, implementing partners and other stakeholders in assuring systems conform to regulations and satisfy the workers’ safety and health requirements.

## Conclusions
Findings from this study highlight three crucial needs for the safety and health of workers at social venues: (1) the creation of a more supportive policy environment by aligning the existing policies and or formulating explicit policies on venue worker’s safety and health; (2) defining the HIV service package for this subpopulation, and extending the delivery of this package to social venues; and (3) there is an urgent need for smart ways to apply the existing safety and health standards, that is, by incorporating health protection measures in an existing arrangement such licensing. The regulatory compliance gaps identified in this study can be used to inform improved planning and prioritisation of workers’ safety and health matters at social venues through streamlined implementation mechanisms and stakeholder coordination, as well as the training of venue managers in the existing and new workers’ safety and health standards.
