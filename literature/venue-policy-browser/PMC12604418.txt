## Background
The impact of screen exposure on children’s language development has been widely debated. However, some characteristics of screen use, particularly the influence of the pandemic over the past three years have yet to be sufficiently investigated.

## Objectives
To examine the association between screen exposure, particularly during the pandemic, and language development delays in children aged 18–72 months.

## Methods
A total of 2100 children who attended the children’s language development clinic at Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center and Yuncheng Street Community Health Service Center in Guangzhou, China, were recruited into the study between January 2020 and September 2023. Children’s demographic and developmental characteristics were obtained through a structured questionnaire, and their language development delays were evaluated through the Sign-Significate Relations (S-S) assessment, which was subsequently validated by the China Developmental Scale for Children method based on the voluntary principle. Multivariate logistic regression models were employed to analyze the association between screen exposure and language development delay. In order to gain further insight, the data was stratified according to whether or not the children had experienced a period of social isolation due to the COVID-19 pandemic, the parenting environment and the quality of screen usage.

## Results
More than half of the children (53.29%) were exposed to screens for over 1 h daily. After adjusting for potential confounders, a dose-response relationship was observed between children’s daily screen exposure exceeding 1 h and the risk of delayed language development. The stratified analyses confirmed the main findings, revealing stronger associations in children who had experienced at least one period of quarantine, whose primary language spoken at home was Cantonese, whose caregivers had an intermediate personality, whose daily outdoor activities were less than 2 h, who did not have interaction with caregivers during screen time, and who watched educational videos.

## Conclusion
This study suggested that excessive screen exposure in children was associated with a heightened likelihood of language development delays, especially among children who experienced COVID-19 quarantine with prolonged screen time.

## Introduction
 Screen exposure includes activities such as watching television, playing video games, and using mobile phones, tablets, and other electronic devices [ ]. The accessibility and convenience of electronic media have driven the ongoing development of, software applications and electronic devices designed aimed at children’s education and entertainment, highlighting the growing trend of their screen exposure [ ]. A study in the United States found that the daily screen exposure time among children aged 1–3 increased from 53.45 min at age 1 to 150.65 min by age 3 [ ]. A study conducted in Singapore also indicated that a majority of children under the age of 2 were exposed to electronic screens for approximately one hour daily [ ]. About one-third of children aged 1–3 in Shanghai, China, engaged in daily computer screen time exceeding two hours [ ]. Children’s average daily screen exposure time has been steadily increasing, while the age of initial screen exposure is getting younger [ ].
The existing literature on the impact of screen use on children’s language development remains inconclusive. Some studies have indicated that high-quality screen usage, which includes watching educational programs and co-viewing with caregivers, showed a positive correlation with children’s language skills [ – ]. Participating in advanced virtual reality games and watching well-paced educational videos enhanced children’s language learning effectiveness [ – ]. Certain studies have also demonstrated that co-viewing screen time with caregivers could improve children’s attention and vocabulary [ , , ]. Conversely, excessive screen exposure in early life is believed to have a detrimental effect on children’s language development. Especially in cases where parents do not engage in screen-viewing activities, extended screen time may function as a passive stimulus rather than facilitating active language learning, and in turn, has potential to disrupt children’s normal language development [ , ].
The process by which children learn from media is influenced by the 3 C parenting model (child, content, and context) [ ]. However, previous studies have mainly focused on the duration of screen usage, neglecting screen content quality and screen-viewing context. Further investigation is anticipated to ascertain the influence of varied screen exposure and content on children’s language development delays [ , , ]. Additionally, the activities of caregivers also play an important role in a child’s screen viewing [ – ]. Notably, during the COVID-19 pandemic, there was a marked increase in children’s use of digital devices as a substitute for outdoor activities and social interaction, and in the overall time spent by parents with their children, which may have further amplified the impact of 3 C parenting [ ].
Given that language development is foundational for other areas of development, including cognition, social and academic skills [ , ], understanding its potential delays is critical. Furthermore, most children with speech and language delays do not exhibit clear histological damage or visible indicators in their early stages, making it challenging for caregivers to identify these delays [ ]. Therefore, it is essential to focus on the impact of screen exposure on children’s language development in the critical period of growth (from birth to age 6) [ ]. In addition, the impact of complex screen exposure environments on children’s language development under the prolonged home confinement due to COVID-19 isolation policies remains unclear [ , , ].
To address the gap, we conducted a more comprehensive survey and analysis of the multidimensional characteristics of screen exposure among 2100 children aged 18–72 months in southern China. We focused various factors, including the type of video content, interaction between the caregivers and the child during screen time, children’s outdoor exercise, the unique circumstances of the COVID-19 period, and the language environment. The objective of this study is to examine the association between screen exposure and language delays in children and to contribute to the growing body of research in this field.

## Methods

## Study population
From January 2020 to September 2023, we recruited children who attended the children’s language development clinic at Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center and received routine physical examinations at Yuncheng Street Community Health Service Center in Guangzhou, China. During this research period, a total of 2188 children were initially enrolled. After excluding those who had difficulty following medical advice (n = 2), exhibited poor compliance during treatment (n = 5), or refused to participate in the study (n = 9), and 2 participants declined the questionnaire interview, and 2170 children finally completed the questionnaire in full. Further exclusions were made for children with congenital anomalies (n = 12), congenital metabolic disorders (n = 36), or neurological disorders (n = 2). Additionally, secondary records of patients who repeatedly visited in the short term (n = 20) were further excluded (see Fig.  ). Finally, 2100 children aged 18–72 months were included in the study, including 1657 children with language delay and 443 children with normal language development. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center (Approval No.2017053002). 

## Questionnaire information
Based on a review of previous relevant studies [ – ], a structured questionnaire was designed, considering the risk factors for language development delay and the multiple dimensions of screen use (see Supplementary Information File S1). Following the acquisition of informed consent, face-to-face questionnaire interviews are conducted by trained investigators with guardians in a quiet, warm, and confidential setting. Guardians provide self-reported information on multidimensional characteristics of children’s screen usage based on a thorough understanding of the questionnaire content, particularly regarding the types of screen content, such as educational or entertainment, and the presence of interactive screen behaviors, such as conversation, singing, dancing, imitating, or playing games. A total of 2172 caregivers participated in the questionnaire survey, of which 2170 were deemed valid questionnaires; the questionnaire validity response rate was 99.91% because two caregivers refused to participate in the interview and were therefore excluded from the analysis (see Fig. ).
The detailed questionnaire contained children’s demographic data, including gender (male, or female), age (in months) and birth information, like gestational age (in weeks), birth weight (in kg), delivery way (eutocia, cesarean, forceps delivery), whether there were any special circumstances during birth (pneumonia, hypoglycemia or hydrocephalus, etc.), or during the mother’s pregnancy (such as hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism, or gestational diabetes). Preterm infants were defined as those born at gestational age < 37 weeks. Whether the children had passed hearing screening, genetic family history (language development delay, autism, hereditary hearing loss and deafness, inherited mental retardation, etc.), and whether the child had any challenging behaviors (such as hyperactivity, inattention, losing temper easily, or hitting people) were also collected. The age at the initial vocalization and language development status (current vocabulary size, language comprehension, and expression status) were further recorded. Considering that the age at which a child first walks, the quality of sleep (poor, fair, or good), and poor dietary habits (picky eating, frequent eating of soft foods, holding food in the mouth) affected language development to varying degrees [ – ], so these factors were also collected.

## Screen exposure
The average daily screen time for children (i.e., viewing electronic devices such as television, computers and tablets, and any form of screen interaction) was set to different levels of options with the aim of improving response accuracy. These included categories such as “never watched”, “occasionally watched”, “< 1 hour”, “1–2 hours”, “2–3 hours” and “>3 hours”. Interaction between the caregivers and the child during the viewing screen time (not watching, no interaction, or yes) was also investigated which included caregivers explaining complex content, asking and answering questions, or imitating characters. Additionally, we collected information on video content type (including educational videos, or other types), and the age of first screen exposure (never, within 6 months of birth, 6 months to 1 year, 1–2 years, 2–3 years, >3 years).

## Family nurturing environment and experience of the COVID-19 pandemic
The questionnaire included primary caregivers (mother, father, grandmother, grandfather, nanny, other), mother’s and father’s education (primary school and below, junior high school, senior high/vocational school, junior college, undergraduate, graduate), the personality of primary caregivers (introversion, extroversion, intermediate), the primary language spoken at home (Cantonese, Mandarin, other), family members living with the child in the past six months (mother, father, grandmother, grandfather, nanny, other), the average daily outdoor activity time (< 1 h, 1–2 h, 2–3 h, >3 h), and average daily parent-child interaction time (< 1 h, 1–2 h, 2–3 h, 3–5 h, >5 h) which included parents engaging in activities such as reading, exercising, and crafting together with the child. Furthermore, the Chinese government enacted a rigorous quarantine policy in response to the COVID-19 outbreak, the specifics of which were illustrated in Figures S1 and S2 of the Supplementary Information. The present study also sought to ascertain whether children had undergone at least once quarantine prior to this visit and assessment (yes or no).

## Language developmental delay assessment
The Sign-Significate Relations (S-S) assessment is currently one of the most commonly used qualitative testing tools in the diagnosis and rehabilitation evaluation of language disorders in China [ ]. It assesses the language communication, comprehension, expression, and basic operational skills of children aged 1 to 6.5 years, determining whether they exhibit language developmental delays. This method was originally developed by the Speech Committee of the Japanese Phonetics and Speech Medical Association [ ], and introduced and revised according to the Chinese language system by the Language Department of the China Rehabilitation Research Center in 1990 [ ]. It divides the level of language development into five phases, with further subdivisions within each stage, and the specific standard corresponding to language development levels for each age group were presented in Table of the Supplementary Information. The S-S assessment was conducted by researchers who have received professional training in a setting that was quiet, comfortable, and private. And the S-S followed the same diagnostic criteria as previous research with different populations and backgrounds [ , ], children in our study were deemed to have language development delay if the result of their language expression ability age was lower than their actual age, or if both language expression and comprehension ability age fell below their actual age [ ].
In addition, to further validate the reliability of the S-S diagnosis, a subset of the children voluntarily underwent the China Developmental Scale for Children assessment which was formulated by the National Health Commission and widely used in children’s healthcare services [ ]. This assessment comprises five domains: gross motor, fine motor, adaptability, language, and social behavior, represented by developmental quotient 1 (DQ1), DQ2, DQ3, DQ4, and DQ5, respectively [ ]. The DQ for each area is calculated based on the measured developmental age (DA) and the child’s chronological age (CA), with the formula DQ = DA ÷ CA×100, and the synthetical DQ was the average of DQ1-5. A domain is considered developmentally abnormal when its DQ score < 80 [ ].

## Statistical analysis
Data analysis was conducted using the Statistical Analysis Software SPSS25.0 (Statistical Product and Service Solutions). A comprehensive set of descriptive statistics was conducted on the demographic characteristics, screen exposure, language development delay assessed by the S-S method, and DQ values of the 2,100 children who were ultimately included in the analysis. children. The key data points and relevant covariates were complete and free of missing values. Based on extensive research and expert consensus, which advised limiting screen time to a maximum of one hour per day for children [ , ], appropriate parametric or nonparametric methods were used to examine whether there were any differences in demographic characteristics and screen exposure between average daily screen exposure time < 1 h and ≥ 1 h. Multivariate logistic regression was employed to analyze the association between average daily screen exposure time and children’s language development delay.
Confounders were selected based on both biological and statistical considerations. Factors with significant differences in univariate analysis were included as covariates in the model. Finally, age (months), gestational age (weeks), gender (boy vs. girl), delivery way (eutocia, cesarean, or forceps delivery), any genetic family history (yes vs. no), dietary habits (normal vs. abnormal), mother’s education (primary school and below, junior high school, senior high/vocational school, junior college, undergraduate, graduate), video content type (not watching, educational videos, other), interaction between the caregivers and the child during the viewing screen time (not watching, no interaction, yes), personality of primary caregivers (introversion, extroversion, or intermediate), primary language spoken at home (Cantonese, Mandarin, or other), experienced at least once quarantine before assessment (yes vs. no), the child’s average daily outdoor activity (< 1 h, 1–2 h, 2–3 h, >3 h), average daily parent-child interaction time (< 1 h, 1–2 h, 2–3 h, 3–5 h, >5 h) were retained in the model as covariates. Additionally, the gestational age was transformed logarithmically to approximate normal distribution.
To further examine the association between average daily screen exposure characteristics and the language development delay, stratified analyses were conducted based on six variables: having experienced at least once quarantine before assessment (yes vs. no), the primary language spoken at home (Cantonese, Mandarin, or other), primary caregivers’ personality (introversion, extroversion, or intermediate), average daily outdoor exercise (< 1 h, 1–2 h, 2–3 h, >3 h), interaction between the caregivers and the child during the viewing screen time (no interaction vs. yes) and video content type (educational video or other). The study divided participants into homogeneous subgroups based on these variables and calculated the odds ratios for each subgroup. Statistical significance was determined by a two-sided p-value < 0.05 in all analyses.

## Results

## Demographic characteristics
The demographic characteristics of the study population were detailed in the Table  . Out of the 2100 participants, 1500 were boys (71.43%). More than half of the children (53.29%) were exposed to screens for over 1 h per day. Compared to the < 1 h group, children in the ≥ 1 h group were slightly older and more likely to be male. They also had higher rates of genetic family history and delayed language development, lower parental education, and less time for daily outdoor activity and parent-child interaction (all p < 0.05). 

## Screen usage and the language development delay
Table  showed that over half of the participants (55.14%) had their first screen exposure between the ages of 1 and 2 years old. The majority of children (76.43%) watched educational videos, and about half of the families (51.05%) accompanied them. (all p < 0.05). A total of 1657 children (78.90%) were diagnosed with language developmental delays through the S-S assessment (see Table  ). Furthermore, 48.24% of participants (n = 1013) completed the China Developmental Scale for Children assessment. Table  presented that the median values for DQ1, DQ2, DQ3, DQ4, DQ5 and the synthetical DQ (arithmetic mean of DQ1-5) were 91.00, 73.00, 77.00, 55.00, 66.00, and 73.00, respectively. In excess of half of the individuals displayed anomalous DQ values (61.90%). 

## Associations of average daily screen exposure time with language developmental delay
In the model 1, we found that any level of screen exposure was associated with delayed language development for children. After adjusting for various factors such as age, gestational age, gender, mode of delivery, presence of genetic family history, eating habits, video content type, interaction between the caregivers and the child during the viewing screen time, personality of primary caregivers, primary language spoken at home, experienced at least once quarantine before assessment, child’s average daily outdoor activity and average daily parent-child interaction time, the model 2 showed that the incidence of delayed language development in children who were exposed to 1–2 h, 2–3 h and > 3 h of daily screen time was 1.91 times (95%CI: 1.03, 3.55), 2.58 times (95%CI: 1.29, 5.15) and 4.08 times (95%CI: 1.79, 9.28), respectively, compared with those who were not exposed to screen. The model 3 further adjusted the mother’s education based on model 2, and showed that children exposed to 1–2 h, 2–3 h and > 3 h of daily screen time exhibited a higher risk of delayed language development, with odds ratios and 95%CI of 1.99 (95%CI: 1.06, 3.70), 2.68 (95%CI: 1.33, 5.39) and 4.24 (95%CI: 1.86, 9.70), respectively (see Fig.  ). Further analysis in the Table S2 (see the Supplementary Information) revealed a statistically significant correlation between prolonged screen viewing time and a low synthetical DQ score, particularly evident in DQ4 (all p < 0.05). These relationships were also observed in children with language developmental delays but not in the normal children (all p < 0.05). 

## Stratified by whether experienced at least once quarantine before assessment
When the average daily screen exposure was > 3 h, the increased risk of developmental delay was only found among children who had experienced at least once quarantine period to the assessment with an odds ratio of 3.23 times (95%CI: 1.02, 10.25), compared to children who had not been exposed to quarantine (see Fig.  ). 

## Stratified by the parenting environment
A stratified analysis by primary language at home revealed that children whose family primarily spoke Cantonese and were exposed to > 3 h of daily screen time had 6.28 times (95%CI: 1.18, 33.28) increased likelihood of developmental delay compared to those without electronic screen exposure. In households where Mandarin is the primary language, children who were exposed to 2–3 h of daily screen time exhibited 9.94 times (95%CI: 1.68, 58.76) increased likelihood of developmental delay, compared to those with no exposure to electronic screens. The results showed that children reared by caregivers with intermediate personalities (between introverted and extroverted) and who were exposed to screens for 2–3 h and > 3 h daily exhibited 3.40 times (95%CI: 1.32, 8.75) and 4.84 times (95%CI: 1.58, 14.84) increased likelihood of developmental delays, compared to those who were not exposed to electronic screens. Furthermore, children who spent less than 1 h or 1–2 h outdoors per day, and were exposed to > 3 h daily screen time, respectively had 14.99 times (95%CI: 1.07, 210.02) and 4.92 times (95%CI: 1.43, 16.87) higher odds of developmental delay compared to those not exposed to screens (see Fig.  ). 

## Stratified by screen usage environment
Children who had no caregiver-child interaction and were exposed to screens for 2–3 h or > 3 h per day were more prone to have language development delay than those exposed to screen occasionally, with odd ratios of 2.46 (95%CI: 1.04, 5.85) and 14.93 (95%CI: 3.12, 71.54), respectively. Additionally, children exposed to screen time for 1–2 h or 2–3 h in the presence of family were more likely to experience developmental delay than those exposed on an occasional basis, with odds ratios of 1.94 (95%CI: 1.18, 3.18) and 2.01 (95%CI: 1.06, 3.83), respectively. For children who watched educational videos, screen time for < 1 h showed a weak protective effect against language development delays (OR = 0.82, 95%CI: 0.54,1.24), but the association was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). However, compared with children who were occasionally exposed to educational videos, those who were exposed to screen time for 1–2 h, 2–3 h, and > 3 h had 1.99 times (95%CI: 1.29, 3.06), 2.25 times (95%CI: 1.31, 3.85) and 3.51 times (95%CI: 1.76, 7.02) risks of language developmental delays, respectively (see Fig.  ). 

## Discussion
In this study, we evaluated the association between screen exposure and language development delay among 2100 children aged 18–72 months. Our findings indicated a significant dose-response relationship between average daily screen exposure exceeding 1 h and an increased risk of delayed language development, which was further validated by regression results using the DQ scores. Stratified analyses revealed that the association between screen usage and language development delay was more pronounced among children who had experienced at least once quarantine prior to assessment, whose primary household language was Cantonese, whose caregivers exhibited intermediate personality traits, who engaged in outdoor activities for less than 2 h per day, who did not have caregivers-child interactions during screen time, and who watched educational videos for longer durations.
It is of great importance to evaluate the impact of screen exposure on children’s language development in early life, which is the foundation for other areas of development [ , ]. Consistent with previous studies, our findings suggested that prolonged screen time can have a negative impact on children’s language development [ , , – ]. For instance, an Indian study found that children who watched TV for more than two hours daily were almost six times more likely to have delayed language development [ ]. Similarly, the research from Canada indicated that each additional 30-minute increment in daily mobile media device use was associated with increased odds of parent-reported expressive speech delay [ ]. Furthermore, a Chinese study reported that screen time was negatively correlated with language DQ scores [ ]. This may be attributed to the potential detrimental effects of prolonged exposure to electronic screens on the integrity of the brain’s white matter microstructure, which is a crucial region for the development of language and literacy skills [ ].
Furthermore, our study revealed that children who spend more time watching screens tend to have parents with lower levels of education and engaged in less daily interaction with them. Previous studies have shown that parents with lower levels of formal education are considered as a risk factor for delayed language development in children [ – ]. Such parents may rely more heavily on electronic devices for parenting support [ , – ], resulting in a decrease in parent-child interaction, which is an important factor emphasized in the 3 C model as context [ ]. Indeed, this provided a further explanation for the findings of this study, which indicated that children who watched screens without caregivers-child interactions were at a higher risk of delayed language development than those who were accompanied by family members. Evidence from previous studies also supported the finding that positive parent-child interaction could buffer the risk of delayed language development in children with high-frequency media use [ , ], and also suggests that the effects of screen exposure on language development are not solely attributable to excessive duration, and that the contexts shaped by the family’s patterns of interactions are a non-negligible focus.
Children’s language development was profoundly influenced by both family and societal environment. Guangzhou, the capital of Guangdong province and one of the most populous megacities in Southern China, exhibits a diverse linguistic ecology. Our findings revealed that children whose primary household language is Cantonese faced increased risks of language development delays associated with prolonged screen viewing. This may be attributed to Cantonese’s richer tonal system compared to Mandarin and most other Chinese dialects [ , ], which can present additional learning challenges for children [ , ]. Furthermore, our results indicated that, under the same screen time, children whose caregivers exhibited intermediate personality traits and engaged in less than two hours of outdoor exercise per day had an increased risk of delayed language development. Although it is possible that this association was identified by chance in children whose caregivers exhibited an intermediate personality due to the limited statistical power of the study, outdoor settings did prove to have a considerable impact on children’s communication abilities [ ].
Interestingly, while educational videos are often believed to enhance language skills, our study found that watching such videos for less than one hour did not significantly increase the risk of language developmental delays. Contrary to expectations, children who watched educational videos for over 1 h exhibited an increased risk of delayed language development. This observed counterintuitive finding may be explained partly by passive viewing, lack of caregiver interaction, and screen viewing patterns. Previous research indicates that while the content of most educational videos is intended to be instructive, they primarily employ a one-way transmission approach. These materials lack mechanisms to encourage children to actively respond, pose questions, or engage in two-way communication, leaving children in a passive state of knowledge acquisition [ , ]. Furthermore, caregivers’ belief in the benefits of such videos may result in relaxed viewing time limits, leading to excessive passive stimuli from screen exposure for children, a similar suggestion also made in Kilag et al.’s research [ ]. This evidence indirectly suggests that a synergistic relationship may exist between screen content and the interactive context during viewing.
Of particular concern, our study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, and children who experienced at least once quarantine prior to assessment were more susceptible to delayed language development was observed. Social interactions and outdoor activities can promote children’s language development by providing abundant language input and multi-sensory stimulation [ , ]. However, the lockdown policies led to the closure of schools and public venues, significantly reducing children’s social interactions and outdoor time, which may cause loneliness and anxiety, further affecting language development [ , ]. Meanwhile, the social instability during the pandemic heightened the caregivers’ stress, potentially resulting in negative parenting attitude that diminished the quality of parent-child interaction and increased children’s screen exposure [ ]. This also partially explained why educational videos may increase the risk of language development delays, as mentioned above. Furthermore, the widespread use of masks hindered children’s language acquisition by obstructing facial movements [ ]. It was hypothesized that quarantine measures likely worsened the healthcare challenges [ , ], preventing timely medical access or early intervention for children with language development issues. Therefore, quarantine during the COVID-19 may have amplified the negative effects of prolonged screen exposure on the risk of delayed language development in children through various dimensions.
Strengths of this study included a large sample size, comprehensive information on screen usage (e.g., interaction between caregivers and the child during screen time, video content type, family nurturing environment), and including consideration of the quarantine experience during the COVID-19 pandemic. However, several limitations of our study should also be acknowledged. First, despite the expansion of our survey on screen usage characteristics, the information it collects remains limited. For example, the study did not take into account the specific types of screen interaction and duration, the proactivity of caregiver-child interactions during screen time, and the participants’ additional responses to the COVID-19 pandemic. Additionally, it is possible that the detailed information obtained from caregivers may lead to recall bias. Second, the interaction between genetic and environmental factors on the outcomes was not examined. Third, the study participants were children residing in economically developed Guangdong Province who preferred pickled products and seafood, leading to a complex diet structure. Therefore, the results cannot be extrapolated to the general population. Finally, the cross-sectional study design cannot determine the causal relationships.

## Conclusion
Our findings indicate that prolonged screen exposure in children was associated with an increased risk of delayed language development, particularly among those who lack caregivers-child interactions or who have experienced at least once quarantine prior to assessment. Given the widespread screen exposure among children and the growing evidence of the potential risks associated with excessive screen time, further research is necessary to substantiate the impact of screen exposure on children’s language development and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms involved.
