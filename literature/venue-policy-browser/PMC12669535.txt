## Abstract
Using LGBTQ+ Social Networks, Aging, and Policy Study Wave 3 data (N = 982), this study examines how caregiving for individuals with mild cognitive impairment, Alzheimer’s disease, and related dementias (MCI/ADRD) impacts everyday stressors, discrimination, and health outcomes among LGBTQ+ older adults in the Southern United States. Using chi-square tests, Fisher’s exact tests, and logistic regression models, we assessed associations of caregiver role with stress and health outcomes. We then conducted moderation analyses to test interactions between caregiver role and LGBTQ+ minority stress. LGBTQ+ caregivers of individuals with MCI/ADRD were 3 times more likely to experience proximal minority stress (P < 0.05), twice as likely to report suicidal ideation (P < 0.1), and more likely to report cognitive problems (P < 0.05) and high blood pressure (P < 0.05) than non-caregivers. Interaction effects suggest that minority stress may exacerbate mental health problems among MCI/ADRD caregivers. Findings underscore the need for targeted supports and interventions for LGBTQ+ older adult dementia caregivers.
Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, caregiving, LGBTQ+, minority stress, older adults

## Background
An estimated 7.2 million adults aged 65 and older live with Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD) in the United States (U.S.), a number projected to nearly double to 12.7 million by 2050.
 
 As ADRD prevalence rises, so does the demand for ADRD caregiving support. Currently, 83% of community-dwelling older adults receive unpaid care from family and other informal caregivers,
 
 including 11 million dementia caregivers who provide 19.2 billion hours of unpaid care annually.
 
 These estimates underscore the increasing need to support family caregivers of persons living with ADRD.
In the case of lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer (LGBTQ+) older adult communities, LGBTQ+ caregivers of individuals with ADRD in the U.S. face well-documented health and social support disparities. - LGBTQ+ ADRD caregivers have a heightened risk of elevated psychological distress, chronic health conditions, fair or poor overall health, and self-reported poor physical and mental health days compared to their heterosexual and cisgender peers. , - Much like their counterparts, LGBTQ+ older adult caregivers often engage in time-intensive and emotionally taxing caregiving responsibilities for their care partners living with ADRD, which can result in lost wages, reduced work hours, and limited time for self-care. , Additionally, they face barriers to accessing ADRD caregiver services, support, and resources (e.g., limited logistical and emotional social support) due to systemic factors concerning historical exclusion from traditional family and marriage systems. , Consequently, LGBTQ+ older adult caregivers of individuals with ADRD have reported limited access to kinship-based caregiving networks, as well as insufficient or inconsistent LGBTQ-identity social support from relationships with family, coworkers, and neighbors. , 
In addition to limited social support and networks, mechanisms of health disparities among LGBTQ+ older adult caregivers of individuals with ADRD include interpersonal stigma and discrimination related to their LGBTQ+ identities, relationships, and experiences, as well as structural erasure from ADRD caregiver groups and settings. , , LGBTQ+ older caregivers face higher rates of violent victimization, discrimination, and stress compared to their heterosexual and cisgender peers. , - Yet, LGBTQ+ adults in the U.S. lack adequate sociolegal protections due to limited anti-discrimination laws related to sexual orientation and gender identity and the increasing rise of anti-LGBTQ+ legislation and policies.
 
 In healthcare settings, clinicians may face substantial knowledge and training gaps in addressing the needs of LGBTQ+ older adults
 
 or effectively communicating with LGBTQ+ caregivers and their family care partners with ADRD,
 
 which can hinder care coordination and utilization. LGBTQ+ erasure in care environments, coupled with provider implicit biases and miscommunication, further intensifies these barriers and restricts access to LGBTQ- and gender-affirming care for LGBTQ+ older adults.
 
 Overall, such mechanisms create a dual burden: LGBTQ+ caregivers must manage the progressive demands of ADRD care while simultaneously navigating healthcare and other social institutions that are often unwelcoming or discriminatory towards them, even as ADRD caregivers.
These challenges are particularly acute in the Southern U.S., where more pronounced anti-LGBTQ+ enacted laws and limited non-discrimination protections further restrict access to affirming care, public services, and spaces for LGBTQ+ people.
 
 Especially in the U.S. South, for example, the collectivistic emphasis on family obligation, faith community belonging, and group loyalty can create a paradoxical situation in which LGBTQ+ older adults feel compelled to provide care for immediate familial members who may have previously rejected or harmed them.
 
 As such, family caregiving obligations may not function based on trust or mutual affection, but instead on social assurance mechanisms that enforce compliance. In this context, LGBTQ+ stigma functions as a persistent sanctioning force that reinforces both LGBTQ+ individuals’ marginal status within family networks and maintains their perceived obligation to fulfill caregiving duties.
 
 The threat of further exclusion or intensified familial rejection can also compel caregiving compliance among LGBTQ+ adults, even in relationships marked by the non-acceptance of their LGBTQ+ identities. This dynamic has significant public health implications for LGBTQ+ caregivers, as it can heighten caregiver burden and distress, further compromising their health.
 

Minority stress theory offers a valuable conceptual framework for understanding the unique, chronic stressors faced by LGBTQ+ older communities. This framework notes that (a) internalized feelings and experiences of anti-LGBTQ+ prejudice, stigma, and discrimination create unique “minority stressors” for LGBTQ+ individuals, and (b) these stressors contribute to negative mental and physical health outcomes over the life course. - Researchers posit that LGBTQ+ people encounter three distinct types of stressors: (1) general stress, (2) distal minority stress, and (3) proximal minority stress.
 
 General stressors are external pressures not directly tied to LGBTQ+ status (e.g., housing and food insecurity). Distal minority stressors stem from external, LGBTQ+ identity-related experiences of prejudice, discrimination, or stigma in social settings (e.g., familial rejection due to anti-LGBTQ+ stigma).
 
 And finally, proximal minority stressors are internal and anticipated stressors that involve fears of potential anti-LGBTQ+ discrimination and the internalization of negative societal beliefs or stereotypes about LGBTQ+ people.
 

In the case of LGBTQ+ older caregivers of persons living with ADRD, such minority stressors may intersect with caregiver stress,
 
 compounding their overall burden and exacerbating levels of psychological distress and stress-related physical symptoms.
 
 For example, LGBTQ+ ADRD caregivers may have increased levels of minority stress when caregiving requires frequent interaction with non-affirming environments.
 
 This dual burden creates a uniquely stressful context in which LGBTQ+ caregivers must manage the escalating demands of ADRD care and associated caregiving challenges while also confronting the persistent effects of societal stigma and exclusion faced by LGBTQ+ individuals.
Despite a growing body of literature on this population, substantial gaps remain in understanding the stress and health experiences of LGBTQ+ older adult caregivers of individuals with ADRD. These gaps persist in part due to: (a) the limited measurement of LGBTQ+ specific stressors and discrimination, which often rely on single-item or binary indicators that fail to capture the chronic, cumulative, and context-specific nature of minority stress; (b) the tendency for LGBTQ+ ADRD caregiver studies to focus on general adult populations, rather than midlife and older caregivers; (c) a lack of distinction in survey measurements between ADRD caregivers and those supporting individuals with other conditions; and (d) the predominance of qualitative or small-sample studies in this area, which limits the statistical generalizability of findings. Notably, little is known about how the unique demands of ADRD caregiving may shape, exacerbate, or alter the effects of minority stress on LGBTQ+ older adult health. Although minority stress and caregiver stress models have each been used to explain adverse health outcomes in their respective domains, few studies have considered how these frameworks might jointly inform our understanding of LGBTQ+ ADRD caregiving. Advancing research in this area is crucial for creating targeted screenings and interventions that address the complex realities faced by LGBTQ+ caregivers.
In this study, we examine how mild cognitive impairment and/or ADRD (MCI/ADRD) caregiver role impacts various types of LGBTQ+ minority stress and health outcomes among LGBTQ+ older adults in the Southern U.S. Utilizing Wave 3 data from the LGBTQ+ Social Networks, Aging, and Policy Study (QSNAPS; N = 982), we first use descriptive and bivariate analyses to examine differences in minority stress exposure and health outcomes by caregiver role. We then apply logistic regression models to assess how MCI/ADRD caregivers differ from age-related caregivers and non-caregivers in the QSNAPS Wave 3 sample across a range of LGBTQ-specific everyday stressors, discrimination experiences, and health indicators. Finally, we conduct moderation analyses to explore how caregiver role interacts with minority stress exposure to influence health outcomes among QSNAPS respondents. Specifically, we propose the following research hypotheses:

H1LGBTQ+ minority stress and health outcomes will significantly differ by caregiver role, with LGBTQ+ MCI/ADRD caregivers reporting the highest levels of minority stress and adverse health outcomes, compared to caregivers of individuals with other age-related conditions and non-caregivers.

H2aCompared to non-caregivers, LGBTQ+ MCI/ADRD caregivers will report significantly higher levels of minority stress exposure and poorer health outcomes.

H2bCompared to non-caregivers, LGBTQ+ caregivers of individuals with other age-related conditions will report significantly higher levels of minority stress exposure and poorer health outcomes.

H3Caregiver role will moderate the relationship between LGBTQ+ minority stress and health outcomes, with minority stress having a stronger negative impact on health for caregivers (particularly MCI/ADRD caregivers) than for non-caregivers.

## Methods

## Participants and Data Collection
For this study, we analyzed Wave 3 data from the LGBTQ+ Social Networks, Aging, and Policy Study (QSNAPS; N = 982), a panel study funded by the National Institute on Aging (R01AG063771). This project has also benefited from support provided by Vanderbilt University, the Vanderbilt University LGBTQ+ Policy Lab, and the University of Texas at Austin’s Center on Aging and Population Sciences (P30AG066614) and Population Research Center (P2CHD042849). Across three waves of data collection, QSNAPS has gathered longitudinal data on the social networks, major life events, chronic stressors, health behaviors, healthcare access, social support, and health outcomes among a cohort of LGBTQ+ older adults in the Southern U.S. To be eligible for study participation, QSNAPS respondents had to: (a) self-identify as lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, gender diverse, and/or queer; (b) be aged 50 or older at the time of Wave 1 enrollment; (c) reside in Alabama, Georgia, North Carolina, or Tennessee at Wave 1; and (d) provide informed consent.
Participants were recruited through targeted online and venue-based sampling, linked referrals, and outreach to LGBTQ+, older adult, and faith-based community organizations across the four states. Wave 1 data (N = 1256) were collected from April 2020 to September 2021, Wave 2 (N = 1138) from December 2021 to September 2022, and Wave 3 (N = 982) from March to December 2023.
 
 The Wave 1 sample is broadly representative of LGBTQ+ adults aged 50-76 in the Southern U.S., as compared to the U.S. Census Bureau’s Household Pulse Survey (HPS), Phase 3.2 (weeks 34-39) , Compared to weighted HPS estimates, QSNAPS participants reported higher educational attainment, were less likely to identify as bisexual, and were less likely to identify as Hispanic/Latino.
 

The study received ethical approval from the Vanderbilt University Institutional Review Board, and the secondary analysis of de-identified QSNAPS Wave 3 data was classified as exempt from human subjects research by the University of South Florida Institutional Review Board.

## Measures

## Caregiving Measures
To determine whether respondents held an MCI/ADRD caregiver role, QSNAPS Wave 3 respondents were asked: “Do you currently help take care of an adult with memory problems such as dementia, Alzheimer’s, or another cognitive impairment?” with 1 “Yes” and 0 “No” response options. To assess whether respondents held a non-MCI/ADRD age-related caregiver role, they were asked: “Are you currently assisting an adult who needs help with daily activities due to age or disability?” with 1 “Yes” and 0 “No” response options. Based on these responses, we created a trichotomous variable: (1) “MCI/ADRD caregiver” for those who answered “Yes” to the first question; (2) “Age-related caregiver” for those who answered “No” to the first but “Yes” to the second; and (3) “Non-caregiver” for those who answered “No” to both questions. We also utilized two binary variables regarding caregiver role: (a) the original MCI/ADRD caregiver measure; and (b) a caregiver role variable, where QSNAPS respondents who answered “Yes” to either caregiving question were coded as 1, and those who answered “No” to both were coded as 0.

## Everyday Stressors and Discrimination Measures
To determine experiences of discrimination in the past year, respondents were asked two questions: “In the past year, did anyone verbally harass you for any reason?”; and “In the past year, did anyone physically attack you (such as grab you, throw something at you, punch you, use a weapon) for any reason?” Each item included yes/no response options.
To assess LGBTQ-specific everyday stressors in the past year, we used a short form version of the 50-item Daily Heterosexist Experiences Questionnaire (DHEQ), a validated measurement tool that captures LGBTQ+ minority stress through four domains as described by Balsam et al. (2013): “(1) coverage across the range of types of stresses experienced by LGBT people, (2) identification of and ability to distinguish between whether a stress is experienced and how much subjective distress it caused, (3) assessment of a clearly specified time frame, and (4) usability across LGBT subpopulations.”
 

QSNAPS employed a modified version of the 50-item DHEQ to minimize participant burden and target the unique minority stressors most pertinent to LGBTQ+ older adults in the Southern U.S. Respondents were presented with the prompt: “The following is a list of experiences that LGBTQ people sometimes have. Please read each one carefully, and then respond to the following question: How much has this problem distressed or bothered you during the past 12 months?” Participants then rated a series of situations. Example items included: “having few people you can talk to about being LGBT,” “being verbally harassed by people you know because you are LGBT,” and “feeling invisible in the LGBT community because of your gender expression.” Each response was recorded on a six-point Likert scale: 0 “Did not happen/not applicable to me” to 5 “It happened, and it bothered me extremely.” For analysis, responses indicating that the stressor occurred—regardless of how distressing it was (i.e., responses 1 through 5)—were recoded as 1 “[X] stressor occurred” and 0 “[X] stressor did not occur.” In addition to evaluating each individual measure within the DHEQ, we also assessed the short form DHEQ index measure. presents the 21-item DHEQ.
 Using this 21-item DHEQ, we developed a modified Victimization/Harassment and Discrimination – DHEQ subscale
 
 to assess distal minority stressors (i.e., external stressors from social groups or institutions impacting LGBTQ+ people). , Items for this index included the following measures: “being verbally harassed by strangers because you are LGBT,” “being verbally harassed by people you know because you are LGBT,” “people laughing at you or making jokes at your expense because you are LGBT,” “being raped or sexually assaulted because you are LGBT,” “having objects thrown at you because you are LGBT,” “having your property (like a car, house, locker) damaged because you are LGBT,” and “people staring at you when you are out in public because you are LGBT.”
To assess vicarious or anticipated distal minority stressors experienced in the past year, we utilized a modified Vicarious Trauma - DHEQ subscale.
 
 Items for this index included the following measures: “hearing about LGBT people you know being treated unfairly,” “hearing about LGBT people you don’t know being treated unfairly,” “hearing about hate crimes (e.g., vandalism, physical or sexual assault) that happened to LGBT people you don’t know,” “hearing someone make jokes about LGBT people,” and “hearing politicians say negative things about LGBT people.”
We also developed a modified Vigilance - DHEQ subscale
 
 to assess LGBTQ+ proximal minority stressors characterized by internalized or subjective stress responses among LGBTQ+ people, such as identity concealment, hypervigilance, and discomfort.
 
 Respondents reported distress related to “watching what you say and do around heterosexual people,” “hiding part of your life from other people,” “avoiding talking about your current or past relationships when you are at work,” “being misunderstood by people because of your gender expression,” and “difficulty finding clothes that you are comfortable wearing due to your gender expression.”
To capture proximal minority stressors related to LGBTQ+ community belonging, we also created an LGBTQ+ community belonging-related minority stressors index. This community belonging-related stressors index includes distress associated with “feeling like you don’t fit in with other LGBT people,” “feeling invisible in the LGBT community because of your gender expression,” “feeling like you don’t fit into the LGBT community because of your gender expression,” and “having very few people you can talk to about being LGBT.”
Finally, to assess proximal minority stressors related to gender, we utilized a modified Gender Expression - DHEQ subscale.
 
 Items for this index included the following measures: “feeling invisible in the LGBT community because of your gender expression,” “feeling like you don’t fit into the LGBT community because of your gender expression,” “difficulty finding clothes that you are comfortable wearing because of your gender expression,” and “being misunderstood by people because of your gender expression.”
Responses for each of the aforementioned DHEQ subscale indices were recorded on a six-point Likert scale: 0 “Did not happen/not applicable to me,” to 5 “It happened, and it bothered me extremely.” For analysis, we dichotomized each index into two categories: 0 “'No [X] stressor exposure reported in the past year” (i.e., all responses were ‘did not happen/not applicable’) and 1 “[X] stressor exposure in the past year” (i.e., at least one item was rated between 1 and 5).

## Health Measures
The QSNAPS instrument includes a range of validated measures to assess respondents’ self-reported health outcomes. Overall health was evaluated by asking participants, “Much of this study concerns health. Would you say your health is excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor?” Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale, with 1 “Poor” to 5 “Excellent” response options. Those who selected “Excellent” or “Very good” were coded as having 1 “Excellent or very good overall health,” while all other responses were categorized as 0 “Good, fair, or poor overall health.”
Psychological distress was measured using the Kessler 6 (K6) scale, a widely used and validated tool developed for the U.S. National Health Interview Survey.
 
 A score of 5 or higher on the K6 scale indicated moderate to severe psychological distress.
To assess feelings of restlessness or fidgetiness, respondents were asked, “During the past 30 days, about how often did you feel restless or fidgety?” with response options ranging from 1 “All of the time” to 5 “None of the time.” Responses were recoded into a binary variable, with 1 “Most or all of the time” and 0 “Some, a little, or none of the time.” Depressive symptoms were measured with the item, “During the past 30 days, how often did you feel so depressed that nothing could cheer you up?” Life enjoyment was measured by asking, “During the past 30 days, about how often did you feel that you enjoyed life?” Both items used the same five-point response scale and were similarly recoded into binary variables.
To assess whether respondents managed subjective cognitive problems, a modified version of the 16-item Short Form of the Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly (IQCODE) was used.
 
 This measure captures self-reported changes in memory, thinking, and decision-making over the past two years, with items such as “learning new things in general,” “remembering things that have happened recently,” and “understanding what’s going on and reasoning things through.” Responses were recoded into a binary variable, where 1 indicated “One or more self-reported cognitive problems” and 0 indicated “No self-reported cognitive problems.”
To examine suicidal ideation in the past year, respondents were asked, “During the last year or so, have you ever seriously thought about suicide?” with yes/no response options. Presence of chronic health conditions was also assessed by asking QSNAPS respondents whether a doctor had ever diagnosed them with specific conditions, including “high blood pressure or hypertension,” “arthritis or rheumatism,” “stroke,” “HIV,” and “depression, anxiety, or another mental health condition.”

## Covariates
Covariates included a range of sociodemographic characteristics, including gender identity, sexual orientation, race/ethnicity, education level (less than a bachelor’s degree, bachelor’s degree, or graduate/professional degree), personal income, marital/partner status, and employment status.
Race and ethnicity were assessed using a two-step approach. First, respondents selected their race from the following options: “White,” “Black/African American,” “American Indian/Alaskan Native,” “Asian,” and “Other.” Second, they were asked whether they identified as being of Hispanic, Latino, or Spanish origin, with yes/no response options. This two-step classification was used to account for the distinct and well-documented experiences of structural racism faced by LGBTQ+ people from minoritized racial and ethnic communities, particularly Black LGBTQ+ older adults.
In the QSNAPS Wave 3 sample, 5.1% of respondents identified as Black, non-Hispanic/Latino; 5.6% as Hispanic/Latino (any race); 4.68% as Other Race or Multiracial; 0.4% as Asian, non-Hispanic/Latino; and 84.2% as White, non-Hispanic/Latino.
 
 Due to small sample sizes, participants who identified as Asian, Hispanic/Latino, American Indian or Alaskan Native, Multiracial, and/or Other Race were combined into a single “Other Race/Ethnicity or Multiracial” category to improve statistical power. As a result, race/ethnicity was categorized into 3 groups: “White, non-Hispanic/Latino,” “Black, non-Hispanic/Latino,” and “Other Race/Ethnicity or Multiracial.”

## Data Analyses
All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 18.5. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the sample characteristics. Chi-square tests, Fisher’s exact tests, and logistic regression models were conducted to examine associations between caregiving role and health outcomes, as well as outcomes related to LGBTQ-specific everyday stressors and experiences of discrimination. Moderation analyses were also conducted by including interaction terms relating to caregiver role and the 21-item DHEQ index in models predicting health outcomes.
All adjusted models controlled for sociodemographic factors previously shown to influence stress and health outcomes among LGBTQ+ older adults. These included age (continuous); gender identity (cisgender man [reference], cisgender woman, or transgender and gender diverse); sexual orientation (lesbian or gay [reference], bisexual, or heterosexual, something else, or don’t know); race/ethnicity (White, Non-Hispanic/Latino [reference], Black, non-Hispanic/Latino, or Other Race/Ethnicity or Multiracial); marital or partnered status (not married/partnered [reference] or married/partnered); education level (less than a bachelor’s degree [reference], bachelor’s degree, or graduate/professional degree); personal income (<$45,000 [reference], $45,000–$74,999, $75,000–$124,999, or >$125,000); and employment status (employed full-time [reference], employed part-time, unemployed, or retired).

## Results
In the QSNAPS Wave 3 sample, 8.1% (1 in 12) of respondents reported providing care for someone living with mild cognitive impairment, Alzheimer’s disease, and related dementia, while 12.6% (1 in 8) reported caring for individuals with age-related conditions. Four out of five respondents (79.3%) identified as non-caregivers. Regarding sociodemographic characteristics, transgender and gender diverse (TGD) older adults were more likely to report caring for an individual with MCI/ADRD compared to TGD age-related caregivers (10.0% vs 9.7%, P < 0.1) and TGD non-caregivers (10.0% vs 5.4%, P < 0.1). LGBTQ+ MCI/ADRD caregivers were also more likely to report annual household incomes below $45,000 than both age-related caregivers (16.3% vs 13.5%, P < 0.05) and non-caregivers (16.3% vs 7.6%, P < 0.05). And finally, full-time employment was more frequently reported by LGBTQ+ non-caregivers compared to age-related caregivers (46.3% vs 35.5%, P < 0.05) and MCI/ADRD caregivers (46.3% vs 42.0%, P < 0.05).
 

Compared to age-related caregivers and non-caregivers, LGBTQ+ older adult caregivers of individuals living with MCI/ADRD more frequently reported experiencing instances of LGBTQ+ everyday stressors and discrimination. Specifically, MCI/ADRD caregivers were significantly more likely to report: having very few people to talk to about being LGBT (6.4% vs Age-Related Caregiver 1.6% vs Non-Caregiver 1.3%, P < 0.05, Fisher’s P = 0.01); hearing someone make jokes about LGBT people (26.6% vs 13.0% vs 18.1%, P = 0.05); hearing about LGBT people they know being treated unfairly (27.9% vs 11.4% vs 14.6%, P < 0.01); hearing about LGBT people they do not know being treated unfairly (36.7% vs 19.5% vs 30.2%, P < 0.05); watching what they say and do around heterosexual people (5.1% vs 0.8% vs 1.6%, P < 0.05, Fisher’s P = 0.073); people laughing at them or making jokes at their expense because they are LGBT (3.9% vs 1.6% vs 0.8%, P < 0.05, Fisher’s P = 0.032); and hiding part of their life from other people (7.6% vs 3.3% vs 2.5%, P < 0.05, Fisher’s P = 0.042). Age-related caregivers more frequently reported being verbally harassed by strangers because of their LGBTQ+ identity compared to MCI/ADRD caregivers (4.1% vs 1.3%, P < 0.05, Fisher’s P = 0.04) and non-caregivers (4.1% vs 1.0%, P < 0.05, Fisher’s P = 0.04).
Regarding LGBTQ+ minority stress, MCI/ADRD caregivers were more frequently reported experiencing vigilance-related proximal minority stress (11.5% vs 4.1% vs 3.6%, P < 0.01) and LGBTQ+ community belonging-related minority stress (9.0% vs 2.4% vs 3.2%, P < 0.05; Fisher’s exact P = 0.044) than other groups. Compared to non-caregivers, MCI/ADRD and age-related caregivers were also more frequently reported experiencing distal minority stress related to LGBTQ+ victimization, harassment, and discrimination (6.5% vs 3.4%, P < 0.05).
Compared to age-related caregivers and non-caregivers, MCI/ADRD caregivers more frequently reported adverse health outcomes, such as experiencing suicidal ideation in the past year (16.3% vs 5.7% vs 8.1%, P < 0.05); feeling restless in the past 30 days (21.3 vs 8.1% vs 7.5%, P < 0.001); managing subjective cognitive problems (60.0% vs 50.0% vs 40.4%, P < 0.001); managing arthritis or rheumatism (42.5% vs 40.3% vs 31.8%, P < 0.05); and feeling a lack of life enjoyment during the past 30 days (43.8% vs 28.2% vs 38.7%, P < 0.05). Compared to non-caregivers, MCI/ADRD and age-related caregivers were more likely to report previously having a stroke (5.9% vs 2.8%, P < 0.05) and managing depression, anxiety, or another mental health condition (51.0% vs 43.5%, P < 0.1). Finally, MCI/ADRD caregivers were less likely to report living with HIV compared to non-MCI/ADRD caregivers (3.8% vs 9.8%, P < 0.1, Fisher’s P = 0.104, one-sided Fisher’s P < 0.05).

## Discussion
This study is among the first to examine how caregiving for individuals with MCI/ADRD is associated with everyday stressors, discrimination, and health outcomes among LGBTQ+ older adults, while incorporating specific measures to capture LGBTQ+ minority stress. In QSNAPS Wave 3, 1 out of 12 respondents (8.1%) reported providing care to someone with MCI/ADRD. Compared to non-caregivers, MCI/ADRD caregivers reported significantly higher levels of minority stress, including greater odds of hearing anti-LGBTQ+ comments, concealing aspects of their lives, and experiencing proximal stress and LGBTQ+ community belonging-related proximal stress. MCI/ADRD caregivers also reported higher odds of adverse health outcomes, such as feeling restless, past-year suicidal ideation, managing subjective cognitive problems, and managing high blood pressure. In contrast, age-related caregivers reported fewer experiences of minority stress and positive health outcomes, including excellent or very good overall health and greater life enjoyment, compared to non-caregivers. Moderation analyses indicated that LGBTQ+ minority stress was marginally associated with being diagnosed with a mental health condition among MCI/ADRD caregivers, although other interaction effects were not statistically significant. Overall, this study sheds light on the psychosocial challenges that LGBTQ+ older adults may encounter while caring for those living with neurocognitive disorders.
Our findings reveal that LGBTQ+ individuals providing care for someone with MCI/ADRD experience significantly higher levels of minority stress compared to their LGBTQ+ peers who are age-related caregivers or non-caregivers. Prior research notes how ADRD caregiving may not only demand more time, financial resources, and emotional labor,
 
 but may also intensify or be intensified by existing LGBTQ+ minority stressors among LGBTQ+ caregivers.
 
 LGBTQ+ MCI/ADRD caregivers in this study reported elevated experiences of proximal minority stress, including hypervigilance, concealment, and feelings of alienation from LGBTQ+ communities. For LGBTQ+ older adult caregivers, longstanding exposure to stigma and discrimination—both within families and across broader social contexts, including LGBTQ+ community spaces—shapes their MCI/ADRD caregiving experiences and may contribute to behavioral health disparities compared to their cisgender, heterosexual peers.
 

One possible explanation is that LGBTQ+ older adult caregivers of individuals living with ADRD have documented no to limited family support, including both the care recipient and those involved in care coordination. , , Often rooted in LGBTQ+ stigma and sociolegal barriers, strained familial relationships can complicate ADRD caregiving roles and exacerbate both emotional, logistical, and financial burdens for LGBTQ+ caregivers.
 
 Even when LGBTQ+ individuals take on primary caregiving roles, they may face exclusion from medical decision-making, receive limited LGBTQ-affirming social support from immediate family members, experience political or ideological differences with their care partners living with ADRD, and encounter barriers to accessing LGBTQ+ affirming care for themselves.
 
 Given that unavailable and low-quality social support is a well-established predictor of stress and negative health outcomes, , , these findings underscore the compounded risks faced by LGBTQ+ caregivers navigating ADRD caregiving within unsupportive social environments.
In addition to elevated exposure to LGBTQ+ minority stress, LGBTQ+ MCI/ADRD caregivers in the QSNAPS Wave 3 sample reported significantly worse health outcomes, including past-year suicidal ideation, feeling restlessness or fidgetiness, subjective cognitive problems, and high blood pressure—behavioral and physical health issues that indicate cumulative effects of caregiving under chronic social stress. LGBTQ+ age-related caregivers reported more favorable health indicators, such as greater life enjoyment and better self-rated health. This may be partly explained by the nature of their caregiving relationships—LGBTQ+ older adult caregivers are often caring for a partner, close friend, or chosen family member, relationships that may offer greater affirmation and social support. In such contexts, LGBTQ+ affirmation may be more central to ADRD care partnerships. Who LGBTQ+ ADRD caregivers are caring for plays a significant role in shaping both the caregiving experience and related health outcomes. Although prior studies have examined minority stress and caregiver burden separately, , this study highlights how LGBTQ+ MCI/ADRD caregivers uniquely experience the compounding effects of both. These intersecting stressors may not only worsen behavioral health outcomes but also hinder access to health-promoting resources, underscoring the urgency of enhancing targeted programming for LGBTQ+ older adult caregivers.
Notably, our moderation analyses did not find that LGBTQ+ minority stress significantly intensified the relationship between MCI/ADRD and age-related caregiver role and the aforementioned health outcomes. At first glance, this appears to contradict our primary findings. However, this lack of statistical interaction is consistent with emerging critiques of additive or interaction-based stress models when applied to minoritized populations. In the case of LGBTQ+ MCI/ADRD caregivers, LGBTQ+ minority stress may not merely compound caregiving stress; it may already be inseparable from the caregiving context itself. That is, identity-based stigma and social exclusion are embedded within the everyday caregiving experience rather than acting as external or secondary pressures. Overall, these overlapping sources of stress may not interact in statistically detectable ways because they form the caregiver’s baseline environment.
Prior international research offers valuable insights into the everyday stressors, discrimination, and health experiences of LGBTQ+ older adult caregivers of individuals with ADRD. For example, Leszko (2025) reported that LGBTQ+ ADRD caregivers in Poland experienced familial alienation, heightened minority stress, and concerns about the future, largely driven by structural anti-LGBTQ+ stigma such as the lack of legal recognition for same-sex marriages.
 
 Similarly, Stinchcombe et al. (2025) reported that LGBTQ+ individuals with cognitive impairment and their family caregivers in Canada experienced marginalization within dementia care settings and consequently advocate for LGBTQ-affirming clinical environments.
 
 Finally, Waling et al. (2022) examined the family caregiving experiences of lesbian and gay older adults in Australia, highlighting caregiving as an expression of love and commitment, while noting challenges due to limited access to formal supports.
 
 Collectively, these studies illustrate that LGBTQ+ older adult caregivers across the globe face unique minority stressors, but also draw resilience from familial and community networks and personal coping strategies to protect their health. Our study meaningfully contributes to this international literature by examining how ADRD caregiving may shape the impact of minority stress on LGBTQ+ older adult health.
Findings from the present study are especially relevant in the Southern U.S., where LGBTQ+ ADRD caregivers often provide care within sociopolitical environments that emphasize family duty and faith community involvement, yet lack comprehensive legal protections and operate in environments that sustain anti-LGBTQ+ attitudes. , , These structural conditions may contribute to strained caregiving relationships characterized by unresolved trauma and limited reciprocity. On a structural level, the absence of nondiscrimination protections and a shortage of LGBTQ+-affirming healthcare providers can restrict access to formal support for LGBTQ+ ADRD caregivers.
 
 On an institutional level, ADRD caregiver support groups and services that do exist may not feel affirming or inclusive of LGBTQ+ individuals’ experiences, leading many caregivers to avoid or underutilize these resources. LGBTQ+ caregivers may also feel disconnected from LGBTQ+ community spaces that fail to meaningfully include older adults, caregivers, or people living with ADRD.
 
 Such forms of structural erasure and exclusion may compound caregiver strain and help explain the elevated minority stress and adverse health outcomes observed among LGBTQ+ MCI/ADRD caregivers in our sample.

## Implications
This study has notable research, clinical, and policy implications. From a research perspective, elevated levels of minority stress and adverse health outcomes observed among LGBTQ+ MCI/ADRD caregivers underscore the critical need for culturally tailored ADRD caregiver supports and interventions that address the unique intersection of LGBTQ+ minority stress and caregiver stress in this understudied population. Future research should continue to develop, incorporate, and test these and other frameworks (e.g., intersectionality theory, weathering hypothesis) that can illuminate how LGBTQ-specific stigma and discrimination compound caregiver stress and contribute to elevated health risks among LGBTQ+ caregivers.
Additionally, researchers could explore differing experiences of discrimination among LGBTQ+ older adult caregivers from minoritized racial and ethnic groups,
 
 rural communities,
 
 bisexual, transgender, and/or intersex individuals,
 
 and those residing in the Global South.
 
 Investigating the unique needs and experiences of these understudied subpopulations can substantially improve culturally tailored caregiver programming for LGBTQ+ communities. To achieve this effort, research recruitment and engagement strategies must intentionally prioritize meaningful inclusion of underrepresented LGBTQ+ caregiver communities.
Importantly, the generalizability of these findings may be influenced by this study’s setting in the Southern U.S., a region typically characterized by conservative political, social, and religious contexts, which may disproportionately heighten the levels of minority stress experienced by LGBTQ+ older adults. State-level restrictions on LGBTQ+ rights, inadequate gender-affirming care resources, and anti-LGBTQ+ stigma rooted in faith community rejection can collectively exacerbate minority stress and may increase caregiver burden for LGBTQ+ older adult caregivers.
 
 Future research in both U.S. and international settings is needed to assess whether similar findings emerge among LGBTQ+ older adult caregivers of individuals with ADRD, particularly across sociopolitical contexts that differ in levels of LGBTQ+ social support.
 

From a clinical perspective, providers conducting health screening and consultations should inquire about caregiver status and further assess caregiving intensity, relationship type and quality, and duration. These factors likely interact with minority stress to shape LGBTQ+ caregivers’ experiences with managing stress and health. General education and training on LGBTQ+ affirmation are also needed across healthcare settings—for providers of both caregivers and their care partners with ADRD. Additionally, ADRD-friendly training should incorporate an understanding of how diverse family caregiving experiences affect LGBTQ+ people’s care needs. Finally, routine behavioral health screenings and supports tailored to LGBTQ+ caregivers should be integrated into ADRD care protocols to help mitigate serious health risks such as suicidal ideation, psychological distress, and subjective cognitive decline.
From a policy perspective, expanding access to affordable caregiver services, supports, and resources is essential to meet the needs of LGBTQ+ ADRD caregivers. Strategic partnerships between general ADRD caregiver organizations and LGBTQ+ community organizations can enhance outreach, build caregiver engagement, and increase the utilization of available supports. Additionally, targeted federal and state funding is needed to expand access to respite care programming and to provide LGBTQ-affirming training for memory care centers, ensuring that both caregivers and care recipients receive inclusive, culturally responsive services. Recognizing sociopolitical contexts is essential for policy planning since targeted interventions for LGBTQ+ ADRD caregivers may be required in geographical regions where sociolegal protections for LGBTQ+ people are limited or absent.

## Limitations
This study has several limitations. First, caregiver measures were first collected in QSNAPS Wave 3, limiting our analyses to a cross-sectional design that prevents an assessment of causality or directionality across the observed associations. Future QSNAPS waves will offer more robust longitudinal results on LGBTQ+ MCI/ADRD caregiving experiences. Second, although the sample broadly reflects LGBTQ+ older populations in the Southern U.S., QSNAPS data underrepresents racial and ethnic minorities, bisexual individuals, and those with lower educational attainment—groups that often face heightened caregiving burdens and adverse health outcomes. Oversampling these specific populations in future studies is essential to capture the full scope of LGBTQ+ ADRD caregiver disparities. Third, the MCI/ADRD caregiver measure includes all respondents providing care for adults with neurocognitive disorders without distinguishing between ADRD and milder cognitive impairments (e.g., MCI), which potentially obscures noticeable differences in caregiving experiences. The dataset also did not capture caregiving intensity, relationship quality, and duration. Such factors are likely to influence stress and health outcomes. Fourth, the study used a modified version of the 50-item DHEQ and its subscales to examine minority stress among respondents. However, these modifications were not psychometrically validated, leaving the reliability and validity of the measures in capturing minority stress among LGBTQ+ older adult caregivers uncertain. Future research should incorporate more detailed measures that differentiate caregiver and care recipient experiences and oversample ADRD caregivers in studies targeting LGBTQ+ older adults.

## Conclusion
LGBTQ+ older adult caregivers of persons living with MCI/ADRD face compounded stressors stemming from both LGBTQ+ minority stress and the demands of MCI/ADRD caregiving. These intersecting challenges contribute to heightened risks for adverse health outcomes among this medically underserved population. Rather than treating LGBTQ+ identity and caregiver role as separate sources of chronic strain, our findings suggest these experiences are deeply interconnected—shaped by LGBTQ+ stigma, sociolegal barriers to family and marriage formation, and societal expectations surrounding informal caregiving. Future research should continue to incorporate caregiver measures and targeted recruitment strategies in LGBTQ+ ADRD studies, with a particular emphasis on developing and evaluating tailored supports and interventions for caregivers in LGBTQ+ communities.
