## ABSTRACT

## Background
This study aimed to clarify the structure of the individual matching support process within Team Orange, a community‐based dementia support initiative in Japan.

## Methods
Using reflexive thematic analysis, we qualitatively examined semi‐structured interviews with seven coordinators who facilitate matching between people living with dementia and community supporters. We also analysed 14 implementation reports documenting actual support cases from community comprehensive support centres in Sapporo, Hokkaido.

## Results
Analysis identified seven themes constituting a cyclical support process: ‘diverse triggers for initiating matching’, ‘assessing needs and evaluating support potential’, ‘safe and sustainable support coordination’, ‘building trust relationships to facilitate matching’, ‘reaching consensus among people living with dementia, families and supporters’, ‘implementing individual matching support’ and ‘reviewing and readjusting toward sustainable support’.

## Conclusions
These findings suggest that individual matching is not simply a combination of volunteer activities, but rather a support process in which the coordinator's clinical reasoning and relational coordination cyclically support the agency of people living with dementia, family members and supporters. This practice has the potential to serve as an implementation model that flexibly connects informal community resources with individuals whose needs fall outside the scope of formal care services.

Keywords: care coordination, individual matching, informal support, qualitative research, Team Orange

## Introduction
The global burden of dementia is increasing rapidly. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that approximately 55 million people worldwide live with dementia, projected to reach 139 million by 2050 [ ]. The WHO Global Action Plan on the Public Health Response to Dementia 2017–2025 emphasises the importance of community‐based support and social participation for people living with dementia (PLWD) [ ]. Japan is experiencing this trend acutely. According to national estimates, approximately 4.43 million older adults aged 65 years or over in Japan will be living with dementia in 2022 (prevalence: 12.3%), representing approximately one in eight older adults [ ]. If current trends continue, this number is projected to reach 5.84 million by 2040. Enabling PLWD to live with dignity in familiar communities is the central goal of Japan's dementia policy. The National Framework for Promotion of Dementia Policies emphasises community support based on the pillars of ‘inclusion’ and ‘prevention’ [ ]. New policies published in January 2026 further emphasise the importance of community participation and decision‐making support for PLWD and their families, strengthening the integrated community care perspective [ ].
Against this policy background, ‘Team Orange’ initiatives have been promoted nationwide across Japan. Team Orange is an informal support framework institutionally positioned within the national framework for promotion of dementia policies, in which trained community members, such as dementia supporters, collaborate with PLWD and their families to provide daily living support [ ]. These practices are diversely implemented according to local needs and available community resources [ ]. Team Orange activities include both group‐based activities at community hubs (referred to as ‘hub activities’) and individualised coordination approaches tailored to specific needs [ ]. Individual matching support (IMS), which connects what PLWD ‘want to do’ with what supporters ‘can do’—plays an important role in realising person‐centred support aligned with individual preferences and needs, particularly when participation in hub activities is difficult [ , ].
However, most reports on Team Orange have been limited to introducing activities or presenting model examples, with few studies systematically examining how relationships are built or how support is coordinated in practice. In particular, the process of coordinating support according to individual needs, regardless of the availability of hub activities, has not been explored qualitatively.
This study aims to clarify the structure of the IMS process within Team Orange through qualitative analysis using reflexive thematic analysis. Elucidating the structure of IMS will provide foundational knowledge for examining the generalisability and applicability of this practice as a model for community‐based dementia support.

2.

## Methods
2.1.

## Study Design
This exploratory qualitative study used reflexive thematic analysis [ ] to examine the structure of the IMS process within Team Orange from the coordinator's perspective. Data were collected from two sources: (1) semi‐structured interviews with seven coordinators who facilitated the IMS and (2) implementation reports (n = 14) documenting actual support cases. Reflexive thematic analysis was selected because it enables inductive exploration of processes while acknowledging the researcher's interpretive role in constructing themes from the data.

2.2.

## Participants
2.2.1.

## Coordinator Participants
Seven coordinators from seven community comprehensive support centres (CCSCs) implementing Team Orange IMS in Sapporo, Japan participated in this study. All seven coordinators met the inclusion criteria agreed to participate (Table  ). Inclusion criteria were experience coordinating IMS within Team Orange, at least six months of experience as a coordinator, and willingness to participate in an audio‐recorded interview. The mean age of the participants was 42.9 years (SD = 9.1). Participants included four public health nurses and three social workers, all affiliated with CCSCs in Sapporo, Japan's fifth largest city, with a population of approximately 1.96 million.
 
2.2.2.

## Support Cases (Implementation Reports)
In addition to the coordinator interviews, we analysed 14 implementation reports documenting IMS cases. These reports were created by the CCSCs as part of routine programme documentation.
All cases were diagnosed with dementia. Age distributions were as follows: 50s (n = 2), 70s (n = 3), 80s (n = 7) and 90s (n = 2). The sample comprised four males and 10 females. In terms of long‐term care insurance certification status, three had no certification, one was eligible for preventive care, one was classified as support level 1, two as support level 2, six as care level 1 and one as care level 2.

2.3.

## Data Collection
2.3.1.

## Semi‐Structured Interviews
Individual, semi‐structured interviews were conducted with each coordinator in a private setting to ensure confidentiality. Interviews were scheduled to last approximately 30–40 min and were audio‐recorded with the participants' informed consent. An interview guide was developed based on the study aims, covering four main areas: (1) policies and approaches to IMS within Team Orange, (2) the coordinator's role in the matching process, (3) the process leading to individual matching (how matches were initiated and developed) and (4) perceived changes in participants (PLWD, families and supporters) through matching support. During the interviews, the coordinators referred to specific cases documented in implementation reports, allowing for a detailed exploration of the support processes and decision‐making contexts.

2.3.2.

## Implementation Reports
We obtained access to implementation reports created by the CCSCs with the approval of the centre's directors and coordinators. These reports included the number of individual matching cases, the frequency of support activities and brief descriptions of the support content. The reports served primarily as contextual supplements to the interview data, providing case background information (demographics, support types and activity frequency) that helped situate coordinators' narratives within the broader programme implementation. However, detailed information about the support processes, decision‐making rationale and relational dynamics was obtained exclusively through coordinator interviews, with interviewees referring to specific cases documented in the reports to illustrate their experiences and perspectives.

2.4.

## Data Analysis
Interview transcripts and implementation report texts were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke's six‐phase framework [ ]. We began by repeatedly reading the data to gain deep familiarity with the content (Phase 1: familiarisation). The initial codes were then generated inductively to identify meaningful units within the data (Phase 2: coding). Related codes were subsequently clustered to explore candidate themes and examine commonalities and differences across codes (Phase 3: theme development). Candidate themes were reviewed to ensure that they reflected the dataset as a whole, with themes refined or reorganised as needed (Phase 4: review). The meaning and scope of each theme were then clarified and the relationships between themes were articulated (Phase 5: refinement). Finally, representative examples of data were selected to illustrate each theme, and the relationships between themes were clearly described (Phase 6: reporting). The final thematic structure was visualised as a thematic map showing the cyclical support process. The analysis was led by the first author, an occupational therapist with experience in community‐based dementia support and qualitative research. Authors 2–5 are also occupational therapists with research and clinical experience in dementia care. The research team engaged in ongoing discussions throughout the analysis process, bringing multidisciplinary perspectives to enhance the interpretive validity. Preliminary findings were presented at the Sapporo Coordinator Information Exchange Meeting on 16 February 2026 and feedback from practitioners was incorporated to refine the theme categories and descriptions, strengthening the credibility of the analysis.

3.

## Results
The IMS comprises seven themes (Table  ). As shown in Figure  , the process is dynamic and iterative, with arrows from [Reviewing and Readjusting Toward Sustainable Support] returning to [Assessing Needs and Evaluating Support Potential] and [Safe and Sustainable Support Coordination], indicating that the adjustments are repeated in response to changing circumstances. Themes are indicated in square brackets [ ] and codes in angular brackets < >. Support processes for individual cases were primarily obtained through coordinator interviews.
 3.1.

## Theme 1 [Diverse Triggers for Initiating Matching]
Triggers for initiating matching were multi‐layered, encompassing not only responses to explicit needs but also professionals' detection of risks and recognition of changes in the person's statements or expressions.
The first code was <Expressed needs of PLWD and their families>, where specific concerns or wishes articulated by PLWD or families were clarified through hub activities (community‐based group programmes) or consultation support, initiating coordination. Examples included a family's request to ‘secure daytime activities’, which led to IMS prior to formal service utilisation (Case 11), and a PLWD's desire to resume their hobby of photography (Case 4).
The second code was <Responding to challenges and potential risks>, where professionals detected issues among those not connected to hub activities, those at high risk of isolation, or those with needs beyond existing formal services, and intervened through outreach. Examples included a case initiated by consultation with a community organisation where continuing volunteer activities independently became difficult (Case 5), and a case where a care manager identified isolation among senior housing residents as problematic (Case 2).
The third code was <Noticing subtle cues in everyday interactions>, where changes in the PLWD's statements or facial expressions noticed by others during routine conversations or hub activities served as triggers. Examples include a PLWD's comment ‘I'd like to go there’ during information sharing at a dementia café (Case 6), and attention to ‘memorable places’ mentioned during hub activities leading to outing support (Case 7).

3.2.

## Theme 2 [Assessing Needs and Evaluating Support Potential]
Coordinators assessed the explicit wishes of PLWD and families and their latent needs while simultaneously evaluating the availability and capabilities of community supporters.
The first code was <Understanding the intentions and life situations of PLWD and their families>, in which the coordinators examined the feasibility of individual matching based on information from routine interactions and related organisations. Examples included a coordinator considering matching feasibility to prevent isolation after a person's participation in hub activities ceased (Case 1), and a case where motivation to ‘be useful to others’ despite physical limitations was identified through care manager information (Case 8).
The second code was <Identifying latent needs>, where life history interviews and observation during hub activities uncovered needs that were not spontaneously expressed. Examples included identifying interest in outings from the content the person repeatedly talked about (Case 7), discovering activity potential from past volunteer experience and expressing personal preferences, such as enjoyment of singing (Case 10).
The third code was <Understanding supporters' skills and preferences>, where coordinators grasped each supporter's strengths and available support scope by systematically organising information from training programmes and ongoing interactions as well as through group work during step‐up training sessions that enhanced supporters' skills in meeting individual needs. Examples included considering coordination with a community welfare officer who indicated ‘neighbourhood monitoring is feasible’ (Case 9), and examining matching where the supporter's hobby aligned with the PLWD's interests (Case 5).

3.3.

## Theme 3 [Safe and Sustainable Support Coordination]
To make IMS sustainable, a detailed specification of the activity framework, beyond simple pairing, is essential. Coordinators adjusted activity locations and times based on the PLWD's needs and supporter characteristics, establishing manageable arrangements for both parties.
The first code was <Determining the match between PLWD and supporter>, where matching was determined considering living environment, transportation methods for outings and activity times and content. Examples included determining and coordinating the means for a PLWD with difficulty managing schedules to arrive at the venue and timing of reminders with the supporter (Case 12), and matching a PLWD and a supporter who reside near each other for transportation convenience (Case 3).
The next code was <Planning support that ensures safety>, where risk management and accountability were clarified based on municipal activity guidelines, establishing arrangements allowing stakeholders to engage with confidence. Examples included setting conditions such as limiting the outing range to nearby parks when fall risk was high (Case 4), and coordinators and multiple supporters constructing backup systems when specialised intervention was anticipated due to dementia progression (Case 5).
The third code was <Support planning based on strengths and burden>, where coordinators identified strengths and activity potential of both PLWD and supporters, planning manageable support. Examples included setting visit frequency as ‘weekday mornings only, once monthly’ according to the supporter's life situation (Case 2), and sharing the responsibility for safety monitoring among multiple supporters to avoid dependence on specific individuals (Case 10).

3.4.

## Theme 4 [Building Trust Relationships to Facilitate Matching]
Building a sense of security and trust between the PLWD and their supporters was crucial to smoothly establish and sustain IMS. Trust, relationship building and coordinating support with attention to safety and continuity sometimes occur simultaneously, following a reciprocal process.
The first code was <Respecting the wishes of PLWD and their families>, in which supporters grasped the PLWD's life history and latent wishes, reflecting these in concrete activities. Examples included coordinators sharing wishes elicited from the PLWD at hubs activities with supporters, leading to discussions between the PLWD and supporter about specific activities based on mutual experiences (Case 4), and sharing necessary information with supporters based on the PLWD's and family's intentions, leading to family reassurance that ‘we don't have to bear this alone’ (Case 11).
The second code was <Respecting supporter autonomy>, where coordinators' intentional facilitation led to concrete support planning. Examples included a supporter naturally offering accompaniment when the PLWD's wishes were shared during hub activities (Case 6), and a supporter autonomously adopting an approach that did not interfere with peer‐to‐peer interaction, respecting the mutual support relationship between PLWD (Case 14).
Associated with these two codes, the third code was <Forming familiar relationships>, where CCSCs articulated a policy of mutual acquaintance and familiarity. Examples included existing relationships from regular hub activity contacts, which formed the foundation for the PLWD's sense of security (Case 13), and trust among the PLWD, family and supporters forming quickly because they were neighbours and already acquainted (Case 3).

3.5.

## Theme 5 [Reaching Consensus Among PLWD, Families and Supporters]
Before initiating support, a consensus‐building process was emphasised to ensure that PLWD, families and supporters engaged in activities with conviction and security.
The first code was <Collaborative planning among team members>, where decisions about matching feasibility and approach involved the coordinator deliberating with staff at the CCSC. Examples included multiple staff collaboratively developing support proposals and presenting them to PLWD for approval even when needs assessment was challenging (Case 7), and discussing how the PLWD's role should be maintained with the care manager when physical function declines (Case 8).
Following coordination of support content, the second code was <Confirming arrangements through preliminary meetings>, where coordinators and supporters jointly visited the PLWD's home or met at hubs when matching was established. Examples included specifically confirming and determining activity content and schedule during preliminary meetings (Case 4), and confirming the PLWD's intention to ‘participate’ and supporter's willingness to ‘accompany’, sharing pickup times in advance (Case 13).
Subsequently, the third code was <Consent for support and information sharing>, where sharing activity‐necessary information was premised on prior consent from PLWD and families, with coordinators thoroughly confirming consent for information sharing and explaining confidentiality obligations. Examples included sharing necessary information within the team to reduce anxiety for PLWD and supporters (Case 11) and determining information handling based on the person's intentions, even when dementia was disclosed (Case 6).

3.6.

## Theme 6 [Implementing IMS]
During the IMS implementation, rather than rigidly applying pre‐established plans, flexible adjustments were made according to the conditions of the PLWD and on‐site circumstances.
The first code was <Ensuring safety by coordinators>, where coordinators prepared for immediate risks that might arise during support, responding according to the day's situation. Examples included helping during outings when fall risk was high (Case 4) and coordinators adjusting responses during support when unexpected behaviours emerge due to cognitive impairment (Case 5).
The second code was <Reports, communication and consultation from supporters>, where supporters, from their position of routine involvement, observed and shared changes in clothing, statements, or behaviours of PLWD with coordinators. Examples include mismatches between clothing and season or increased item loss leading to status change recognition (Case 13), and increasingly apparent memory impairment being shared among supporters, leading to discussions about responses and safety monitoring (Case 10).
The third code was <Flexible judgment led by supporters>, where prompt adjustment of verbal approach methods occurred in situations showing cognitive decline effects, considering the PLWD's self‐esteem. Examples included adjusting confirmation methods and the timing of reminders according to circumstances (Case 12) and close friends inviting each other to hub activities, demonstrating flexible on‐site responses (Case 14).

3.7.

## Theme 7 [Reviewing and Readjusting Toward Sustainable Support]
Following IMS implementation, smooth activity continuation was confirmed, issues arising during the support process were identified, and support content was reviewed as needed.
The first code was <Monitoring changes and readjusting to maintain support continuity>, where continuous monitoring and flexible review occurred because the situations of PLWD, families and supporters changed over time after activity initiation. Examples included considering alternatives when initial activities became difficult due to dementia progression (Case 8), readjusting outing support in response to changes such as ‘walking has become harder’ (Case 9) and readjusting visit intervals and role distribution according to supporter busyness or health changes (Case 1).
The second code was <Considering transition to formal services>. When support continuation became difficult, decisions to connect to formal services at appropriate timing were discussed among Team Orange coordinators and related professionals. Examples included considering connections to formal services when living conditions deteriorated for a PLWD living alone (Case 10), parallel planning for care manager selection and service utilisation as the family caregiving burden increased, and reconstructing support systems coordinated with community resources (Case 11).

4.

## Discussion
This study identifies seven themes that constitute the IMS process. These themes form a cyclical and dynamically interconnected process. This process can be understood from the perspective of complex adaptive systems [ ] in which stakeholders mutually influence each other while responding flexibly and creatively to unpredictable situations in a dynamic process.
Themes 1 [Diverse Triggers for Initiating Matching] and 2 [Assessing Needs and Evaluating Support Potential] highlighted that individual matching was initiated through diverse triggers, including subtle cues and risk detection, and involved the identification of both explicit and latent needs. Formal services often prioritise physical care and activities of daily living (ADL) support, frequently neglecting psychosocial needs, such as company and daytime activities [ ]. Addressing unmet needs is imperative for promoting quality of life and reducing agitated behaviour [ , ]. The IMS presented in this study functions as a means of identifying and addressing these needs. Additionally, interventions that leverage familiar social networks may be more readily accepted by both PLWD and caregivers seeking a safe and understanding environment [ ]. This suggests that an IMS may function as a mechanism to connect community support for individuals prior to formal service utilisation or those falling between existing service systems. Within this framework, Team Orange organises informal support in a structured manner, bridging formal services and community resources [ ].
Theme 3 [Safe and Sustainable Support Coordination] highlighted the importance of developing a support plan that considers supporter capacity and intentions while attending to safety and continuity. Engagement of PLWD in meaningful activities has been described as ‘a conditional and fragile process’ that emerges only when supporters create appropriate ‘spaces’ for engagement [ ]. Without addressing contextual barriers through proper assessments, providing stable and sustainable opportunities for engagement remains a challenge. Volunteer activities in the community often face limitations due to a lack of shared knowledge, a common understanding of roles, mutual respect and formal structure [ ]. The Team Orange framework addresses these challenges by providing an institutional structure in which coordinators manage safety and risk using established guidelines, thereby creating a stable environment that leverages the unique strengths of supporters. Such carefully coordinated and tailored pairing and planning may have formed the conditions that enabled PLWD to participate stably in meaningful activities.
Theme 4 [Building Trust Relationships to Facilitate Matching] demonstrates that providing opportunities for mutual acquaintances before initiating support serves as an important trigger for the formation of trusting relationships. The sense of security derived from ‘unspoken acceptance and understanding’ within the community is known to alleviate psychosocial stress in both PLWD and their families, effectively reducing the psychological burden on both individuals and their caregivers [ , ]. The formation of ‘familiar relationships’ observed in this study extends beyond instrumental support and is associated with the development of emotional support. Such egalitarian relationships and diverse interactions with the local community constitute key elements of dementia‐friendly communities (DFC) [ ], and the formation of support relationships through routine relationship building observed in this study may be positioned as a practice embodying DFC principle at the implementation level. Moreover, as routine interaction‐sharing activities have been shown to alleviate dementia symptoms [ ], support developed within such familiar relationships may build relationship foundations based on reassurance, which is difficult to achieve through formal services.
Theme 5 [Reaching Consensus Among PLWD, Families and Supporters] revealed a consensus‐building process in which support content is shared among PLWD, families and supporters before the initiation of support. While informal support does not always ensure the systematic identification of needs and proactive support planning [ ], advanced goal setting has been shown to be an essential prerequisite for individualising care and improving goal achievement compared with usual care [ ]. Furthermore, relational coordination theory suggests that knowledge sharing and the establishment of common goals motivate stakeholders to act for the collective good beyond their individual roles [ ]. The consensus formation observed in this study may have functioned not only as a formal procedure but also as a foundation for nurturing safe and secure relationships, clarifying support directions and ensuring support sustainability.
Finally, Theme 6 [Implementing IMS] demonstrated that safety assurance by coordinators and flexible judgment by supporters were important elements in maintaining support quality. Theme 7 [Reviewing and Readjusting Toward Sustainable Support] highlighted the importance of ongoing readjustment premised on dementia progression and life situation changes, as well as support design anticipating transition to formal services. To ensure the sustainability of informal support, it is essential to share responsibilities and establish flexible care routines, while attending to the physical and mental health of families and their support networks [ ]. This need for ongoing adjustment is particularly salient in dementia care, where support needs change with disease progression and increased caregiving burden, making various care transitions, particularly the move from informal home care to formal services, critical and vulnerable periods for PLWD and their informal caregivers [ ]. Individual matching is not a one‐time arrangement but requires a design that anticipates reconnection to formal services, with supporters detecting changes in everyday interactions and coordinators making decisions to connect individuals to formal support as needed to sustain continuity.
This study had several limitations. First, it was conducted exclusively in Sapporo, a specific urban setting representing an early stage of hypothesis generation regarding the IMS process. Informal support may depend on local social resources and community attitudes toward mutual assistance. Further verification is required to determine whether similar processes function across different social contexts, geographic settings (rural versus urban) and coordinators of varying professional backgrounds. Second, the data were derived solely from coordinator interviews and case reports without directly capturing the subjective experiences of PLWD, families and supporters. Therefore, the interpretations may be biased toward the support provider's perspective. Nevertheless, this study contributes foundational knowledge for theoretically understanding the implementation process of community‐based dementia support by elucidating decision‐making dynamics and relational coordination from the perspective of the support provider. Future research should incorporate multi‐perspective validation, including the viewpoints of PLWD, families and supporters, along with a longitudinal assessment of IMS.

5.

## Conclusion
This study revealed that IMS within Team Orange in Sapporo is structured as a cyclical process encompassing diverse triggers for initiating matching, assessing the needs of PLWD and families, coordinating support with attention to safety and continuity, building trust relationships to facilitate matching, reaching consensus among PLWD, families and supporters, implementing IMS and reviewing and readjusting toward sustainable support. The novelty of this study lies in theorising Team Orange practice as a support process in which the agency of PLWD, families and supporters is centred, while the coordinators' clinical reasoning, relational coordination and risk management cyclically underpin this agency. Our findings suggest that the IMS can be positioned as a community‐based integrated care practice model that safely organises informal support within an institutional framework, continuously connecting local human resources to address individual challenges not fully met by formal services, thereby bridging formal and informal support systems.
