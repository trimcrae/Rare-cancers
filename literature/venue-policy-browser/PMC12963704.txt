## Abstract
Prophylactic vaccination is a powerful tool that changes exposure to infections and associated morbidity of preventable diseases. We discuss the impact of pneumococci and human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination on the population biology of the two micro‐organisms and related public health effects. Data on HPV type‐replacement in communities where vaccine‐covered HPVs are almost eliminated, and interactions of the remaining HPV types on the risk of cervical cancer are reviewed. Results of comprehensive models for European country‐specific conduction of cervical screening among HPV‐vaccinated and unvaccinated women, assuming different HPV‐vaccination coverage and strategies, are discussed in our policy‐oriented review. An acceptable balance of benefits and harms of cervical cancer screening in HPV vaccinated populations requires an understanding of cancer risks in differently vaccinated birth cohorts. Finally, the challenges are complex but can be met if strategies are applied that (i) as fast as possible achieve herd effect and (ii) use a risk‐based design of HPV screening.
Keywords: cervical cancer, human papillomavirus, screening, type‐replacement, vaccination

## INTRODUCTION
Prophylactic human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination is a powerful tool that changes exposure to HPV infections and the risk of HPV‐associated cancer morbidity. It also impacts the fundamental basis of how to perform optimal cervical screening.
We have previously reviewed the scientific approaches that could be used to achieve the WHO call for action to globally eliminate cervical cancer, which is threatened by low compliance. , For example, gender‐neutral HPV vaccination is particularly effective in achieving fast herd immunity, even with moderate vaccination coverage. , , , Herd effect, that is, protection of unvaccinated (including marginalized) women and men in contact with vaccinated individuals takes place even with a lower than the desired HPV vaccination or screening coverage. , Finally, vaccination can also be used to increase the efficiency of screening. , 

In this policy‐oriented review, we focus on the impact of vaccination programs on microbial population biology and the associated implications for optimization of vaccination and screening strategies. Microbial type‐replacement after elimination of vaccine‐covered types of a given micro‐organism by vaccination was first established as pneumococci serotype replacement three decades ago. , The findings are elaborated and compared to observations on type‐replacement following HPV vaccination. Disease replacement with less invasive pneumococcal diseases caused by the replacing serotypes is another lesson learned from the population‐level impact of pneumococci vaccination , and serves as a model for HPV and associated diseases.
School‐based prophylactic HPV vaccination among young adults has rapidly changed the population dynamics of HPV types that cause genital infections and cancer, and after 12 years of follow‐up, there is replacement of vaccine‐covered, most oncogenic HPV types 16/18/31/45 with less oncogenic HPV types. The phenomenon is proving to be more or less sustainable with different vaccination strategies and different vaccines.
Both serological and DNA studies have demonstrated that previous infection with the non‐oncogenic HPV types confers protection against neoplasia following infection with an oncogenic type. , , Basis for possible interactions of the remaining non‐vaccine covered HPV types is elaborated.
Screening for cervical cancer is most effective as HPV‐based screening but rapid changes in HPV prevalence and associated disease risk of the total HPV exposure make it difficult to design optimal strategies for the future. , 

There is a highly significant difference in the magnitude of the herd effect induced by gender‐neutral vs. girls‐only HPV vaccination strategies with the bivalent vaccine. Overall protection against infections with oncogenic HPV types, immediate cervical pre‐cancer, and cervical cancer varies depending on both the vaccination strategy and HPV vaccination coverage. This is important for unvaccinated women, but also of note for women who have received just one vaccine dose. Modelled prospects for cervical screening in the different groups of women are presented, assuming varying implementation of HPV vaccination. The current analysis is done within the EU‐funded JA EUCanScreen.
Depending on the applied strategies of national HPV vaccination programs, their over‐time impact on the elimination of vaccine‐targeted oncogenic HPV types will differ. Also the resilience of the different vaccination program generated protection against cervical cancer differs. Different cervical screening scenarios have been modelled for different European countries within the EU‐project on risk‐based screening for cervical cancer (RISCC) and will be compared, assuming different residual risk of cervical cancer.

## UNDERSTANDING THE EFFECTS OF VACCINATION ON INFECTIONS AND DISEASES
Both Streptococcus pneumoniae and HPV are genetically and antigenically diverse micro‐organisms that induce complex and multi‐armed immune responses, imperatively involving antibodies. Each can colonize and infect without causing disease. Furthermore, different S. pneumoniae and HPV types vary in their disease manifestations and frequencies of causing diseases per person. Finally, highly efficient multivalent vaccines that have an impact on the prevalence of types of these micro‐organisms in the population are widely used in national vaccination programs.

S. pneumoniae is a gram+ diplococcus colonizing the nasopharynx and transmitted between healthy carriers. Annually, it causes up to 4 million severe episodes worldwide. IPDs (invasive pneumococcal diseases) comprise meningitis, bloodstream infections, and pneumonia that in 2015 caused 295.000–515.000 deaths in minors under age 5. Determined by the capsular polysaccharide, there are more than 92 different serotypes. The number of S. pneumoniae serotypes covered by the highly efficient multivalent capsule—protein conjugate vaccines has increased from seven (PCV7, 2000) via PCV13 (2010) to current PCV20/21.
Initial clinical trials of PCV7 showed no evidence of type‐replacement in IPD. However, theoretical models suggested type‐replacement in IPD might be non‐existent or undetectable in clinical trials, yet become important when vaccines are used on a population scale. Indeed, 12 years ago, a meta‐analysis confirmed that the percentage of IPDs caused by vaccine‐covered pneumococcal types is reduced to 25% in a year and below 5% in 7 years. During the same time, the number of IPDs caused by non‐vaccine‐covered tripled, resulting in a plateauing 50% net reduction. Recently, administration of PCV10/13 in children has resulted in somewhat higher reduction of all serotype IPDs, albeit with the tripling of remaining non‐vaccine IPDs. , In older people (65+), somewhat more subtle changes in the reduction of all serotype IPDs and an increase in non‐vaccine IPDs have been reported, and post‐COVID‐19 years have seen a resurgence in IPD and community‐acquired pneumonia incidence in adults in several populations that has not been fully explained. As for pneumococcal carriage, while most vaccine types nearly disappear, non‐vaccine types increase, and total carriage does not substantially change. 

Why and how are so many serotypes maintained in the first place? Even though the structure of high‐prevalence serotypes (e.g., 19A, 19F, 6A) makes them more fit/competitive with higher acquisition rates and longer duration of infection, they do not take over. How do the low fitness serotypes (e.g., 1, 4, 5) continue to persist in a vaccination naïve population?
The long‐term co‐existence of multiple pneumococcal types appears to stem from two factors: serotype‐specific immunity and age‐related convergence in serotype fitness. Due to serotype‐specific immunity, common serotypes become less able to circulate in the population. This increases opportunities for transmission of other, less‐fit serotypes that have not created immunity.
On the other hand, the fitness of specific pneumococcal serotypes flattens with the age of the non‐immune host. This is seen as the outstanding duration of infections caused by serotypes 6A, 10A, and 19A in children under 22 months of age gets lost in a couple of years. Together, these two factors explain the pre‐vaccine polymorphism of mostly the same pneumococcal serotypes in a wide variety of populations. 

Further work has sought to understand in more detail the patterns of type‐replacement, focusing on carriage. Accessory genes are defined as genes present in some but not all S. pneumoniae isolates. Remarkably, it appears that characterizing the pneumococcal population by the presence and absence of these genes, and positing that populations will evolve in response to vaccination in a direction that restores the pre‐vaccine frequencies of these accessory genes (which have been perturbed by vaccination) has shown strong predictive power in multiple settings, a hypothesis bolstered by the similarity of these frequencies across disparate host populations. Because there are strong (though imperfect) correlations between serotypes and accessory gene frequencies, this in turn has predictive power for the serotype pattern post‐vaccination. , 

Post pneumococcal vaccination, the overall IPD incidence has declined, especially in the 0–4 age group (some very recent trends following the COVID‐19 pandemic raise questions about the permanence of this decline, especially in adults, as noted above). This indicates that, in addition to type‐replacement of pneumococcal carriage, also disease replacement has taken and is taking place, but has been more limited than carriage replacement because vaccination covers the most invasive serotypes. Continuous surveillance of carriage, non‐bacteremic pneumonia, and IPD, which constitute major portions of S. pneumoniae‐associated disease burden, is critical to ascertain the effect of pneumococcal vaccines and other factors on the incidence of pneumococcal disease over the long term.
Returning to HPV, Finnish and Dutch studies, , report modest replacement by types HPV52, 56, and 58 following marked reduction of bivalent HPV16/18 vaccine‐covered (via cross‐reactivity) HPV types 16, 18, 31, 45. The order of infections by HPV types may reduce the risk of cervical neoplasia if a non‐oncogenic HPV infection precedes an infection with an oncogenic HPV type. , Mathematical modeling studies suggest that HPV type‐replacement may take between 10 and 20 years. The modeling studies imply that more cross‐protection against non‐vaccine HPV types means less type‐replacement. On the other hand, more cross‐immunity from natural HPV infections means more competition between HPV types and more potential for type‐replacement. 

In conclusion, causal evidence on type‐replacement by a number of (sero)types following pneumococcal vaccination is unambiguous, and the evidence is also emerging for HPV vaccination. The replaced pneumococci serotypes and oncogenic HPV types have been associated with less severe disease. For HPV vaccination, this can be seen in the strong reduction of vaccine‐covered HPV prevalence and in the incidence of immediate cervical precancer. However, the dynamics of vaccine and non‐vaccine types are poorly understood, and it is difficult to predict the long‐term outcomes of HPV type‐replacement.

## EVALUATING HPV TYPE‐REPLACEMENT IN A COMMUNITY‐RANDOMIZED TRIAL WITH LONG‐TERM FOLLOW‐UP
Genital HPVs comprise a large and diverse population of viruses. The introduction of prophylactic HPV vaccines through comprehensive girls‐only vaccination or randomized girls‐only or gender‐neutral programs over 15 years ago , has already altered host‐virus dynamics of genital HPVs across countries and communities. Most notably, this appears to have led to an ecological niche clearance, with a near‐complete depletion of oncogenic HPV types 16 and 18 among vaccinees. , , 

In 2007, Finland initiated a community‐randomized trial to compare the effects of gender‐neutral versus girls‐only HPV vaccination on the prevalence of oncogenic HPV types among young women. Out of 39,420 girls and 40,852 boys invited, 20,512 (52.0%) girls and 11,661 (28.5%) boys born between 1992 and 1995 participated in the trial from 2007 to 2010. In total, 12,403 girls received three doses of the bivalent HPV16/18 vaccine around the age of 14 (range 12–15). Serial cervical samples for cytology and HPV DNA typing were collected at ages 18, 22, 25, and 28 from 5988, 5626, 5112, and 4778 vaccinees, respectively. To our knowledge, this is the largest consented and randomized long‐term follow‐up cohort of women, 99.4% of whom received three doses of the HPV16/18 vaccine as early adolescents. 

We have earlier reported on the community‐level occurrence of genital HPV types up to 8 years post girls‐only or gender‐neutral vaccination beyond pairwise HPVs comparison using an evolutionary and ecological framework approach (Pimenoff et al. 2023). Both the gender‐neutral and girls‐only vaccination communities' HPV type diversity distribution showed differential clustering from the control communities, mostly due to depletion of vaccine‐targeted HPV types 16/18/31/45 and clustering due to the enrichment of non‐vaccine‐targeted HPV types 35/51/52/56/58/59/66 in vaccinated communities. Moreover, we observed that 8 years post HPV vaccination, only the gender‐neutral vaccination communities' HPV type distribution showed significantly increased alpha‐diversity measures. It is important to note that this fits well with our observation of a significantly stronger herd effect in the gender‐neutral communities compared to girls‐only vaccination communities. 

Twelve years after vaccination, our repeated analysis revealed that the community‐level prevalence of non‐vaccine targeted HPV types 39, 51, 52, 56, 58, and 59 had indeed increased in the gender‐neutral vaccination communities. Based on the diversity profiles of HPV types, these communities formed a distinct cluster separate from the control communities, driven both by the rising prevalence of the non‐targeted types and the depletion of vaccine‐targeted HPV types 16/18/31/45 (Figure  ). Finally, in the gender‐neutral communities, we observed that HPVs alpha‐diversity remained consistently high 12 years after vaccination, closely resembling the levels measured 8 years post‐vaccination (Figure  ).
 Altogether, a decline in vaccine‐targeted HPV types is clearly observed among young adults living in communities where gender‐neutral vaccination was adopted. Concurrently, these communities exhibit an increase in non‐vaccine‐targeted HPV prevalence and sustained high ecological diversity up to 12 years after vaccination. This evidence supports the idea of niche occupation by non‐vaccine targeted HPV types, that is, HPV type‐replacement, and has significant implications for the design of cervical cancer screening programs.

## INTERACTIONS OF INFECTIONS WITH BENIGN AND ONCOGENIC HPV TYPES, AND OTHER MICROBES
There are at least 40 anogenital HPVs, but only 13 of them are defined as oncogenic. Early register studies on external genital warts (EGW) and HPV‐cancers showed a slightly increased risk associated with EGW , while seroepidemiological studies found that HPV6 seropositivity decreases the HPV16‐associated risk of cervical cancer. , A case‐cohort setting with serum samples taken at two consecutive pregnancies from altogether 94.349 Finnish women before cervical carcinoma in situ (CIS) diagnosis enabled a unique longitudinal setting to study the effect of the order of the different infections. In a HPV16 seropositivity adjusted analysis, compared to seropositivity for both HPV6 and HPV31 already at the first pregnancy, CIS RRs related to HPV6 seropositivity before and after HPV31 seroconversion were 0.4 (95% CI 0.0, 4.4) and 10 (95% CI 1.8, 57), suggesting that a preceding infection with non‐oncogenic HPV affected the oncogenicity of an oncogenic HPV type. On the other hand, concomitant infection, plausibly in the adenosquamous cells of the upper cervix with HPV types 18/45 and Chlamydia trachomatis, might synergistically increase the risk of CIS/adenocarcinoma in situ. 

Antagonistic interaction has also been found in HPV DNA detection studies. A large nested case–control study exploited the Swedish National Cervical Screening Register's records of altogether 1.4579.258 women who had participated in organized cervical screening to identify prediagnostic smears for 621 and 457 women, who later were diagnosed respectively with cervical CIS and squamous cervical cancer (SCC). Cervical smears of the CIS and SCC cases and 1:1 matched healthy controls with similar screening history were analyzed for non‐oncogenic HPV types 6/7/11/42/43/70/90 and oncogenic or possibly oncogenic HPV types 16/18/1831/33/35/39/45/51/52/56/58/59/66/68/73/82 with PCR. Compared to women who were infected with an oncogenic HPV type only, women who were also infected with a non‐oncogenic HPV had a decreased relative risk of SCC (RR = 0.2, 95% confidence interval 0.04–0.99). Also, the lead time to SCC diagnosis from the last cervical sampling was 4.8 years longer in women with concomitant non‐oncogenic and oncogenic HPV infections than in women with only oncogenic HPV infection, indicating that the non‐oncogenic HPV types may interfere in cervical carcinogenesis. Now that the HPV type distribution is rapidly changing due to HPV vaccination, understanding the nature of interactions between oncogenic and non‐oncogenic HPV types is more and more important.

## RISK‐BASED SCREENING AFTER GIRLS‐ONLY AND GENDER‐NEUTRAL HPV VACCINATION
In most countries with an organized program, cervical cancer screening is performed through one‐size‐fits‐all programs in which women are invited at fixed ages. These programs lead to frequent screening of women at low risk, and a more effective and efficient approach would be to direct screening resources toward those at the highest risk. Cervical cancer screening offers a unique opportunity for risk‐based screening as virtually all cases of cervical cancer are caused by oncogenic HPV types, and the natural history of cervical cancer, type by type, is one of the best studied of all cancers. Furthermore, screening registries already hold data on file about screening histories, which is highly predictive of the risk of cancer and pre‐cancer, and these data can be used directly to optimize the screening program.
Risk‐based screening becomes particularly important with the influx of vaccinated cohorts. The risk of HPV in vaccinated cohorts is decreasing rapidly, so multiple adaptations to the screening protocol will be needed in the coming years in order to maintain a program with a favorable benefit‐to‐harm ratio. The first adaptations concern the start age of screening, the primary screening and triage test(s) in the first round, and the screening interval after the first screen. These will be discussed below.
The start age for screening is currently discussed in many countries, as the prevalence of the vaccine types is consistently declining in vaccinated birth cohorts. Substantial declines have been observed in vaccinated women and also in unvaccinated women, being protected through herd effects. For example, in Sweden, a country with a vaccine coverage above 80%, the HPV16 and HPV18 prevalence declined by 98% and 99%, respectively, in the 2000 birth cohort compared to the unvaccinated 1984 birth cohort. This decline in HPV16 and HPV18 prevalence was much greater than the vaccine coverage, indicating the presence of substantial herd effects. Substantial herd effects were also observed in countries with moderate vaccine coverage. In the Netherlands, a country with coverage between 50% and 60%, HPV16 and HPV18 declined by 70% and 45%, respectively, in unvaccinated women from the 2001 birth cohort compared to unvaccinated women from the 1993/1994 birth cohort, the first to be offered vaccination. 

Herd effect plays an important role in the design of screening strategies. If herd effects are limited, screening could, in principle, be made more efficient by using different screening start ages for unvaccinated and vaccinated women. So far, this risk‐based strategy has only been implemented in Italy, where vaccinated women are recommended to start screening at age 30 and unvaccinated women start at age 25. However, this strategy has a number of drawbacks. For example, it requires linking vaccination and screening registries involving logistical challenges and possible violation of privacy, and vaccinated women may view a reduction in screening intensity as a downgrade to their healthcare services. Additionally, herd effects accumulate over time, so that stratification by vaccination status may only lead to temporary efficiency gains.
An alternative approach is to maintain the same start age for vaccinated and unvaccinated women and to adjust the start age based on the prevalence of HPV vaccine types in the first screening round(s). While this approach circumvents the challenges associated with stratification by vaccination status, it may be less cost‐effective until the herd effects are large enough to increase the start age for all women. In this regard, a strong reduction in HPV vaccine types among unvaccinated women is predicted to happen earlier with a gender‐neutral vaccination program than a girls‐only vaccination program. Models have predicted that 80% coverage of gender‐neutral vaccination is possibly sufficient to reach elimination of HPV vaccine types, whereas a higher coverage is needed for girls‐only vaccination. 

As vaccination leads to a decrease in the prevalence of the most oncogenic HPV types, the risk of cancer and high‐grade lesions in HPV‐positive women will also decrease. To maintain an effective and efficient triage strategy, an increasing number of countries manage HPV‐positive results by defining separate strategies for different HPV genotypes or genotype subgroups. Several countries, including Australia, the USA, Denmark, Sweden, and the Netherlands, have adopted HPV16/18 genotyping for the management of positive HPV results. Sweden also distinguishes HPV31, HPV33, HPV45, HPV52, and HPV58 from the remaining seven lesser oncogenic HPV types since these lesser oncogenic types rarely cause cancer in young women and are associated with a relatively low risk of high‐grade lesions. , Specifically, Sweden has recommended that women under 33 who test positive for the seven lesser oncogenic types should be treated as a normal finding and require no additional action (https://kunskapsbanken.cancercentrum.se/globalassets/vara‐uppdrag/prevention‐tidig‐upptackt/gynekologisk‐cellprovskontroll/vardprogram/nationellt‐vardprogram‐cervixcancerprevention.pdf).
Vaccination will also decrease the risk of future cancer after the first screening round, both in vaccinated women and unvaccinated women, following a reduction in the circulation of the most oncogenic HPV genotypes, in the latter solely due to herd effect. This supports extending the screening interval based on the HPV result rather than on vaccination status. In the Netherlands, this approach has been implemented for older unvaccinated age groups, with HPV‐negative women aged 40 or 50 being invited back after 10 years, whereas HPV‐positive women are invited back after 5 years due to an increased risk of high‐grade lesions. , The same approach can be implemented for vaccinated cohorts, as reported in a recent Dutch study (https://medrxiv.org/cgi/content/short/2025.06.12.25329488v1).
To conclude, in the risk‐based screening context, HPV genotyping is a strong risk stratifier in vaccinated cohorts and can be used to inform the start age for screening, the management of HPV‐positive women, and the screening interval after the first screen.

## SCREENING OF VACCINATED AND UNVACCINATED WOMEN WITHIN VACCINATED BIRTH COHORTS
HPV vaccination strategy and coverage vary significantly between countries. Previously, we used mathematical modeling to study the outcomes of cervical cancer screening in differently vaccinated birth cohorts. Now, we are examining how the outcomes differ between vaccinated and unvaccinated women within vaccinated cohorts.
We formed various vaccinated cohorts with either no, low (40%), or high (80%) coverage, using either girls‐only or girls‐and‐boys strategy. Additionally, we used vaccines providing standard protection (HPV16/18) or wide protection (HPV16/18/31/33/45/52/58), assuming 95% efficacy against infection of target types, lasting for 20 years and thereafter waning in a mean of 20 years. The evaluated screening scenarios included 0 to 9 rounds at preset ages (Figure  ) with assumed 95% attendance rates, using HPV‐primary screening with Pap triage, except for HPV16/18‐primary test at age 25 years. The Finnish HPV transmission and progression model , , was used to simulate cervical cancer cases for each screening scenario in each vaccinated cohort. For comparison, the lifetime risk of cervical cancer (LRCC), that is, the lifetime number of cases per 100,000 vaccinated/unvaccinated women, was presented for selected cohorts. For the low vaccine coverage cohorts (Figure  , orange and green), the LRCC of unvaccinated women (o, x‐axis) was close to that of no vaccination, reflecting only weak herd effects. The LRCC of vaccinated women (x) was much lower, but a few screening rounds were still required to improve from the realized good levels of the pre‐vaccination era (gray zone), especially with the standard protection vaccine.
 For the cohorts vaccinated with high coverage using the girls‐only strategy (light pink and light blue), the LRCC of unvaccinated women was clearly reduced from the low coverage cohorts, reflecting improved herd effects. Similarly, the LRCC of vaccinated women was also reduced as the improved herd effects compensated for the <100% vaccine efficacy used in the analysis. For the cohorts vaccinated with high coverage using the girls‐and‐boys strategy (red and blue), the LRCCs of unvaccinated and vaccinated women were similar because the herd effects were strong enough to eliminate the vaccine‐protected HPV types from these cohorts. In the analysis, the number of lifetime screening tests of a cohort (Figure  , y‐axis) depended only on the screening scenario, not on HPV vaccination. Thus, when HPV vaccination was stronger and prevented more cervical cancer, increasing the number of screening tests (screening rounds) had little effect.
The analysis illustrates how the difference in cervical cancer risks between vaccinated and unvaccinated women depends on the strength of herd effects produced by the HPV vaccination program. Country‐specific variation likely exists so that similar vaccine coverage may not produce exactly similar herd effects in different countries. Nevertheless, with weak or only moderate herd effects, the cancer risks between vaccinated and unvaccinated women differ significantly. In such a situation, providing enough screening for unvaccinated women and at the same time not over‐screening vaccinated women is a challenge. Either an individual woman's vaccination status needs to be used as a basis for screening, or screening needs to be otherwise adapted to the different risks. On the other hand, strong herd effects not only help in reducing cancer risks among both unvaccinated and vaccinated women but also diminish the difference between these groups. Under strong herd effects, equally de‐intensified screening will likely be similarly sufficient for both vaccinated and unvaccinated women.

## RISK‐BASED SCREENING FOR CERVICAL CANCER IN EUROPE—RESULTS OF MODELING STUDIES
To identify and evaluate effective and cost‐effective risk‐based screening algorithms based on screening history, vaccination status (both individual and at the population level), and patient‐derived risk factors, we have developed and validated a microsimulation model of multiple oncogenic HPV infection progression to cervical cancer. The model was designed to compare the effectiveness and cost‐effectiveness of different cervical cancer screening algorithms adapted to specific risk profiles of the women in a population. To account for heterogeneity of screening programs across Europe, we have also shortlisted different cervical cancer screening algorithms reflecting the status of risk‐based screening across the continent. The effectiveness and cost‐effectiveness of the proposed algorithms have been evaluated using a unique framework for 27 EU countries. To maximize interpretability of our findings, the framework was designed to summarize estimates of the resources needed in each country, as the costing of screening procedures is highly variable between countries or jurisdictions and may change over time.
The detailed findings of this exercise are reported elsewhere (https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/847845/results). Overall, however, moving toward risk‐based cervical cancer screening was found to be cost‐effective in all EU countries, but required a tailored approach to identify algorithms for optimal resource allocation in each local setting. The exact configuration of local screening algorithms needs to be adapted to each setting, based on feasibility and cost‐effectiveness. The reported work is currently being used to support the European Commission Initiative for Cervical Cancer designed to develop evidence‐based recommendations for cancer screening (https://cancer-screening-and-care.jrc.ec.europa.eu/en/ec-cvc, EC‐CvC; EU4Health grant agreement no. 101122251) and to assess the robustness and feasibility of integrating HPV vaccination and HPV‐based screening in selected vulnerable populations across Europe, such as among migrant populations and commercial sex workers (https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/101155975, HPV‐Faster Implement; EU4Health grant agreement No. 101155975).
In Figure  , we illustrate the main types of outputs of this model‐based evaluation of risk‐stratified cervical cancer screening for Italy; similar outputs have been obtained for all 27 EU countries. In this example, we have compared a one‐size‐fits‐all cervical cancer screening algorithm (lower left panel), switching from cytology screening (repeated every 3 years) in women aged 24–29 years to HPV‐DNA screening (with liquid‐based cytology triage of HPV‐positive women) every 5 years for women between 30 and 65 years of age, with a risk‐based algorithm (upper left panel) offering HPV‐DNA screening every 10 years in women of the age range 25–65, but with a shorter interval (5 years) for those positive to HR‐HPV and triaged negative after liquid‐based cytology assessments.
 Impact and efficiency of the two screening algorithms, by impact of HPV vaccination in the population, are compared in the right panel (Figure  ). In an unvaccinated population, the number of disability‐adjusted‐life‐years (DALYs) avoided by the two algorithms was the same (i.e., 114 per 1000 women), whereas the number of colposcopies necessary to screen the population was 55% less with the risk‐based algorithm, that is, 146 versus 326. As expected, with an increasing impact of HPV vaccination, i.e. reduction of HPV prevalence of types targeted by the vaccine, the number of DALYs avoided decreased up to 28 per 1000 women; the decrease was similar with both one‐size‐fits‐all and risk‐based algorithms; however, the efficiency of the risk‐based algorithm steadily increased with the impact of HPV vaccination in the population, that is, 64% and 80% less colposcopy was necessary to screen the target population with 40% and 80% HPV vaccination impact, respectively.
Note, in the EU‐wide exercise, we have assessed 177 different risk‐based algorithms, and the impact, harms, and efficiency of each algorithm have been evaluated for each EU representative population.

## CONCLUSIONS
There are several important messages of the studies on the effect of vaccinations on HPV prevalence. Type replacement phenomena are not expected to occur until vaccine‐targeted HPV types are essentially eliminated, and in settings where this has happened, type replacement with less oncogenic types is seen. When the HPV types that are left are increasingly less associated with cervical cancer but are still progressing to high‐grade lesions, the high‐grade lesions also become increasingly associated with less oncogenic HPV types. This type and disease replacement toward lower oncogenicity worsens the balance of benefits and harms of cervical cancer screening, as detected and treated precursor lesions are increasingly less progressive.
Addressing the challenge by launching different screening strategies for vaccinated and unvaccinated women is fraught with problems related to logistical, ethical, and scientific issues. On the other hand, if vaccination strategies that rapidly reach strong herd immunity are used, the protection will be similar for all women in highly vaccinated birth cohorts, and screening strategies simply based on the HPV prevalence in the birth cohorts can be used. In addition to the well‐known strategy of high‐coverage vaccination in girls‐only programs, gender‐neutral vaccination results in a more rapid and stronger herd immunity that is seen already with moderate vaccination coverage. Also, vaccination of adult women in the younger screening ages can result in a more rapid reduction of HPV incidences, and furthermore, has the advantage that the screening strategies that apply to populations with strong herd immunity can be applied.
The quest for the optimal screening algorithm is becoming increasingly complex and difficult as the HPV prevalence and the progressive potential of lesions are changing fast, making it uncertain if any given strategy will still be useful even just a few years later. The research is therefore increasingly focused on risk‐based screening strategies, where there is less consideration of old‐fashioned questions like age to start screening and which screening interval to use. A risk‐based program is instead based on assessing risk and issuing screening invitations only if the risk is sufficiently high, such that a favorable benefit‐to‐harm ratio is likely.
In the emerging scenario of how to design high‐impact interventions in the post‐HPV vaccination world, two key components will be vaccination strategies that achieve strong herd immunity as fast as possible and risk‐based HPV screening programs. Basic research will have a fundamental role in deepening our understanding and prediction of HPV population biology and associated oncogenicity. The HPV prevalence will have a central role because they (i) are an early endpoint on the effectiveness of a vaccination strategy to achieve population immunity against HPV as fast as possible and (ii) they will determine the cancer risk in a population and form the basis for the design of risk‐based screening programs. As we today have many populations that have achieved strong declines in HPV prevalence, and as HPV‐based screening programs are increasingly using extended HPV genotyping, the basic data exists to explore and predict the optimal interventions for optimal cervical cancer control.
