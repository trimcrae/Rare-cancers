## Introduction
The global burden of chronic kidney disease, which has been exacerbated by the high number of patients requiring dialysis to sustain their lives. This makes the dialysis units a high-pressure unit prone to severe burnout. Nurses are particularly prone to burnout due to the demanding nature of their roles, combined with administrative burdens, staffing shortages, and organisational constraints. However, there is a dearth of studies examining burnout among nurses in dialysis units in resource-constrained settings.

## Aim
The study explored burnout among nurses working in the dialysis unit of a Teaching Hospital, Ghana.

## Methods
A facility-based descriptive qualitative study research design was used to collect data from ten purposively selected nurses working in the dialysis unit of a Teaching Hospital in Ghana. Individual interviews were conducted with the participants using a semi-structured interview guide. Data were analysed using thematic analysis.

## Results
Three themes emerged from the data analysis: causes of burnout, impact of burnout, and coping with burnout among nurses. The causes of burnout identified included high workload with fewer staff, physical demands of dialysis, inadequate material resources, and emotional strain of dialysis care. Burnout impacted the physical, psychosocial, and emotional well-being of nurses, as well as impacting patient care. The nurses coped with burnout by employing personal resources such as physical and emotional self-care strategies, social support, as well as very little job resources like workplace adjustments.

## Conclusions
The phenomenon of burnout is a serious occupational health challenge among nurses in dialysis units in resource-constrained settings like Ghana. The findings emphasise the need for increased job resources rather than individual coping mechanisms to effectively deal with the negative impact of burnout on nurses in the dialysis unit.

## Introduction
Globally, there is a huge burden of chronic kidney disease (CKD), with over 843 million individuals living with the condition [ – ]. A significant number of11.3 million new cases of CKD are recorded annually in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), contributing to a global estimate of 13.3 million new cases of CKD each year [ ]. Furthermore, the prevalence of CKD in Africa is notable, with an estimated 15.8 million individuals affected by the condition [ ]. Yet in many of these countries, the facilities to manage the disease are rudimentary or scarce.
It must be pointed out that, haemodialysis is an essential therapy that significantly extends the lifespan of patients with renal failure. Dialysis serves the vital functions of managing fluid and electrolyte imbalances, and toxin accumulation when the kidneys can no longer do so on their own effectively [ ]. Haemodialysis relies heavily on a skilled nursing workforce who perform various roles, including vascular access management, primary care of patients, family support, nutrition guidance, infection control, safety protocols, and machinery oversight [ ].
The role of nurses in dialysis in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) is even expanded, as nurses often have an increased scope of practice in the management of dialysis centres [ ]. This is due to an inadequate number of nephrologists, compelling nurses to assume roles that are originally not meant for nursing staff [ ]. Additionally, the global shortage of nephrology nurses means that many nurses who are providing dialysis care have not been adequately trained to perform these tasks [ ]. However, task shifting without adequate supervision in the face of limited resources could increase the level of stress experienced by these nurses [ , ].
Indeed, recent studies have underscored the severity of burnout among nurses [ – ]. As frontline caregivers, nurses are particularly vulnerable to burnout due to the demanding nature of their roles, which often entail long hours of work, high patient loads, and emotional intensity [ ]. In addition, systemic challenges of healthcare delivery contribute to burnout among nurses [ , ].
Nurses in high-pressure environments such as the dialysis unit are particularly prone to even higher levels of burnout stemming from the multifaceted challenges within the dialysis unit, as well as from a combination of workplace stressors and inherent challenges within the healthcare environment [ – ]. For example, dialysis nurses are tasked with managing complex procedures, administering treatments, and monitoring patients with CKD, often in high-pressure settings characterised by tight schedules and intense patient care needs [ , ]. The intricate operation of advanced, life-sustaining technology contributes to the cognitive and technical demands placed on these healthcare professionals [ , ].
In effect, nurses’ responsibilities extend beyond the technical aspects of dialysis to encompass emotional support, patient education, and care coordination [ ]. Nurses in dialysis units are continually exposed to patient suffering and the challenging dynamics of chronic illness, demanding sustained emotional engagement [ , , ]. Further, the need for ongoing, meticulous care and the limited options available for treatment, especially in LMICs, expose dialysis nurses to stress [ , , ]. These factors lead to feelings of helplessness and frustration among nurses, thus contributing to emotional and compassion fatigue among dialysis nurses [ , ]. Besides, organisational factors play a significant role in contributing to burnout among dialysis nurses. Staffing shortages, inadequate resources, and high patient-to-nurse ratios can increase workload pressures and intensify job stress [ , ]. Again, administrative burdens, such as documentation requirements and regulatory compliance, may detract from direct patient care time, leaving nurses feeling overwhelmed and unsupported [ ]. This imposes an exceptional burden on individual nurses, making them particularly prone to emotional, physical, and mental exhaustion.
In effect, burnout among nurses in dialysis units exerts significant impacts on multiple facets of healthcare delivery and workforce dynamics [ ]. For it tends to compromise the quality of patient care by diminishing nurses’ capacity to provide empathetic and attentive assistance [ ]. Emotional exhaustion and depersonalization can lead to communication breakdowns, decreased patient satisfaction, and heightened risks of medical errors, ultimately jeopardizing patient safety and outcomes [ , ]. At the individual level, burnout takes a toll on the well-being of the nurse, causing feelings of fatigue, detachment, and diminished job satisfaction [ ]. Such emotional strain contributes to high turnover rates within healthcare settings, exacerbating staffing shortages and impeding the continuity of care [ – ].
The World Health Organization (WHO) defines burnout as physical, emotional, or mental exhaustion accompanied by decreased motivation, lowered performance, and negative attitudes toward oneself and others [ ]. The phenomenon is characterised by emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment [ – ]. It results from performing at a high level until stress and tension, especially from extreme and prolonged physical or mental exertion or an overburdening workload, take their toll [ , ].
Notwithstanding the severity and impact of burnout on nurses working at the dialysis units, studies examining the phenomenon in Ghana have not yet addressed this phenomenon in the context of dialysis nurses [ – ]. The phenomenon of burnout among dialysis nurses has been explored outside the Ghanaian context [ , , , ]. However, there exists a notable gap in the understanding of burnout among nurses in the dialysis units within the Ghanaian context. This study fills this research gap by exploring burnout among nurses working in the dialysis unit of a Teaching Hospital in Ghana.

## Theoretical framework
We applied the job demands-resources (JD-R) model to explore burnout among nurses working in the dialysis unit of a teaching hospital in Ghana. The JD-R model is a widely used framework for studying job stress [ ]. The model identifies two job characteristics that relate to burnout: job demands and job resources. Whereas job demands normally will result in job stress, and thus are associated with exhaustion, job resources, on the other hand, support goal achievement and they are associated with job engagement and personal growth [ ]. Job resources tend to boost work engagement. Hence, they are particularly important when demands are high. In the case of burnout, it results from an imbalance between job demands and job resources [ ]. In other words, job strain is negatively related to job performance, while motivation is related to positive job performance [ ]. In work settings like the nursing profession, it is impossible to keep job demands low; however, providing employees with enough job resources leads to work engagement [ ]. Burnout and work engagement are important concepts that predict important outcomes for individual employees and organisations at large [ ].
In high-pressure environments like dialysis units, nurses are particularly prone to burnout’s three classical symptoms of emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment [ ]. The situation is even worse in resource-constrained environments where essential resources for patient care may be lacking. In such environments, job resources that could help nurses deal with the job demands are generally woefully inadequate. While specific statistics for burnout among nurses working in the dialysis units in Ghana are not readily available, the overall trend suggests that a significant proportion of the nurses report exhaustion and burnout in their workplaces [ ].

## Methodology

## Design
We conducted a facility-based qualitative study using a descriptive research design to explore nurses’ experiences with burnout in the dialysis unit of a resource-limited facility. This design enabled us to capture the essence and subjective meanings of participants’ experiences, thus, allowing for an in-depth exploration of how nurses perceived, interpreted, and made sense of burnout and the impact of these factors within the context of their daily work [ ]. This study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines [ ].

## Study site
The study was conducted at the dialysis unit of a teaching hospital in Ghana. The teaching hospital serves as a tertiary healthcare facility in Ghana. The dialysis unit of the hospital operated with five haemodialysis machines, with approximately 13 nurses working at the unit at the time of data collection. The unit operates for about 15 h per day, serving an average of six patients during that time. Depending on the number of patients, however, the unit sometimes runs 24 h depending on the number of patients requiring dialysis in a day.

## Selection of participants
The study participants consisted of nurses working in the dialysis unit of the hospital, who were directly involved in the provision of care to patients undergoing dialysis. The eligibility criteria used listed registered nurses who had worked in the dialysis unit for at least three months, who were directly involved in the care of dialysis patients. It must be pointed out that they are those consented to take part in the study. Three months was considered sufficient time for nurses to have gathered enough experience regarding the phenomenon of burnout at the dialysis unit. Notably, all the nurses had worked within dialysis for periods longer than three months.
The researchers and participants shared a common professional relationship, as they are all nurses. However, none of the researchers worked in the dialysis unit. Being conscious of this relationship, the researchers ensured that their personal biases and shared relationships with the participants did not interfere with the data collection, analyses, and discussion of the study. The corresponding author (FKN), a qualitative researcher with a PhD in nursing, provided supervisory support during data collection, while the interviewers did not have prior personal relationships with the participants. The corresponding author (FKN), provided supervisory support during data collection, while the interviewers did not have prior personal relationships with the participants.

## Sampling
Nurses who met the eligibility criteria for inclusion in the study were selected using a purposive sampling technique. The researchers chose nurses who had sufficient experience in the provision of care to dialysis patients and, thus, could provide relevant information for addressing the research question [ ]. These nurses were contacted by the researchers. Afterwards, a printed copy of the participant information sheet which contained the full details of the study was shared with each of them before being given the option to freely participate. The participant information sheet contained detailed information about the research, including the objectives and the number of participants in the study. A sample size of twelve participants was anticipated to be adequate prior to data collection. However, the actual number of participants was determined through the concept of data saturation, which is, the point at which collecting additional data no longer provided new insights or information related to the research questions [ ].

## Entry to site
The researchers obtained written permission from the management of the teaching hospital to conduct the study in their facility. Nurse managers of the Internal Medicine sub-BMC and the dialysis unit further granted verbal permissions before data collection began. The nurse managers of the dialysis unit served as the gatekeepers who introduced the researchers to the participants. To guard against potential coercion and undue influence from the nurse managers on participants’ autonomy, the researchers adopted confidential recruitment of participants. Beyond introducing the researchers to potential participants, the gatekeepers were not aware of which participants were contacted or recruited into the study. All participants who were contacted duly agreed to participated in the study. There is the possibility that power dynamics could have influenced the decision of some of the participants to take part in the study. However, each participant was given the full assurance of the voluntary nature of their participation. Additionally, participants were encouraged to share their experiences during the interviews with the assurance that these views would be held confidential.

## Data collection
Data collected spanned the period from the 10th to the 29th of December, 2024. Nine face-to-face and one online semi-structured interviews were conducted using an interview guide containing open-ended questions that were developed based on the literature review (supplementary file). The corresponding author (FKN), provided supervisory support during the data collection process. The interview guide was written in English and was structured into three sections: Section A, which explored the causes of burnout among nurses working at the dialysis unit. Sections B and C respectively, focused on the impacts of burnout as well as coping strategies utilised by dialysis nurses to mitigate burnout and promote resilience. The demographic characteristics of the participants were collected using a separate information sheet. All interviews were conducted in the English language by four of the authors (VKP, MDA, CTW, and EA).
The interviews were scheduled to meet the convenience of the participants. The interviews were conducted in a separate, empty, quiet room within the hospital to ensure confidentiality and they were recorded with the participant’s consent. Each interview lasted approximately 40–60 min. Field notes were taken to capture non-verbal cues and additional observations during data collection. All collected data, including audio recordings and transcripts, were securely stored in password-protected files which are accessible only to the research team members.

## Data analysis
Data collection and analysis were conducted simultaneously, in keeping with the protocols in qualitative research [ ]. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim, and participants were allowed to verify their responses by providing them with transcripts of their interviews. This decision was taken to ensure credibility of the data. Data analysis employed thematic analysis that followed Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework [ ]. First, the researchers repeatedly read through the interview transcripts to become familiar with the data. Then, they gained an initial understanding of the scripts, while, at the same time, taking note of their preliminary thoughts. The data were then systematically coded to identify interesting features and organize them into meaningful groups across the entire dataset. Coding of data was done by the corresponding author (FKN) and an independent coder. Both coders have doctoral degrees in nursing with several years of experience in qualitative research. Codes were generated deductively guided by the conceptual framework. Thereafter, coding was manually done, starting with open and in vivo codes, which were later converted to axial codes after repeated engagement with the data. These codes were then grouped into sub-themes and themes, allowing the researchers to capture patterns and insights relevant to the research questions. Themes were reviewed to ensure they accurately represented the data. Subsequently, we refined them for clarity and coherence. Later the datasets were named to reflect their essence. A consensus meeting was held among the coders to reconcile and agree on the final codes and themes. The final analysis involved selecting illustrative extracts to substantiate each theme and crafting a cohesive narrative that addressed the research objectives.

## Rigor
Rigor was ensured using Lincoln and Guba’s criteria of credibility, confirmability, transferability, and dependability [ ]. To ensure credibility, the researchers conscientiously followed the interview guide and kept to the subject matter of the interview during data collection as part of reflexivity. We acknowledge that our backgrounds as nurses may predispose us to our interpretation of certain behaviours or statements through specific lenses. To mitigate these biases, the coders maintained a reflexive journal in which they regularly recorded their thoughts, reactions, and potential assumptions during the coding and interpretation stages. They also engaged in peer debriefing and conducted consensus meetings to reconcile codes. The raw data were consulted multiple times during the process to ensure that emerging themes were grounded in participants’ voices, rather than the coders’ expectations. Only participants who met the inclusion criteria were involved in the study. Also, fieldnotes made during the data collection were incorporated into the final transcripts before data analysis. The researchers have also provided a thorough description of the entire research process to enable other researchers to replicate the study in similar settings or with similar participants, as well as providing a clear definition of the eligibility criteria, thus upholding transferability. To uphold confirmability, the audio recordings were transcribed verbatim, and the researchers maintained a neutral point of view in the data analysis by including direct quotes from participants. A consensus meeting was held among the researchers to reconcile and agree on the final codes. An audit trail was employed to uphold the dependability of the study.

## Ethical considerations
All methods were carried out in accordance with the ‘Declaration of Helsinki’. Ethical clearance was granted by the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Health and Allied Sciences (protocol number: UHAS-REC A.11 [12] 23–24). Formal permission was granted by the management of the teaching hospital ahead of the data collection. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant before the start of data collection. Participants received hard copies of detailed information about the study’s purpose, procedures, and potential risks, with the right to withdraw at any time, before signing the consent form. Confidentiality was strictly maintained by anonymising all data, ensuring that only the research team members had access to identifiable information. Respect for participants’ autonomy and dignity were upheld during interview sessions. Each participant was allowed to choose a suitable time and venue for the interview session to ensure their comfort and convenience. Again, the interviewers maintained a neutral point of view by being non-judgmental about the responses provided by each participant during the interview. Interviews were conducted in private rooms to safeguard the privacy of participants.

## Results

## Sociodemographic characteristics of participants
Ten nurses, which are five females and five males, participated in the study. Their ages ranged from 28 to 38 years. Seven of the participants were single, with the remaining three being married. Only two of them had at least one child. In terms of rank, seven of the participants were Senior Staff Nurses, and only one each was a Nursing Officer and Senior Nursing Officer. The years of work experience ranged from two to eight years. However, the duration of working in the dialysis unit ranged from seven months to eight years (Table  ).

 

## Themes and sub-themes
From the qualitative data analysis, three themes and 10 subthemes emerged (Table  ).

 

## Causes of burnout
This theme explored the causes of burnout among nurses working at the dialysis unit of a Teaching Hospital in Ghana. It emerged that burnout among nurses at the dialysis unit is a regular occurrence. The nurses experienced burnout primarily due to a combination of job demands, including high workload, inadequate resources, and emotional strain.

## High workload and demands of dialysis
The participants described consistently experiencing high workload in the dialysis unit, which was a major contributor to burnout among the nurses at the unit. According to the nurses, the increased workload was a result of the increasing number of patients requiring dialysis services, coupled with insufficient staff numbers. This situation led to long working hours, physical exhaustion, and emotional strain.
The participants described being overwhelmed by the number of patients they had to attend to, often with limited number of staff. They explained that the situation caused them to overwork. A participant described the situation in the following quote:The number of patients has increased drastically, but the number of staff remains the same. We end up working extra hours, which we are not supposed to do. (Nurse 6).
The main challenge is that the patients are more than the number of [dialysis] machines in the unit (Nurse 10).

To provide round-the-clock services to patients and clients, health facilities in Ghana usually run three shifts over 24 h. However, the nurses in this study explained that they have had to cut down the number of shifts due to inadequate nursing staff numbers.Currently, because we don’t have adequate staff, we are unable to do the three shift systems, so we only do the morning and afternoon shift. (Nurse 3)

This arrangement, however, did not solve the problem as the shifts are often extended beyond the scheduled hours due to the high patient load and equipment breakdowns. This left them with little time for rest or personal life.Sometimes, we close very late, like midnight. You go home after 12am, and the next day you have to report to work early. It’s not easy. (Nurse 2)
We are supposed to close at 8 p.m., but with the number of patients, we end up closing at 11 p.m. or later. You have to come the next day to continue your work. (Nurse 7)

The physical demands of the job, such as lifting patients and moving heavy equipment, were also highlighted as a cause of burnout. According to the nurses, they get exhausted from having to manually lift patients or move heavy equipment around to attend to patients. This situation is often compounded by the inadequate staff numbers.We have to lift patients from their beds to the dialysis chairs, and some patients are completely dependent. It’s physically exhausting, especially when we are short-staffed. (Nurse 4)
Sometimes we have to push heavy trolleys with consumables from the store to the unit. It’s very tiring, and we don’t have enough staff to share the workload. (Nurse 8)

## Inadequate material resources
Shortages of essential consumables, frequent breakdown of over-stretched machines, and procurement delays contributed to stress and burnout among nurses working at the dialysis unit of a Teaching Hospital in Ghana. The participants indicated that stockouts of essential consumables such as blood lines and dialyzers were a recurring issue. Nurses often have to spend extra time sourcing these items, which adds to their workload, and, sometimes, disrupts patient care. This practice frustrated the nurses and contributed to burnout.Sometimes, we come to work, and there are no consumables. Patients are waiting, and we have to run to the stores to get what we need. It’s very stressful. (Nurse 2)
We used to have shortages almost every week. It’s better now, but we still have to go to the stores frequently to get what we need. (Nurse 6)

Delays in the procurement of necessary supplies were also a source of stress that was highlighted by most participants.The procurement process [of consumables and equipment] is very slow. We often run out of supplies, and it takes a long time to get more. (Nurse 8)
Sometimes, we have to wait for days for the consumables to arrive. In the meantime, we have to manage with what we have, which is not enough. (Nurse 4)

The high patient numbers overstretched the few dialysis machines of the machines causing them to break down frequently. This situation led to a pileup of patients requiring dialysis services, thus increasing the turnaround time. The workload on nurses has increased as a result.Currently, one of our machines is not working. If I come in the morning and I am to do five patients, because one machine is down, it means I can only do four cases. The extra patients have to wait for the next session. (Nurse 1)
We have five machines, but only four are working. When one breaks down, it puts a lot of pressure on us because we have to manage more patients with fewer machines. (Nurse 5)

In such situations of non-functioning equipment, the nurses are often left frustrated and stressed.So, the patient is in, I’m coming to check blood pressure or any other vital signs, only for you to start and it’s giving you Error!, Error!, Error!. Because you don’t have sufficient monitors, it means you have to wait for the patient to go inside before you can do this. So, it all affects the plan of care for the patient. And, in the long run, it is you, that the stress comes back to. (Nurse 1).

The expertise required to fix the broken-down machines is not readily available in the dialysis unit. The lack of skilled technicians to quickly repair the machines thus worsened the backlog of cases awaiting to undergo dialysis. Nurses described having to wait for repairs to be done, which delayed patient care.When the machines break down, we have to call the technician, and, sometimes, it takes hours for them to come. In the meantime, we have to manage the patients with the remaining machines, which is very stressful. (Nurse 3)
We have only one technician who knows how to repair the machines. If he is not available, we have to wait, and that delays everything. (Nurse 7)

Some participants explained that frequent equipment breakdowns prolonged wait times by the patients. This situation extended the nurses’ shifts by several hours, creating a chaotic work environment and increasing fatigue.If machines break down, patients wait… you close at 11 p.m. instead of 8 p.m. (Nurse 1)
When the machines break down, they cause you to overstay at work. (Nurse 10).

Participants mentioned that physical design of the unit was too small with a poorly designed workspace to accommodate the number of patients and staff, leading to chaos during emergencies. This situation contributed to burnout among the nurses.The space is not enough. We are like in a cubicle, with machines, beds, and consumables all crammed together. It’s chaotic, especially during emergencies. (Nurse 1)
The unit is too small. We don’t have enough space to move around, and it’s very stressful. (Nurse 5)
When you look at the unit, it is not spacious at all. We are, therefore, congested and squeezed into this small space (Nurse 10).

The lack of adequate facilities and well-laid-out workspace, such as rest areas and changing rooms, added to the physical strain experienced by the nurses. Nurses reported that the small, jam-packed space made their work more difficult and stressful.We don’t have a proper place to rest or change. It’s very uncomfortable, and it adds to the stress. (Nurse 7)
The unit is not well-designed for dialysis. It’s just a small room that was converted, and it’s not suitable for the work we do. (Nurse 8)

Participants also discussed how delays in patient referrals from other units, such as the emergency ward, further contribute to delays in care. These delays often result in extended working hours for nurses and increased stress levels.When a patient is on the ward, then we call for the patient. Most of the time, the ward people delay before bringing the case. So, when that happens, it extends our day, our number of hours that we are supposed to wait, it extends it into the night. (Nurse 4)
We call and tell them to bring the patient at 3:30 pm, but the patient is not brought until 4:30 or 5:00 pm. This pushes our closing time further, and we end up working late into the night. (Nurse 3)

## Emotional strain
The emotional burden of caring for critically ill patients, which often times results and dealing with deaths was a significant source of stress for the nurses. Participants expressed the emotional toll of losing patients, especially those they had cared for over a long period.It’s not easy to see patients you’ve been caring for pass away. It affects you emotionally, especially when you’ve built a relationship with them. (Nurse 3)
Sometimes, you come to work, and you hear that a patient you saw yesterday has passed away. It’s very hard to deal with. (Nurse 6)

ESRD is a debilitating condition that leaves patients seriously ill and at increased risk of death. The constant exposure to critically ill and dying patients led to compassion fatigue among the nurses.You see patients suffering every day, and it takes a toll on you emotionally. You start to feel numb after a while. (Participant 4)
It’s hard to keep giving emotionally when you’re so tired. You start to feel like you have nothing left to give. (Nurse 8)

The dialysis unit requires teamwork to reduce the high level of stress associated with the unit. Increased stress from colleague workers can therefore increase the burden of burnout among nurses. Some participants mentioned that the lack of cooperation and conflicts among staff added to their stress.Sometimes you have colleagues who are not cooperative. They don’t share information, and it creates tension in the unit. (Nurse 1)
When there is no teamwork, it makes the work even harder. We need to support each other, but, sometimes, that doesn’t happen. (Nurse 5)

Participants felt that they were not adequately recognized or rewarded for their hard work, which affected their morale.There is no motivation from management. We work long hours, but there is no appreciation. It’s demoralizing. (Nurse 2)
We don’t get any incentives for the extra work we do. It would help if management recognized our efforts. (Nurse 7)

## Impact of burnout
The impacts of burnout on the nurses were multifaceted. They encompassed physical, psychological, social, and professional dimensions. The far-reaching consequences of burnout experienced by the nurses extended from the work environment. It intrudes in personal lives, affecting their professional performance, and the quality of patient care they render to clients.

## Physical impact
Nurses in the dialysis unit reported significant physical strain due to prolonged standing, repetitive tasks, and inadequate workspace. This situation contributed to chronic pain, exhaustion, and workplace injuries among the nurses. Participants described persistent pain in their backs, waists, and joints due to long hours and physical demands.Physically, right now, I’m having back pain, and waist pain. My feet are paining; I can’t even stand for long. When I sleep, my legs are paining. So, physically, yeah, that is how it [burnout] has affected me. (Nurse 6)
The back pains,…, in fact, it got to a time I barely can bend myself. (Nurse 8)

Additionally, participants shared the physical and emotional toll of exhaustion that they have experienced. They mentioned struggling to wake up for work due to accumulated fatigue from previous shifts.Sometimes in the morning, when we are waking up to come to work, it’s difficult. You know that you have to get here early to start work, but because of the way you have been exhausted, maybe the previous days and other things, you realize one morning you start waking up and you are so tired that you can’t even. (Nurse 1)
So, if the situation happens that way, it makes you exhausted. You will be exhausted because you are doing extra beyond your normal limits without receiving any input as such. (Nurse 7)

Some participants shared that the demands of the work at the dialysis unit often led them to neglect their basic needs, including food. They described getting caught up in patient care and emergencies, sometimes forgetting to eat, a development which took a toll on their physical health and well-being.Sometimes, you come to duty, and you have even forgotten that you have to eat. Because patients are coming in, emergencies and all. That’s why you don’t realize [that], even the food you brought, you have not eaten it. And you know the effect of starving yourself. So, as for the physical aspect, it’s there. (Nurse 1)
And looking at our situation, all the time, we can’t even close at 11 pm, 12 a.m. We go home, we are hungry. You can’t even eat at that time. You have to sleep and come back the next day. (Nurse 7)

Some participants highlighted instances of accidents resulting from the ergonomics of poorly designed workplace and cramped workspace at the dialysis unit.
Some nurses pointed out that the cramped and poorly designed workspace in the dialysis unit often led to accidents and physical injuries. The limited space required them to exert additional effort to move equipment and create room for patient care, which increased their fatigue and risk of injury. According to the nurses, incidents such as hitting their legs against beds or equipment due to congestion and poor layout of the place were common occurrences.So, me hitting on the spacing issue, it’s actually just a one cubicle unit and we don’t have much space, so bringing beds in and out (are) things that if space were there, could have been easy. It makes us put in more energy in trying to create space. You end up in an emergency, hitting your leg, your body parts on other surfaces, like at the end, you feel so tired because of the limited space. (Nurse 3)
One time, because of the inadequate space, I hit my knee on one of the beds. You get it?. (Nurse 1)

## Psychological and emotional impact
Participants described the psychological and emotional impacts of burnout, which affected their mental health, job satisfaction, and overall well-being. The nurses described that they experienced emotional drain from witnessing patients’ sufferings and unmet expectations. This situation led to compassion fatigue and emotional detachment among the nurses.Emotions! When we experience a loss [of a patient]; we go through a lot, especially unexpected loss. Somebody who is very okay doing the dialysis, who is very stable, you’re not even thinking that this person can expire. Unexpected death! It affects almost all of us. (Nurse 6)
You come to work, you will just be reflecting on everything that happened… God!, this is not easy. (Nurse 2)

Participants expressed that when they experience equipment breakdown, they become emotionally strained trying to figure out what to do with the patients or what could happen to the patients.We plan our shifts to reduce stress…, but sometimes patients come for dialysis, and you realise that only three machines are working…. You are also thinking of the patient’s health, whether they will survive the night; this places a whole of psychological burden on you. (Nurse 10).

However, participants expressed that the hospital provides little very support during such emotionally draining and traumatising situations. The lack of support meant that the nurses had to handle the burden on their own.The hospital is doing nothing. Everything is on you, the staff. You come and suffer. (Nurse 1)
If I’m sacrificing my best, and there’s no appreciation. it doesn’t motivate me to do more. (Nurse 5)

Lack of recognition and support eroded job satisfaction among the nurses in the dialysis unit. The participants felt frustrated and unappreciated because of the absence of motivation from management.Lack of motivation from management. you’re doing your best, but no one sees it. (Nurse 2)
You don’t get any motivation. God will bless you. That has been the nature of this work. (Nurse 9)

## Social impact
This subtheme explored how burnout disrupted the social lives of nurses at the dialysis unit. According to the participants, burnout from work affected the personal lives of nurses, limiting social interactions and straining their relationships.
Participants shared that the long hours at work left them with little time for family or leisure. They indicated that their social lives were severely limited due to their demanding work schedules.I think my social life is gone, because you come in the morning, even the weekends that your friends are going for weddings and funerals and things, you are here. (Nurse 1)
The last time I saw my boyfriend was in June [about six months ago]. Because two days off; even those two days off, you don’t know when they will tell you, ‘can you please come and cover for this person?’ You sit in a car[travelling] and they will call you to come back. (Nurse 8)

Some participants revealed the emotional toll of not making time for their families and relations because of the demands of their work. They explained that they struggled with being away from their families and missing out on social events. The burnout left them exhausted, with little time for rest or socialization leading to isolation.Your family, your children are at home… you’ll be here. It’s not easy. (Nurse 6)
I can’t remember the last time I’ve gone to any social event. The little time that I used to have I use it to sleep and rest. So, I don’t, I have become someone who doesn’t socialize even though that wasn’t the kind of person I am. But since the burnout is too much. (Nurse 4)

## Impact on patient care
Some participants expressed that burnout compromised care quality and patient safety as it led to errors and delays. They shared that exhaustion left them feeling physically drained and unable to perform their nursing duties optimally. The situation made it difficult for the nurse to stay alert, increasing the likelihood of mistakes and affecting patient care.Because if you are stressed, I’m not sure you’ll be able to deliver [quality care]. You’ve come to work very tired, feeling sleepy, your body is paining you. You can’t perform very well, you cannot perform. So you might end up not giving the best that you should give to your patients. (Nurse 2)
You are not matched… you cannot perform. You might end up making mistakes. (Nurse 7)

As expressed by a participant, the issue of fatigue can be detrimental as it raises concerns about oversight. The participant shared the impact of burnout on missed nursing care and compromised patient care in the following quote:I put a patient on the machine and I didn’t give heparin. By the time they noticed, the patient had clotted. (Nurse 8)

Participants expressed that physical and emotional exhaustion often prevented them from delivering the best possible care to their patients. One participant mentioned that the long hours and lack of rest make it difficult to maintain the same level of care throughout the day.When you are tired, you can’t give your best. You are physically and emotionally drained, and it affects how you interact with patients and the care you provide. (Nurse 5)

Other participants highlighted that burnout from high workload and exhaustion increased the risk of errors, such as miscalculations in medication administration, which can have serious consequences for patients.If somebody is overwhelmed and stressed, it can easily lead you to make some errors. (Nurse 7)
When you are overwhelmed, you might forget to give a patient their medication on time or miscalculate the dosage. It’s a risk we face every day. (Nurse 6)

Participants reported that patients often express frustration with the long waiting times and the reduction in the duration of their dialysis sessions, which are necessary due to the high patient load.Patients complain about the reduced time for dialysis. They feel it’s not enough, but we have no choice because we have to accommodate everyone. (Nurse 8)
We used to do four hours for the patient. You know this dialysis, the lower [shorter] the time you give to the patient the higher the risk; and then, the greater the time, I think the likelihood that you may help the patients to recover easily …Currently we are doing three and a half hours instead of the four because of the number of patients that we were having. (Nurse 9)

Again, the patients sometimes feel neglected because the nurses are overworked and unable to give them the attention they need. This can lead to dissatisfaction and a sense of being uncared for.Patients feel neglected because we are always rushing. They want more attention, but we are too busy to give it to them. (Nurse 7)

## Coping with burnout
This theme explored the coping strategies that are adopted by participants to adapt to the burnout they experience while working at the dialysis unit. The subthemes derived from the data analysis include physical and emotional self-care; social and spiritual support; and workplace adjustments and support.

## Physical and emotional self-care
The physical and emotional strategies adopted by the nurses to cope with burnout include physical rest, emotional self-care, and self-motivation.
The nurses adopted both psychological and physical approaches to managing their well-being. They emphasized mindfulness and movement, such as taking strolls to clear the mind.What I do is, you know, practicing mindfulness and movements. Those are my two aims. So, if there are sometimes when I go home and I feel like I am bored, like thinking about all these, especially the psychological aspect, I just take a stroll around my area, to, you know, do away with some of the things going through my mind (Nurse 1).

Some participants emphasized the importance of self-motivation and emotional fulfillment in their work. They highlighted that intrinsic motivation was the drive that spurs them on. Equally, they expressed the desire to make a difference in the lives of the client they serve.The work, you’ve decided to do. So, that alone should be enough. And the desire to save life. It’s kind of have some emotional toll on you to do the right thing. (Nurse 7)
Psychologically, well, actually that aspect I think we are coping it on ourselves. (Nurse 3)

For some participants, they gained satisfaction from seeing the patients’ conditions improve. The fulfilment gained served as a motivation and possible safeguard against burnout among these nurses.By seeing our patients who used to be bedridden and then now walking, …we are actually doing something good. Yeah! (Nurse 3)

While some nurses used the services of physiotherapists, others relied on self-medication and other therapies, including back massages, to relieve discomfort and continue working effectively.We resort to taking analgesics or over-the-counter medications to relieve our pain. For instance, I said my legs. I have some pain in my legs, so I have to take some analgesics before coming to work so that I’ll be able to work today. (Nurse 2)
Physically, I’m already having waist and back pains. Last month, I was with the physio department. I told them I’m coming to book for weekly back massages. Because it’s bad. (Nurse 8)

## Social and spiritual support
Social and spiritual coping mechanisms adopted to manage the impact of burnout involve seeking support from social networks, family, and spiritual practices. Participants discussed making use of their off-duty time to engage in social activities such as visiting friends, going to the gym, or taking walks.Socially, we cope by making sure we make good use of our off times. When you are off duty, you might use that opportunity to visit your friends, go to the gym, take walks on the street, or visit your loved ones or they will come to you, and you chat. That’s what is about the offs. (Nurse 2)
So you try to find time to mingle with your friends or family or anything. (Nurse 6)

Participants highlighted the role of religious and recreational activities in maintaining their well-being and dealing with the impact of burnout. They mentioned attending church programmes to socialize, listening to sermons and music, and watching favorite movies as ways to relax and stay motivated.During your off days or when there is a church programme, you can go there just to socialize with people. (Nurse 2)
You get time to go to church, listen to the Word of God, listen to some music, watch your favorite movies. Those things keep us going. (Nurse 7)

## Workplace adjustments
Additional coping mechanisms adopted by the participants involved job resources including adjustments in the workplace, such as changes in work schedules, hospital support, and teamwork.
Participants acknowledged the hospital’s efforts in providing transportation support for staff working late shifts. They noted that arrangements with the transport unit have helped alleviate concerns about getting home safely after work, reducing stress and providing a sense of security.They [management] spoke with the transport unit to find a way to convey us back when we close late. To actually relieve our mind on ‘I’m closing late, how do I get home?’ That aspect I think is being solved. (Nurse 1)
We also lobbied for the hospital’s transport so that when we close late into the night, you know, instead of you being worried as to where you get a car, the car will just take you home safely. (Nurse 7)

A flexible shift system adopted at the dialysis unit reduced working hours and improved efficiency, thus allowing the nurses to attend to more patients effectively.The shift system is fine. It isn’t like today I’m for afternoon[shift], tomorrow I’m coming for morning [shift]. Because this place is not like the ward. We don’t close at the stipulated time. You can close at any time. (Nurse 6)
We reduced the time [for dialysis]. We’ve reduced the time and because of that, I mean, we’re able to do for quite many patients. (Nurse 8)

Furthermore, the participants emphasized the importance of peer support and teamwork in managing their workload and reducing burnout. They acknowledged that working together ensures efficiency and prevents undue burden on any individual.The staff here, we all know our situation, we know our number of machines, we know our patient level, so none is ready to like to make other staff bear all the weight, everyone tries to work hand in hand, so that at the end we have an effective workout. (Nurse 3)
We help each other to move. And that often makes us feel very supported. (Nurse 7)
We also make sure the wards bring their patients ahead of time to avoid delays (Nurse 10).

## Discussion
We explored the phenomenon of burnout among nurses at the dialysis unit in a teaching hospital in Ghana, using the job demands-resources (JD-R) model as a theoretical framework. Specifically, the causes and impact of burnout, and the coping mechanisms adopted by nurses to mitigate the impact of burnout were explored in this study. Nurses working in the dialysis unit encountered significant burnout caused by a myriad of job demands including increased workload and the physical demands of dialysis care. Others include shortage of essential material resources for dialysis care, emotional strain, as well as exhaustion from caring for ailing patients. The nurses reported chronic exhaustion from increased workload, extended shifts, stretching beyond midnight, and minimal recovery time before returning to work the following day. This situation created an unsustainable cycle of physical and emotional strain. Increased job demands contribute to significant stress among workers [ ]. Similar trends have been observed by previous studies in sub-Saharan Africa, including Ghana and Libya, and in China, highlighting how nurses in dialysis units face long shifts, excessive patient loads, and deteriorating job performance [ , , ]. Excessive workload and emotional strain are well-documented drivers of burnout among nurses, yet their impact varies significantly across different healthcare settings depending on staffing levels, institutional support, and regional healthcare infrastructure [ – ]. The current study setting, like other public health facilities in Ghana, typically operates under significant job resource constraints, making it difficult to implement staff rotation systems, redistribute workload, or provide adequate rest periods. Moreover, the limited number of dialysis centres within the country exacerbates the strain on nurses, as all referrals and critical cases are directed to the few available facilities, increasing the job demands beyond manageable limits.
Limited resources and frequent equipment failures increased stress and contributed to burnout among nurses in the dialysis unit. Also, the intricate operation of complex, life-sustaining technology increases the cognitive and technical demands on nurses. In resource-constrained settings, these inherent stressors are severely compounded by systemic deficiencies, intensifying the risk and severity of burnout among nurses [ , ]. For example, persistent shortages of essential dialysis consumables such as dialyzers, bloodlines, and sterile solutions frequently disrupt treatment schedules and force nurses to either ration supplies or delay patient care, adding to the nurses’ frustration and stress. Additionally, frequent breakdowns of dialysis machines caused nurses to manage an already overwhelming patient load with fewer functioning machines. This contributed to prolonged treatment sessions and extended working hours. Similar job demands were found in previous research with dialysis nurses in Ghana and Rwanda [ , ]. Health facilities in Ghana struggle with systemic supply chain inefficiencies and financial constraints, making it difficult to maintain a steady stock of consumables or repair malfunctioning machines promptly [ ].
Again, the nurses reported experiencing deep emotional fatigue due to witnessing the suffering and gradual decline of patients with ESRD. However, these nurses often lacked sufficient job resources like psychological support to help them cope. This is consistent with findings by previous studies [ , , ]. The lack of job resources in the study setting, including structured support systems and resources, leaves nurses to cope with grief, stress, and trauma on their own. Nurses thus resorted to emotional detachment as a coping mechanism, leading to reduced job satisfaction, diminished empathy, and a higher likelihood of professional burnout. Studies have documented that the availability of job resources such as structured debriefing sessions, peer mentorship programmes, and counselling services can help ameliorate burnout among dialysis nurses [ , , ].
It emerged that burnout had severe repercussions on nurses working in the dialysis unit, with many reporting chronic fatigue, body pain, and general body weakness due to prolonged standing, repetitive tasks, and physically demanding patient care responsibilities. Some nurses struggled with exhaustion to the extent that waking up for work became difficult, affecting their ability to function optimally. Others suffered workplace-related injuries due to the congested work environment. Similar findings have been reported in Sweden and Denmark, Australia and New Zealand, where dialysis nurses experienced severe physical exhaustion, musculoskeletal pain, and workplace injuries due to heavy workload and prolonged shifts [ , , ]. In a similar study in Ghana, Boateng and his colleagues found the emotional burden of burnout among dialysis nurses to be profound, as many nurses reported feeling unmotivated and disconnected from their work [ ]. The increased job demands led some nurses to experience persistent anxiety, mood disturbances, and feelings of helplessness, further exacerbating their stress levels and contributing to professional dissatisfaction. The current findings are consistent with previous studies among nurses working in the dialysis and other high-dependent units [ , ].
Some of the nurses indicated that they lacked the motivation to participate in professional networking, training, or career advancement programmes, further isolating them from their peers and limiting their opportunities for professional growth. This aligns with previous studies, where burnout among nurses led to reduced engagement in workplace collaborations and professional development activities, ultimately affecting job satisfaction and career progression [ , ]. This is an indication that burnout among dialysis nurses is a widespread phenomenon that has a significant social impact on nurses.
The impact of burnout among dialysis nurses extended beyond individual well-being to compromise patient care. The nurses reported that exhaustion, stress, and understaffing frequently led to delayed treatments, reduced attentiveness, and increased patient safety incidents, thus raising serious concerns about patient safety during dialysis. One of the most alarming effects reported was the shortening of dialysis sessions due to nurse fatigue and workforce shortages. These findings are consistent with previous research in which burnout has been shown to contribute to increased patient safety incidents and declining care quality [ , ]. Shortening dialysis sessions has been linked to fluid overload, electrolyte imbalances, and cardiovascular complications in patients [ , ].
To cope with the stress and exhaustion from burnout, the nurses employed both personal and job resources in this regard. For the personal resources to manage burnout, the nurses engaged in both physical and psychological self-care activities. These coping strategies were at both the personal and social levels. At the personal level, the nurses engaged in mindfulness techniques, such as deep breathing, relaxation exercises, and taking short walks, to maintain mental well-being. At the social level, the nurses drew strength from their social networks to help them cope with burnout. Similar findings have been reported by studies in Rwanda [ , ]. Additionally, some nurses reported resorting to medical interventions such as physiotherapy sessions and analgesics to relieve musculoskeletal pain caused by prolonged standing, lifting, and handling dialysis patients. Medical interventions to manage musculoskeletal discomforts are common among nurses who work with obsolete equipment, particularly in LMICs [ , ]. Other personal resources, including social and spiritual support, also played a vital role in helping nurses manage burnout. Again, many nurses reportedly sought emotional support from friends and family, while others turned to religious practices, such as attending church services, listening to gospel music, or engaging in personal prayer to find emotional relief. These findings align with findings from similar settings, where dialysis nurses identified peer support, family interactions, and religious coping as essential in managing workplace stress [ ]. Findings of other studies, albeit from high-income countries, suggest that job resources in the form of structured workplace interventions, such as peer-support networks, debriefing sessions, and professional counselling services rather than social or spiritual support, are more effective in reducing burnout [ , ].
Workplace-based strategies such as emotional support from colleagues, teamwork, shift flexibility, and informal lobbying for better working conditions emerged as job resources that helped nurses cope with the job demands of the dialysis unit. Nurses reported that team collaboration helped distribute workload pressure, ensuring that no single nurse was overwhelmed. Additionally, some participants mentioned that lobbying efforts had led to minor workplace improvements, such as securing transport arrangements for late-night shifts. Workplace support, including shift flexibility and a strong teamwork culture, are effective job resources for burnout mitigation [ , ]. However, the job resources available to nurses to help them cope with burnout seemed disproportionate to the job demands at the dialysis unit. The nurses thus, employed more personal resources as coping strategies.

## Implications
The findings of the study have implications for quality nursing care, the well-being of nurses, turnover intention, and patient satisfaction with dialysis care. The high job demands experienced by the nurses with very few job resources as safeguards, contribute to a higher prevalence of burnout. While workload remains a universal challenge in dialysis nursing, the findings of the current study also demonstrate that systemic inefficiencies can amplify the effects of burnout, making it an almost unavoidable consequence. The experience of compassion fatigue, emotional detachment, and a reduced sense of personal accomplishment emanating from caring for ailing patients, makes it increasingly difficult for nurses to maintain empathy and motivation in their roles. The inability of the nurses to engage in social and career-building activities not only weaken their support networks but also diminishes their overall well-being. This could result in dissatisfaction and increased turnover intention. Nurse burnout negatively affects the quality of care, leading to the increased likelihood of hospital readmissions, prolonged recovery periods, and even mortality among dialysis patients.

## Limitations
The study contributes to the growing body of research on burnout among nurses in dialysis units, particularly in resource-limited settings. This is a single-site qualitative study conducted in the dialysis unit of a teaching hospital. Therefore, these findings may be unique to this context and not applicable elsewhere. Secondly, relying on self-reported experiences from participants through semi-structured interviews might have introduced biases, as their responses could be shaped by personal perceptions and individual experiences. Nonetheless, this study fills an important gap in exploring the perspectives of nurses engaged in dialysis care in a resource constrained setting.

## Conclusion
Burnout among nurses in the dialysis unit negatively impacts the physical and social well-being of nurses. This, in turn, threatens the quality of nursing care for dialysis patients. Chronic exhaustion and emotional detachment among dialysis nurses reduce their ability to provide high-quality care, leading to medical errors, miscommunication, and compromised patient safety. Increased burnout and its impact on nurses working in the dialysis unit could aggravate turnover rates among nurses, thereby worsening the already critical nurse-to-patient ratio. Addressing the causes of burnout among nurses in the dialysis units will improve the well-being of nurses and, eventually, improve patient outcomes.
Addressing the impact of burnout among nurses in the dialysis unit requires policy reforms in staffing, resource allocation, and mental health support to create a healthier work environment. Policymakers should prioritise the training of more nephrology nurses to ease the workload of nurses in the dialysis units. Hospital management should prioritise job resources, including the establishment of mental health support units to provide counselling and emotional support within healthcare facilities to assist staff in managing burnout. The hospital management should also ensure an adequate supply of dialysis consumables and well-maintained equipment to prevent stress brought on by perennial shortages of these essential commodities. It is further recommended that future studies should focus on assessing the long-term impacts of burnout in dialysis units on staff retention and patient outcomes. Future studies comparing burnout levels and impact in dialysis units across multiple healthcare facilities could provide a more holistic picture of the phenomenon in the country.
