## HIGHLIGHTS
•Military bases offer a variety of food venues, which vary in food options and quality.
•The Military Nutrition Environment Assessment Tool evaluates the local food landscape.
•The average Military Nutrition Environment Assessment Tool score is 52%, with a wide range among food venue types.
•Leadership support is a key facilitator of food environment improvements.

## Introduction
Americans have increased their intake of food away from home, which is lower in quality and higher in calories than food prepared at home. The increase of operations that serve food also impacts the military nutrition environment—including all foods, beverages, and dietary supplements available to the military community—and its role in nutritional fitness.

## Methods
As part of a pilot study, 5 military installations used the online Military Nutrition Environment Assessment Tool to evaluate their local food landscape. Each site then developed an intervention to address a specific venue (e.g., dining facility, express) and a key category (food policy, food availability, or behavioral design) through a targeted intervention.

## Results
Site teams conducted assessments on a total of 103 venues across 9 venue types. Scores varied widely among and within installations. The overall average Military Nutrition Environment Assessment Tool score was 52%, with individual installation scores ranging from 39% to 69%. The most commonly targeted facility for intervention was the dining facility. In-person feedback sessions revealed that leadership awareness and support are key facilitators to improving the local food environment. Most users agreed or strongly agreed that the Military Nutrition Environment Assessment Tool application was easy to navigate.

## Conclusions
Similar to trends in the general public, the military community has more ways to purchase food away from home, so improving food-away-from-home quality in military settings can have a major impact. A review of local military food environments showed areas of success and opportunities for improvement. Given the prevalence of food away from home, providing and promoting nutritious options can help optimize Service Member nutritional fitness.

## INTRODUCTION
The percentage of U.S. household food budgets spent on food away from home (FAFH) has increased over the past 30 years and, in 2010, surpassed money spent on food from home for the first time. The amount and types of venues that serve prepared foods (e.g., grocery stores, quick-service restaurants) also have grown. With this development, Americans’ average total daily energy intake of FAFH increased from 17% in 1977–1978 to 34% in 2011–2012. The nutritional quality of FAFH across all income levels and types (except school food) is lower in quality and higher in calories than that of food prepared at home. This is especially concerning because the WHO identifies unhealthy diets as one of the leading risks for the global burden of disease, specifically cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and cancer. In addition, 41.9% of Americans have obesity, to which unhealthy eating patterns are known as a contributor. Identified barriers to choosing nutritious foods include limited availability, proximity, resources (time, finances), knowledge, culinary skills, and willpower, along with taste preferences. , , 
Offering affordable, accessible, nutrient-dense foods at a variety of venues provides more opportunities for the public to make choices that are beneficial to their health and wellness. Thus, efforts to create a supportive food environment are a vital part of public health work. Currently, strategies are in place to build a health-focused food environment for general, federal, and military American communities. , , One of the pillars outlined in the 2022 White House National Strategy on Hunger, Nutrition, and Health—“Empower All Consumers to Make and Have Access to Healthy Choices”—highlights this concern. This strategy document further outlines action steps, including “Expand access to healthier environments in federal facilities,” specifically “expanding its [Department of Defense] Go 4 Green program (a joint-service performance-nutrition initiative that improves [food choices] where military service members live and work).” 
The military nutrition environment (MNE) consists of all foods, beverages, and dietary supplements available within military settings. A supportive MNE improves access, availability, and awareness of nutritious choices—essential aspects of nutritional fitness. Nutritional fitness is defined for the military as “the provision and consumption of food in quantities, quality, and proportions sufficient to preserve mission performance and to protect against disease and injury.” Optimal nutritional fitness is critical to service members’ physical and mental performance and their ability to perform operational duties. In addition, it establishes a healthy foundation, setting service members on the right path throughout their military careers and beyond. However, military personnel's eating patterns are suboptimal, with insufficient intake of nutrient-rich foods (fruits, vegetables, and whole grains) and excessive intake of less-nutritious foods (refined grains and saturated fats). , These patterns may be exacerbated by MNEs that do not favor desirable eating behaviors. , Food environments that negatively impact health include not only food deserts, areas with limited access and availability of affordable nutritious foods, but also food swamps, areas with a high density of establishments selling high-calorie fast food and junk food. Research suggests that both types of food environments have an impact on obesity risk, with food swamps potentially playing a larger role. Exploration of the MNE could help to identify where food deserts and food swamps exist and to what degree. This highlights the potentially positive impact of creating awareness about the current MNE landscape and developing community-level strategies to promote nutritional fitness.
Despite the relationship of a supportive MNE to nutritional fitness and overall mission readiness, objective data to assess the Department of Defense (DoD)’s MNE are limited. In 2011, a joint-services MNE working group developed the first standardized Military Nutrition Environment Assessment Tool (mNEAT) to assess the unique military food environment. The first version was adapted for military use from the Michigan Nutrition Environment Assessment Tool and the Nutrition Environment Measures Survey. , Over the years, the tool has been refined on the basis of DoD pilot projects (e.g., Healthy Base Initiative, Healthy Army Communities) along with input from operators of food-service venues and MNE working-group members.
The MNE working group decided to modify content and transition mNEAT from a spreadsheet-based platform to a web-based application (app). In 2021, the Consortium for Health and Military Performance at the Uniformed Services University received funding to develop the mNEAT app to standardize data collection, conduct analyses of food-service-venue assessments, and track the military food landscape over time. Transition to an app addressed previous limitations to provide a more sustainable system to assess and improve MNE. The simpler and faster platform, process, data repository, and reporting features aimed to encourage participation among and across military branches and installations. The app was launched in October 2021. The current app version of mNEAT is designed to evaluate 9 types of food-service venues across 4 key categories.
To address gaps in MNE information, this article presents the use of the mNEAT app in a pilot study with the following objectives:1.Describe the current MNE landscape across food venues using the web-based mNEAT application, including users’ app experience.
2.Describe the factors influencing the development and implementation of the mNEAT intervention.

## METHODS

## Study Sample
Researchers with expertise in the MNE, specifically the mNEAT app, and participants in the DoD's MNE work group designed and conducted a mixed-methods program evaluation pilot study. These researchers worked closely with study sites between January and August 2022 to test the new mNEAT app at 5 military sites representing 4 military branches and 1 joint-service installation. The Uniformed Services University IRB approved the study (DBS.2020.182), and each site's leadership signed a letter of support to participate. Each site built a team with 2–5 members, mostly health and wellness professionals, including at least 1 registered dietitian on each team.

## Measures
Researchers gathered data over approximately 6 months to address study objectives. Between January and March 2022, each site team conducted a baseline mNEAT assessment at their local installation. Researchers encouraged site teams to assess at least 1 venue of each type ( ). The table indicates which of the 4 key categories can be evaluated in each venue type; the key categories include the following:•Food policy: formal written statement or philosophy that encourages nutritious eating;
•Food availability: number and variety of available healthy (as defined in mNEAT) foods and beverages;
•Behavioral design: strategies to create an environment where nutritious choices are the standard, including the following:○Choice architecture: accessibility and placement of food and beverages to entice diners toward more nutritious choices;
○Economics: price strategies and incentives that increase the likelihood of purchasing nutritious food and beverages; and
○Health messaging: promotion, advertisement, and education to encourage healthy choices; and

•Community involvement: existence of partnerships to promote healthy MNE.
 
To obtain quantitative data, teams completed mNEAT assessments across a variety of venues. The built-in flexibility of the app allowed teams to tailor their evaluation approach according to installation resources, time, and support. Teams were encouraged to take photos and document observations while conducting the assessments. After completing the baseline mNEAT assessment, each site identified one area to improve their local MNE.
Site teams then developed an intervention to address a specific venue (e.g., dining facility, express) and a key category (food policy, food availability, or behavioral design) through a targeted intervention. When feasible, teams executed the intervention within the study time period with researchers’ assistance.
To communicate progress and provide support, researchers met with site teams monthly through virtual meetings. Site teams were also asked to keep monthly tracking logs. Researchers provided email assistance between meetings as needed. In June 2022, the researchers conducted 1-day site visits to observe the local MNE along with any proposed or implemented action-plan intervention while on site. To obtain qualitative data, researchers led a feedback session at each site using a semistructured interview format; sessions were audio recorded with a primary and backup recorder. A total of 18 team members and 8 stakeholders (e.g., food-venue operators) explored factors that influenced their MNE intervention. Finally, given the tool's new platform, researchers assessed the mNEAT app user experience (specifically, the ease of navigation) through an electronic survey.

## Statistical Analysis
For quantitative analysis, data from the electronic survey were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 26 (Released 2019, IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). Descriptive statistics (e.g., measures of central tendency, cross-tabulations, and frequencies) were computed by installation, assessment type, and category. The mNEAT app delivers assessment scores as percentages, with the earned score divided by the maximum score possible. Scores include overall and key-category mNEAT scores for each individual venue (e.g., 1 dining facility), venue type (e.g., all dining facilities), and an entire installation or ship (average of all venue-type scores). A higher percentage indicates a more supportive food environment.
For qualitative analysis, each feedback session was transcribed verbatim and checked for accuracy. Using a qualitative descriptive approach, researchers (who were not involved in data collection) identified key themes and constructed a codebook with those themes. Qualitative description is an inductive approach to data analysis that identifies relevant themes as they emerge from the data. The codebook was then refined through an iterative process that involved testing codes against the data and developing agreed-upon code definitions. Once the codebook was finalized, all transcripts were cocoded using MAXQDA qualitative analysis software and adjudicated for consensus. Findings were compiled on the basis of emergent themes that came out of the coding process.

## RESULTS
The pilot study provided information on the current state of MNE at 5 participating military installations as well as the usability of the new mNEAT web-based app. Site teams were able to conduct assessments on 15–30 venues for a total of 103 venues across all 9 venue types (commissary; community; dining facility/galley; express; food trucks; Morale, Welfare, and Recreation; vending Levels 1 and 2; worksite) ( and ). 

## Study Aim 1: Describe the Current Military Nutrition Environment Landscape Using the Military Nutrition Environment Assessment Tool
The overall average mNEAT score was 52%, but scores varied widely within and among the 5 sites ( ). The commissary had the highest average score, followed by community, express, and then dining facility or galley. Fast food had the greatest number of completed assessments, although the commissary and community had the least, which is appropriate because there was only 1 commissary and 1 community per installation. The mean score was consistent among the 3 key categories (food policy, food availability, and behavioral design) at 46%–51%.

## Study Aim #2: Describe Factors Influencing Development and Implementation of Military Nutrition Environment Assessment Tool Interventions
Each site team chose 1 venue for an intervention to improve MNE: dining facilities (3 sites), micro markets (1 site), and express shops (1 site). The top reasons for selecting venues were that they were the biggest, had the most direct impact on service members, were easiest to implement (i.e., smallest changes, less pushback from stakeholders and customers), and had existing stakeholder relationships. Conversely, certain food-service venues were not selected because of perceived difficulty in implementing MNE changes due to contracts and profitability.
As for key categories, 2 sites targeted food availability, whereas 3 sites targeted behavioral design, particularly food placement (1 site) and marketing or education (2 sites). Specifically, common strategies for smaller venues included ways to increase the appeal of nutritious options, optimize menu-item placement, and target menu items on the basis of service member needs and interests. The least-targeted key category was food policy, because of views that certain policies were immutable or required significant time and buy-in from multiple stakeholders to enact change.
Both research and site teams noted limited feasibility for MNE interventions that require headquarters or corporate approval. In general, interventions within local control were more likely to be viewed as feasible. Several teams were able to implement an intervention during the study time period. Researchers provided supplies to support interventions, including equipment (infused water containers, sign holders, and poster frames) and customized print materials (posters).
The feedback sessions provided valuable insight into factors impacting the mNEAT assessment and development process, especially understanding of key facilitators. One key facilitator in conducting the mNEAT assessment and interventions was the support and buy-in from the leadership, along with the support of stakeholders. One site team member described the importance of this facilitator as “[stakeholders who had direct authority over the locations] had lots of great insight on what could be done, what couldn't be done…. And when we got them on board, it just really enhanced I think what our aims were and kind of the conversations that we had …what were going to be our barriers, but also the possibilities that we had that were beyond what we originally had discussed, I think was awesome. So, it just helps to have great, great stakeholders who are just willing to say, hey, that's great, but let's do this.” Each site team was supported by 1 or 2 leaders, either at the local installation or service branch headquarters level. In addition, leadership who assisted with mNEAT execution and adherence, collaborating with site teams to sustain interventions, and leverage existing MNE efforts helped enable success. Site teams reported that researchers also helped by providing support for the MNE process through accountability, sharing of best practices, technology troubleshooting, and provision of resources (e.g., printed materials and equipment). One site team member stated, “…the ability to ask and receive some assistance from your team has been tremendous, because we would not have gotten those frames, we would have not bought [them]. Based on funding that I don't think that would have been priority going up the chain.”
The feedback sessions also revealed the following barriers to success: lack of leadership support, insufficient awareness of mNEAT and its potential benefits (“…we do mNEAT every single year, [yet] we have no authority at all in which to change your behaviors”), limited resources to execute intervention, and the negative impact of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) on operations of food-service venues and supply chains affecting food and beverage availability. In addition, existing food-service–venue contracts or profitability were strong barriers to overcome, for example, “[the food venue] is a business, at the end of the day, as much as [the operators] would love to have all healthy food ..they still have to make money….that's probably always going to be the struggle,[...], if people aren't eating and going to support the bowling alley and the fast food restaurants here, then everyone loses money and it goes away.”
A total of 13 users across the 5 site teams accessed the mNEAT app during the study. Site teams’ experience with conducting the mNEAT assessment varied from none to several years of experience with previous mNEAT versions. No site teams had used the mNEAT web-based app previously, but most users found the app easy to navigate for assessments and scoring, an improvement from previous spreadsheet-based versions. Most users (77%) agreed or strongly agreed that it was easy to navigate the mNEAT app sections (assessment creation, assessment editing, assessment results, and overall score report) ( ). 
On the basis of researcher observations during site visits and regular meetings, regardless of a site team's mNEAT experience or evaluation approach, all teams were familiar with their local military community culture given their direct contact with service members and length of job tenure (≥10 years for some).

## DISCUSSION
Since 2011, DoD health and wellness personnel have conducted mNEAT assessments, but there is limited research on the current food landscape in military installations. Through a collaborative effort, researchers and site teams successfully provided baseline assessments of the food environment impacting an estimated 98,200 people, including 36,757 active-duty service members who live, work, and train at the 5 installations. This is also the first research study that evaluated the web-based mNEAT app.
The average overall score of 52% suggests that there are areas of success but also many opportunities for improvement. The high number of fast-food assessments, with one of the lowest mean scores by venue type (35%), indicates that these are more common on military installations than on other types of food venues. It is not surprising that the commissary is the venue type with the highest score owing to the variety of options and headquarters-level standards and wellness initiatives, such as the Dietitian Approved Thumb program. 
DoD lacks a policy that requires installations to conduct the mNEAT assessment. Policies and guidelines vary among service branches, and only the U.S. Air Force requires their installations to conduct an annual mNEAT assessment; the U.S. Space Force currently also follows Air Force policy. All 5 study sites received 0 points on their installation's worksite assessment, which assesses the availability of a healthy worksite policy or philosophy statement. This is another score that could be improved with the implementation of policy or philosophy to promote a culture of health. 
Dining facilities were the venue type targeted most for intervention, which is not surprising given that these military-funded facilities are already mandated to follow menu, nutrition education, and labeling policies. In addition, many registered dietitians and health promotion educators have an established relationship with dining-facility operators, making collaboration easier to offer nutritious foods and increase the number of customers.
In-person feedback sessions provided valuable insight into facilitators and barriers, a previous gap in knowledge. Lack of DoD-wide policy likely contributed to variable leadership awareness and support, ranging from active to passive. Leadership support often precedes prioritizing MNE improvement, which can be operationalized through policy, resource (time, personnel) allotment, and funding. In addition, stakeholder engagement among nonmilitary-funded venues (the majority of food-service venues on military installations) is essential to creating mutually beneficial partnerships. Similarly, a previous systematic review of existing methods and tools to assess implementation of government policy to promote healthy environments identified infrastructure support, resources, and stakeholder engagement as key barriers and facilitators. Site teams cited a limited ability to improve their local food environment because existing food-service contracts, guidelines, and standards are often determined at the headquarters level. The key categories assessed by mNEAT are consistent with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's strategies to help improve healthy food access, so it provides opportunities for collaboration and synchronization of resources, knowledge, and initiatives among public, federal, and military entities. 

## Limitations
This pilot study has several limitations. The study represented 4 U.S. service branches (Army, Air Force, Space Force, and Marine Corps) and 1 joint-service installation, but recruitment efforts failed to secure a site from the Navy. Other limitations include a small sample size (5 sites); only 3 months to implement an MNE intervention; and in-person sessions being the only method to collect site teams’ feedback, which limited feedback to those able to attend. Available funding limited study length and the ability to travel to all sites for baseline observations. Thus, site teams needed more detailed instructions for the mNEAT observation comments and venue photos, and owing to inconsistent data, researchers did not analyze some results. The next cohort will include slight modifications to address some of these limitations, including recruitment of Navy installations, a longer intervention period (6 months), additional methods (electronic surveys) to collect local feedback, and inclusion of a photographic observation record with detailed instructions to obtain usable photos.

## CONCLUSIONS
This pilot study describes the current MNE landscape across food venues at 5 U.S. military installations using the web-based mNEAT app and provides insight into the factors influencing the development and implementation of mNEAT intervention. Leveraging technology to centrally and systematically compile information about the MNE landscape can inform leaders, stakeholders, and advocates about the current successes and opportunities. These efforts contribute to the desire to create a culture of health in the military because the current MNE norms are not ideal for nutritional fitness. As of February 2024, a total of 442 individual users have completed 3,849 assessments in the app, with an average overall mNEAT score for nonstudy assessments identical to this pilot study's average overall mNEAT pilot score of 52%.
Leadership support is crucial to MNE improvement. Therefore, the military community would benefit from DoD leaders working at all levels to optimize MNE and promote nutritious FAFH offerings. Locally, food-service venues can address food availability by adding nutritious options to dining facilities and other venues where service members eat; improving access to nutritious foods by implementing choice architecture; and strengthening food policy by implementing or increasing adherence to policies, such as DoD's Go for Green program. In addition, subject-matter experts, such as registered dietitians and health promotion personnel, can educate leadership and service members on the importance of a supportive MNE for optimal nutritional fitness. At a DoD level, leaders can implement a department-wide policy to require an annual mNEAT assessment, dedicate funds and resources to support MNE-improvement interventions, and support initiatives to transform MNE, ultimately impacting nutritional fitness.
This study provides an opportunity to translate the knowledge gained through research into educational resources to support MNE improvement efforts at military installations across the world (https://www.hprc-online.org/nutrition/mne). The MNE group at Consortium for Health and Military Performance will continue efforts to develop and communicate more evidence-based practices to improve DoD's MNE.
