## Abstract

## Background
Neurologic research output is extensive, yet influential contributions remain unevenly distributed across countries and institutions. Prior studies have emphasized productivity rather than excellence, limiting understanding of where high-impact neurologic scholarship is concentrated and which structural conditions may support its development.

## Methods
Seven annual releases (2017–2023) of the Stanford-Elsevier Lists (SELs) were analyzed between July and August 2025, and Excellent Neurologic Scholars (ENS) were extracted from the career-long and single-year SEL datasets of each release. A multilevel ecological framework was used to examine national, institutional, and individual determinants of neurologic research excellence. The main outcome measures were ENS counts, composite score, modified H-index, and citation count excluding self-citations.

## Results
High-income countries accounted for 97.8% and 95.8% of ENS, respectively, with the USA alone contributing nearly half (50.1% and 47.1%). English-speaking countries collectively represented over two-thirds of ENS, while German ranked a distant second (∼9%). The top 20 institutions hosted 23.1% and 24.1% of ENS, with the University of California (4.5% and 4.4%) leading, underscoring marked institutional elitism. Gender disparities were evident: women comprised 18.3% of ENS in the career-long SEL and 22.4% in the single-year SEL, with males consistently achieving higher bibliometric indicators. Academic age emerged as the only stable predictor of excellence, with each additional year increasing citation counts (β = 135.1 and 2.4). Regression analyses confirmed independent effects of male gender, English language, income level, and HDI, while attenuating bivariate associations.

## Conclusion
Neurologic research excellence is disproportionately concentrated in high-income, anglophone settings and elite institutions, with persistent gender gaps. Academic age remained the strongest predictor, underscoring the need for more context-sensitive and equitable policies to support neurologic scholarship across diverse settings.

## Introduction
Neurologic disorders contribute substantially to global morbidity and mortality, yet the scholarly landscape that informs prevention, diagnosis, and care is unevenly distributed [ ]. Most prior bibliometric analyses in neurology and neurosurgery have focused on productivity, such as publication counts, rather than excellence as a marker of outstanding and field-normalized scholarly influence [ – ]. As a result, current evidence remains limited in explaining where the most influential neurologic scholarship is concentrated and which conditions may support its emergence. To address this gap, we used the Stanford-Elsevier Lists (SELs) of the top 2% scholars, which combine multiple citation indicators, exclude self-citations, and account for authorship role, to identify Excellent Neurologic Scholars (ENS) and characterize their distribution [ , ]. This reorientation from output volume to excellence allows the analysis to move beyond publication counts and to focus instead on where the most citation-visible and field-normalized scholarship is concentrated across countries, institutions, and individual careers [ ]. In this sense, this operationalization should be interpreted cautiously, as citation-based indicators reflect only one dimension of scholarly influence and do not fully capture educational, clinical, societal, or policy contributions. Accordingly, the present study treats excellence as a bibliometric construct suitable for comparative analysis rather than as an exhaustive judgment of research quality.
Structural features plausibly shape where excellence concentrates [ ]. At the national level, excellence may covary with health system capacity, human development, budgetary priorities, and gender equity; at the institutional level, prestige ecosystems reflected in global rankings can amplify visibility and resources; and at the linguistic level, the dominance of English can mediate access to audiences, citations, and indexing [ – ]. These forces coincide with well-described global asymmetries: high-income and anglophone settings produce a disproportionate share of influential scholarship; elite universities host large fractions of high-impact researchers; and non-English venues often cite anglophone sources to remain legible in global discourse [ ]. An excellence-focused approach therefore requires an explicitly ecological design that can situate individual careers within institutional and national contexts, moving beyond simple tallies to the determinants that sustain or impede high-impact scholarship [ ].
At the individual level, two considerations are salient: first, gender – women remain underrepresented among top-cited neurologic scholars and in senior authorship and faculty ranks, although patterns vary by subspecialty and venue [ ]; second, academic age – a proxy for career duration – tracks consistently and positively with citation-based excellence, reflecting life-course accumulation of outputs and roles; yet, it also risks conflating generational cohort effects and does not capture career interruptions [ ]. These observations, combined with evidence that departmental milieu and program size correlate with trainee output, motivate a multilevel analysis capable of disentangling national, institutional, and individual predictors of excellence. By assembling seven SEL releases (2017–2023) and applying rigorous field filters, gender inference thresholds, and institutional harmonization, we provide the first panoramic description of neurologic research excellence – rather than mere productivity – and its determinants.

## Study Objectives
Primary objectives were (1) to map the global distribution and density of ENS across countries and institutions using SEL (2017–2023) and (2) to quantify associations between excellence (citations, modified H-index, composite score [c-score]) and national (health, development, gender equity, budgets), institutional (global rankings), and individual (gender, academic age) determinants. Secondary objectives were (1) to examine gender differences after conditioning on academic age and context and (2) to describe authorship role patterns and age-related trajectories as correlates of excellence.

## Materials and Methods

## Study Design
A bibliometric analysis was undertaken, structured around an ecological model consisting of three independent levels of determinants of neurologic research excellence. The national level included health system attributes, gender equity, human development, budgetary policies, and disease burden. The institutional level was represented by general and field-specific university rankings. The individual level comprised gender and academic age. These domains are outlined in the conceptual model shown in . The design and reporting followed the Checklist for Bibliometric Reviews of the Biomedical Literature (BIBLIO) guidelines [ ].
 

## Data Sources
The primary data source for this analysis was the science-wide author databases of standardized citation indicators, commonly referred to as the SELs [ , ]. Seven successive annual releases spanning the years 2017 to 2023 were extracted and analyzed between July and August 2025, each comprising both the career-long and single-year datasets. All datasets were retrieved in full from the Elsevier Data Repository® [ ]. Secondary data sources were utilized to capture national- and institutional-level determinants, as follows:
Health system attributes, such as the Universal Health Coverage (UHC) Index, were obtained from World Health Organization (WHO) data repository [ ].
Gender equity indicators, such as the Gender Inequality Index (GII) and its components, were accessed via the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) Human Development Reports [ ].
Human development indicators, such as the Human Development Index (HDI), were also drawn from UNDP Human Development Reports [ ].
Budgetary policy indicators, such as national gross domestic product (GDP) expenditure on education, health, and R&D, were derived from the World Bank Open Data portal [ ].
Disease burden measures, such as disability-adjusted life years attributed to Alzheimer’s, epilepsy, migraine, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson’s disease, and stroke, were extracted from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 database [ ].
The official language of each country was obtained from the CIA World Factbook [ ].
Institutional ranking data were retrieved from Quacquarelli Symonds (QS), Times Higher Education (THE), and the Academic Ranking of World Universities (ARWU) by ShanghaiRanking [ – ].

## Data Cleaning and Preprocessing
The first step of data processing involved filtering the raw career-long and single-year SEL datasets to include only scholars whose disciplinary classification in either primary (subfield 1) or secondary (subfield 2) domains was Neurology and Neurosurgery. Records that did not satisfy this condition were excluded from further analysis.
Gender identification was subsequently conducted using Genderize.io, a tool developed by Demografix ApS (Roskilde, Denmark) [ ]. This platform predicts gender by combining first names with country-specific context, drawing on a repository of more than 900 million name-gender associations collected from social media profiles [ ]. Within the retrieved datasets, a total of 142,457 entries were analyzed. Of these, 10,495 cases (7.4%) could not be assigned a gender because the first name was represented by a single letter or was too uncommon. This was a consequence of applying a certainty threshold of 99%.
The final step consisted of manual review and harmonization of institutional names. Inconsistencies between native-language forms, acronyms, and English transliterations were resolved, and duplicate records were standardized and consolidated.

## Independent Variables
National-level determinants (n = 28) were grouped into five thematic domains.
1.Health system attributes included the UHC index, reflecting population access to essential health services, and general government expenditure on health (%), indicating national investment in healthcare.
2.Gender equity indicators comprised the GII, maternal mortality per 100,000 live births, adolescent birth rate (per 1,000 women aged 15–19), education gap among adults aged 25+ (male − female), employment gap among adults aged 15+ (male − female), and female share of parliamentary seats (%), collectively representing structural gender disparities.
3.Human development was captured by the HDI, life expectancy at birth (years), expected years of schooling, mean years of schooling, and gross national income per capita (USD), covering basic capabilities and resources.
4.Budgetary policies were measured by % GDP spent on research and development, % GDP spent on health, and % GDP spent on education, reflecting public funding priorities.
5.Disease burden was represented by disability-adjusted life years attributed to Alzheimer’s disease, epilepsy, migraine, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson’s disease, and stroke.
Finally, geopolitical and linguistic variables included World Bank level, WHO region, and official language of each country. Institutional determinants (n = 12) were derived from three global ranking systems: QS (medicine [overall score, academic reputation], general [overall score, academic reputation]), THE (medicine [overall score, research quality], general [overall score, research quality]), and ARWU (medicine [overall score, research impact], general [overall score, per capita performance]). Individual determinants (n = 2) comprised gender, inferred with the Genderize.io tool, and academic age, calculated as the number of years between a scholar’s first and most recent Scopus-indexed publication, serving as a proxy for career duration.

## Dependent Variables
The principal outcome measure was the number of ENS per country and institution, as identified in both the career-long and single-year SELs. Four core bibliometric indicators were employed to reflect research excellence: (1) citation count excluding self-citations; (2) modified H-index, an adjusted version of the H-index that accounts for the number of authors per publication and excludes self-citations; (3) c-score, a SEL-specific metric that integrates six citation-based indicators while adjusting for field and authorship position, with self-citations excluded; and (4) percentage of self-citations. In addition, several authorship role-specific bibliometric indicators were considered to augment the analysis of academic age, namely, the number and citation counts of (1) single-authored publications, (2) single- and first-authored publications, and (3) single-, first-, and last-authored publications.

## Statistical Analyses
Descriptive statistics were first employed to summarize the datasets. Categorical variables (e.g., WHO region) and ordinal variables (e.g., World Bank level) were expressed as frequencies (n) and percentages (%). Numerical variables (e.g., citation counts) were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Normality of numerical variables was assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test, with p values <0.05 considered to indicate non-normal distribution. Univariable analyses were subsequently applied. Associations were examined using chi-squared tests, Fisher’s exact tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, and Spearman’s rho correlations, with significance set at <0.05.
Regression analyses were then conducted for several purposes: (1) simple logistic regression was applied to estimate female gender group membership; (2) linear regression models were used to explore bibliometric outcomes with academic age as the sole predictor; (3) linear regression models were fitted for core bibliometric outcomes with each national-level determinant as predictor, adjusted for gender and academic age; and (4) multivariable linear regression models were constructed for core bibliometric outcomes incorporating both individual- and thematic national-level determinants simultaneously, in order to identify their independent contributions. All statistical analyses were conducted in R within the RStudio environment, version 2024.12.0 (Posit Software, PBC, Boston, MA, USA).

## Results
A total of 142,457 ENS records were analyzed, of which 78,569 (55.2%) originated from the career-long SEL and 63,888 (44.8%) from the single-year SEL. Information on country affiliation was available for 98.4% of ENS, institutional affiliation for 98.8%, gender for 92.6%, and academic age for 93.8%. The number of ENS showed consistent growth over time, rising in the career-long SEL from 10,318 in 2017 to 12,506 in 2023, and in the single-year SEL from 8,865 to 12,283 over the same period.

## National-Level Analyses of Neurologic Research Excellence
The majority of ENS were based in high-income countries, accounting for 97.8% in the career-long SEL and 95.8% in the single-year SEL. By WHO region, AMRO contributed the largest share (56.4% and 53.2%), followed by EURO (34.7% and 35.7%) and WPRO (8.3% and 9.7%). Language distribution showed clear dominance of English-speaking countries (67.2% and 63.8%), followed by German-speaking countries (9.1% and 9.4%) ( ). At the country level, the USA accounted for the largest share of ENS (50.1% and 47.1%), followed by the UK (9.1% and 8.6%), Germany (6.9% and 6.9%), and Canada (5.5% and 5.2%) (online suppl. Tables S1, S2; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000551993).
 The global density of ENS per 100,000 population was 1.253 in the career-long SEL and 0.898 in the single-year SEL. This measure varied greatly by World Bank level and WHO region; for example, high-income countries showed values of 5.493 and 4.338, compared with 0.013 and 0.009 in low-income countries ( ). At the country level, Switzerland scored the highest density (15.04 and 13.56), followed by Sweden (14.59 and 9.79) and the USA (11.41 and 8.70) ( ).
 The number of ENS per country showed positive, moderate-to-strong correlations with health system attributes, human development indicators, and budgetary policies, while negative correlations were observed with the GII and most of its components. For neurologic disease burden, epilepsy and stroke were negatively correlated, whereas Alzheimer’s disease, migraine, multiple sclerosis, and Parkinson’s disease were positively correlated. Similar patterns were also evident for bibliometric outcomes ( ).
 

## Institutional-Level Analyses of Neurologic Research Excellence
The top 20 institutions accounted for 23.1% of ENS in the career-long SEL and 24.1% in the single-year SEL. Nearly, all were located in English-speaking countries, with the only exception being one Swedish institution. The University of California contributed the highest share (4.5% and 4.4%), followed by Harvard University (2.4% and 3.0%), University College London (1.7% and 1.6%), and Johns Hopkins University (1.4% and 1.5%) ( ). The number of ENS per institution correlated most strongly with the ARWU general ranking (0.735 and 0.741). Other notable correlations were observed with QS medicine academic reputation (0.546 and 0.592) and THE general research quality (0.601 and 0.489), indicating the importance of both general and field-specific ranking indicators ( ).
 

## Gender-Based Analyses of Neurologic Research Excellence
Males constituted the majority of ENS, representing 81.7% in the career-long SEL and 77.6% in the single-year SEL. The share of female ENS was significantly higher in AMRO compared with other WHO regions (19.5% and 23.9%; p < 0.001) ( ).
 At the country level, variation in female representation was marked. In the career-long SEL, among countries with >100 ENS, Poland had the highest female proportion (33.0%), followed by New Zealand (31.9%) and Portugal (27.5%). The lowest shares were observed in Japan (4.4%), Singapore (5.2%), and Belgium (7.8%). In the single-year SEL, Portugal ranked first (39.3%), followed by New Zealand (38.9%) and Poland (37.1%), whereas Japan (5.4%), Singapore (11.7%), and Belgium (14.9%) recorded the lowest values (online suppl. Tables S3, S4).
Gender-based comparisons of bibliometric outcomes showed consistent male advantages. Median citation counts were significantly higher among males than females in both the career-long SEL (9,397 vs. 8,542; p < 0.001) and the single-year SEL (947 vs. 872.5; p < 0.001). Similarly, median academic age was longer for males (38 vs. 35 years; p < 0.001, and 32 vs. 28 years; p < 0.001, respectively) ( ). Core bibliometric indicators showed the same pattern: the modified H-index was significantly higher in males in both the career-long SEL (21.2 vs. 19.6; p < 0.001) and the single-year SEL (6.4 vs. 6.0; p < 0.001) (online suppl. Table S5).

## Age-Based Analyses of Neurologic Research Excellence
The median academic age was 37 years (IQR 30–44) in the career-long SEL and 31 years (IQR 23–40) in the single-year SEL. In the career-long dataset, among countries with >100 ENS, Russia showed the longest academic age (44 years [32–48]), whereas South Korea reported the youngest (27 years [23–32]). In the single-year dataset, Russia again ranked highest (37 years [28.2–47]), while Iran reported the lowest values (16 years [11–19]) (online suppl. Tables S6, S7).
Correlations with core bibliometric outcomes revealed that academic age was most strongly related to the modified H-index in the career-long SEL (ρ = 0.366) and to the c-score in the single-year SEL (ρ = 0.190). The proportion of self-citations correlated negatively in both datasets (ρ = −0.166 and −0.272). Female ENS tended to exhibit slightly stronger coefficients compared to males ( ).
 For secondary bibliometric outcomes, correlations were stronger in the single-year SEL than in the career-long SEL. For example, academic age correlated with the number of single-authored publications (ρ = 0.592 vs. 0.397) and with the number of single-, first-, and last-authored publications (ρ = 0.602 vs. 0.394) (online suppl. Table S8).

The disciplinary classification of Neurology and Neurosurgery was listed as the primary subfield in 59.5% of ENS records and as the secondary subfield in 40.5%. The adjacent subfields are displayed in .
 

## Time-Trend Analyses of Neurologic Research Excellence
Between 2017 and 2023, the share of ENS from high-income countries declined modestly (−0.96% and −3.32% in the career-long and single-year SELs, respectively), while contributions from other income groups increased. Regionally, AMRO showed a notable decrease (−3.24% and −6.47%), whereas other WHO regions expanded, particularly WPRO (+1.5% and +3.5%) ( ). The proportion of female ENS rose from 16.9% to 18.3% in the career-long SEL and from 20.3% to 23.5% in the single-year SEL ( ). Geographic coverage also widened, with 85 countries represented in the career-long SEL compared to 100 in the single-year SEL, reflecting the broader global spread of more recent scholarly activity (online suppl. Table S1, S2).

## Determinants of Female Representation among ENS
Female group membership was associated with a younger academic age (career-long OR = 0.968; single-year OR = 0.970). Compared with AMRO, women were less likely to be represented in EURO (OR = 0.799; 0.865) and WPRO (OR = 0.560; 0.781). Representation was also lower in non-English-speaking countries than in English-speaking ones (OR = 0.663; 0.765). Among national-level determinants, gender inequality was positively associated with female membership (OR = 5.502; 2.219). Other significant predictors included the HDI (OR = 0.239; 0.405), GDP spent on education (OR = 1.194; 1.114), and GDP spent on health (OR = 1.026; 1.006) ( ).
 

## Effects of Academic Age on Bibliometric Outcomes
The regression models demonstrated a stable influence of academic age on all bibliometric outcomes. With every additional year, citation counts increased (career-long β = 135.1; single-year β = 2.4), as did the modified H-index (β = 0.294; 0.039) and the c-score (β = 0.007; 0.006). Comparable associations were observed in both male and female ENS. Positive links with academic age were also evident for the secondary authorship-specific indicators ( ).
 

## Regression Analyses of Neurologic Research Excellence
Two regression approaches were applied to investigate the determinants of neurologic research excellence. The first approach incorporated each national-level variable separately, adjusted for gender and academic age, whereas the second combined individual-level characteristics with thematic national-level factors (World Bank income group, WHO region, official language, GII, HDI, and UHC) in a single model.
In the first approach, non-English-speaking countries were associated with lower citation counts (adj. β = −1,248; −30), while higher values of the GII (adj. β = 148; 231) and greater GDP allocation to education (adj. β = 469; 18) predicted more citations ( ). In the second approach, male gender (adj. β = 1,252; 146), academic age (adj. β = 138; 3.62), upper-middle income status (adj. β = 2,847; 593), English-speaking countries (adj. β = 1,555; 229), and HDI (adj. β = 26,449; 3,570) were all significant positive predictors. These associations were consistently reproduced when using the modified H-index and c-score as outcomes ( ).
 

## Discussion

## Key Findings
The present analysis of 142,457 ENS records revealed pronounced disparities in the distribution of neurologic research excellence. Career-long and single-year SELs were dominated by high-income countries, accounting for 97.8% and 95.8% of ENS, with the USA alone contributing nearly half of the global total. Appearance-weighted densities underscored this divide, reaching 5.493 ENS per 100,000 in high-income countries compared with only 0.013 in low-income countries; Switzerland, Sweden, and the USA recorded the highest national values.
English-speaking countries were disproportionately represented. Institutional elitism was evident, with the top 20 institutions hosting 23.1% and 24.1% of records; institutional counts correlated most strongly with ARWU ranking (ρ = 0.74), and also with QS medicine reputation and THE research quality. National macro-determinants correlated positively with human development and budgetary investments, and negatively with gender inequality.
Gender composition remained male-skewed, with men comprising 81.7% of career-long and 77.6% of single-year records. Men achieved higher citations, modified H-index, and c-score, with differences attenuated after conditioning on academic age. Academic age was the most consistent predictor of bibliometric outcomes and authorship role metrics. Logistic models showed that younger academic age increased odds of female membership, while non-English settings lowered odds. Multivariable models confirmed independent associations for male gender, academic age, income level, English-speaking status, and development metrics, while attenuating several bivariate patterns.

## Global Disparities of Neurologic Scholarship
The global distribution of neurologic scholarship is markedly uneven, with research production heavily concentrated in high-income settings. Previous bibliometric analyses have consistently demonstrated that national economic strength is strongly correlated with scientific output in neurology and neurosurgery [ – ]. For instance, Simard et al. [ ] examined Web of Science (WoS)-indexed brain research from 1991 to 2020 and found a clear concentration of activity in North America, Western Europe, and Oceania, with the USA leading (36.4%), followed by China (11.9%), the UK (10.1%), Germany (8.5%), and Canada (8.4%). Similarly, a recent analysis of WoS-indexed systematic reviews in neurosurgery (2002–2022) reported that the USA accounted for 34.5% of publications, ahead of China (8%), the UK (8%), and Canada (6.6%) [ ]. In research on treatments for multiple sclerosis (1980–2021), the USA again ranked first (27.6%), followed by Italy (13.9%), Germany (13.8%), and the UK (10.3%) [ ].
The proliferation of the open-access publishing model, supported by government mandates such as the recent US policy on publicly funded research and by concerns over the sustainability of subscription-based systems, has not narrowed these inequalities [ , ]. Koester et al. [ ] reported that open-access articles in neurosurgery are still predominantly produced in high-income countries, with the USA contributing 27.5%, while low-income countries accounted for only 0.01% of the total output. An analysis of neurosurgery scholarship coauthored by HIC-LMIC institutions showed that, despite ongoing efforts to foster collaborations between the global North and South, most publications were led by high-income countries. US authors were the most frequent contributors (41.1%) and occupied the majority of first-author positions (55.5%), whereas authors from low-income countries remained the least represented [ ].
Education-related scholarship shows a comparable imbalance [ , ]. For example, the USA accounted for more than half of global research output on neurology education (57%), followed by the UK (8.6%) and Canada (7.8%) [ ]. Neurosurgery education literature displays a similar pattern, with the USA contributing 44.5%, compared with 7.5% from the UK and 6.6% from Canada [ ]. These findings are directly relevant to the present analysis, as they demonstrate that even in fields central to workforce development and capacity building, knowledge production remains disproportionately concentrated in high-income settings.
Taken together, these bibliometric findings demonstrate that the global inequalities in neurologic research productivity are mirrored in our analysis of neurologic research excellence. Moreover, even leading neurology journals have been criticized for giving limited priority to issues of equity, such as racial disparities, underscoring those systemic imbalances extend not only to who produces research but also to what is prioritized for publication [ ].

## English-Language Preponderance in Neurologic Scholarship
In a meta-science study of 74 neurosurgical journals identified through WoS, Scopus, and Ulrich’s databases, Shlobin et al. [ ] found that 70.3% of journals were published in English, with English-language titles achieving a higher median impact factor (1.77) compared with Spanish (0.55), German (0.10), and Japanese (0.06). Although many journals originated from non-anglophone countries, most still published in English, while only 4.1% were multilingual and 6.8% provided abstracts in another language, reflecting limited efforts to enhance linguistic accessibility [ ]. The findings indicate that language concordance continues to act as a significant barrier to research equity in neurosurgery, with researchers from non-English-speaking LMICs being most disadvantaged [ ]. Similarly, a WoS-based bibliometric analysis of “brain abscess” publications (1980–2022) showed that 89.8% of 994 indexed articles were published in English, illustrating that topic-level searches within WoS predominantly retrieve English-language outputs [ ].
Anglophone dominance influences which findings are internationally visible, who participates in global discourse, and how local knowledge is disseminated [ – ]. National actors often adopt English outlets or translate key materials to expand their reach; for instance, several German clinical guidelines were translated from German into English specifically to improve accessibility for the international scientific community [ ]. In the same context, the German Neurological Society established Neurological Research and Practice as an English-language journal to attract worldwide submissions and enhance visibility, exemplifying institutional shifts toward English publishing [ ]. Evidence of this reliance is also observed in non-English journals: a Cuban neurology and neurosurgery journal published entirely in Spanish (2017–2021) nonetheless cited predominantly English sources, with 2,529 of 3,123 references (81%) drawn from anglophone literature, underscoring dependence on English scholarship even within local outputs [ ].
This illustrates that English functions not only as a medium of publication but also as the dominant reference framework for scientific credibility. The present analysis reflects this pattern, with anglophone countries (e.g., USA, UK, Canada, and Australia) collectively accounting for more than two-thirds of ENS counts, while the next most common language, German, represented only about 9%, a marked disparity that underscores the overwhelming linguistic advantage of English.

## Institutional Elitism
Neurologic research production is disproportionately concentrated within a few highly resourced institutions at both national and international levels. In Saudi Arabia, for example, an analysis of 1,292 PubMed®-indexed neurology publications (1996–2015) found that three institutions produced 53% of the national output: King Faisal Specialist Hospital and Research Centre (23%), King Saud University (21%), and King Abdulaziz University (9%) [ ]. A Pan-Arabian analysis of medical research outputs indexed in WoS (2017–2023) likewise revealed marked institutional elitism, with a single Lebanese institution accounting for 50.8% of national output, two Qatari institutions 67.6%, three Saudi institutions 51.3%, and four Egyptian institutions 55.6% [ ]. Similarly, in India, between 2010 and 2014, five of 92 neurology training institutes accounted for 55.7% of PubMed®-indexed publications, led by the National Institute of Mental Health and Neuro Sciences (15.2%), the All India Institute of Medical Sciences (11.9%), and the Postgraduate Institute of Medical Education and Research (10.6%) [ ]. Globally, a bibliometric analysis of neurosurgery and social media research reported that 50% of the total output came from just 10 institutions, led by the University of Cambridge, followed by the University of Toronto and the All India Institute of Medical Sciences [ ]. Our analysis echoed these patterns, with ENS disproportionately concentrated within a small number of world-elite institutions.
Evidence at the national level suggests that scientific growth is extremely unevenly distributed across institutions [ – ]. Using Google Scholar Citations data from more than 4,400 institutions worldwide, Prathap [ ] demonstrated that as countries accumulate greater “scientific wealth,” excellence tends to cluster within a small number of premier institutions, thereby intensifying inequality in its distribution. In the USA, the so-called Howard-Harvard effect illustrates how institutional prestige not only concentrates research output but also reinforces intersectional inequalities, with elite universities favoring the visibility of White male scholarship while research associated with marginalized groups is more often situated in mission-driven institutions [ ].
One explanation for the persistence of institutional elitism lies in the strong link between research funding, bibliometric indicators, and prestige [ , ]. The case of the National Institutes of Health (NIH) has shown that a small fraction of elite investigators and organizations consistently capture a disproportionate share of funding, which in turn generates more publications, citations, and patents, reinforcing their dominant position and creating a “rich-get-richer” cycle that limits mobility and diversity in the research ecosystem [ ]. In neurosurgery, NIH-funded faculty exhibited significantly higher scholarly impact than their nonfunded peers, with H-indices rising in parallel with larger funding ranges, underscoring the reciprocal relationship between research impact and grant success [ ]. Moreover, mean H-indices increased with academic rank across both cohorts, and MD faculty demonstrated higher scholarly impact than their PhD colleagues, highlighting how rank and degree further shape patterns of productivity and funding [ ].

## Gender Disparities
Female ENS constituted less than one-fifth to one-fourth of career-long and single-year SELs, with males achieving higher bibliometric outcomes such as citation counts, modified H-index, and c-scores in our present analysis. In a bibliometric analysis of WoS-indexed publications (2009–2010) across 29 high-impact neuroscience journals (n = 11,590), gender was identified for 53,351 authorships, revealing that women constituted 32.9% of authors overall [ ]. Female representation was higher in first-author positions but markedly lower as last or senior authors, declined with increasing journal impact factor, and varied geographically, while men on average published more papers and received slightly more citations per authorship [ ]. Contrarily, a review of 14,020 neuro-oncology articles (1944–2021) showed that women were underrepresented not only in last authorship positions but also in first authorship roles, while female-led teams demonstrated greater gender diversity in research composition [ ]. Likewise, an analysis of 2,798 first and 2,762 last authors of skull base research presented at the North American Skull Base Society (2011–2020) showed low female representation (17.8% of first authors and 9.7% of last authors), although participation steadily increased over the decade and varied considerably across specialties and countries [ ].
In an analysis of 1,052 academic neurosurgeons across 84 US institutions, women made up only 8.8% of faculty and 3.7% of full professors; although men demonstrated higher overall H-indices (13.3 vs. 9.5; p. <0.001), rank-stratified comparisons showed no significant gender differences in productivity, suggesting that underrepresentation in senior positions rather than lower output accounted for much of the disparity [ ]. In a 2020 survey of 700 US-based neurology researchers, women were younger, more often in junior ranks, and reported fewer middle-author publications, fewer invited presentations, and less satisfaction with institutional equity, despite receiving comparable research funding and mentorship to men [ ]. Across neurologic journal categories, rehabilitation (45.3%) and neurogenetics (44.6%) had the highest proportions of female authors between 2002 and 2020, whereas neuroradiology (22.4%) and neurotology (25.0%) showed the lowest representation [ ]. A similar pattern was seen in 1946–2001, with rehabilitation (34.6%) having the greatest female representation and neurotology (0%) the least [ ].

## Academic Age
In the present analysis, academic age emerged as the only consistent positive predictor of bibliometric outputs among ENS, echoing prior evidence linking research productivity to career progression. Duclos et al. [ ] analyzed 44,723 publications authored by 1,835 medical researchers at Lyon University Hospital between 1995 and 2014 to explore performance curves across academic careers. They found that full professors, averaging 5.28 publications per year compared with 0.82 among nonacademic physicians, followed a three-phase trajectory of initiation (age 25–35), maturation (35–50), and stabilization (50+), with productivity peaking before plateauing or declining in later years. Younger birth cohorts demonstrated substantially higher outputs at comparable ages, indicating a generational acceleration in publication productivity [ ]. Likewise academic productivity in the neurosciences often peaks around the biological age of 50–55, after which senior investigators, despite their extensive expertise in grant writing, mentoring, and leadership by age 60 and beyond, are frequently regarded as “seasoned” and face sharp declines in funding opportunities [ ].
Among academic neurosurgeons, academic rank was strongly correlated with bibliometric performance in an analysis of 1,225 faculty across 99 US institutions [ ]. Median WoS H-indices increased steadily with rank, from 3 for instructors and 6 for assistant professors to 11 for associate professors, 23 for full professors, and 26 for department chairs, underscoring the close association between seniority and research productivity [ ]. In another analysis of 3,249 academic neurologists from 120 US residency programs, mean WoS H-indices rose from 9.4 for instructors to 12.1 for assistant professors, 15.9 for associate professors, and 28.5 for professors, with similar trends in Scopus values (1.7, 3.6, 8.7, and 18.1, respectively) [ ].
Prior to neurosurgery residency matching, an analysis of 932 US applicants (2017–2021) found that Scopus-indexed publications more than doubled over 5 years, with mean values increasing from 3.7 in 2017 to 8.1 in 2021 [ ]. However, this rapid escalation has raised concerns about research misrepresentation, applicant burnout, and inequities in access to research opportunities, particularly since evidence suggests pre-residency publication counts do not reliably predict clinical performance during residency [ ]. In a bibliometric study of 1,352 residents across 105 US neurosurgery programs, resident productivity strongly correlated with faculty productivity and was higher in larger programs, underscoring the importance of departmental environment and program size in shaping academic output during training [ ].

## Strengths
This study is, to the best of our knowledge, the first to investigate excellence rather than mere productivity in the field of neurology, shifting the focus from raw publication counts to the recognition of outstanding scholarly performance as captured in SEL. By systematically analyzing seven annual SEL releases (2017–2023), the study draws on more than 140,000 records with high completeness of core variables, enabling robust examination of temporal trends, national densities, and institutional clustering. The use of a multilevel ecological framework, integrating national, institutional, and individual determinants, offers a comprehensive perspective on how excellence is shaped by broader socioeconomic, health system, and gender equity factors. Rigorous data processing – including careful field-specific filtering, conservative gender inference, and harmonization of institutional names – enhances the validity and reproducibility of the analysis. Finally, the study provides novel, policy-relevant insights by identifying academic age as the most consistent predictor of bibliometric excellence and documenting persistent inequalities by language, gender, and institutional concentration, thereby laying a foundation for future strategies to support equitable advancement in neurologic scholarship.

## Limitations
First, excellence was defined using the SEL, which are citation-based and, despite methodological refinements such as exclusion of self-citations and adjustment for authorship position, remain imperfect indicators as they are sensitive to disciplinary norms, language, and cohort effects [ ]. In addition, citation-based systems may reward visibility more than intrinsic quality, may favor hyperprolific authors, and may remain vulnerable to distortion from retracted publications [ ]. Second, gender assignment was algorithmically inferred and restricted to binary categories with a certainty threshold, leaving a proportion of records unclassified and risking misclassification across cultures, thereby limiting the accuracy of gender-stratified analyses [ , ]. Third, academic age, calculated from the interval between first and most recent Scopus-indexed publications, is only a proxy for career duration and does not capture parental leave or career interruptions [ ]. Fourth, the disciplinary classification based on Scopus thematic labeling may misallocate scholars who publish across domains, producing false positives and negatives when identifying neurologists and neurosurgeons [ ]. Fifth, reliance on Scopus indexing introduces coverage and language biases that may disproportionately represent anglophone and highly resourced institutions while underrepresenting outputs from low- and middle-income countries, potentially exaggerating the observed geographic and institutional concentration of excellence [ , ]. Finally, the bibliometric indicators used in this study represent only one dimension of scholarly activity and should not be interpreted as substitutes for qualitative assessment of research quality, societal contribution, or academic leadership [ ].

## Implications
The findings of this analysis have several implications for policy, medical education, and future research. First, the disproportionate concentration of ENS in elite institutions highlights the need to rebalance investment and incentives. Policymakers and funders should consider targeted capacity-building programs, equitable collaborative networks, and dedicated funding mechanisms to support emerging hubs in underrepresented regions and non-elite centers. Second, the documented dominance of English in neurologic publishing underscores the importance of mitigating language-driven visibility gaps. Journals and funders could promote bilingual abstracts, translation support, more inclusive editorial and reviewer structures, and improved indexing and recognition of high-quality non-English outputs to broaden global accessibility. Third, gender disparities in excellence call for gender-equitable assessment practices. Evaluation systems should incorporate adjustments for academic age and career interruptions, ensuring that structural disadvantages do not obscure the contributions of female researchers. Fourth, the correlation between departmental environments and scholarly output suggests implications for medical education policy. Residency and training programs should emphasize mentored, high-quality research rather than sheer output volume, while expanding opportunities beyond highly resourced institutions. Fifth, this study identifies a clear agenda for future research. Analyses should investigate ENS career curves to map productivity trajectories and adjust for birth cohorts to account for generational differences, while linking bibliometric indicators to broader measures of impact. Finally, these findings also carry implications for research assessment itself. Academic and funding systems should reward methodological rigor, policy relevance, and context-sensitive impact, rather than relying narrowly on publication volume or citation visibility alone.

## Conclusion
This study is the first to examine excellence, rather than mere productivity, in neurology and neurosurgery, providing a novel perspective on global scholarly achievement. Excellence was disproportionately concentrated in high-income, anglophone countries, with limited representation from low- and middle-income settings, highlighting persistent global inequalities. At the institutional level, a small group of world-elite universities accounted for a substantial share of ENS, reflecting entrenched advantages linked to resources and prestige. Gender disparities remained evident, yet differences in bibliometric outcomes diminished after adjusting for academic age, underscoring the importance of life-course factors. Academic age emerged as the most consistent predictor of bibliometric performance, while English dominance and citation-based measures reinforced structural imbalances, suggesting the need for more equitable frameworks to support neurologic scholarship.
