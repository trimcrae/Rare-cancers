## Abstract
In reviewing a video series that they created for the website of the Health Physics Society (HPS), past leaders of the Health Physics Society have treated as authoritative and trustworthy the scientific misconduct theories of University of Massachusetts Professor Edward Calabrese. No mention is made of detailed critiques of Calabrese’s work. I show that Calabrese’s historical work as presented by HPS’s authors is unreliable because it overlooks key historical text and key statistical concepts about the limits of an early atomic bomb genetics study. When these errors are corrected, claims of scientific misconduct on the part of historical figures evaporate. Claims of threshold behavior in early radiation genetic experiments are wrong for atomic bomb data. Calabrese’s unique claims about thresholds in early animal genetic data are not credible for human cancer, given the doses at which they were carried out (>30 R). Recent epidemiological studies of both acute and protracted exposure in humans fail to show dose-rate effects or a dose threshold above 30 R. Such results from human data should be more relevant for most regulators and review committees than Calabrese’s claims about old data on animals. Disclaimers, errata, and links to critiques should be added to the HPS webpage hosting the 22-part video series. Failure to do so can cause damage to reputations and historical accuracy because it erroneously validates Calabrese’s inflammatory claims of scientific misconduct against past scientists, including three Nobel Prize winners, members of the NAS, and presidents of the AAAS.

## INTRODUCTION
as a
Health Physics Journal author and reviewer, I am concerned for the Health Physics Society (HPS) when people I respect, including two former presidents and Society staff members, embrace the scientific misconduct theories of Edward Calabrese, a critic of the linear no-threshold model, without any acknowledgement of the criticisms that have been published about his claims. This unreserved embrace appears in the forum article in Health Physics, “The History of the Linear No-threshold Model and Recommendations for a Path Forward,” by John Cardarelli II, Barbara Hamrick, Dan Sowers, and Brett Burk ( ). Their article features a summary of interviews of Calabrese that make up a 22-part series that remains in original form on the HPS website.
Calabrese’s historical work is treated by Cardarelli et al. as authoritative and trustworthy in two ways: first by being placed on the HPS website and second by receiving a glowing review in the HPJ article. The authors give his claims of scientific misconduct credibility that I will argue is not deserved. I question neither Calabrese’s sincerity nor character, but I do question the historical accuracy of key aspects of his work and the absence of statistical analysis supporting it. Nor is this paper a defense of the LNT. It is a defense of the integrity of historical figures and committees. I do not expect anyone reading this article to change their ultimate views about the LNT, dose thresholds, hormesis, or supralinearity because of it. My hope is that they will support the addition of errata information and links to critiques on the series website.
The affiliation of all the authors is given as the Health Physics Society, which suggests that at the time of publication, they presented consensus HPS views. This presumption of widespread acceptance of Calabrese’s version of LNT history by HPS leadership is consistent with the video series having been organized by Cardarelli et al. and by its appearing and remaining unchanged on the official website. No disclaimer was made in the paper that their views did not necessarily represent the views of the HPS. As a result, I refer to the Cardarelli et al. authors herein as the “HPS authors.”
Calabrese’s views are presented in the article as the true history of the linear no-threshold model (LNT), not his personal, contested opinion on it. Members are given professional credit for watching the HPS video series. However, they do not receive professional credit for studying the critiques that have been written about Calabrese’s misconduct claims ( ; ; , ; ; ) or previous papers in the Health Physics Journal on the history of the LNT ( ; ), which differ from Calabrese as to the importance of committees of the National Academy of Sciences (NAS) in radiation regulation and the reliability of the tolerance dose concept that preceded the LNT.
In the HPS authors’ telling, the history of the LNT stops before the end of the 20th century, which is misleading, because support for the LNT is primarily based on more recent data, as exemplified in the 2018 report of the National Council of Radiation Protection (NCRP), which stated that current human epidemiological data, not historical data, formed the basis for recommending continuation of the LNT model for regulation ( ).
One can differ with the NCRP as to the validity of various pieces of the current epidemiologic data. One can prefer older data or even non-epidemiological data for dose-response. However, to reach balanced judgments about dose-response and the basis for the LNT, one cannot ignore the history of the atomic bomb cancer data or the history of the last 20 y, when large sample-size studies of protracted exposure to humans were developed ( ). It is necessary to look at and argue about current data to evaluate the LNT, as well as to make sense of the limited datasets collected in the early days.
By labeling a series that was essentially about animal data as the history of the LNT, the impression is given that LNT choice was settled long ago based on animal data and not continually reevaluated up to the present day with human data in the mix. By avoiding the full history, it can be suggested that, if the animal data were wrongly judged in the past, the LNT should be replaced today. This is what the HPS authors essentially suggest at the end of their piece. It is not a logical argument. To prevent any such false assumption, the video series should have been labeled as the early history of the LNT. Moreover, it should have been acknowledged that the Calabrese version was contested in parts.
Lead HPS author, Cardarelli, has engaged in respectful debate in the past, and I believe the authors are all sensible people trying to do what they think is best for society and science. However, in this case, he and his co-authors have put their trust in Calabrese’s words without any apparent review by persons knowledgeable about the historical period or any direct reading of the contested historical reports.
We are to believe, for example, that
Researchers who did groundbreaking research before 1950 relied on data they knew to be faulty to reject a dose threshold model, thereby engaging in scientific misconduct;
To increase funding for genetics and to support an ideology, a 1956 committee of the National Academy of Sciences engaged in scientific misconduct in support of dose linearity by suppressing genetic data from Hiroshima and Nagasaki and by suppressing disagreements among committee members about risk magnitude; and
A 1972 NAS committee failed to take into account genetic mice data that showed dose-rate effects.
Carlson’s detailed rebuttal of many of Calabrese’s historical arguments ( ) is neither referenced nor discussed in the HPS authors’ paper, nor are my own critiques mentioned – neither the first one ( ), to which Calabrese made a partial response ( ), nor the second ( ), to which there has been no response. Although Calabrese has widened his scope of historical personalities since 2017, most of his arguments about scientific misconduct had been made earlier. Without mention of the contrary published papers, the viewers of the series and the readers of the article are not “informed.” Rather, they are misled. A reader gets no sense that the scientific misconduct information is deeply contested.
These omissions and the absence of debate have motivated my response. When I first read the article, I saw it as a de facto trial where only the prosecutor was allowed to simultaneously present evidence, frame the case, and give testimony, all without cross-examination. No defense was allowed for the accused historical figures and committees. With that one-sided trial, I saw the HPS authors, spokespersons for the Health Physics Society at the time, proceed to posthumously convict historical figures, no longer able to defend themselves, of “scientific misconduct.”
Replete with scientific misconduct, as stated in the article? What an incredible claim for an organization’s leadership to have made about three Nobel prize winners (Hermann Muller, George Beadle, and Edward Lewis) and other prominent scientists, just because Dr. Calabrese, a critic of the LNT, says so. Calabrese is a good writer with great rhetorical skills. He can write excellent, objective papers ( ), but when it comes to his treatment of Hermann Muller and other early supporters of the LNT, I take issue. One of my goals in this article is to demonstrate that Calabrese routinely overlooks contradictory material about his historical intellectual competitors, even if that contradiction is located in the sentence immediately following the one that he quotes. I list clear examples in this article based on new and past research.
Based on such evidence, I shall argue that Calabrese has not been a reliable reporter of historical events. I will argue that it is his overlooking of key sections of historical text that leads to false premises that seemingly make plausible his claims of scientific misconduct.
The purpose of this commentary is to begin to provide sufficient evidence of errors of fact, reasoning, and statistics to show that not only is the Calabrese prosecutorial case irrelevant to the validity of the LNT but that the case for scientific misconduct falls apart. I will quote from the actual criticized reports to show that Calabrese and the HPS authors, by their acceptance of his scientific misconduct claims, have distorted the record, I believe inadvertently. If there is any “shocking exposé,” as the HPS authors claim, I shall argue it is of their own work.
At the outset, I acknowledge that these authors and Calabrese have a role to play in challenging conventional wisdom. An example of the benefits of challenges can be seen in the 2023 update of the INWORKS worker epidemiology study, where researchers have now addressed criticisms about medical exposures of early workers, such as those raised by Cardarelli and Ulsh ( ). That important role, however, cannot justify endorsement of what has been called character assassination ( ), nor can it justify sloppy historical analysis.
My article is organized to address three questions:
Isn’t the modern history of the LNT where the debate lies, with modern data, including epidemiological studies, superseding the previous animal data?
Was the early history of the LNT replete with scientific misconduct by noted researchers?
How did past peer reviewers miss the overlooking of historical text that I have identified in my critique?
Because Calabrese has repeated assertions of deception ( ) or scientific misconduct ( ) in many different journals since 2011, a response to the HPS authors requires a long analysis of Calabrese’s claims. For readers without the patience to go through the whole analysis, there is a summary at the end of this article.
Based on the reaction of a reviewer, this article might be disturbing for some supporters of Calabrese who see him as a champion for scientific views that they feel are marginalized and ignored by the scientific establishment. I want to make it clear from the beginning that I view Calabrese as a serious researcher in that he lists his sources in his citations, which allows other researchers like me to try to reproduce his work. Unfortunately, it turns out that I could not support (replicate) his claims of misconduct.
Mistakes happen all the time in research. I’ve made my share of them. Providing sources is what allows the scientific enterprise to work, not the absence of mistakes. The appropriate response to the making of mistakes is to correct them, pick up the pieces, and move on. Calabrese can still criticize the same people and committees for their scientific process, judgments, and decisions, but his “shortcut” reliance on claims of scientific misconduct to negate a person’s work falls apart upon careful review. Claims of threshold must be buttressed with statistical support. Statistical analysis provides the guardrails against false interpretation of count data that can be caused by statistical fluctuations and uncertainties.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS
I have reviewed the 2023 article by the HPS authors to identify statements based on premises that were falsely assumed. The raw data used included relevant Calabrese papers from 2011–2024, most of which are listed in the citation section. Additional data were mainly historical documents, especially reports about which Calabrese makes questionable claims. Sources also included the full range of historical studies on radiation dose-response accessible through modern digital searches such as Google Scholar and other public databases. Biographical sources were also used, including those written by the historical figures defamed or criticized by Calabrese.
One of the critiques of Calabrese is that he has no way of proving negative claims, such as what was not done or not said in a document. To avoid the same problem in this article, all documents collected were converted into digitally searchable form if necessary, so they could be searched for key words and author names. Links to searchable PDFs available without needing third party permission to access are provided among the citations below. For example, to check the claim that a 1956 genetics report by the National Academy of Sciences (NAS) failed to discuss the early atomic bomb genetic studies in Japan, it was possible to search for “Japan” and show by the word’s presence, along with the context, that the genetics atomic bomb data actually did inform the report.
In addition, using the keyword method, statements about the absence of certain words became verifiable negative findings. For instance, Calabrese claimed that Curt Stern and Delta Uphoff promised in a 1949 paper to provide “...a subsequent highly detailed paper with all the necessary methods, materials, and data” ( ). On searching for such words, it was possible to show that the 1949 paper made no such expansive promise, saying only at the end of the article, “A more detailed account of the work will be presented later” ( ).
I have avoided making factual claims for findings about which I can find no information, stating instead that “I was unable to find any relevant wording in the document(s).” A negative finding could reflect a failure in historical research rather than an omission on the part of historical figures (this is a risk for me and for Calabrese). As for building statements of claimed fact, I have relied heavily on quotations, with the full digitized text available in a link for context when possible.
I have cited two versions of the report of the 1956 Genetics Committee, one of which ( ) is accessible using the link provided. A partial version of the Committee’s report was reprinted in Science, with the introductory background information excluded ( ). The complete version is contained in the summary reports for all of the six committees that were part of the overall NAS effort to describe the biological effects of atomic radiation, including the Genetics Committee ( ).
A mixture of historical radiation units and their typographical conventions comes into play when discussing data ranging over 80 y. For all reports and articles before 1960 discussed in this article, the common unit for dose was roentgen written as a lower-case r. The unit of dose was an upper-case R for roentgen in subsequent reports. To make reading easier in this article, r is replaced with R, except in direct quotations. Also, R, cGy, and cSv are treated as having approximately the same magnitude for low-LET radiation. Over the same 80-y period, changes also occurred in the names of institutions. The National Academy of Sciences became the National Academies of Sciences, and then National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (NASEM). The acronym “NAS” is used herein to refer to reports published by the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, its predecessors, and its constituent organizations, including the National Research Council.
Because this work involves many disciplines beyond health physics, I asked for comments on the original manuscript from a variety of researchers, including a historian of early debates about radiation, a geneticist, and a former director of the Nuclear and Radiation Studies Board of NASEM, as well as others. They have not seen the final manuscript. They are acknowledged by name at the end of the article.

## RESULTS

## Question 1. Isn’t the modern history of the LNT where the debate lies, with modern data, including epidemiological studies, superseding the old data on animals?
A puzzling feature of the HPS authors’ article is how the authors can leap to a call for changes to regulatory policy without consideration of the recent history of the cancer LNT. Their article ignored recent studies, but regulators and courts are unlikely to do the same. The doses in the animal studies discussed by Calabrese are at 50 R and above, which makes them less relevant to cancer dose response now that there exists so much epidemiological data on human exposure, both acute and protracted, at lower doses ( ; ; ; ; ; , ). Scientific debate about cancer dose response below 10 R is certainly fair game, but the debate at 50 R and above is settled even for the HPS authors for acute exposures and for dose rates above 50 mSv y−1 ( ). The latter dose rate is lower than the rates delivered in the animal studies. Thus, there is no threshold claimed by the HPS authors at these elevated doses and dose rates. The early atomic bomb data primarily on pregnancy outcome (Neel data) span the right dose range, with mean doses of a few R ( ), but the animal genetic data produced by Muller, Stern, Uphoff, Russell, and other researchers in the first generation of postwar scientific work in the 1940–1975 period is no longer relevant to regulatory decision making.
For this article, I date modern LNT history from roughly 2006, which saw the publication of the 500-page BEIR VII report from the US NAS, with its 18 authors and 16 reviewers ( ). No controversial claims about low-LET radiation effects should be made without first reviewing what this comprehensive report has to say about the topic.
Was this 2006 study influenced by the researchers condemned by the HPS authors and Calabrese? Of the early researchers, only Hermann Muller is mentioned in the report (twice) according to a digital search. The first mention is to note his formulation of the doubling-dose (DD) concept. The second mention of Muller (along with Stadler) is purely historical and has nothing to do with dose response: “The discoveries by Muller (1927) of the mutagenic effects of X-rays in fruit flies (Drosophila) and by Stadler (1928a, 1928b) of similar effects in barley and maize…conclusively established the genetic damage-inducing effects of radiation” ( ).
There is no mention of Stern, Uphoff, or Lewis in the report. Only the data of the Russells is discussed, but their baseline mouse data (spontaneous-rate data), which are thought to be too low by Calabrese, were not used in genetic risk assessments in the 2006 BEIR VII report for the reasons stated at pp. 96-97. Thus, any hypothetical correction to the mouse baseline rate is irrelevant today, as it was in the 1972 BEIR I report, which also did not use the mouse spontaneous rate ( ) at pp. 12 and 123.
Similarly, the 2018 NCRP report mentioned earlier has no text with the words Muller, Stern, Uphoff, Lewis, or Russell, nor does it mention genetic data.
The publication of the first major cancer outcome study of protracted exposures to residents of Techa River followed in 2007 ( ). Since then, additional Techa River studies have been published ( ), as was the study of the large INWORKS worker cohort, updated just this year ( , ). There is no threshold evident in radiation-induced cancer at protracted dose levels comparable to the 50-R fruit fly genetic studies of interest to the HPS authors, and thus there is no threshold either at the dose levels in the mouse genetic studies they discuss. When the results from the epidemiological studies for protracted exposures are compared to the atomic bomb cancer data, they allow assessment of dose-rate effects. No evidence of a dose-rate effect shows up at 50 R. Thus, the dose levels of these early animal studies are too high to be of any obvious use in resolving issues of dose or dose-rate threshold for cancer. This should have been stated. From the scientific perspective, the animal data have been superseded.
It seems as if Calabrese does not notice the dose levels at which he claims there are dose thresholds and hormesis. For instance, he claims that mouse genetic data collected by the Russells reveal a threshold or hormesis when Calabrese’s post-1995 corrections to baseline are made ( ). His calculation, however, takes place at doses of 37.5 R and 86 R ( ), where there is near universal agreement that radiation-induced cancer occurs in humans based on atomic bomb data and the protracted epidemiological studies mentioned above. If anyone had tried in 1972 to follow Calabrese’s hindsight approach for cancer, they would have been contradicted by now.

## Question 2. Was the early history of the LNT replete with scientific misconduct?
I will argue in this section that the answer is “no” and that “selective stakeholder perception” by Calabrese is part of the explanation for his claims. By the coined phrase “selective stakeholder perception,” I mean the inability of some advocates to see words in documents that contradict their thesis. It is common. I have a bit of it myself. It is not a moral failing, which is why I appropriate a psychological term, “selective perception,” for the wording. Some of it will inevitably go uncaught but can be minimized by rereading key documents as a final check from the perspective of someone who takes a different side. If it occurs repeatedly, an author’s work can be one-sided and misleading.

## 2.1: The 1956 NAS Committee and atomic bomb pregnancy outcome data
The HPS authors, copying Calabrese, repeatedly make claims about what researchers or committees did not do without specifying the methodology they used to prove a negative. For instance, it is said that the 1956 Genetics Committee did not consider Neel’s atomic bomb pregnancy termination data, unlike the parallel British committee, suggesting some nefarious behavior or incompetence: “So the BEAR [Biological Effects of Atomic Radiation] Panel decided not to discuss the only human study conducted to date…” ( ).
The claim is strange on its face because the lead author, James Neel, was a member of the committee. Presumably, a correct statement would be that Calabrese himself did not find evidence of committee consideration/discussion. The evidence is clearly there, I found, but Calabrese apparently did not find it. The data were indeed considered and deemed useful to the extent allowed by the limited data counts. During the first meeting of the 1956 committee at Princeton in November of 1955, Bentley Glass, according to page 10 of the transcript, summarized the report of an AEC conference in 1954, including “Neel’s discussion of the effects of radiation on human populations (Hiroshima-Nagasaki)” ( ).
Neel himself felt that the 1956 report did justice to his data. As he indicated in his autobiography (p. 321), it was proper in 1956 for the human data to play a minor role: “The principal sources of data for these recommendations were the mouse experiments of WL Russell, the data from human studies playing at that time—properly—a minor role” ( ).
He thought highly of the report: “The result (I am prejudiced) was a document which for clarity at all levels has not been superseded by any of the many subsequent reports” ( ). Also, “The Report was generally received for what it was, an honest effort to deal with a difficult problem. I do not recall any significant criticism to the effect we had been too lax” ( ).
The most important evidence that the 1956 Committee considered the atomic bomb genetic data is found in the text of the report itself, which discusses the implications for the doubling dose of “the excellent information on such lower forms as fruit flies, the emerging information for mice, the few sparse data we have for man:”
“The lowest figure which has been responsibly brought forward for the doubling dose is 5r, and the largest estimates range up to 150r or even higher. Recent work with mice (which are, after all, mammals) gives some basis for thinking that the doubling dose is not as high as 150r. The experience in Japan gives some basis for thinking that the doubling dose is larger than 5r” ( ) (italics added).
Although the central estimate was not statistically significant, it was possible to use the lower 95% confidence limit on Neel’s doubling dose to set a modest lower limit, which the committee did as quoted above about the Japan results.
James Neel, who “had the privilege of serving” on the Committee ( ), discusses the numbers in the first two sentences in a lecture he gave on the atomic bomb genetic data published in 1963 ( ). He goes on to explain the numerical logic for the Committee’s doubling dose estimates and the stated ranges, indicating that Neel was intimately involved in the risk calculations and was not the outsider that Calabrese claims ( ).
In addition to the text of the 1956 report itself, which indicates in the quoted paragraph above that the atomic bomb data can only bound by doubling dose calculations at the riskiest end of the responsible range (5 R), the limitations of the data had been widely discussed prior to the 1956 Committee meetings. Yet, Calabrese never mentions these well-known and well-discussed limitations that Neel had expressed in his reports, beginning in 1953 ( ; ): “Even in this preliminary note, however, it should be pointed out that these findings, if taken at face value, are entirely consistent with what is known of the radiation genetics of a wide variety of plant and animal material, including Drosophila and mice” ( ).
Why did Neel and others believe that the data had limitations? For one reason, only a small subset of genetic defects was studied, mainly pregnancy terminations ( ) at p. 479, which lowered expected count rates. Commenting on the lack of statistical significance, Neel stated that the “possibility of such an ‘inconclusive’ outcome was clearly foreseen from the outset” ( ). It was not until 1991 that Neel was able to get a solid estimate for genetic defects by using subsequently obtained cytological and biochemical data ( ). With these new data, he obtained a doubling dose that was 4 times higher than the 1956 Committee’s central estimate, corresponding to a linear slope 4 times lower. No claim of threshold behavior in the data was made.
As for the idea of a threshold, Neel wrote:
“It should be emphasized that in the search for genetic effects, we pursued the traditional ‘null hypothesis’ namely, that no effect on a particular indicator trait existed unless a statistically significant difference could be demonstrated between the children of control parents and those of radiated parents. However, as already mentioned, in view of the vast body of data regarding the mutagenic effects of radiation, it can scarcely be doubted that the survivors of Hiroshima and Nagasaki sustained genetic damage; as I have stated previously (146), the question is not, ‘Is there damage?’ but rather, ‘Can the damage be detected?’” ( , p. 11).
Although Neel never claimed a threshold, in his autobiography, he held out the possibility that a genetic threshold might exist at very low doses ( ). Thus, there was no censoring of threshold effects in the atomic bomb data because, as had long been known, there was no suggestion of a threshold in the data.

## British report
Calabrese and the HPS authors claim that the parallel British report ( ) reached different conclusions about the atomic bomb data. The HPS authors say: “Neel’s study on atomic bomb survivors was shared with a British committee addressing the same question as the NAS BEAR committee, but the British committee chose to include it in their deliberations and final report.”
True, the British report did discuss in more detail the atomic bomb data that Neel had given them, reporting on their own de novo analysis of it. However, they too adopted LNT for genetic effects.
Furthermore, the British committee concluded that the data suggested not a threshold but an actual effect, despite the “weak statistical significance”: “In our view, however, the data suggest an effect of the bomb radiations on genetic factors in prenatal survival, as shown, by the sex-ratio at birth” ( , p. 41).
So, the British committee actually went further than the U.S. committee. This is another example of selective stakeholder perception on Calabrese’s part. He also did not report that the British Committee, like Neel, was unable to extract a doubling dose estimate: “We cannot, therefore, derive from the Atomic Bomb Casualty Commission's data any estimate of the mutation-rate-doubling radiation dose for man” ( , p. 42).
As for genetic linearity: “There is no known threshold for the induction of gene mutations by radiation” ( , p. 29), and “In this chapter of our report we are chiefly concerned with doses well below this level [25 rem]; but, for the present, there does not appear to be sufficient evidence to warrant the assumption that there is any real departure from this law even at the lowest doses, and proportionality has therefore been accepted as the basis of what follows” ( , p. 29).

## Overlooked sentences in RERF blog on atomic bomb pregnancy outcomes
The HPS authors provide their own example of apparent selective stakeholder perception. Citing a sentence from a blog of the Radiation Effects Research Foundation (RERF), they wrote, “Today, there remain no observable genetic effects in humans resulting from the radiation exposure studies of atomic bomb survivors.”
However, the next sentence from the RERF blog, which the HPS authors apparently overlooked, provides much needed context:
“Given the relatively low average dose to survivors (median doses of about 0.14 Gy for both the fathers and mothers), this result is not surprising. It is consistent, in fact, with the predictions of mouse experiments and suggests that humans are not more radiosensitive with respect to heritable changes” ( ).
Thus, once again, there was no indication of any dose threshold for genetic effects in the early atomic bomb data. I note that, since 2022, the RERF website has slightly changed the sentence quoted by the HPS authors. It now reads, “While high doses in experimental animals can cause various disorders in offspring (birth defects, chromosome aberrations, etc.), no evidence of clinical or subclinical effects has yet been seen in children of atomic bomb survivors.”
The latest reanalysis of the Neel data from RERF ( ) used individual dose estimates rather than distance from the bomb hypocenter and other corrections and fit to a linear dose-response function. Based on these modern improvements in analyzing the old data, the authors concluded:
“The analyses showed that parental exposure to  radiation was associated with increased risk of major congenital malformations and perinatal death, but the estimates were imprecise for direct radiation effects, and most were not statistically significant. Nonetheless, the uniformly positive estimates for untoward pregnancy outcomes among children of both maternal and paternal survivors are useful for risk assessment purposes, although extending them to populations other than the atomic bomb survivors comes with uncertainty as to generalizability” ( ).
Probability (P) values for perinatal death within 14 d were 0.08, 0.12, and 0.03 for maternal, paternal, and conjoint dose, respectively. The P value for major malformations was 0.08 when conjoint dose was used as the exposure metric. These results were called weak evidence in an invited commentary ( ), which is not evidence of “no effect.” The uniformly positive findings are especially telling for a clinical effect being found at some dose, but they do not address the possibility that there might be a threshold at some currently unknown dose.

## 2.2: Supposed suppression of disparate views of geneticists in the 1956 committee report
Calabrese claims that disagreements about risk magnitude were suppressed in the 1956 report: “In response, the Panel hid the disagreement/uncertainty in its Science journal paper and committed scientific misconduct [5, 13, 26]” ( ).
What disagreement was Calabrese talking about? Disagreement about the linear no-threshold model of genetic effects? That is what a reader might expect from the writings of Calabrese and the HPS authors, but that was not the case. The 1956 Committee as a whole accepted the genetics LNT at the beginning of the first meeting, as Calabrese has acknowledged at times (Calabrese 2015a). The committee members did disagree strongly about the magnitude of the damage per unit dose (i.e., the slope of the linear curve), but that is not what the HPS authors and Calabrese are trying to undercut. What Calabrese is essentially claiming is that linearity should be dropped because the 1956 Committee supposedly messed up the slope calculations. That is an illogical conclusion. The inflammatory charges of misconduct by Calabrese are a distraction from the 1956 Committee’s support for treating genetic dose response as linear without a threshold.
What about the differences in the committee about the strength of the radiation effects and the ability to calculate them? The 1956 Committee does not hide the disagreement. It was made clear in the 1956 report that there was disagreement among the committee members on the meaningfulness of this type of calculation:
“Concerning these estimates of total number of  mutants, three things should be said. First, they are clearly not really satisfactory to any geneticist. Too much has to be assumed, too little is dependably known. Second, this kind of estimate is not a meaningful one to certain geneticists. Their principal reservation is doubtless a feeling that, hard as it is to estimate numbers of mutants, it is much harder still, at the present state of knowledge, to translate this over into a recognizable statement of harm to individual persons” ( ; ; at p. 26 and p. 1163, respectively).
The 1956 report goes on to add another reservation of some committee members: “They recognize that there is a risk involved in extrapolating from mouse and Drosophila data to the human case” ( ; ; at p. 26 and 1163, respectively).
The differences among panel members were repeated, not hidden, later in the report:
“This calculation is admittedly somewhat complicated and disappointingly vague. It is, to some geneticists, not a very meaningful way of looking at the problem. To others it adds up to something at least reasonably clear, and in any event very serious” ( ; ; at p. 27 and 1163, respectively).
There was no suppression of the limitations of the calculations, no hiding of their disappointing vagueness nor suppression of disagreements among committee members. This is not surprising, because, as I have repeatedly experienced, the only way to get committees to sign off on NAS reports when there is substantial controversy among members is to state the range of positions. I have been on nine of these committees dealing with a variety of topics, and the approach invariably has been to emphasize what the committee members agree upon and mention disagreements to the extent that committee members think it is necessary for them to sign their names to the report.
Some will wish to see every last detail of disagreement aired ( ), an outcome that can be hidden by an NAS policy to focus on agreement. The problem for committees is that controversy between members tends to draw the public’s attention away from agreed-upon recommendations. Decisions in these matters are judgment calls and can be criticized. They are not scientific misconduct, although reporters and historians may feel frustrated with them ( ). Calabrese bases his claim about suppressed differences, not by references to the text of the 1956 report but to selected unpublished correspondence that led him to the following claim, which is repeated in the HPS article: “Inexplicably, the Science publication asserted that six geneticists took up this challenge when in fact three others refused and another three had their estimates removed…”.
What is inexplicable here is how Calabrese and the HPS authors could have overlooked the clearly stated self-critical language in the 1956 report. Anyone serious about assessing their accusations should read the full report.
This false theory that disagreements were suppressed can be traced to specific Calabrese publications, for instance: “In this way, the Panel [1956 Committee] falsified the record by dropping three estimates, and then reporting that only six geneticists accepted the invitation to provide the assessment (Calabrese 2019a). Given this misrepresentation of fact in the journal Science, the Panel can be seen to have committed scientific misconduct” ( ).
Calabrese is apparently unaware that one of the excluded three committee members (Sewall Wright) did not want his estimate used (Beatty 2006), and the other two did not include all expected mutations in their calculations, so their efforts were reported separately in the report. As I wrote in 2017: “Calabrese apparently wanted the committee to combine all the damage estimates regardless of the fact that two of the estimates were for a different problem (tangible mutations, as opposed to total mutations). In any case, the committee did not suppress the other two calculations; they contribute to a separate estimate for tangible mutations appearing in the report...” ( ).
Calabrese also makes claims about the committee hiding uncertainty, not just disagreements, but he overlooks the self-critical statements in the report, focusing on his own arithmetic calculations of one piece of uncertainty, namely the uncertainty in the calculations of the six geneticists who were willing to submit a number. If one reads the report section where the calculations are called disturbingly vague and not meaningful to some geneticists, it is hard to see how anyone could consider that the 1956 report hid uncertainty.
Calabrese’s claim about uncertainty hinges on a narrow point; it is a technical matter that cannot be resolved by the casual reader. As I have written, Calabrese appears not to accept the fact that the estimates were best considered as geometric means, not arithmetic means, which means the uncertainties were geometric standard deviations that appear smaller to the uninitiated. Anyone wishing to delve into this relatively minor matter can read the contesting papers ( ; ).
In addition, Calabrese overlooked the fact that neither the committee members’ disagreements nor calculations of genetic damage affected any of the NAS Committee’s formal recommendations, despite what Calabrese would have us believe. As James Crow wrote, “Yet there is a supreme irony. The whole rancorous debate had no effect on the specific recommendations” ( ).
Overall, Calabrese’s claims of suppression of either disagreement or uncertainty do not hold up to critical analysis.

## 2.3: Overlooking key sentences in 1947–1949 reports leads to slander of Delta Uphoff and Curt Stern
Calabrese overlooked key sentences in a 1947 document that undermine his claim that Delta Uphoff, who later made important contributions to medical research, was a biased investigator. He similarly overlooks sentences that contradict his claim that Curt Stern, a highly respected geneticist, had engaged in scientific misconduct in a 1949 paper about fruit fly mutations at a dose of 50 R. This 1949 paper about fruit flies was important to geneticists of the time, but it is today irrelevant to cancer dose response because of the epidemiological evidence against a cancer dose threshold at 50 R. Despite this irrelevancy, the charges against Stern play a prominent role in Calabrese’s vision.
What were Calabrese’s accusations? From 2011 to 2018, repeated in nine journal articles, Calabrese said that Curt Stern had labeled Uphoff’s lab work as biased. For example:
“The troubled Stern would go so far as to blame her for having been biased [i.e., ‘may reflect a personal bias of the experimenter’ ( )], with this leading to the low control group values ( ). This phrase was stated in the Discussion of the manuscript that was sent to the Atomic Energy Commission (AEC) (and was immediately classified). This amazing statement should have raised a plethora of questions by the scientific community for Stern and Uphoff, but it was hidden from view. For example, how did the alleged bias start? How long did it continue? How might it have affected other experiments, other team members and others, the data analysis and manuscript write up” ( ).
Calabrese has built his accusations on half of a quotation, overlooking the other half. The first half is rhetorical, not a statement of fact: “The earliest control value is particularly low, and the question may be raised whether at this initial stage of the project it may reflect a personal bias of the experimenter” ( ).
This rhetorical question is answered in the negative in the following sentence: “It is unlikely that this is the case” ( ).
Uphoff and Stern back up this statement by presenting results of a statistical test. The experiment had been broken into separate replications, with the first control value being the lowest. The sequence in numerical order was: 0.052, 0.11, 0.17, 0.20, 0.21, 0.26, and 0.27 percent “lethals.” The low value of 0.052 is in line with expectations (P = 0.03) based on a chi square statistical test, which is reported: “A test for homogeneity of controls resulted in a f 2 value of 13.8 (d.f. = 6; P = 0.03)” ( ).
I also checked for homogeneity of the control data in 2017 using a different method, arriving at the same conclusion ( ). What is confusing here is that, homogenous or not, the total number of controls, adding up the replications, is lower than Uphoff and Stern initially expected. These are two separate issues, both of which are discussed in the 1947 paper.
Calabrese gets the language about possible bias completely backwards and distorted: “Stern wrote in his subsequent report to the U.S. Atomic Energy Commission that the data were uninterpretable, with problems being most likely due to investigator bias” ( , italics added).
To be fair, the actual language in the 1947 report is unusual. Uphoff and Stern could have handled the issue by simply asking a rhetorical question about a possible outlier: “Might the earliest control rate be an outlier?” Also, there are two control rates to keep straight, the first one and the total. Nevertheless, unusual language is not an excuse for misreading it.
The backstory for the language about “personal bias of the experimenter” is not known, and there are too many plausible possibilities to pick one out. Whatever the origin, the authors felt it necessary to present a statistical check, which essentially eliminated concern about the low first control value. Note that in contrast to Calabrese’s implication, the report was declassified, as is stated on the front page of the version that is available today.
A second claim about the 1947 report is also a distortion. According to Calabrese, the report said that the results were “uninterpretable.” Because no such admission appears in Uphoff and Stern’s important 1949 paper in Science, which relied in part on the 1947 data, Calabrese finds a coverup on behalf of a non-threshold model (Calabrese 2017a; ). In fact, the word, “uninterpretable,” which Calabrese sets off in quotes, does not appear in the 1947 report. The authors say no final interpretation can be made due to concerns about the total control value being higher than expected in 1947. In their subsequent 1949 paper, they no longer think the control value is too high ( ), because it is consistent with a second measurement. They indicate that the variance in control rates is high. So Uphoff and Stern are consistent in 1949 in interpreting the 1947 results as another support for the single hit model and considering it as evidence against threshold at 50-R protracted dose. There is no scientific misconduct here. Calabrese has misread the situation and jumped to inflammatory accusations. The HPS authors apparently accepted his claims on faith.
The full quote from Uphoff and Stern in 1947 is: “In view of the former results on chronic irradiation, as well as of the fact that the control rate of the present report is unexpectedly low, a final interpretation of these results cannot be offered. Several alternative interpretations are discussed” ( ).
They gave several alternatives to explain the difference, including chance or one of the two studies being wrong. As Uphoff and Stern wrote, “Additional data are required to decide between the various alternatives.” That data they believed had been delivered by Uphoff in two subsequent experiments. The total control rate seemed low to them in 1947, but not by the time they published the 1949 paper in Science.
By overlooking the answer to the rhetorical question (“It is unlikely to be the case”) and by misconstruing the language about interpretation of results, Calabrese came to unjustified conclusions, embarking on a decades-long posthumous attack on the character of Curt Stern. How were Stern’s ethics viewed by his contemporaries? I quote first from two of his students, whom Calabrese champions over other researchers. James Neel had this to say about Stern:
“His tact and consideration of others were legendary. Yet beneath that gentle exterior was one of the toughest, most uncompromising (on principle) men I have ever met” ( ).
Ernst Caspari had similar positive views:
“While his manner was always friendly and polite, there was a very strong character under the smooth surface…he was generally regarded as a wise, humane, and upright human being in addition to being an outstanding scientist” ( ).
My third quote about Stern’s ethics comes from another memoriam, this one written for the journal Genetics by yet another student of Stern’s, John C. Lucchesi: “Yet he never resorted to, nor did he excuse in others, any relaxation of the exercise of fair play or any subversion of standards, no matter how seemingly innocuous, in order to be the first to proclaim some scientific truth” ( ).
Compare the above words with the wide-ranging character attacks against Stern leveled by Calabrese: Calabrese accused Stern of obfuscations and orchestrating coverup ( ), of manipulation or collusion ( ), of deceptions ( ), of scientific deviousness ( ), of twisted lies to save the LNT single-hit theory ( ), of research, editorial, and scientific community manipulations ( ), of bending his ethical standards ( ), and of conspiring with Muller ( ). This is a relentless barrage of unjustified criticisms, diatribes really, based on a distorted set of false premises and interpretations about Stern and Uphoff that can easily be contradicted by checking the original sources.
Delta Uphoff is not as well-known as other Calabrese-maligned researchers, such as Curt Stern and Ed Lewis, but she too would be praised later in the literature for groundbreaking research. The basic methods used by Uphoff in the fruit-fly research had been outlined in earlier papers, particularly the Caspari paper and in the 1947 declassified Uphoff and Stern report. I, for one, had no problem understanding the methods used, what the weaknesses of the study were, and what the strengths were ( ). Unfortunately, Uphoff’s contributions to medicine and science are now obscured in search engine results, because articles by Calabrese falsely accusing her of bias, solely or in cooperation with Curt Stern, now dominate her mentions on the web. This unfortunate and likely irreversible reputational harm has occurred because Calabrese has made it a practice to publish the same ideas in multiple articles across journals and research fields, which skews search engine results.
What were Delta Uphoff’s accomplishments? In addition to the three fruit-fly experiments, hailed by almost everyone except Calabrese as key experiments in the late 1940s extending the no-threshold idea down to 50 R, she did important research on mice. For instance, as an assistant to Egon Lorenz in 1950, she carried out the first successful experiments showing that radiation sickness could be cured in mice by transplantation of normal bone marrow ( , p. 506).
Uphoff made major contributions to medicine through her research that appeared in two single-authored papers published in 1958 while she was at the National Cancer Institute. The notion that graft-versus-host disease can be eliminated by T-cell depletion of grafts originated from this research ( ). One reason this was so important was that it enabled the treatment of babies with severe combined immune deficiency in the 1980s ( ). In addition, Uphoff was singled out in the Nobel prize address of Donnall Thomas for her findings that methotrexate was able to ameliorate graft-versus-host disease in some strains of mice, leading to Thomas’s own experiments that in turn led to the first successful bone transplants ( ).
Calabrese tries to dig behind the scenes in the various reports and papers of the Stern group to find errors in all but the one study he favors; in this case, Caspari and Stern. I have dug just as deeply as Calabrese into this work ( ), including the statistics, which is one of my research areas ( ), and I find the Uphoff/Stern work to be straightforward and logical. There are limitations as in all studies, and researchers can make different methodological choices. As to limitations, Muller eventually came to believe that the uncertainties were too great at this 50-R dose to reach solid conclusions about mutation rates ( , p. 27), and I agree that is a real possibility. Like more recent researchers, the standard practice is only to consider count rate statistics in computing P values, not wider experimental uncertainties. Fortunately, we no longer need fruit fly data at 50 R to support any kind of dose response for regulation given the wide availability of human data at doses equal to or lower than 50 R.

## 2.4: Overlooking the fact that the 1972 NAS report actually did incorporate the Russells’ dose-rate reduction factor for male mice
Calabrese claims that the National Academies committee that authored the 1972 BEIR report ignored the dose rate dependency of genetic mouse data. That would be incredible, if true, especially since William Russell was a member of the committee. Despite what Calabrese would have us believe, it was not true. The committee discussed cancer dose response and genetic dose response separately, in separate sections. The 1972 Committee adopted the Russell dose-rate findings for mice for genetic effects in humans, with an average over males and females for dose response. As for cancer risk, it is correct that the committee did not adopt a dose rate or other non-linear factor into cancer dose response, but the committee explained its reasoning in the section on cancer risk:
“Although experimental evidence indicates that the dose-effect relationship for x rays and gamma rays may not be a linear function that is invariant with dose and dose rate, the use of a nonlinear hypothesis for estimating risks in support of public policy on radiation protection would be impractical in the present state of knowledge, since it would require consideration of individual variations in temporal and spatial distribution of tissue dose, as well as allowance for other variables which cannot be analyzed at this time…” ( , p. 89).
“At this time, then, the linear hypothesis, which allows the mean tissue dose to be used as the appropriate measure of radiation exposure, provides the only workable approach to numerical estimation of the risk in a population. Further, since there is no means at present of determining the value of the dose-effect slope in the low-dose region of interest, use of the linear extrapolation from data obtained at high doses and dose rates may be justified on pragmatic grounds as a basis for risk estimation…” ( , p. 89).
“It is emphasized that the risk estimates lack precision  but do indicate that the mean dose both to the population and to each individual must be kept as low as practicable…” ( , p. 91).
What a different impression is left, when one actually reads the committee’s reasoning for not incorporating nonlinear effects into cancer dose response. Furthermore, the committee had direct cancer data to analyze, especially data on leukemia, not just mouse genetic data. There is no basis for inferring scientific misconduct.

## 2.5: Overlooking what Muller actually says in his Nobel prize address
Parroting Calabrese, the HPS article ( ) claims scientific misconduct by Muller: “Most visible was when Muller misled the Nobel Prize audience in his December 1946 acceptance lecture by stating there is ‘no escape from the conclusion that there is no threshold dose…’. ”
This partial quotation refers to the only sentence I could find in the entire speech about threshold. The few words left out of the quotation, specifically, “...we believe...”, are quite significant and change the essence of the remark. Referring to his research findings in collaboration with colleagues, his statement was as follows: “They leave, we believe, no escape from the conclusion that there is no threshold dose…” ( , p. 4).
Thus, in his Nobel Prize address, Muller clearly stated a belief. He did not make a statement of scientific fact in this 1946 presentation. Who can doubt he believed in linearity down to zero dose, any more than anyone can doubt that Calabrese believes in radiation hormesis, despite lack of evidence accepted by the scientific community? Muller spoke the truth in stating what he believed. That is not scientific misconduct.
By 1948, Muller would drop the “we believe” caveat in a presentation he made trying to convince doctors to reduce high doses in medical practice ( ), but this was 2 y after his Nobel Prize address.
Actually, as I have shown ( ), the Caspari and Stern study that Calabrese thinks should have led Muller to change his views is really not that different from the others carried out by the Stern group. Specifically, the two 24-d Caspari and Uphoff studies are not inconsistent. Both get positive effects, and their confidence intervals overlap such that the difference is not inconsistent with zero. The Stern group eventually treated all their experiments as valid, which is what scientists do when they can’t find something disqualifying for any of them.
As a sidenote, it is not scientific misconduct to publish work in journals that do not require external peer review, which was almost always the case in the historical period about which Calabrese writes ( ; ), including for Science Magazine. The external peer-review process we know today did not become standard in the U.S. until the 1970s ( ). Research standards vary over time, but replication remains the gold standard. Muller’s production of mutations by x rays, for which he won the Nobel Prize in 1946, was replicated and published ( ; ; ; ) within approximately 1 y of his 1927 Science publication ( ). He presented details of his method at scientific meetings: “In the summer of 1927 MULLER gave a major paper at the Fifth International Congress of Genetics in Berlin…. The talk is said to have been confusing, but the message was clear. This time he gave the full details and the skeptics were silenced” ( ).
A summary of Muller’s talk was published in Nature ( ). Muller also presented details at a U.S. meeting (Muller 1927a). A reader would never find these or any other defenses of Muller in Calabrese’s articles. The HPS authors should have let readers know that Muller’s biographer has defended him, arguing that Calabrese’s “characterization of Muller and his supporters is unjust, misleading, and hurtful” ( ).
Calabrese has published historical papers since the video series appeared, including new attacks on the integrity of Hermann Muller ( ; ). The substance of the new material is not directly relevant to my specific criticisms in this article. Given the frequency of historical errors identified here and the absence of statistical methods, it would be prudent to maintain a skeptical attitude toward claims of misconduct in new papers until modifications of research methods have been made, including the addition of statistical checks to support claims about count data.
As an example, consider a 2023 paper that claims Herman Muller did not deserve his Nobel prize for gene mutations ( ). The claim sets up a false premise, because Muller’s prize was not for gene mutations but for mutations alone, which were known to include many types, including deletions ( ; ; ). Although Muller always argued that his induced mutations included more than deletions and losses, the Nobel Committee stayed out of the controversy, awarding him a prize for inducing mutations in general. The lead sentence of the award reads: “Prize motivation: ‘for the discovery of the production of mutations by means of X-ray irradiation’” (NobelPrize.org 1946).

## 2.6: False claim that the 1956 NAS committee engaged in financial misconduct
The 1956 Committee accepted the linear no-threshold (LNT) model for mutations at the start of their first meeting in 1955 and never wavered, although arguing vehemently about the slope of the linear rise. Thus, the idea that Warren Weaver’s promise of potential funding from the Rockefeller Foundation, made in 1956 at the second meeting, influenced support for the LNT is inconsistent with the timing. Furthermore, as described above in 2.2, the Committee’s report made clear that the numerical calculations presented by six geneticists were very soft and were not thought meaningful by some other members of the group.
In their biography of George Beadle, who was a member of the 1956 Committee and soon-to-be Nobel Prize winner, the author/scientists Maxine Singer and Paul Berg attributed Weaver’s “bribe” offer in the second meeting as reflecting Weaver’s determination to get a report finished ( ). As I know from my own experience on such committees, a major job of the chair of an NAS committee is to get members to sign off on a report.
As Bentley Glass, the rapporteur for the committee, wrote in a 1991 commentary: “Weaver's contribution to this momentous scientific report remains, I believe, the very greatest scientific contribution of his own life, a fitting crown to the solid work he had done as the officer of The Rockefeller Foundation who undertook to launch ‘molecular biology’ by providing crucial support in the days before great government agencies took over the primary role of funding pure science in America and elsewhere” ( ).
Singer and Berg certainly did not attribute scientific misconduct to Weaver’s management of the committee nor to any other aspect of the 1956 committee.
The 1956 committee report did contain a paragraph at its end ( ; ) claiming that funding for radiation genetics was out of balance with respect to funding for atomic and nuclear physics, but it would be a rare group of researchers, in my experience, whose members did not think their field deserved more funding.
Charges of financial conflicts can be made about most researchers because they need funding to produce results. Leaders of research teams need to convince funding sources that their research is worthy. If a detractor says a researcher modified views to get funding, there may be little way to prove innocence to the public from such a charge, which may have been based on speculation. Somewhat ironically, Calabrese himself is not immune to such speculation ( ), given his funding from industry sources. Fortunately for Calabrese, he can respond to charges about character ( ), unlike the dead scientists whose reputations have borne the brunt of his unfounded criticisms.
There are four trained historians who have studied this committee in detail ( ; ; ; ; see also ). Although they have some complaints about the committee, along with some praise, none of them called any committee actions scientific misconduct, according to my digital search of their work. One of the historians, in his doctoral thesis ( ), expresses surprise at a 1957 letter by a famous geneticist, Theodore Dobzhansky, to the effect that he didn’t mind stretching a point: “Let us be honest with ourselves—we are both interested in genetics research, and for the sake of it we are willing to stretch a point when necessary. But let us not stretch it to the breaking point! Overstatements are sometimes dangerous, since they result in their opposites when they approach the levels of absurdity” ( , p. 304).
Calabrese has quoted Dobzhansky’s remarks as if they contaminated the 1956 report ( ). However, Dobzhansky did not serve on the 1956 Committee, and his remarks were made after the report was published. Nevertheless, this comment by Dobzhansky forms the basis for one of the major accusations about 1956 Committee members, namely that they had spun the science for funding purposes, as is implied in the HPS article: “Personal letters among the NAS BEAR genetics panelists and transcripts of their meetings showed that some members of the radiation genetics community had a vested interest in maintaining this ideology so that they would continue to receive research funding” ( ).
Although Dobzhansky was certainly a “member of the radiation genetics community,” he was not part of the 1956 Committee, as implied by the above language.

## Question 3. How did past peer reviewers miss the overlooking of historical text identified in this article?
In establishing scientific credibility for a theory, peer review is a preliminary hurdle prior to replication. Peer review does not guarantee every part of an article to be true. Peer-reviewed papers regularly fail to replicate, in part or in full. Reviewing Calabrese’s claims about scientific misconduct is a special challenge for those journals in which Calabrese publishes, which are not to my knowledge in the field of history of science, which is where articles generally appear concerning the people he writes about. Nor does he publish his historical claims about radiation in radiation journals. These factors may have hindered the identification of specialists in the history of radiation science who have the time to serve as reviewers of Calabrese’s claims.
Furthermore, it is assumed in peer review that authors correctly report their findings in raw data, which in Calabrese’s case are findings or their absence in historical documents. Unless there are apparent inconsistencies, a reviewer will generally trust author statements about raw data. To do otherwise in Calabrese’s case, reviewers would need to ask for, and read, key historical documents about which extraordinary claims are made.
I am sympathetic to the difficulties involved in peer reviewing Calabrese’s historical statements. A reviewer of Calabrese’s historical work is faced with evaluating premises that are based on statements about historical documents. If the premises are correct, they make his conclusions reasonable. If the premises are incorrect, as is often the case here, the manuscript should be sent back for revision. However, unless a reviewer is well-versed in the relevant documentary history, it would not be feasible to read the raw documents to be sure they are used appropriately. Nor can reviewers be expected to find relevant uncited material.
Reviewers are volunteers and generally do not have the time to look for Calabrese-type errors in extensive original texts. A reviewer can search for analyses that others have made. However, a web search may not be fruitful, because Calabrese has saturated the search engines with so much repetitive and self-cited material that page ranks for critiques of his work are low. As a result, critiques of his work do not stand out. Finally, if Calabrese in a submitted manuscript cites to a prior publication as the basis for a premise, a reviewer may have no basis for requiring a revision. Thus, once a few papers make it to publication, the cascade of additional repetitions based on self-citation is not surprising.
Calabrese’s charges of scientific misconduct have never been replicated, only criticized ( ; ; , ; ). I am aware of only one claim by Calabrese for which someone else ( ) came independently to the same conclusions based on original historical sources: “Muller’s actions have also been recently reviewed by Kesavan (24) who found that he made selective citations in his Nobel Prize lecture to buttress the LNT model” ( ).
However, Kesavan based his conclusions on two Calabrese papers, their wording and their citations ( , ), which makes paper for Calabrese mostly an indirect self-citation: “In his two recent papers, Calabrese3,4 concludes that Muller ignored the scientific papers which had indeed revealed a ‘threshold’ dose for the genetic effects and chose only those which supported his ideology” ( ).
By itself, the lack of replication after so many years and Calabrese’s reliance on self-citation should be warning signs to future reviewers. Hopefully, the types of textual errors I have identified here will help future reviewers of his historical papers insist on seeing digital keyword searches or evidence of some other objective strategy to justify a claim of what was, or was not, covered in reports, articles, and correspondence. In general, reviewers and editors might follow Elof Carlson’s advice to find “...a proper venue for discussions of a scientist’s personality or mental habits as a scientist” ( ).
According to Carlson, “These might more properly be submitted to journals of the history, sociology, or philosophy of science where both editors and referees are more familiar with this aspect of science” ( ).

## SUMMARY AND CONCLUSION
In their recounting and support of Edward Calabrese’s historical claims ( ), previous and current leaders of the Health Physics Society (HPS) endorsed a narrative that accuses prominent deceased researchers of scientific misconduct. In essence, the HPS authors endorsed a prosecutor’s case, made by a stakeholder who is a prominent opponent of linearity in dose response and a prominent proponent of radiation hormesis. Not surprisingly, their article lacks a balanced perspective, as no defense was allowed in the HPS authors’ article despite the availability of such defense in the literature.
Like any stakeholder adhering to different theories, Calabrese and the HPS authors are free to attack past research. However, science-based arguments have not been enough for them. They wish us to believe that dose threshold models have been rejected for regulation in the past for nefarious reasons, not because they have been found unconvincing or impractical for regulation. Dose threshold has been rejected, they claim, because of betrayal by corrupt researchers who falsified the record out of financial and professional interest or engaged in scientific misconduct. These supposed betrayers include three Nobel-prize winners in genetics, one woman who materially contributed to the work on which a Nobel in medicine depended, noted researchers, multiple college presidents, two presidents of the AAAS, as well as scientists active in operations research during World War II. Thus, Calabrese’s villains are a who’s-who of the 1950–1960s scientific establishment ( ). Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. In this case, the evidence evaporates upon close scrutiny. The misconduct allegations by Calabrese and the HPS authors that are analyzed in this article are not supported by the original text, leading to false premises that have persisted in Calabrese’s papers since at least 2014. These are examples of what I have called, “selective stakeholder perception.” Calabrese’s rhetoric, often relying on a tone of righteousness and moral superiority, can be so powerful that the initial premise about what was, or was not, done by historical figures gets forgotten and goes unchallenged.
Some of these false premises have led to a furor over a supposed dose-response threshold for mutations in fruit-fly data at 50 R. This fruit fly debate is not even about a dose threshold or about low doses. What does a furor at 50 R have to do with the HPS authors’ proposal of a dose threshold at 10 R? We are not told.
The older papers were pathbreaking for their time but have been superseded by later research. Neither Calabrese nor the HPS authors mention that there are enormous human datasets that tell us about excess cancer at 50 R, both acute and protracted. There is no cancer dose threshold in these datasets at 50 R. There is a broad consensus that any cancer dose threshold will lie below 10 R. Clearly, the fruit fly data cannot be suggesting that there is a cancer dose threshold for humans at 50 R.
Even if 50-R data were relevant, the charges of scientific misconduct in the fruit fly experiments of Curt Stern and Delta Uphoff are obviously false based on textual evidence. Stern and Uphoff did not say that their 1947 report involved investigator bias, just the opposite; and by 1949, when their paper in Science appeared, they had resolved their concern about the total control rate in the experiment reported in 1947, given the results of later work. Thus, there is no basis for savaging Stern, as Calabrese does repeatedly in his papers, claiming that Stern misled the world about the 1947 declassified report, thereby falsifying the research record. Stern was considered a man of integrity by those who knew him in and outside of the lab ( ; ; ).
Most of the other errors I found involve false claims of actions taken or not taken by the 1956 NAS committee. Calabrese claimed that the committee censored the record in the interest of future financial gain, which it did not. No censoring occurred, as shown by examination of the actual committee report.
The atomic bomb genetic results? It turns out that the 1956 NAS Committee did discuss early atomic bomb results at meetings and did mention them in their report. As for conflicting views on the committee about the magnitude of the linear risk, they are also found in the text. Furthermore, the report does not suppress certain “low” numerical values, as Calabrese suggested. They were presented in a separate section of the report and for good reason: the two low estimates only covered first-generation damage, unlike the others that went out to 10 generations. Having overlooked all this information, Calabrese charges the committee with scientific misconduct, repeating the charge over the years in 25 published papers by my count.
Furthermore, the early atomic bomb genetic data did not, as Calabrese and the HPS authors claim, show or suggest a threshold dose response. This was stated repeatedly in print by the lead author, James Neel, who was on the 1956 Committee. The Committee’s report used the early Japanese data from Hiroshima and Nagasaki to set a crude limit on the doubling dose for genetic health effects. The data, limited to a narrow slice of genetic outcomes, did not have sufficient statistical power to allow anything more. In his 1994 autobiography, Neel praised the report that Calabrese denigrates: “The result (I am prejudiced) was a document which for clarity at all levels has not been superseded by any of the many subsequent reports” ( ).
How could the language in the 1956 NAS report itself, matching language in a contemporaneous British report, and the statements of Neel and others have been overlooked by Calabrese? Whatever the explanation, I label these oversights as examples of selective stakeholder perception.
It is worth asking: if the 1956 U.S. committee members were engaged in scientific misconduct and changed their views to help themselves financially in hopes of more funding, how can it be that the British committee came to essentially the same conclusions at the same time, including acceptance of no-threshold linearity? Were they, too, corrupt? Or did the views of both committees fairly reflect the views of geneticists of the day, whereas Calabrese’s views do not reflect radiation geneticists’ views then or now ( )?
In the 1972 NAS report, despite Calabrese claims to the contrary, the Committee did present the latest mouse data on genetic effects. In fact, they applied what would be called today, a dose-rate-effectiveness factor, which they based on data for mice obtained by William and Liane Russell at Oak Ridge. On the other hand, the Committee did not incorporate the same genetic dose-rate-effectiveness factor in their cancer assessment for pragmatic reasons, as clearly stated. Calabrese appears to have mixed up the two dose-response cases considered, mutations and cancer.
As a result of these and other text errors, Calabrese has unfortunately brought the politics of betrayal and blame into regulatory science. The HPS leadership has gone along, handing out professional credit, according to the video website, for watching these video interviews of Calabrese that promote falsehoods about historical events and personalities.
A lesson here is that Calabrese’s historical claims about people, articles, and reports must always be treated with great skepticism, particularly his claims about what was not said or done.
Calabrese and the HPS authors have an important role to play in challenging conventional wisdom about dose response, but when errors creep into their work and remain unresolved for long periods of time, their credibility will suffer. While contrary views are needed in the scientific enterprise to help guard against hardening of the intellectual arteries, conspiracy theories and character assassination ( ) are not needed. I believe that Calabrese and the Health Physics leadership of Cardarelli, Burk, and Hamrick were all doing what they thought was best for science and society, as were Curt Stern, Herman Muller, and Delta Uphoff. The scientific enterprise, including regulatory science, has its contested territory. No villains need to be created to explain strong disagreements among researchers and within advisory committees.
The challenge now is to correct the record and prevent such errors from happening in future historical work. Mistakes are unavoidable in a body of work but need to be corrected when identified. Calabrese has great rhetorical skills. He could almost convince you that the world is flat and the scientists who said otherwise in the old days engaged in scientific misconduct, avoided peer review, and their studies had grave errors, which they knew and hid from the public.
With great power comes great responsibility. Going forward,
Calabrese should publish corrections to papers. He should reformulate his criticisms without reliance on the false premises on which much of his historical work heretofore has been based. This would include correcting false impressions given about the work of the 1956 committee and Uphoff and Stern. If this is not done, journals should consider the advisability of issuing statements of editorial concern about articles that appear to slander historical figures because key sections of original text were overlooked.
He should present digital keyword search strategies that underlie statements about what was or was not written in historical documents. Reviewers should insist on such evidence.
Ideally, Calabrese would apologize in a thoughtful retraction to his readers and to individuals whose relatives, mentors, or friends were falsely attacked in any way.
He could benefit from hiring someone without a stake in the dose-response debate to check his claims about reports, papers, and correspondence, particularly claims of omission and claims about concepts like thresholds that have a complex literature.
The HPS authors have recommended that everyone view the videos and make up their own minds. How can viewers make up their minds when they have only been presented with a one-sided, prosecutor’s case about scientific misconduct?
The HPS authors should:
Make clear that they did not check the original documents adequately, which is apparently the case.
Update their article and the website of the video series to indicate that questions have been raised about the factual accuracy and relevance to more recent dose-response assessments of many of Calabrese’s historical claims. An errata sheet would be helpful.
Provide references and links to this commentary, to earlier publications, and later, to other properly documented commentaries that they receive in the future that differ from the current website or support it.
Arrange for corrections, references, and links to be provided to anyone who obtained professional credit for viewing the interviews of Calabrese.
Recommend that the video series be renamed, “The early history of the LNT by Professor Edward Calabrese.”
It is time to move on beyond these false claims of scientific misconduct and keep focus on scientific and philosophical arguments about best regulatory policy.
