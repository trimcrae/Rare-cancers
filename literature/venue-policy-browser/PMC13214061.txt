## Abstract

## Background
Parenting interventions in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) have largely focused on mothers, with limited attention to fathers, family relationship dynamics, and gender norms that influence caregiving. To address this gap, we co-designed Familia Bora, a multicomponent, father-inclusive parenting program for couples with young children. The program integrates gender-transformative content on parenting, couples’ relationships, mental health, and gender equality to support both mothers and fathers and in turn promote early childhood development (ECD). This paper describes the protocol for a hybrid type 1 effectiveness-implementation pilot cluster-randomized controlled trial (RCT) evaluating Familia Bora, delivered by community health workers (CHWs) in rural Tanzania.

## Methods
Thirty-three villages in Misungwi and Sengerema districts in Mwanza Region, Tanzania were randomly assigned to receive the Familia Bora program or continue standard-of-care services. In each village, 12 couples with a child aged 0–24 months were randomly selected, for a total of 396 enrolled father-mother-child triads. The intervention will be delivered over 17 weekly sessions by trained CHWs in community group sessions with fathers and mothers participating together throughout. The primary outcome is maternal and paternal engagement in stimulation activities with their young child. Secondary outcomes include various measures of caregiving as reported by mothers and fathers, including parenting attitudes, discipline practices, couples’ relationship quality, parenting stress, gender attitudes, and ECD. Analyses will follow an intention-to-treat approach using difference-in-differences to compare changes in outcome scores from baseline to endline between the intervention and control groups. A mixed-methods process evaluation will also be conducted alongside the RCT to assess program fidelity, quality, reach, adoption, potential mechanisms of change, and contextual factors influencing implementation. The process evaluation will draw on multiple data sources, including routine quantitative program monitoring tools, session observations, and longitudinal qualitative in-depth interviews with mothers, fathers, CHWs, and group leaders.

## Discussion
This study will generate robust evidence on the effects of a multicomponent, couples-based parenting intervention intentionally co-designed to engage both mothers and fathers in the Tanzanian cultural context. The evaluation will examine outcomes at a family-systems level, including impacts on mothers, fathers, couples, and young children. Findings will inform refinements to strengthen program delivery and lay the groundwork for a larger-scale effectiveness trial focused on evaluating effectiveness for ECD outcomes. If successful, Familia Bora has the potential to serve as a scalable model that jointly engages both mothers and fathers, supports parenting holistically by addressing couples’ relationships, caregiver mental health, and gender equality, while building CHW capacity to deliver complex parenting programs. Overall, this study will advance the design and evaluation of father-inclusive, multicomponent parenting programs and inform policy, practice, and future research in Tanzania and similar low-resource settings.

## Background
Globally, an estimated 43% of children younger than 5 years are at risk of not reaching their full developmental potential, with the greatest regional burden in sub-Saharan Africa (66%) [ ]. Nurturing care – including opportunities for early learning, responsive caregiving, adequate nutrition, and safety and security – is central to promoting early childhood development (ECD) [ ]. However, in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), poverty, family stressors, and entrenched gender inequities constrain parents’ capacities to provide nurturing care.
Parenting interventions are well established as effective for improving nurturing care and ECD outcomes in LMICs [ , ]. Yet, most programs have concentrated narrowly on select aspect of parent-child relationships, such as play, stimulation, or provision of learning materials, and have also exclusively targeted mothers [ ]. Fathers remain largely excluded from parenting programs, despite the fact that the majority of children in LMICs live with both parents [ ]. Moreover, few interventions have broadened caregiving support during early childhood beyond early learning to include the prevention of harsh discipline or adopted a family systems perspective that recognizes the interconnections between parenting, co-parenting, couples’ relationships, and caregiver wellbeing as key determinants of ECD [ ].
In sub-Saharan Africa, evidence shows that fathers are increasingly engaging in childcare activities such as play and communication with young children [ ]. Fathers’ support for their partners – such as through shared caregiving, participation in household chores, and emotional support – has also been recognized as a culturally meaningful dimension of fatherhood [ ]. At the same time, restrictive gender norms and patriarchal values continue to shape how parenting roles are defined and distributed between mothers and fathers [ , ]. In Tanzania, these dynamics are reflected in findings showing that while most mothers engage in daily stimulation with young children, less than half of fathers participate in such activities [ ]. At a programmatic and policy level, parenting interventions largely remain mother-focused and rarely address broader family relationship dynamics or gender equity [ ]. There is a critical need for holistic, father-inclusive parenting interventions in Tanzania that integrate gender-transformative approaches to strengthen parenting, improve couple relationships, enhance caregiver wellbeing, and ultimately support young children’s development.
To address this gap, Emory University and the Tanzania Home Economics Association (TAHEA-Mwanza) co-developed Familia Bora (“Good Family”), a multicomponent parenting program for couples with young children designed to improve maternal and paternal caregiving and ECD. The intervention was developed from 2022-2024 using an evidence-based, participatory, iterative process with fathers, mothers, and community stakeholders [ ]. The intervention features mixed-gender group sessions and integrates content on responsive caregiving and early learning, child nutrition and health, couples’ relationships, and caregiver mental health, with gender-transformative principles embedded throughout. A pre-post feasibility pilot with 60 couples in 2024 demonstrated high acceptability and preliminary improvements in maternal and paternal parenting, gender attitudes, couple relationships, and mental health [ ].
Building upon this foundational work, we will conduct a mixed-methods hybrid type 1 effectiveness-implementation pilot cluster-randomized controlled trial (RCT) of Familia Bora in Mwanza, Tanzania. In this trial, the intervention will be delivered through existing community health workers (CHWs) to assess real-world effectiveness and implementation processes. The specific objectives are to (1) estimate intervention effects on maternal and paternal caregiving and related family-level outcomes, and (2) evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and quality of delivery by CHWs. Findings will inform program refinement and scale-up strategies through the CHW platform, while generating preliminary evidence to support a future, fully powered trial evaluating impacts on ECD outcomes.

## Methods

## Trial design
We will conduct a hybrid type 1 cluster-RCT to test the effectiveness and implementation of Familia Bora, our newly co-developed, father-inclusive parenting intervention delivered by CHWs in Mwanza, Tanzania. Thirty-three villages will be randomized in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention (17 villages) or a standard-of-care control arm (16 villages) using a stratified design by district with blocking by ward. In each village, 12 couples (fathers and mothers of children aged 0–24 months) will be recruited, for a total sample of 396 couples (i.e., 396 fathers and 396 mothers). Quantitative outcome data will be collected from the study cohort at baseline and endline (i.e., post-intervention, designated as the primary endpoint, occurring approximately 5 to 6 months after baseline) to compare changes in maternal and paternal outcomes between trial arms and over time.
To evaluate program implementation, we will draw on routine monitoring data, pre/post surveys with CHWs, and qualitative interviews with various stakeholders at midline and endline. This design enables evaluation of both intervention effectiveness and the implementation processes that may shape program impact. The trial is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, #NCT07025447.

## Study setting
This study will be conducted in Misungwi and Sengerema districts of Mwanza Region, northwestern Tanzania. Mwanza Region is the second most populous region in Tanzania. Both Misungwi and Sengerema border Lake Victoria and are predominantly rural districts located to the south and west of Mwanza city, respectively, and approximately 1–2 h by road from the TAHEA office. The majority of households primarily engage in subsistence farming, with other common sources of livelihood including fishing, small-scale trade, livestock rearing, mining, and seasonal employment in nearby towns [ ]. Educational attainment in Mwanza Region is relatively low, with an estimated 25% of men and 20% of women having completed secondary school [ ]. Indicators for early child outcomes are also poor in Mwanza: 28% of children below 5 years of age are stunted, 52% of children aged 2–5 years are considered developmentally off-track, and access to early child care and education centers is lacking [ ]. Childcare responsibilities are also highly divided between couples, with mothers providing the majority of daily caregiving at home, while fathers are less frequently involved due to restrictive gender norms [ ].
In Tanzania, the CHW workforce has been expanding and gradually becoming more formalized as part of the government’s community-based health program. Although it remains a largely part-time and dependent donor funding and volunteerism, the government is undertaking efforts to strengthen and formalize the CHW cadre through improved training, supervision, and the provision of regular monthly stipends under the Integrated and Coordinated Community Health Worker (ICCHW) program, which is being rolled out in selected regions and districts from 2024-2028. At the time of this study, Misungwi and Sengerema districts are not included in the rollout. 
 Under the current national model, each village typically has at least two CHWs (one male and one female CHW) who are trained, recognized, and supervised by local government health authorities [ ]. Their roles include promoting maternal and child health, supporting immunization and nutrition programs, and facilitating referrals to health facilities. CHWs have also demonstrated the capacity to deliver parenting support in other recent programs in Tanzania, such as in Mara [ ] and Tabora [ ]. Given their established presence in villages and trusted relationships with caregivers of young children, CHWs are well positioned to serve as delivery agents for Familia Bora in Mwanza [ ].
The trial will be conducted in partnership with TAHEA-Mwanza, a community-based organization founded in 1980 with deep roots in Mwanza Region. TAHEA has extensive experience implementing programs in parenting, nutrition, early learning, women’s empowerment, and community health, in collaboration with local government, other community-based organization, and international NGOs. Building on a four-year research-practice partnership with Emory University (2022-2025), including formative research, intervention co-design, rapid pretesting, and a pilot feasibility study for Familia Bora, TAHEA brings critical knowledge of the local context and will lead the implementation of this trial, including training, supervision, and program monitoring.

## Sampling plan
Misungwi and Sengerema districts in Mwanza Region were purposively selected in consultation with government stakeholders to ensure geographic variability while maintaining feasibility of implementation. These districts were prioritized because they were relatively accessible, and to our knowledge, no other NGOs were currently delivering parenting programs using CHWs in these areas.
Prior to sampling, the team conducted sensitization meetings with district, ward-level, and village-level government stakeholders in both Misungwi and Sengerema. These meetings provided an opportunity to describe the study objectives and sampling procedures, and to obtain official approval for the study. Stakeholders confirmed the feasibility of the proposed design, the availability of CHWs to support implementation, the absence of overlapping large-scale programs, and their willingness to facilitate community entry and household recruitment. These consultations confirmed the feasibility of conducting the trial in both districts and secured government support for its implementation.
A sampling frame of eligible wards and villages was developed for each district in collaboration with local government officials. Wards located within a two-hour drive from TAHEA’s office were considered for inclusion. Slightly more wards were selected from Misungwi than from Sengerema due to its closer proximity to TAHEA’s office and greater logistical feasibility. Ultimately, ten wards in Misungwi were randomly selectedand stratified by administrative division at the request of district stakeholders to ensure geographic representation. In Sengerema, six wards were randomly selected.
Within each selected ward, two villages were chosen and randomly assigned to either the intervention or control group. To minimize contamination, selected villages within the same ward were required to be at least 4 km apart and not directly neighboring. If this condition was not met, the randomization procedure was repeated within the ward. In Sengerema, an additional ward containing one sampled village was included in the intervention arm as a precaution, given that one of the originally selected intervention villages was located directly along the lakeshore and could potentially present logistical challenges. This resulted in a final sampling frame of 33 villages across 17 wards: 10 intervention villages and 10 control villages across 10 wards in Misungwi, and 7 intervention and 6 control villages across 7 wards in Sengerema.

## Eligibility criteria and recruitment
Following village selection and random allocation to intervention or control arms, CHWs were recruited as delivery agents of the intervention. District CHW coordinators recommended initial candidates, and study staff independently visited each to assess eligibility. CHWs were required to meet the following eligibility criteria: (1) completion of at least Standard VII education; (2) a minimum of two years of experience as a CHW; (3) good standing within the community with no criminal record; and (4) provision of informed consent and agreement to TAHEA’s child protection policy. CHWs of any age were considered eligible. During eligibility verification visits, CHWs’ interpersonal skills and overall suitability for program delivery were informally assessed, but no candidates was excluded. All approached CHWs met eligibility requirements and were retained for the trial.
Once CHWs were enrolled, household recruitment commenced. Village leaders and CHWs jointly compiled initial census lists of families with at least one child under 2 years of age. These lists were reviewed by the research team for completeness and accuracy, after which village leaders and CHWs updated them as needed. An independent researcher at Emory then applied simple random sampling in Excel to generate a randomized, ranked household recruitment list for each village.
Project staff and enumerators subsequently visited households in a randomized sequence to verify eligibility and invite participation. Male and female caregivers in partnered relationships were each met in person and separately to assess eligibility and obtain informed consent. Eligibility criteria were as follows: (1) each individual was an adult aged 18–65 years; (2) each individual was the primary female or male caregiver of a child younger than 2 years; (3) the two individuals were currently in a marital or partnered relationship with each other; (4) both individuals had resided in the same household as each other and the child during the past month; and (5) both individuals provided written informed consent to participate. Child date of birth and age were verified using birth registration, mother-child booklets, or other available records. Couples were excluded if they anticipated migrating outside the study area during the trial period or if either caregiver declined participation. Enumerators provided detailed information about the study objectives, procedures, roles, and potential risks and benefits.
At enrollment, enumerators collected basic demographic information, caregiver contact details, household location, and any potential scheduling constraints relevant for baseline data collection. Recruitment continued until 12 households per village were enrolled, with each household comprising a father-mother-index child triad. This target of 12 households per village was informed by prior pilot experience and evidence from similar projects indicating that 12 households is feasible within typical villages and represents a manageable group size for couples-based parenting interventions [ , ]. In total, 396 father-mother-child triads were enrolled.

## Randomization and blinding
The unit of randomization was the village. A total of 33 villages were randomly allocated at a 1:1 ratio using computer-generated random numbers in Stata 16 to either the parenting intervention arm (17 villages) or a standard-of-care control arm (16 villages). Randomization was stratified by district and blocked by ward, with villages randomized to study arms after selection within each ward. Randomization of villages was done prior to study initiation by a research assistant who was not directly involved in study procedures. Caregivers were recruited after randomization. Due to the nature of the community-based social and behavior change intervention, it is not possible to blind participants, CHWs, or the implementation team to the intervention group assignment. Survey enumerators and data analysts will be blinded to treatment group assignment.

## Intervention description

Familia Bora is a multicomponent, couples-based, group parenting intervention designed to strengthen caregiving and nurturing care practices of both fathers and mothers, improve couples’ relationships dynamics, support caregiver mental health, and promote gender equity, with the ultimate goal of improving ECD. We co-designed Familia Bora between 2022 and 2024 through a research-practice partnership between TAHEA and Emory and in consultation with mothers, fathers, and community stakeholders in Mwanza [ ]. We followed a six-step process to develop the intervention. First, we conducted two systematic reviews to synthesize evidence on the effectiveness and implementation of father-inclusive caregiving interventions in LMICs, identifying effective strategies, delivery models, and evidence gaps to guide our design [ , ]. Second, we carried out formative research using in-depth interviews and focus group discussions with fathers, mothers, and community stakeholders in Mwanza to identify barriers and enablers to father engagement and to prioritize culturally-relevant and feasible content in our curriculum [ ]. Third, we collaboratively co-developed draft session content and program implementation strategies through participatory methods with community members to ensure cultural relevance and a gender-transformative focus. Fourth, we pretested draft sessions with fathers and mothers and refined them iteratively based on participant feedback, producing a contextually feasible and acceptable curriculum and identifying effective strategies to enhance engagement. Fifth, we consolidated the curriculum and materials into a manualized set of sessions and finalized the implementation model and delivery strategies to ensure efficiency and consistency. Finally, we developed a theory of change linking intervention activities and behavior-change techniques to expected outcomes.
Between July and November 2024, we conducted a pre-post pilot of Familia Bora in Ilemela district in Mwanza to assess program fidelity, satisfaction, and initial changes in participant outcomes. Findings from the pilot indicated that the program was highly accepted by both fathers and mothers, with strong support for its content, format and delivery approach [ ]. Participants also reported positive changes in parenting behaviors, couples’ relationship quality, gender equity, and mental health. At the same time, it identified opportunities to strengthen content, enhance participant engagement, and improve the preparation and training materials for CHW-led delivery, which were incorporated into the refined intervention.
The main version of the intervention being tested in this trial consists of a 17-session, weekly, in-person, group-based program facilitated by CHWs and delivered over approximately 4 months. In each village, 12 couples with a young child under 2 years of age will be enrolled to form one parenting group. Group sessions will be held at community locations selected by participants and scheduled at times preferred and agreed upon by group members to reduce barriers to attendance for both mothers and fathers while promoting community decision-making. Each group session is expected to last approximately 1.5 h.
CHWs will deliver the intervention using a structured, manualized curriculum. The Familia Bora curriculum is multicomponent and addresses a broad range of interconnected topics related to caregiver, child, and family wellbeing, including responsive caregiving, positive discipline, early child learning, child nutrition, caregiver mental health, couples communication, conflict resolution, and gender equity. Table provides an overview of the sessions in the curriculum. Some sessions are more focused on parents and couples, covering topics such as parenting stress, couples’ communication, and conflict resolution; while other sessions are child-focused and emphasize child engagement during the session by guiding caregivers to practice responsive interactions, early learning activities, and age-appropriate play. The parenting and child-focused content and strategy was adapted from and expanded upon the UNICEF/WHO Care for Child Development (CCD) approach, which promotes responsive caregiving and early learning by strengthening caregiver-child play and communication and enhancing the caregivers' sensitivity, responsiveness, and ability to understand and respond to child cues. Familia Bora further extends CCD by offering a more structured, session-based curriculum with in-session demonstrations, active coaching on age-specific serve-and-return interactions, and iterative practice with feedback to scaffold skill-building and support sustained improvements in responsive caregiving. The program also incorporates locally developed media (i.e., images, videos) to illustrate responsive caregiving and explicitly engages both mothers and fathers in promoting responsive caregiving. Taken together, by intentionally integrating parent-focused and child-focused sessions, Familia Bora provides a holistic approach to strengthening families, parent-child interactions, and ECD. Across all sessions, explicit attention is also given to examining and challenging restrictive gender norms related to caregiving and family relationships.
 The program is delivered through mixed-gender group sessions designed to foster dialogue between mothers and fathers and to shift norms related to caregiving, men’s engagement, and gender equality. All sessions are conducted jointly with mothers and fathers, which was informed bypretesting and pilot feasibility findings that indicated strong participant preference for mixed-gender sessions and perceived benefits for couples’ communication, shared decision-making, and coparenting when mothers and fathers attend together. Of the 17 sessions, approximately half are considered more parent-focused, while the remaining sessions have structured parent-child activities, with caregivers encouraged to bring the index child. However, caregivers are not restricted from bringing the index child or other children to any session.
All sessions follow a standardized structure, including a recap of the previous session, introduction of key concepts, interactive group activities, and a brief of homework or a practice assignment. Sessions incorporate multiple behavior change techniques, including information sharing, participatory learning activities, guided discussion, use of visual media, goal setting and homework. In the sessions that include child-focused activities, CHWs additionally demonstrate responsive caregiving behaviors and provide active coaching and feedback to both mothers and fathers to support engagement in responsive play and communication with young children. Caregivers also participate in hands-on learning activities to reinforce key concepts, such as creating simple toys from locally available materials and participate in cooking demonstrations on preparing nutritious age-appropriate meals for young children.
These strategies are intended to activate several interrelatedmechanisms of change, including increased parenting knowledge and confidence, strengthened caregiving skills, greater father engagement, improved partner communication and emotional connectedness, critical reflection on gender norms, enhanced caregiver stress management, and increased peer support. Collectively, these mechanisms are hypothesized to improve early learning and responsive caregiving practices, promote more gender-equitable attitudes, strengthen couples' relationships, reduce intimate partner violence, and improve caregiver mental health, thereby enhancing the quality of the family caregiving environment and supporting improved ECD outcomes.  While group sessions are the primary delivery modality, CHWs will be encouraged to conduct home visits on an as-needed basis; for example, when participants miss multiple sessions, when caregivers or children face health or wellbeing challenges, or for follow-up after any referrals. CHWs will receive a monthly stipend of 80,000 TSH (~$30 USD), which was established in consultation with district stakeholders (i.e., District Executive Directors, District Medical Officers, District Community Development Officers, CHW coordinators and District Social Welfare Officers) and comparable to stipends provided to CHWs delivering other partner-supported programs.
In addition to the CHWs, each group will elect one father and one mother to serve as “group leaders.” These volunteer leaders will help remind participants about upcoming sessions and provide light support to CHWs during the group sessions, such as assisting in showing pictures from flipcharts and setting up the space and necessary materials. Group leaders will receive a small monthly phone voucher of 5,000 TSH ($2 USD) to cover airtime costs for contacting participants but will not receive a stipend. All caregivers attending sessions will receive a financial incentive of 2,500 TSH ($1 USD) per session to help offset transportation expenses and time costs. Allowances will be provided individually to each caregiver (mother and father) who attends in-person and will be distributed by the CHW in cash at the end of each session on the same day.

## Community health workers: training and supervision
CHWs will receive structured, phased training from TAHEA to develop competence with the curriculum content and enhance facilitation skills. Trainings are convened at a central venue from 8am to 5pm, combining classroom-based instruction with at least one day dedicated to field-based group practice. Three sequential training sessions will be held monthly, with each session covering roughly one-third of the curriculum. Training 1 will span five days and will provide an overview of the program, CHW responsibilities, and core concepts, including ECD, responsive caregiving, and gender-transformative approaches, followed by the content in sessions 1–5. Training 2 will last three days and will cover sessions 6–10. Training 3 will last three days and will cover sessions 11–17. During all trainings, CHWs will be provided with facilitator guides, flipcharts, and participant booklets corresponding to the sessions being covered.
The training pedagogy is highly participatory and experiential, including small-group practice, role plays, live rehearsal of activities, and peer feedback. Videos, photos, and observations from field sessions will be used as concrete examples to stimulate discussion, reflection, and problem-solving. As part of the training, CHWs will also conduct field-based piloting in nearby non-study communities, practicing delivery of specific activities under the guidance of trainers to gain hands-on experience with children and parents.
In addition to these multi-day trainings, TAHEA will provide ongoing, field-based experiential support to reinforce CHWs’ facilitation skills and delivery quality. Monthly refresher trainings will be held throughout the intervention period in non-study communities. These field-based refresher sessions will be approximately one day long and will bring together a small group of nearby CHWs at a common location. During these sessions, CHWs will share feedback and any challenges experienced, engage in small group reflections, and review or clarify any session activities in upcoming sessions that require reinforcement. Refresher activities will include peer observation, small-group reflection, structured feedback on facilitation, and in-depth reviews of selected sessions. Video recordings of CHWs delivering sessions will be reviewed to illustrate strong facilitation practices for peer learning and to provide individualized constructive feedback as needed.
Beyond trainings, TAHEA will also provide continuous supervision to CHWs on at least a monthly basis, or bi-weekly for CHWs that require additional support. Supervision will include pre-session meetings to review content and responsibilities, structured observation visits to assess delivery quality, and post-session debriefs to provide tailored feedback. Supervisors will document facilitation strengths, areas for improvement, and implementation challenges using a structured observation guide, which will inform the personalized coaching. These observations will also contribute to a continuous feedback loop, informing the content of subsequent trainings to address common challenges and reinforce key facilitation skills.When in-person debriefs are not feasible, supervisors will provide feedback via phone calls or follow-up meetings. CHWs will also be encouraged to seek support between sessions as needed.

## Impact evaluation study procedures
Questionnaires will be administered to mothers and fathers at baseline and endline to assess changes in outcomes between groups over times. The primary endpoint will be measured at endline (i.e., immediately following the implementation of the intervention; approximately November 2025). We will also conduct a follow-up evaluation approximately 8 months post-intervention (i.e., June 2026) to assess short-term sustainability of program impacts. All interviews will be conducted individually by a trained enumerator and each parent separately in a private setting either at a central location in the community (e.g., village leader’s office, near clinic) or at the participant’s household. At endline, in addition to parent surveys, we will conduct direct assessments of child development using the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development. The direct ECD assessments will be exploratory and deliberately not preregistered, as the study is not powered at this stage to evaluate ECD outcomes. The surveys with parents will take approximately 1.5 h and ECD direct assessments will be around 1–1.5 h depending on the child’s age and performance. Enumerators will make up to three attempts to complete each interview or child assessment.
Data collection will be conducted by a local team of university-educated enumerators who were not involved and independent from the intervention delivery. A specialized subgroup of local enumerators with prior experience in ECD assessments will conduct the endline child assessments. All enumerators will participate in a 7-day training that includes background content, theoretical concepts, interactive discussions, role-plays, pilot testing in non-intervention sites, and daily debriefs. Enumerators responsible for child assessments will complete an extended 10-day training with additional practice dedicated to the ECD assessment tools.
All questionnaires will be administered in Kiswahili. Data will be collected electronically using Kobotoolbox on password-protected tablets. At the end of each day, enumerators will upload data to a secure, password-protected server, ensuring both real-time monitoring and data security.

## Outcome measures
The primary outcome for this pilot RCT is paternal and maternal parenting, which is the core target of the intervention and central to establishing intervention proof of concept. We will also measure a range of secondary outcomes among fathers and mothers, as well as ECD outcomes. Given the lack of prior evidence on the potential effect sizes of Familia Bora on ECD, caregiver-reported ECD will be considered a secondary outcome, while direct assessment of ECD will be treated as exploratory in this initial evaluation (due also in part to its cost and logistical intensity) and primarily used to inform future research.
Table summarizes the preregistered outcomes to be evaluated and their corresponding measurement tools. All measures listed in this table will be collected at both baseline and endline. The primary endpoint will be at endline or post-intervention, which will be approximately six months after baseline. The primary outcome will be paternal and maternal parenting, and specifically stimulation practices, as measured using the Family Care Indicators (FCI) [ ]. This tool has been widely used in Tanzania and East Africa, including by our study team, and has demonstrated reliability, construct validity across multiple parenting domains for both mothers and fathers, and predictive validity with ECD outcomes [ – ].
 Secondary outcomes will include additional domains of paternal and maternal parenting (e.g., disciplinary practices [ , ], parenting attitudes), couple’s relationship dynamics (e.g., decision-making power [ ], intimate partner violence [ ]), parenting stress [ ], and gender attitudes. Child dietary diversity in terms of the foods consumed in the past 24 h will be reported by mothers. Early child development will be measured using the Caregiver Reported Early Development Instruments (CREDI) long form version [ ], and reported by mothers. We will also collect sociodemographic covariates, including parental education, age, marital status, employment, household composition, household wealth, household food security, and child age and sex.
Finally, we will consider several exploratory outcomes aligned with the Familia Bora curriculum and theory of change. These will include caregiver social support, depressive symptoms, positive psychological wellbeing, women’s empowerment, parenting self-efficacy, gender equitable caregiving roles, and responsive caregiving. We will also conduct a direct assessment of ECD at post-intervention (without a baseline assessment) using the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, 3rd edition, as an exploratory measure to triangulate results with CREDI.
Most measures have been previously used by the study team in Tanzania or in similar contexts in western Kenya. Measurement tools not previously validated in this setting were forward-translated into Kiswahili and back-translated into English by independent translators who were native Kiswahili speakers and also fluent in English. Discrepancies were resolved through a consultative review process with bilingual team members. The translated tools were piloted prior to baseline with 20 mothers and 20 fathers to assess comprehension and cultural appropriateness. Based on feedback, items were refined to ensure cultural relevance, accuracy, and clarity before final approval by the research team.

## Power
This efficacy trial is powered to detect a minimum detectable effect of 0.40 SD in mothers’ and fathers’ parenting, based on pooled effect sizes from a meta-analysis of parenting interventions in LMICs as well as findings from a recent father-inclusive parenting trial in Mara, Tanzania [ ]. Considering this is the first experimental evaluation of Familia Bora and given limited resources, we selected parenting as the primary outcome because it represents the main intermediate outcome in our theory of change and the most proximal marker in relation to ECD. Assuming 80% power, α = 0.05, an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.03, an average cluster size of 12 fathers/mothers per parenting group, and 85% retention, the required sample is 16 clusters (villages) per study arm. Accordingly, we will enroll at least 192 fathers and their partners in each arm. We acknowledge that the trial is not powered to detect smaller effect sizes, such as those anticipated for ECD outcomes, based on average treatment effects observed in ECD from other multicomponent parenting programs [ ]. Therefore, analyses of ECD will be exploratory, with the goal of generating preliminary efficacy estimates to refine power calculations and inform the design of a subsequent, fully powered effectiveness trial.

## Data analysis
We will first examine descriptive statistics for all measures and compare baseline characteristics between study arms. We will assess the psychometric properties of all outcome measures to confirm reliability and validity. We will evaluate whether any baseline characteristics are associated with attrition to inform the strategy for managing missing data. Variables associated with attrition will be included as covariates and/or incorporated into the multiple imputation model to account for missing data.
We will use mixed-effects models to estimate the effect of the intervention on primary and secondary outcomes. Nearly all outcome measures are based on scales and will be analyzed as continuous variables using linear regression models, whereas binary outcomes (e.g., any intimate partner violence victimization) will be analyzed using logistic regression models. All models will include a random intercept at the village level to account for clustering, fixed effects for trial arm and survey round, and an interaction term between treatment and survey round. The coefficient on this interaction will estimate the difference-in-differences intervention effect and indicate whether the changes in outcomes over time differ between intervention and control groups. Models will further adjust for baseline outcome values, sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., child age and sex, caregiver age, education, marital status, household wealth quintile), and any variables associated with attrition. All analyses will be based on intention-to-treat principles. Quantitative data analyses will be conducted in Stata version 18.

## Process evaluation: design and procedures
Alongside the impact evaluation, we will conduct a process evaluation guided by the RE-AIM framework [ ]. The process evaluation will examine the reach, adoption, implementation, and maintenance of the intervention. The process evaluation will uncover implementation successes and challenges and inform refinements to the program model for future delivery. Our process evaluation will include two complementary components: (1) routine monitoring and internal process evaluation, which will capture ongoing, quantitative and qualitative implementation data to support continuous program improvement; and (2) an in-depth longitudinal qualitative process evaluation, which will provide detailed insights into how the program was delivered and experienced over time by key stakeholders.

## Routine monitoring and internal process evaluation: design and procedures
Our first source of process evaluation data will be routine information collected by CHWs about their group sessions or TAHEA M&E officers during their field visits to sessions. These data will be gathered internally on an ongoing basis to continuously monitor implementation. Quantitative process data will be captured using four primary tools: (1) attendance forms, (2) CHW feedback forms, (3) M&E observation forms, and (4) participant feedback surveys.
CHWs will complete attendance forms to track father, mother, and child participation at each session and any known reasons for absence. They will also complete a CHW feedback form after each session to document perceptions of session quality, ease of delivery, fidelity, and overall satisfaction. Both forms will be completed on paper, collected by M&E officers, and digitized weekly using KoboToolbox for tracking and follow-up support.
M&E officers will conduct periodic field visits across all intervention villages to complete structured observation forms (via KoboToolbox) that assess CHW performance, participant engagement, session fidelity, and session quality. During these visits, officers will also administer short participant feedback surveys (i.e., exit interviews) to a randomly selected subsample of one to two participants per village. To capture a diversity of perspectives, individuals selected for the feedback survey will be tracked to ensure the same caregivers are not repeatedly interviewed. All process monitoring data will be reviewed regularly by the study team to guide course corrections, inform supportive supervision and refresher trainings, and provide both generalized and individualized support to CHWs throughout the trial.
In addition to these quantitative monitoring tools, TAHEA staff will also collect qualitative feedback from CHWs as part of their routine supervision and training. During field visits, supervisors and M&E officers will hold informal debrief meetings with CHWs to review session delivery, discuss successes and challenges, and identify areas needing further support. These discussions will be audio-recorded and transcribed to capture both the feedback provided as well as CHWs’ reflections during these discussions. Recordings will be collected at multiple points during implementation to capture how support, performance, and nature of discussions evolve over time, with at least several obtained from each CHW.
Furthermore, during the second and third CHW trainings, TAHEA staff will facilitate semi-structured focus group discussion (FGDs) with CHWs. These FGDs will provide a structured space for collective reflection on implementation, sharing of challenges and solutions, and discussion of strategies for overcoming barriers. All FGDs will be audio-recorded, transcribed, and translated for review by the broader research team. Insights from debriefs and FGDs will directly inform supervision and refresher training content, ensuring continuous feedback loops remain active and responsive throughout implementation.

## In-depth longitudinal qualitative process evaluation: design and procedures
To complement the routine monitoring and internal process evaluation, we will conduct an in-depth longitudinal qualitative process evaluation to generate more detailed and systematic insights into program implementation and stakeholders’ experiences over time. Using a qualitative longitudinal design, we will conduct in-depth interviews (IDIs) with key informants at both midline and endline to examine how experiences and perceptions evolve, and to explore mechanisms of change driving outcomes for participants and CHWs.
At midline, we will purposively sample one parent and one group leader from each intervention village, all CHWs delivering the program, and the two district CHW coordinators. Parent participants will be randomly selected but monitored to ensure gender balance (female and male caregivers) and representation of both low (< 50% sessions attended) and high (> 50% sessions attended) attendance. Separate interview guides will be developed for each stakeholder group to ensure questions are tailored to their roles and experiences.
IDIs with caregivers will explore their experiences with CHWs and group leaders, fidelity to program content, and satisfaction. Interviews with group leaders will focus on their dual role as participants and peer facilitators, while interviews with CHWs will explore their experiences delivering sessions, perceived alignment with their roles, and areas for improvement. IDIs with district CHW coordinators will gather their perspectives on the Familia Bora program, supervision structures, CHW performance, and alignment with district-level priorities. In total, approximately 60 interviews are planned at midline, each lasting between 60 and 90 min.
At endline, we will conduct longitudinal follow-up interviews with participants from the midline sample. We will re-interview all parents, half of the group leaders, all CHWs, and both district CHW coordinators across all 17 intervention villages. In addition, we will include endline interviews with village leaders in half of the intervention villages. These interviews will explore experiences and perceived impacts over time, including reflections on program content and delivery, as well as mechanisms by which the program may have contributed to changes in outcomes (e.g., parenting, couples’ relationships, women’s empowerment, caregiver wellbeing). They will also help us understand the broader community context and any changes that may have influenced or resulted from the program, along with participants’ suggestions for improvement.
All IDIs at midline and endline will be conducted by an independent team of trained research assistants (RAs) who are separate from the implementation team to minimize potential bias. RAs will be trained on study objectives, qualitative interviewing techniques, ethical considerations, and standardized data collection procedures. Prior to data collection, the RAs will pilot the interview guides and procedures with a small number of participants, and feedback from this pilot will be used to refine the tools for clarity, cultural appropriateness, and comprehensiveness. All interviews will be conducted in Kiswahili, audio-recorded, and subsequently transcribed and translated into English for analysis.

## Qualitative data analysis
The qualitative data will be analyzed through deductive thematic content analysis. A codebook will be developed from the study objectives, research questions, and structure of interview guides, and will be further refined after each team member reviews a handful of translated transcripts. The full research team – comprising of local and international investigators as well as local and international research analysts – will independently conduct line-by-line. Each interview transcript will be double-coded using Atlas.ti, the qualitative data analysis software. We will hold regular analysis meetings with members representing both the local and international research teams to confirm coding agreement, assess intercoder reliability, refine the codebook, resolve any disagreements, iteratively refine the codebook as needed and discuss emerging themes. Final themes will be synthesized by the team jointly and triangulated across stakeholder perspectives.
Overall, the qualitative analysis will complement quantitative monitoring indicators and impact evaluation results by contextualizing feasibility, fidelity, and acceptability; elucidating mechanisms of change underlying observed outcomes; and identifying barriers, facilitators, and other contextual factors. Insights from the qualitative data will also be integrated with quantitative process and impact data to provide a comprehensive understanding of program implementation, interpret trial results in context, and inform refinements for future implementation.

## “Booster” phase

## Booster intervention description
Following completion of the core Familia Bora program (17 weekly group sessions delivered over approximately 4 months), a post-intervention booster phase will be implemented to explore whether continued, light-touch support can help sustain or even enhance improvements in parenting behaviors and ECD outcomes beyond the main intervention. The booster phase will span three months and include one group session and one home visit per month. It is intentionally designed to be less intensive than the core program and have a targeted focus on early learning and responsive caregiving, given the central role of these componentsin the program’s theory of change and their direct links to ECD outcomes. 
The booster phase will be delivered using a new curriculum and session guide that will build upon content previously introduced during the core program. Booster group sessions will provide additional, structured opportunities to reinforce responsive caregiving practices and support caregivers in adapting play and communication activities to children’s progressing developmental ages and stages. Other core Familia Bora messages (e.g., caregiver mental health, nutrition, couples’ relationships) will also be briefly integrated and reinforced within sessions. Booster group sessions are expected to last approximately 1.5 h and consistent with the duration of the core program. 
In addition to the group sessions, the booster phase will also introduce a more structured and universal approach to home visiting. While home visits will be encouraged during the core program, they are intended to be on an as-needed basis, primarily to support caregivers who miss sessions or require additional assistance. In contrast, CHWs in the booster phase will be expected to conduct monthly home visits with all participating families (i.e., three total home visits per family). CHWs will be provided with a dedicated home visit guide consisting of three structured sessions. The content of these home visit sessions will focus primarily again on reinforcing responsive caregiving and early learning through individualized coaching of caregivers, including practicing age-appropriate play and communication, integrating responsive caregiving into everyday routines, and promoting the use of locally available materials to support play and learning in the home. The home visit strategy will also continue to actively engage fathers by scheduling visits at times when fathers can attend and encouraging joint participation of mothers and fathers in caregiving activities. In addition, home visits will briefly revisit other core program themes, including caregiver mental health, nutrition, couples’ relationships, and gender norms. Booster home visits are expected to last approximately 30 min.
CHWs delivering the booster phase will receive a four-day refresher training prior to implementation. In addition, they will participate in monthly one-day refresher trainings throughout the booster period and will continue to receive ongoing mentorship and supervision from TAHEA to support implementation fidelity and quality.

## Study design for the booster component
At the end of the core intervention, villages originally assigned to the intervention arm (17 villages) will be re-randomized, stratified by district, to either receive the booster extension or no additional intervention. This design will yield three study arms: (1) core Familia Bora plus booster sessions (8 villages), (2) core Familia Bora only (9 villages), and (3) control villages with no intervention (16 villages). 

## Analysis plan for the booster component
Evaluation of the booster phase will be primarily implementation-focused, using mixed methods to assess feasibility, acceptability, and perceived value of the booster sessions and associated home visits. Quantitative implementation indicators (e.g., attendance, dose delivered, dose received, session quality) and qualitative data from caregivers and CHWs will be used to understand how the booster was implemented and how it may support sustained caregiving behaviors. 
In addition, exploratory quantitative analyses will examine changes in maternal and paternal outcomes – particularly stimulation and responsive caregiving behaviors, but also other secondary outcomes such as couples' relationships and ECD – across the three study arms from endline of the core intervention to follow-up after the booster period. Pairwise comparisons (i.e., booster vs. non-booster intervention villages; booster vs. control; non-booster vs. control) will be conducted to assess differential changes in outcomes over time. Given the limited sample size and statistical power, these analyses will be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Overall, the goal is to assess preliminary signals of sustained or enhanced impacts associated with the booster phase, identify potential mechanisms or program components driving these effects, and uncover insights to inform a future, more rigorous evaluation to explicitly test the effectiveness of the booster component.

## Timeline
Figure presents the overall study timeline. CHWs were recruited in May 2025, followed by recruitment of parents in June 2025. Baseline data collection was conducted in June-July 2025. The intervention is being implemented from July to October 2025, with internal monitoring and evaluation data collected routinely throughout this period. Endline data collection will occur in November-December 2025. The qualitative process evaluation, consisting of semi-structured in-depth interviews with a purposive sample of stakeholders, will be conducted at midline (August 2025) and endline (December 2025). The booster phase will be implemented from March-May 2026. Finally, a mixed-methods follow-up evaluation will be conducted in June 2026 (approximately 8 months after the completion of the original Familia Bora intervention) to assess implementation and impacts of the booster component within selected intervention villages and explore short-term sustainability of intervention effects.

## Discussion
This manuscript describes the trial design and study protocol for the evaluation of Familia Bora, a multicomponent, father-inclusive parenting program delivered by CHWs to couples with young children aged 0–24 months in Misungwi and Sengerema districts of Mwanza, Tanzania. The trial will examine whether Familia Bora improves maternal and paternal parenting practices as primary outcomes, along with a broader set of caregiving-related outcomes for both mothers and fathers. By intentionally engaging fathers and couples together throughout in a gender-transformative program, the trial addresses a major gap in existing parenting interventions, which have primarily focused on mothers and rarely considered targeting couples’ relationship dynamics or gender norms to promote family caregiving more holistically. Familia Bora will engage both mothers and fathers jointly and integrate parent-focused, couple-focused, and child-focused content and activities across sessions to provide a more holistic approach to supporting caregivers and ECD. The mixed-methods evaluation design, integrating quantitative and qualitative data, will enable triangulation and provide a comprehensive understanding of program effects as well as the lived experiences of participants and CHWs. By embedding a robust process evaluation within the impact evaluation and utilizing diverse program monitoring data sources, the study will allow us to examine not only whether the intervention works but also the program components and mechanisms driving observed changes in this context.

## Limitations
This study also has several limitations. As a pilot proof-of-concept trial, the study is not powered to detect small effects that may potentially arise for some secondary outcomes, and confidence intervals for some effect size estimates should therefore be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, findings will inform power calculations for a fully powered trial. For ECD outcomes in particular, the modest sample size and the relatively short program duration of 4 months may limit our ability to detect measurable changes, which could require larger samples or more time to emerge. Longer-term follow-up assessments, such as the planned 8-month post-intervention assessment, will help uncover whether immediate program benefits translate into measurable impacts on ECD over time. 
Overall, this study will generate some of the first robust evidence on the feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary effects of a co-designed, father-inclusive, multicomponent parenting program delivered by CHWs for couples with young children in Tanzania. Findings from this pilot trial will inform refinements to strengthen program quality and lay the groundwork for a fully-powered effectiveness trial of Familia Bora delivered through CHWs at a larger scale. If successful, this research will show that Familia Bora can improve both mothers’ and fathers’ parenting while also supporting a broader range of outcomes related to nurturing care, couples’ relationships, and gender-equity. This program can offer a model for engaging fathers alongside mothers in parenting interventions and establishing the potential of CHWs as effective agents of change for parenting programs. In doing so, the trial can inform policy, practice, and future research on scalable approaches to promote family wellbeing and ECD in Tanzania and similar contexts.
