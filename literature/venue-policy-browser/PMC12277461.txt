## Abstract
Multinational conglomerates and other major food corporations dominate food options in the UK. At Bite Back, a youth-led movement, we have extensively documented the ways in which big food businesses target children as part of their market monopoly. These businesses employ tactics such as misleading health claims, advertising incessantly, promoting junk food every chance they get, as well as excluding young people from critical discussions about public health and reflections on their own impact. However, their negligence extends beyond individual wellbeing. Our research uncovered their significant contribution to environmental degradation, with many of these corporations responsible for alarming levels of carbon emissions. This article explores the dangerous liaisons between multinational conglomerates and the health of the public and the planet. This article also aims to show what youth-led activism looks like in a system rigged against young people, particularly in the face of such powerful and influential businesses.

## The food environment: A system designed to exploit
In the UK today, one in three children faces a future of food-related ill health. This statistic is only the tip of the iceberg. Public spaces are inundated with junk food advertisements, while high streets and school routes are dominated by cheap, ultra-processed foods. These strategies are deliberately designed to lure young consumers into the grip of corporate food giants. Misleading health claims – such as those made by some of the UK's biggest breakfast food providers – further compound the issue, as these companies knowingly promote products containing excessive amounts of sugar to children. Additionally, these corporations, namely Coca-Cola, Pepsi and more that I will discuss further in this article, are significant contributors to environmental destruction, posing an existential threat to the very future that young people will inherit.
Young people are acutely aware of these issues and are taking action. For over 5 years, Bite Back has held these corporations to account through research, advocacy, petitions and public demonstration. Here I will talk about the scale of the problem and the strategies that the young people of Bite Back have employed to fight back.

## The research: A dire outlook for young people
Bite Back’s research reveals a deeply troubling reality: this generation is set to be one of the first that is less healthy than our predecessors. Nutritional illnesses, such as obesity, are rising at an alarming rate, yet the issue is far more complex than the public might be aware. One of our most valuable sources of research comes from young people themselves. Their testimonies reveal the profound social and physical consequences of poor nutrition and poor food options, particularly in relation to the availability of free school meals and stigma faced by those who receive them. From the isolating experiences of standing in separate queues to receive free lunch, or stories of school teachers making out-of-pocket provisions for children due to limited access to nutritious food from school food suppliers, all of which severely impact young people’s life chances. We have spent years making sure that our voices reach big food and policy makers. The response from companies and our government have been wholly inadequate.
The transformation of the food system over generations is stark. What once prioritised sustenance and innovation has spiralled into a profit-driven industry reliant on heavily processed, high fat, salt and sugar (HFSS) foods, which typically end up being touted as the most convenient and affordable options. In addition, big food companies in the UK spent over £50 million in 1 year on online advertisements of products associated with children’s excess sugar intake, in what seems to be a less-than-ethical bid to secure lifelong consumers. Our research found that children in the UK are exposed to 15 billion junk food ads online and 3.6 billion on television every year. This figure does not even account for the numerous advertisements on the way to school at bus stops and tube station escalators. The result? An illusion of choice, where exposure to such pervasive marketing tactics, which we have shown, strongly influences consumer behaviour.
In 2019, Bite Back conducted the ‘Triple Dipped Chicken’ experiment to examine advertising’s impact on young people’s food choices. The participants were shown Triple Dipped Chicken advertisements, at various stages of their travel to the venue, when they arrived and ordered their meal, every participant chose the same thing … Triple Dipped Chicken. This experiment highlighted how relentless marketing directly and somewhat subconciously shapes the choices one makes – on a scale of billions, its impact is undeniable. What we perceive as autonomous choice is, in reality, the product of relentless marketing tactics – ultimately, we are far more likely to consume the options most aggressively pushed upon us.

## The illusion of choice
A common assertion suggests that promoting healthier food options in advertisements, as frequently or even in lieu of junk food advertisements, could counteract the effects of junk food marketing. However, systemic barriers make this impractical. Healthy food is disproportionately expensive, often placing it out of reach for many families. Moreover, genuinely healthy options are scarce. Popular ‘health’ products like Innocent smoothies, Glaceau Vitamin Water and Naked smoothies are all owned by Coca-Cola and PepsiCo respectively – corporations whose primary sales come from unhealthy products. At the very least, perhaps finding a healthy and affordable breakfast will be easier? Cereals such as Special K, Crunchy Nut and Coco Pops are all produced by Kellogg’s, a company where 77 % of sales stem from unhealthy products. But there are so many options … right? These corporations mask their dominance behind various brand names, ensuring that even those attempting to make healthier choices inadvertently support their monopoly. Between the marketing mind games and the illusion of choice, young people, often with fewer financial resources and limited access to alternatives, are not left with very many healthy and affordable options. Young people are left with little choice but to consume these products.

## The environmental toll of big food
Bite Back didn’t stop at just researching the physical and social effects of poor options and malnutrition. Bite Back’s research has also exposed the environmental consequences of big food. The top 10 global food and drink companies that sell products in the UK, including PepsiCo, Coca-Cola and Kellogg’s, collectively produce more carbon emissions than the entire aviation industry and the UK as a nation. In 2022 alone, they were responsible for 477 million tonnes of carbon dioxide emissions. Despite public commitments to sustainability, many of these top 10 companies have failed, or are behind, to meet their own emissions-reduction targets. Their negative impact extends beyond damaging physical health to actively jeopardising the planet’s future. Young people find themselves fighting not only for their right to nutritious food, but also for a liveable world.

## Fighting back: The power of youth activism
It is generally accepted to observe that young people are often excluded from political discourse, dismissed as uninformed or uninterested. Bite Back’s activists have challenged this perception at every turn. One of our star campaigners, Anna, addressed the House of Commons youth parliament, highlighting how policy makers fail to engage meaningfully with young people on issues that directly affect them, stating, ‘it seems that policy makers don’t ‘really want to speak to us’. At times in our 5 years when the government has proven inaccessible, we turned to the corporate boardrooms. However, even there, young people were patronised and dismissed. At the Royal College of Physicians’ launch of the Dangerous Liaisons report, I even spoke of times where big food retailers and advertising giants refused our messaging near their stores, in favour of these powerful companies. Despite this, we persist.
We sought new, bold ways to make our voices heard. One of our notable successes came with the ‘Don’t hide what’s inside’ campaign, which took on Tony the Tiger (Kellogg’s). At the time, Kellogg’s was facing legal action for misleading health claims on their breakfast products. Our campaign amplified public scrutiny and noted the legal ruling against the company, as a victory for child health and our movement. This was just the beginning of our journey as a movement.
Bite Back activists also made headlines when some of our star activists were invited to speak at a Houses of Parliament Select Committee, a moment that went viral. Of course, Bite Back’s young people were not finished. Recognising the overwhelming presence of junk food advertising, we took direct action by purchasing billboard space around Westfield shopping centre to disrupt the junk food barrage and demand change, as testing ground for our larger-scale campaign on advertising spaces in Southwark and Lambeth. With this, we took centre stage in the news cycle,beating big food at their own games and turning public attention onto the issue.

## Dangerous liaisons and a dangerous reality
Having attended the Dangerous Liaisons event previously mentioned, it really opened my eyes to the way that many industries, including those beyond the food industry, can be incredibly pervasive and, in every case, purposefully destroy the health of our nation. Since I reflected on the issue, I have derived three overarching ideas; any institution that affects the health of a nation ought to be free from monopoly, decision-makers in any industry related to public health ought to be completely uninfluenced by companies, and profit-over-people needs to become a thing of the past. Having co-facilitated Fix My Food sessions for activists, in collaboration with UNICEF in Jamaica, I have seen first-hand the adverse effects of powerful food corporations worming their way into every part of a nation with little to no legislative limits. I have seen a nation where fast food was one of the largest sponsors of school time activities, even providing school dinners, scholarships and debate meets. Their power goes so far that these big food businesses had sponsored government campaigns, with some government officials sitting on their boards and committees. I have seen fast food make itself the centre of a nation, so much so that hundreds of years of vibrant, rich food culture is undermined by the sheer marketing and monetary might of multinational conglomerates. So much so that local fresh produce,was relegated to a corner at the back of a shop. All of this is inextricably linked to highly preventable non-communicable diseases (NCDs) such as type 2 diabetes and cancers, which became farmore prevalent in this community than ever before. When the industries that impact our health can be bought or lobbied by any business or for-profit organisation, it is us who pays the price and it is always the most vulnerable that will suffer the most. That is why Bite Back is on a mission towards corporate responsibility. We lead by example to set the standard for limiting conflicts of interest.

## Conclusion
What began as a small, grassroots movement tackling food deserts, accessibility to and high quality free school meals has evolved into a national force for change. Over the past 5 years, Bite Back has become a trusted source of evidence and advocacy, regularly consulted by policy makers for insights on the food crises affecting young people in the UK. From billboards to boardrooms and parliamentary committees, our message remains clear: young people are being targeted by big food businesses and bombarded with junk food marketing, and it poses a severe threat to our health.
The hundreds of schools participating in our Bite Back in Schools programme, our network of star activists, youth leaders, ambassadors, alumni, staff and trustees are all united in the fight for a healthier and fairer food system. The young people of Bite Back are not just aware; we are mobilised, determined and demanding meaningful change. We want to be healthy, we need a healthy planet and we want our government to get out of bed with big food. We asked the government for the TV watershed, we asked TfL to take the ads off the escalators; now, we want government to prioritise public health and get these junk food giants out of all our public spaces. We’re demanding thorough regulation and an outright ban in schools so that these big food businesses can’t target children. Let’s raise the standards that big food is held to. Join us to protect the food environment for all.
