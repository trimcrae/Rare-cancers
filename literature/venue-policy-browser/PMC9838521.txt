## Abstract
This theme-based book review considers four recent titles related to reform: They Eat Our Sweat: Transport Labor, Corruption, and Everyday Survival in Urban Nigeria, by Daniel E. Agbiboa; Assessing Public Management Reforms, by Patrick Gibert and Jean-Claude Thoenig; Combating Money Laundering in Africa: Dealing with the Problem of PEPs, by John Hatchard; and The Effects of Technology and Institutions on E-Participation: A Cross-National Analysis, by Pragati Rawat and John C. Morris.
Keywords: Reform, Corruption, Governance, France, Africa, Nigeria, Book reviews

## Introduction
Trust in government in the United States is quite low: “Only two-in-ten Americans say they trust the government in Washington to do what is right “just about always” (2%) or “most of the time” (19%)” (Pew Research Center, ). The Wellcome Global Monitor estimated, for ‘Share of people who trust their national government’ in 2020, that 52.5% of the public had trust for national government in the U.S., and 56.3% in France, but only 23.1% in Nigeria (Wellcome Global Monitor, ).
Calls for greater accountability and transparency have frequently accompanied a lack of trust in government, even if greater transparency does not necessarily lead to improved perceptions of honesty or competence (Grimmelikhuijsen, ). Politicians themselves are sometimes targeted for corrupt public behavior. From an administrative perspective, programs that do not achieve their goals, poorly structured organizations and processes, and inefficiency in service delivery may all be indicative of institutions, programs, and policies in need of reform (Brillantes & Fernandez, ). Official corruption negatively impacts public trust. Low levels of public trust can reduce the potential for citizen involvement (Clausen et al., ), and this may call into question the validity of any efforts for public participation, or meaningful reform measures.
While calls for reform are common enough to be taken for granted in public life, assumptions are made about the nature of reforms, and the validity of such efforts. It is worth noting that efforts toward good governance in certain places are often blocked by the activities of elites, so citizen preferences are seldom a concern. The public agenda for reform may bear little resemblance to the elite agenda (Bratton, Coulibaly, & Machado, ). Problems with corruption may undermine efforts toward legitimacy in some states. People often expect government to do what it says it will do (Bratton, Coulibaly, & Machado, ). When reform efforts are loudly proclaimed but accomplish little, it is worth asking whether these efforts are intended on a purely symbolic level, for political purposes, or on a performative basis.
This theme-based book review considers four recent titles related to reform: They Eat Our Sweat: Transport Labor, Corruption, and Everyday Survival in Urban Nigeria, by Daniel E. Agbiboa; Assessing Public Management Reforms, by Patrick Gibert and Jean-Claude Thoenig; Combating Money Laundering in Africa: Dealing with the Problem of PEPs, by John Hatchard; and The Effects of Technology and Institutions on E-Participation: A Cross-National Analysis, by Pragati Rawat and John C. Morris.
Daniel E. Agbiboa ( ).They Eat Our Sweat: Transport Labor, Corruption, and Everyday Survival in Urban Nigeria.Oxford. 288 pp. ISBN 9780198861546 (hbk, $100 USD); ISBN 9780192605962 (e-book, Redshelf, $89.99 USD).
Daniel Agbiboa’s book They Eat Our Sweat ( ) is a pathbreaking look at corruption in Nigerian society. Told with a view that combines well-argued theory and an uncompromising sight into the stark realities of urban transport, the book restores corruption from a flippant, inaccurate caricature to a standpoint where all hold some accountability. This is a rare academic book that grabs readers and holds on for the duration – a real page-turner – its scathing, fiery prose burns with knowing intensity throughout.
The reference to eating of sweat, explained in the book, is part of a larger discourse in Africa about the relationship of eating to ‘chopping’, an ambiguous expression in Nigeria, but in respect of chopping money, it means gaining wealth illegally. Eating then means getting paid or stealing. The book’s title is a reference to corrupt officials and people in positions of authority (of various sorts) ‘eating’ others’ legitimate work and the proceeds of it.
The book begins with an introduction inviting readers to rethink the very notion of corruption, with corruption becoming the system, anti-corruption efforts rendered ineffective, and standard notions of a system devoid of corruption made ludicrous. Expectations in such societies are different than other cultures might have them. Dealing with corruption in Nigeria, among people engaged in provision of transportation, is about survival, and decisions about how to interact with officials are made on this basis. Taking a principled stand might lead to a driver being held up for hours, robbed, or killed.
In chapter one, the variations of corruption are discussed. Citizens are made to become active participants in the corrupt system, and it becomes less clear over time what corruption even means. Chapter two explores the language of corruption. Against this backdrop, Agbiboa discusses aspects of Nigerian culture, including magic and other forms of eating, referring again to the book’s evocative title. In this context, buying votes as promising a piece of the ‘national cake’ when a leader takes office might seem appropriate if not warranted. Chapter three takes us into the world of minibus (danfo) drivers. Chapter four takes a remarkable look at the slogans painted on these danfo buses.
Chapter five examines the agbero, a feared group that prey on urban transport workers. Chapter six affectingly highlights the very different views between the rhetoric of Lagos as a ‘world class city’ and the reality that sits unmoving for those in the transport sector. In this chapter, we meet the okada drivers – motorcycle drivers that cut perilously through the hazardous city traffic. In this scenario, the aspects of legality are shown to essentially make corrupt activities more and more commonplace. The book’s conclusion draws the pieces of the story together. Given the presence and connection of street- and state-level corruption, it might be suggested that the system in practice society-wide – the system that is protected and nurtured - is the crooked one, and the ‘lawful system’ exists more as a put-on to serve symbolic purposes.
I have read about Nigerian public policy and reviewed some papers for journals, but this book offers something else entirely – it makes the culture, people, and place real. Lagos might “have it all,” as the saying says (Agbiboa, , p. 173), but we ride along uneasy in our danfos, thinking about the beautiful Lagos that seems distant from the reality of the street we see before our eyes, through Agbiboa’s perspective. We wonder whether, as Agbiboa indicated, our danfo driver like many on the road might be suffering from hypertension or even partially blind ( , p. 102). We sit on the back of our okada, and hope we make it to our destination as our driver, who is probably thinking about what he owes to the agberos, weaves dangerously through traffic. Corruption is all around us – we too are surrounded and threatened by it – and the system enables its continuation. Traditional views of corruption are too simplistic to describe the situation; corruption has made itself permanent by supplanting official rules and via pretenses and protestations to fairness. Down is up and up is down – corruption is not an exception, but the rule itself. All the while, as a reader, I felt for these drivers and their families. With Agbiboa’s help, readers can better understand just how dark it can be, and yet, these drivers nevertheless believe in themselves and their futures, and persevere, hoping it will be better one day.
This is a tremendous, at times startling book, and recommended not only to readers interested in public policy in Nigeria and in Africa, but those who want a mesmerizing book that takes them on a journey and leaves them with a changed perspective.
Patrick Gibert and Jean-Claude Thoenig ( ). Assessing Public Management Reforms.Palgrave Macmillan. 231 pp. ISBN 978-3-030-89798-7 (hbk, $119.99 USD); ISBN 978-3-030-89801-4 (pbk, $119.99 USD); ISBN 978-3-030-89799-4 (e-book, $89 USD).
Public management, due in no small part to the influence of politics, has long been concerned with the themes of change and reform. Societal challenges demand serious consideration and problem-solving. There is an ongoing need to evaluate change in public organizations, to understand their evolution; there is some reason to question the contribution of major reform efforts, and whether they actually achieve their intended goals.
Assessing Public Management Reforms provides an absorbing exploration of organization management, given review of a range of French modernization programs since the 1960s. It is common in public management to experience regime change, where new leadership calls upon administration to implement an assortment of initiatives branded as reforms. These are often intended as major transformation and as branded moves on the part of incoming administrations. But change need not be limited to these major initiatives, and as often becomes clear to those working within public administration, some of the most consequential changes occur outside of major transformational movements.
After an introductory chapter, the book unfolds in four five additional parts. The second part outlines the analytical perspective, defining conventional assessment compared with an alternative assessment approach. Part three highlights the comprehensive approach to reforms, centering on rationalization of budget choices, public service renewal, reform of public service, revision of public law, as well as various audit and modernization programs. Part four highlights appropriation of analytical toolkits including dashboards, benefit-cost analysis, and ex-post evaluation. Part five counters transformational reforms with agencification, ‘theories in use’, and human resources structures. Part six wraps the book with a review of developments and assessment of public reforms.
Gibert & Thoenig ( ) note the difficulty that exists in translating the symbols of reform into practices. The reforms are ‘artefacts’ that might be used to establish reason and legitimacy, with the announcement of reform being more important than any long-term obligation. To open the ‘black box’ of reform, any analytical approach needs to recognize that the rhetoric at the top of the organization does not represent the mindset throughout the organization. This is striking in the case of France, with its well-worn administrative system, covered well in this text, existing more or less separately from the sphere of political rhetoric over a period of many decades. France has been known for large-scale reform measures, making it an appropriate venue for analysis.
Throughout the text, I found myself agreeing with the assessments being made. Issues like siloization are especially cumbersome, and this was a serious issue in contending with the COVID-19 crisis. The points made also find plenty of examples outside the French case. Because major reforms are not linear in approach, administrative segments know enough to lay low: “A reform is…rendered all the less credible in the eyes of the administrative services expected to comply with it. All they have to do is…wait for the incumbent government’s next whim or reform” as a “low risk” response (Gibert & Thoenig, , p. 66). I found the treatment of dashboards and benefit-for-cost analyses to be on-point; in particular, the Dashboard chapter raises essential international cases, like New York’s Compstat program, and the problem of indicators and their implementation in a striking, practical manner. Interpretation of abstractions is a consistent issue; I also found the point about the usefulness of vagueness and ambiguity to be worthwhile. There may be a desire to see, as Prime Minister Michel Rocard put it, “no autonomy without accountability, no accountability without evaluation, and no evaluation without consequences” (Gibert & Thoenig, , p. 109), but in reality, there is great complexity within a system supposedly devoted to evaluation, including much subjectivity and a need for sensitivity even to diplomatic behaviors. The desire and support for and carrying out of evaluation may be looked upon with some suspicion, for a variety of reasons, as the authors illustrate.
Because agencies work at a level close to the public, there is great potential in lower-level adjustments and quiet reforms, so the authors’ work on agencification and comprehensive review of French administration is interesting and informative. The concluding chapters are concise and hard-hitting, well-supported by the preceding chapters.
I enjoyed reading this book and am pleased to recommend it. It would be of interest to scholars and researchers of French administration, but also to those interested in public management and responses to reform more broadly, and why transformational reforms do not always measure up to their stated intents.
John Hatchard. ( ). Combating Money Laundering in Africa: Dealing with the Problem of PEPs.Edward Elgar. 328 pp. ISBN 9781789905298 (hbk, $147 USD); ISBN 9781803926049 (pbk, $45 USD); ISBN 9781789905304 (e-book, $40 USD).
John Hatchard begins Combating Money Laundering in Africa ( ) in a preface by asking whether there is hope for the future. He notes that there have been appalling instances of official corruption in Africa, but that this must also be balanced by the fact that there are dedicated public servants in place who act honorably every day, serving the public good. The book differentiates itself from the larger lot of books about public corruption in its focus on African cases, but also in its book-length exploration of the role of politically exposed persons (PEPs). PEPs represent special cases of potential corruption that are notably difficult to address. The book is useful in its analysis of how strategies to combat money laundering have been constructed and implemented, and their eventual efficacy in practice.
The book is organized into four parts. The first part provides a foundational examination of money laundering and the role of PEPs. The second part examines international and African approaches to addressing money laundering. Part three provides an overview of various mechanisms utilized in African states to combat money laundering. The final part discusses transnational approaches to combating money laundering in Africa, from efforts to persuade private companies to reveal those that would have them participate in bribes and money laundering, to the possible role of the African Court in addressing the problem of money laundering. The final chapter offers concluding thoughts on efforts thus far and ways to strengthen anti-money laundering efforts across the African continent.
There is no doubt that Africa is intensely resource-rich, and issues of public corruption, self-dealing, and money laundering take on the potential for exceptionally damaging impact with regard to development and devastation for citizens’ lives. History has shown that the sweep of a single corrupt incident can be breathtaking in size and scope of the crime. Hatchard ( ) makes important points about the difficulties that exist in properly identifying PEPs and hazy concepts like ‘close associates’ with respect to policy. The chapter on PEPs and abuse of constitutional pardon power is remarkable and skillfully written, and is recommended beyond to those interested in that topic, specifically, beyond the specific scope of this text.
Hatchard makes use of a number of case studies in explaining aspects of combating laundering; a significant example is the Mahmoud Thiam case. While the Thiam case provides ample grist for discussion of arrangements that were personally advantageous to an official while being wholly detrimental to national interests, there are surely a lot of African corruption cases, and there were opportunities to bring in other examples as well.
One might ultimately examine the question of money laundering in Africa and wonder not whether the glass is half full or half empty – whether there is supposed hope for the future – but whether the glass (meaning the system) is simply broken – failing to serve useful public purposes. Hatchard provided evidence that naming and shaming, and various forms of persuasion, can have an effect on the prevalence of money laundering (at least at the most visible levels). The fact remains that reformist agendas aside, it can be incredibly difficult to enforce such mechanisms in practice, particularly when powerful elites and their allies are involved. Even with enhanced measures against money laundering, perpetrators continue to engage in illegal behaviors – one might reason that the perceived risks do not outweigh the rewards. While Hatchard’s treatment of the efforts to address money laundering is ample and wide-ranging, I could not help but think throughout that much of the official measures could be considered positive movements, but could also be seen as a cover for continued corrupt practices (which then are less visible, or engaged in less obvious manners), or so much window dressing to serve international expectations.
This noted, some of the reasons that Hatchard points out as official reasons that provided cover for corrupt practices in Africa seem eerily similar to those seen outside the continent. Avoiding transparency for “state and security purposes” or even more vaguely “national security” ( , p. 23 − 4). are rationales heard commonly elsewhere. So much of what occurs in government is, frankly, a matter of discretion, and discretion exists to not investigate instances of corruption. Often, even given serious and compelling evidence of a crime, no action may be taken (Hatchard, , p. 26); this undermines the rhetoric of reform and change, and makes official players, who have the role of keeping control and ensuring that rules are followed, into seeming participants in fraud and dishonesty. There are also instances of policy writing where nations are encouraged to engage in some action, or to foster adoption of some requirements in a non-mandatory way. When the political will to act is all important, it is questionable whether weak policy language will contribute to the maintenance of actionable will.
There are instances where the discussion of this material is very absorbing and enlightening. There are also instances where the text is too focused on overview and brief definition. Examples would include points on external oversight and enhanced due diligence, and later criminalization and mutual legal assistance in the African Union Convention, where elaboration might be helpful. I was also not so clear on Hatchard’s intent for the role of civil society organizations in combating official corruption, though this is mentioned several times throughout the text; as far as potential to make recommendations that result in meaningful policy, this is possibly a missed opportunity.
The book will most likely be valuable for researchers engaged in study of money laundering efforts in Africa, or students in a course on this or a related topic.
Pragati Rawat and John C. Morris ( ). The Effects of Technology and Institutions on E-Participation: A Cross-National Analysis.Routledge. 134 pp. ISBN 9780367755492 (hbk, $59.95 USD); ISBN 9781003164326 (e-book, $22.95 USD).
In The Effects of Technology and Institutions on E-Participation, Pragati Rawat and John C. Morris ( ) explore public participation in governance and specifically e-participation from a cross-national perspective, examining institutional and technological effects on potential for participation. The book begins with a section on e-participation as a field of study – this chapter also introduces the research project and research questions, which, given the cross-national nature of the study, seek to explain the differences between national experiences in degree of e-participation. A section on public participation follows, along with a segment on e-participation. The book continues with a discussion of the framework for its analysis, and a chapter on the study’s findings; the text concludes with a chapter on implications and outlook for e-participation.
The book is part of the Routledge Focus series, so the text is shorter than a typical academic book; in terms of describing the approach, each of the chapters acts as sort of a lengthened segment of an academic paper – taking a more comprehensive approach to, for example, public participation and e-participation. As introductions to the topic areas, these chapters in particular provide for strong introductions to the material, especially for an audience that may be unfamiliar. This is a point in the book’s favor, especially if the potential audience includes readers not in formal academic programs, but rather involved in participation efforts in practice. The literature-oriented chapters are well-rooted in important works of public administration. The authors do a good job of explaining the increasing levels of involvement in participation, from informing, to consultation, and finally to greater involvement and enfranchisement through active decision-making participation.
In Chap. 2, the authors point out that there has been no shortage of disagreement about public participation, the rationale for pursuing it, and how to achieve it in the interest of better public governance. The chapter raises points about the problems of authentic participation, which tend to resonate, especially in climates where participation can be meager or minimal – just another box to check to accomplish some pre-determined goal. While e-participation is differentiated from other forms of public participation on a variety of bases, it is essential to make clear the reasons participation is desired, so that desired goals may be achieved. Beyond simply providing information to the public, the authors note that participation can identify needs and allow for co-creation of knowledge. The means of e-participation can fail, though, for many of the same reasons other approaches to gaining public participation fail – including a lack of awareness about opportunities for involvement, lack of knowledge about policy-related issues, and lack of awareness of benefits of public involvement (Rawat & Morris, ).
Chapter 4, which focuses on the study’s framework, incorporates policy feedback theory and socio-technical theory. I was disappointed that more of the references included here were not more recent, especially given the reliance on information and communication technology. Chapter 5 is a fairly straightforward academic treatment of study results, based upon the statistical analysis conducted. While the book is targeted to students and researchers, I nevertheless see this as a missed opportunity in some respects, because the book’s short form might invite interest from the practitioner community; while they are interested in the sufficiency of research in terms of quality, they would be less interested in a long treatment of methods, especially in a text that is of shorter overall length. In implications and outlook, the authors raise some interesting questions about differences in national experiences with e-participation, noting that lower technology does not necessarily mean a lower degree of e-participation (notable, given the prevalence in some societies of mobile phones). The detail on this, though is lacking. I would have been very interested in knowing more about individual countries that show especially unique or striking cases, but the short form of the text perhaps limits the opportunity for this. That institutions have a role “as precursor to technology in promoting e-participation in a country” (Rawat & Morris, , p. 102), is useful, but the authors also point out a decline in some cases of civil liberties, which is particularly worrying. Discussions of e-participation and the ideals of inclusion and involvement in governance matter little in those cases where rights and liberties are being subverted.
The Effects of Technology and Institutions on E-Participation is an intriguing short read, if necessarily limited given the format. It will likely be of greatest interest to those needing a quick introduction to public participation and e-participation; some of the questions raised by the authors are very likely worthy of future research, as well.

## Synthesis
The books included in this themed review address reform in some way. Agbiboa’s book ( ) amply demonstrates reasons one might question official pronouncements about a city for all when real-life divisions are so clear for transportation workers. The presence of corruption undermines trust, and assertions of reform are almost too much to stomach, continuing with the book’s metaphor. Gibert & Thoenig ( ) offer a skillful analysis of why large-scale reform measures fail to achieve their stated goals, and of the importance for work being done at the agency level, in their review of the French case. Hatchard ( ) offers the most positive outlook of these three books, in his coverage of anti-money laundering reform efforts in Africa, expressing genuine hope for the future. I find myself somewhat more reticent in joining in a full endorsement of this optimism. I sincerely hope I am proven wrong. The work by Rawat & Morris ( ), on public participation and e-participation, is key to any sort of reform effort that ultimately works, because public involvement in governance is central to moving beyond simple information provision to decision-making and meaningful collaboration.
