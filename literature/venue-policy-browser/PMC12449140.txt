## Abstract
Homelessness is a global challenge that impacts many population groups, including adults, families, and children. People become homeless for various reasons, and international data underestimates the prevalence of homelessness. The impact of homelessness on health and wellbeing is well described, including reduced life expectancy and significant morbidity. A lack of housing stock, increased waiting lists, and provision of temporary and emergency accommodation exist globally, with inconsistencies in approach and provision. Limited data are reported on the impact of the living conditions on health and wellbeing for people experiencing homelessness. This feasibility study was co-designed with one charitable partner organization that sought to understand the impact of providing a lower-density accommodation unit at the individual and service levels. The intention was to provide evidence of the impact to enable further funding and development. This paper reports on qualitative interviews with previous residents, service providers, and local key informants to understand the impact and experience of this type of accommodation unit. A workshop was subsequently facilitated to review the data and co-develop recommendations for practice. Ethical approval was provided (2023), and data collection was completed (October 2023 to February 2024). Thematic data analysis of interviews was completed, and four themes were reported: ‘Sense of Home Environment’, ‘Empowerment to Independent Living’, ‘Building New Identities’, and ‘Future Planning’. Nine recommendations support the development of service planning and delivery. This study, which assessed the feasibility of a type of accommodation, demonstrates the strengths of using engaged research methods.
Keywords: homelessness, social determinants of health, patient and public involvement, co-design

## INTRODUCTION
Homelessness is a global challenge impacting population groups, including adults, families, and children. People become homeless for various reasons, including personal factors and structural and environmental barriers ( , , ). Significantly, 1.6 billion people worldwide are known to live in inadequate housing conditions ( ), with approximately 15 million forcibly evicted every year. The Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) estimates that at least two million people in OECD countries experienced homelessness in 2024 ( ). Data sources on homelessness vastly underestimate prevalence rates, as no uniformly accepted international definition or criteria are used, and four types of homelessness are described in the literature. The European Federation of National Organizations Working with the Homeless ( ) developed a broader definition, the European Typology of Homelessness and Housing Exclusion (ETHOS), which distinguishes between rooflessness, houselessness, living in insecure housing, and living in inadequate housing. The definition was updated in 2007 to ETHOS Light. ETHOS ( ) identifies the domains that constitute what a home is: the physical domain (space), the social domain (privacy and social engagement), and the legal domain (legal entitlement to live in a dwelling or space) ( ). The four types of homelessness across the scale, described in the literature, are rough sleeping, statutory homelessness, hidden homelessness, and people at risk ( ) and are explained further in .
The detrimental impact of homelessness on the health and wellbeing of individuals is well understood, with reported reduced life expectancy and a higher incidence of morbidity. describe the syndemic effects of poverty, trauma, and exclusion on mental and physical health for people experiencing homelessness (PEH). People experiencing homelessness have two to five times higher rates of mortality and morbidity ( , ), an estimated five times as many hospital admissions ( ), and they continue to have limited access to primary care. The challenge in defining homelessness results in resources and logistics for assessing the extent of homelessness diverging across countries globally. note in a recent review of evidence that ‘health systems lack a comprehensive understanding of the needs of all population groups and fail to plan adequately, which maintains fragmentation. Policy makers must set policies and plan health services based on a comprehensive understanding of the needs of all population groups’ (p. 1).
In Ireland, the exact number of PEH is unknown. Still, prevalence data collected from specific ETHOS Light categories highlight the increasing rates of homelessness year on year and are currently estimated at 15 286 individuals ( ). In 2023, this was noted as 13 179, representing 0.26% of the population ( ). Many factors may contribute to homelessness. The impact of becoming homeless varies for each person, depending on their personal circumstances. What is consistent for PEH is the loss of social connection and belonging to society, which manifests as feelings of stigma and exclusion and is the opposite of what is required for thriving ( ). highlights the significant impact of the breakdown of relationships as a leading factor for homelessness, and it is often underestimated in society, with addiction and mental health factors prioritized. Recent self-reported data from a Californian survey of 3042 PEH also indicates economic factors as well as history of anxiety and depression over the life course as factors associated with pathways into homelessness ( ). Substance use was not a factor for becoming homeless for almost a quarter of respondents. The ‘use of illicit drugs regularly (≥3 times per week)’ [was found for] ‘41.6% (95% CI, 39.4–43.8%)’ PEH ‘who began using regularly before their first episode of homelessness’ and almost a quarter of respondents started to use drugs regularly ‘after they became homeless’ [‘23.2% (95% CI, 20.5–25.9%)’] ( , , p. 1222).
The Housing First model has emerged as a transformative approach to addressing chronic homelessness, recognized for improving housing stability and its broader public health benefits. By providing immediate, unconditional access to permanent housing alongside integrated support services, the model addresses key social determinants of health, including housing insecurity, mental illness, and substance use disorders ( , , ). In the Irish context, the government’s Housing First National Implementation Plan 2022–2026 signals a policy shift towards evidence-based, health-oriented responses to homelessness ( ). Nonetheless, implementation challenges—such as regional disparities in service delivery, inadequate housing stock, and limited health system integration—hinder the model’s full realization. These gaps raise concerns about equity and effectiveness, suggesting that Ireland’s Housing First strategy may fall short of achieving its public health potential without strengthened intersectoral coordination and investment ( ). The recent Irish strategy on homelessness also acknowledges the challenges and highlights 10 priority areas for the next three years, including prevention, housing first, collaborative working, outreach, and mental health and addiction supports ( ). Overall success in policy actions is possible through intersectoral collaboration and leveraging existing government policies, such as the ‘Housing for All’ housing policy ( ) and the National Strategic Plan to Improve the Health of People Experiencing Homelessness in Ireland (2024–27) ( ). At the transnational level, the United Nations recognized housing as a human right over 34 years ago ( ). Twenty years ago, Sir Michael Marmot identified ‘housing’ as a key social determinant of health. Yet, in 2025, Ireland still lacks a legal right to housing. Despite the investments in services, there is limited evidence of the impact of urban housing interventions ( ). In an evidence review of housing interventions for PEH, identified several gender-specific challenges. These include the provision of gender-neutral housing units, congregated living spaces with little privacy and the lack of child-friendly environments. These issues may be particularly problematic for women with children ( , ). also suggest that a limited evidence base exists when examining housing interventions. The physical type of accommodation and infrastructure provided for PEH and its impact on their wellbeing or ability to live independently is less well understood. Within this context, a feasibility study was conducted to address this question for a single service provider.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Aim
This study aimed to assess the feasibility and impact of one model of emergency homelessness accommodation at micro and meso levels in Ireland. The terms ‘micro’ and ‘meso’ are used here in reference to Bronfenbrenner's Ecological Systems Theory ( ). The micro level refers to the individual and personal level, while the meso level refers to the organizational and institutional elements of homelessness accommodation. The study sought to answer the following research questions:
What are the experiences and perspectives of PEH, who lived in a recently introduced model of accommodation and its impact on health and wellbeing?
What are the perspectives of key informants who were engaging with residents on the impact of this accommodation?
What recommendations do previous residents and key informants have regarding the feasibility of the homeless accommodation model and its future development?

## Study design
This study was funded by the Irish Health Research Board and one University’s Patient and Public Involvement (PPI) seed funding scheme. Participatory engaged research methods guided the conception and design of the research question, and throughout the project's lifecycle, were led by a third-party charity (service provider) organization.

## Accommodation model (Intervention)
The accommodation model for this study was a low-density living environment (not a congregated community) with a low level of direct support (service provider was not on site permanently, as would be the usual level of support). The four-bedroom emergency accommodation unit was operated under a 7-day support by the service partner. The accommodation was a house located in an urban residential area of an Irish border county. The service was opened in August 2021, and 17 unique individuals had lived there by the time the evaluation commenced in 2023. Individuals assigned to the accommodation were required to engage in education and training programmes as a condition for allocation. Before recommending accommodation, the provider completed an assessment to ensure that selected individuals did not require 24-h in-person support services and were aware of the conditions attached to the allocation.

## Positionality and Phases of the Study
Underpinning the four phases of the study [ ] was provider-led engagement, as required by the funding conditions of the PPI award and reported using the Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and the Public Version 2 (GRIPP 2) checklist ( ) ( ). The study partner established a study steering committee and a working group; however, due to personnel changes, the service provider lead changed three times during the period from when funding was announced to the completion of the study (October 2022 to May 2024). While this resulted in project delays, the reality for the study team was building a trusting and collaborative partnership, which was prioritized in discussions. When formed, the study steering committee's membership included a peer worker (PEH) and external members from the service provider. The academic team comprised healthcare professionals, all of whom were experienced researchers. Two team members had expertise in conducting research with PEH and marginalized communities (T.K., K.F.). The service partner led the development of study documents and interview guides in collaboration with the academic research team, and transparent reporting is provided using COREQ ( ) ( ).
 

## Recruitment and data collection

## Interviews
The service partner initially led purposive recruitment via telephone, endeavouring to contact all previous residents. They successfully contacted nine residents, and five individuals agreed to participate in the study. Interviews were scheduled locally at a location that suited the recruitment of previous residents. Four participants were interviewed following consent in October 2023. The service partner guided the recruitment of key informants (employees and community partners), and virtual interviews were conducted from October to November 2023. A pilot interview was conducted with a former resident before the commencement of data collection. This interview was conducted to ensure comprehensibility of the semi-structured interview questions and flow, and to gain insight into potential improvements to the interview schedule. Following consent, all interviews were recorded ( and ). The duration of interviews ranged from 30 to 60 min.
 

## Workshop
The service partner coordinated invitations to a purposive sample of individuals, asking them to participate in a subsequent half-day workshop for follow-up policy and planning. The workshop schedule was agreed upon during working group meetings, and the study partner provided information to confirm the date and location of the workshop. A 4-h, facilitated in-person workshop was held in February 2024 at a local, independent venue with suitable space for an active workshop and the availability of refreshments and services to meet the needs of the invited participants. A total of 16 people attended, including previous residents, key health and social care partners, employees, peer support workers, and two academic facilitators. Preliminary findings from the interview data were presented at the beginning of the workshop to set the scene following the initial introductions and start of the session. The workshop participants were divided into two groups and worked collaboratively to answer three rounds of questions, addressing key questions and next steps, including the challenges, solutions, and resources needed. Data were collected on flip charts and Post-it Notes, and feedback was provided by each group. A final summary of the discussions was presented by the facilitators and agreed upon by the participants as accurate.
Data collection and processes throughout the study’s phases were guided by the service partners’ knowledge of their system’s capacity and what was appropriate to ensure participants were always supported ( ).

## Data analysis
Interviews were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) ( , ). All interview recordings were transcribed verbatim. Following the interpretive principles of RTA, the analysis in this study was conducted collaboratively by two authors (A.D., N.B.). Broadly, the six steps of analysis involved in this study are: (i) familiarization with dataset; (ii) coding; (iii) generating initial themes; (iv) developing and reviewing themes; (v) refining, defining, and naming themes; and (vi) write-up. RTA was chosen to analyse this data given its inductive nature, the pre-understanding the research team held from working with the homeless charity, and insights gained from working with them.
During the initial phase, the data were thoroughly examined by reading the interview transcripts multiple times, with a specific focus on the language and metaphors used to describe their experiences. In Phase 2, A.D. assigned open codes to meaningful data units referring to a specific topic or concept. The initial open coding session involved analysing the transcripts. A.D. and N.B. met via Zoom to discuss any ambiguities or uncertainties regarding the data and direction of the open coding process. Once agreed upon, the key informant transcripts were coded by A.D. All participant transcripts were iteratively coded until no new codes emerged that were relevant to the research questions and aim. This process facilitated the exploration of a diverse range of potential interpretations of the data. In the following phase, codes were refined and organized into initial themes, which were further reviewed by A.D. and N.B. in Phase 4, leading to additional iterations of re-reading the transcripts and coding. This process resulted in the identification of four distinct themes. The fifth phase involved defining and naming these themes and finalizing the most important themes that could make the most meaningful contributions to understanding the impact of living in the novel housing model. Finally, Phase 6 involved composing the written report, which was then shared with the wider author team for consensus. To enhance the rigour and transparency of our analysis, the research team (including members of the service partner organization) engaged in regular discussions to facilitate challenging initial interpretations and identify alternative perspectives on the data.
Regarding the management of the workshop data, the two academic facilitators collated and summarized the workshop notes from flip charts and observations, and a final summary of key outcomes was presented to the group at the end of the session. The summary was confirmed as an accurate record of the discussions, supporting the content and data produced during the meeting. A finalized summary and recommendations, based on the workshop data and the analysis of interview data, were developed and shared within the study working group for feedback and clarification ( ).

## RESULTS
Nine interviews were completed. Individual in-person interviews were conducted with former residents (n = 4), and online interviews were conducted with service providers and key informants (n = 5) ( ). Following thematic analysis, four major themes and associated sub-themes were identified among residents, service providers, and key informants: Sense of Home Environment, Empowerment for Independent Living, Building New Identities, and Future Planning. This is further outlined in and below. The extracts presented in this section have been selected as deemed most insightful or powerful in embodying the essence of each theme amongst former residents, service providers, and key informants.
 

## Theme 1. Sense of home environment
A pivotal benefit of the residential accommodation model was the sense of home it provided residents with while they lived there. All residents valued having their own key to the house and own bedroom, which was not available to them in other homeless accommodation settings, whereby one resident referred to their experience of being in emergency hostel accommodation to that of being in a ‘cell’:
It felt like a home. And that was the first time in about three years that I felt I had a home. (IP3)
Residents consistently compared the physical space of the house to the temporary emergency accommodation in the local community, and they discussed a feeling of overwhelm and overcrowding when they stayed there. In comparison, the house had ‘No drama. No shouting. No screaming. No trouble. No drugs. No nothing’ (IP3), which they appreciated. Residents referred to the accommodation and its atmosphere as being like a ‘normal home’ (IP3) and ‘just like sharing a house’ (IP2). Residents frequently remarked on the sense of ‘peace’, ‘quiet’, and ‘calm’ in the house, compared to emergency accommodation, which could involve ‘running around with 30 or 40 people’ (IP4). One resident commented that being exposed to both emergency accommodation and the current model highlighted ‘how they can affect someone mentally’ (IP3) and encapsulated the sense of ownership and belonging they experienced in the house: ‘I felt like it was my place’ (IP3). Residents valued being able to socialize in the communal spaces and the option of going to their bedroom if they wanted time to be alone:
I had my own room and I had my own telly and stuff. If I didn’t wanna sit down with the boys I could go up and chill in my room… So if I wanted my own bit of space, you had your own bit of space. (IP1)
Residents appreciated the lack of surveillance and having to report when entering or leaving the premises, compared to the emergency accommodation, which had ‘cameras over the doors’ (IP4). There was an evident sense of independence and freedom in residents’ accounts while living in the house:
It was calmer and more free to come and go as you please. And you could eat when you wanted to. You didn’t only eat when you were fed at certain times. Yeah, you could eat freely. (IP2)
Key informants also appreciated and echoed how this model promoted residents’ sense of independence compared to other local homeless accommodations:
Everyone has their own front door key, people can come and go and there’s just that little bit more independence. It’s like a step down from that high support that you would get in (name of emergency accommodation). (IP7)
Residents and key informants also discussed how it was a more supportive environment for those recovering from addiction, as they experienced exposure and availability to drugs in prior settings ‘just being waved in your face the whole time’ (IP1). Some participants discussed that this change in environment supported them in their drug-use recovery, by removing the ‘temptation’ (IP9) as they were not being exposed to or being offered them.
A service provider referred to this new type of accommodation as the ‘middle man’ (IP5) between the large emergency accommodation and transitional housing. Residents appreciated that while this was a different type of accommodation, there were still rules to adhere to when sharing the house, including kitchen, bathrooms, sitting room, and groceries:
If you just obey the rules you know. Don’t have people calling… It’s not a house like a normal corporation house. It’s a (homeless organisation) house so it’s looking after people. (IP3)
Residents highlighted a sense of connection that residents developed with those they were living with at the time. The smaller scale of the residency resulted in them developing a sense of trust and safety with each other, which led to bonding and friendships forming. Participants discussed that the intimate environment of the house facilitated the formation of friendships in a natural way and provided space for them to have organic conversations.
Residents further discussed the rapport and relationship that they built with each other while living there—a routine developed of doing the shopping, cooking, and watching television together in the shared living area. Participants highlighted the enjoyment they experienced from having a structure and routine to support their day-to-day wellbeing, as well as the closeness they formed with other residents in approaching tasks together, which they have not experienced in previous accommodation models.

## Theme 2. Empowerment to independent living
Central to this new model of accommodation is the focus on supporting the development of home maintenance skills, which were required while living there and are intended to be further developed. Participants discussed how these key practical skills included paying your rent, purchasing food, as well as interpersonal aspects such as being accountable for tidiness and being respectful to other guests. Participant accounts suggested that these skills can be forgotten when experiencing homelessness and that other accommodation models do not encourage them to engage in them, whereby food is bought and prepared for them and cleaning is done by staff. Participants indicated their belief in their importance, especially if they are to share with others in the future, within their own property:
I went from there into a house cooking for myself, cleaning for myself, doing your own washing. You had to do everything for yourself. It was basically putting you back in society. (IP3)
Residents understood that the model was aimed ‘to try to help them to help themselves’ (IP4) in learning skills to maintain a household and education and employment. A service provider who worked in the accommodation unit during the pilot phase commented on their role in their lives:
I help them develop ways to do it independently as well whether it be using their phones or using diaries or stuff. It's just to help them build the skills needed to manage their own lives independently without the staff’s help. (IP9)
As the house was based in an urban residential area, a service provider emphasized the importance of promoting ‘good neighbourhood policy’ and supporting residents in integrating into their community, as well as the importance of supporting homeless people to engage in the community directly:
It’s very important that you blend into the community and that you don’t become you know the centre point of the community. We want all our participants and services to blend and you know really it’s about all that amalgamation. And that was really how it kind of happened…I’m a big believer in community-orientated supports… We believe in bringing people out into the community as opposed to bringing services in. (IP5)
This point echoes another key informant’s view regarding the potentially undermining impact that support staff entering the space where homeless people are staying can have:
It felt like I was nearly disempowering them by coming into their home and asking to see them as opposed to giving them the option to leave their space and come to the day service to meet me. (IP7)
A service provider gave the example of paying the rent as a way of instilling the responsibility of paying for their place:
The financial contribution is really to get them into the understanding because they’ve been in homelessness, so they haven’t been used to having that responsibility. So it’s teaching that without really because you still want to help them to be able to have a service that they’re able to go out and provide for themselves… it’s about building the skill and the awareness. It’s not about crippling them. (IP5)
While residents reported having a positive experience within the house, two residents said the experience made them aware that they would prefer not to house share in the future when securing their accommodation. Living in the accommodation, some residents became aware of their need for more personal space and desire to be on their own within their living environment. One participant discussed how living in the shared accommodation model was a motivator for them to secure individual living space in the future and what they want their home to be like.
However, a key informant noted that the shared housing accommodation is a likely housing arrangement for those who progress from the low-support housing accommodation, which the current model prepares them for:
I think a small number of people together you know, it almost gives them the feeling of house sharing which is what a lot of them are going to be moving into anyway. (IP8)
Key informants also commented on PEH’s desire to secure their own house (‘they get out from [name of emergency accommodation] that they just want to go straight into independent living’ (IP7)), while also highlighting gaps in knowledge that would inhibit their ability to live independently and maintain their own accommodation, which the model seeks to address. Residents understood the temporary nature of the accommodation and viewed it as a ‘pitstop’ to support the next point in securing a home, while another resident referred to how securing their own house felt like ‘just a stop away’ while living in the accommodation. The lower support model was viewed as a positive step in guiding individuals out of homelessness: ‘It helps people move on without throwing them into the deep end’ (IP7). Similarly, the need to learn these independent living skills before entering transition houses is crucial, as one resident acknowledges, ‘Some succeed and some don’t’ (IP2) when transitioning.

## Theme 3. Building new identities
Building on the practical skills that residents developed, the experience of living in the house had a significant impact on their personal lives and how they viewed themselves:
It’s made me find who I am. I didn’t know who I was for a very long time. I didn’t realise the potential I had. But it gives me positive motivation, positivity… if they didn’t put the right thing in front of me, I wouldn’t be sitting here with you now. (IP3)
A cornerstone of supporting these new identities is that residents are working towards gaining employment or education, and residents are engaged in a range of educational and employment training, such as woodwork and computer IT skills. Although a staff member’s office was located on the top floor of the house, there was no sense of being monitored, and residents developed a sense of rapport with the staff member. This is reflected in how residents became independent as they learned new skills: ‘You’re not so much co-dependent as in staff-wise’ (IP2). The staff member who was based in the house during the daytime was critical to guiding and supporting their development while staying in the residence:
He pushed me to my limit. I told him to because I always need, I think I work better under pressure. So the more pressure on me, the harder I work. He got me into the course. He got me into counselling. (IP3)
One resident noted that the house was crucial in ‘getting my headspace back’ (IP2). Interestingly, one resident observed how homeless services tend to over-focus on self-improvement when being homeless, while another commented that while it was a supportive environment, it also gave him a place to relax:
I was just settling. I was trying not to worry about the future. You just don’t know what’s gonna happen when you’re being housed… Just trying to relax while my time was being spent there, you know? (IP3)

## Theme 4. Future Planning
Residents and stakeholders acknowledged that homeless incidence in Ireland is ‘really bad at the moment’ (IP4) and described it as being at ‘rock bottom’ (IP3) in the context of Ireland. Regarding recommendations for the future improvement of this accommodation model, there was a resounding need described among residents and stakeholders for more building space to develop further, pilot, and implement this model of accommodation in Ireland. It was acknowledged that a key challenge of this is navigating ‘the finite budget and resources’ (IP8).
It’s helpful for me because you don’t be among all them people. It’s like hard on anyone to be homeless but if you have your own room and there’s only not many of you in the building… I’m sure if they have more buildings like (name of residence) I’m sure it would be a lot better for the homeless situation. (IP4)
Some residents commented on the importance of rules and boundaries in the house, particularly regarding cleaning and cooking, which could be more enforced, so it is not left to the same individuals living there. It was recommended that greater awareness and education about the model need to be provided to potential eligible residents. This would ensure that residents would be aware of how the accommodation is arranged and what would be expected of them:
I think there should be more information about it to let them know what’s involved in it. People think you’re just thrown in there and that’s it. (IP2)
Both residents and stakeholders commented on the importance of being aware of the personalities and addictions of those living in the house [‘dynamics of personalities together’ (IP5)] and how some individuals may not be suitable for living together. They discussed the need for screening for compatibility, where possible, for individuals who may be challenging for others due to where they are in their addiction journey or personality type:
You were just getting used to one personality and then it was gone and then a new personality would come in and it could have been a hostile personality. (IP2)
However, a service provider also discussed refining the referral system for the accommodation. However, they noted that there is always some level of uncertainty and risk about whether an individual is ready for a lower support model in the homelessness care pathway:
I think there was maybe some of the wrong people referred and I think it was because there was an excitement naturally you know from the community in general and staff… I think it’s something that we really challenge ourselves on every in social care that what you want for someone is not necessarily what they want for themselves and where you believe them to be is not necessarily where they are (IP6)
The accommodation model was developed to change and reflect the user profile who needs their services, which was observed to continue in the future due to changing economic circumstances likely:
Like the dynamic of homelessness has changed drastically over the last number of years and the dynamic of people presenting. Like stereotypically homelessness was you know people in addiction or the classic street drinkers… whereas now you’ve so much more and even just economical homelessness. (IP5)

## Summary of workshop
The workshop began with a reflection on a memorable word from the initial presentation of the findings from the interviews. Key words and discussions centred on freedom in decision-making and choice, as well as independence and developing skills for independent living and transitioning from a larger, chaotic environment to a smaller facility. The challenges, solutions, and resources targeted at the micro and meso levels are summarized in , representing the discussions from the workshops. A final set of recommendations was developed and agreed upon within the study team following careful reflection on the findings from the interviews, the summary report from the workshop, and the discussion ( ).
 

## DISCUSSION
This study aimed to assess the feasibility of a novel low-density model of emergency homelessness accommodation in Ireland, following its implementation in the previous 18-month period and the development of recommendations for service planning. The findings from this study, based on interviews with former residents, service providers, and key informants in the locality, present the transformative impact of using this space for the people who created a home within the emergency accommodation unit. The findings are crucial in considering a broader system-based approach to homelessness. suggest that transitioning into permanent housing is a significant milestone and that ‘humanistic needs’, including thriving and communicating life goals, receive ‘less attention and as a result are less well understood in this population’ (p. 421). Currently, Irish homeless services are publicly funded, and they often fail to meet demands due to a tripling of PEH over the last decade ( ). In Ireland, homelessness was acknowledged through legislation passed in 1988 ( ). Considering the broader systems approach that is necessary in Ireland to manage homelessness and provide accommodation, the 1988 legislation used male pronouns to consider who is homeless, and this established housing solutions for PEH to be limited in design. confirm that homeless accommodation in Ireland is ‘traditionally centred on the provision of hostels offering congregate, temporary, transitional supported housing’ (p. 38), and noting that there is not a ‘clear operational distinction between what constitutes “emergency” and “temporary accommodation”’. (p. 38). This lack of clarity impacts expectations of what a home could and should be and the transition process within the sector generally, and this was confirmed by participants in the current feasibility study, noting that the intervention was ‘a middleman’. highlight that the development of supported housing in Ireland is designed around population groups, including ‘lone parents, young people leaving social services care, ex-offenders and people with addictions, and that it provides temporary accommodation for homeless people who are also within these groups’. also highlight housing interventions predominantly focused on males and report cost containment through the use of supportive housing in the US.

 review of evidence of service for women experiencing homelessness, for example, highlighted challenges for this community that represent around 50% of PEH in Ireland. Recently, suggests women experiencing homelessness have challenges because providing services for PEH is stigmatizing as they focus on personal ‘inherent characteristics’ and ‘life circumstances’ at a point in life (p. 4), specifically fleeing domestic violence, for example.
The model of accommodation examined in this study was a lower-density living environment, and the service provider sought to obtain external real-time feedback to support conversations for continued funding and to understand the applicability and acceptability of providing an alternative housing unit. Higher-density living accommodation units are usual for PEH ( ), so introducing this type of intervention was unusual. The accounts of participants in this study suggest that the supportive physical environment was positive overall, and comments note the positive impact on remaining drug-free, as there was no one around who had addiction problems or was dealing. The rules within the accommodation were welcomed and valued, and the lower-density living conditions led to friendships and connections, as well as empowerment and assistance in building oneself, fostering trust, and gaining independence. suggest that ‘eudaimonic wellbeing may be important to persons transitioning out of homelessness’ (p. 31). NICE guidelines ( ) emphasize the importance of integrating health and social care services for PEH, which aligns with evidence supporting changes to address poorer health outcomes and reduced mental health support ( , ). Thus, the impact of the accommodation provided, as well as the environment, is critical to the wellbeing of individuals who experience homelessness.
The lack of available housing stock in Ireland has led to increased waiting lists and precarity for PEH and at-risk individuals with traditional accommodation targeting single adults and primarily males ( ). While Ireland remains committed to the Declaration of Lisbon ( ) and its goal of ending homelessness by 2030, acknowledge the inconsistencies and limited services available to women and lone parents, as well as the reliance on not-for-profit sectors for providing emergency housing solutions in Ireland. In 2021, more than 50 non-governmental organizations received €100 million to provide mainly congregated residential services. An additional €87 million was paid to Bed and Breakfast (B&B) and hotel owners to provide temporary and emergency accommodation ( ). distinguish (i) private emergency accommodation, which includes private hotels, B&Bs and other residential facilities; (ii) Supported Temporary Accommodation as purpose-built family hubs with support services; and (iii) Temporary Emergency Accommodation provides minimal (or no) onsite support services (p. 4). Funding for accommodation providers continues to increase, with the current estimate for 2024 being €361 million ( ).
Participants in this study focused on the value of the space provided, noting the silence and freedom to live, and being personally trusted, while living within the house's rules, highlighted the normality of the experience as they learned to adjust to living with fewer people. For PEH, these challenges differ from living in usual congregated settings and experiencing fear and constant noise ( , ). highlight the fear of violence and harm for people accessing shelters as ‘predominant concerns’ in their recent review of evidence. Fear and violence were not reported in the evaluation but were previously evident among participants.

Data from this study emphasizes the benefits of step-down and step-up housing, transitioning housing, and alternatives to a larger congregated setting approach to housing solutions on wellbeing and self, consistent with previous evidence ( ). Participants in this study note previous challenging experiences during the period of homelessness, and the current findings provide a strengths-based lens, emphasizing specifics in supporting individual growth and potential during their transition to independent living. Positives include learning skills to be independent again, budgeting, cooking, and engaging in education and training for employment. ## Limitations
Although this study offers novel insights into the feasibility, experiences, and future development of low-support homeless accommodation in Ireland, some limitations must be acknowledged when considering the study findings. The strength of completing a feasibility study is recognized in the literature ( ). The sample size in the study was small as a purposive recruitment strategy was adopted to recruit individuals who had lived in specific accommodation or were relevant key informants. We acknowledge the limitations of the data, which are based on a small sample size, and we accept that no study data would have existed without the leadership of the PPI partner, who wanted to build a partnership with the academic team and undertake the study. The PPI partner led the recruitment process and access to all participants. At the time of recruitment, the service had been running for 18 months, and data collection was based on a limited population, which may not be generalizable. The workshop was not recorded in compliance with ethical approval. Although transcribing data from the workshop meeting would have been beneficial, the recording of conversations may have acted as a barrier, affecting attendance. As the meeting was not recorded, note-taking and summaries of information were used as techniques to support accuracy in reporting.
The importance of the data from this study was acknowledged by the local county council, which invited the study team to present at a meeting [October 2024]. The council members are engaging with the service provider to continue funding, examine the access pathways to the accommodation, and enable the service provider to facilitate direct admissions. The project team experienced some challenges due to personnel changes within the homeless charity. This experience echoes previous evidence ( ), which highlighted that staff time and commitment are needed to ensure both the relationship and expertise are maintained within PPI research practices. The team overcame staff turnover by holding regular meetings, where they openly communicated project goals and offered support to new PPI team members. Additionally, a short timeline extension was implemented to reflect personnel changes and ensure project completion. While the majority of team communication was held online, the team also held a meeting in person, before data collection, to view the accommodation model and discuss the project rollout, which facilitated rapport and trust within the team.

## CONCLUSION
This is the first study to assess the feasibility of a type of accommodation from the perspective of residents and key informants from a service provider-led study. Evidence from this study will inform the development of future service initiatives, training, and collaborations across micro and meso levels, supporting individuals experiencing homelessness and all those involved in providing health and social care, as well as advocating for further funding. The novel accommodation model provided residents with a transitional space to develop a sense of independence, acquire new skills, and establish a personal identity. Although the findings are based on a small purposive sample, they provide rich insights into a new homelessness support used in the co-design process to evaluate the feasibility, impact, and improvement needs of this accommodation model.
