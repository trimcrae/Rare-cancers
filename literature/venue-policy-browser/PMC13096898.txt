## Introduction
The health care sector contributes significantly to greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, accounting for 8.5 % of total U.S. emissions. This study identifies leading strategies for health plans to reduce their carbon footprint and outlines a practical pathway for incremental implementation.

## Methods
Using a modified Delphi approach, representatives of nine health plans were recruited via convenience sampling to evaluate 29 GHG reduction strategies. Participants rated each strategy using criteria adapted from the National Quality Forum (NQF) and provided qualitative feedback.

## Results
Seven top strategies emerged as the most impactful, with four in the Resource Conservation and Management domain, two in Governance and Building Capacity, and one in Government Policies. These strategies represent a focused starting point for health plans seeking to take meaningful climate action. Findings highlight the importance of integrating climate considerations into quality improvement initiatives to align with organizational priorities. Strategies within the direct control of health plans were deemed more actionable, and framing climate action as a population health issue was found to foster broader consensus across the sector.

## Conclusion
Health plans have a unique opportunity to reduce GHG emissions while improving health outcomes, even without mandatory climate-related reporting requirements. As climate policy priorities shift, voluntary action remains a powerful lever for change. Focusing on these top seven strategies offers a clear, incremental pathway forward. Further research is needed to identify effective accountability mechanisms that support sustainable, measurable progress in health care decarbonization.

## Introduction
The health care sector plays a substantial role in greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, contributing approximately 8.5 % of total GHG emissions in the U.S., primarily from energy use, supply chains, transportation, and waste [ , ]. Government leadership and policy have historically played a limited role in driving climate health efforts, although recent initiatives have signaled a growing commitment to change [ ]. In 2022, the U.S Government's Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) recommended approaching health care decarbonization aligned with the Greenhouse Gas Protocol (GHGP) [ ]. The GHGP is a globally recognized framework for measuring, managing, and reporting on Scope 1 (direct), Scope 2 (indirect from energy use) and Scope 3 (indirect from the value chain) emissions [ ]. While government can shape the broader climate agenda, organizations are essential in designing and executing strategies that directly advance decarbonization efforts within the health care sector.
Current research on health care decarbonization emphasizes strategies within delivery systems, such as through care coordination, appropriate prescribing, virtual care, and avoiding duplication of tests or procedures [ , ]. Post-COVID-19 studies show telemedicine served as a positive contributor to reducing health care’s carbon footprint due to the rise of virtual visits during the pandemic [ , , ]. One California health system avoided approximately 17,000 metric tons of GHG emissions between 2019–2021 through increased virtual visits, for example [ ]. Despite progress, standardized measures that systematically track GHG emissions across the sector are missing [ ]. Organizations such as Practice Greenhealth and Health Care Without Harm offer resources for health systems [ , , ], but opportunities exist for health plans to adopt and scale these strategies. Health plans, also known as payors, have significant influence over care delivery and resource allocation, yet their potential to drive decarbonization remains largely unexplored.
The primary objective of this study was to identify the leading U.S.-based strategies for health plans to reduce their carbon footprint. A health plan is considered an organization that finances or arranges health care services and is responsible for managing benefits, provider networks, and access to care [ ]. Through a consensus-making process, this study establishes a practical pathway for implementing incremental changes at the health plan level, leveraging research focused on delivery systems as a foundation. This work initiates efforts to bridge the gap between climate science and operational decisions, demonstrating how small, evidenced-informed steps can collectively advance decarbonization efforts and promote sustainability across the health care sector.

## Methods
This study used a modified Delphi panel method to reach consensus on the top decarbonization strategies for health plans [ ]. Two survey rounds were conducted between August and October 2024, with one Delphi panel discussion meeting convened between survey rounds.
2.1

## Decarbonization strategies selection
The National Committee for Quality Assurance (NCQA, a nonprofit organization for healthcare quality improvement) implemented the study, and selected strategies for the Delphi panel survey by reviewing literature on health care decarbonization, interviewing two external consultants, and gathering recommendations from an advisory panel. The literature review, which included peer-reviewed journals and grey literature, was conducted between 2023 and–2024 and focused on identifying GHG strategies within the health care sector. The primary author led the categorization of these strategies into seven topic areas: 1) development of conceptual frameworks, 2) initiation of cross-collaborations or partnerships, 3) development of health policies, 4) development of standard concepts, 5) adoption of new clinical and environmental practices, including technological advancements, 6) creation of educational tools, and 7) climate indicators or measures development. Overall, 26 climate-related strategies were identified. Based on these activities, NCQA initially developed Survey 1 to include 26 GHG reduction strategies.
Upon advisory panel review, Survey 1 was modified to include 27 strategies, including the 26 strategies identified during the literature review and one that was added based on subject matter expertise in the field. The GHG reduction strategies were organized by the advisory panel and primary author into six domains: technology and innovation, governance and building capacity, incentives, resource conservation and management, reporting and disclosures, and government policies. Two strategies deemed important by Delphi panel members during the facilitated discussion were added to Survey 2, for a total of 29 strategies.

2.2

## Composition of advisory panel members
A convenience sampling approach was used to recruit experts from within NCQA’s and the funder’s existing network to form an advisory panel, based on expertise in health care decarbonization strategies. The advisory panel was comprised of six nationally recognized experts knowledgeable in GHG reduction, sustainability, health policy, measure concepts, and principles of quality measurement and improvement in the health system space. The role of these members was to vet key assumptions relevant to GHG reduction, provide content expertise, and support strategy selection for the Delphi panel survey.

2.3

## Recruitment and composition of Delphi panel members
The Delphi panel was comprised of a diverse set of health plan representatives knowledgeable in the areas of environmental sustainability, GHG reduction, and decarbonization. In total, we recruited nine health plans across NCQA’s internal networks to participate in the modified Delphi panel through convenience sampling. Delphi panel members represented not-for-profit and for-profit health plans across all lines of business (commercial, exchange, Medicare, Medicaid) and were involved in health care delivery in the U.S., including the Northwest, Pacific, Southwest, Central, Great Lakes, Upper Midwest, Atlantic, and Northeast. Seven plans had a membership of >500,000, while two plans had a membership of 10,000 to 500,000. All panel members had a title related to climate sustainability or were actively working on climate sustainability efforts within their organization. The panel size was reduced to eight during the second survey round due to attrition.

2.4

## Survey 1 and 2 rating methodology and delphi panel meeting
In August 2024, each health plan representative completed Survey 1 using Qualtrics XM, v.2024, rating 27 strategies across three criteria adapted from the National Quality Forum (i.e., importance, usability, and feasibility) using a 9-point scale ranging from 1 (low) to 9 (high) for each criterion (e.g., low importance to high importance). To capture qualitative findings, participants had the opportunity to provide comments about their ratings for each strategy in the survey.
The three criteria for rating each strategy were defined as follows:•Importance: Extent to which the strategy focus is important to health plans in making significant gains in tracking and reducing GHG emissions in health care organizations for a specific high-priority aspect of health care where there is variation in or a less-than-optimal carbon footprint.
•Feasibility: Extent to which the identified strategies require infrastructure and resources that are readily available or could be captured without undue burden and can be implemented to reduce GHG emissions.
•Usability: Extent to which health plans are using or could use the identified strategies to facilitate uptake and improvement of carbon reduction efforts.

In October 2024, a virtual meeting was held for panelists to discuss Survey 1 ratings. Prior to the meeting, panelists were provided with their individual ratings across all strategies compared to the group's ratings and distributions. The discussion focused on importance, usability, and feasibility ratings across strategies and aimed to explore consensus and collect feedback through tailored questions on the facilitators and barriers to implementing these strategies. The meeting also served as a venue to discuss qualitative themes compiled from Survey 1. Eight of nine participants joined the facilitated discussion and participated in Survey 2, which included two additional GHG reduction strategies identified as important during the discussion. Health plan representatives completed Survey 2 by the end of October 2024.

2.5

## Quantitative analysis of strategy ratings
Descriptive analyses were conducted for survey results from each round. To guide analysis of panel ratings, we used the RAND Appropriateness Method (RAM), a two-step method that first identifies the level of agreement based on the distribution of panel ratings ( ) and then determines whether the median panel rating was “low” (≤3), “uncertain” (>3 but <7), or “high” (≥7) [ ]. Strategies were determined to exhibit agreement if at least 75 percent of panelists rated in the same range (i.e., ≤3, >3 but <7, or ≥7) on the 9-point scale for a given criterion (i.e., importance, usability, feasibility). Disagreement occurred when at least 33 percent of panelists (i.e., at least three) rated in opposite median range extremes. All other scenarios were considered indeterminate. 
Median ratings were classified based on where they fell along the 9-point scale (i.e., lower third = low, mid-range = uncertain, or upper third = high). Based on the distribution of panel ratings, a measure could reach agreement, disagreement, or be indeterminate. The median panel rating and the absolute deviation from the median, which measure the central tendency of panel ratings and the dispersion of the panel ratings, respectively, were calculated for each strategy and by each rating criterion (importance, usability, feasibility). Then, the level of agreement was assigned to each strategy rating by criterion based on the RAM approach. There were nine possible classifications for each strategy in terms of median rating and level of agreement ( ). Strategies for potential implementation ideally would exhibit agreement and be highly rated across each criterion. 
Lastly, a prioritization category was applied to each strategy according to . This approach prioritized a strategy’s importance followed by usability and then feasibility according to median ratings (i.e., high, uncertain, low). 
Survey 2 ratings were used to determine the Delphi panel’s final level of agreement.

2.6

## Qualitative thematic analysis
Open-ended comments from both survey rounds were analyzed using an inductive thematic approach to identify patterns in participant feedback. To ensure rigor, the team maintained an audit trail of coding decisions and engaged in peer debriefing throughout the process. Intercoder reliability was established through consensus rather than statistical measures, and participant anonymity was preserved.
For Survey 1, two independent coders reviewed all comments and generated initial codes, which were then reconciled through discussion with a senior reviewer to ensure consistency. For Survey 2, due to the limited number of comments, the senior reviewer conducted coding directly using the same inductive process. Feedback from the Delphi discussion was incorporated to validate and refine these themes. Final synthesis resulted in three central themes under each strategy domain.

## Results
3.1

## Quantitative results
For Survey 1, there was a 100% response rate (n = 9). Eight of nine participants completed Survey 2, for a response rate of 89%.
Across importance, usability, and feasibility, the agreement percentage increased for Survey 2 ( ). Importance received the highest proportion of agreement among the panel members for both surveys, with the percentage increasing by approximately 3.5% between rounds. The agreement percentage increased by about 6.5% for usability and 20% for feasibility between survey rounds. Panelists reached agreement on the importance criterion for 15 strategies, on usability for three strategies, and on feasibility for seven strategies. While the mean strategy rating was slightly lower for each category after Survey 2, the overall level of consensus was greater based on the increase in agreement within each category. 
Of note, there was disagreement in Survey 1 for six strategies for usability and three strategies for feasibility; however, there was no disagreement for importance, usability, or feasibility in Survey 2 across any strategies. All strategies either reached agreement or were indeterminate.

The other strategy (Implement Energy-Efficient Lighting, Heating, Cooling and Other Equipment in Health Plan Controlled or Owned Buildings) achieved agreement on importance but remained indeterminate on usability and feasibility. 3.2

## Qualitative results

These sub-themes contributed to the rankings of importance, feasibility, and usability for strategies within the domain. Domains are listed in order of overall ranking following Survey 2. 
In the Governance and Capacity Building domain, support from executive leadership and training opportunities were deemed necessary for successful implementation. Difficulty in measuring impact was also noted, as staff need to understand GHG goals and be trained on which data are important. Tying equity and social determinants of health to climate goals was recommended to develop goals over time and align with data collection efforts.
In the Resource Conservation and Management domain, most comments emphasized the ineffective approach of the strategies due to high barriers for implementation, specifically due to limited data availability, the geographic location of the health plan, or access to rebates. Similarly, implementation challenges for the Reporting and Disclosure domain were characterized as expensive, specifically for health plans to obtain certifications. However, the use of the ENERGY STAR® by the Ohio Hospital Association was raised as an example that could be investigated further.
The politicization of climate change was raised as a concern for the strategies within the Governance Policies domain, as they are dependent on federal and state leadership goals and support is needed to implement specific policies. Panelists noted that specific policies may limit innovation, but if new policies are developed, a phased approach for implementation would be needed.
For the Technology and Innovation domain, panelists indicated it would be difficult to measure impact at the health plan level, given challenges with data sharing and interoperability between providers, health systems, and health plans. They also emphasized that technology is often used for strategies related to health equity goals rather than sustainability goals. This theme crossed over into the Incentives domain, where it was not clear to panelists that sustainability incentives would be patient-focused, highlighting misalignment between quality and sustainability goals. Panelists stated they did not have enough information or details on the structure of the incentive, but if given the choice, they would prioritize quality incentives over sustainability incentives due to financial constraints. ## Discussion
The modified Delphi process revealed three central themes that offer insight into how health plans can advance climate actions. First, the broader national climate narrative and federal leadership play a major role in shaping policy environments and accelerating sector-wide decarbonization. Second, focusing on strategies within a health plan’s direct control or ownership enables organizations to make gains in reducing GHG emissions. Third, aligning climate sustainability with health care quality goals ensures that environmental efforts not only reduce emissions, but also enhance patient outcomes and operational efficiency. Together, these themes underscore the importance of integrating climate action into the core functions of health plan financing and care delivery.
4.1

## National climate narrative plays a major role in progress
Qualitative findings revealed that climate-focused sustainability is often viewed as highly politicized, leaving participants unsure of how to make progress in this space, or hesitant to act until legislative goals are clear. Health plans that declined participation also exhibited some level of hesitation in participating in this study, particularly if they did not already have decarbonization initiatives in place. Even though specific policies for reporting GHG emissions in the health care space are optional, health plans in this study identified appointing a sustainability officer as a top strategy, aligning with the previous White House and HHS pledge to designate an executive-level lead for reducing emissions by 2023 [ ]. Additionally, participants ranked government fines and penalties for failing to report GHG emissions as a top strategy, which was the only top-rated strategy identified in this study that is beyond the direct control of health plans. This highlights that health plans recognize the critical role of government in setting expectations and driving accountability in health care.
Although the government plays a critical role in setting direction, starting in 2025, previous climate related policies have been revoked [ ]. But accrediting bodies also have significant influence over organizational behavior, by setting expectations for performance, accountability, and continuous improvement. Currently, no accrediting bodies in the health care sector mandate the use or integration of the GHG Protocol to measure Scope 1, Scope 2, or Scope 3 emissions [ , ]. The Joint Commission, however, has developed optional standards for Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions for health system reporting [ ]. No health plan-level accreditation standards were found during the literature review.
Among the three emission types, Scope 3 emissions are often the most difficult to measure and reduce, as this scope often requires close collaborations with multiple suppliers [ ]. Given the complexity of measuring Scope 3 emissions in health care, which requires more time to fully understand and standardize, it follows logically that early accreditation standards focus on Scopes 1 and 2. Delphi panel members similarly prioritized strategies within a health plan’s direct control, especially since their influence over suppliers or external partners is often limited.

4.2

## GHG strategies focused on health plan controlled resources
Panelists agreed that it was more manageable to target strategies for which health plans could collect data directly and for which they had direct leadership oversight. While most strategies were within health plan oversight, participants acknowledged that more work must be done to develop and disseminate case studies to help build the evidence base, create educational tools for staff, and develop climate-related guidelines, as the current evidence base is limited.
Interestingly, of the top seven strategies identified, only one strategy (Measuring Scope 2 Emissions (Energy) at Health Plan Controlled or Owned Facilities) reached agreement across all three rating criteria. Additionally, all the top strategies in the Resource and Conservation Management domain were related to energy use. During the modified Delphi process, the Ohio Hospital Association’s use of ENERGY STAR® was identified as a case study of interest, as it noted that achieving sustainability in health care requires investment in infrastructure to track environmental performance while balancing cost and accessibility. While this program alone does not provide all the necessary standards and measures, panelists acknowledged that this program could be prioritized as an intervention or best practice. By leveraging programs like ENERGY STAR®, health plans have an opportunity to demonstrate leadership in sustainability, building an evidence base of case studies and best practices that can guide the development of effective standards and metrics to build resilient health systems.

4.3

## Alignment of climate sustainability with quality goals
Panelists noted persistent organizational misalignment between sustainability efforts and broader climate goals and this was a central conversation across the Governance and Capacity Building, Technology and Innovation, and Incentives domains. However, they acknowledged that certain strategies—such as telemedicine—were positively received because they aligned with existing organizational priorities, particularly increasing access to care. This suggests that when decarbonization strategies can be clearly mapped to patient-centered benefits, they are more likely to gain traction and be viewed as part of the quality improvement agenda. Strengthening this connection could help demonstrate that climate action and quality improvement are not competing priorities but mutually reinforcing efforts.
Yet, despite this need for alignment, climate sustainability remains largely absent from national quality improvement frameworks used in practice. The Triple Aim for Healthcare Improvement (of 2008) has played a major role in outlining the goals of health care in larger society and was updated recently to the Quintuple Aim (of 2023), by adding care team well-being and health equity dimensions [ , ]. There is an opportunity to expand the current Quintuple Aim framework to include a sixth dimension of quality—climate sustainability—where decarbonization goals are aligned with quality improvement initiatives to enhance overall health outcomes. Sustainability is increasingly recognized as an essential dimension of high-quality care within and beyond the U.S [ , ]. Positioning sustainability as a core quality principle ensures that health plans advance patient outcomes while contributing to reducing climate impacts, aligning with the broader mission of delivering safe, effective, and high-quality care.

## Limitations
5.1

## Sample size for Delphi panel
The panel’s small sample size fell below the recommended minimum size of at least 12 participants for a modified Delphi process [ ], limiting the generalizability of findings to health plans. All panelists were already engaged in climate strategies within their organizations, which may bias the prioritization of strategic domains. Nonetheless, the results offer a valuable starting point for identifying actionable steps health plans can take toward climate decarbonization and future sustainability efforts.

5.2

## Decarbonization strategies identified in literature
Our list of decarbonization strategies was derived from literature focused primarily on interventions within health delivery systems, which limit the breadth of strategies considered. In contrast, strategies specific to health plans are less represented in the literature, resulting in a list that is not fully comprehensive. Consequently, some important approaches relevant to health plans may have been omitted, and future research should explore additional strategies tailored to payor-level operations and impact.

6

## Future work
Given health plans’ request for additional guidance, there is an opportunity for accrediting bodies to develop standards and metrics related to health care decarbonization. For instance, NCQA’s Population Health Accreditation or Health Plan Accreditation programs could incorporate the leading strategies identified by panelists in this study as a starting point [ , ]. These programs focus on identifying the health care needs of individuals and improving systems for better health outcomes; incorporating decarbonization strategies would align with these overarching goals and further align climate sustainability with quality goals.
There are additional opportunities to explore how health plans can work with delivery systems to achieve mutual health outcomes through learning collaboratives or case studies. For example, health plans could offer financial incentives for providers to adopt energy-efficient practices, adjust coverage policies to favor lower-impact treatments, and prioritize sustainable investments such as renewable energy. They can also promote member education on health care choices that reduce carbon emissions, adopt technology solutions like telemedicine to cut emissions, and emphasize preventive care to reduce long-term disease burdens [ ]. Collaborating with delivery systems and stakeholders to define the strategy and integrate GHG reduction into quality standards can further align health care with consensus-based, climate-conscious goals.
Although not explicitly studied in this work, health plans could also partner with policymakers and environmental experts to set impactful targets and incentivize innovation through bonus payments and sustainability-linked funding opportunities, as is done within Medicare Advantage programs [ ]. Another recent example is MassHealth, a Medicaid program that requires inpatient hospitals to report on third-party validated Scope 1 and 2 emissions. It is the first and only public health plan to do so [ , ]. This example demonstrates how payors can leverage their contracting power to drive climate accountability, and there is more to learn as this regulation was implemented in 2025. By leveraging their influence, health plans can become catalysts for change, improving both health outcomes and environmental sustainability.

7

## Conclusion
The health care sector must address its significant contribution to GHG emissions to protect public health. This study highlights the top strategies that health plans can implement to reduce their carbon footprint. Emphasizing climate sustainability as essential to promoting high-quality care, even as climate policy priorities continue to shift, creates an opportunity for broader stakeholder engagement and collaboration. Focusing on these top seven strategies offers a clear, incremental pathway forward. Additional research is needed to identify effective mechanisms for measuring progress in reducing GHG emissions and improving climate-related health outcomes.
