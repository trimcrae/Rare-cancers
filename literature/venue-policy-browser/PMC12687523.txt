## Background
With China’s rapidly aging population, addressing the health management of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) in community settings has become crucial for dementia prevention. This study focuses on developing an integrated primary care intervention (IPC) within primary care system to support home-dwelling older adults with MCI and explore the underlying barriers based on the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) to the implementation and further scale up of the IPC intervention.

## Methods
This study employs a hybrid effectiveness-implementation research type II design. The randomized controlled trail will be conducted to evaluate the IPC intervention for older adults with MCI through primary care networks. The intervention group receiving 16-week family doctor contract services (including online cognitive training, enhanced health management, and usual care) and the control group receiving usual care only. Effectiveness will be measured through changes in participants’ cognitive function (primary outcome), self-efficacy and quality of life (secondary outcome), complemented by cost-effectiveness analysis. Implementation factors will be examined through the CFIR before and during the intervention process.

## Discussion
This study is expected to demonstrate the effectiveness and implementation of the IPC intervention in managing older MCI adults within primary care setting. The implementation analysis will identify key factors for scaling up the intervention within China’s primary care system, including necessary policy adjustments and training requirements for primary care providers. The findings will provide both practical guidance for China’s dementia prevention strategies and contribute to global knowledge about implementing community-based cognitive health interventions in primary care settings.

## Background
The global aging population has created a pressing public health challenge, with a vast number of community-dwelling older adults living with Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) [ , ]. MCI is characterized by progressive decline in memory or other cognitive functions that does not significantly impair activities of daily living and does not meet diagnostic criteria for dementia [ ]. As a high-risk prodromal stage of dementia, MCI represents the optimal intervention window for dementia prevention and management [ ]. Evidence suggests that timely interventions at this stage may delay disease progression in approximately 30% of cases [ ].
In China, proactive prevention and management of dementia has emerged as a critical public health imperative in China’s healthy aging initiative. By the end of 2024, adults aged 60 years and older accounted for 22.0% of China’s total population [ ]. Epidemiological data from 2020 revealed a dementia prevalence rate of 6.04% (representing 15.07 million cases) and MCI prevalence of 15.54% (38.77 million cases) among this aging demographic [ ], imposing substantial burdens on individuals, families, and society at large. This pressing challenge has been formally recognized in major national policy documents, including the Healthy China 2030 Blueprint and the Healthy China Action Plan (2019–2030), all of which explicitly mandate intensified efforts for dementia prevention and control. However, the majority of individuals with MCI reside in home-community settings in China, with less than 0.4% actively seeking medical attention. It is estimated that over 50% of untreated MCI cases will progress to clinical dementia within five years [ ]. These findings underscore the critical need for scientifically validated, community-adapted intervention strategies for older adults with MCI as a vital approach to mitigate China's growing dementia burden.
Primary care, characterized by first-contact accessibility, care coordination, continuity, and comprehensiveness [ ], has demonstrated significant positive associations with improved health outcomes and cost-effectiveness [ ]. Given the limited availability of specialist medical resources and hospital beds, coupled with the generally lower socioeconomic status of older adults in China, primary care represents the most accessible, economical, and feasible approach to deliver health services for older adults with MCI [ , ]. The critical role of primary care providers in managing this population has gained international recognition, as evidenced by multiple World Health Organization (WHO) reports [ ]. While existing evidence supports the cost-effectiveness of primary care-based integrated interventions for older adults with MCI, implementation research from China and other developing countries remains notably scarce. Although China’s essential public health programs currently include health management services for older adults, few studies have specifically focused on MCI populations or explored the effective integration of non-pharmacological interventions with basic public health services through family doctor teams. Further research is urgently needed to address this gap and inform evidence-based practice.
Current research primarily focuses on three key areas: (1) analyzing the potential roles and responsibilities of primary care providers in managing community-dwelling older adults with MCI or dementia [ , ]; (2) examining the effectiveness of integrated care interventions for older adults with MCI within primary care settings [ – ]; and (3) systematically reviewing evidence-based intervention strategies, assessment tools, and clinical guidelines for MCI management [ , ]. A 2021 systematic review revealed that while substantial theoretical support exists and numerous technical tools/strategies are available for MCI management towards older adults [ ], there remains a significant lack of high-quality evidence regarding their practical implementation in routine primary care practice.
Aligned with China’s healthy aging priorities for dementia prevention, this study employs an effectiveness-implementation hybrid type II design to assess the effectiveness of integrated primary care (IPC) intervention through cluster randomized controlled trail and identify the implementation determinants of IPC intervention within primary care setting.

## Conceptual model
This study employs a dual theoretical framework integrating self-efficacy theory and social support theory to address the needs of older adults with MCI.
Grounding our intervention design in Bandura’s (1977) self-efficacy theory [ ], we conceptualized that the IPC intervention would enhance participants’ belief in their capability to manage cognitive challenges. Self-efficacy, defined as one’s conviction to successfully execute required behaviors in specific situations [ ], operates through mastery experiences, vicarious experiences, verbal persuasion, and physiological states. Our IPC intervention incorporated cognitive training (providing mastery experiences) and group seminars (facilitating vicarious learning and verbal persuasion) to target these mechanisms among older adults with MCI.
The study simultaneously applied social support theory [ ], which posits that health outcomes are influenced by instrumental support (practical assistance), informational support (knowledge sharing) and emotional support (psychological comfort). We operationalized this through a tripartite structure: (1) family doctor teams delivering contracted services as support providers, (2) home-dwelling older adults with MCI as recipients, and (3) regular health management and monitoring as mediating mechanisms [ , ].
The intervention enhances individual self-efficacy through structured psychoeducation and skill-building components while simultaneously strengthening professional support systems via family doctor teams providing continuous care and community monitoring. These synergistic mechanisms, combining personal empowerment with systemic support, collectively target both the psychological and practical challenges faced by this vulnerable population, as illustrated in the conceptual model (Fig.  ). The self-efficacy components (including cognitive training and group seminars) develop participants’ confidence in managing cognitive challenges, while the social support elements (through family doctor team contracting service) ensure sustained environmental support, creating a comprehensive approach to community MCI care. 

## Methods

## Study design
This study adopts a hybrid effectiveness-implementation research design (type II) [ ], integrating a cluster randomized controlled trial and implementation research design, aiming to comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of IPC intervention for older adults with MCI and the influencing factors of implementation. The study is conducted in the communities (urban area) and villages (rural area) in Beijing for 16 weeks.

## Stage I: The design of randomized controlled trial

## Inclusion criteria
Participants must meet all of the following criteria: (1)Age ≥ 60 years; (2) Clinical diagnosis of MCI (without dementia), or confirmed Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score below education-adjusted cutoff: ≤ 13 for illiterate participants, ≤ 19 for elementary education, ≤ 24 for secondary education or higher[ ]; (3) Medically stable with capacity to participate in intervention activities; (4) Capable of using WeChat (minimum basic functionality for intervention delivery); (5) Absence of major psychiatric disorders, significant visual/hearing impairment, and other conditions limiting study participation.

## Sample size
Assuming α = 0.05, power = 0.80, Cohen's d = 0.6, the sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1) with an estimated requirement of 45 participants per group. This was subsequently adjusted for the cluster randomization design using the formula S = [1 + (n-1) × ICC] × N, where n = 25 (average cluster size), ICC = 0.03 (intraclass correlation coefficient, conservatively estimated based on analogous studies when direct references were unavailable), and N = 45, yielding an adjusted sample size of 78 participants per group. To accommodate potential 20% attrition due to missing data and loss to follow-up, we further increased the sample size to 98 participants per group, resulting in a total required sample size of 196 participants (98 in the intervention group and 98 in the control group).

## Recruitment

Participant screening and enrollment

Trained research staff and community health workers will jointly screen potential participants against the predefined inclusion criteria. For individuals without a documented MCI diagnosis, certified community health workers will administer the MoCA using standardized protocols to determine eligibility. All eligible participants will undergo a structured informed consent process, which includes detailed explanations of study procedures, risks/benefits, and voluntary participation rights

(b)Family doctor teams involvement

Enrolled participants will contract with a study-affiliated family doctor teams (typically consist of one general practitioner, one nurse, and one public health worker) which is common practice in China’s primary care system. These contracted family doctor teams, trained in the study protocol, will serve as the primary intervention implementers and care coordinators.

## Randomization and blinding
Randomization will be conducted at the cluster of family doctor teams. Interventions will be coded for both the intervention and control groups to ensure that neither family doctors nor participants are aware of the group assignments. Unique random numbers will be generated for each team using a random data sequence generator. An independent researcher, not involved in participant inclusion or grouping, will assign teams to intervention or control groups based on a predefined random sequence to ensure randomization and prevent selection bias. Group assignments will not be disclosed, and interventions will be implemented separately for the two groups according to the trial protocol. The double–blind principle will also be adhered to during data collection and analysis. Unblinding occurs post-database lock but pre-analysis to maintain objectivity.

## Intervention design: Integrated primary care intervention (IPC intervention)
The IPC intervention will be delivered through family doctor teams utilizing an integrated approach combining:

Structured psychoeducational group sessiosn

The four-week core educational component, implemented during the first month (Weeks 1–4), comprises weekly 90-min group seminars covering: (1) Fundamental konwledge of MCI and dementia, (2) Evidence-based prevention strategies, (3) Practical coping mechanisms for daily living, and (4) Orientation to the WeChat-based cognitive training programs.

(b)Cognitive training

The cognitive training component employs evidence-based, multidomain cognitive tasks to improve cognitive function [ ], constituting a well-tolerated non-pharmacological approach with Grade 1 A recommendation in the Chinese Guidelines for Cognitive Training (2022) for MCI management [ ]. This patient-centered intervention adheres to guideline-recommended parameters (≥ 30-min sessions, ≥ 3 sessions/week, totaling ≥ 20 training hours).
To ensure intervention feasibility and accessibility while maintaining clinical effectiveness, we plan to implement a hybrid delivery model through collaboration with Peking University Sixth Hospital’s online cognitive training platform. This approach was designed considering three key factors: participant engagement in cognitive training, workload optimization for family physician teams, and leveraging WeChat’s widespread adoption in China. The intervention features two complementary components: (1) Digital cognitive training platform: Hosted on WeChat for universal accessibility, provides structured, evidence-based cognitive exercises, enables convenient home-based participation; and (2) Family doctor team’s supervision: Serves as health gatekeepers through regular check-ins, performs three core functions (Adherence support such as training reminders and motivation, progress monitoring and technical assistance, and psychosocial support for cognitive health concerns). This dual-component model combines the scalability of digital health solutions with the personalized support of primary care providers, addressing both implementation challenges and participant needs.

## Usual care
In China, community health centers provide basic public health services for older adults, including health check-ups, health management, and chronic disease management (focused on hypertension and diabetes). These services form the standard family doctor contract services (Usual care). The control group receives usual care without proactive outreach. Family doctor teams respond to caregivers’ queries upon request but maintain a passive, naturalistic approach to intervention.

## Outcomes measures

Cognitive function assessment (primary outcomes measure)

Cognitive function was evaluated using the MoCA, a widely validated screening instrument developed by Nasreddine [ ]. This 30-point scale assesses eight cognitive domains: attention and concentration, executive functions, memory, language, visuospatial abilities, abstract reasoning, calculation, and orientation. The MoCA demonstrates superior sensitivity to MCI compared to the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), particularly in detecting early executive dysfunction and subtle memory deficits [ ]. The validated MoCA-Beijing version will be used in this study [ ].

(b)Self-efficacy

The Chinses version of General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES) [ , ] was employed to assess participants' self-efficacy. This 10-item instrument uses a 4-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree), with total scores categorized as low (10–20), moderate (21–30), or high (31–40) self-efficacy. Higher scores indicate greater confidence in one's ability to cope with challenges and more positive psychological adaptation.

(c)Health–related quality of life (HRQoL)

It was evaluated using the Chinese version of the EQ-5D-3L (EuroQol-5 Dimensions-3 Levels) instrument, a standardized measure developed by the EuroQol Group in 1987 [ , ]. This generic preference-based tool consists of two components: a descriptive system assessing five dimensions (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression), each rated at three severity levels (no problems, some problems, extreme problems), and a visual analog scale (EQ-VAS) ranging from 0 (“worst imaginable health state”) to 100 (“best imaginable health state”) for self-rated health evaluation. It will subsequently be employed to calculate quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) for health economic evaluation.

(d)Cost measurement

Cost evaluation will be conducted from primary care provider perspective and included two main components: (1) human resource costs, calculated based on the time inputs of family doctor teams and multidisciplinary teams (including direct service time, case preparation, and coordination), converted using local salary benchmarks; and (2) direct intervention costs, covering expert consultation fees, team meeting expenses (venue and travel), and material expenditures (standardized assessment tools and patient education materials). These cost measurements will be utilized in the health economic analysis to assess intervention cost-effectiveness.

## Data collection and analysis
Data will be collected through questionnaire surveys. A basic information questionnaire collects demographic characteristics (age, gender, education level, occupation) and chronic disease status. Validated scales are used to assess participants’ cognitive function, self-efficacy, and HRQoL. Data collection will be conducted at three time points: the baseline survey (T0), week-8 survey (T1 for primary outcome only), and the final survey (T3 for outcome measures).
The statistical analyses will adhere to the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle to handle missing data. Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics (means ± standard errors for continuous variables; proportions for categorical variables). For between-group comparisons, we will employ: (1) independent t-tests or ANOVA for normally distributed continuous variables; (2) Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis tests for non-normal distributions; and (3) χ2 or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables, as appropriate. Furthermore, this study plans to analyze the quantitative results with the Generalized Linear Mixed Model (GLMM), which utilizes the idea of mixed models and has a unique advantage in dealing with longitudinal data.

## Stage II: implementation barriers based on CFIR

## Theoretical framework
This study adopts the CFIR as its theoretical foundation. Originally developed by Damschroder et al. in 2009 through a systematic synthesis of over 500 implementation research articles across 13 disciplines, the framework integrates key theoretical elements from innovation diffusion, organizational change, and knowledge translation [ ]. In 2022, the research team conducted a comprehensive update based on emerging evidence and user feedback [ ]. The updated CFIR framework comprises five core domains: Innovation, Outer Setting, Inner Setting, Individuals, and Implementation Process, encompassing a total of 48 constructs organized into 19 sub-elements. Since its publication, CFIR has been widely applied and validated for assessing complex intervention implementation across nursing practice, public health programs, and mental health services. This study utilized this framework to develop the interview guide and guide thematic coding of qualitative data.

## CFIR data collection
Qualitative interviews were conducted with key stakeholders (including primary care providers, MCI patients, and health policymakers) at baseline and post-intervention using CFIR-informed interview guides tailored to each group. Theoretical sampling continued until reaching thematic saturation to ensure comprehensive perspective capture across implementation contexts. Interviewees will be encouraged to discuss factors influencing implementation, communication patterns among family doctors, time required to complete intervention tasks, and team consensus. All recordings will be transcribed verbatim and imported into NVivo 11.0 to ensure data integrity.

## CFIR data analysis
Transcripts will be analyzed using framework analysis in NVivo 11.0, applying a hybrid approach: deductive coding based on CFIR constructs complemented by inductive theme emergence. Special attention will focus on characterizing reported implementation barriers through iterative codebook to ensure analytic rigor.

## Discussion
This hybrid effectiveness-implementation study pioneers an integrated primary care model (IPC intervention) for China’s home-dwelling MCI elders by synergizing family doctor team services with digital health solutions. The intervention innovatively combines evidence-based cognitive training (delivered via WeChat mini-programs to alleviate provider burden), brain health seminars, and coordinated care management, while systematically evaluating both health outcomes and implementation barriers using the CFIR framework. This approach addresses critical gaps in dementia prevention for resource-limited settings by: (1) leveraging China’s established family doctor contract system; (2) adapting evidence-based interventions to local primary care capacities through digital delivery; and (3) generating actionable data on scalability constraints, ultimately informing national dementia control strategies and providing a replicable model for other developing countries facing similar demographic challenges.
The intersection between MCI (as dementia’s most intervenable stage) and primary care providers (as the most accessible health care resource) creates a crucial opportunity for developing effective community-based intervention models with significant real-world applicability [ ]. However, existing evidence predominantly originates from high-income countries with fundamentally different primary care infrastructures and service capacities compared to developing nations. This systemic disparity severely limits the applicability of current intervention models to resource-limited settings. Compounding this issue is the paucity of research focusing on home-dwelling older adults with MCI in developing countries, a critical knowledge gap demanding urgent attention to develop context-appropriate solutions. Meanwhile, two major barriers hinder their effective implementation among elderly populations: limited public awareness of cognitive training benefits, and inadequate knowledge regarding proper training protocols [ , ]. Within this context, China's contracted family doctor service system (nationwide implementation since June 2016) positions primary care providers as optimally accessible and cost-effective channels for delivering MCI interventions, including cognitive training, to this vulnerable population.
This study innovatively engages family doctors as the cornerstone for delivering integrated cognitive interventions to home-dwelling older adults with MCI. They are uniquely positioned to: (1) educate patients about evidence-based cognitive training while supervising the WeChat-based program implementation, and (2) concurrently provide routine health management services that enhance both self-efficacy and social support. This dual approach builds upon China’s robust primary care infrastructure, including the essential public health services launched in 2009 that mandate elderly health examinations, thereby creating an ideal platform for scalable dementia prevention interventions. The family doctor-led model not only optimizes intervention effectiveness through continuous, personalized support but also provides a replicable framework for other LMICs facing similar challenges in aging populations and resource-limited settings.
This study anticipates two primary implementation challenges. First, to overcome barriers in technology adoption and sustained engagement among older participants, we will implement a tiered support system combining WeChat-based reminders (transitioning from daily to weekly), real-time usage monitoring with targeted outreach for low-adherence participants, and dedicated telephone support for those with limited usage of cognitive training. This multi-faceted approach leverages China’s existing community health infrastructure to optimize intervention fidelity and participant retention. Another challenge is motivating family doctors to participate in the intervention, as this responsibility is not currently part of their routine duties. To address this, the research team will provide expert resources and timely support to primary care institutions, which are often prioritized by family doctors. Additionally, incentive mechanisms will be established to encourage primary care providers' involvement, particularly during the initial stages of implementing family doctor contract services in China. These implementation strategies and lessons learned provide valuable insights for scaling similar interventions in resource-limited primary care settings.
To our knowledge, this study represents the first hybrid effectiveness-implementation research examining the integration of digital cognitive training with comprehensive health management for home-dwelling older adults with MCI, conducted within the framework of China’s family doctor contract service system. The findings will provide critical evidence on both the effectiveness of this IPC intervention and its real-world implementation feasibility in primary care settings. However, two important limitations warrant consideration. First, regional variations in health literacy levels and digital infrastructure may affect the generalizability of results to other LMIC contexts. Second, the intervention’s success may be influenced by health system characteristics unique to China’s primary care reform context, particularly the maturity of family doctor contract services in different regions. These limitations highlight the need for future adaptation studies when implementing this model in diverse health care systems.
