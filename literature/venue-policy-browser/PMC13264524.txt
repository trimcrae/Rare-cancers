## Abstract

## Introduction
The Stages of Change model describes how individuals move through different levels of new behaviour adoption, supported by internal cognitive processes and external infrastructure. In Nigeria, mass gatherings were leveraged to raise awareness about antimicrobial resistance (AMR). We aimed to review the use of mass gatherings for AMR awareness in Nigeria from 2017 to 2022, describe the 2023 Joint External Evaluation (JEE) AMR findings, and propose a SoC model for sustainable behaviour change.

## Methods
A desk review of AMR awareness initiatives from 2017 to 2022 was conducted. Additionally, secondary analyses of a 2018 U-report survey and a 2021 qualitative study on antibiotic use and AMR were conducted. Further, the 2023 JEE-AMR findings for the country were reviewed. Frequencies and proportions were generated, and co-occurrence network analysis and thematic coding were performed for qualitative data.

## Results
Overall, 125 AMR awareness activities were documented, mostly in 2019 (44%). Fifteen methods were used to engage stakeholders, including mass gatherings and social media. Behaviour change efforts occurred mainly in healthcare settings. Nigeria leveraged mass gatherings to launch a national Antibiotic Guardian Pledge Campaign virtually and to establish a hybrid multisectoral Community of Practice. A 2018 national poll showed that 49% of Nigerians had heard of AMR, while stating that taking antibiotics prevented AMR. A study in the Federal Capital Territory found that rural residents were unfamiliar with the term "AMR," while urban residents linked drug resistance to improper medicine use. Many respondents obtained antibiotics from pharmacies without a prescription. In selecting antibiotics, rural residents prioritised affordability, while urban residents focused on drug authenticity. The JEE-AMR score was 2.4/5 in 2023, with a multisectoral coordination mechanism for AMR response in place. However, the response was still mainly reliant on donor funding, and the enforcement of antibiotics being prescription-only was weak despite sustained awareness.

## Conclusion
Widespread practice of self-medication and purchasing antibiotics without prescriptions remains a significant challenge in Nigeria, driven by regulatory weaknesses and high healthcare costs. The SoC model emphasises the need for structural enablers to drive consistent behaviour change to combat AMR in Nigeria namely improving appropriate antibiotic use by strengthening the national antimicrobial stewardship programme and expanding access to health insurance .

## Introduction
Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) poses a significant and ongoing threat to both human and animal populations and health systems , . Low public knowledge and awareness are major contributors to antimicrobial resistance. Public awareness of AMR remains inadequate even in high-income countries , and this is more pronounced in low- and middle-income countries, where antibiotics are frequently used without a prescription , . In these contexts in Nigeria, additional factors such as the circulation of substandard antibiotics , limited access to healthcare , expectations from patients or caregivers to receive antibiotics , and widespread self‑medication , , , further compound the challenge, making AMR increasingly difficult to control.
Nigeria launched its first 5-year National Action Plan (NAP 1.0) on AMR in 2017 , built around five pillars aligned with the WHO Global Action Plan. Following its expiry in 2023, a second plan (NAP 2.0) was introduced in 2024 under the same multisectoral One Health framework, implemented by a Technical Working Group and coordinated through the AMR Coordination Centre to the ministerial One Health Technical Committee. NAP 2.0 adds a pillar on strengthening leadership, collaboration, coordination, and AMR governance at national and subnational levels. Since implementation began, Nigeria has also participated annually in World Antimicrobial Resistance Awareness Week, using activities held each 18–24 November to raise AMR awareness and promote best practices among the public, health workers, and policymakers.
Awareness of AMR and appropriate antimicrobial use , remains essential for an effective response, as emphasised in both NAPs. To achieve meaningful reductions in inappropriate antibiotic use, awareness initiatives and interventions must support people through the full process of behaviour change. The Transtheoretical Model (TTM) is a behaviour change theory that explains how individuals progress through different levels of readiness as they modify a behaviour. Its main components are the Stages of Change; the Processes of Change (cognitive and behavioural strategies used to progress through these stages); Decisional Balance (weighing the pros and cons of changing); and Self-Efficacy (confidence in maintaining the new behaviour, especially in difficult situations) , . According to the Stages of Change construct, individuals typically move through a series of stages : precontemplation, when they are not yet considering change; contemplation, when they begin to recognise the issue and think about taking action; preparation, when they start planning concrete steps; action, when they modify their behaviour; maintenance, when they sustain the new behaviour over time; and sometimes may relapse, when they return to previous habits.
Since AMR awareness activities began, mass gatherings have been used to advance messages, as these platforms often host large, diverse populations and create opportunities for rapid dissemination of health information and for shaping social norms. AMR is a complex issue, as it requires a shared definition and understanding of what “resistance means” and the role of antibiotics, how the use of antibiotics impacts AMR, and which new behaviours should be adopted to mitigate emergence. Guided by the Stages of Change construct of the Transtheoretical Model, we synthesised data on mass gatherings used to raise awareness of AMR and antibiotic misuse in Nigeria from 2017 to 2022 and examined the 2023 Joint External Evaluation findings to assess the impact of these initiatives. We then proposed a theory-driven framework for sustainable behaviour change.

## Methods
To understand the landscape of AMR awareness activities, we conducted a desk review of initiatives implemented in Nigeria between 2017 and 2022. A standardised Excel-based data extraction tool was developed to capture key variables, including the activity title, focus (awareness or behaviour change), activity description, date, year, and any additional relevant notes. The stakeholder group was categorised into predefined groups, including healthcare professionals, policymakers, community members, media personnel, and others. The method of implementation (e.g., symposium, campaign, press conference, digital and traditional media) and the venue/site (e.g., hospital, community, policy platform, media outlet) were also recorded to allow for thematic analysis.
We conducted a secondary analysis of a national U-report survey on AMR conducted during the 2018 awareness week. U-Report is a mobile-based social monitoring platform developed by the United Nations International Children’s Emergency Fund (UNICEF) to engage communities, particularly young people, in providing real-time feedback on key social and health issues. Data are collected via Short Message Service (SMS), WhatsApp, Facebook Messenger, Viber, or the U-Report website. We reviewed the responses, which were automatically aggregated on a central server by UNICEF (https://nigeria.ureport.in/opinion/3063) .
We also performed a secondary analysis of a qualitative study on AMR and antibiotics conducted in Abuja, Federal Capital Territory, in 2021. Twelve focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted across three different communities. The participants in each FGD were stratified by age, and twenty-four (24) people participated from each community. The composition was 8 persons aged 18–24 years (4 females, 4 males); 8 aged 25–44 years (4 females, 4 males); and 8 aged 45 years and above (4 females, 4 males). The FGD guide used open-ended questions to explore participants’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to antimicrobial use and resistance, including perceived barriers to behaviour change. Cue cards with images of commonly used antibiotics, other local medicines, and AMR-related concepts were used to support discussion through familiar examples. This helped elicit detailed accounts of AMR understanding, antibiotic use practices such as self-medication, procurement pathways, perceptions of medicine quality, and decision-making around antibiotic selection. All sessions were audio-recorded with consent and transcribed verbatim.
We reviewed the findings on the national AMR response from the 2017 and 2023 JEE, which is a voluntary, collaborative, multisectoral process for assessing a country’s capacity to prevent, detect, and respond to public health threats. Within the JEE Prevent domain, AMR is assessed across five attributes: coordination, surveillance, prevention, and antimicrobial stewardship in human and animal health. Response capacity is measured using five indicators scored from 1 (no capacity) to 5 (sustainable capacity), enabling longitudinal assessment .
Data management: All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.2). Frequency tables and summary statistics were used to describe trends in activity, geographic distribution, methods, and stakeholder engagement. Qualitative data were analysed using NLP-assisted methods, including text pre-processing, tokenisation, and word co-occurrence analysis to generate network maps. Key phrases and sentences on community antimicrobial use and resistance were also coded inductively, grouped into categories, and used to build a hierarchical concept map showing major factors and subthemes. These visualisations supported exploration and interpretation rather than inference. Ethical approval for the FGD was obtained from the Federal Capital Territory Health and Human Services Ethical Review Committee, and participants provided voluntary consent, could withdraw at any time, and were assured confidentiality.

## Results
Overall, 125 AMR awareness activities were captured, and most (55; 44.0%) occurred in 2019 ( ). The main type of activity was awareness-raising activities. A total of 15 methods were used to engage multiple stakeholder groups ( ). Community-focused interventions were conducted throughout the study period. These included public awareness campaigns, art exhibitions, and social media engagements targeting the general public and students. Behaviour change-focused activities were deployed mainly in hospitals during ward rounds.
 Healthcare professionals and university or hospital communities were engaged primarily through trainings, symposia, and ward rounds, particularly from 2018 onward (see ). Media personnel and journalists participated in dialogues, press conferences, and radio programs across the study period. Policymakers were engaged through symposia and dialogues, mainly between 2019 and 2021. Engagements involving secondary and tertiary students, veterinary actors, and food product traders increased after 2019. From 2020 onward, additional student-targeted activities, such as spelling bees, quizzes, and debates, were implemented.
 

## Case studies demonstrating the use of mass gatherings for AMR awareness activities in Nigeria
Community and school-based events: Annual AMR and antibiotic awareness walks were held across Nigeria over the years. These walks took place at selected markets, parks, and peri-urban areas and were used to engage the public directly about the risks of inappropriate antibiotic use. These walks combined community outreach with education, including hand hygiene demonstrations and distribution of informational materials. Schools also served as key platforms for engaging young populations through competitions such as spelling bees, debates, and AMR-themed games. Student clubs were formed to sustain peer-to-peer education, while media training and press conferences amplified AMR messaging to broader audiences, reinforcing positive behavioural norms across communities.
Policy and technical engagement through mass gatherings: In 2019, Nigeria launched the national Antibiotic Guardian Pledge Campaign as part of the Africa Antibiotic Guardian initiative, in collaboration with the UK Health Security Agency and the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention and endorsed by the NCDC. The campaign encouraged individuals, including policymakers, healthcare workers, and the public, to commit to responsible antibiotic use by taking a personal pledge on the campaign website.
The official launch took place at a high-level national event, with the Chair of the Senate Committee on Health and the Chair of the Committee on Agriculture and Productivity serving as champions, demonstrating how mass gatherings can mobilise policy attention and build advocacy momentum. Between 2019 and 2021, these activities expanded to 12 states, engaging commissioners through interactive education and pledge activities. This not only increased awareness but also catalysed state-led initiatives, including the implementation of antimicrobial stewardship programmes in Osun State and the establishment of a State-level AMR Technical Working Group (TWG) in Borno State, effectively transforming awareness into tangible policy and governance structures.
Hybrid AMR communities of practice (AMR CoP): In December 2020, Nigeria launched the National AMR/AMU Community of Practice (CoP), a hybrid (in-person and online) community that brings together policymakers, technical experts, and sectoral stakeholders across human, animal, and environmental health (https://amrcop.ncdc.gov.ng/). The AMR CoP provides a platform for knowledge exchange and collaboration, allowing institutions to learn across sectors and to operationalise highlighted strategies. By integrating both in-person and virtual platforms, these initiatives demonstrate how mass gatherings can extend beyond public education to foster multisectoral coordination, policy engagement, and sustained systems-level action.

## National survey on antibiotic misuse and overuse in Nigeria
In 2018, U-Report Nigeria conducted a nationwide poll to assess awareness and perceptions of antibiotic resistance. Of 2755,820 individuals polled, 154,601 responded, yielding a response rate of 5.6%. The majority were young adults, with the largest age group being 25–30 years, representing 64,932 (42%) of respondents. Other age groups included 20–24 years (40,197; 26%), 31–34 years (14,687; 9.5%), 35 years and above (22,417; 14.5%), and 15–19 years (12,368; 8%). Overall, 130,740 of those polled responded to the question on sex: 78,178 (60%) were male. Geographically, Jigawa State in Northern Nigeria had the highest number of responders, with 7583 individuals participating out of 128,780 polled.
Regarding awareness of antibiotic resistance, 75,755 (49%) of respondents reported knowing what it was. The highest level of knowledge was among those aged 20–30 years, and the lowest was among those aged 15–19 years. Of 133,502 respondents who answered the question on AMR awareness, 69,421 (52%) thought it was possible to have an untreatable infection. This concern was noted to increase with age, with a male-to-female ratio of about 1:1. The top actions for preventing AMR identified by 65,555 respondents were taking antibiotics, avoiding self-medication, and following prescriptions.

## Qualitative study on AMR and antibiotics in the Federal Capital Territory
In the Federal Capital Territory, AMR was understood as a situation in which drugs become ineffective due to improper use, including self-medication, treatment failure, and limited laboratory testing, in urban communities, especially among those aged 25–45 years. With further probing, participants more readily recognised treatment failure in tuberculosis and HIV. In one rural community, AMR was described as the body “rejecting” medicines when drugs are not taken consistently or as prescribed.
“If you don’t take it according to the dosage, then it will not work" (FGD 45 years and above, Karomanjiji, Rural)
In an urban area, AMR was understood to mean that the “person” was resistant to the antibiotic, rather than the microorganism or bacterium.
“So, drug resistance is when someone abuses a particular drug, and the drug becomes resistant to him. Even though you take the drugs, it will no longer become effective for you" (FGD 18–24 years, Garki, Urban)
In urban areas, younger participants identified antibiotics as drugs that fight infections in both humans and animals, and the most used antibiotics were ampicillin, amoxicillin, metronidazole and tetracycline ( ). Participants commonly named specific products when asked about antibiotics, such as “Amoxil’ and “Septrin”, for treating cough or “Flagyl” for treating diarrhoea. Participants in rural communities recognised tetracycline by its local Hausa name, “ja da yellow” (meaning red and yellow in the Hausa language), indicating some community-specific terminology for several antibiotics.
 Most participants obtained medicines directly from chemists or pharmacies, often without prescriptions, because they were seen as more accessible than doctors ( ). Many visited pharmacies with old prescription cards or empty medicine packs to obtain the antibiotic previously used. Others relied on advice or medication from family members. Most did not complete the full course once they felt better, only returning to the pharmacy or the hospital when the symptoms returned.
“I visit the hospital (when I am ill). When I am better, I think that it is time for me to stop taking the antibiotics since I feel ok" (FGD 45 years and above, Dutse, Rural)
 Those who visited hospitals reported following the health worker’s instructions to complete the full antibiotic course, particularly for “deep” infections or tuberculosis. In rural communities, respondents believed that injections were more effective than tablets and should be used when oral medications failed. In urban communities, people described keeping leftover antibiotics for future use or sharing them with family members with similar symptoms.
“In case you fall sick again, you can make use of your leftover antibiotic drug” (FGD 25–44 years, Garki, Urban)
In both rural and urban areas, young adults strongly highlighted handwashing, environmental cleanliness, and vaccination as ways to prevent infections. However, participants in a rural community felt that antibiotics aided wound healing and could prevent infections.
“I have seen an Ampiclox capsule before, and it is a pain reliever. For example, if you have an injury, most especially if you are a farmer, you're hurt by a hoe or cutlass; when you take the Ampiclox, it hastens the healing of the wound" (FGD 18–24 years, Karomanjiji, Rural)
Medicine authenticity and safety influenced antibiotic choice, especially in urban areas ( ). Patients checked expiry dates, manufacturing dates, searched for NAFDAC registration numbers on medicine packs, and often avoided unfamiliar medicines. Availability was a major factor in antibiotic selection in rural areas. Hospital-prescribed drugs were frequently unavailable on-site and had to be purchased from private pharmacies. Local shortages in community pharmacies and some health facilities often forced patients to travel to towns or larger clinics to obtain antibiotics.
We have a lot of challenges. There was a certain time when I was sick and I needed these antibiotics. I went to three chemists/pharmacists, and they said all these drugs are not available. I needed to transport myself to the general hospital to get the drugs. In this (our) primary health care, not all drugs are always available, and we will find this very difficult" (FGD 18–24 years, Karomanjiji, Rural)
 Across all locations, cost was the primary consideration when selecting an antibiotic. Most participants purchased their medicines from private pharmacies, where the original antibiotic was often substituted with a more affordable alternative when it was too expensive, either by the client or the pharmacist. This concern was greater among older participants.
“When they say branded, one may be 10,000NGN or 6,000NGN, and the other one will be 4,000NGN … when I don't have 6,000NGN, I'll go for one of 4,000NGN” (FGD 25–44 years, Dutse, Rural)

## Joint external evaluations AMR scores in 2023
Nigeria’s JEE-AMR capacity was assessed across five indicators: multisectoral coordination, AMR surveillance, prevention of multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs), and optimal antimicrobial use in human health and in animal health/agriculture. In 2023, Nigeria’s composite AMR score was 2.4/5 , up from 2.0 in 2017 .
Nigeria showed its strongest performance in multisectoral coordination (score 4), supported by a functional national AMR action plan and coordination mechanism, although funding remained largely donor-driven. Human health AMR surveillance (score 2) was partially established through mandated reporting at sentinel sites, but surveillance had not yet begun in the animal, agricultural, and environmental sectors. Nigeria had no capacity to prevent MDROs (score 1), with an ad hoc plan for detection and response.
Regarding antimicrobial use, Nigeria had developed capacity in human health (score 3) through treatment guidelines and hospital antimicrobial stewardship programmes. In animal health and agriculture (score 2), legislation existed to regulate antimicrobial products, but enforcement of prescription-only use requirements and growth-promoter bans remained weak.

## Discussion
We describe the temporal patterns in AMR awareness activities across Nigeria, with an early focus on community-level engagement through awareness campaigns and a gradual broadening to include key stakeholders leveraging routine activities. This illustrates how the country began to focus more on behaviour change activities, particularly after the COVID-19 pandemic. The country’s early emphasis on leveraging mass gatherings to reach large numbers of people simultaneously enabled broader dissemination of information on AMR and antibiotics. Structural and economic constraints also shaped antibiotic selection in Nigerian communities.
We found that Nigeria’s 2019 launch of the Antibiotic Guardian Pledge Campaign represented a major advance in the country’s AMR efforts, providing a clear platform for individuals to pledge to responsible antibiotic use. Pledge-based, community-driven strategies like this align with evidence from Tanzania, where community knowledge-attitudes-practices studies show that improving AMR education at the grassroots level can significantly shift perceptions of antibiotic use and resistance . Over the years, we observed increasing engagement with healthcare professionals, influenced by increased interest in antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) and capacity development within health facilities. However, AMS continues to be a concept that many health workers in Nigeria do not fully understand , which poses a problem, considering how antibiotic misuse contributes to the development of AMR. Evidence from South Africa shows that training and implementing AMS approaches in hospitals and primary care can actually improve prescribing practices. Health workers already possess a foundational knowledge of antimicrobial use that can be further strengthened through targeted technical training. Enhancing their capacity enables them to serve as trusted sources of information, positively influencing patient behaviour and promoting responsible antibiotic use across the communities.
We noted increasing engagement with policymakers, the media, students, veterinary stakeholders, and food value chain actors, indicating a gradual shift toward a multidisciplinary, One Health approach. A regional review of AMS implementation across African countries highlights the role of political will, multisectoral collaboration, and national commitment as major enablers of progress . The involvement of policymakers facilitates priority setting, resource allocation, and accountability mechanisms crucial for sustainable AMR control . Consistent involvement of media actors illustrates their function in translating technical AMR information for wider audiences. The introduction of virtual and interactive methods during mass gathering events during the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic reflected an adaptation to using multimodal communication platforms and an effort to promote behaviour change messages beyond traditional media.
We found that 1 in 2 Nigerians was aware of the term “antibiotic resistance”, particularly amongst younger age groups. This is higher than another survey in Nigeria, which found that fewer than 5% understood what AMR meant and that 66% of respondents had taken antibiotics without a prescription , highlighting how limited awareness may be linked to antibiotic misuse. Other studies in Southern Nigeria also reported that more than half of respondents were aware of antibiotic resistance , , similar to our study, although the place of residence influenced the level of awareness . We reported that younger individuals may be more familiar with the term “AMR” which could be due to greater engagement with digital health information from the increasing number of health influencers , particularly in urban areas. However, misinformation persists despite rising digital literacy. In our study, AMR was often misdefined: resistance was attributed to the human body rejecting drugs rather than to microbial evolution, especially in rural areas, where knowledge was primarily shaped by personal experience with antibiotics rather than by formal health education or scientific training.
The most commonly recognised or used antibiotics in our study were also reported in several studies in Nigeria and across Africa: ampicillin/cloxacillin, amoxicillin , , , , , and metronidazole . Antibiotics were deemed appropriate based on personal judgment , the symptoms experienced, or information gained from previous use of particular brands. Drug efficacy is linked to brand names rather than a clear understanding of antibiotic classes, Access-Watch-Reserve categories, or the link between use and laboratory diagnosis. The implications are significant: without targeted, culturally adapted education, especially in rural and older demographics, or otherwise, misconceptions will persist, undermining national AMR strategies and further fueling inappropriate antibiotic use. The need for community-specific terminology underscores the importance of localised messaging.
Most participants in our study bought drugs or received advice from patent medicine vendors or pharmacies, sometimes without prescriptions. Self-medication prevalence has been reported at 91.5% in Nigeria , markedly higher than the 67.5% estimated across sub-Saharan Africa . Community pharmacies also dispense medications without prescriptions; a 2025 national prevalence of 98.1% has been reported . Similar to our study, cough , , , and diarrhoea are common illnesses for which antibiotics were requested in Nigeria. This occurs within a vicious cycle driven by customer demand , limited access to hospital care, high medical consultation fees , commercial pressures and regulatory weaknesses , .
Similar to our study, many Nigerians retain leftover antibiotics for future use, with up to 71% reported by Otu et al . even among educated individuals , . This may be facilitated by easier access to medicines in urban areas, which, when combined with regulatory inefficiency , leads to antibiotic misuse . Conversely, in contexts where healthcare access is constrained, such as rural regions, individuals may prioritise their “perceived” rapid relief from injections over oral therapies. In a global survey, 50% of Africans preferred the oral route for medications compared to 98% of Europeans . Notably, the proportion of injectable antibiotics prescribed among military populations in Nigeria has been reported to exceed World Health Organization recommendations , highlighting a broader, systemic pattern of overuse within the society.
We found that affordability and cost emerged as critical determinants, especially among older participants. Limited geographic access to healthcare facilities , particularly in rural areas, stockouts in public hospitals, and the high cost of hospital care , are persistent challenges. This drives clients to cheaper, often substandard antibiotic alternatives from unqualified persons or premises. We also noted that providing instructions on how to use antibiotics does not necessarily ensure adherence, as patients may discontinue antibiotics once they feel better . In addition to strengthening antimicrobial stewardship efforts, interventions must address supply-chain constraints and account for patient-centred realities, i.e., practical and social factors that influence how individuals use antibiotics, such as financial constraints and prior experiences with illness and treatment. Nigeria’s composite JEE AMR response score improved by 20%, compared with the 150% increase required to reach the maximum score of 5 points over a 5-year period.
Raising awareness alone remains insufficient to drive appropriate antibiotic-use behaviours; structural interventions are also needed to reduce barriers and sustain behaviour change. Moreover, behaviour change is neither linear nor irreversible. The Stages of Change model, a construct within the Transtheoretical Model (TTM), provides a useful framework for understanding behaviour change within Nigeria’s AMR response because it accommodates heterogeneity in knowledge and structural capacity across contexts. We applied it as a dynamic, cyclical framework rather than a prescriptive linear pathway, emphasising structural enablers aligned with stages of behavioural readiness to prevent “systems relapse”. Although the model is traditionally applied at the individual level, our framework recognises that both individual- and group-level behaviour changes drive AMR action. Sustaining behaviours by individual actors over time (Stages 1–3) requires system-level forces such as governance structures, policy continuity, and institutional support (Stages 4–5).
We therefore propose this framework ( ):
•Stage 1: Precontemplation is where knowledge remains abstract. Prevention of MDROs remains poor, and public understanding of AMR is still limited or inaccurate. Key stakeholders include facilities reporting AMR and multidrug resistance, as well as community members. The main infrastructure need is knowledge governance: co-developing terms that communicate AMR clearly across populations and disseminating them through digital influencers to counter misinformation. Messaging should shift from “AMR is a global threat” to “What happens when antibiotics stop working for you in?”
•Stage 2: Contemplation, in which awareness exists but ambivalence remains. Nigeria recognises AMR as a national and One Health issue, but system-wide commitment is uneven, especially outside the human health sector and public hospitals. Key stakeholders include One Health policymakers, educated urban populations, and private-sector health workers. Our framework recommends framing AMR as an economic issue that affects all sectors and informing all actors that their representation in the national response is critical.
•Stage 3: Preparation refers to an intent to change that exists, but constraints dominate. Enforcement gaps, weak supply-chain resilience, and limited diagnostic access were identified during awareness activities and the JEE. Recommended actions should focus on disseminating information about initiatives strengthening antibiotic supply chains, the national essential medicines list, regulations guiding appropriate antibiotic use in humans and animals and health insurance schemes that improve access to healthcare services
•Stage 4: Action is when behaviour change is attempted. A functional multisectoral AMR coordination mechanism exists, but it is concentrated in national institutions and lacks adequate feedback loops to states, health workers, and communities. Communication should report national progress on indicators, including the Muscat Manifesto on AMR , subnational AMR progress, and visible recognition of compliance. The required infrastructure must be in place as a reinforcement mechanism.
•Stage 5: Maintenance involves sustaining appropriate behaviour and preventing relapse. Priorities include regular communication on AMS/AMR response indicators alongside routine health system performance metrics, population health insurance coverage rates, and the availability of quality-assured antibiotics at scale. These efforts require continued implementation of established policies, provision of domestic funding, and tracking of AMR response through an accountability framework.
 Our study identified several limitations in reviewing national awareness campaigns implemented over five years. Variations in sampling, survey administration, funding, messaging, delivery methods, and stakeholder engagement reduced comparability across time. As a result, the observed changes cannot be confidently attributed solely to the campaigns, since other public health programs and contextual events may also have influenced the outcomes. In addition, although mass gatherings play a critical role in AMR awareness campaigns, the current design does not explicitly incorporate behaviour change theory. This important tool is now being considered for inclusion in future campaigns in the country.
In conclusion, shifting community norms around perceptions of AMR and antibiotic use, while leveraging mass gatherings in Nigeria, requires efforts beyond individual behaviour and knowledge alone. This should be reinforced by societal capacity and systems that support positive behaviour change. We recommend that future research should focus on piloting TTM-informed behaviour change approaches during implementation of awareness campaigns, with clear links to AMR surveillance systems, antimicrobial stewardship interventions and health financing solutions. Such research should also evaluate continued alignment with national and global AMR and AMS targets.

## Consent to participate
Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Federal Capital Territory Health Research Ethics Committee (FCTHREC) board in Nigeria. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study. Each participant received detailed information about the purpose, procedures, potential risks, anticipated benefits, and their rights as participants. They were informed that participation was entirely voluntary and that they could withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences. Confidentiality and anonymity of all collected data were ensured, and all information was handled in accordance with applicable ethical and data protection guidelines. Only participants who provided written informed consent were enrolled in the study.
