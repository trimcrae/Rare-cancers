## Abstract

## Introduction
Clinical training is crucial for diagnostic radiography students, bridging theoretical knowledge with practical skills. In resource‐constrained settings, this training may face unique challenges that might significantly impact learning outcomes and future practice. Despite its importance, the experiences of diagnostic radiography students during clinical placements remain understudied, particularly in Sub‐Saharan Africa. This study aimed to explore and describe the experiences of diagnostic radiography students during clinical training at two resource‐constrained Sub‐Saharan public hospitals.

## Methods
A qualitative phenomenological study was conducted using face‐to‐face, semi‐structured interviews with 18 diagnostic radiography students (six each from years 2 to 4) from one participating university. Interviews were audio‐recorded, transcribed and analysed using thematic analysis. Trustworthiness was ensured through prolonged engagement, member checking, and researchers' reflexivity. Ethical approval was obtained before data collection commenced.

## Results
Three main themes were generated: (1) Clinical Environment Challenges, including equipment shortages and understaffing; (2) Independent Learning and Development, highlighting students' resilience and self‐directed learning; and (3) Patient Care Impact, demonstrating students' commitment to quality care despite constraints. Students reported increased confidence and adaptability but also noted negative interactions with healthcare workers and supervision inconsistencies.

## Conclusions
Despite significant challenges, diagnostic radiography students demonstrated remarkable resilience and commitment to patient care. However, resource limitations and interprofessional issues pose substantial barriers to optimal learning experiences. These findings highlighted the need for targeted interventions in diagnostic radiography education within resource‐constrained settings, including increased investment in equipment and staffing, enhanced interprofessional education, improved supervision models and curriculum enhancements fostering self‐directed learning and resilience.

Keywords: Clinical training, qualitative research, radiography education, resource‐constrained settings, student experiences

The study explored diagnostic radiography students' clinical training experiences in resource‐constrained settings. Findings reveal challenges like equipment shortages and understaffing, but highlight students' resilience and commitment to patient care. Results underscore the need for targeted interventions, including increased resources, improved supervision and curriculum enhancements to optimise learning outcomes in challenging environments.

## Introduction
Diagnostic radiography, a critical component of modern healthcare, relies on the skilful use of X‐rays to obtain images of internal body structures for diagnostic purposes. The preparation of competent radiographers hinges on effective clinical training, a process designed to bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge and practical application in real‐world healthcare settings. , , This training is not just an academic exercise but a crucial requirement for the award of any diagnostic radiography qualification and licensing by the regulatory bodies. , , 

Clinical training in diagnostic radiography usually serves as the platform whereby students transform theoretical understanding into practical competence, developing the skills, attitudes and knowledge essential for their future roles. , Moreover, clinical placements offer students invaluable opportunities to apply their learning in authentic healthcare environments, fostering the development of critical decision‐making skills and professional behaviours. 

However, the path from student to competent radiographer is full of different challenges as reported from previous literature in Ghana, Ethiopia and Zimbabwe. The studies highlighted that diagnostic radiography students face a theory‐practice gap, insufficient exposure to specialised procedures, inadequate resources, poor supervision, financial issues, unprofessional behaviours, COVID‐19 impacts, and limited advanced training, highlighting the need for curriculum reform and better support systems. Anecdotal evidence and preliminary observations from our context suggest that diagnostic radiography students may be facing similar hurdles during their clinical training.
The disparity between theoretical instruction and practical application—often referred to as the theory‐practice gap—further complicates the clinical learning experience. , , This misalignment can lead to inadequate skill development and diminished confidence among students, ultimately affecting their readiness for professional practice. , 

In the context of this study, the diagnostic radiography students at one higher education institution potentially face comparable experiences. These experiences may significantly impact their ability to demonstrate high levels of clinical competence. This study was set in a low‐income country where both the healthcare and higher education sectors face chronic underfunding, evidenced by limited imaging equipment and a shortage of radiographers and radiologists. , Understanding these experiences is crucial for enhancing the quality of diagnostic radiography education and, by extension, the quality of healthcare provision. Despite the recognised importance of clinical training, there remains a paucity of research specifically exploring the experiences of diagnostic radiography students in resource‐constrained settings such as ours. This study aimed to address this knowledge gap by exploring and describing the experiences of diagnostic radiography students during their clinical training at two resource‐constrained Sub‐Saharan public hospitals. This study's findings have the potential to catalyse positive changes in radiography education, not only in the local context but also in similar contexts globally.

## Methods

## Study context
This study was based in a low‐income country where both the healthcare and higher education sectors face chronic underfunding evidenced by limited imaging equipment and a shortage of radiographers and radiologists. , Diagnostic radiography training is provided by a single higher education institution as a 4‐year degree and includes clinical placements distributed across five sites: two public hospitals and three private imaging centres. Students enrolled in this diagnostic radiography degree complete a minimum of 2500 hours of clinical placements over 4 years. Clinical training begins in the first year and follows a 2‐week block‐release system that alternates between academic coursework and hands‐on clinical practice. Public hospitals serve as the primary training sites and assessment locations with 70% (1750 h) of the clinical training conducted at these sites. Clinical rotations in private imaging centres only begin in the second year of study providing additional placement opportunities. These private centres contribute 750 hours of clinical training over 3 years, reflecting their limited placement capacity. The public hospitals were the focus of this study, as the students spend most of their time in these facilities thereby shaping their clinical competencies.

## Study design
A qualitative phenomenological approach with a descriptive design was used to conduct the study. The qualitative approach was chosen to ensure the collection of accurate unlimited individual student accounts of their experience during clinical training, in public hospitals. 

## Population and sampling
The study population comprised 44 students registered for the Bachelor of Radiography (Diagnostic) Honours degree in their second to the fourth year of study at the participating university as they were assumed to have had significant exposure to clinical practice to build meaningful experiences. The first‐year students were excluded as they had not had significant clinical exposure when data was collected.
The study sample was selected purposely aiming for maximum variation per group. The total sample size was 18 students, which was adequate to produce data saturation, a point where no new information emerged but a repetition of reported experiences. Invitations to participate in the study were sent to each year group through their class representatives and recruitment was done when the students were attending their theory classes on campus, in between lectures, to avoid disruption of learning. The participants were fully informed about the study objectives, procedures, risks and benefits of participation during the information‐sharing session using an information sheet. All students who agreed to participate signed an informed consent form before data collection commenced.

## Data collection
Data were collected between August and September 2023 using face‐to‐face, semi‐structured interviews conducted in English by a trained final‐year radiography student researcher to minimise lecturer–student power dynamics. This also aided in enhancing familiarity as the student was part of the study population. The interviewer training, use of a standardised semi‐structured interview guide and peer review and supervision were strategies implemented to reduce interviewer bias. The interview guide consisted of four questions with the main question asking, ‘Can you tell me about your experience regarding clinical training in imaging departments at public hospitals’. The other three questions were:

Can you describe any challenges you faced in the clinical environment during your training?
How did you approach your learning and development during your clinical training?
How did your clinical training impact your ability to provide patient care?
Several questions were also asked to probe for clarity and depth, in response to the participant's answers. The interview guide was piloted using two students to check for its clarity and validity. No amendments were recommended or made based on the pilot study. The interviews were conducted at a time convenient to the participants in a secure, quiet venue on campus that provided privacy for the participants. Interviews ranged between 15 and 35 min and were audio‐recorded using digital voice recorders, with participants' permission. Interview recordings were transcribed into text using Microsoft 365 upon interview completion and verified and corrected by one researcher together with the interviewer before analysis. Non‐verbal cues were observed during the interviews and recorded in an interview diary, but none were deemed significant to impact or alter the verbal information provided by participants during data analysis.

## Data analysis
Data analysis followed the thematic analysis process outlined by Braun and Clarke. The process was conducted by two researchers with expertise in qualitative analysis. Initially, the researchers independently familiarised themselves with the data by repeatedly reading the transcripts to ensure immersion. Each researcher then performed inductive coding, identifying and labelling significant text passages using descriptive terms that captured the essence of the data. These codes were reviewed collaboratively to ensure consistency and agreement.
The next step involved grouping codes with similar meanings into preliminary categories. This was an iterative process, with both researchers working collaboratively to refine and consolidate categories. The categories were then reviewed to identify overarching patterns, which were synthesised into broader themes. The researchers named the themes using terms that reflected their core ideas while remaining inclusive of the underlying categories. The categories were then classified as sub‐themes. 

## Trustworthiness
To ensure the trustworthiness of the study, several strategies were employed :

Credibility: Prolonged engagement with participants, triangulation with existing literature, member checking through summarisation and confirmation during interviews, and bracketing of the researchers' personal experiences. For the lecturer‐researchers, bracketing involved setting aside preconceived judgements about students' abilities, awareness of authority and power dynamics, and prior knowledge of the clinical settings. The student researcher bracketed their shared experiences with participants, familiarity with peers, and any perceived pressure to represent participants or the institution favourably.
Dependability: Maintaining an audit trail of the research process and codifying data into themes and subthemes.
Confirmability: Employing an audit trail approach and practising reflexivity to minimise researchers' bias.
Transferability: Providing thick descriptions of the research methodology and using purposive sampling to gather comprehensive contextual data.

## Ethical considerations
Ethical approval was obtained from the School of Allied Health Sciences Decentralised Ethics Committee (SAHS27/23). The study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Belmont Report :

Respect for Persons: Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from each participant. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without consequences. They were also assured that if they chose to withdraw during the data collection phase, any data they had provided up to that point would be discarded. However, participants were advised that, after analysis, it might be difficult to isolate individual data due to the anonymisation process, which ensured that no identifying information was retained in the dataset.
Beneficence: The researchers discussed the benefits of the study with participants and ensured that risks were minimised, however, measures to manage any psychological distress were put in place.
Justice: All participants were given an equal chance to participate and were asked the same questions.
To maintain confidentiality, each participant was assigned a code, and a voice‐changing application was used to disguise participants' voices in the recordings. All data were stored securely in password‐protected files and accessed only by the researchers.

## Results
A total of 18 interviews were conducted with students in the second (6) third (6) and fourth (6) year of study. ## Theme 1: Clinical environment challenges
In the current study, the participants expressed various challenges they encountered in the clinical environment that hindered clinical learning during clinical placements. These challenges included a lack of resources and equipment, understaffing and workload, negative interactions with healthcare workers and challenges in providing optimal patient care.

## Subtheme 1.1: Lack of resources and equipment
Participants consistently reported challenges related to the lack of necessary equipment and resources in the public hospitals.
For example, we are really behind because there are no working mammography machines as well as fluoroscopic studies which are not being done here. Also, we do not have MRI exposure. (Participant 3)

The modalities that are not available, for example, mammography, MRIs and fluoroscopy. I can see that with just CT scans and general X‐ray procedures only, we are not getting the optimal diagnosis for some pathologies. (Participant 17)

This lack of resources negatively impacted students' learning experiences and their ability to practice essential skills.
This has affected me negatively because the clinical practice basically bridges the gap between what we are taught in class and what we do in the real world. So now there is a gap there because we do not have the equipment here so that we can bridge that gap. (Participant 5)

## Subtheme 1.2: Understaffing and workload
The clinical environments were often understaffed, leading to increased workload for both students and qualified staff.
And the other thing is the staff support which is very limited in state (public) hospitals. The departments are understaffed, so sometimes the students are the ones performing the procedures. (Participant 1)

There were multiple times where it was only one qualified radiographer on the floor and several students. (Participant 8)

This understaffing situation often resulted in students taking on more responsibilities than they were prepared for, potentially affecting the quality of care provided.
I feel we work for very long hours, sometimes 8 am to 8 pm. Also, when you are working night shift and late shift, I feel that it's too much for students, for people that are not yet qualified for the job. (Participant 12)

## Subtheme 1.3: Negative interactions with healthcare workers
Participants reported negative experiences when interacting with other healthcare workers within and external to the department.
When I get to the ward, sometimes the nurses on the floor can be rude. There is nothing you can do basically but put up with the attitude and get the procedure done. (Participant 7)

Sometimes you are doing a procedure, and you are positioning the patient, but you haven't put the lead stand yet, then the radiographer starts screaming at you and telling you what to do in front of the patient. It makes you feel like you do not know what you are doing. (Participant 10)

These interactions negatively affected the learning process and students' confidence.
Maybe I can say it is difficult to work with qualified radiographers. Some are difficult to approach, and you need to be careful the way you carry yourself around. (Participant 14)

## Subtheme 1.4: Challenges in providing optimal care
The various challenges in the clinical environment often result in difficulties in providing optimal care to patients.
So basically, it also negatively affects the patient and the quality of care that they get. (Participant 2)

Patient care is affected because the patients must wait for longer because it's only the two rooms that are working. (Participant 6)

These challenges sometimes led to compromises in patient care or incomplete procedures.
Sometimes when the ward staff don't want to assist, we have to cancel the procedure because there is nothing we can do without that help. Due to staff shortages sometimes all the nurses are busy, and the doctor won't help you because the doctor feels like that's the nurses' job. (Participant 17)

## Theme 2: Independent learning and development
Despite the challenges, students demonstrated resilience and adaptability in their learning process through self‐directed learning, indirect supervision and support that boosted their confidence.

## Subtheme 2.1: Self‐directed learning
Despite the clinical environment challenges, participants demonstrated a strong commitment to self‐directed learning to fill gaps in their clinical education.
What we always do is extra reading and watch YouTube videos to basically enlighten us on how the procedures should be done in real life. (Participant 5)

Whenever I find anything that is interesting in the department during the day, I write it down and then I'll put it up in my journal later as a reflection. (Participant 13)

This self‐directed learning approach helped students to supplement their practical experience and deepen their understanding of diagnostic radiography.
Reflection helps in a way as I get time to sit down when I'm not busy and reflect on the challenge with a bit of research on it. If I find an interesting pathology in the department, for example, I will be able to research and know more about it when I go home. (Participant 18)

## Subtheme 2.2: Indirect supervision and support
While direct supervision was often lacking, some participants reported receiving indirect support from qualified radiographers.
With one qualified radiographer overseeing several students… So, all students must rely on that qualified radiographer to give them advice and to evaluate their images. (Participant 3)

If we don't understand anything the radiographers will explain it clearly, if you ask. (Participant 10)

However, this support was not consistent across all clinical placements or situations.
What we are told by our lecturers is that we must always work under a radiographer's supervision but most of the time the qualified radiographers are not always there to provide the supervision. (Participant 11)

## Subtheme 2.3: Confidence building
Despite the challenges, many participants reported that their clinical experiences contributed to building their confidence and independence.
I can already say that I'm confident and can work independently. As a final year, you do get rostered for the night shift and often times you and the other students are attending to general procedures because the radiographers are busy with CT procedures. (Participant 9)

OK, I can say I have improved in a lot of ways because as much as you know when you are in the third year you are almost done. So comparing with my first year I can say I have improved very much and can perform most procedures with minimal support. (Participant 13)

This increased confidence and independence was seen as a positive outcome of their clinical experiences, despite the challenges faced.
Yeah, I learned how to be independent and handle the patient myself if the qualified is not there. Yeah, so I learned that here (public hospitals). (Participant 16)

## Theme 3: Patient care impact
Students demonstrated a strong commitment to patient care and quality service provision despite the challenges they faced.

## Subtheme 3.1: Patient‐centred care
Participants reported developing a strong sense of patient‐centred care through their clinical experiences.
I have learned to be patient with certain patients, not to judge patients harshly because you get different kinds of patients in public hospitals. Like I had a patient, who came in for a forearm x‐ray which was badly fractured and in severe pain, but I remained very calm. (Participant 4)

Okay so helping patients is now a passion for me, it feels great whenever I'm doing anything that involves helping people. (Participant 8)

These experiences helped students develop empathy and understanding for their patients.
The experiences also taught me to just have compassion and be kind to patients because they have so much more going on than what the clinical history indicates… (Participant 14)

## Subtheme 3.2: Commitment to quality service
Despite the challenges in their clinical environments, participants demonstrated a strong commitment to providing quality care to patients.
One night shift we had only one qualified radiographer and together we were running X‐ray room to room, ensuring that we do what we're supposed to be doing and doing it correctly. (Participant 1)

Having a helpful radiographer is very important because it means that instead of trying to correct my mistake blindly and exposing the patient again, I could get assistance and produce quality images. (Participant 7)

This commitment to quality service often requires students to adapt to challenging situations and find ways to provide the best possible care.
The challenges do not stop us from providing quality service to the patient. It might just delay services rendered to the patient at times… (Participant 12)

## Discussion
This study provides a deep understanding of diagnostic radiography students' experiences during clinical training in resource‐constrained public hospitals, revealing a complex interplay among institutional constraints, personal growth and professional development. The findings, categorised into three main themes—clinical environment challenges, independent learning and development, and patient care impact—offer valuable insights into the realities of diagnostic radiography education in resource‐constrained settings.
The challenges encountered in the clinical environment, particularly the lack of resources and equipment, echo findings from similar studies in other developing countries. Ugwu et al. reported comparable issues in Nigerian radiography education, where inadequate facilities hindered students' ability to practice essential skills. Other studies in sub‐Saharan Africa also echoed the lack of equipment as a training limitation. , , This suggests that resource limitations are a critical challenge in radiography education across developing nations, potentially compromising the quality of training and, by extension, future healthcare delivery.
Compounding these resource constraints, the issue of understaffing and increased workload emerged as a significant concern, supporting previous reports from a similar context. Interestingly, this challenge is not unique to resource‐constrained settings. Similar workforce shortages were identified in diagnostic radiography departments across the United Kingdom (UK), indicating that staffing issues represent a global challenge in healthcare systems. In the context of student training, these staffing shortages can lead to reduced supervision and increased responsibilities for students, potentially affecting the quality of their learning experiences and patient care.
The negative interactions with healthcare workers reported by participants highlight another critical aspect of the clinical learning environment. A previous study emphasised the importance of interprofessional relationships built through interprofessional learning in creating positive dynamics and preventing the transfer of negative discipline stereotypes into the workforce. These findings collectively underscore the need for improved interprofessional education and collaboration in healthcare settings, not only to enhance student learning but also to foster a more cohesive and effective healthcare team. A successful healthcare system is founded on good communication, respect and an understanding of each other's role. 

Despite these challenges, the study revealed a remarkable resilience and adaptability among students, manifested through their commitment to self‐directed learning. This aligns with previous literature that identified self‐regulated learning as a core component of medical education that supports students in achieving their learning goals and improving their performance. , The students' proactive approach, including the use of online resources and reflective practices, suggests a positive adaptation to their constrained learning environment ensuring the attainment of competencies. This adaptability is a crucial skill in the ever‐evolving field of medical imaging and bodes well for their future professional practice.
The concept of indirect supervision, as experienced by the participants, presents both opportunities and challenges. While it may develop independence and problem‐solving skills, it also raises concerns about the quality of supervision and potential patient safety issues. The importance of adequate clinical supervision in diagnostic radiography education was previously highlighted, with recommendations for a careful balance between student autonomy and appropriate guidance, especially in this study context. 

A positive outcome of the experienced challenges is the increased confidence reported by students, particularly in their final year. This finding aligns with the work of Inocian et al., who found that clinical placements significantly contribute to the development of critical decision‐making skills and professional behaviours in diagnostic radiography students. This growth in confidence and competence, despite the challenging environment, speaks to the resilience of the students and the possibility that challenges can foster professional development.
Perhaps most encouragingly, the study revealed a strong commitment to patient‐centred care among the students. This aligns with current healthcare trends, especially the emphasis on the importance of patient‐centred care in diagnostic radiography practice. , , , , The student's ability to maintain a focus on quality service despite challenging circumstances reflects positively on their professional development and augurs well for the future of diagnostic radiography practice.
These findings have several important implications for diagnostic radiography education and healthcare policy locally and in similar contexts. First, there is an urgent need to review the diagnostic radiography training equipment needs and engage more clinical facilities to ensure students have adequate exposure to all essential modalities. Second, addressing understaffing issues should be a priority to improve both patient care and student learning experiences possibly through an increase in the number of clinical lecturers and supervisors. Third, there is a need to develop and implement programs to foster better interprofessional relationships that could significantly enhance the clinical learning environment. Furthermore, there is a need to develop and implement more effective supervision models that balance student independence with adequate support and guidance. Finally, diagnostic radiography curricula should incorporate strategies to foster self‐directed learning and resilience in students, preparing them for the realities of clinical practice in resource‐constrained settings such as ours.

## Limitations
While this study provides valuable insights, it is important to acknowledge its limitations. The study was conducted in a single institution in one country, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other contexts. The sample size, while adequate for a qualitative study, may not capture the full range of experiences across all diagnostic radiography students in the program. Additionally, the reliance on self‐reported experiences has the potential to introduce recall bias and social desirability bias.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, this study sheds light on the complex realities of diagnostic radiography education in resource‐constrained settings. Despite significant challenges in the clinical environment, students demonstrated remarkable resilience, adaptability, and a strong commitment to patient care. These findings highlight the need for targeted interventions to improve the clinical learning environment, including increased resource allocation, better staffing strategies, and enhanced interprofessional education.
Future research should focus on developing and evaluating interventions to address the identified challenges, particularly in resource‐constrained settings. Additionally, longitudinal studies tracking students' experiences throughout their program and into early career practice could provide further insights into the long‐term impact of clinical training experiences. Addressing the challenges and enhancing the strengths identified in this study has the potential to positively impact diagnostic radiography education locally and in similar contexts, ultimately leading to improved patient care and a more robust healthcare workforce.
