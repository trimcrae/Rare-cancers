## Abstract

## Background
There is an increasing public, societal and policy imperative for effective integration of healthcare delivery systems. Central to integration in healthcare is a focus on people-centred health, access, patient empowerment, interprofessional teamwork and collaboration between all healthcare stakeholders – difficult to achieve in current silo-driven bureaucratic health organisations. Therefore, actor-network theory (ANT) offers a theoretical approach to understanding the complexities of healthcare delivery by unpacking the type of actor’s interplay between social elements and immaterial objects, their interactions, interdependencies and power dynamics.

## Aims
The first of its type, this systematic review aims to identify, synthesise, and appraise extant literature on the use and application of ANT in healthcare contexts.

## Methods
This systematic review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines and registered with PROSPERO. The authors generated a search strategy utilising 31 Boolean terms, conducting electronic searches of MEDLINE, CINAHL Complete, SCOPUS, PubMed, APA PsycINFO, Business Source Complete and Academic Search Complete. The studies obtained were evaluated for inclusion based on their alignment with the specified inclusion and exclusion criteria. Studies were independently evaluated by the authors, with all data synthesised using a thematic analysis.

## Results
From an initial 2,533 studies, the systematic review included 103 studies which utilised ANT within a healthcare context. The analysis of the studies identified trends in the application of ANT across healthcare which we categorised into four themes: healthcare delivery systems, technology and data, integrated care, and innovation management. The findings demonstrated variability and fragmentation in the application of ANT, often diverging from its fundamental principles.

## Conclusions
Decluttering the literature suggests three dimensions for understanding the relationships of actors, unidimensional ANT – based on single actors, bi-dimensional ANT, the relationship between two actors and multi-dimensional ANT, where human and non-human actors interact to impact healthcare outcomes. The limited number of studies on the use of ANT for integrated healthcare research highlights both its importance to the topic and the considerable research gap that must be addressed.

## Introduction
Healthcare organisations represent an interlinked matrix of complex regulatory dimensions, challenging the current bureaucratic non-agile models of healthcare delivery [ , ]. Healthcare is confronted by staff shortages, emerging health conditions, an aging population, access to funding, societal changes, e-health, regulatory and governance issues [ – ]. Additionally, healthcare providers need to address social healthcare inequalities, stronger patient advocacy demands, multicultural diversity, patient safety and align with SDG Goal 3, to increase access and promote health and well-being for all ages [ ].
In the face of such pressures and change, it can be easy to overlook or misunderstand the innate considerations and complexity that underpin the structuring and associated network of actors, their role, and subsequent influence in healthcare delivery. Consequently, there is a move towards a more, collaborative, integrated approach to healthcare delivery which will further enhance access, facilitating the more efficient and timely delivery of healthcare in changing societal contexts [ – ]. Research addressing the integration of healthcare is in an emergent stage as are discussions on its impact or benefits with questions raised about integration efforts getting lost in implementation due to the bureaucratic penetration of current structures [ ]. These bureaucratic structures promote individual and group behaviours which impact the level of interprofessional collaboration as professional groups may have differing expectations about how care should be delivered [ – ]. Given the focus on developing agile patient-focused healthcare integration, it is important to identify and understand the role and interactions of various healthcare actors (human and non-human) and how they contribute to or hinder the integration of healthcare for societal benefits. We suggest that actor-network theory (ANT hereafter) offers a theoretical framework to achieve this understanding.
ANT is a methodological approach to social theory which sensitises researchers to complex realities which might otherwise remain obscure [ ] and is identified as a sociological theory that takes the fundamental stance that the world comprises of hybrid entities which have both human and non-human elements [ ]. ANT acknowledges that collective efforts to alter social conditions are multifaceted systems of action, supported by sociotechnical networks [ ]. Within ANT, networks are labelled sociotechnical because of how they connect human and non-human entities [ ]. Since its inception in the 1980s by Bruno Latour and Michael Callon [ ], it has risen to prominence by being applied across various fields in the social sciences [ ]. A distinctive feature of ANT is how human and non-human actors engage in a seamless activity bringing networks together based on their association, with all actors assumed equals and approached on a symmetrical plane [ , – ]. As such, ANT is relational with Lower (2006) referring to the actors’ abilities to act being influenced by the relationship between actors [ ]. In ANT, organisations, society, and the underpinning processes are the product of their respective actors’ interactions which culminate in the formation of heterogeneous networks [ , ].
Networks are composed of actors and actants, both of which have fluid boundaries and are imbued with their agency as they are not fixed within rigid networks [ ]. Actor networks emerge when actors converge around mutual interests and subsequently consent to be involved in an association [ ] referred to as a process of ‘translation’ comprising four key stages: (1) problematization, (2) interessement, (3) enrolment and (4) mobilization [ ]. Problematization orientates around bringing together actors with a common interest whereby a focal actor assumes a position of dominance establishing itself as a gatekeeper in the network [ ]. Interessement entails efforts to incentivise other actors to support the network and the shared objective that it encompasses [ ]. It represents the embracing of a series of actions by the focal actor to gain the support of other actors for the network [ ]. Following interessement, the translation process proceeds to enrolment signalling the intent of actors to engage having accrued all the necessary knowledge [ ]. The final stage of the translation process culminates with mobilisation, a period of consideration for actors as a final iterative step to reaffirm that their roles align with their fellow actors in the network [ ]. The translation process provides a means of establishing the identities and conditions of actors’ interactions which is an important foundation in guiding the exchanges and success of the network in delivering outcomes.
Given the characteristics of ANT, it is viewed as a relevant framework to identify and understand the dynamics of healthcare actors to their roles and influence on healthcare delivery in line with research by [ – ]. ANT provides an approach that considers the role of ordinary material objects and practices influencing healthcare, something which often fails to receive wider sociological attention [ ]. Whilst some studies exist on the use of ANT in healthcare, as far as the authors are aware, there has been little attempt to bring the literature on this theory in the healthcare context together in a comprehensive research synthesis to inform future research. Therefore, this systematic review aims to identify, synthesise, and appraise extant literature on the use and application of ANT in healthcare contexts.
In addition, it will provide a classification of the dominant healthcare themes emerging from the literature review and signal aspects of ANT which are worthy of future research relevant to the ever-changing models of healthcare delivery. The first of its type, this systematic review will establish a strong theoretical foundation identifying what is known about the use of ANT in healthcare, the dynamics and interactions of healthcare actors and propose a future research agenda. This review investigates the integration of healthcare delivery, distinguishing it from ‘integrated care’. Integrated care is a model defined by its focus on coordinated, patient-centred care across services and is often described as a structured framework [ ]. In contrast, ‘integration’ represents a noun that refers to the active, collaborative process by which diverse actors, resources, and infrastructures come together to enhance care delivery [ ]. Thus, this research examines integration as an evolving process that seeks to understand how healthcare actors organise and align to achieve shared goals, especially in response to shifting healthcare demands. The review will provide a valuable resource for researchers, policymakers, and practitioners who wish to understand the complexity of healthcare actors and advance integration within patient-centred healthcare models.

## Methods
This systematic review will comprehensively identify, synthesise, and appraise extant literature on the use and application of ANT in healthcare contexts. The paper addresses the objectives as outlined in the published protocol paper for this study [ ] and listed below:
Identify and appraise the scope and quality of material to produce a contemporary detailed account of the evidence and learnings on the use of ANT in healthcare with a focus on the role and interactions of the various actors.
Generate a classification of dominant themes emerging from the review of the literature on the use of ANT in healthcare settings.
Uncover aspects of ANT in healthcare less researched and worthy of future research for changing dynamics of healthcare.

## Search strategy
We adhered to standard procedures for systematic reviews [ ], conforming to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [ ]. This systematic review is registered with Prospero (ID 455283) the international register for systematic reviews (CRD42023455283). We searched seven databases: MEDLINE, CINAHL Complete, SCOPUS, PubMed, APA PsycINFO, Business Source Complete and Academic Search Complete using Boolean terms (See supplemental table for full search terms) to identify peer-reviewed literature concerning ANT in Healthcare. All authors ensured they were knowledgeable about the use of Boolean systems and worked with the librarian from the Glucksman Library at the University of Limerick to ensure the most appropriate terms were selected for the systematic review [ ].

## Inclusion/exclusion criteria
To assess eligibility for inclusion, results had to conform with the following inclusion criteria: (1) Studies that discuss and outline the use and application of ANT within a healthcare context; (2) Studies which examine the role of ANT in comprehending the dynamic and influence of human/inanimate actors within healthcare settings; (3) be available in English; (4) be a published peer-reviewed study; (5) Studies published between 2013 and 2023. The following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) Studies published in languages other than English; (2) Conference abstracts and/or accompanying minutes, opinion/editorial articles; (3) Studies not within the healthcare context; (4) Unpublished or grey literature.

## Procedure
Database searches took place on 15th September – 22nd September 2023. Initial searches yielded a total of 2,533 records (Table ) with 1,881 records subsequently removed due to the 10-year publication restriction and all duplicate results were removed using Endnote V.X9. All authors independently screened the remaining 652 records for eligibility based on the title, abstract and keywords which led to the removal of 455 records. Where necessary, full-text articles were retrieved, and a full screening of the relevant articles was then completed to assess the eligibility of the remaining results. Where discrepancies occurred, all reviewers were consulted, and a decision was reached via consensus. All three reviewers independently completed a full-text sifting of the 197 records remaining to assess eligibility. Upon the completion of the full-text screening process, 103 records were included in the final review. ## Syntheses methodology and risk mitigation
Following the completion of the screening process, 103 articles were identified for inclusion in the final review, with the vast majority qualitative in nature. The thematic analysis represents a method for the identification, reporting and analysis of patterns within data [ ] and is widely adopted across qualitative studies as a broad schema for its application [ , ]. Holloway and Todres (2003) inserted caveats surrounding the complex, diverse and nuanced orientation of qualitative approaches advising that a thematic analysis is the most suitable method for qualitative analysis [ ]. Given this study data was synthesised through narrative synthesis using thematic analysis.
To ensure clarity, robustness and consistency across our approach, Braun and Clarke’s (2006) six-phase process was used to guide our work [ ]. In the first phase, ‘familiarization with the data’, each author read the identified literature and noted points of interest. The second phase, the ‘development of initial codes’ highlighted interesting aspects of the literature which were coded systematically. The third phase, ‘identifying, forming and describing themes’ involved collating codes into potential themes. The fourth phase, ‘reviewing and revising themes’ assessed their alignment with the coded extracts. In phase five an analysis to refine the specifics of each theme, and subsequent generation of clear definitions and labels for each theme was completed. Finally, the sixth phase entailed an analysis and selection of compelling extracts which culminated with the completion of the final review relevant to defining and labelling of themes.
Following the removal of duplications, each author was randomly allocated a sample of 10 articles to complete a title, abstract and keyword screening to assess for suitability with the inclusion/exclusion criteria. To ensure the consistency and accuracy of the application of the inclusion/exclusion criteria and to reduce bias, authors independently reviewed each other’s sample allocations with disagreements resolved in consensus. Strict adherence to the inclusion/exclusion criteria was applied throughout with studies not pertaining to the use of ANT within a healthcare context being omitted. The authors then began to familiarize themselves with the data through the identification of emerging themes in conjunction with the full article screening. Each author identified preliminary themes for their respective articles which were discussed and agreed upon following the cross-referencing and collation of each author’s independent notes. Four themes were agreed upon: delivery systems, innovation management, technology and data, and integrated care. One reviewer randomly allocated an equal distribution of articles for the completion of full-text screening and drafting of findings on the use and application of ANT in healthcare. To ensure accuracy, all authors independently cross-checked each other’s summary of their findings to identify errors of omission and inaccuracies in the application of the inclusion criteria.
To mitigate the risk of bias a variety of measures were applied during the planning and development process. Before commencing the review, advice was sought from the relevant expert librarians at the UL Glucksman Library to select the relevant search strategy. After completing the search across the seven databases, all records were exported to EndNote and allocated into respective folders. Each team member was given access to these folders to ensure clarity, coherence, and transparency. Weekly team meetings were held to resolve ambiguities and maintain transparency throughout data collation and screening and in the process we aimed to minimise subjectivity. The fact that all team members agreed on the studies to be included in the final list serves to reduce the potential for bias [ ].

## Findings
The review entailed 102 qualitative studies and 1 mixed-methods study. There has been continued interest in this theory in recent times (Fig. ), with further illustrations (Fig. ) highlighting that over 23% of included studies come from 4 key journals. The studies included in the review were drawn from a variety of healthcare settings including 29 studies from the Acute setting, 33 from the primary care setting, 17 from the public health setting, and 5 from a residential care setting with the remaining 19 studies dispersed across mixed settings. Of the included studies, 75 of them opted to use ANT as their standalone theory for analysing their research whilst the remaining 28 utilised varying other theories and philosophies in conjunction with ANT. Many of the included studies came from high-ranking journals as 63 studies were from Q1 journals, 23 studies were from Q2 journals, 7 studies were from Q3 journals, 2 studies were from Q4 journals, with the remaining 8 studies belonging to journals which were not allocated a ranking from the Scimago ranking site. Table showcases the characteristics of each included study in further detail.
 Each of the 103 articles within this systematic review were categorised thematically under the following headings: delivery systems, technology and data, innovation management and integrated care respectively. The delivery systems theme (N = 39) is a selection of studies addressing the delivery and provision of care and treatment services. This theme also caters for the accompanying developmental practices, interventions, education, and public directives which influence the manner and future direction of how healthcare is imagined and delivered. Within the healthcare delivery theme, articles mainly focused on non-human actors and the role they play in the delivery of care and its associated activities. The technology and data (N = 32) theme seeks to detail studies which focus on the role of technology and data within healthcare operations. It delves into the implications for the operational, management, and clinical domains of healthcare following the implementation and introduction of respective technologies. Innovation management (N = 22) considers studies revolving around the management and organisational structure of healthcare in addition to the innovative efforts which underpin how healthcare is imagined and subsequently delivered. Efforts to better understand the process and the constituents which are actively engaged in these processes have led to the use of ANT being utilised in both isolation and combination with varying theories, frameworks, and philosophies. Finally, the integrated care theme (N = 10) represents a collection of studies which focus on the collaborative and integrative efforts of healthcare professionals both within traditional organisational structures and within integrated care settings.
21% of the papers in this review adhered to the core principles of ANT as they utilised literature from the seminal authors, incorporated all phases of the translation process and acknowledged the agency of both human and non-human actors in actor-networks. To illustrate, our study found that almost 10% of included articles failed to reference the workings of ANT’s founding authors, John Law [ ], Bruno Latour [ ], and Michael Callon [ ]. Additionally, a fundamental aspect of ANT is the symmetrical attribution of agency to both human and non-human actors [ ]. The term ‘non-human’ serves as a broad category within ANT, encompassing a wide array of entities. However, it explicitly excludes purely symbolic entities [ ], supernatural beings [ ], and those that combine both human and non-human elements [ ]. In ANT, non-humans are conceptualised as ‘full-blown actors’ [ ] with agency equal to humans, forming a core principle of the theory. Our review highlighted a notable gap in considering the theoretical concept of agency in ANT; 50% of the included studies made no mention of agency, while 17% referred to it no more than twice. Of the studies included in this review, 28% used ANT alongside an additional theory, framework, or philosophy, adopting a theoretical triangulation approach to extend ANT’s application [ ]. Meanwhile, 58% of the studies did not refer to the translation process, indicating a divergence from a core principle of ANT [ , ]. Additionally, qualitative approaches, especially interviews, focus groups, and ethnography, dominated methodological choices among studies employing ANT, with only one study [ ] implementing a mixed-methods design.

## Delivery systems
Sub-themes within the delivery systems theme focused on the role of human and non-human actors, health disasters, drugs and the processes attached to the application of ANT within the domain of healthcare delivery. It highlights the prevalence of sociomateriality as a force which shapes relationships throughout care delivery and the broader healthcare context [ ]. A cohort of articles exhibited minimal reference to or application of ANT, with most articles in this classification failing to make any inference to the process of translation. The focus on non-human actors in this theme was evident among several articles (N = 13) which identified the importance of non-human actors in the delivery of healthcare given their capacity to regulate care delivery and shape stakeholder relationships. These actors included physical buildings and equipment, treatment therapies and tracking systems. Architectural space is a resilient actor with strong enrolling capacity in buildings used to deliver respite care [ ] and as well in the provision of care within an aged care setting [ ]. Interestingly Ivanova et al. (2016) warn us that buildings should be considered as part of an overall ontology as they contributed systematically to care, suggesting the crucial roles they play as actors in the care emplacement process [ ].
Medical equipment as non-human actors in the delivery of care have been identified in healthcare delivery, for example, the use of ventilators and genetic testing as non-human actors in the management of genetic disorders [ ] and extending human life [ ]. Notably, Jepsen and colleagues considered the cardiotocography machine in their research as enacting multiple and typically human roles including a babysitter, a midwife, a partner, a protector, an agent of shared responsibility, a guest, and a disturber of normal birth [ ]. Similarly, McKinnon considered the bed, stirrups, IV drip and a measuring tape as non-human actors which assist the birthing process [ ]. Interestingly, a geriatric assessment tool solely established patient fitness for chemotherapy [ ] with Halpin (2016) showcasing the significance of a statistical manual called DSM [ ], both of which pervasively influenced the enactment of care.
The emergence of treatment tracking and evaluation practices in healthcare delivery emerged with Gardner and Cribb (2016) acknowledging the Canadian occupational performance measure as a power storage tool [ ]. Studies also sought to track associations and identify barriers to care delivery within the home [ ], whilst Rossi and Pereira (2014) unearthed components impacting patient adherence to AIDS treatment [ ]. In addition, Keane (2013) found that Nicotine Replacement Therapy (NRT) could alter both the effects of nicotine and the characteristics of smokers [ ].
The importance of the role played by human actors in the delivery of healthcare was prevalent across multiple articles (N = 10). The influence of human actor perceptions such as those of patients and healthcare professionals were outlined and unpacked to inform varying aspects of care delivery, across chronic illness treatment, surgery, and rehabilitation. Multiple articles (N = 8) centred on the provision of care within the field of chronic illnesses. Studies on chronic illness care sought to unpack the role of patients and their perceptions of treatments for kidney failure [ ] chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [ , ] and heart failure [ ]. Within the cancer and rehabilitation sphere, human actors developed information pathways in breast cancer survivorship care [ ] whilst active patient participation was identified as imperative in a cervical cancer programme [ ] and neurological rehabilitation [ ]. Notably, Dehl et al. (2021) observed that shortcomings in intersectoral transitions impeded the discharge management of older people with cognitive impairment [ ].
The literature also exhibited a focus across the areas of pre-surgery preparation, obesity care assemblages and performance improvement systems. Ibrahim et al. (2015) outlined the importance of mental imagery in the preparation for orthopaedic surgery [ ], with Vogel (2018) contributing to how actor agency is distributed through obesity surgery and treatment [ ]. Nestel et al. (2017) showcased how surgical training was strongly influenced by non-human actors such as logbook numbers and departmental meetings [ ]. Interestingly, Rapin et al. (2022) leaned on critical realism to conceptualise how feedback to interprofessional care teams shapes performance improvement systems [ ].
In the process of delivering healthcare, authors applied ANT in different ways. Broughton et al. (2023) showed how ICU guidelines triggered government control and strengthened the physicians’ profession [ ] whilst Ufodike et al. (2022) revealed that accountability and governance forced actors to converge on a purpose [ ]. Gilman (2022) observed that music therapy relies upon intersecting resources and identities rather than fixed understandings of clients [ ]. Two studies focused on breastfeeding as Newell (2013) showed it to be a complex articulation of materialities, spaces and relations [ ], whilst Umbelino-Walker et al. (2023) discovered that countries carrying out WBTi (world breastfeeding trends initiative) assessments garnering better outcomes [ ]. Meanwhile, Rooney et al. (2023) acknowledged that to achieve success, single-handed care required duties of care across well-being, efficiency, and innovation to be satisfied [ ]. Representing new insights, Dew (2016) highlighted how the clinic room, the home and multidisciplinary meetings were integral to the process of legitimatising health knowledge [ ].
Various articles (N = 7) represented a consistent thread focusing on the development and use of drugs as a root cause and source of remedy within multiple contexts in the delivery of care. Drugs and alcohol were labelled as an actor and an actor-network within methadone maintenance treatment and unhealthy alcohol use care respectively [ , ] and as a boundary object in Geneyro and Tarado (2015) [ ]. Additionally, Ekendahl and Karlsson (2021) showed how recipients of drug and alcohol treatment were interpellated based on several logics [ ]. Interestingly, studies investigated Covid-19, as Blanco et al. (2022) identified the vaccination as a technoscientific artefact [ ], whilst Frimpong et al. (2022) showed how community-based organisations (CBOs) problematized Covid-19 as a health risk, signalling disadvantaged communities were better placed to tackle health disasters [ ]. Additionally, Tirado et al. (2015) asserted that socio-technical operators such as protocols and guidelines, developed a network which turned the A (H1N1) influenza into an epidemic, resulting in major implications for healthcare of the broader global society [ ].

## Technology and data
In recent times, the role of technology has become increasingly pertinent and subsequently lauded as a remedy to the profusion of challenges arising across healthcare and the broader sociology of health and illness [ ]. Whilst much of the research to date has focused on the contributions of technology in the delivery of healthcare services, attention has also been directed towards the implications of its implementation on the network of actors embroiled in these seismic shifts in the healthcare landscape.
Within this major theme, several sub-factions came to the fore such as the role of technology as an assistive care mechanism, the technology implementation process and associated data and medical records compiled within healthcare. The use of technology as an assistive care mechanism was prevalent across multiple articles (N = 8). The adoption of assistive technologies was visible in-home care for the elderly [ ], diabetes self-management [ ], tuberculosis care delivery [ ] and through the conceptualisation of an app for stroke patients [ ]. Similarly, technology was pivotal in dementia care [ ] and medication adherence using GPS devices [ ]. Maurizio and Petroccia (2023) concluded that e-health/e-care serves as a transforming venue for global social communication [ ]. Meanwhile, Morgan et al. (2022) captured the dossette box as a lo-fi technology which made end-of-life care for the elderly more manageable [ ]. Sociologically, this paper advances upon existing accounts of the power circulation between two settings, the hospital and the home [ ].
Further studies focused on the technology implementation process in healthcare settings [ ] across areas such as infection prevention [ ], hybrid operating rooms [ ], speech recognition [ ] and electronic medical records recording systems [ , ]. The work of Silvis and Alexander (2014) elaborated upon the development and implementation of health information systems and how graphical syntax can assist researchers in following the actor [ ]. Furthermore, information system implementation increased service mobility and overall care quality in Namibia [ ]. Within contexts where nursing and technology interact [ , ], usability and fidelity were highlighted as essential to the implementation of a point-of-care information system [ ], whilst Nguyen et al. (2015) warned about how electronic documentation redefined actor roles [ ]. Additionally, the implications of technology introduction for healthcare professionals as attempts to answer ethical questions were seen in Heinsch et al. (2019) [ ], whilst Dupret and Friborg (2018) explored the navigation of technology workarounds [ ]. Nyella and Kimaro (2015) identified software selection as key to mitigating HIS (health information systems) standardisation challenges [ ]. A key observation from Greenhalgh et al. (2019) noted the prioritisation of basic dependability as key for those introducing new technologies in healthcare settings [ ], as Cavalcante et al. (2019) showcased how actors at a federal level impeded the introduction of a computerization strategy in Brazil [ ]. Compellingly, Russo-Spena and Cristina (2019) identified third-party actors as; brokers, mediators and coalitionists as developers of innovative e-health solutions [ ].
With the volume and variety of data in healthcare increasing exponentially, the importance of transforming it into a more purposeful resource to aid care trajectory and planning is imperative [ ]. ANT has become an integral component of this shifting data landscape as efforts to collate, retain and preserve medical records and patient data intensify. Iyamu and Mgudlwa exhibited the use of ANT in three of their studies [ – ] which all revolve around big data and transforming it into a resource for healthcare stakeholders. Dong (2015) expressed the importance of preserving medical records due to their role in defining and treating illnesses [ ], whilst Donetto et al. (2021) showcased how patient experience data opens new avenues for improvement interventions [ ]. Notably, Donetto et al. (2021) generated sociologically relevant insights which shed light on the emergent orientation of both healthcare and society as a whole [ ]. Contrastingly, prediction records highlighted how the presence of bias impedes the use of new methodologies [ ].

## Innovation management
Health systems and those coordinating their efforts have proved relatively effective in past decades in delivering upon what they were intended for; provision and management of treatment for those with access. As the influence and exposure to external stimuli continue to alter the landscape of healthcare, rendering the role of management and innovation increasingly pertinent in negotiating the change process whilst maintaining quality care delivery to society. Within this broader theme, the emergence of sub-themes such as non-human actors, knowledge transfer and learning proved to be prominent within innovation management in healthcare.
Much of the literature (N = 13) centred on the influence of non-human actors in the management and governance of healthcare settings. For instance, studies showed how audit practices [ ] and ranking systems [ ] influenced governance regimes. Dupret (2019) identified silence as a key actor in change processes [ ] and Stoopendaal and Bal (2013) explained how sustained improvements occur not because of one central actor, but because of all those within the relevant network [ ]. Change and improvement processes emerged, as personnel changes in clinical management [ ], implementation of value-based healthcare [ ], improvements to performance monitoring [ ] and quality improvement measurement systems [ , ] enhanced care and governance practices [ ]. Interestingly, nurses’ organising work [ ] and uniform design [ ] were obligatory passage points that influenced care delivery and the future of their role. Hellman et al. (2015) found that the realisation of process orientation is hindered by neglect and resistance from physicians [ ].
The importance of knowledge transfer and learning in the innovation process was pivotal in the implementation of critical event cards [ ], health partnership programmes [ ], health promotion initiatives [ , ] and harm reduction policies [ ]. However, Hanssen et al. (2015) warned that understanding the human struggle in social work undermines the knowledge that underpins the discipline [ ]. Waldorff (2013) showcased health centres as new organisational concepts sustained by non-human actors [ ], whilst Turner et al. (2018) signalled that deficiencies in organisational capabilities impede innovation management [ ]. Finally, Koivisto and Pohjola (2015) showed how an innovation model for healthcare co-design comprised mutually constitutive sections which should be performed to achieve sustainable change [ ].

## Integrated care
As healthcare systems face persistent pressures, a shift from traditional models towards the integration and collaboration of care delivery has been widely advocated [ – ]. Pioneering a willingness to integrate system configurations and encourage collaborative practices has become integral as efforts to respond to the rising complexity of healthcare and prioritize societal well-being have become increasingly pertinent. However, the surprisingly low number of studies (N = 10) in this theme considered interprofessional collaboration and integration. Studies analysed interprofessional collaboration between healthcare professionals [ ], speciality care teams in a cardiology unit [ ], and the power of process orientation in enhancing cooperation and collaboration in healthcare organisations. Notably, Allan et al. (2023) and Rioux-Dubois and Perron (2016) focused on how collaborative practices influenced work and recruitment practices within acute and primary care settings respectively [ , ]. Allan et al. (2023) emphasized how sociomateriality sheds light on the human and non-human interplay within a healthcare practice environment [ ]. Further studies focused on learning and development as Bilodeau et al. (2017) identified challenges associated with intersectoral action and actor convergence [ ], whilst Rapin et al. (2019) and McMurtry et al. (2016) unearthed how feedback is dispersed and how learning takes place within a collaborative interprofessional healthcare context [ , ]. Additionally, studies sought to track the integration of healthcare organisations in the UK [ ]and patients’ vision for a tailored integrated care network for those living with Parkinson’s disease [ ]. Finally, Asplin (2023) contributed to the practice of patient-centred care by signalling the importance of patient voice in service and facility design [ ].

## Discussion, future research and implications for policy and practice
Our thematic assessment clearly demonstrates a dimensional approach employed when utilizing ANT in research to deconstruct the sociology of healthcare. From this analysis, we propose a dimensional categorisation approach that can organise existing research and guide future studies. The relationships can be considered from three distinct levels, unidimensional ANT – based on single actors, bi-dimensional ANT – considering the relationship between two actors and multi-dimensional ANT, where multiple actors both human and non-human interact to impact healthcare outcomes.
Our review captures the unidimensional insight, where a singular actor dictates the process and distribution of the network’s power relations. Actors such as the hybrid operating room expose the importance and influence of clearly negotiated and constructed discipline boundaries [ ]; critical event cards as actors that track complex interventions and support future dynamic patient care [ ]; and CBO’s (community-based organisations) as a coordinating actor in the response to health disasters including Covid-19 [ ]. Though this unidimensional ANT approach is important we suggest the development of a clearly defined classification process for the actors chosen in researched studies to circumvent ambiguities by ensuring the appositeness of the chosen actor. This approach could benefit from future research as we continue our efforts to ‘follow the actors’ ([ ], p.12) and enhance the ‘ordering, sorting and selection’ ([ ], p.115) process for the actors as a way of maximising the utility derived from future ANT studies. For example, research should consider a process for the development of clearly defined or formalised typologies and/or classifications of actors based on power and influence characteristics which are centred on the focus of the research. By establishing clearer typologies, healthcare policymakers and practitioners can more effectively anticipate and leverage the roles of various actors which shape healthcare outcomes. This approach could enhance targeted interventions and optimize resource allocation, ultimately strengthening the integration, resilience and responsiveness of healthcare networks. Such clarity is particularly valuable for translating research findings into actionable policy and practice [ ].
From a bi-dimensional perspective, ANT portrays how the interactions and relationships of two actors serve as a significant contributor to the configuration and functioning of a healthcare network. For example, in substance abuse care, the context and individual moderate the effects and diagnosis [ ]. From a bi-dimensional standpoint, ANT elucidates how interactions between two actors significantly contribute to the structure and functioning of healthcare networks. For instance, in substance abuse care, the interplay between context and individual identity can moderate diagnostic outcomes [ ]. This perspective extends to the critical human-nonhuman relationships, such as those between nurses and technology, emphasizing the necessity of a sociotechnical lens in understanding care delivery [ ] and how non-human actors, such as the built environment are intrinsically linked to the sustenance of human agency in care facilities [ ]. This perspective offers a useful way of comprehending the often-ignored complex bi-dimensional relationship between human and non-human actors in practice. Policymakers designing healthcare delivery frameworks should consider the agency of non-human actors, to create more supportive and responsive healthcare environments. Given the growing prominence of technology and AI, future research should uncover nuances in the human and non-human relationship [ ], considering for example the power distribution within the network between human and non-human stakeholders.
Acknowledging the complexity of multi-actor interactions within healthcare networks offers valuable insights for practice by guiding practitioners in designing systems that better accommodate diverse sociomaterial relationships, thereby optimizing information flow and coordination across various contexts, cultures, and practices. The majority of studies found through this review resided within the multidimensional portrayals of ANT where the interrelationships of multiple actors speak to the complexity of network configurations and functionality. It extends upon previous iterations to explain how the varying interactions between sociomaterial entities (human and non-human) play a pivotal role within the societal context of healthcare [ ], Our review also articulates the processes and assemblages involved in the development of networks and the multiplicity of actors which contribute to healthcare activities such as the geopolitics of birth [ ]; unpacking the black box in breast cancer survivorship [ ]; performance measurement as a translation process in healthcare management (Leotta and Ruggeri 2017) [ ] and the use of artificial intelligence in tuberculosis detection and diagnosis [ ]. Future research should consider the complexity of the multi-actor exchange, for example, the parameters which regulate the information flows such as context, culture, conditions and behaviour.
The findings of our systematic review show that ANT is a common theoretical construct used to explain the networks of relationship between human and non-human actors in healthcare systems. It extends to provide sociological insights into how ordinary, mundane entities/objects can play a pivotal role in the provision of healthcare [ ]. Our review, the first of its type, systematically identified studies within the healthcare domain which utilised ANT as a way of advancing both this theory and its use to enhance healthcare practice. By collating and analysing 103 studies our review established a comprehensive understanding of the research to date on ANT in healthcare by clearly delineating its use under four key themes including healthcare delivery systems; technology and data; integrated care and innovation management. This review highlights the need for policy frameworks that acknowledge and integrate the roles of both human and non-human actors, ensuring more comprehensive and effective approaches to healthcare delivery and improved health outcomes. In healthcare delivery, the review also cohesively extends our knowledge of the importance of considering both human and non-human actors in healthcare delivery and the particular pervasive importance of these actors in health outcomes.
Our review also found several overarching themes which are notable for future research on ANT in the integration of healthcare delivery including theoretical triangulation and a significant concern around a fragmented theoretical approach. ANT helps us uncover how various actors, both human and non-human integrate to influence positive patient and societal outcomes. Such sociomaterial assemblages illustrate the entanglement of social and organisational actors/networks within healthcare settings [ ]. The paucity of studies found in this review on the use of ANT in integrated healthcare suggests a considerable gap that should be filled to potentially strengthen policy frameworks by offering a deeper understanding of how actor collaborations enhance service integration and improve resource efficiency in practice. For example, future studies could focus on the effectiveness of actor collaborations in integrated care based on community health improvement [ ] or the design and efficacy of a unique context or disease-specific network of actors in the delivery of integrated care [ ].
Combining ANT with additional theories or frameworks, as seen in many studies, indicates a growing trend toward theoretical triangulation to enhance ANT’s analytical power. This approach reflects the value researchers find in complementing ANT with other perspectives to better capture healthcare’s complex dynamics, highlighting ANT’s flexibility in integrated healthcare applications where diverse methodologies can uncover otherwise overlooked interactions. The hesitance to use ANT as a standalone framework received support based on perceived inadequacies of ANT including; acknowledging the wider macro context [ ]; recognising its limitations when analysing the core dimensions of network assemblages [ ]; the implications of ANT’s flat ontology and the subsequent need for theoretical assistance to closely engage with the concept of relationships between human and non-human actors [ ] and concerns about its imperative that knowledge could only be empirically obtained at the micro-level which impedes its wider application [ ]. These studies enacted the Trip model (Translating Research into Practice), Postphenomenology and the Normalisation Process Theory respectively to help alleviate their perceived inadequacies of ANT. Based on these concerns and subsequent findings, future research could consider the benefit of taking a theoretical triangulation approach when using ANT to enhance its broader application based on the topic of study [ ].
Our paper offers valuable insights into how ANT is practically applied across studies, shedding light on the varied mannerisms and practices researchers adopt when utilizing the theory. This highlights the adaptability of ANT to diverse contexts and underscores its role as a flexible framework for examining complex networks in healthcare. Even though all studies preserved the term actor and the heterogeneity of the respective networks as intended in the seminal work by recognising and ascribing agency to both human and non-human actors, a selective and fragmented implementation of ANT emerged. Our review found that studies did not always consider the foundations upon which the theory was built, resulting in the use of ANT that was characterised by an implementation which was selective, varied and invariably incomplete. A limited number of the studies in our review fully adhered to the foundational principles of ANT by incorporating the work of seminal authors, following all phases of the translation process, and recognizing both human and non-human agency within actor-networks. By underemphasizing agency, the current literature may be overlooking crucial insights into how both human and non-human actors assert influence, adapt, and shape healthcare processes. Future research should therefore explore the nuances of agency in healthcare networks, examining how different actors negotiate roles and exert influence. This lack of comprehensive engagement suggests that many researchers selectively apply elements of ANT, potentially limiting the theory’s analytical depth and distorting its capacity to capture complex interactions within healthcare. The failure of included studies to highlight the key contributions of ANT’s seminal authors indicates a disconnect from the theoretical rigour required to maximize ANT’s utility in healthcare contexts. Such selective applications may hinder a nuanced understanding of the interactions shaping healthcare practices, suggesting a need for more structured adherence to ANT principles in future studies. The lack of emphasis on the translation process in a substantial portion of studies suggests a shift from foundational ANT principles. This deviation may indicate a selective application of ANT, potentially limiting its full analytical potential and highlighting the challenges researchers face in adhering to all aspects of the theory within complex healthcare contexts. For example, several studies restricted their analysis to incorporate just two of the four phases outlined in the translation process, enrolment, and mobilisation [ , , , ]. The failure to acknowledge all four phases in the process of translation raises a potential knowledge gap surrounding the specificities of the development of the actor-network and how the margins of manoeuvre are negotiated and delineated [ ]. Although research pioneered ANT to understand the complex web of healthcare, as previously suggested, there is a marked lack of completeness in the application of the theory and the concepts which underpin it. Policymakers should consider this selective application of ANT in healthcare as it highlights the need for policy to support holistic frameworks that fully address complex actor interactions which aim to enhance the adaptability and effectiveness of healthcare practice. We suggest the importance for future studies to focus and align their research on the origins of ANT to ensure the existence of its true and complete form.
This review reveals a strong tendency toward qualitative methodologies in studies applying ANT, with only one study [ ] utilizing a mixed-methods approach. This dominance of qualitative methods, such as interviews, focus groups, and ethnography, reflects ANT’s focus on capturing complex actor relationships and nuanced, context-specific insights. While qualitative approaches align with ANT’s interpretive strengths, the scarcity of mixed methods suggests a potential area for expansion; combining qualitative and quantitative insights could enhance our understanding of actor-network dynamics and broaden ANT’s applicability in healthcare research. We recommend that future research explore the potential of integrating quantitative methodologies with ANT to enhance its adaptability and deepen its application across diverse healthcare settings. This approach may yield valuable insights into actor relationships and expand the versatility of ANT across varied healthcare contexts.

## Study limitations
Only studies published in English were included in the review which potentially omits studies of relevance in non-English languages. Studies were limited to the ten years between 2013 and 2023, introducing bias, as earlier studies may have been appropriate for inclusion [ ]. While we employed an inclusive search strategy, the findings remain limited by the specific search terms utilized. Given the diversity in the terminology used within healthcare literature, it is possible that our search may not have captured all pertinent studies, particularly those that might frame Actor-Network Theory in healthcare differently or emphasize other key concepts not explicitly covered in our search terms. Consequently, we may not have captured terminology utilised by others which may have led to the omission of relevant studies.

## Conclusion
This systematic review comprehensively collated relevant studies using the theoretical lens of ANT to develop an understanding of the role, influence and interaction of human and non-human actors in dynamic healthcare settings. The review enhanced our understanding of the broader sociology of healthcare and showcased how a sociological lens such as ANT can add value at both a theoretical and practical level. The results highlighted variation and fragmentation in the use of ANT that did not always align with the core principles of the theory. In a healthcare context, our analysis reveals a dimensional approach to applying ANT in research, from which we proposed a categorisation framework to guide research to establish a better understanding and strengthen ANT’s contributions to healthcare delivery and operations. By imploring cognisance of the seminal literature and ANT’s core principles which encompass actors, actants, translation and network assemblages, this review can provide a platform to guide future research and contribute to the sustenance of a theory which reimagined the social.
