## Abstract

## Background
Early prevention of Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is critical not only for protecting maternal and infant health but also for reducing the long-term healthcare burden associated with chronic diseases. Physical activity is a well-established modifiable behavior for GDM prevention; however, existing physical activity interventions during pregnancy have shown limited effectiveness. Partner involvement in behaviour change represents a promising approach theoretically, but empirical evidence remains scarce. This study protocol describes a randomized controlled trial designed to evaluate the effectiveness of a couple-based physical activity intervention for the prevention of GDM.

## Methods
This single-blind, two-arm, parallel-group randomized controlled trial will be conducted at an obstetrics and gynecology hospital in Guangzhou, China. A total of 244 pregnant individuals at high risk for GDM and their partners will be randomly assigned to either an intervention group receiving a couple-based physical activity intervention or a control group receiving usual antenatal care. The intervention starts at 5–12+ 6 weeks of gestation and continues until 24 weeks. It consists of one face-to-face group dyadic education session, one online group dyadic education session, biweekly online personalized dyadic counseling sessions, and a daily behavior change booster delivered via WeChat. Intervention delivery is supported by a dedicated WeChat Official Account, Pregnancy Support Station (孕期加油站), developed by the research team. Primary outcomes are the incidence of GDM, blood glucose levels, and physical activity levels. Secondary outcomes include gestational weight gain, depressive symptoms, potential mediators (physical activity knowledge, fear of physical activity, dyadic coping, and physical activity self-efficacy), and maternal, paternal, and neonatal health outcomes. Data will be collected at 5–12+ 6, 24–27+ 6, and 35–36+ 6 weeks of gestation, and within 3 days after delivery.

## Discussion
This intervention views pregnant individuals and their partners as an interdependent dyad, frames GDM prevention as a shared responsibility within couples, and empowers couples to mobilize interpersonal resources to collaboratively improve maternal physical activity. Findings from the forthcoming randomized controlled trial may help optimize GDM prevention strategies in high-risk populations and support the integration of partner involvement into routine antenatal care.

## Trial registration
Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2500107402 (registered 11 August 2025).

## Background
Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is defined as glucose intolerance first identified during pregnancy [ ]. It is one of the most common pregnancy complications, with a global prevalence estimated at 14.0% [ ], and exceeding 20% in China [ ]. Pregnant individuals with high-risk factors for GDM, such as polycystic ovary syndrome, a family history of diabetes, or advanced maternal age, have an higher increased risk of GDM, with reported risks ranging from 39 to 66% [ – ]. GDM increases the risk of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, preterm delivery, macrosomia, and neonatal hypoglycemia (Sweeting et al., 2022). Although GDM often resolves after birth, individuals with GDM have a substantially increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes, obesity, and cardiovascular disease in life [ , ]. These increased risk can also pass along to the offspring, perpetuating a cycle of metabolic dysfunction across generations [ ]. Therefore, early prevention of GDM is critical not only to protect maternal and infant health but also to reduce the long-term healthcare burden associated with chronic diseases. However, the current focus in countries continues to be on early diagnosis and treatment of GDM. Practice level protocols targeting pregnancy for preventing GDM are lacking.
Physical activity, defined as any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that results in energy expenditure [ ], is widely recommended for both the general population and pregnant individuals because of its broad health benefits [ ]. It is also recognized as one of the most effective preventive strategy for GDM [ , ]. Regular physical activity improves glucose homeostasis through its strong effects on insulin sensitivity [ ] and has been shown to reduce the risk of GDM by up to 39% [ ]. Moreover, it contributes to healthier gestational weight gain, reduced depressive symptoms, lower rates of instrumental delivery, and fewer adverse birth outcomes [ – ].
Despite these well-established benefits, physical inactivity during pregnancy remains a major public health concern. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that pregnant individuals without contraindications engage in at least 150 min of moderate-intensity physical activity per week [ ] However, approximately 60% of pregnant individuals worldwide are physically inactive [ ], and the rate in China may be as high as 79% [ ].
Previous studies have developed interventions to improve physical activity during pregnancy; however, these interventions have generally yielded only small effects [ ] or, in some cases, no significant effects [ ]. A key limitation of these interventions is their predominant focus on the pregnant individual in isolation. Responsibility for behavior change and adherence is implicitly placed solely on the pregnant individual, relying heavily on personal willpower, which is not always easy to maintain [ ]. This individual-oriented approach reflects a disconnect between intervention strategies and evidence demonstrating that maternal physical activity behaviors are influenced by both intrapersonal and interpersonal factors [ ].
Couple-based physical activity interventions may represent a promising approach for the prevention of GDM. Support from intimate partners is one of the most important interpersonal factors of physical activity during pregnancy [ ]. Beyond exerting direct effects on health behaviors, intimate partners also shape how individuals respond to behavior change interventions [ ]. These synergistic processes are well described in dyad-level models from relationship science, such as the Cognitive-Transactional Model (CTM). According to the CTM, pregnant individuals and their partners form an interdependent dyad in which each person’s cognitions, emotions, and behaviors mutually influence the other [ , ]. When couples perceive GDM prevention as a shared responsibility requiring joint appraisal and coping, both self-efficacy and dyadic efficacy are strengthened, resulting in synergistic effects on behavior change that exceed the impact of individual efforts alone [ – ]. Consistent with the CTM, a meta-analysis indicates that interventions involving partners are more effective in promoting physical activity than equivalent interventions targeting individuals alone [ ].
Nevertheless, the partners of pregnant individuals are usually excluded from routine antenatal care and often lack clear guidance on how to provide effective support [ ]. Most existing couple-based physical activity interventions have been conducted among non-pregnant couples, limiting their direct applicability to pregnancy. Also, reports of these interventions often do not clarify what mechanisms of action of health behavior change, as well as how partners are actively involved and instructed to interact within dyadic interventions [ ]. In some cases, although partners attend sessions and receive health information alongside pregnant individuals, they remain passive recipients rather than active collaborators, limiting the added value of a couple-based approach [ – ]. In addition, many interventions do not empower couples to tailor behavior change strategies to their own preferences, daily routines, and contextual constraints, instead relying on standardized, one-size-fits-all approaches that underutilize couples’ internal resources and relational strengths.
In response to these gaps, we developed a dyadic theory-informed, couple-based physical activity intervention tailored for pregnant individuals at high risk for GDM and their partners. The present intervention emphasizes GDM prevention as a shared responsibility, leveraging the mutual influence and support within couples to facilitate behavior change. Given that interventions requiring frequent in-person sessions impose substantial logistical and financial burdens on pregnant individuals [ ], the intervention is delivered both face-to-face and via WeChat, the most widely used social media platform in China. This study protocol describes the design of a randomized controlled trial (RCT) to evaluate whether the couple-based physical activity intervention can reduce the incidence of GDM, improve physical activity levels, and promote maternal, paternal, and infant health outcomes.

## Methods

## Trial design
This single-blind, two-arm, parallel-group RCT will be conducted within a superiority framework. Pregnant individuals and their partners will be randomly assigned to either the control group, receiving usual antenatal care, or the intervention group, receiving usual antenatal care in addition to the couple-based physical activity intervention. The trial was prospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500107402; August 11, 2025). Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the Guangdong Women and Children Hospital (approval no. 20250066) and the School of Nursing at Sun Yat-sen University (approval no. L2025SYSU-HL-006). The study protocol was developed in accordance with the SPIRIT 2025 statement [ ] and the CONSORT 2025 statement [ ]. ## Theoretical framework
The intervention is guided by the Cognitive-Transactional Model (CTM), as illustrated in Fig. . The CTM, proposed by Badr and Acitelli [ ], emphasizes dyadic coping in the context of chronic illness. According to the model, pregnant individuals and their partners form an interdependent dyad in which each person’s cognitions, emotions, and behaviors mutually influence the other [ , ].
 Within this framework, dyadic processes are initiated when couples develop shared illness representations and assign ownership of GDM prevention. Illness representations include cognitive beliefs about GDM (e.g., its causes, risk factors, consequences, and the preventive role of physical activity) as well as emotional responses to GDM (e.g., fear or worry). When increasing maternal physical activity to prevent GDM is appraised as a shared responsibility (“our problem”), couples engage in shared appraisal, jointly evaluating feasible actions, potential barriers, and available resources. In contrast, when prevention is perceived as an individual responsibility (“my problem”), appraisal and coping tend to remain at the individual level [ ]. Inadequate physical activity knowledge and fear of physical activity among both partners represent key modifiable barriers within the shared appraisal process [ ]. Shared appraisal provides the foundation for dyadic coping, defined as the ways in which couples interact when managing illness-related stressors [ , ]. Dyadic coping can be problem-focused or emotion-focused and take on positive (delegated, supportive, or common) and negative (hostile, ambivalent, or superficial) forms. The CTM further posits that effective dyadic coping strengthens both self-efficacy and dyadic efficacy (i.e., the confidence in the couple’s ability to work as a team), which in turn facilitates sustained behavior change and improved health outcomes [ ].
Guided by this model, the present intervention targets shared responsibility for GDM prevention, supports shared appraisal of physical activity–related barriers and resource, enhances dyadic coping, and strengthens self- and dyadic efficacy to promote maternal physical activity. Dyadic behavior change techniques (DBCTs) are integrated throughout the intervention to translate these theoretical mechanisms into actionable intervention components [ , ].

## Study setting
The randomized controlled trial will be conducted at a tertiary obstetrics and gynecology hospital in Guangzhou, China. Guangzhou is a sub-provincial city with a population of approximately 19 million and ranks among the top cities in China by GDP. The study hospital manages over 9,600 births each year, ensuring a sufficient pool of eligible participants. The hospital also operates a maternity school that can accommodate up to 50 attendees, providing an appropriate venue for delivering the intervention sessions.

## Participants
Eligible participants include pregnant individuals at high risk for GDM and their partners. Inclusion criteria for pregnant individuals are: (1) aged ≥ 18 years; (2) singleton pregnancy; (3) gestational age between 5 and 12+ 6 weeks; (4) living with a partner; (5) willingness to provide informed consent; and (6) having at least one recognized risk factor for GDM [ , ], including (a) age ≥ 35 years; (b) pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) ≥ 24 kg/m2; (c) polycystic ovary syndrome; (d) family history of diabetes; (e) history of unexplained stillbirth, miscarriage, or neonatal death; (f) history of delivering a large baby (birth weight ≥ 4000 g); (g) previous fetal anomaly; (h) history of GDM, abnormal glucose tolerance, or polyhydramnios; (i) current pregnancy with fetus measuring larger than gestational age, polyhydramnios, or recurrent vulvovaginal candidiasis; or (j) conception via assisted reproductive technology.
Exclusion criteria for pregnant individuals include: (1) preexisting diabetes; (2) contraindications to physical activity, including severe cardiac or respiratory disease, severe preeclampsia/eclampsia, uncontrolled hypertension, threatened miscarriage, persistent vaginal bleeding, premature rupture of membranes, cervical incompetence, severe anemia, placenta previa, uncontrolled thyroid disease, or fetal growth restriction; (3) physical disabilities or trauma limiting physical activity; (4) psychiatric illness or cognitive impairment that affects communication or adherence; (5) current use of medications such as metformin or corticosteroids; or (6) participation in other physical activity-related intervention programs.
Inclusion criteria for partners are: (1) without uncontrolled cardiovascular, pulmonary, or metabolic disease; (2) without physical disabilities or trauma affecting the ability to engage in physical activity; (3) without psychiatric illness or cognitive impairment preventing effective communication; and (4) willingness to provide informed consent.

## Sample size
The sample size estimation is based on the primary outcome, the incidence rate of GDM at 24–27+ 6 gestational weeks. Based on a previous study [ ], we assumed an incidence of 22% in the intervention group and 40.6% in the control group. To achieve 80% power to detect a between-group difference in GDM incidence at a significance level of 0.05, a total of 196 couples (98 per group) are required. Allowing for a potential 20% dropout rate, the total target sample size is 224 couples, with 112 couples allocated to each group.

## Recruitment
Pregnant individuals at high risk for GDM and their partners will be recruited by research nurse in a dedicated obstetrics clinic room at the study hospital. In this clinic, pregnant individuals undergo comprehensive assessment of medical, surgical, family, and obstetric history, as well as risk stratification. Based on these assessments, physicians issue relevant tests, including routine early-pregnancy genetic screening for thalassemia, which requires the participation of both partners. As most pregnant individuals attend these appointments with their partners, this setting provides favorable conditions for intervention recruitment.
Eligible pregnant individuals will be screened using a questionnaire, and the eligibility of their partners will be assessed subsequently. In accordance with hospital policy, partners are required to wait outside the consultation area during routine visits. Therefore, researchers will first provide eligible pregnant individuals with detailed information about the study objectives and intervention procedures. If the pregnant individual provides written informed consent, the same information will subsequently be provided to her partner, who will then undergo eligibility assessment. For partners who are unable to attend in person, study information will be delivered and eligibility assessed via telephone or WeChat. Reasons for non-participation will be recorded. Written informed consent will be obtained from both members of the couple prior to enrollment. Baseline questionnaires will be completed separately by pregnant individuals and their partners to minimize mutual influence.

## Randomization, concealment, and blinding
Randomization and group assignment will be conducted at the couple (dyad) level by an independent research assistant (RA) who is not involved in participant recruitment, intervention delivery, or outcome assessment. The RA will generate the allocation sequence using a computerized randomization tool (http://www.randomization. com). To ensure a 1:1 allocation ratio while minimizing predictability, block randomization with randomly varying block sizes (e.g., 4 and 6 couples) will be used. Allocation concealment will be ensured using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed, and tamper-evident envelopes prepared by the independent RA. After eligibility confirmation and completion of baseline data collection, envelopes will be opened sequentially by the RA according to the order of enrollment, and participants will be informed of their group assignment. Intervention providers will subsequently receive the names and contact information of participants allocated to the intervention group.
Due to the nature of the behavioral intervention, blinding of participants and intervention providers is not feasible. However, outcome assessors and data analysts will remain blinded to group allocation. Group labels will be coded (e.g., Group A/Group B), and unblinding will occur only after completion of the primary statistical analyses to minimize the risk of bias.

## Control
Couples in the control group will receive usual antenatal care throughout the study period. Usual antenatal care includes establishment of an electronic health record, routine prenatal visits (every four weeks until 28 gestational weeks, every two weeks from 28 to 36 weeks, and weekly thereafter), and standard health education sessions. Both pregnant individuals and their partners are encouraged to attend these sessions, which are available in person or online. Educational content typically addresses nutrition, preparation for delivery, breastfeeding, and newborn care. At the first antenatal visit, couples will also receive an informational brochure outlining basic recommendations for physical activity during pregnancy. In addition, participants in the control group will be invited to join a WeChat group used for administrative communication and general peer interaction. The group allows participants to communicate with one another; however, no structured physical activity guidance, counseling, or behavior change support will be provided through this group.

## Intervention
Participants in the intervention group will receive a couple-based physical activity intervention in addition to usual antenatal care. The intervention begins between 5 and 12+ 6 weeks of gestation and continues until 24 weeks. As presented in Table  , it consists of one face-to-face group dyadic education session, one online group dyadic education session, biweekly personalized dyadic counseling sessions, and a daily behavior change booster. All intervention components are delivered to both pregnant individuals at high risk for GDM and their partners and follow a fixed sequence. The intervention is implemented by a registered nurse with international certification in physical activity for pregnant individuals. Intervention delivery is supported by WeChat. An overview of the WeChat functions used in the intervention is presented in Table  and Figure S1. A dedicated WeChat Official Account, “Pregnancy Support Station” (孕期加油站), was developed by the research team. It consist of three function model, “Self-monitoring Center”, “Education Center”, and “Assessment Center”.
 

## Face-to-face group dyadic education session
This session will be delivered to groups of 8–12 couples at the maternity school of the obstetrics outpatient clinic. To maximize attendance, sessions will be scheduled on weekends or public holidays. A reminder message will be sent through WeChat one day prior.

## Shared responsibility “our problem”
This component aims to help couples recognize that increasing physical activity to prevent GDM is a shared responsibility. The research nurse will first explain the adverse maternal and neonatal consequences of GDM and its associated risk factors to establish a shared understanding of the importance of prevention. She will then introduce the preventive effects of physical activity on GDM and briefly explain the underlying physiological mechanisms.
Subsequently, the research nurse will facilitate communication between the pregnant individual and her partner regarding their views on shared responsibility for increasing physical activity to prevent GDM. Both the pregnant individual and her partner will be provided with a brief worksheet containing a single-choice question: “Who do you think is responsible for increasing physical activity to prevent GDM?” Response options include: (a) the pregnant individual, (b) the partner, or (c) both of us together. Each partner will be asked to complete the item independently and to briefly explain the rationale for their choice in their own words.
The research nurse will then review the responses and accompanying explanations to explore each partner’s perceptions of shared responsibility and to identify potential discrepancies between partners. If GDM prevention is perceived as solely the pregnant individual’s responsibility by either partner, the nurse will provide targeted education to reframe this perception and emphasize the importance of collaborative involvement.

## Shared appraisals

## Shared appraisals of physical activity knowledge and related fears
To facilitate shared appraisals, the research nurse will invite couples to openly discuss two guiding questions: (1) What do you know about physical activity during pregnancy? and (2) What fears or concerns do you have regarding physical activity during pregnancy? Couples’ responses will be documented and used to inform subsequent education.

## Dyadic physical activity knowledge and skills education
This component aims to support the development of a shared and accurate understanding of physical activity during pregnancy and to alleviate couples’ concerns and fears related to physical activity. Education on physical activity will be provided according to current guidelines [ , ], covering recommended frequency, intensity, type, duration, contraindications, safety precautions, and methods for assessing activity intensity. Pregnant individuals are recommended to engage in 30–60 min of moderate-intensity activity per day, five or more times per week, to achieve optimal preventive effects for GDM [ , ]. Given that walking is the most commonly preferred form of physical activity among Chinese pregnant individuals [ ], couples will receive instruction on proper posture and pacing for moderate-intensity brisk walking. This instruction will be supported by three sets of audio walking guides with different cadences, allowing couples to select the cadence most appropriate to the pregnant individual’s activity level.
Based on couples’ responses elicited during the shared appraisal phase, the research nurse will provide tailored explanations to correct misconceptions, address safety concerns, and reduce fears related to physical activity during pregnancy. In addition, the research nurse will guide couples through an on-site 10-minute moderate-intensity physical activity practice using a standardized exercise video developed by a professional sports coach specifically for pregnant individuals [ ].

## Dyadic goal setting and action planning
Based on a shared appraisal of the pregnant individual’s physical condition, feasible actions, potential barriers, and available personal and environmental resources, couples are empowered to collaboratively engage in goal setting and action planning to increase maternal physical activity. Couples will be instructed to set individualized weekly physical activity goals for the pregnant individuals based on the FITT principle (frequency, intensity, time, and type), taking into account pre-pregnancy activity habits, physical capacity, and current activity level. As shown in Figure S2, couples will then be guided to identify mutually enjoyable activities and collaborative strategies to achieve their goals and to document them on a structured worksheet. Building on this process, couples will develop a detailed action plan specifying when, where, and how each goal will be implemented, as well as identify potential barriers and corresponding coping strategies using available personal and environmental resources (an example is shown in Table S1). Partners will be encouraged to support goal attainment by modeling active behaviors, fostering a supportive interpersonal environment, and using strategies such as encouragement, supervision, reminders, and rewards.
Safety is prioritized throughout the intervention. Couples will be guided to increase maternal physical activity gradually, particularly among pregnant individuals who were previously inactive or are overweight or obese. Couples may choose activity types provided within the intervention, including brisk walking and standardized prenatal aerobic exercises, or select other preferred options such as yoga, swimming, dancing, or stationary cycling. All selected activities will be reviewed by the research nurse to ensure safety and appropriateness. To further facilitate integration of physical activity into daily routines, couples will also be encouraged to incorporate “zero-time” exercises, such as light movements or stretching while sitting or standing. If contraindications arise or physical activity suitability is uncertain, couples will be advised to consult the research nurse or antenatal care providers.
Self-monitoring is an effective behaviour change technique for improving physical activity during pregnancy [ , ]. Accordingly, after goals and action plans are established, pregnant individuals will be guided to record the frequency, intensity, type, and duration of daily physical activity, as well as related experiences and partner support, in the “Self-Monitoring Center.” Partners will also be encouraged to document their own physical activity and supportive behaviors. Shared records within the intervention group will be visible to participants, facilitating social comparison and peer engagement.

## Online group dyadic education session
This session will be delivered to groups of 8–12 couples via Tencent Meeting mini program one week after the face-to-face session. A reminder message will be sent via WeChat one day before the session.

## Dyadic coping strategies
Dyadic coping strategies are introduced as tools to enhance couples’ capacity to jointly manage physical activity–related stressors during pregnancy and to address barriers within the behavior change process, drawing on established skills outlined by Epstein and Baucom (2002).
Couples will be guided to practice stress communication skills, including expressing stressors, emotions, and support needs, as well as recognizing verbal, behavioral, and emotional indicators of stress in their partner. Emphasis will be placed on empathic listening, the use of open-ended questions, and validation of partners’ emotional experiences.In addition, couples will learn supportive coping strategies, such as providing emotional reassurance, practical assistance, and problem-solving support in ways that align with the stressed partner’s preferences. The importance of seeking feedback and avoiding negative coping patterns (e.g., criticism, ambivalence, or superficial support) will be highlighted.Finally, couples will be introduced to common dyadic coping approaches that involve addressing stressors together as a team through joint appraisal, shared problem solving, adaptive reframing, and engagement in mutually enjoyable activities that promote positive affect and relationship connection. Brief, pregnancy-specific examples related to common barriers to physical activity (e.g., fear, fatigue, and time constraints) will be used to illustrate skill application.

## Role-model experience sharing
This component involves sharing successful experiences to reinforce self-efficacy and dyadic efficacy. Prior to the session, the research nurse will review pregnant individuals’ self-monitoring records in the “Self-Monitoring Center.” Two to three pregnant individuals who have achieved their physical activity goals, or partners who have demonstrated effective collaborative support, will be invited to share their experiences. Presenters will receive guidelines to structure their sharing (see Table S2). At the start of each presentation, the research nurse will introduce key participant characteristics, including GDM-related risk factors. Following each presentation, the nurse will provide positive feedback, highlighting effective strategies and strengths that can serve as examples for all participating couples.

## Online personalized dyadic counseling session
These one-to-one WeChat sessions aim to provide individualized support. The research nurse will tailor the duration and content of each session according to the couple’s specific barriers and needs. The first two sessions focus on helping pregnant individuals adapt to increased activity levels and strengthening partner support behaviors. Subsequent sessions address emerging barriers, review progress, adjust weekly goals and plans, and encourage consistent use of self-monitoring and positive dyadic coping.

## Behavior change booster
To reinforce adherence, daily reminders will be sent through the WeChat group encouraging physical activity, self-monitoring, and partner involvement. Partners will be prompted to supervise, remind, and support the pregnant individual in following the plan, fostering sustained behavior change.

## Educational materials
All session materials and educational resources are delivered through the “Education Center” within the “Pregnancy Support Station” (Table  ) and delivered to each couple via WeChat at predetermined time points. Materials related to group dyadic sessions are sent immediately after each session, whereas trimester-specific health information is delivered in alignment with participants’ gestational stages. Delivery schedules are standardized across participants to ensure intervention fidelity.

## Strategies to reduce intervention contamination
Contamination may occur if couples in the control group are exposed to intervention materials or receive information from participants in the intervention group. The risk of contamination is expected to be minimal because the couple-based behavior change components used in this trial, such as dyadic coping, collaborative goal setting, and action planning, are complex and not easily replicated or communicated informally. To further minimize contamination, all intervention materials are delivered exclusively through the “Pregnancy Support Station” and closed WeChat groups managed by the research team, which are accessible only to participants in the intervention arm. Participants will also receive clear written and verbal instructions emphasizing that intervention content should not be shared with individuals outside their assigned group. Nevertheless, it is not technically feasible to fully prevent participants from forwarding intervention materials, and some degree of contamination cannot be entirely ruled out. Therefore, at the end of follow-up, couples in the control group will be asked whether they have been exposed to any intervention-related content. If meaningful contamination is identified, additional sensitivity analyses will be conducted alongside the primary intention-to-treat analysis. These will include: (1) a contamination-adjusted analysis excluding control participants who report exposure; and (2) an as-treated analysis comparing outcomes by actual exposure status, regardless of allocation. Participants who report any exposure to the intervention content will be analyzed as the ‘exposed’ group, and those without exposure will be analyzed as the ‘non-exposed’ group.

## Outcome measurement and data collection
Primary outcomes are the incidence of GDM, average blood glucose, and physical activity levels. Secondary outcomes include gestational weight gain, depressive symptoms, mediating variables (physical activity knowledge, fear of physical activity, dyadic coping, and physical activity self-efficacy), and maternal, paternal, and neonatal health outcomes. Data will be collected at 5–12+ 6, 24–27+ 6, and 35–36+ 6 weeks of gestation, and within 3 days after delivery.

## Primary outcomes

## Incidence of GDM
The 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) will be conducted between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation as part of routine prenatal care, in accordance with the criteria established by the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) [ ]. Pregnant individual will be instructed to maintain their usual level of physical activity and consume a diet containing at least 150 g of carbohydrates per day for three consecutive days before the test. They will fast for at least 8 h after their evening meal on the day prior to testing and be advised to obtain adequate sleep to minimize external factors that may affect glucose metabolism. On the morning of the test, participants will ingest 300 mL of a 75-g glucose solution. Venous blood samples will be collected at fasting, and at 1 h and 2 h after ingestion. According to the IADPSG criteria, GDM will be diagnosed if any of the following plasma glucose thresholds are met or exceeded: fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, 1-hour plasma glucose ≥ 10.0 mmol/L, or 2-hour plasma glucose ≥ 8.5 mmol/L.

## Average blood glucose levels
The fasting, 1-hour postprandial, and 2-hour postprandial plasma glucose levels measured during the 75-g OGTT.

## Physical activity levels
Pregnant individuals’ physical activity levels will be assessed using the Chinese version of the Pregnancy Physical Activity Questionnaire (PPAQ-C) [ ]. The PPAQ-C comprises 31 items across four domains: household/caregiving activities (14 items), occupational activities (5 items), transportation activities (4 items), and sports/exercise activities (8 items) [ ]. Each activity is classified by intensity as sedentary (< 1.5 METs), light (1.5–2.9 METs), moderate (3.0–6.0 METs), or vigorous (> 6.0 METs). The PPAQ-C has demonstrated high reliability and validity, with a content validity index of 0.940 and a test-retest intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.944 [ ].
Partners’ physical activity levels will be measured using the Chinese version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ-C), which evaluates the frequency and duration of physical activity over the previous week across four domains: occupational, transportation, household, and exercise [ ]. Activities are further categorized into walking, moderate-intensity, and vigorous-intensity, with corresponding energy expenditures of 3.3, 4.0, and 8.0 METs per minute. Total weekly physical activity volume will be calculated by summing energy expenditure across these three intensity levels. The IPAQ-C exhibits strong psychometric properties, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.82 and test-retest ICCs ranging from 0.77 to 0.96 [ ].

## Secondary outcomes

## Gestational weight gain
Body weight data will be extracted from the maternity clinic’s electronic medical record system. At each prenatal visit, pregnant individuals’ height (cm) and weight (kg) will be measured using the hospital’s electronic scale while wearing light indoor clothing and no shoes. These measurements are automatically synchronized to the electronic medical record system. Gestational weight gain (GWG) will be calculated as the difference between baseline weight (gestational week 5–12+ 6 ) and the follow-up measurement at 24–27+ 6 and 35–36+ 6weeks. The weekly rate of weight gain will be calculated by dividing the total weight gain by the number of weeks between time points. Participants will also be categorized as having excessive, inadequate, or adequate GWG according to the Institute of Medicine guidelines [ ].

## Depressive symptoms
Depressive symptoms will be assessed for both pregnant individuals and their partners using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) (Cox et al., 1987). The EPDS comprises 10 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 3, yielding a total score between 0 and 30. Higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms. The scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity among Chinese pregnant women and their partners [ ].

## Potential mediators

## Knowledge of physical activity
Knowledge of physical activity will be assessed for both pregnant individuals and their partners using the Pregnancy Physical Activity Knowledge Scale (P-PAKS) [ ]. The P-PAKS consists of 20 items, each with three possible responses: “yes,” “no,” or “don’t know.” Correct responses are scored as 1, while incorrect or “don’t know” responses are scored as 0. Higher total scores reflect greater knowledge of physical activity during pregnancy. The scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity among Chinese pregnant individuals [ ].

## Fear of physical activity
Fear of physical activity during pregnancy will be assessed using the Pregnancy Physical Activity Fear Scale (P-PAFS) developed by the research team (in press). The scale includes two versions, one for pregnant individuals and one for their partners. It consists of four dimensions: risk perception (7 items), catastrophic thinking (6 items), avoidance coping (4 items), and positive coping (6 items), with a total of 23 items. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 4 (“strongly agree”). Items 3, 7, 10, 14, 18, and 23 are reverse-scored. The total score is calculated as the sum of the four dimensions, ranging from 18 to 72, with higher scores indicating a higher level of fear of physical activity during pregnancy.

## Dyadic coping
Dyadic coping will be assessed for both pregnant individuals and their partners using the Chinese version of the Dyadic Coping Inventory (DCI-C) [ ]. The DCI-C consists of 31 items grouped into two domains: positive dyadic coping (23 items) and negative dyadic coping (8 items). Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 to 5, with higher scores within each domain indicating greater levels of positive or negative dyadic coping when facing shared stress. The DCI-C has demonstrated good reliability and validity among Chinese pregnant couples [ ].

## Physical activity self-efficacy
Physical activity self-efficacy will be assessed only for pregnant individuals using the Pregnancy Physical Activity Self-Efficacy Scale (P-PASES), which includes 10 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1–5). Total scores range from 10 to 50, with higher scores indicating greater self-efficacy for engaging in physical activity during pregnancy. The Chinese version of the P-PASES has demonstrated good reliability and validity among Chinese pregnant individuals [ ].

## Maternal, paternal, and neonatal health outcomes
Maternal and neonatal health outcomes will be extracted from the maternity clinic’s electronic medical record system within 3 days after delivery. Maternal health outcomes include hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (gestational hypertension and preeclampsia), mode of delivery (elective or emergency caesarean section, vaginal delivery, or operative vaginal delivery), blood loss at delivery (> 1000 mL or > 2000 mL), referral to a diabetic antenatal service following the oral glucose tolerance test, and the requirement for insulin or metformin during pregnancy.
Neonatal health outcomes will include gestational age at delivery, preterm birth (delivery before 37 weeks), birth weight, macrosomia (≥ 4.0 kg), low birth weight (≤ 2.5 kg), birth length, neonatal hypoglycemia (blood glucose < 2.2 mmol/L [40 mg/dL] within 24 h of birth), hyperbilirubinemia, respiratory distress, shoulder dystocia, admission to the neonatal intensive care unit, and perinatal mortality.
Paternal health outcomes include body mass index (BMI) measured at baseline, 24–27+ 6, and 35–36+ 6 weeks of gestation.

## Process evaluation of the intervention
The process evaluation will follow the UK Medical Research Council (MRC) framework for evaluating complex interventions, aiming to provide insights into how and why the intervention produces its effects and to inform potential refinements for future implementation [ ]. A mixed-methods process evaluation will be conducted from three aspects: (1) implementation, (2) mechanism, and (3) contextual factors. Table provides an overview of the dimensions, definitions, indicators, and data sources used in process evaluation.
 Quantitative data will be collected from program records and documents, WeChat records, self-monitoring records in the “Self-Monitoring Center,” satisfaction questionnaires, outcome data, and sociodemographic and obstetric information. Qualitative data will be obtained through individual semi-structured interviews with a purposive subsample of participating couples to explore their perceptions of the intervention’s feasibility, acceptability, and perceived benefits or challenges.

## Data management
All study data will be managed according institutional data protection policies and ethical guidelines to ensure confidentiality and integrity. Participant information and study data will be collected, stored, and managed using a secure electronic database accessible only to authorized research personnel. Each participant will be assigned a unique study identification number to anonymize data and prevent identification in analyses and publications. Electronic data will be regularly backed up to prevent loss, and access will be password-protected. Paper-based records, including informed consent forms and hard-copy questionnaires, will be stored in locked cabinets. Data quality will be ensured through double-entry verification, routine consistency checks, and periodic audits. Any discrepancies identified will be resolved by referring to the original source documents. Participant files will be retained in storage for 5 years after the study’s completion.

## Statistical methods
The primary analysis will follow the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, including all randomized participants according to their original group allocation, regardless of intervention adherence. Per-protocol analyses will be conducted as sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the findings. All statistical analyses will be performed using R (version 4.1.0 or later), with a two-sided significance level of 0.05.
Descriptive statistics will be used to summarize participant characteristics. Continuous variables will be presented as means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges, as appropriate, while categorical variables will be reported as frequencies and percentages. Baseline characteristics will be compared descriptively between groups to assess balance achieved by randomization. The effect of the intervention on GDM incidence will be evaluated using logistic regression, with treatment group as the main independent variable. Models will be adjusted for prespecified baseline covariates to improve precision, and results will be reported as adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. OGTT fasting, 1-hour, and 2-hour plasma glucose levels will be analyzed as continuous outcomes using linear mixed-effects models, treating OGTT timepoint as a within-subject factor and including a participant-level random intercept to account for within-person correlation. Physical activity levels measured repeatedly in both members of the couple will be analyzed using dyadic linear mixed-effects models. Fixed effects will include treatment group, time, and partner role (pregnant participant vs. partner), with random intercepts specified for couples and for individuals nested within couples, to account for repeated measurements within individuals and interdependence within couples. Missing data patterns will be examined descriptively. Likelihood-based mixed-effects models will be used for longitudinal outcomes, which accommodate incomplete data under a missing-at-random assumption. Multiple imputation will be performed as a sensitivity analysis if the extent of missingness is substantial. Secondary outcomes will be analyzed using methods appropriate to the outcome type and are considered exploratory.
A mixed-methods approach will be used to evaluate implementation fidelity and other implementation outcomes in the intervention group. Quantitative data will be summarized using descriptive statistics. Semi-structured interviews will be transcribed and analyzed thematically using an inductive–deductive approach informed by the intervention framework and emergent participant experiences. Coding will be conducted iteratively by at least two independent researchers to ensure inter-rater reliability, with discrepancies resolved through discussion and consensus.

## Discussion
Pregnancy is a critical window of opportunity for the prevention and reduction of chronic disease burden. For pregnant individuals at high risk for GDM, initiating preventive strategies in early pregnancy can improve perinatal outcomes and reduce long-term metabolic risks for both mothers and their offspring. Physical activity is a modifiable behavior with potential to reduce GDM risk. However, behavior change rarely occurs in isolation; it is embedded within daily social interactions and shaped by close relationships. We developed a couple-based physical activity intervention grounded in the Cognitive-Transactional Model. This intervention views pregnant individuals and their partners as an interdependent dyad and empowers couples to mobilize their own interpersonal resources to improve maternal physical activity collaboratively. If this new intervention proves effective in reducing GDM incidence, it may inform future research, policy development, and clinical practice.
Collaborating as a couple to improve maternal physical activity for GDM prevention offers several important advantages. For pregnant individuals, the intervention shifts the burden of behavior change away from sole reliance on personal willpower by actively engaging partners in supportive roles. This couple-based approach helps pregnant individuals better cope with common barriers and sustain behavior change [ ]. From a healthcare system perspective, the intervention leverages couples’ existing interpersonal resources and relational strengths rather than relying heavily on intensive professional supervision or healthcare inputs, enhancing cost-effectiveness compared with individual-oriented approaches [ ]. Third, although the primary focus is on maternal physical activity, partners may also benefit, as evidence suggests high concordance of health behavior change within couples, whereby positive lifestyle changes in one partner increase the likelihood of similar changes in the other [ ].
The Cognitive-Transactional Model (CTM) provides a robust theoretical foundation for identifying key mechanisms of action, guiding intervention design, and explaining why the present intervention is expected to be effective. However, evidence from meta-analyses and systematic reviews indicates that merely stating a theoretical basis does not necessarily lead to improved intervention outcomes [ ]. Rather, stronger effects are observed when intervention components are explicitly linked to theoretical mechanisms of action [ ]. Accordingly, the present intervention applies dyadic behavior change techniques to translate the CTM’s core elements into concrete, actionable strategies.
The intervention empowers couples to consider their circumstances and preferences, jointly set activity goals, develop action plans, and identify strategies to overcome barriers. These tailored and interactive components may facilitate the integration of physical activity into daily and family routines and may be more attractive. In addition, this study is the first to identify fear of physical activity as a key barrier and to include targeted strategies to address this barrier.
Several limitations need to be acknowledged. First, we did not restrict enrollment to couples with high relationship satisfaction, as those with lower satisfaction may also benefit from this study. However, this may result that couples with poorer relationship satisfaction may have greater difficulty adhering to the intervention, and disparities in partner motivation may affect engagement. Second, the assessment of physical activity relies on self-report and is therefore susceptible to recall bias and social desirability effects. Third, participants are recruited from a single tertiary hospital, which may limit the applicability of the findings to other populations or healthcare settings. Finally, as with most behavioral interventions, some degree of contamination between groups is possible, as participants may access physical activity information from other sources outside the study.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, this study describes a couple-based physical activity intervention for the prevention of GDM. Guided by the Cognitive-Transactional Model, pregnant individuals and their partners are a dyad as an interdependent unit. The present intervention will frame maternal health as a shared responsibility within couples and position maternal physical activity behavior change as a collaborative dyadic process rather than an individual effort. Dyadic behavior change techniques and dyadic coping strategies are incorporated to strengthen couples’ capacity to collaborate and solve barriers together. The findings from the forthcoming RCT have the potential to inform the optimization of GDM prevention strategies for pregnant individuals at high-risk for GDM and support the integration of partner involvement into routine antenatal care.
