## Abstract
In this article, we present the state‐of‐the‐art on socioeconomic health inequalities with a focus on the Nordic countries. Health inequalities have increased over time and can be observed for both mortality and morbidity. We show that cross‐national comparisons reveal surprisingly high inequalities in the Nordic countries. It is now well established that health and mortality inequalities prevail also at older ages. We show, with data from Sweden, that the interpretation of how mortality inequalities evolve over the life course is markedly different depending on whether we focus on absolute or relative inequalities. Although there is a consensus on basic descriptive facts, disagreements on how these facts are best explained and why they persist and even increase remain. We present a general discussion on how to explain health inequalities, as well as a discussion on why patterns may differ between European regions and across the life course. We introduce a framework for understanding health inequalities, fundamental cause theory and discuss to what extent the evidence aligns with the theory. We review contemporary discussions on health inequalities in light of recent evidence based on novel methods for causal inference. We argue that health inequalities have important ramifications for population health regardless of whether they are primarily shaped by social causation or social selection and end by noting that as modern societies aspire to become more meritocratic, it is possible that socioeconomic position becomes increasingly important for health.

## Health and mortality inequality at older ages
Most health problems and deaths occur at older ages. Despite this fact, it is worth noting how few health inequality studies have focused on, or even included, older adults. Quite many studies look specifically at child mortality or mortality during working ages, and even today, many studies still use an upper age limit at age 65, 75 or 85. Given the ageing of our populations, such an age ceiling becomes more and more problematic over time. Theoretically, there are certain rationales behind the focus on younger age groups, often summarized under the label ‘age‐as‐a‐leveller’, which hypothesize that health inequalities will decrease rapidly at older ages. There are at least four different processes that could lead to such an outcome: the increasing importance of biological processes for health, the levelling impact of social protection schemes for pensioners, the lower impact of work‐related social stratification and selective mortality removing those with the worst health from the population [ ]. However, a competing framework—common in sociology and social gerontology—instead suggests that advantages and disadvantages tend to accumulate over the life course and lead to increasing diversity with age (see, e.g., [ ]). Although these two perspectives often are presented as contrasting, Fritzell and Rehnberg [ ] have shown—both by a mapping review of the literature and a novel empirical analysis—that the empirical support for each perspective is dependent on how inequality is measured and interpreted. Although the results from studies based on relative inequalities tend to support the age‐as‐a‐leveller hypothesis, results based on absolute measurements most often support the cumulative advantage/disadvantage hypothesis. We will return to this issue later in the article.
However, regardless of how it is measured, we now have firm evidence that substantial health inequalities prevail also beyond working ages. Moreover, trend studies on inequalities in old‐age mortality in the Nordic countries largely reflect the findings as reported above. Remaining life expectancy at both 65 and 80 has increased in all Nordic countries, but at a more rapid pace among those with high socioeconomic positions compared to those with lower socioeconomic positions, leading to an increasing mortality gap [ , ]. The results from a study that synthesized the results from several surveys of the oldest old in the Nordic countries using meta‐analyses showed that educational inequalities in several health measures could be observed even in the group aged 95+ [ ].

## Inequalities beyond mortality
Although the most robust evidence on health inequalities is based on mortality data rather than for other outcomes, we can safely say that most types of ill‐health follow a social gradient, and, again, this is also true for older adults [ ]. Fig.  shows the sex‐ and age‐standardized prevalence of multimorbidity among older adults (aged 50 and older) by income tertiles, across European regions [ ]. The data come from the Study of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE) and compile data on self‐reported chronic conditions and household income. Multimorbidity is defined as reporting two or more chronic conditions.
 The results largely reflect the patterns for mortality described above. The social gradient in multimorbidity can be observed in all regions. In Northern Europe, the prevalence is relatively low in all income groups, but the gradient is sharp, indicating substantial inequalities. In Southern Europe, the prevalence is higher for all groups, except among those with the lowest income, yielding lower levels of inequalities. The pattern for Central Europe is characterized by both relatively high prevalence and substantial inequalities. Central‐Eastern Europe, finally, exhibits relatively high prevalences of multimorbidity in all income groups, but relatively low levels of inequalities.
As a kind of mixture indicator, including both mortality and morbidity, indicators of healthy or disability‐free, life expectancy aiming at disentangling healthy from unhealthy years of life are sometimes assessed. Such calculations almost always find even stronger socioeconomic inequalities in healthy life expectancy than for regular life expectancy. For example, a recent UK report states that the differences in life expectancy between the most wealthy and deprived areas were about 8 years for women and close to 10 years for men, whereas the corresponding differences for healthy life expectancy were above 18 years for both sexes [ ]. Different studies use different measures and methods to define healthy or disability‐free life expectancies, yielding different results, but by and large, most evidence suggests that inequalities in healthy life expectancy are around twice the magnitude of inequalities in life expectancy as such (e.g., [ , , ]).

## Absolute and relative inequalities in health
As noted earlier, focusing on absolute versus relative inequalities in health can lead to substantially different conclusions. This raises the critical question of whether researchers and policymakers should prioritize absolute or relative inequalities. Historically, emphasis has often been placed on relative inequalities; however, arguments have been made in favour of reorienting the focus towards absolute inequalities. Several compelling arguments support this shift. To illustrate these points, we present data on income‐related inequalities in mortality among Swedish adults between 1995 and 2017 (Fig.  ). Income is measured by the average of household equivalent disposable income over the 5 years preceding the 2‐year mortality follow‐up (1995–1996, etc.).
 The upper part of the figure shows the patterns for adults aged 30–64. In 1995–1996, age‐standardized mortality among the 30% with the lowest incomes was 538 deaths per 100,000 persons, compared to 222 deaths per 100,000 among the 30% with the highest incomes. In relative terms, mortality was 2.4 times higher in the low‐income group. In absolute terms, there were 316 more deaths per 100,000 persons in the low‐income group than in the high‐income group.
By 2016–2017, mortality rates had declined significantly for both groups, reaching 387 deaths per 100,000 persons for the low‐income group and 97 deaths per 100,000 for the high‐income group. In absolute terms, excess mortality had slightly decreased to 290 deaths per 100,000 persons. However, in relative terms, mortality was now nearly four times higher in the low‐income group than in the high‐income group.
This discrepancy arises because relative inequalities depend heavily on the overall mortality rates. As mortality rates decrease, even small absolute differences can translate into substantial relative inequalities. Consequently, reducing relative mortality inequalities alongside overall mortality reductions is extremely challenging. In contrast, absolute inequalities are not rate‐dependent, making it easier to reduce them as overall mortality declines.
In the middle part of the figure, we show similar calculations, but now for older ages 65–79. In this case, both relative and absolute measures basically tell the same story. Mortality inequality by income has increased substantially both in relative and absolute terms.
In the bottom part of the figure, the calculations are once again repeated for those aged 80+. Again, the results are similar for both relative and absolute measures of inequalities. For both metrics, the inequalities increase marginally over the period, but overall, the inequalities are largely dwarfed by the high rates of mortality in both groups.
Thus, the choice of absolute or relative measures can dramatically influence interpretations of health inequalities, underscoring the importance of assessing the implications when choosing a measure. When the base rates of mortality are low, even small absolute differences between groups can generate substantial relative inequalities. Thus, paradoxically, levelling relative health inequalities may become increasingly difficult during periods of improving population health. It has been argued that trying to reduce relative inequalities in mortality as overall mortality declines is like ‘swimming against the current’, whereas reducing absolute inequalities is like ‘riding the waves’ [ ]. Although relative measures still hold a prominent position in research, it has become increasingly common to present inequality trends in both ways to get a fuller picture. From a policy perspective, a prime focus on absolute measures is in most cases preferable because they give primacy also to overall population health improvements. As the eminent epidemiologist Geoffrey Rose once noted ‘relative risk is only for researchers; decisions call for absolute measures’.

## Explaining health inequalities

## Do socioeconomic conditions cause health inequalities?
The causal nature of socioeconomic inequalities in health has been the subject of ongoing debates over recent decades. Fundamentally, these debates concern the causal architecture of the associations observed between socioeconomic position and health.
Perhaps the most foundational debate concerns whether socioeconomic conditions cause differences in health at all. This debate arises primarily from the fact that most research on health inequalities is based on observational data. Consequently, there is always a chance that the observed associations are, partly or wholly, confounded by factors not accounted for in the statistical models. A special case of confounding, sometimes called reversed causation or health selection, occurs when the prior health status affects both the socioeconomic position (e.g., the income) and a later health outcome of an individual—generating a confounded association.
Although this discussion has been going on for a long time, it has gained momentum with the renewed emphasis on methods for causal inference from observational data that have developed within epidemiology and the social sciences over recent years. Research based on such frameworks for causal inference has yielded ambiguous results concerning the causal effect of socioeconomic position on health. These studies—which typically attempt to emulate experimental conditions using observational data—tend to report weaker, or completely absent, effects of socioeconomic conditions on health compared to previous research [ ]. Some researchers interpret this as evidence that the observed health inequalities largely reflect ‘non‐causal’ effects of confounding factors such as prior health, cognitive abilities and personality traits [ ]. This has even been described as an ‘inconvenient truth’ for the field [ ]. Conversely, others have argued that many of the novel methods used to explicitly estimate causal effects from observational data are inherently ill‐suited for studies on the causal nature of population‐level health inequalities and highlight that, even using such methods, numerous studies now support the existence of causal effects of socioeconomic position on health [ ]. Recently, attempts have been made to develop a framework for causal inference from observational data in population health research to better fit the analysis of the complex, multicausal, long‐term effects that are believed to lead to health inequalities [ ].
Although the debate remains unresolved and is likely to continue for the foreseeable future, we will settle for now by interpreting the debate as a caution against making overly broad generalizations regarding the causal nature of health inequalities. Moreover, one can make strong arguments that health inequalities should be understood and taken seriously irrespective of the causality issue. From a policy perspective, there are good reasons to address health inequalities regardless of their causal nature. When certain groups in the population have higher disease burdens than others due to preventable causes, it serves as a bottleneck for population health improvements in general and for the vulnerable groups in particular. Thus, regardless of the causal nature of the health inequalities, addressing preventable health problems in socioeconomically disadvantaged groups may turn out to be an efficient way to both improve population health overall and to reduce health inequalities. Moreover, one can make arguments that if group differences in life expectancy and healthy life expectancy amplify further, it may have repercussions on many other societal issues. However, achieving a greater understanding of the causal mechanisms generating health inequalities remains an imperative task for both researchers and policymakers. Efficient policymaking is contingent on a correct understanding of the mechanisms at play.

## Potential mechanisms leading to health inequalities
Assuming socioeconomic conditions do causally affect health—at least under certain conditions—another central discussion concerns the mechanisms through which these effects occur. Historically, discussions have often focused on the relative importance of psychosocial, material and behavioural mechanisms [ ]. Psychosocial mechanisms emphasize socioeconomic differences in stressors that can cause health problems in the long term, such as financial stress, limited workplace autonomy or feelings of inferiority arising from social hierarchy. Material mechanisms, on the other hand, refer to socially stratified differences in tangible conditions such as income, physically strenuous working conditions and the safety of one's physical environment. Behavioural mechanisms, finally, focus on socioeconomic differences in health‐related behaviours such as smoking, diet and exercise. Although it is reasonable to debate the relative importance of these types of mechanisms, it is important to keep in mind that it is possible, and even likely, that these three types of mechanisms are interrelated, can coexist and mutually reinforce one another. For instance, financial strain may cause psychosocial stress, and smoking may serve as a coping mechanism for prolonged stress.
It has sometimes been argued that it may be unhelpful to think of socioeconomic inequalities in health as a homogeneous phenomenon, and that it can be better understood in the light of different socioeconomic conditions [ , ]. The three most common facets of socioeconomic conditions are education, social class (based on occupation) and income. These three facets partly line up along a causal chain: Education affects social class, which affects income, which in turn may affect health [ ]. Some of the mechanisms may also be shared across the different sources of socioeconomic variation, such as feelings of inferiority and status concerns. However, these socioeconomic conditions may also affect health independently, via distinct pathways. Education may affect health through improved cognitive abilities and better access to and more efficient processing of relevant information, but staying in education longer may also reduce the uptake of hazardous behaviours during formative life stages (e.g., smoking and binge drinking). Social class, in turn, is strongly associated with exposures to different types of working conditions but may also affect one's peer‐group affiliations during adulthood [ ]. Income, in turn, affects access to a host of material conditions that can have consequences for health, such as non‐subsidized health care, healthy foods and safe neighbourhoods.

## Why are health inequalities so large in the Nordic countries when compared to other European regions?
As delineated above, surprisingly large health inequalities have been observed in the Nordic countries (the Nordic paradox). Several hypotheses on the mechanisms underpinning these relatively large health inequalities have been proposed: First, the social policy programmes of the Nordic welfare states have become less generous over time, and income inequalities have increased continuously since the 1980s. Second, there is a strong social gradient in most health‐related behaviours, such as alcohol drinking and smoking, where unhealthy behaviours are more common among groups with lower socioeconomic positions [ , ]. Third, it has been argued that social mobility based on individual characteristics, including health, may have played an especially large role in the Nordic countries [ , ]. Research on social mobility has indicated that personal characteristics may play an increasingly important role for educational attainment and social class position as an adult [ ]. Such characteristics may also be of importance for health and thereby lead to socioeconomic inequalities in health. That is, the differences between socioeconomic groups in terms of average health and other factors linked to health may have increased over time due to selective social mobility across socioeconomic groups. This may be particularly true in the Nordic countries due to their relatively high degree of social mobility across generations, which could potentially lead to substantial socioeconomic sorting based on health‐related individual characteristics.
In contrast, the countries of Southern Europe are characterized by relatively low levels of health inequalities (the Southern miracle). There are likely to be many important historical, societal and behavioural factors behind these patterns. One such mechanism is related to the smoking epidemic. First, it occurred later in Southern Europe than in the rest of Europe. Second, the subsequent trend of decreasing prevalence of smoking, especially marked in socioeconomically privileged groups, which has been evident for many decades in Northern‐Western Europe, is, if at all existing, a more recent phenomenon in Southern Europe [ ]. For women, the proportion of daily smokers is still, according to the latest available data from Eurostat [ ], on par between high and lower educated groups in Southern European countries. In contrast, recent findings from Sweden [ ] indicate that the proportion of daily smokers is more than three times higher among those with only primary education (11%) compared to those with tertiary education (3.5%).
In addition, the population health of the Central‐Eastern European countries has been characterized by high levels of both overall disease burden and of health inequalities (the Eastern disaster). These patterns can, at least in part, be attributed to high levels and inequalities in poverty, alcohol consumption, smoking and unequal access to high‐quality medical care [ ].

## Understanding health inequalities in old age
Although the inequalities in health observed in old age are likely to be partly a carryover of health inequalities established earlier in life or from socially stratified living conditions earlier in the life course, there is now some evidence suggesting that there may be mechanisms that cause health inequalities specifically during old age. Some studies have shown evidence of social differences in the health care and long‐term care (LTC) received by older adults [ ]. However, most cross‐national studies on LTC focus on country variation, for example, in the prevalence of unmet needs, rather than within‐country inequalities [ ]. In terms of social and health inequalities, it has been suggested that a market‐oriented LTC system leads to higher inequalities, but these inequalities refer to the provider of care or alternatively to the use of formal LTC rather than any effect on the health of the care recipients [ ].
As an important life transition, the impact of retirement on health, and to some extent also socioeconomic health inequalities, has received quite a lot of attention in research. This is especially so because many countries are taking measures to extend working lives and increase the statutory pension age. In a Swedish study [ ], the health effects of prolonging working life above earlier statutory ages were studied from a social class perspective. Contrary to expectations that the extension of working ages would be more beneficial to higher social strata, the results found no substantive class differentials. Another line of research on the mechanisms behind health inequalities among older adults stems from the hypothesis that the socioeconomic resources of older adults’ children may contribute to shaping their health. There is some descriptive evidence suggesting that older adults who have children with higher socioeconomic positions tend to have better health, net of their own socioeconomic position, than their peers whose children have lower socioeconomic positions [ , , , ]. However, the causal nature of these patterns is still unknown.

## Fundamental cause theory
One theoretical framework that has gained considerable prominence over the recent decades is the fundamental cause theory (FCT), which is comprehensive enough to encompass all the previously mentioned mechanisms [ , ]. The starting point for this theory was the observation that the socioeconomic mortality inequality persists and increases across time despite substantial population‐level health improvements. This suggests that health inequalities are generated through differential rates of health improvement. FCT hypothesizes that individuals with greater socioeconomic resources tend to utilize those resources to avoid health risks, improve their health and recover from ill health once it occurs. Nobel laureate Angus Deaton has summarized this as ‘Power and money seek out health improvement’ [ ]. Given the flexibility in how socioeconomic resources can be employed to protect and improve health, inequalities in health tend to persist even when specific pathways are eliminated. Consequently, socioeconomic resources can be viewed as ‘fundamental causes’ of population health.
Drawing on FCT, one would expect that the introduction of new interventions, technology or knowledge for health improvement would lead to increasing health inequalities, as the resourceful groups would be more likely to adopt the new innovations at an early stage. There is some empirical evidence supporting this [ , , ]. In an extension of the framework, Clouston et al. [ ] suggested that historically, the social impact of diseases tends to transition through four stages. The first stage, the natural stage, is characterized by little or no knowledge of the causes, treatments or preventive strategies for the disease. Thus, socioeconomic resources yield little or no health benefits. The second stage, producing inequalities, occurs in populations where there is a (relatively) newfound capacity to prevent and treat the disease. When such information and innovations are diffused through a population, some groups are better positioned than others to acquire and use it, leading to increasing health inequalities. The third stage, reducing inequalities, occurs as the new information and innovations become more accessible and evenly distributed in the population. As the use of the information and innovations has been saturated in the most privileged groups, their relative advantage tapers off, whereas the disadvantaged groups catch up by making use of their increasing access to the information and innovations. The fourth and final stage, reduced disease/disease elimination, occurs when access to the health innovation has become universal and its benefits have been maximized for all groups. This stage may imply that the incidence of the disease stabilizes at a lower level or, in the best case scenario, that the disease is eliminated from the population.
Another empirically testable hypothesis that can be drawn from the FCT is that health inequalities should be greater for mortality from preventable causes than from non‐preventable causes. Several studies have now explicitly tested this hypothesis, and overall, the results suggest that health inequalities are indeed greater for preventable than for non‐preventable causes of death [ ]. Interestingly, a recent Swedish study looking at long‐term changes in the degree of mortality inequality by income finds that a major part of the increase in inequality from around 1990 is due to preventable and treatable causes of death, whereas the inequalities in non‐preventable causes are relatively stable over time [ ].

## Concluding discussion
In this article, we have tried to give a broad overview of what is known about socioeconomic health inequalities in modern societies, with a focus on the Nordic countries. Despite many societal, medical and technological advances, they persist and tend to increase over time in most countries. They can be observed for overall mortality, most causes of death as well as for a wide range of morbid conditions. From cross‐national comparative research, we have now robust evidence showing that health inequalities are substantial in the Nordic countries. Such findings were considered surprising at first, given the Nordic countries’ comprehensive welfare state programmes and their strong focus on universalism and equality. The discussion following these findings has led to a discussion of ‘the Nordic paradox’, in stark contrast to the surprisingly low inequalities in health in Southern Europe (i.e., ‘the Southern miracle’). Central‐Eastern Europe is a situation of its own, with extremely high mortality rates and inequality following the collapse of the Soviet Union (i.e., the ‘Eastern disaster’), but also with notable improvements more recently.
Today, we also have numerous national and international studies confirming the existence of a social gradient in a wide array of health outcomes. Taken together, these inequalities typically compound and lead to wider inequalities in health expectancies than in life expectancies. Over the past decades, a growing number of studies have also shown that health inequalities can be observed across the life course, including among the oldest old.
If there is now a consensus among researchers regarding the existence of these empirical regularities, the causal nature of these associations and, consequently, how they best could be addressed remains a topic of debate within the scientific community. Although the issue of causality versus selection is important, it is equally important to note that health inequalities caused by social selection should not be considered as mere statistical artefacts, because health inequalities have important population health ramifications irrespective of whether they are caused by social causation or social selection.
In terms of the direct causes of the health inequalities in Europe today, behavioural factors are likely to play a key role. However, lifestyles and behaviours are, in turn, shaped by culture, commerce, social policies and individual characteristics. In social epidemiology, there has been a long‐standing debate about upstream and downstream factors in the production of (ill)health. The social determinants of health movement, following the WHO commission led by Michael Marmot [ , ], have argued that upstream social conditions should be the prime focus of attention. Many countries in Europe have since then produced policy documents, implemented various actions in which health equality is highlighted as a key topic of concern for population health and decided that the consequences for health equality should be considered before implementing any policy reform. However, despite this, observable health inequalities continue to increase in many countries.
In this overview, we have also discussed the consequences of choosing different metrics when analysing the magnitude and development of health inequalities. We argue that the choice is crucial, as we may get fundamentally different answers depending on whether absolute or relative inequalities are in focus. Our example, based on Swedish mortality data, shows clearly that our conclusions, and possibly policy suggestions, might be altered when simultaneously looking at absolute and relative measures.
We argue that both absolute and relative measures of inequalities, as well as the absolute rates within the groups, should routinely be reported together. When it comes to statistical monitoring, we also believe that the routinely reported national mortality and life expectancy figures should always be supplemented by measurement of inequalities and variation (see also [ ]).
The role of social policies in shaping health inequalities is complex and relates back to the uncertainties around the underlying mechanisms generating the inequalities. International evidence shows that health inequalities in the Nordic countries have tended to increase even during periods when income inequalities have declined and the welfare states have expanded, showing that the associations between egalitarian social policies and health inequalities are tenuous [ , ]. Thus, rather than assuming that egalitarian social policies and extensive welfare arrangements naturally lead to fewer health inequalities, the impact of social policies on health inequalities needs to be assessed on a case‐by‐case basis. The assessments should go beyond associations at country levels or between welfare regime clusters and study the possible impact of specific social policy programmes and reforms [ ]. Novel frameworks have suggested that an equalization of life chances and increasing social mobility may lead to increasing health inequalities [ ].
In a series of papers, the sociologist Sasson [ , ] has suggested that the locus of health inequalities is currently shifting in high‐income countries from being based on sources of ascribed status, such as sex and race, to being based on sources of achieved status, such as education and income. The distinction between ascribed and achieved status has a long tradition in the social sciences and basically distinguishes between sources of status that are rigid and ascribed to individuals at birth and sources of achieved status that are acquired throughout the life course and can be affected through the agency of the individual. Consequently, inequalities by ascribed status tend to be aligned with systematic prejudice and discrimination, whereas inequalities by achieved status are more likely to be aligned with meritocratic ideals and arrangements.
Sasson has noted that the long‐standing inequalities in mortality by sex and race (in the United States) have declined over time, whereas inequalities in mortality by social class, education and income have tended to increase over time. This, he suggests, has happened over a period where societies have become more and more characterized by meritocratic ideals and practices—heralding the emergence of a ‘meritocracy of longevity’. The dominance of achieved attributes over ascribed ones also goes well in hand with the meritocratic values of personal responsibility also for health and raises important, but difficult, questions of fairness and unfairness. Although the ideas of personal responsibility and meritocracy might legitimize socioeconomic health inequalities, it is important to note that Sasson instead argues that the shift from ascribed to achieved status should lead research to focus more on the structural determinants of population health [ ].
At any rate, the observations made by Sasson are in line with recent evidence from Sweden, suggesting that the socioeconomic gradient in longevity has consistently increased since the mid‐20th century [ , ]. The sex gap in longevity, on the other hand, has been decreasing since around the same time [ , ].
Although it is impossible to tell what the future holds in terms of the development of socioeconomic health inequalities in Europe, there is little to suggest that they will be reduced in the near future. Income inequalities have been on the rise in many countries, not least in the Nordic countries. In several countries, the social distribution of health‐related behaviours has become increasingly skewed over time as new drugs and other health‐ and longevity‐enhancing technologies are introduced to the market. As for the latter, the introduction of the GLP‐1 agonists for the treatment of obesity (e.g., Wegovy) may turn out to be an interesting example to follow. Drawing on FCT, we would assume that the introduction of a new, efficient treatment for obesity will lead to a widening of the already stark socioeconomic inequalities in obesity and obesity‐related health problems, unless efforts are made to counteract an unequal uptake of the treatment. This neatly illustrates what currently may be the most tractable venue for policy strategies to counter health inequalities in the Nordic countries—namely, to intervene on systematic inequalities in the mechanisms that lead to health inequalities, rather than on the socioeconomic inequalities themselves. That is, reducing social inequalities in hazardous working conditions, smoking, obesity and residential safety may be more politically feasible than substantially reducing socioeconomic inequalities per se. From a population health perspective, it may also turn out to be more efficient, as the causal nature of the links between income and health is complex.
As we have seen, socioeconomic inequalities have been characterized by both inertia and change, and by both explicable and (currently) inexplicable patterns. Thus, we will end this overview by arguing for both a continuous monitoring of how the overall patterns of health inequalities develop over time and differ across contexts, and an increase in the efforts to understand the causal architecture of the inequalities. Also important is the development of evidence‐based strategies for ameliorating the inequalities and improving health for all.
