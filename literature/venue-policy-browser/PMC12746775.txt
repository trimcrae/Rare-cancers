## Abstract

## Purpose of review
Despite the proven efficacy of preexposure prophylaxis (PrEP), global coverage remains far below targets, with the greatest gaps among key and underserved populations. This review is timely as it highlights how inequitable access undermines progress toward ending AIDS by 2030 and introduces the “O for KP” framework – a preferential option for key populations and the underserved. This equity-focused principle redefines program success by how well services reach and sustain those most structurally excluded.

## Recent findings
We synthesized evidence on persistent barriers faced by men who have sex with men, transgender people, migrants, people who inject drugs, and others disproportionately affected by HIV. Effective strategies include community-led and peer-driven approaches, integration of gender-affirming and harm reduction services, digital and self-care innovations, task-sharing, and supportive policy reforms. Methods such as human-centred design, preference research, behavioural science frameworks, and implementation science tools have been critical in tailoring and scaling interventions.

## Summary
Adopting an O for KP lens requires shifting beyond pilots and aggregate coverage metrics to approaches that centre the most underserved. Embedding community leadership, diversifying delivery models, and reforming restrictive policies are essential to maximize PrEP's impact and ensure progress toward global HIV prevention goals.

## INTRODUCTION
HIV prevention is central to achieving and sustaining low HIV incidence, yet it remains chronically underfunded and inaccessible to those who need it most. Implementation and equity gaps drive an annual 1.3 million new HIV infections, the vast majority of which are among those with the greatest vulnerability to and burden of HIV, particularly key and underserved populations [ ].
Underserved populations are those experiencing structural, social, or individual barriers to engaging with HIV services. While the terms ‘hard-to-reach’ and ‘marginalised’ have been historically used, there is increasing preference for ‘underserved’, as it emphasises systemic shortcomings rather than attributing responsibility to the populations themselves.
Many underserved populations are also key populations due to specific risk behaviours, structural and social determinants. This includes criminalization, stigma, discrimination, social and legal challenges and limited access to healthcare. Key populations, as defined by the World Health Organization, specifically refer to men who have sex with men (MSM), sex workers, transgender people, people who inject drugs and people in prisons or closed settings [ , ].
The principle of the “preferential option for the poor”, sometimes referred to as “O for P”, has long guided efforts to close health inequities and has been instrumental in shaping the HIV response and informing broader global public health. At its core, this principle asserts that the true measure of health systems is whether they reach those most structurally excluded. In HIV prevention – and particularly in the delivery of preexposure prophylaxis (PrEP) – this principle can be adapted to emphasise the implementation of a ‘preferential option for key populations and the underserved’ (O for KP). At a time of constrained resources and increasing pressure to generalise service delivery, in an effort to drive impact, this framework provides a critical lens to evaluate evidence focused on those with the greatest need.
PrEP is a highly effective HIV prevention intervention. When used as prescribed and delivered at scale [ , ], it reduces HIV infection at both individual and population levels [ , ]. Despite consensus on the importance of PrEP, within the broader HIV response, access remains limited, especially among key and underserved populations [ ]. In 2024, only 3.9 million people globally used PrEP, far short of the 2025 target of 21.2 million users [ ]. MSM are up to 7.5 times more likely to acquire HIV than other adults, while transgender women are 8.5 times more likely [ ]. Migrants, refugees, asylum seekers and undocumented individuals face multiple overlapping HIV-related risks, with prevalence up to 55-fold higher among asylum seekers when compared to the general population [ ]. To date, PrEP has remained highly medicalized and is primarily delivered in health facilities, such as sexual health clinics, often requiring multiple laboratory-based tests. Although differentiated models, including TelePrEP, have emerged, these remain unavailable in many contexts. Recent funding constraints have further jeopardized community-based and peer-led prevention programs that have proven effective in reaching these populations [ , ]. More recently, long-acting injectable PrEP (LAI-PrEP), such as cabotegravir and lenacapavir, have expanded prevention options, but access remains constrained by limited supply, high costs, health system readiness, staffing limitations, and stigma [ , – ]. Despite recent efforts to reduce costs [ ], access particularly among key populations and underserved groups is limited due to restrictive implementation guidance.
This review aims to summarize existing evidence on interventions that can increase PrEP uptake among key and underserved populations. We also assess the methods to place those with the greatest need, and most excluded, at the centre of delivery to drive PrEP scale-up and contribute toward the global goal of ending AIDS by 2030. 
 

## KEY BARRIERS TO ACCESSING PREP AMONG KEY AND UNDERSERVED POPULATIONS
Despite global commitments [ ], key populations continue to face structural and context-specific barriers to PrEP (Table ) [ , , ]. Among MSM, uptake remains suboptimal in many low- and middle-income countries due to punitive laws criminalizing same-sex activity [ ]. Transgender women and men encounter transphobia, poor integration of PrEP with hormone therapy, and mental health challenges [ , ]. Migrants face restrictive policies, ineligibility for health insurance, high costs, language barriers, and fear of deportation; many also lack knowledge of PrEP if it is unavailable in their home countries [ ]. People who use drugs remain excluded from PrEP programmes, with stigma, criminalization, and police harassment deterring use [ ]. Rural and mobile populations face geographic isolation, mobility, and limited provider training [ , , ]. Heterosexual groups, including sex workers and women at elevated risk, often underestimate HIV risk, while structural inequalities reduce access [ , , , ]. Former PrEP users face high discontinuation due to stigma, pill fatigue, side-effects, or cost [ , ]. Addressing these gaps is critical for equity and expanded PrEP coverage.
 

## INTERVENTIONS TO ENHANCE PREP UPTAKE IN KEY POPULATIONS
Multiple strategies have demonstrated effectiveness in increasing PrEP uptake, but they remain fragmented and insufficiently scaled. In an effort to apply an ‘O for KP’ framework, we summarise evidence for interventions that prioritise impact specifically among key and underserved populations, namely by: community-led approaches, task-sharing, self-care, digital health, and enabling policies [ , ] (see Table ).
 

## Integrated community-led approaches
Community-led PrEP approaches, [ ] grounded in trust and cultural competence, have been highly effective, especially when led by key populations, specifically MSM and transgender people. For example, Thailand's Tangerine Clinic integrates gender-affirming care, mental health, and sexual health alongside HIV prevention for transgender clients. [ ] In the Philippines, Luxecare, a community-run private clinic established by LoveYourself, offers sexually transmitted infection (STI)/HIV diagnostic and prevention services for MSM. Since 2020, it has served more than 15 400 MSM, linking 95% of the 695 newly diagnosed HIV cases to treatment. [ , ] In Australia, Health Equity Matters launched a YouTube campaign featuring Chinese MSM influencers and clinicians, aiming to increase PrEP uptake and awareness among Mandarin-speaking MSM [ ].

## Leveraging task-sharing, self-care and digital interventions in health systems
Systems–level approaches are essential for meeting the needs of key and underserved populations, [ ] as they consider how to reduce structural barriers, improve service efficiency, and ensure continuity of care. Historically, strategies such as task sharing to lay providers, pharmacists and those with short-term training have been critical to expanding access-driven approaches [ – ]. While these remain essential, new opportunities to apply self-care and digital platforms – self-tests, web-based, mobile applications, artificial intelligence, TelePrEP and other online navigation tools and appointment bookers – have expanded on these methods offer convenience, discretion, simplification and potential reduction in frequent facility-based follow-up [ , , , – , , – , , ]. When coupled with self-care more broadly, there is ample opportunity to utilize digital tools to open up new delivery models, including multimonth dispensing and nonfacility-based approaches [ – ]. It remains critical, however, to ensure transitions to self-care and new technologies are implemented with an ‘O for KP’ approach in mind and therefore do not exacerbate gaps, lead to abandonment or undermine community health systems.
Tailored approaches within the system remain important, especially in a time when programmes seek to streamline and integrate services. Evidence has shown how population-specific approaches have effectively improved PrEP uptake. [ ] For example, the reduction in HIV infections was observed among people who inject drugs attending evidence-based harm reduction programs, such as syringe services programs and opiate agonist therapies [ ]. Additionally, the PrEPMe Clinic (Australia) in parallel removed barriers to PrEP – Medicare eligibility (the Universal health insurance scheme in Australia). The clinic provides a free PrEP service for individuals without Medicare, attracting 57 out of 100 first clients who are PrEP-naïve users [ ].

## Policy and structural reform
Critical enablers and supportive policies that prioritize key and underserved populations are essential for achieving and sustaining meaningful impact in PrEP, and HIV prevention more broadly [ ]. This requires revising restrictive laws and fostering enabling environments that promote equitable access to prevention services. Examples include the UK and Australia, which, after some programmatic experience, shifted to provide PrEP free of charge to all eligible individuals, regardless of their immigration status [ , ].
Recent evaluations in France highlight the importance of expanding PrEP delivery within primary care, including training general practitioners and healthcare workers in underserved communities and enabling more providers to prescribe PrEP [ ]. In the United States, an increasing number of states now allow pharmacists to prescribe PrEP, creating additional access points and improving convenience [ ]. Ensuring genuine PrEP choice – particularly as new prevention modalities emerge – requires timely policy and regulatory updates, along with the removal of complex or costly requirements that hinder uptake [ ]. Recent studies highlight that requiring expensive testing, without same-visit results, is hindering PrEP access and is often unimplementable even in high-income settings [ ].

## METHODS USED TO DEVELOP “O FOR KP” INTERVENTIONS
Advancing research to address the needs of key and underserved populations requires participatory and equity-focused strategies (Table ). Preference research highlights what service attributes matter most, and approaches such as discrete choice experiments (DCEs) show what drives the uptake of PrEP. This includes cost, location, dispensing interval, or requirement for HIV testing, as most valued by different populations [ ]. For example, the PrEP-APPEAL study used a multicountry DCE to identify key service features influencing uptake among MSM and transgender women in Asia, informing differentiated service delivery models [ ]. Further, qualitative methods, using in-depth interviews and focus group discussions, help to understand diversity and nuance in experiences, which are essential to understand for populations that face layers of marginalization and provide insight for early phase design and refinement of service delivery approaches [ ].
 Behaviour science frameworks such as the COM-B model and the Behaviour Change Wheel, which provide structured approaches to mapping behavioural determinants of PrEP uptake, can also be most effective. For example, mapping the capability, opportunity, and motivation of target populations can highlight whether interventions should prioritize skills-building (e.g. pill-taking routines), structural access (e.g. pharmacy dispensing), or motivational messaging. In addition, behavioural economics approaches, such as the MINDSPACE framework, offer complementary strategies by leveraging cognitive biases and heuristics to “nudge” individuals toward desired behaviours without restricting choice. A recent scoping review identified 124 HIV prevention and management interventions that used MINDSPACE elements, with incentives, messenger effects, and commitment devices being most frequently applied, suggesting considerable potential for integrating these techniques into PrEP service design to improve uptake and adherence [ ].
Human-centred design approaches, which include key and underserved populations, are also critical as they combine qualitative insights, prototyping, and iterative testing to ensure that interventions meet the real-world needs of users, particularly those that may have challenges due to persistent. This process is particularly valuable in digital health interventions, such as the PrEP reminder applications or telehealth platforms, where usability and acceptability are critical for sustained engagement. While approaches to adapt diagnostic tools to the needs of the most marginalized have advanced, these same tools have yet to exist for prevention and need further development and scale-up. Better yet, when research is deployed using participatory methods and community leadership, such as through designathons (time-bound, collaborative workshops) [ ], diverse stakeholders are engaged. This promotes buy-in and enhances sustainability by embedding user ownership in the intervention design, ultimately incorporating critical context and cultural needs from the outset.
Implementation science frameworks, such as RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance) and CFIR (Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research), have been used to systematically evaluate the reach, adoption, implementation, and maintenance of PrEP interventions. These approaches help to bridge the gap between pilot programmes and national scale-up by identifying contextual facilitators and barriers to effective delivery [ , ]. Ultimately, research tools, methods and implementation frameworks must drive evidence-informed policy development. Policy-focused approaches analyse legal, regulatory, and funding landscapes to identify opportunities for structural reform. These approaches often combine desk reviews, stakeholder consultations, and economic modelling to support advocacy effort and resource allocation. Without such translation, the evidence base remains disconnected from practice, limiting progress toward equitable and tailored PrEP implementation.

## KEY CHALLENGES AND GAPS
Despite innovations, critical challenges persist. Community-led and demedicalized models remain fragile, often donor-dependent and vulnerable to shifting political priorities and financing [ , ]. LAI PrEP holds great promise, but unless it reaches key and underserved populations, and is scaled, its potential will not be realized [ , ]. Efforts to address real-world challenges due to cost, regulations and health system readiness need to be addressed. Data systems need to be simplified, yet without visibility and tracking that includes gender identity, migration status, and other intersectional identities, inequities remain invisible, and the impact of investment in key and underserved populations may not be fully understood and therefore affect future opportunities.

## FUTURE DIRECTIONS
At a time when prioritizing key and underserved populations may be politically unpopular, it is critical to follow the science and lessons learned from decades of HIV prevention. Historically and empirically, placing the least represented and most affected communities at the centre of the response has yielded the greatest reductions in new infections. Data show that this equity-focused approach contributed to a 61% decline in global HIV infections since the peak in 1996 [ ].
Now is the time to reorient programme metrics. PrEP delivery must be judged by equity outcomes coverage and retention among key and underserved populations rather than aggregate numbers alone. Unless services reach those most structurally excluded, ending AIDS as a global public-health threat by 2030 will remain out of reach.
Now is the time to move beyond pilots. Scaling up PrEP requires integrated, system-level approaches that centre disenfranchised populations. Long-term financing and political commitment are essential for sustainability. Embedding self-care, community-led, and demedicalized models into national health-benefit packages, alongside negotiations for affordable pricing of LAI-PrEP, will be critical to equitable and sustainable delivery.
Now is the time to institutionalize participatory design. HIV prevention programmes must embed community co-creation within broader health-system reforms that emphasize regionalization and self-reliance. Locally driven programme designs grounded in trust, lived realities, and cultural relevance are essential to ensure uptake and effectiveness.
Now is the time to scale choice. Expanding PrEP options from daily oral pills to long-acting injectables can close persistent gaps in uptake and adherence, especially among key and underservced populations missed by clinic-based care. Innovative delivery models such as pharmacy-led and online PrEP services, supported by affordable and discreet HIV self-testing, can streamline initiation and continuity. Integration with services such as STI testing and gender-affirming care can address holistic needs, though preference research indicates that integration models must remain flexible across settings [ ]. Long-acting PrEP, whether injectable or oral, demonstrates high acceptability among underserved groups and should be prioritized wherever feasible [ , ].

## CONCLUSION
From the earliest days of the HIV response, equity has always been the engine of impact. The “O for P” principle has been transformational in showing on how focusing on impact among the most marginalized and excluded from mainstream systems leads to wide scale impact and change.
While PrEP is recognized as an essential prevention tool, it has not yet become a standard part of the response in many settings, as too few people, especially those with the highest vulnerability, have access. Continuing along the current trajectory will not close prevention gaps. What is needed now is a fundamental shift: an “O for KP” framework that redefines success by how well HIV prevention systems reaches and sustains services for those most excluded. Now is the time to reorient programme metrics to measure equity, not volume; to move beyond pilot projects toward integrated, system-level delivery; and to institutionalize participatory design that embeds community leadership and co-creation into every stage of implementation.
History warns us that when prevention neglects key and underserved populations, the epidemic persists; when they are prioritized, the benefits extend to all. The choice today is no different. By embracing O for KP, the HIV response can honour its past, confront present inequities, and chart the only credible path to ending AIDS as a public health threat by 2030.
