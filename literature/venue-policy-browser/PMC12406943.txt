## Abstract

## Abstract

## Objectives
To measure the prevalence and magnitude of industry payments to neurologists prescribing multiple sclerosis (MS) drugs and determine whether payments are associated with prescribing.

## Design
Retrospective observational study.

## Setting
Data on neurologists prescribing MS drugs from 2015 to 2019 in the Medicare Part D database linked to the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services Open Payments database.

## Participants
7401 neurologists prescribing MS drugs from 2015 to 2019 to Medicare beneficiaries.

## Main outcome measures
The primary outcome was the proportion of physicians’ annual prescriptions manufactured by a given company. Generalised linear mixed models were used to evaluate associations between the presence and magnitude of payments and prescribing. The association between prescription volume and the likelihood of receiving payments as well as the value of payments was also assessed.

## Results
Among 7401 neurologists, 5809 (78.5%) received payments totalling US$163.6 million between 2015 and 2019. While the median amount per physician was US$779 (IQR, US$188–US$2587), US$155.7 million (95.2%) accrued to the top 10% of payment recipients. Higher prescription volumes were associated with a higher likelihood of receiving any payment type, particularly for consulting services, non-consulting services and travel/lodging (p<0.001). Among payment recipients, the amount received was positively associated with prescription volume (p<0.001). Receipt of payments was associated with greater likelihood of prescribing the company’s drugs compared with those who received no payments from that company (OR 1.13 (95% CI 1.11 to 1.15)), with the largest association for non-consulting services, such as being a speaker at an event (OR 1.53 (95% CI 1.44 to 1.62)). Larger payments were associated with a greater likelihood of prescribing (OR 1.10, 1.26, 1.29 and 1.50 for US$50, US$500, US$1000 and US$5000, respectively), as were longer durations of payments (OR 1.12 for single year to 1.78 for 5 consecutive years) and more recent payments (OR 1.03 for payments made 4 years prior to 1.34 for payments made in the same year).

## Conclusions
Nearly 80% of neurologists prescribing MS drugs received at least one industry payment, with higher volume prescribers being more likely to receive payments. Physicians receiving payments were more likely to prescribe the company’s drugs, with a stronger association for payments that were larger, sustained and recent.

## STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY
By assessing several facets of industry payments, including their magnitude, recency and frequency, this analysis provides a more holistic examination of the relationship between industry payments and physician prescribing in a drug market that has not been studied to date.
The study sample was limited to Part D Medicare prescriptions, potentially limiting its external generalisability to other settings.
Granular data regarding payment details were not available, limiting the study’s ability to identify specific contexts in which industry payments may be most likely to influence prescribing.
The observational nature of the study inherently subjects it to residual confounding.

## Introduction
 Multiple sclerosis (MS), a leading cause of neurologic disability in young adults, often requires lifelong treatment with immunomodulatory drugs prescribed by a neurologist specialising in the disease. Once established, physicians are reluctant to switch patients between products, and effective therapies are usually continued indefinitely unless a patient’s clinical response changes. , Several companies manufacture disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) to treat MS, including brand-name drugs that recently entered the market and generic versions of older therapies. Brand-name drugs are relatively expensive, with annual average costs per DMT user ranging from ∼US$57 000 to US$93 000 in the USA. As a result, MS drug prescriptions are Medicare’s largest neurologic drug expense despite accounting for a relatively small portion of total claims. Further, a small number of physicians in the USA are responsible for most prescriptions. These MS drug market features create a highly competitive environment.
While company efforts to market and promote pharmaceutical products typically include detailing and educational seminars accompanied by meals, recent litigation suggests that companies manufacturing MS drugs may have engaged in legally questionable behaviour. Though they did not admit wrongdoing, three companies in the MS drug market recently finalised settlements exceeding US$1.5 billion with the US Department of Justice , to resolve allegations of improper payments seeking to influence physician prescribing.
Previous studies have demonstrated that industry payments are associated with increased physician prescribing of marketed products. , However, the existing literature has not focused on a market as competitive as the MS drug market. Accordingly, we sought to characterise industry payments to neurologists prescribing MS drugs and assess the association between receipt of payments from a company and the likelihood of prescribing that company’s drugs.

## Methods

## Data source
We used publicly available data on payments made by industry to physicians from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid (CMS) Open Payments platform from 2015 through 2019. Payments are classified into three categories: research payments, ownership and investment interests, and general payments. To focus on payments related to marketing and promotion to physicians prescribing MS drugs, we excluded payments to non-physicians and academic medical centres, as well as research payments (typically paid to institutions) and ownership/investment interest paid to physicians, focusing on general payments made to physicians.
We linked the Open Payments data to Medicare Part D data, which covers prescription drugs, using National Provider Identification numbers and drug names. We did not have access to prescribing data for physician-administered drugs covered by Medicare Part B. We analysed data from 2015 to 2019 to avoid potential distortion by the COVID-19 pandemic.

## Study population
We limited our study to neurologists who prescribed MS DMTs from 2015 to 2019. A neurologist was a prescriber of MS DMTs if they had prescribed any MS DMT at least 1 year, with a minimum of 11 prescriptions or more times, as CMS censors records containing fewer than 11 prescriptions. In total, 20 DMTs manufactured by 10 companies were eligible for inclusion. In a secondary analysis, we categorised drugs into moderate-efficacy and high-efficacy DMTs, as done by prior reports. , 
 lists these drugs and their respective companies (manufacturers).

## Main outcome measure
Our primary outcome was the proportion of physicians’ annual prescriptions of DMTs manufactured by a given company. This was calculated as the number of prescribed drugs manufactured by each company divided by the total number of MS prescriptions for each physician. Prescriptions were defined as all claims for a given drug, including both new prescriptions and refills, an approach that is consistent with previous research. Censored prescriptions (fewer than 11 counts) were imputed using three alternative methods to test the robustness of our findings. Additional details are provided in the .

## Exposures of interest
Our primary exposure was whether prescribers received payments from a drug’s company in a given year. We also included indicators for whether physicians received payments from that drug’s company only, from that drug’s company and also a company marketing a competing drug, did not receive payments from that drug’s company but did receive payments from a company marketing a competing drug or did not receive any payments at all. We also studied payments stratified by type: food and beverages, travel and lodging, consulting services, non-consulting services (eg, serving as a speaker or faculty at a venue), education and other types of payments (eg, gifts, charitable contributions, honouraria and entertainment fees).

## Statistical analysis

## Quantifying industry payments to MS physicians
We calculated the total number and value of payments made by each company to physicians, the number and proportion of physicians who received payments from companies and the distribution of payments per physician (median, IQR and the amount paid to the top 10%). We also calculated the proportion of physicians who received payments from one company only versus more than one company. Payments were only included if they were recorded as being related to an MS drug.

## Association between industry payments and choice of prescriptions

## Same-year association between payments and prescriptions
We estimated the association between receiving payments from a company and prescribing that company’s drugs in the same year. We used a generalised linear mixed model with a binomial distribution (with logit link) and a first-order autoregressive correlation structure with constant variance across time to account for the temporal clustering of prescriptions belonging to a given company within physicians.
Four models were estimated to test different prescriber–payment scenarios as predictors of the proportion of a physician’s annual prescriptions accounted for by a given company’s drugs. The first model included a binary indicator for whether a physician received payments from the company manufacturing the drug. The second model included a 4-level categorical variable composed of payments from: the manufacturing company only, the company manufacturing the drug and one or more competitors, one or more competitors only, or no payments from any company. The third model included six binary indicators, each denoting the presence or absence of a given type of payment (food and beverages, travel and lodging, consulting services, non-consulting services, education and other types of payments). The fourth model included the total value of payments. All models (aside from the second model) included a covariate for the value of payments received from competitors, since physicians who receive payments from a given company may also be more likely to receive payments from competitors, particularly if payments are directed based on certain physician characteristics. A secondary analysis using the same models linked payments in a year to prescriptions in the following year. Additional details are provided in the .

## Association between duration and recency of payments with prescriptions in 2019
We investigated two additional factors. First, we analysed the association between duration of payments (denoted by the number of years during which payments were received) and prescriptions in 2019. This was performed using a categorical variable ranging from 0 (no payments received in any year) to 5 (payments received each year from 2015 to 2019). Second, we analysed the association between the recency of payment (denoted by the last year during which a payment was received from 2015 to 2019) and prescriptions in 2019. This was performed using a categorical variable with six levels, each denoting a given year from 2015 to 2019, in addition to a level denoting the absence of payments in any year. An additional model investigated whether duration versus recency of payments was more strongly associated with prescriptions. All three models included, as covariates, the cumulative amount received from at least one competitor from 2015 to 2019, the number of years during which the physician received payments from at least one competitor and the most recent payment from a competitor.

## Association between industry payments and total volume of physician prescriptions
Additionally, we determined the association between the receipt of payments and the average annual volume of MS DMT prescriptions written by physicians from 2015 to 2019. This was performed in two ways. First, we compared the volume of prescriptions written by physicians who received no payments and who received different types of payments using a Wilcoxon rank sum test. Second, we tested the association between receiving different types of payments and the total volume of physicians’ prescriptions using logistic regression.
All analyses were performed using R, V.4.2.0. Models were built using the ‘glmmTMB’ and ‘mgcv’ packages, contrasts and estimated proportions were obtained using the ‘emmeans’ package and graphical visualisation was performed using the ‘ggplot2’ package. Restricted cubic splines were used to model continuous variables. Statistical significance was denoted by p<0.05 and 95% CIs.

## Patient and public involvement
There was no patient or public involvement in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this study.

## Results

## Characteristics of payments made to neurologists prescribing MS drugs
From 2015 to 2019, a total of 7401 neurologists prescribed MS DMTs 11 or more times in any year. Of these, 5809 (78.5%) received payments from at least one company ( ), and 4999 (67.5%) received payments from two or more companies. In total, these physicians received 626 290 distinct payments amounting to US$163.6 million ( ); the median amount per physician was US$779 (IQR, US$188–US$2587). Physicians in the highest total payment decile received 95.2% (US$155.7 million) of the total value in payments. Sanofi (manufacturer of Aubagio & Lemtrada) made the highest total value of payments (US$71.3 million) to 4183 physicians (56.5% of total physicians), whereas Biogen (manufacturer of Avonex, Plegridy, Tecfidera and Tysabri) had the greatest market penetration, paying US$33.1 million to 4898 physicians (66.2% of total physicians). Non-consulting fees comprised the largest share of payments (US$108.4 million; 66.3% of the total) and were associated with the largest median payment value (US$54 610). In contrast, the greatest proportion of physicians (5623; comprising 76% of total MS neurologists) and the greatest number of payments (512 052) were related to foods and beverages.

From 2015 to 2019, Copaxone (24.6% of total DMT prescriptions) and Tecfidera (16.6% of total DMT prescriptions) were the most commonly prescribed DMTs. On a per-company basis, Biogen had the greatest market share (32.5% of total DMT prescriptions). ## Association of payments with the proportion of prescriptions from a company’s portfolio

## Receipt of payments
Overall, physicians who received payments were more likely to prescribe a company’s drugs in the same year compared with those who received no payments from that company (OR 1.13 (95% CI 1.11 to 1.15), ). Including only the 3451 neurologists who prescribed >10 MS drugs every year from 2015 to 2019 showed a similar association (OR 1.13 (95% CI 1.11 to 1.15)).
 

## Source of payment
Compared with those who received no payments from any company, physicians who received payments from one company only were more likely to prescribe that company’s drug (OR 1.09 (95% CI 1.05 to 1.13)), as were physicians who also received payments from a company and one or more competitor(s) (OR 1.06 (95% CI 1.04 to 1.08)). In contrast, physicians who received no payments from a company but did receive payments from a competitor were less likely to prescribe that company’s drugs (OR 0.96 (95% CI 0.94 to 0.97)) ( ).

## Type of payment
Most payment types were associated with an increased likelihood of prescribing a company’s drugs compared with those who received no payments from any company. However, associations varied in magnitude and were greatest for non-consulting services (OR 1.53 (95% CI 1.44 to 1.62), ) and weakest for education (OR 0.97 (95% CI 0.94 to 1.01)). Additional analyses linking payments received in the year preceding prescriptions (rather than payments received during the same year) showed similar findings ( ).

## Value of payments
Small payment values were associated with an increased likelihood of prescribing a company’s drugs compared with those who received no payments from that company (US$50 vs US$0, OR 1.10 (95% CI 1.08 to 1.12)). This association was greater in magnitude with greater payment amounts (OR 1.26, 1.29 and 1.50 for US$500, US$1000 and US$5000, all compared with a reference value of US$0, respectively; ).
 

## Duration and recency of payments
Compared with physicians who received no payments, those who received payments for 1–5 years consecutively were more likely to prescribe the company’s drugs ( ), with progressively greater effect estimates (OR 1.12 for 1 year of payments to OR 1.78 for five consecutive years of payments; ). Compared with physicians receiving no payments, physicians receiving payments from 2015 to 2019 were progressively more likely to prescribe a given company’s drugs in 2019 the more recent the payment (OR 1.03 in 2015 to OR 1.34 in 2019; ).
When both duration and recency of payments were jointly assessed ( ), duration was more strongly associated with prescription patterns. Compared with physicians receiving no payments, the greatest effect estimates were found for physicians who received payments every year from 2015 to 2019 (OR 1.78 (95% CI 1.75 to 1.80)) or those who received payments every year from 2015 to 2018 (OR 1.75 (95% CI 1.72 to 1.77)). However, payments received during a single year in 2015 were also associated with preferential prescribing 4 years later in 2019 (OR 1.07 (95% CI 1.05 to 1.09)).

## Subgroup analysis according to drug efficacy
The associations between payments and prescription patterns, stratified by drug efficacy, are shown in . The association between receiving payments and prescribing that company’s drugs was more pronounced for moderate-efficacy drugs (OR 1.22 (95% CI 1.19 to 1.24)) than for high-efficacy drugs (OR 1.07 (95% CI 1.01 to 1.13), p value for interaction <0.001). This pattern was similar for other payment variables.
 

## Proportion of prescriptions according to payments
The estimated adjusted proportion of prescriptions issued by a physician for drugs manufactured by a company that paid them was 15.5% (95% CI 15.2% to 15.7%), compared with 13.9% (95% CI 13.7% to 14.1%) if they had not received payments from that company. Proportions of a company’s drugs prescribed by a physician according to other payment variables are shown in . The estimated proportion that belonged to a company that paid them if they had not received payments from other companies was 14.8%, compared with 14.6% if they had additionally received payments from other companies, 13.9% if they had not received any payments and 13.3% if they had only received payments from a competitor ( ). By payment type, the estimated proportion was 13.9% if they had not received payments of any type, 15.2% for food and beverages, 14.8% for travel and lodging, 13.4% for educational activities, 16.2% for consulting activities, 19.7% for non-consulting activities and 14.0% for other types of payments ( ). By value of payments, the estimated proportion was 13.9% if they had received no payments, 15.0% if they had received US$50, 16.7% if they had received US$500, 17.0% if they had received US$1000 and 19.2% if they had received US$5000 ( ). By the duration of payments, the estimated proportion was 17.6% for those receiving no payments, to 27.4% for those receiving payments consecutively for all 5 years from 2015 to 2019 ( ). By recency of payments, the estimated proportion was 17.6% for those receiving no payments and 22.2% for those receiving payments in 2019 ( ).

## Discussion
In this analysis of the highly competitive market for MS DMTs that examined neurologist prescribing from 2015 to 2019 linked to industry payments from the CMS Open Payments database, there were three key findings. First, nearly 80% of physicians received industry payments from at least one company, with two-thirds receiving payments from two or more companies. Second, there was a clear association between physicians’ prescription volumes and the likelihood of receiving industry payments, particularly with respect to payments for consulting services, non-consulting (speaking) services and travel and lodging. Third, payments from a company were associated with a greater likelihood of prescribing that company’s drugs and a dose–response effect was found with respect to the value of payments, duration of payments and recency of payments. These findings raise concerns about excess pharmaceutical promotion efforts and their implications for physician prescribing for patients.
The finding that most physicians prescribing DMTs for MS receive industry payments, often from two or more companies, highlights how commonly payments are made to prescribers of MS drugs. Moreover, the finding that the vast majority of payments accrued to the top 10% of prescribers suggests that companies selectively target high-volume prescribers. Further, the finding that physicians who receive payments from competitors were less likely to prescribe a company’s drugs if they otherwise received no payments suggests that companies that do not aggressively market their drugs may be placing themselves at a competitive disadvantage.
Moreover, we demonstrated a dose–response relationship between the value of payments and prescription patterns, with greater associations being found for payments of greater monetary value. However, the association of lower values with prescribing behaviour, compared with receiving no payments at all, was not negligible. This suggests there was no ‘threshold’ of payments below which prescribing the company’s product is not more likely, a finding consistent with prior studies of anticoagulation and diabetes medications. A dose–response relationship was also found for the duration of payments and the recency of payments.
Promotional efforts to influence prescribing are especially concerning given the drugs’ substantial costs, particularly if more expensive brand-name drugs are being prescribed instead of appropriate, effective, generically available alternatives. Disease-modifying MS drugs accounted for US$33.9 billion in excess medical costs between 2017 and 2019, constituting Medicare’s largest neurologic drug expense. A Medicare analysis of trends in MS drug costs between 2006 and 2016 showed that out-of-pocket costs increased by sevenfold for patients and federal expenditures increased by 10-fold. Whether aggressive marketing of more expensive drugs is related to this uptrend remains an unanswered question.
In the context of MS, choosing which DMTs to initiate for newly diagnosed persons, or which DMTs to switch to for persons who experience relapse, often requires a substantial amount of clinical judgement and careful weighing of patient preferences. For instance, the choice of when to initiate DMTs is not always clear-cut. In patients with single demyelinating events, the choice of whether to initiate DMT depends on carefully weighing the risk of future relapse and patient preference. When patients and physicians agree to start DMT, different options are available without a sole clear first-choice DMT. Although initial therapy with highly active DMTs may reduce the risk of relapse in persons with active MS, it also carries a greater risk of adverse events compared with older treatment options. , Likewise, switching patients to different DMTs often involves clinical judgement because ‘non-responders’ to a given class of DMT are not always simple to identify. Most persons with MS will experience some residual symptoms, and so the choice to deem a given treatment ‘ineffective’ and switch DMTs is not always straightforward. In the context of this analysis, the key concern is whether the payments reported herein are potentially unduly influencing the physician’s decision (eg, to trigger an unnecessary switch in a patient who is otherwise doing reasonably well on a certain DMT, or to promote more aggressive upfront therapy with high-activity DMTs in patients with more conservative treatment preferences).
Industry payments and their relation to physician prescribing patterns have been studied in other fields. , The most relevant to the current study are those which study highly expensive drugs prescribed by a relatively smaller pool of physicians. An analysis of 2013–2015 Medicare Part D data of drugs used to treat renal cancer, lung cancer, prostate cancer or chronic myeloid leukaemia showed that physicians who received payments in three consecutive years were more likely to prescribe the payor’s drugs in all four cancers except prostate. An analysis of 4822 rheumatologists spanning Medicare Parts B and D data showed similar findings. An analysis of glatiramer prescribers showed that those who had financial relationships with the branded drug manufacturer were less likely to prescribe generic alternatives after their introduction. These findings also extend to costly medical devices, including the use of mechanical circulatory support devices and implantable cardiac defibrillators. 
The Physician Payments Sunshine Act, which led to the creation of the Open Payments Database, was an important step forward in making transparent the financial conflicts of interest among physicians receiving industry payments. However, it remains unclear whether increased transparency has mitigated these conflicts of interest and their impact on prescribing behaviour, or simply given the public greater insight into the large scale of industry payments made to prescribers. Our analyses and those of others document the large volumes of industry payments to physicians , and their impact on prescription patterns. , 
Several policies aimed at actively limiting industry payments to physicians—rather than simply making them transparent—have been proposed. These include institutional policies limiting the access pharmaceutical representatives have to physicians and imposing restrictions on non-research payments to physicians. , Additionally, policies enforcing greater transparency with respect to the physicians’ services related to payments may make it easier to differentiate legitimate reimbursements (eg, serving on clinical trial committees) from payments mainly intended to influence prescriptions. Currently, terms for some payment types are broadly descriptive (eg, ‘non-consulting’) and do not entail sufficient detail to differentiate between the two. Further, most proposals have placed the responsibility on physicians to self-regulate financial conflicts of interest, without sufficient focus on the institutional problems which propagate such conflicts. Further, many professional organisations, scholarly conferences and journals are highly dependent on industry funding.

## Limitations
This analysis has several limitations. First, our study was limited to the prescribing of Part D-covered drugs and does not account for physician-administered MS disease-modifying agents. Nor does our study account for payments to non-physicians or academic medical centres, which may influence physician prescribing. Second, our study accounts only for direct payments from industry to physicians but cannot capture the entirety of physician–industry relations (eg, drug samples distributed). Third, our study cannot account for the appropriateness of prescribing, nor for which patients more expensive brand-name drugs are most suitable. Fourth, the observational nature of these comparisons is inherently subject to potential unmeasured confounding. The most pertinent is that physicians who treat more MS patients (and thus prescribe more MS drugs) are, by virtue of their clinical practice, more likely to attend conferences and events where interactions with industry are common. However, we attempted to mitigate confounding by studying the proportion, rather than the absolute number, of prescriptions. Further, data after 2020 were not analysed due to the COVID pandemic, which may have significantly curtailed relations between industry and physicians. Investigation of how these associations may have evolved as pandemic measures subsided would be of interest. Further, the limited data on payment details make it difficult to ascertain the appropriateness of any given payment. They also make it difficult to ascertain whether a greater volume of payments reflects a company being more invested in the MS market due to its drugs enjoying greater commercial success, or whether these payments are aimed at influencing prescribing patterns. Moreover, ownership structures (eg, subsidiaries and parent companies) may have nuanced effects on marketing efforts insofar as a subsidiary may compete more aggressively with drugs marketed by different companies and less so with drugs marketed by another subsidiary or directly by the parent company. These elements are difficult to investigate without access to more detailed data but are nevertheless important limitations. Finally, a physician’s decision to prescribe a given drug is subject to a myriad of factors, including their familiarity with a specific set of drugs, national guidelines and/or institutional protocols, manufacturing shortages, insurance coverage policies and patient preferences. These determinants are difficult to assess using publicly available data but are important to consider when interpreting our findings.

## Conclusion
From 2015 to 2019, nearly 80% of US neurologists treating patients with MS received US$164 million in industry payments, of which US$156 million accrued to the top 10% of physicians receiving payments. High-volume prescribers were more likely to receive industry payments—particularly consulting services, non-consulting services (eg, speaking fees), and travel and lodging. Payments from a company were associated with preferential prescribing of the company’s drugs, with dose–response relationships between preferential prescribing and the value of payments, duration of payments and recency of payments. These findings suggest industry selectively targets higher-volume prescribers with large payments, which were associated with prescribing, and raise concerns about excess pharmaceutical promotion efforts and their implications for physician prescribing for patients.
