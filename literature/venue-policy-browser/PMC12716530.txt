## Abstract

## Background
Immigrant populations, particularly undocumented immigrants, are often considered ‘hidden’ or ‘hard to reach’ in research. This invisibilisation—under-representation or exclusion in data collection—leads to data inequities and biased findings that fail to capture their unique experiences. Starfish sampling mitigates selection bias and improves access to invisibilised populations by recruiting ‘seed’ participants at randomly selected times and locations and leveraging their social networks to recruit the next wave of participants. In this protocol paper, we outline the sampling strategy for the BRAVE (Building community, Raising All immigrant Voices for health Equity) study, a multi-site, cross-sectional survey examining the relationship between immigration history and sexual and reproductive health (SRH) service utilisation among Asian immigrant women in the USA. This protocol is an adaptation of novel starfish sampling in combination with various data tools and a community-based participatory research approach.

## Methods and analysis
Using data from the American Community Survey and insights from community partners, we will conduct community mapping across four study sites (Atlanta, Houston, Los Angeles County and New York City). We will select census tracts that reflect the primary ethnic groups of interest and diverse socioeconomic backgrounds. From these selected census tracts, we will construct a venue universe by identifying key activity areas for Asian immigrant women through data scraping from online sources. We will then randomly select venue-date-time combinations and deliberately choose various community engagement events for recruitment. Culturally competent field officers who are fluent in Asian languages will recruit participants at these events. Participants can refer up to three peers from their social networks to take part in the survey. Results will be presented as descriptive statistics and logistic regression models to test the association between immigration history and SRH service utilisation.

## Ethics and dissemination
The overarching BRAVE study protocol was approved by the University of California Los Angeles Institutional Review Board (IRB) (IRB-22–0493-AM-016). The results will be disseminated through academic journal publications and relevant data will be shared with our community partners.

## STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY
This protocol outlines a scalable, adaptable strategy for using starfish sampling, offering guidance for other researchers aiming to recruit and include invisibilised populations in their research.
By integrating starfish sampling into complex settings and combining it with various methodological tools and community-based participatory framework, this study advances efforts towards data equity and more inclusive research practices.
Our venue selection may limit data relevance for immigrant communities, as it is based on Census data that often exclude or undercount these populations. To address this, we will work closely with community partners to ensure our analysis reflects the lived experiences of immigrant communities, particularly Asian immigrant women.
The restrictive US immigration climate may deter participation. To mitigate this, we will enhance our collaboration with community partners to identify safe, trusted venues or online spaces for potential participants.

## Introduction
 Invisibilisation is the process of marginalisation of a group of people, deeply rooted in structural inequity and social marginalisation, including the systems that erase, ignore and dismiss the existence, experiences and needs of populations. Examples include the historical erasure of indigenous people ; the cultural erasure by dominant majorities to shape the narratives and stereotypes of society; and exclusion from research and data. We define invisibilised populations as those excluded, under-represented or masked in data, such as migrants, transgender people, people with disabilities and incarcerated persons, or the lack of disaggregated data on certain populations. Invisibilisation leads to incomplete or biased findings that fail to document their unique experiences and challenges, further (re)producing inequity through exclusion from policies, interventions and resources. In this paper, we use the term ‘invisibilised’ rather than ‘invisible’, ‘hard-to-reach’, or ‘hidden’ to indicate that people do not conceal themselves willingly but rather are left out or constrained in participating in research.
Globally, immigrants’ experiences and needs are often invisibilised due to social, economic and legal factors. In the United States (US), Asian immigrants are frequently neglected in research despite being the fastest-growing racial/ethnic and undocumented group. At almost 20 million in the USA, the number of Asians has nearly tripled in the past three decades, 60% of whom are immigrants. Yet national sexual and reproductive health (SRH) estimates often lump Asians into an ‘Other’ category or exclude them completely. The lack of disaggregated data by ethnicity and other intersectional identities, such as legal status, sexual orientation or income, creates data inequity. This erasure obfuscates Asian women’s health needs and reinforces the model minority myth, which further excludes Asian immigrants from programmes and policies they may benefit from. Additionally, diverse languages and cultures and immigration histories create a challenging context to deliver high-quality SRH care.
Invisibilised populations pose unique research challenges due to undefined population sizes, unknown sampling boundaries and reluctance to engage in research under criminalising policies and in the face of fear. Studies highlight numerous challenges in sampling immigrants, particularly undocumented immigrants, given the lack of known sampling frames. Simulations and approximations using existing national surveys of undocumented status for Asians are problematic and produce more biased estimates than for Latino populations. Therefore, sampling methodologies using primary data collection are particularly critical for Asian immigrant populations.
Various techniques have been used to recruit invisibilised populations, including snowball sampling, respondent-driven sampling (RDS) and time-location sampling (TLS). In this protocol, we focus on starfish sampling, a novel hybrid approach that complements the limitations of TLS and RDS, where venues may be few or respondents’ social network sizes are small. Traditional TLS recruits participants during sampling events at randomly selected venue-date-time (VDT), which helps reduce selection and participation bias. However, TLS can be inefficient when venues are limited or attract only specific population subsets. Conversely, RDS leverages participants’ social networks, where initial recruits (seeds) invite peers from their network. While effective for populations difficult to reach through standard probability sampling, RDS often generates short or densely clustered referral chains, increasing seed dependence and bias risk. In starfish sampling, the TLS recruits participants at randomly selected VDTs based on mapped locations where the population is found. Subsequently, the RDS facilitates next wave recruitment from these seeds through peer referral. This hybrid method allows flexibility to continue TLS or switch to RDS alone if peer recruitment becomes robust.
RDS, a core component of starfish sampling, differs from snowball sampling in several key ways. Snowball sampling allows an unlimited number of initial recruits, whereas RDS limits them to a small number to reflect the population’s diversity. Moreover, snowball sampling allows participants to recruit as many peers as they wish, while RDS restricts each participant to recruiting only a limited number of peers. These differences enable RDS to achieve longer referral chains and samples independent of the initial participants. Therefore, snowball sampling is theorised to introduce substantial bias and analyses typically do not account for this bias. In contrast, RDS explicitly acknowledges potential bias and mitigates it through recruitment structure and statistical procedures. These procedures assess and adjust for biases from peer recruitment patterns and network size variations. During data collection, sample representativeness is evaluated by comparing observed sociodemographic characteristics with known population data such as the American Community Survey (ACS). After data collection, statistical adjustments account for the theorised biases, yielding estimates that approximate unbiased population parameters and, in turn, a more representative sample.
Starfish sampling represents an improvement over prior studies among immigrants, especially among undocumented immigrants, which relied on convenience samples or secondary data with biases in assumptions for Asian immigrants. Random venue selection and trusted locations mitigate TLS selection bias and enhance peer referral, thereby improving access for invisibilised populations. Furthermore, previous research found that undocumented Asians have limited social network sizes. This understanding of the social ties of undocumented Asians supports our starfish sampling approach, leveraging community network trust and short-chain peer referrals.
Starfish sampling has primarily been used in small populations within geographically defined regions (eg, transmen in San Francisco). Our multi-site cross-sectional study expands it to recruit Asian immigrant women with a wide range of immigration statuses, languages and ethnicities, spanning multiple geographic regions, and then applies justifiable criteria to narrow down to certain geographies and venues. We operationalise immigration statuses to include undocumented, DACA (Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals), individuals in liminal status, individuals with temporary status, legal permanent residents and naturalised citizens. This expanded and refined application of starfish sampling to more complex settings will be useful for researchers who are interested in implementing the methodology in a variety of contexts.

## Methods and analysis

## Overview of study design
The current study is part of the BRAVE (Building community, Raising All immigrant Voices for health Equity) study, an exploratory sequential mixed-method research that aims to understand the association between immigration status and SRH among Asian immigrant women in the USA. In Phase 1, we conducted qualitative interviews with Asian immigrant women and community experts, whose findings informed the development of a survey design, measures and recruitment for Phase II. In Phase II, we plan to recruit a multi-city sample of 2000 Asian immigrant women using the starfish sampling. The sample will participate in a survey designed to capture the association between their immigration history and SRH service utilisation.
Our study population consists of those who identify as female, aged 18–49 years, who identify as Asian, were born outside of the USA, and can provide informed consent. Additionally, these individuals must be able to participate in the survey in one of the five study languages: English, Chinese, Hindi, Korean or Vietnamese. We will recruit 2000 study participants from Los Angeles County, the Greater Houston area, the Atlanta Metropolitan area and New York City. These locations are part of states with diverse immigrant policy contexts and have the highest numbers of Asian and undocumented Asian immigrants. Together, these states are home to over 7 million Asian immigrants. While we will conduct recruiting events at randomly selected venue-day-time in the four locations, living in these cities is not an eligibility criterion for the study, given that the recruited participants may refer peers living outside these geographies. The detailed sampling strategy will be discussed in the following sections.
We will follow a community-based participatory framework (CBPR) throughout the study. CBPR is a research paradigm that combines knowledge and action to improve community health and health equity. Throughout the overarching BRAVE study, we collaborate closely with our community partners to develop study designs and methods, interpret findings and disseminate results. Especially for the starfish sampling in Phase II, the Community Advisory Board (CAB) evaluates study instruments to aid in sampling and ensure that survey measures are appropriate. Community partners also codirect all study research activities with academic partners. In particular, community study coordinators from WomanKind in New York City, NY, Community Resource Corporation from Atlanta, Georgia, and Boat People SOS in Houston, Texas, will participate in a series of training sessions for fieldwork, hire a team of multilingual community field officers and plan to conduct all research activities in the study sites. Community study coordinators and academic partners meet weekly from the four sites to collect input on establishing the sampling frame. Field officers based in each site are fluent in at least one of the five study languages and culturally competent, which also play a vital role in recruiting participants and survey administration.

## Community mapping and establishment of initial venue universe
The methodology for sampling involves community mapping, a tool used to stimulate and share knowledge of spatial and social determinants of health. This tool uses a systematic approach that leverages diverse datasets and community-based participation to establish a venue universe and identify key recruitment sites across our locations of interest. We will first conduct interviews and consult with community experts and community partners. Experts will contribute venue and area recommendations based on local knowledge, which will be cross-checked against our maps and venue lists. Furthermore, community partners will suggest sites frequented by populations of interest and validate the selected venues for their respective sites using their own knowledge and experiences. This collaborative approach ensures that community partners are actively involved, providing input at each step of the community mapping process to ensure comprehensiveness and cultural nuance.
Study site selection will occur at the census tract level to facilitate feasibility and ensure diversity of study participants across ethnicities and immigration statuses. To ensure a standardised and systematic site selection process, we will use a hierarchical approach, choosing geographic levels whose boundaries align with one another. The site selection process begins at a broad geographic level, narrowing down from state to counties, to Census County Divisions (CCDs), and finally to census tracts and venues. For information regarding geographic level site selections, see . We will use Tableau to map each location at every geographic level and Python for data cleaning, merging and calculations for census tract selection. ACS 5-year estimates will serve as a foundation for this process by obtaining community characteristics within the study site boundaries, including percentage of Asian population by ethnicity, median household income and total foreign-born population. The process of selecting counties and CCDs will use these variables and community partner input. We will present partners with counties and CCDs that we believe will yield the desired populations based on Census data. Community partners will contribute their insight on CCDs and counties with large and diverse Asian populations that fit our study populations, and we will work together to determine final selections. The ethnic and socioeconomic composition will be carefully considered, with an emphasis on ensuring representation of the primary ethnic groups of interest and coverage of diverse neighbourhoods.
 Following the selection of counties and CCDs, census tracts and venues will be chosen using a standardised process by identifying tracts with the highest total Asian population and high venue density. Rankings will be created based on the total Asian population and the number of venues within each tract, and these rankings will be combined to generate individual tract scores. To ensure proportional representation, the percentage of the Asian population each CCD contributes (relative to selected CCDs and tracts containing venues) will be multiplied by the number of tracts in the CCD to determine N, the number of tracts to include. This method ensures that CCDs with larger Asian populations are allocated a greater number of tracts. All venues within the selected tracts will be included in the final venue universe, and researchers will manually identify and fill in any gaps. Community partners at each study site will decide on the final census tracts to include, which may involve removing or including additional census tracts that were not captured.
The venue universe will be generated with data scraping, a process of obtaining data from online sources using a computer algorithm or programme. Data scraping will be conducted in two stages. The first stage will create an exhaustive list of venues that serve as important ‘activity areas’ to the population of interest across the various study sites. In the second stage, we will validate the venue universe by cross-referencing the list generated in the first stage and the list suggested by community partners, determining which venues will be included or excluded. This multi-stage data scraping approach ensures that the venue universe accurately reflects where populations of interest gather.
The information for each venue will be data scraped from Yelp (yelp.com) for private businesses and GuideStar (guidestar.org) for community-based organisations (CBOs) using keywords to identify venues that may be frequented by our population of interest (eg, Asian grocery stores). Corresponding busy times of venues will be data scraped from Google (google.com), which will assist in constructing our sampling calendar at a later stage.

## Identification of venues and locations
Primary data collection will involve in-person recruitment at randomly selected venues from our established venue universe, as well as community engagement events. Based on the recommendation of study partners, recruitment will occur over multiple waves centred around relevant times of the year (eg, Lunar New Year, Asian American Pacific Island Heritage Month, Mid-Autumn Festival, Diwali), with the aim of enrolling eligible participants representing a wide range of immigration statuses and ethnic backgrounds. The recruitment protocol will be applied across all participating sites and will be structured around a sampling calendar stratified by CCD and venue type. Venues will be divided into four strata to ensure equal representation across the various types: religious venues, CBOs, grocery stores and stores (eg, Asian beauty stores), and restaurants and bars. For each week in the sampling calendar, a CCD will be randomly selected, followed by a random venue within that CCD in each stratum, totalling around four venues per week. One-off community events will serve as ‘wild cards’ (eg, Lunar New Year festivals, Citizenship workshops and Community events) and will be added sparingly to the sampling calendar at the discretion of the research team and community partners. To ensure successful recruitment events in the event of unforeseen logistical challenges such as unsuccessful venue vetting or low traffic, two alternate venues will be identified for each primary venue. These alternate venues will be within the same CCD and strata as the primary venue.
Sampling calendars will be established with practical considerations in mind, such as the proximity of alternate venues to primary ones, safety and the ability to recruit at certain venues. Time cut-offs will be set for the winter and fall months to ensure researcher safety and partner concerns will be acknowledged regarding unsafe or non-viable venues. The research team will vet venues prior to recruitment to confirm that venues meet established criteria and are likely to yield productive engagement. Recruitment schedules and procedures will be revised as necessary, including rescheduling recruitment waves in line with community calendars and partner feedback. This approach will allow the research team to meet recruitment goals while also maintaining flexibility to address challenges as they arise.

## Venue-based recruiting procedures with community partners
We will recruit a minimum of four field researchers at each study location to aid in recruitment. The selection of field researchers will consider the survey languages and linguistic needs of the communities being served at each study site. Language proficiency data from the ACS 2022 1 year estimates will be used to determine English proficiency levels and the prevalence of Asian languages in the selected counties at each location. In addition, we will gather feedback from community partners to make sure the appropriate representation of languages and backgrounds among field researchers. As a result, the field researchers will be fluent in a variety of languages, including Korean, Chinese (Mandarin and Cantonese), Hindi, Gujarati, Bengali and Vietnamese, and possess diverse backgrounds and experiences. This approach ensures that recruitment is culturally appropriate and accessible for participants from the populations of interest.
The venue-based recruitment process will follow a protocol that includes enumeration, approaching and intercepting (ie, being able to stop and speak to) potential participants and guiding them through the eligibility screening and survey completion process. Enumeration involves the identification of a designated area at the venue from which all potentially eligible participants will be counted and approached. This serves as the primary process for identifying the productivity of a sampling event. Recruitment activity will not be restricted to the selected venue; the venue will be considered a ‘centre point’ of recruitment activity, and researchers may recruit in venues within two blocks of the selected venue if they exhibit higher foot traffic and/or our study population. This will allow for more flexibility and productivity in recruitment events. However, enumeration areas must be redrawn and recounted in these instances.
Researchers will approach and intercept potential participants who enter the enumeration area, provide them with information about the study, and ask them to complete an eligibility screener. If individuals meet the eligibility criteria, they will be encouraged to participate in the survey. Participants will be informed about the various options for survey completion, including on-site participation, online surveys to be completed at their convenience later, in-person surveys conducted by researchers at study site offices, or phone surveys conducted by researchers. Eligible individuals who would like to take the survey with a researcher will be able to schedule an appointment at the venue or by reaching out to the research team later. To ensure that participants can access the screener later if they do not complete it at the venue, each potentially eligible participant approached will be given a flyer containing a Quick Response (QR) code to the eligibility screener and a unique venue code. The QR code provides access to the eligibility screener, while the venue code allows the research team to track the source of recruitment and ensure that the data are accurately linked to the appropriate sampling event. In return for their time, we will provide a US$40 gift card on completion of the survey.

## Peer-referral procedures
Study participants will have the opportunity to refer up to three peers in their network, which may include family, friends, colleagues and acquaintances. To incentivise participation, participants will receive a US$10 gift card for each peer who completes the survey. At the end of the survey, participants will be offered the option to refer peers to the study. If they opt for it, they will receive three personalised referral links. In addition to the online process, physical referral cards will be distributed to participants who take the eligibility screener and/or survey in person to broaden the number of potential referrals.
Participants will manage the distribution of their referrals and will have the choice to either hand out physical cards or forward the digital referral links provided by the research team. Referrals who receive digital links can independently begin the eligibility screening process. Those who receive physical cards will be instructed to contact the research team. The research team will identify who referred whom and send each referral a personalised link for eligibility screening.

## Survey measures
The survey covers several topics to measure primary exposures (immigration history) and outcomes (SRH service utilisation). To assess potential mediators or moderators of this association, we include questions on psychosocial threats (eg, experiences of discrimination or mental health), access to material resources (eg, housing and food insecurity), health insurance and social capital. During the survey development process, we incorporate diverse perspectives and voices from the community. First, we gather input through interviews with immigrant community leaders, legal experts, advocates and service providers at both the study sites and the national level. Second, we conduct cognitive interviews by recruiting participants through venue-based and peer referral strategies. These interviews involve asking participants the key survey questions and probing whether they encountered difficulties in answering, the reasons for their responses, the relevance of the questions and their suggestions for improvements. Third, community study coordinators and field officers test the surveys multiple times, offering feedback on survey flow, as well as culturally sensitive and appropriate translations.

## Analysis plan and sample size calculations
The primary outcome is recent SRH care use, measured as a binary variable. We will compare SRH care use across four immigration status groups (N=500 per group) using both unadjusted and adjusted multivariable models. Since the proportions of SRH care use in our study population are unknown, we based our assumptions on a previous study that reported an SRH care use proportion of 0.62 among Asian immigrants. To remain conservative, we assumed proportions close to 0.5, where variance is highest. We powered the study to detect a difference in proportions of 0.1 (eg, 0.45 vs 0.55), representing a small effect size (Cohen’s h=0.2). Sample size calculations for unadjusted comparisons indicated a need for 391 participants per group. To account for SE inflation in multivariable models (20%) and potential sample loss due to missing covariates (5%), we inflated this estimate by 25%, resulting in a requirement of 489 participants per group. Thus, a sample size of 500 per group (2000 total) should provide adequate power (80%) for a two-sided alpha of 0.05. This ensures sufficient power for both unadjusted and multivariable analyses.

## Quality assurance strategies

## Standardisation of procedures
To ensure accurate, standard and consistent sampling methodology across multiple study sites, we will develop and distribute the standard operational procedure, step-by-step guides and protocols for every step of the starfish sampling process. Study team members and coprincipal investigators will critically review these documents through multiple iterations. For field personnel across the four study sites, the study team will conduct extensive 3-day in-person training, multiple virtual training sessions and virtual information sessions to make sure that all site staff are well aware of and compliant with the standard procedure of starfish sampling. In addition, during multiple rounds of pilot fieldwork, we will identify and implement any necessary amendments to the sampling protocol.

## Data quality
The starfish sampling will continue until the sample size of 2000 is reached. We will monitor the data closely for the desired sample composition across different immigration statuses and add additional seeds through diverse venues (eg, from ethnic-immigrant-serving organisations to adequately recruit specific ethnic participants) depending on the type of respondent needed. Further, we may change the maximum number of referrals based on the characteristics of the seeds.
To ensure the quality of survey data, we will assess for duplicate participation or peer referrals by tracking basic demographic characteristics in eligibility screening responses. Further, we will conduct postvalidation checks of participants by using a series of logic edits to confirm that responses are consistent (eg, those with DACA status should meet DACA eligibility requirements). Participants who fail these checks may be excluded from the dataset. For the peer referral process, we will develop a tracking system and database to ensure accurate tracking of peer referrals and distribution of incentives. Each referral link will be uniquely associated with the participant and can only be completed once. Once a referral link is used, the research team will manually verify eligibility and confirm if the referred individual completed the survey, allowing the team to track each referral’s progress effectively.

## Data management and confidentiality
Eligibility screening, survey administration and peer referrals will use the Qualtrics online survey platform, Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) compliant and approved by the study site’s (University of California Los Angeles) Institutional Review Board. The data stored at Qualtrics will only be accessed by the BRAVE team researchers. Each participant will be assigned a unique, anonymised identification number that will be used for all data forms. Peer referrals will not disclose any information on the immigration status or any other personal information of those they are recruiting. The Certificate of Confidentiality, granted through the National Institute of Health, will also protect study participants by prohibiting disclosure of identifiable, sensitive information. 

## Patient and public involvement
This study is conducted in close partnership with CAB and community partners, composed of immigrant-led and reproductive justice groups. CAB members and community partners have been actively involved throughout the research process, from developing research questions to interpreting findings, to help align the study with real-world community needs. For the starfish sampling specifically, they contributed to reviewing community mapping, venue selection, hiring field officers, sampling procedures, survey instruments, survey translations, outreach efforts and recruitment strategies.

## Ethics and dissemination

## Ethical considerations
The overarching BRAVE study protocol was approved by the University of California Los Angeles Institutional Review Board (IRB) (IRB-22–0493-AM-016). All study team members, including field officers from each site, were added to the IRB and completed Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI) Human Subjects Research Training and HIPAA Training from the University of California Los Angeles.
Survey participants will be provided with the written informed consent form in their preferred languages after completing the eligibility screening and being determined eligible. For participants opting for a phone or in-person survey, field officers will obtain verbal consent by providing a shortened version of the informed consent. Regardless of the survey modality, all participants will have the option to retain the informed consent form in their preferred language.
Additionally, we will take every precaution to minimise risks to participants. Potential risks and harm to participants include discomfort in reporting sensitive topics such as immigration status. To address this, we collaborate closely with community partners to ensure that immigration status questions are asked appropriately. During the survey administration, participants may also experience discomfort when answering questions about SRH, prior experience with psychosocial threats or mental health. To support participants, we will provide resource guides on intimate partner violence, suicidality, mental health and other social support for all survey participants. Further, we have developed a standard protocol to address situations where a participant presents an imminent risk of suicidality. Specifically, the study team will provide all participants of the online self-administered survey with a range of resources following the mental health survey questions. These resources will include mental health support, sexual and domestic violence assistance, health and medical services, as well as general resources such as hotlines and services tailored to Asian immigrant women. In cases where field officers identify signs of imminent suicidality risk during in-person or phone survey administration, they will promptly notify study team clinicians. The clinician will then assess the situation, evaluate the level of lethality or imminent danger, and develop an appropriate treatment plan.
Additionally, we have developed a safety manual for field officers to use during the fieldwork. All research staff are required to report incidents, including protocol deviations, violations and adverse events, that occur during starfish sampling events or survey administration. For timely reporting, we created an adverse event report form that must be completed within 24 hours of the incident and submitted to the IRB within 48 hours.

## Reporting and dissemination
The results will be disseminated through scientific conferences and academic journal publications. Further, relevant data will be shared with our community partners. The findings will be translated into multiple languages and distributed via traditional local and national media, various social media channels, policy briefs and congressional briefings, leveraging the expertise of our community partners. This diverse range of dissemination outlets will ensure the study findings gain both local and national visibility, making them accessible to key stakeholders committed to improving health equity among Asian immigrants.

## Discussion
This paper outlines the protocol for sampling an invisibilised population—Asian immigrant women. We will use a novel starfish sampling method that combines the strengths of TLS with trusted social ties through RDS to recruit participants. Our study is unique in that we adapt and refine starfish sampling for more complex settings to recruit Asian immigrant women across diverse countries of origin, languages spoken and immigration statuses across multiple cities. In addition to starfish sampling, various datasets, tools and frameworks from a diverse array of fields will be used to ensure a responsive sampling framework. ACS 5-year estimates, data scraping, community mapping, data disaggregation and the CBPR approach, in conjunction with one another, will create a holistic and diverse sampling frame for starfish sampling. The combination of these methods was intentionally designed to leverage the strengths of quantitative data and community engagement in addressing the lack of disaggregated data and data inequity.
The primary advantage of this holistic approach is its ability to systematically connect with invisibilised populations. The ACS 5-year estimates will serve as the primary dataset, providing a robust baseline of geographic boundaries, demographic trends and socio-economic patterns, while the data scraped universe of venues will supplement it in providing characteristics of important ‘activity areas’ to Asian immigrant women. Community mapping will create a visual representation of the collected data, offering spatial visualisation of key social and geographic factors while starfish sampling will improve outreach to invisibilised populations by leveraging established social networks through RDS and minimising participation bias through TLS. A strength of starfish sampling is that it is adaptable to recruitment challenges. For example, the numbers and types of venues can be chosen based on populations needed to ensure data disaggregation, such as ethnicities.
Data disaggregation and CBPR also played a crucial role in refining and contextualising insights obtained from big data analytics (ACS 5-year estimates and data scraping). Breaking down data by key sociodemographic variables, such as ethnicity, we were able to optimise our selection of key geographies within the study sites and identify hidden trends that are not present in aggregate data analysis. Furthermore, employing CBPR throughout the entire sampling process allowed us to validate and interpret findings within big data analytics. Community partners’ knowledge and insights guided us in standardising our operating and sampling procedures to better reflect the different political and societal realities across each study site. This study is innovative in that we involved a multi-state team of community partners and academic researchers to ensure that the protocol is feasible and rigorous. Our study team is a strength of the study as we have community partners who are part of the data collection team and facilitate timely feedback on potential challenges that may occur. We will rely on the CBPR approach throughout the study as described in our protocol to ensure that communities are included in each step of the recruitment process in order to facilitate trust, relevance of data and proper dissemination to communities.
The integration of these methods strengthens the study’s potential to generate actionable insights and reach invisibilised populations. By combining starfish sampling with big data analytics and community-driven methodologies, the study offers a rigorous yet flexible model that aligns with academia’s systematic methodologies but is responsive to community partners’ on-the-ground realities. This is especially relevant when sampling ‘hidden’ and ‘hard-to-reach’ communities, where understanding population-level trends requires diverse and complementary data sources, along with collaboration with trusted partners, due to their exclusion from traditional datasets.

## Feasibility and cost consideration
While the starfish sampling method is feasible, it may be costly and labour and time intensive. The venue-based component of the method will present the greatest operational and financial burden, due to the need for at least three to four field officers per site, extensive travel and coordinators to oversee recruitment operations and venues. As a result, staffing and transportation will be the major sources of costs, with additional expenses accrued through printing materials, field officer training and other recruitment fees. To improve feasibility, this approach can be adapted by narrowing the scope of venue locations and staggering recruitment events. These adjustments will optimise staff coverage while reducing travel costs and the number of field officers required. Additionally, to ensure feasibility across sites, the research team will work closely with each study site to address site-specific challenges and make necessary adjustments.

## Limitation and mitigation strategies
Despite these strengths, there are potential challenges and limitations to this sampling frame. First, this study relies on attending venues, which may pose challenges. Certain venues may not give the team permission to recruit at their venues. Factors such as weather, time of year and special events may affect foot traffic and potential participant pool at the time of visitation. As venues are added to sampling calendars based on their Google busy times and dates, these busy times and dates may not necessarily indicate high foot traffic or a productive recruitment event. Additionally, not all venues may have busy times and dates available on Google—these venues will be omitted from sampling calendars until researchers are able to confirm busy times and dates with the businesses through direct contact. Further, even if our venues are selected from census tracts with a sizeable Asian population or have Asian owners, their clientele may not match our study population. Therefore, to optimise each recruitment event, our researchers will use online platforms, such as Yelp, and local knowledge to gather information on selected venues beforehand. We will attempt to gather information on approvals necessary and work with venues to obtain permission. Additionally, all venues will have two alternate venues to attend in cases where permission to recruit at the original venue is denied.
Second, maintaining data accuracy may present some difficulties, given our survey administration and peer referral process take place online. Researchers cannot directly oversee participation or verify authenticity. This limitation makes it more challenging to detect duplicate or fraudulent participants and referral chains compared with in-person studies. However, we will implement various quality monitoring techniques to maintain data integrity and reliability. For example, we will assign unique online survey links to each participant to prevent duplicate submissions, integrate re-Completely Automated Public Turing test to tell Computers and Humans Apart (reCAPTCHA) within the survey to detect bots and develop postsurvey quality assessment criteria. Additionally, we will closely monitor patterns in peer referral chains to identify and mitigate potential data contamination.
Third, using the ACS 5-year estimates, which traditionally exclude and undercount immigrant populations, specifically those with liminal status or who are undocumented, for our site selection may also result in a lack of data relevance for immigrant communities. CBPR, data disaggregation and starfish sampling are mechanisms that we will employ throughout the sampling frame to ensure that our analysis of the Census data reflects the on-the-ground experiences of immigrant communities and addresses the under-representation of Asian immigrant women in the Census data. We will collaborate with community partners to disaggregate collected datasets in a culturally sensitive and nuanced manner, ensuring more effective mapping and outreach strategies.
Fourth, the restrictive immigration climate in the US might limit potential participant participation. We may have lower than anticipated survey participants due to potential participants’ fear of taking part in the survey. Furthermore, the increased presence of Immigration and Customs Enforcement within the study sites after the 2025 presidential inauguration might discourage potential participants from going to venues they would typically frequent or might lead them to visit venues during off-peak hours. Research suggests the chilling effects that mass deportation threats have on immigrant health. We will consult with community partners to identify and purposefully choose venues or online spaces that provide safe spaces for potential participants.
Finally, while starfish sampling strengthens the methodological rigour of existing approaches, we acknowledge that no established statistical method for weighting has yet been developed. Future research should develop statistical procedures to account for differences in the likelihood of individuals being intercepted or referred, or to incorporate variation in venue attendance, network size and recruitment pathways. Moving forward, we will focus on developing statistical procedures tailored to the unique features of the starfish sampling method.
