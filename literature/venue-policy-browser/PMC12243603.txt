MollaeiMina JavazmAmirreza Rafiei MetanatFateme ShirozhanShima https://orcid.org/0000-0001-8132-7416MotieMahdieh This ageing trend is occurring globally. Ageing is characterised by a decline in both physical and mental capabilities, the development of chronic illnesses and functional limits brought on by ageing. Consequently, the WHO defines healthy ageing as the process of maintaining and enhancing an individual’s functional abilities. The management of the ageing process is widely recognised as a major public health concern, presenting significant challenges in healthcare, economic stability and social services. 
As a result, the ageing population poses problems for the health system and the older persons. These problems include financial difficulties, psychological issues like Alzheimer’s, physical issues like chronic diseases and the difficulty of preserving the independence and dignity of older adults. Consequently, in response to these challenges, the WHO proposed a strategy known as community-based rehabilitation (CBR) in 1978, following the Alma-Ata Declaration. This initiative aimed to address the global demand for rehabilitation services within the framework of medical care. Rehabilitation is a crucial component of universal healthcare and a key strategy for achieving Sustainable Development Goal 3: ‘Good health and well-being for all at all ages,’ according to the WHO. CBR is one of the types of rehabilitation services that is a simple and broad way for the well-being of older adults. The CBR model has been implemented in over 90 countries as an effective approach to delivering rehabilitation services. However, only about 1% of older individuals have access to timely and comprehensive rehabilitation services in their communities. 
CBR interventions offer numerous benefits for both the older adults and the community. These benefits include effective physical preparation, the maintenance of the older adult’s independence, positive social interactions and reduced healthcare costs. Additionally, CBR can enhance activities of daily living performance and improve the quality of life by encouraging participation in society. It also helps reduce social isolation, provides more affordable services compared with home healthcare nursing and shortens hospital stays. However, there are some drawbacks to CBR. These include a lack of regulations, insufficient medical resources, a shortage of medical staff and a limited number of available programmes. 
According to the studies, CBR has been implemented in public spaces, taking into account the target population, the types of disabilities present, the study location and the epidemiology of disabilities. Nevertheless, here are instances where the required research is not conducted. Given the increasing ageing population, there is a growing need for further research in this area. 
Some studies have indicated that CBR can be an effective approach for rehabilitating certain common diseases in older adults. For instance, in heart failure patients, CBR therapies can improve psychosocial functioning and physical function (such as muscle strength). It is essential to gather the available evidence on the effectiveness of CBR programmes for older people, considering the presence or absence of specific diseases and disabilities in this age group. Although multiple studies have explored the importance of CBR and the challenges associated with its implementation, thorough evidence of its impact on the healthcare system is needed before it can be prioritised on the policymakers’ funding agenda. Findings suggest that CBR approaches can address the specific rehabilitation needs of older adults. Therefore, this scoping review aims to explore the application of CBR approaches for the older adult population.

## Method

## Study design
This scoping review will be conducted following the methodological framework outlined by Arksey and O'Malley. This scoping review aims to explore the application of CBR approaches for the older adult population. This protocol is designed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items (PRISMA) for Scoping Reviews guidelines. The research question guiding this review is:
How are CBR programmes for older adults designed, implemented and evaluated across different settings, and what evidence exists regarding their outcomes, challenges and areas needing further research?

## Objectives
Therefore, this scoping review aims to explore the application of CBR approaches for the older adult population. Specifically, it seeks to describe the main features of CBR programmes designed for older adults, examine how and where these programmes are implemented and identify the key facilitators and barriers influencing their delivery. Additionally, the review aims to summarise the reported outcomes and evaluation methods used in existing studies and to highlight gaps in the current evidence base to inform future research and practice.

## Information sources and search strategy
A comprehensive search strategy was developed in consultation with a research librarian. The search targets several academic databases, including PubMed, ScienceDirect, Embase, CINAHL and Web of Science. Additionally, Google Scholar will also incorporate as a search engine to identify relevant literature. The search strategy will include relevant terms such as ‘community-based rehabilitation,’ ‘community-based rehabilitation,’ ‘aged,’ ‘older,’ ‘elder’ and ‘geriatric,’ using asterisks (*) for truncation where appropriate ( ).
 

## Study selection
All retrieved records will be imported into EndNote and deduplicated (automatic+manual check). Screening will occur in two successive phases, both of which will be double-screened; that is, two reviewers will independently assess every record:
Title-and-abstract screening: two reviewers will screen all records against the eligibility criteria.
Full-text screening: the same two reviewers will assess the full texts of potentially relevant studies.
Any disagreement at either stage will first be discussed; persisting conflicts will be adjudicated by a third senior reviewer. The entire selection process will be presented in a PRISMA flow diagram.

## Data extraction
A standardised data extraction form based on the review questions and the eligibility criteria will be developed to capture relevant information from the included studies. This form, adopted from the Joanna Briggs Institute’s template for data extraction (see ), will be used. It will be pilot-tested on a subset of studies to ensure consistency and comprehensiveness. Once refined, it will be used to extract data systematically from all included studies.
 

## Data analysis and synthesis
Quantitative variables (eg, sample size, intervention duration, outcome scores) will be summarised with descriptive statistics (medians±interquartile range or means±SD, and simple counts/percentages). Qualitative findings that report programme features, antecedents, facilitators, barriers or other relevant factors will be transferred verbatim from each study’s results section into a structured Excel sheet, arranged under predefined display headings, and presented in the synthesis as a consolidated list of factors.

## Ethical consideration
Since this study is a secondary research paper, ethical approval is unnecessary. However, to ensure adherence to ethical considerations, the current study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Social Welfare and Rehabilitation Sciences (code:IR.USWR.REC.1404.034). The results will be published in a scientific journal through a peer-reviewed publication.

## Patient and public involvement
Although patients and members of the public were not involved in the design or conduct of this study, the findings are expected to be of direct benefit to the older adult population and their caregivers. We are committed to disseminating the results broadly, including through community networks, public health channels and other accessible formats to ensure that the knowledge generated reaches and benefits the target population.

## Discussion
This paper describes a protocol for a scoping review of CBR services tailored to the older adult population. To our knowledge, this is the first scoping review that aims to gather the existing knowledge on CBR services in the older adult target group, examine the positive and negative aspects of these interventions and explore the neglected issues and challenges in CBR for this vulnerable demographic. The importance of paying attention to CBR services, especially for older persons, is highlighted due to the significant growth of this population worldwide. This age group faces various challenges, including chronic diseases, geriatric syndromes and a gradual decline in vital capacities, all of which can impact their quality of life. Therefore, paying attention to and expanding CBR programmes seems to be a suitable and crucial strategy to effectively respond to older individuals’ rehabilitation and empowerment needs. 
However, there is limited evidence to guide healthcare providers and policymakers on CBR services and facilities. The results of this scoping review aim to serve as a roadmap for governments and healthcare systems on how to meet the growing demands associated with the healthcare and rehabilitation needs of older individuals. Therefore, the insights derived from this study can be used in policy-making health and rehabilitation to develop and implement a comprehensive and effective CBR service model for older people. These results can also guide rehabilitation field researchers in planning and designing future CBR interventions to meet the vulnerable older population’s broad physical, psychological and social needs. Ultimately, we anticipate that the findings of this review will pave the way for further clinical research, ensuring that we continue to respond to the evolving needs of our ageing population with effectiveness.
