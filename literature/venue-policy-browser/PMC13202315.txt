A test of nonconsumptive effects on sika deer by an omnivorous Asiatic black bear

In enhancing biological fitness, prey animals must develop antipredator behavior, such as spatiotemporal avoidance, escaping, or defense against predators, to reduce predation risk ( ; ; ). The nonconsumptive effects (NCEs, i.e., the effects of predation risk) by predators and the antipredator responses by prey, which is known as the concept of “landscape of fear,” have rapidly grown interests in wildlife ecology and conservation for the past decades ( ; ; ; , ; ). However, substantial biases for research focus are identified, including species (predator and prey), regions, methods, and metrics on the risk effects evaluated previously, and the methodological frameworks remain to be developed ( ; ). In terrestrial wildlife, gray wolves (Canis lupus), which primarily prey on cervid in North America, are commonly reported, followed by large felids (e.g., lion, Panthera leo) with various prey species in Africa, whereas observations on predators in other taxa and regions are limited ( ; ; ). NCEs are complex and context dependent with regard to hunting modes of predators, escape modes by prey, as well as habitat structures ( ; ; , ). Wide-ranging knowledge from various taxa and regions will provide a comprehensive understanding of NCEs.
The Asiatic black bear (Ursus thibetanus) is not only widely distributed in the eastern parts of the Eurasian Continent, from Far East to Middle East, but also normally distributed in Honshu and Shikoku of Japan; however, it is extinct from Kyushu ( ). This bear species is the only large carnivore in the regions because the Japanese wolf (Canis lupus hodophilax) went extinct more than a century ago. Asiatic black bear is a typical omnivore, which widely consumes vegetative (e.g., herbs, fruits, and nuts) and animal (e.g., colonial insects) materials ( , ; ; ). Black bear also consumes wild ungulates, such as sika deer (Cervus nippon), primarily by scavenging carcasses ( ) but occasionally by predating living animals such as neonates and adults ( ; ; ; unpublished personal observations by authors). Although previous dietary reports demonstrated that Asiatic black bear is not a major predator of wild ungulates (e.g., <10 % of dietary contributions revealed by staple isotope analysis; ), their interspecific interactions have not been assessed empirically.
Cases to assess the NCEs by bears on wild ungulates were limited, specifically for black bears ( ). demonstrated that the moose (Alces alces) exhibited antipredator responses to the olfactory cue (i.e., feces) of American black bear (Ursus americanus), which is similar in trophic niche to Asiatic black bear, that is, omnivore with occasional predation on ungulate neonates ( ; ). On the contrary, reported results that the black-tailed deer (Odocoileus hemionus sitkensis) exhibited no antipredator responses to the urine of black bear. However, whether wild ungulates exhibit antipredator responses to bear species, other than brown and American black bears, remains unclear ( ; ; ). Furthermore, previous studies commonly used a quasi-experimental approach using predator’s olfactory cue (i.e., urine) but not yet directly observed the NCEs of bears.
Thus, this study aimed to elucidate the NCEs of Asiatic black bear on sika deer using camera trapping. Camera trapping is a noninvasive approach with high priority in cost-effectiveness, applying ecological and behavioral studies as well as conservation and management practices on various taxa ( ; ; ). The methods have also become popular in mammalian predator–prey studies ( ; ; ; ). If sika deer recognize threats of presence of black bear, then the predation risk created by bears will provoke antipredator behavioral responses by the deer, such as spatial/temporal partitioning, increasing vigilances, and spatiotemporal avoidance. Furthermore, based on their feeding habits, the black bear threatens the neonates more than the adult deer ( ; ). Adult female deer accompanied with a calf typically exhibits an antipredator response, whereas adult males are less sensitive to predator’s presence ( ; ). Therefore, if the NCEs by black bear is present, then the antipredator responses will be more significant in female deer than in males.

## Materials and methods

## Study area
Camera trapping was conducted in Minami-Aizu, Fukushima Prefecture, northern Honshu Island of Japan ( ). The studied area (ca. 30 km2) is an agroforest landscape interspersed with settlements, rice-paddy fields, secondary deciduous forests (genera Fagus and Quercus), and coniferous plantations (Chamaecyparis obtusa, Cryptomeria japonica, and Pinus densiflora). The climate is cool temperate, with a mean temperature ranging from ca. −9.0 °C (in January) to 31.0 °C (in August) and annual precipitation of 1,000–1,250 mm for the studied period (observed at the Tajima meteorological station of Japan Meteorological Agency: https://www.data.jma.go.jp/stats/etrn/index.php). In general, the study area has a heavy snow fall with a maximum snow depth of ca. 3.0 m. Both sika deer and Asiatic black bear range across the study area with their stable populations ( , ; , ). Density of sika deer was estimated at 15–30 deer/km2, affecting significant degradations of understory vegetations by the browsing pressures ( ; ). Although there were no scientific estimations on bear abundances, the typical home range sizes of bears are 20–100 km2 estimated by 100% minimum convex polygon methods (e.g., ; ). They are typically less territorial, potentially overlapping the home ranges among several bears in our studied area.
 

## Camera trapping
Ten camera traps were installed in forests (574–725 m above sea level) for 3 studied years, 1.0–2.0 km apart between the nearest 2 camera stations ( ). We adopted deliberately biased placement that cameras located on animal trails or footpaths to maximize animals’ detections ( ). The mean and standard deviation of straight distances between 2 closest cameras each were 1.72 and 0.35 km, respectively (estimated from coordinate records at each camera station in field device of eTrex 32×, Garmin Inc.). Passive infrared cameras (TrophyCam 24 MP, Bushnell Inc.) with a trigger speed of 0.3 s were used, which were programmed to take 1-min video clips during detection, with a 5-min delay. The cameras were mounted on trees 1.0–1.2 m above ground level, without baits or lures, at an appropriate distance (5–15 m) from an animal trail/footpath and angle to capture the whole body of large animals (if needed, when a camera site was on a slope). The study period covered summer and autumn seasons (i.e., nonhibernating seasons of black bear) for 3 years, that is, from August 10 to November 23 in 2021, from June 20 to November 22 in 2022, and from October 6 to December 4 in 2023. Batteries were changed, and memory cards were replaced every month for the studied durations. Generally, antler casting in adult and subadult males of sika deer is from March to May, antler regrowth starts within a week and, then, completes for ca. a month after the casting ( ). Fawning season of sika deer is typically from May to July and breeding (i.e., rutting) season is typically in autumn in Japan ( ; , ). Therefore, our sampled periods covered the breeding season of sika deer in each studied year.
After the field survey, the animal species in the video clips were identified (if possible), and the date and time (24:00-h unit) of capture were recorded. Videos that had captured black bear and sika deer were compiled. Consequently, we found that Asiatic black bears were more frequently detected across the whole studied area in 2022 than in the remaining 2 years (i.e., 2021 and 2023; ). Therefore, we divided the datasets into 2 periods, that is, bear-active (2022) and bear-inactive (2021 and 2023) years, to compare the behavioral responses by sika deer. The abundant masting of Fagaceae acorns in 2022 ( ) might be attributed to the increase of bear detections caused by concentrating their uses of Fagaceae forest patches surrounding our camera stations ( ). Furthermore, to test our hypothesis that the antipredator responses by sika deer might vary by sex, we divided the video clips of sika deer captured into females and males by identifying the presence of antlers. Our sampled periods were out of the antler casting season, enabling to identify sexual differences from videos. Videos in which the sex was difficult to identify were removed from our datasets for further analyses.
 

## Spatial cooccurrence and temporal activity analyses
The relative abundance index (RAI) was estimated at each camera-trapping station and the studied year as the total number of animal detections per 100 camera-trapping days ( ). During estimation, 2 or more consecutive images of the same species within 30 min at a station were treated as a single detection event ( ) and the latter detections removed from the datasets for this analysis. In analyzing the patterns in spatial cooccurrences between black bears and sika deer, the coefficients of Spearman’s correlation (rs) between bear and deer RAIs (n = 30 for each sex) were estimated using R ver. 4.3.1 ( ) and the base package performed under R-Studio ver.2023.09.1.
In analyzing temporal activity overlaps between bear and deer, Dhat as the coefficient of temporal overlap was estimated using kernel density interference ( ), which ranges from 0 (complete separation) to 1 (complete overlap). The data for the time of day (0:00–23:59) that the animal images were captured (i.e., the beginning times of video clips) were transformed to circular data (i.e., 0–2π) before the analysis. The latter detections of 2 or more consecutive images of the same species within 30 min were removed as same as the RAI estimations. Dhat was estimated separately between the 2 periods defined above, that is, the bear-active and -inactive years. In assessing the reliability of the estimated Dhat, a smoothed bootstrap test was performed using 10,000 simulated samples to calculate the 95% confidence interval (CI) ( ). By analyzing the procedures, Dhat1 was adopted for datasets containing <50 samples of either species, whereas Dhat4 was adopted for datasets containing >50 samples of both species, in accordance with the criteria given by . Furthermore, Watson’s 2-sample test was performed for circular data to analyze statistically significant differences between bear and deer activity patterns. In this method, the null hypothesis that the patterns in 2 activities homogenize was assessed with P = 0.05 as a critical value. For these analyses, “overlap” ver. 0.3.9 ( ) and “circular” ver. 0.5-0 packages ( ) in R were used.

## Vigilant behavior analysis
During antipredator behavioral analysis, all video clips that captured male and female deer were screened in accordance with the criteria suggested by previous studies ( ; ; ): 1) the whole body of the animal was recorded in a frame; 2) the animal was at an observed venue, ca. 5–15 m from the camera; 3) the sex of the animal could be identified; and 4) the animal was recorded for ≥20 s (i.e., to determine whether the animal is just passing in front of the camera or staying at the observed venue). When an animal was observed in 2 video sequences less than 6 min apart (i.e., a second video was recorded soon after the 5-min delay from the first video by the same camera), it was considered as a single visitation by the same individual. The time length (in seconds) of vigilant behaviors observed in all screened video clips was recorded. Vigilant behavior was defined in accordance with previous studies, that is, deer scans the surroundings with its head raised above the shoulders, sometimes moving the ears ( ; ). We then estimated the proportional duration of vigilant behavior observed (%) by the time length of the behavior observed (seconds) per total length of a video clip. When several animals were observed in a video frame, the behavior of each individual was recorded. Finally, the different identification codes (using strings, e.g., id.001) on all observation events of video clips that recorded deer behavior were encoded.
In assessing the effects of bear activities on deer vigilance, zero-inflated generalized linear mixed-effect models (Zi-GLMMs) were developed. Zi-GLMM has robust statistic power for datasets with many zero values, which is constructed using two-step procedures: the first procedure is discriminating for zero inflation with Bernoulli distribution and logit link function, and the second procedure is count-process modeling with Poisson or negative binomial distribution and log link function ( ; ). During model development, the total time length (s) of the recorded vigilant behavior of an animal in a video clip was used as the response variable, with the offset term of the total time length of each video clip (20–60 s; see above). For explanatory variables, the bear-active/inactive years were used as the fixed effect, and the identification codes for each observed event and camera-trapping station identifiers were used as the random effects (both were text strings). Models for each sex (male or female) were also developed. The statistical modeling was performed using the “glmmTMB” package ver. 1.1.8 ( ) with R.

## Time-interval analysis
Time intervals were calculated between bear and deer captured at a camera station to assess deer avoidance from black bears at a fine spatiotemporal scale ( ; ; ). Consecutive events of bear–deer/deer–bear captured only from our original dataset (i.e., including all animal species captured) were extracted, and then the time intervals were calculated in a day unit as the difference of the timing (i.e., date and time) between the former and the latter animal detected. Therefore, if deer recognizes threats of bears, then the time intervals might be longer in the order from bear (former) to deer (latter) captured than in the order from deer to bear captured ( ; ; ). The odor of predators is a crucial cue for prey species to recognize the risk of encounters, thereby provoking their avoidance ( ; ; ). A GLMM with a log link function and gamma distribution was also developed using the “glmmTMB” package with R. In the GLMM, log-transformed time intervals were used as the response variable; for explanatory variables, the order of the focal species observed (i.e., bear–deer/deer–bear), deer sex (female/male), and its interactions were used as the fixed effects, and camera-trapping station identifiers were used as the random effect. For statistical validity, only the data from the bear-active year (i.e., 2022; n = 38) were analyzed because of the limited sample size in the bear-inactive years (n = 10; the result in the bear-inactive years is provided in ).

## Results

## Camera trapping
In the bear-active year (2022), we observed 73 independent detections of Asiatic black bears (RAI = 4.75), 197 detections of male (RAI = 12.82), and 269 detections of female (RAI = 17.50) sika deer for 1,537 camera-trapping days. On the contrary, in the bear-inactive years (2021 and 2023), only 13 independent detections of bears (5 and 8 detections in 2021 and 2023, respectively; RAI = 0.82 for 2 years), 376 detections of male (RAI = 23.72), and 263 detections of female deer (RAI = 16.59) for 1,585 days ( , and ).

## Spatial co-occurrence and temporal overlaps
RAIs of Asiatic black bear showed no statistical significant correlations with either female (rs = 0.24, P = 0.21) or male sika deer (rs = 0.05, P = 0.77). The extent of temporal overlaps between black bears and female deer was slightly lower in the bear-active year (Dhat4 = 0.72, 95% CI = 0.62–0.81) than in the bear-inactive years (Dhat1 = 0.76, 95% CI = 0.57–0.89; ). In addition, the extent of temporal overlaps between black bears and male deer was lower in the bear-active year (Dhat4 = 0.69, 95% CI = 0.58–0.79) than in the bear-inactive years (Dhat4 = 0.84, 95% CI = 0.67–0.94; ). Watson’s 2-sample tests indicated that the patterns in diel activities of female and male deer were significantly different from bears in the bear-active year (both P < 0.001), whereas no significant differences were observed in the bear-inactive years (both P > 0.10).
 

## Vigilant behaviors
The proportional vigilance observed in female deer was lower in the bear-active year (median = 16.7%) than in the bear-inactive years (median = 33.8%; ), revealed the significant differences by Zi-GLMM analysis (z = −4.91, P < 0.001; ). In contrast, the proportional vigilance observed in male deer was not different between the bear-active (median = 16.7%) and bear-inactive years (median = 15.0%; ), with no statistical significance by Zi-GLMM analysis (z = −0.68, P = 0.50; ).
 

## Time intervals
The time intervals observed in bear–deer (female, n = 11, mean = 2.20 days; male, n = 10, mean = 2.14 days) were longer than those in deer–bear (female, n = 10, mean = 1.18 days; male, n = 7, mean = 1.38 days) for both sexes ( ). However, GLMM analyses indicated no statistical significances in any covariates and their interactions ( ).
 

## Discussion
In this study, we demonstrated that sika deer neither exhibited spatial partitioning and spatiotemporal avoidances from the black bears nor increased their vigilance in the bear-active year, although we only found significant temporal partitioning between them. Furthermore, little significant antipredator behavioral responses were observed in female deer even in the fawning season, compared with males that exhibited clear temporal partitioning from bears. Therefore, the results were substantially inconsistent with our predictions that the NCEs by black bears alter the behaviors of deer, specifically in females. The dangerousness or lethality of predators is a key determinant of their threat level, and it drives the antipredator responses by prey, which are referred to as the “threat-sensitive predator avoidance hypothesis” ( ). Therefore, prey animals adjust their intensity of antipredator responses because of their experiences of predator’s threat levels (i.e., encounter with and attacked by predators their own as well as witnessing other individuals being killed). Predation by Asiatic black bear is very occasional ( ; ), and many individuals of sika deer might not have directly experienced either predation or attacks by this large carnivore. Therefore, the low threat levels recognized by deer might constrain their antipredator responses to black bears. Our findings were consistent with a case in black-tailed deer that little responded to the NCEs by American black bear ( ).
Significant temporal partitioning was found between black bears and sika deer (in both sexes) in the bear-active year ( ). It resulted from the different time window of activity peaks between the 2 species, that is, black bears’ activities peaked around 21:00, whereas male and female deer were active peaked at 4:00–5:00 ( ). Our results potentially support temporal avoidances of deer from bears, in accordance with previous findings on predator–prey interactions on diel activities (e.g., ; ). On the contrary, our time-interval analyses on the timing of species observations did not support the spatiotemporal avoidances by sika deer ( and ). The differences in activity patterns between the 2 species do not always indicate their temporal avoidances ( ; ) because diel activities are typically affected not only by interspecific interactions but also by conspecific interactions (e.g., reproductive status and conspecific interferences; ; ), climatic conditions (e.g., heavy snow fall; ), light illumination (e.g., moon phase; ; ), and anthropogenic disturbances (e.g., hunting pressures and land modifications; ; ; , ). Asiatic black bears are basically diurnal in natural habitats ( ; ; ), but they shift to nocturnal in human-dominated landscapes ( ; ). In general, sika deer is active from dusk to dawn (i.e., exhibiting crepuscular and nocturnal activities) ( , ), but the nocturnality increases under intense human pressures ( ; ). Therefore, it was difficult to conclude whether temporal partitioning from bears by deer were relevant to antipredator response. Diel activities for both species observed in this study were substantially nocturnal or crepuscular ( ) probably because our studied areas were agroforest landscapes with relatively high human activities (e.g., agricultural activities, hunting, forestry, and traffics). This result is consistent with that of previous studies in Japan ( ; ; ; ).
The antipredator behavior by cervids is often context dependent on and associated with habitat characteristics ( ; ). In forested habitats, dense understory vegetation (i.e., shrub or tall grass bush) reduces the visibility of prey to detect and recognize approaching predators, resulting in their avoidance to such places ( ; ). In contrast, sparse or open forest floor facilitates their early detections of predators’ attacks and enables them to flee successfully ( ; ). Understory vegetations in our studied sites were degraded and lost because of the increased intense browsing pressures by sika deer for the past decade ( ; ). These forest conditions might be advantageous for sika deer to early detect and flee from approaching bear (and also attacks) ( ). Thus, exhibiting other antipredator behaviors, such as spatiotemporal avoidances or increasing vigilance, might be unnecessary. In addition, resource condition is another factor driving antipredator behavior and predator–prey interactions. Under poor resource conditions, ungulates were found to use forest edges or anthropogenic linear features (e.g., pipelines or roads) for foraging sites where encounter risks with their predators (including bears) were relatively high ( ; ; ; ). Therefore, such ungulates might select rich resource patches to tolerate predation risks ( ). As mentioned previously, the poor resource conditions in our studied sites (i.e., degradations of understory vegetations by past deer browsing) might mitigate sensitivities to predation risks by sika deer. Thus, further study that involves empirical assessments of habitat characteristics (e.g., proportional coverage of understory vegetations or proportional visibility in forest floors) is necessary for clarification.
Vigilant behaviors of sika deer are also provoked by conspecific interactions, specifically in males in rutting seasons ( ; ). Although we observed that males were more detected in bear-inactive years than in bear-active year at camera trapping sites ( ), there were no significant differences in vigilance levels of males between the 2 periods ( ).
There were some potential methodological limitations in this study. First, our studied area was relatively small and investigated by limited number of camera traps, potentially recorded a small number of individuals repeatedly, whereas similar sampling designs were found in previous studies on antipredator behaviors (e.g., ; ; ). Furthermore, our sampled durations were largely different among the studied years, specifically in limited observations in early fawning season in bear-inactive years (2021 and 2023). Those limitations potentially affected and weakened our results (e.g., antipredator behaviors in adult females for defensing their fawns) for generality at a population level. Further research at large spatial scale (e.g., covered with whole home ranges of several bears) and longer sampling durations (e.g., whole of bear active seasons) would be preferable to assess the generality of our findings. Telemetry research has also high priority in deeply understanding antipredator behaviors of wild ungulates (e.g., ; ), while there are currently some suggestions in methodological equality between camera traps and telemetry for wildlife spatial research (e.g., ; ). Second, the sample size on time interval analysis was relatively small and limited in this study. suggest a new statistical method applying for limited sample sizes by camera traps. Therefore, further research using those research methods will contribute to deeply understand behavioral interactions between sika deer and Asiatic black bears.
In conclusion, the NCEs by omnivorous Asiatic black bears on sika deer were insignificant and relatively weak compared with those by predatory large carnivores, such as wolves ( ; ; ). This empirical study is the first to assess the NCEs by Asiatic black bear on wild ungulates. Our finding was consistent with a previous report on the similar species, that is, American black bear ( ), and it substantially supported the “threat-sensitive antipredator behavior hypothesis” that the dangerousness of predators is a key driver affecting the intensity of the NCEs in mammalian predator–prey systems ( ; ). In Japan, sika deer populations have currently increased across their ranges, resulting in increased human–deer conflicts (e.g., crop raiding, forestry damages, and traffic collisions) and negative effects on forest ecosystems ( ; ). Although Asiatic black bears have gradually increased their populations for the past decades in Japan ( ), they cannot function as the predator of sika deer in direct predations (i.e., consumptive effects; ) and the NCEs (demonstrated by this study). In addressing the overpopulation of sika deer and in conserving forest ecosystems, applying the NCEs by human predators (based on the “hunting for fear” concept) ( ; ) and reintroducing wolves ( ; , , ) will be of great importance to deer management in the depopulating society in Japan.
