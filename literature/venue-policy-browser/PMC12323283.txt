This Anniversary edition of Health & Justice is designed to review the state of the art in various aspects of health and criminology research. Health & Justice was launched to provide a venue for an emerging area of science, which is now referred to as health criminology, health services for the justice/legal system, or criminal legal systems and medical/health research. Back then, there was not a venue for this area of science, with more attention being given to those that work and participate in the health/legal settings. Springer Nature was open to this novel, emerging area and even gave us the opportunity to become an innovator with the open access publication concept. Lior Gideon and I separately approached Springer Nature to discuss a new journal and we then collaborated on launching Health & Justice, bringing attention to both criminology and health issues. We stacked the board with health and criminology researchers from across the world to begin this journey and create opportunities for the dissemination of health and justice-related research.
This novel journal is now ranked 9th out of 111 high quality criminology journals by Web of Science. To be in the ‘Top 10’ journals in criminology is an important milestone. We selected an open access journal specifically to ensure wide reach for the journal, and we have accomplished this: in 2024, journal articles were downloaded more than 400,000 times with an overall Altmetric attention score of 402. In terms of journal impact, SCIMago ranks the journal as in the 89th percentile, with Scopus giving us a source-normalized impact per paper of 1.313, and Web of Science awarding an impact factor of 2.6 in 2025. Articles submitted to Health & Justice can be reviewed quickly, generally within median 196 days, and these articles have a greater impact via open access. In fact, during the first 10 years since its inauguration the journal received nearly 600 submissions, with an average of 14 days before a first decision is rendered. Overall, the journal has grown significantly, and as shown by our rankings, is now considered an important place for health and criminology research. The fully open access nature of the journal means that our work is reaching scientists, practitioners, and policymakers. The work published in Health & Justice is making a mark on everything from research to policy to practice.
Of all publications over the first ten years, 80% were full research articles, with short reports, study protocols and scoping reviews making up the rest. Over a third of the studies were qualitative (38%), 14% were quantitative and 28% were systematic reviews or meta-analyses.

While the leading topics of articles reviewed were related to mental illness (11.4%) and substance use (20.8%), the journal published studies pertaining to special populations such the health of incarcerated women, elderly and geriatric people, veterans, and juveniles. The journal further explored other related topics such as chronic diseases, traumatic brain injuries, suicide and self-harm, and infectious diseases such as HIV and AIDS. The timing of the journal further enabled an important platform to publish research related to the COVID-19 pandemic, a topic that was at the forefront of the journal during the end of 2019, and 2020.
The 10th Anniversary edition is a culmination of articles reviewing the state of knowledge gained from the previous ten years of reviews and published work, and as such has a collection of articles reviewing the state of knowledge in different domains of health and justice. We invited authors who specialize in that work to write articles that highlighted the current research in an area and a future research agenda (and yes, they were peer-reviewed which means some did not make it). Each author provided important information for research, policy and practice, as discussed below.
Faye Taxman and Steven Belenko explore implementation science in health and justice studies (Taxman, F.S. and Belenko, S., ). By reviewing five case studies, Taxman and Belenko illustrate what implementation science is, how it differs from process and outcome evaluations, what can be learned about reforming justice and health settings to offer services, and how implementation science research is needed to further efforts to provide quality services for individuals in the justice system. This article outlines why implementation science is a valuable set of methods and tools to advance reform efforts.
Emily Salisbury and Alison Crawford establish how gender-responsive treatments are needed in justice systems to better address the unique needs of women and girls (Salisbury, E.J. & Crawford, A. ). Salisbury and Crawford provide the theoretical and practical nature of gender-responsive treatment as well as to illustrate the importance of addressing the victimization of women and girls that impacts their behaviors. The work identifies the technological innovations that are improving care but also noting how much more is needed to be accomplished.
Justin Berk, Anna-Maria South, Megan Martin, Michael-Evans James, Cameron Miller, Lawrence Haber and Josiah Rich provide a summary of the use of medications for opioid use disorders in carceral facilities (Berk, J. et al., ). This article not only helps to understand the gold-standard of medications for treating opioid use disorders but also reviews the challenges of implementing medications in prisons and/or jails. Berk and colleagues also illustrate how policy has impacted the availability and use of medications in carceral settings, as well as the new policies on the horizons. The authors conclude with a discussion of future research on retention, system barriers, and enhanced care coordination.
William Schultz and Rosemary Ricciardi assess how individuals that work in the carceral settings—officers, counselors, and treatment providers, impact the operations of the prison and the health of individuals that reside in such settings (Schultz, W.J. and Ricciardeli, R. ). Schultz and Ricciardi document how prison staff tend to be physically, emotionally and socially unhealthy which affects their job performance and the prison culture. The authors identify challenges and future research directions on staff-related issues for the goals of improving the humane nature of prison settings.
Meghan A. Novisky, Stephanie Grace Prost, Benjamin Fleury-Steiner and Alexander Testa outline the implications of an aging prison population in terms of the impact on prison operations and the culture of prisons (Novisky, M.A. et al., ). A review of the incarceration and aging literature reveals how the aging population can impact prison operations, as well as interventions that are important to mitigate the deleterious health impacts of incarceration. A research agenda is outlined including advanced research methods using community participatory and implementation science studies.
Techna Cadet, Katherine S. Elkington, Margaret Ryan, Ali Jalali, Gail A. Wasserman, Faye S. Taxman, Michael L. Dennis, and Sean M. Murphy help us to understand the value of evidence-based, behavioral health electronic screening systems in the juvenile justice system (Cadet, T. et al., ). This article outlines the implementation of an electronic, behavioral-health screening tool to identify youth with behavioral health needs. Besides discussing the implementation issues of electronic screening tools, the study identifies how best to routinize the use of the electronic tools. The study goes through the costs of the electronic screening tool in terms of implementation and sustainment. Cost estimates are provided. The article outlines a research agenda in technology to improve screening and service uptake.
Emily Dauria, Johanna Folk, Sarah Godoy, Evan Halloway, Jeanne McPhee, David Hoskins, Ali Yurasek, Katharine Galbraith, Sheridan Sweet, Eraka Bath, and Marina Tolou-Sham examine how structural racism is a driver of the health issues in the juvenile legal system (Dauria, E. et al., ). Using the Public Health Critical Race Praxis, the article offers recommendations for improving data collection and longitudinal research to address health inequities. Giving guidance to clinical and translational researchers on how to apply antiracist principles and research strategies for the purpose to successfully engage impacted youth and families in longitudinal studies.
Corianna Sichel, Katharine Galbraith and Katherine Elkington examine the factors that affect the delivery of evidence-based practices for youth in the juvenile justice system (Sichel, C. et al., ). In the article, “Breaking the Cycle: Connecting Youth Under Community Supervision to Effective Behavioral Health Services”, they illustrate how intersecting risk factors at the youth (e.g., trauma), family (e.g., parental behavioral health, family relationships), and social/environmental levels (i.e., poverty, neighborhood violence, socioeconomic inequalities, structural racism, barriers to treatment uptake) are frequently inversely correlated with the presence of protective factors. The article outlines the barriers to screening, family involvement, and referrals, as well as providing promising approaches to address these barriers. These include greater collaboration for joint decision-making processes, information/data sharing (e.g., through the building of coalitions and regular workgroups), collaborative communications (e.g., shared language and understanding), and others. The article outlines how to move forward to advance better utilization of the cascade of care.
Finally, Camille Kramer, Denae Bradley, Rebecca Shlafer, and Carolyn Sufrin provide an overview of the state of the maternal health and incarceration for women in the justice system (Kramer, C. et al., ). The article covers the literature on the mass incarceration history of women, pregnancy and motherhood. A central focus is on the current research and relevant policies. The articles will a research agenda to address inequities in maternal and infant outcomes for pregnant and parenting people exposed to incarceration.
This special edition also commences the passing of the baton from me (Faye Taxman) to Lauren Brinkley-Rubenstein as co-Editor-in-Chief. Health & Justice is in good hands with Lauren and Lior, with the added efforts of Wendy Guastaferro as Deputy Editor. Lauren is a Professor in the Department of Population Health Sciences at Duke University and a nationally recognized expert on the intersection of incarceration and health. She brings deep expertise in implementation science, harm reduction, and health equity, and her work has influenced both policy and practice across the U.S. With her addition to the journal leadership and commitment to advancing research in this field, Health & Justice is poised to thrive in its next chapter. Wendy is a Professor in the School of Criminology & Criminal Justice with work on effective behavioral health (primarily substance abuse and mental health) treatments and policies within community, jail, and prison settings. She brings unique efforts on treatment studies including those that focus on family relationships and family treatment courts. This trio will lead the journal to new heights through the next decade.
I (Faye Taxman) have learned a ton through the research submitted to the journal, and of course it is clear that Health & Justice is a vital resource—the downloads and rankings illustrate the importance of this journal. The next decade of articles promises to further the field. With much appreciation for those that helped to grow Health & Justice —without you we would not have achieved our goals of providing a home for research on health and justice.
