## Abstract
The substantial carbon footprint imparted by medical services warrants increased attention to their environmental impact. National guideline organizations such as the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) recommend multiple modalities for average-risk colorectal cancer (CRC) screening with varying resource intensity. The aim of this study was to quantify the environmental burden for 2 of the most used CRC screening modalities, colonoscopy and the multi-target stool DNA (mt-sDNA) test. A validated CRC microsimulation model was used to estimate the number of screening and follow-up tests for a cohort of 1 million average-risk individuals who underwent screening between ages 45 and 75. Component resources used for mt-sDNA, including waste products, energy, and transportation for colonoscopy and mt-sDNA, were collected from January 1, 2023, to January 1, 2024, and converted to carbon-equivalent emissions. Resources used for colonoscopy were captured from the literature. Resources devoted to screening colonoscopy were substantially (59%) higher than those to mt-sDNA, even when including follow-up colonoscopy. Of note, follow-up colonoscopy accounted for the majority (64%) of total emissions for the mt-sDNA screening strategy. Compared with colonoscopy screening, mt-sDNA substantially reduces the carbon emissions attributable to population-level CRC screening. Environmental impact should be included as a factor when choosing among guideline-recommended CRC screening strategies.
Keywords: colorectal cancer screening, sustainability, colonoscopy, mt-sDNA, multi-target stool-DNA, environmental impact

The environmental impact of colorectal cancer screening with noninvasive multi-target stool DNA (mt-sDNA) was compared with colonoscopy. There were 59% more carbon-equivalent emissions with a colonoscopy screening strategy as compared with mt-sDNA. Most emissions incurred by the mt-sDNA strategy were attributable to follow-up colonoscopy after a positive mt-sDNA result. The results demonstrate that the environmental impact of colorectal cancer screening varies significantly across guideline-endorsed options. Environmental considerations could serve as an additional criterion for patients choosing a screening modality.

## Introduction
Rapidly changing climate presents a global health threat, and health systems are increasingly a contributor, with greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions in the sector increasing by 36% since 2016, accounting for approximately 4.6% of global emissions in 2021. Air pollution associated with the health care industry has contributed to 4.6 million disability-adjusted life-years in 2021, and ongoing climate impacts will further increase health risks.
Given the growing recognition of climate change as a major public health challenge, one way to potentially reduce medical care delivery–related carbon effects is to quantify the environmental impact of specific clinical services for which multiple options are recommended by evidence-based guidelines and then prioritize lower-carbon alternatives.
Colorectal cancer (CRC) screening is a frequently utilized clinical service for which such an approach may be applied. Guidelines from the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) recommend multiple CRC screening modalities for average-risk individuals. Two of the most commonly used CRC screening modalities are decennial colonoscopy and triennial multi-target stool DNA (mt-sDNA) testing, which have demonstrated comparable net clinical benefits in model simulation analyses. Given that both mt-sDNA and colonoscopy detect over 90% of CRC cases, , the environmental impact of screening may be one of several factors considered in selecting a screening modality for average-risk individuals. Accordingly, the aims of this study were to (1) quantify the carbon footprint of mt-sDNA screening and (2) compare the calculated environmental impact of mt-sDNA screening with published emissions data for colonoscopy screening on a simulated population. If significant differences in carbon footprint are present among recommended options, such findings can be used to assist in the clinical decision-making process.

## Data and methods

## Unit resource estimates for CRC screening tests
Resource utilization estimates per colonoscopy, including waste materials, recyclable items, water use, and energy consumption, were derived from the published literature ( ). Resource consumption reports from facilities that processed mt-sDNA tests from January 1, 2023, to January 1, 2024, were used to quantify total waste generated per test, including landfill and hazardous waste, energy and water consumption, and recyclable outputs ( ). Details of resource consumption estimation are provided in . The waste and energy consumption data were entered into the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Simplified GHG Emissions Calculator to determine the carbon-equivalent emissions in metric tons (mtCO2e).
 

## Patient and freight travel estimates for CRC screening
Patient travel for screening and follow-up colonoscopy was estimated using the weighted average of the rural and urban population and the travel distance for health care visits from the 2022 National Household Travel Survey (NHTS). , On average, patients traveled 12.8 miles, as the weighted average of 11.5 miles for urban patients and 17.2 miles for rural patients. The distance traveled was multiplied by the US EPA’s emission factor (kg CO2 per mile) for an average passenger vehicle and converted to mtCO2e ( ). 
Emissions for outgoing mt-sDNA test ground shipments were calculated by using the test's shipping weight and applying the 2024 US EPA emission factor for ground freight to the road travel distance between the test manufacturing facility and the center coordinates of each state. The number of test kit shipments varied by state, and travel distances were adjusted by order volume in 2023. Incoming mt-sDNA test (ie, to the sample processing facility) emissions were estimated using the average flight distance from each state to the sample processing facility, incoming test weight, and the EPA air freight emission factor ( ). Consistent with precedent from previous studies, carbon contributions from the consumables' supply chains were out of the scope of the analysis. Also, for mt-sDNA screening, no patient travel is necessary for most patients, although some may opt to drop off their completed screening kit at a nearby shipping retail location. Similarly, colonoscopy may require patients to travel to a pharmacy ahead of the procedure to pick up a bowel-preparation kit. Due to a lack of robust data, such trips were excluded from the analysis.

## Transport sensitivity analysis
To mitigate the uncertainty resulting from the lack of direct measurement of transport parameters, including travel distances and carbon impact, we performed a sensitivity analysis to understand how deviations from the base transport estimates affected emissions results. A 30% range for the sensitivity analysis was selected to obtain estimates that are closer to those calculated using 2018 and 2023 published US EPA emission factors ( ). , Additional details for transportation sensitivity analysis and assumptions can be found in .

## Estimates of CRC screening test utilization
We utilized the Colorectal Cancer and Adenoma Incidence and Mortality microsimulation model (CRC-AIM), a model that simulates the natural progression of CRC through the adenoma-carcinoma pathway. The model's calibration, cross-model, and external validation have been extensively described in previous analyses. An overview of the modeled CRC progression pathway and key model input parameters can be found in . The Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) guideline for reporting of decision analytical model studies was followed. 
Two hypothetical cohorts, each comprising 1 000 000 eligible average-risk individuals, were simulated to initially receive screening by colonoscopy or using the mt-sDNA testing, over the recommended age range from 45 to 75 years. Those with a positive stool-based test were assumed to complete a follow-up colonoscopy to determine the presence or absence of a CRC diagnosis with perfect adherence. When precancerous polyps or malignant neoplasia were detected during the initial colonoscopy screening or the follow-up colonoscopy, the detected lesions were treated based on standard clinical practice, and patients subsequently underwent surveillance colonoscopies according to established guidelines. Patients with a positive mt-sDNA result but a negative colonoscopy are considered up-to-date with screening for the next 10 years, after which they resume mt-sDNA testing. During the screening period, simulated individuals with CRC symptoms also underwent diagnostic colonoscopy and were followed up according to guidelines.
The primary outcomes for each cohort were (1) the number of screening, follow-up, and diagnostic tests resulting from decennial colonoscopy and triennial mt-sDNA screening strategies and (2) the carbon footprint—expressed in mtCO2e—generated from those screening strategies.

## Results
The model estimated that screening between age 45 to 75 years with colonoscopy would require 4.1 million colonoscopies for a simulated cohort of 1 million average-risk individuals over the lifetime. In the mt-sDNA strategy, 7.1 million mt-sDNA tests and 1.7 million follow-up colonoscopies were completed ( ). The calculated carbon-equivalent emissions for the decennial colonoscopy screening strategy were 59% (39 440 metric tons) higher than triennial mt-sDNA screening ( ). The emissions attributable to follow-up colonoscopy comprised 64% of total emissions from the mt-sDNA screening strategy. A stratified comparison of the emissions for waste, recyclable items, water, and energy for each strategy is presented in .
 The sensitivity analysis revealed that the colonoscopy screening strategy produced higher emissions than mt-sDNA screening across all transport estimates. When the smallest gap between emissions estimates was used, the colonoscopy strategy produced 31% higher total emissions than the mt-sDNA screening strategy.

## Discussion
To reduce GHG emissions, the medical community must recognize, evaluate, and modulate the environmental impact of large-volume, population-based services, such as average-risk CRC screening, and carefully consider lower carbon options when multiple alternatives are clinically acceptable. , Since guidelines endorse several CRC screening modalities, this offers an interesting case study for this type of analysis. Our simulation comparing the resources consumed and emissions generated by colonoscopy and mt-sDNA CRC screening strategies estimated that increased proportional utilization of a stool-based mt-sDNA screening (including follow-up colonoscopy for positive tests) would substantially reduce the environmental burden of CRC screening. These averted emissions support the goal to secure better health for current and future generations by reducing the health care sector's contributions to climate change.
The estimated reduction in emissions from mt-sDNA screening ranged from 28 477 mtCO2e to 50 406 mtCO2e per 1 million individuals screened. When these results are extrapolated to the 117 million average-risk individuals eligible for screening, , a complete shift from colonoscopy to mt-sDNA screening could potentially avert a maximum estimate of 4.6 million mtCO2e, equivalent to removing 1.1 million gasoline-powered passenger vehicles from the road or meeting the electricity needs of 912 000 homes for 1 year. The waste avoided by using the mt-sDNA strategy over screening colonoscopy would cover a land area of approximately 3780 American football fields at 1-foot high or 2 times the area of New York City's Central Park.
In addition to reduced emissions, a previous modeling study estimated that CRC cases and deaths averted with mt-sDNA screening would equal or exceed those of colonoscopy in a real-world setting, while another study found that mt-sDNA screenings have high quality-adjusted life-year (QALY) savings and lower costs compared with colonoscopy. Along with environmental impact, clinical benefits and cost-effectiveness can be other considerations in selecting a CRC screening modality.
The findings are not without limitations. First, colonoscopy-related patient travel and mt-sDNA freight shipments were not directly measured. There is uncertainty around the mode and distance traveled by patients, with previous analyses of NHTS data estimating that 88% of trips for medical care are by personal vehicle/rideshare. Due to the effects of sedation after colonoscopy, this is a potential underestimation of travel emissions, as the ride may be completed by a family member or other services, instead of alternative forms of transportation. Additionally, the travel distance to health facilities with colonoscopy may be significantly longer than the NHTS estimates. One study in Nebraska estimated that colonoscopy patients traveled an average of 29.0 miles, 125% more than our base estimate. 
The ingoing and outgoing mt-sDNA kits’ travel distances were based on the location of the test manufacturing facility and the center coordinates of each state while accounting for the order volume of each state. However, the geographic distribution of population centers in each state is an approximation to the actual travel distance and may over- or underestimate the travel distances. To account for these uncertainties, parameters for patient and freight travel were varied, and sensitivity analysis showed that mt-sDNA testing was less environmentally burdensome compared with screening colonoscopy under all transport assumptions. Second, we assumed perfect adherence to mt-sDNA testing as well as screening and follow-up colonoscopy. Incorporating real-world adherence (eg, tests that are sent out but not returned, suboptimal adherence to screening or follow-up colonoscopy after a positive test) would potentially change the environmental impact of each screening strategy. The estimated emissions reductions did not account for the facility energy use required to perform a colonoscopy or the freight transport of consumables used during the procedure, the inclusion of which would further favor noninvasive screening. The environmental impact of the production of consumables was not included in the analysis. As the global supply chain is highly diverse and decentralized, there are large networks of suppliers, products, processes, and logistics. Colonoscopy consumables are sourced from various manufacturers and distributors, each with their own supply chains and environmental practices. Lack of supply chain–related emissions may be a conservative assumption, as inclusion would be more likely to favor stool-based mt-sDNA test based on estimated weight of consumables.
Of note, for this initial analysis, we did not consider all USPSTF-recommended screening tests such as fecal immunochemical testing (FIT), so the results do not paint a complete picture of the environmental impact of all average-risk CRC screening options. Other modalities were primarily excluded due to a limited or no availability of quality estimates for test-specific environmental impacts. Unlike mt-sDNA tests that are manufactured and processed in a single venue in the United States, FIT tests are produced and processed in various facilities, presenting challenges in obtaining comparable data inputs.
Also, this study did not include an analysis of the next-generation mt-sDNA test that recently received Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for average-risk CRC screening, based on robust data from a large, multicenter clinical validation study. The relatively high age-weighted specificity of the mt-sDNA test (94%) should translate to fewer unnecessary follow-up colonoscopies performed for false-positive stool-screening results, which will likely further reduce the environmental burden compared with the current mt-sDNA test (and other guideline-recommended strategies).
In 2021, the USPSTF recommended that the age of initiation for average-risk CRC screening be lowered from age 50 to age 45 years, resulting in approximately 20 million additional Americans meeting eligibility criteria. This increase in the number of people indicated for screening, and limited capacity of screening colonoscopies, coupled with a shortage of endoscopists, jeopardizes CRC screening access and creates a need to better allocate colonoscopy appointments based on clinical benefit.
The ease of access and reduced emissions generated by mt-sDNA screening are critical variables that should be included as we aim to increase the number of individuals undergoing effective, accessible, and responsible CRC screening strategies in a timely fashion. Reserving the finite capacity of colonoscopy for higher-acuity follow-up, diagnostic and therapeutic procedures could yield improvements in both population health and environmental impact associated with increasingly adopted, average-risk CRC screening.

## Conclusion
An mt-sDNA CRC screening strategy substantially reduces the environmental burden of population-level CRC screening compared with a colonoscopy screening strategy. Reduced waste, water, energy use, and emissions by noninvasive screening support climate change mitigation and will abate the human health consequences of climate change. As we strive to increase the number of eligible individuals receiving CRC screening, these findings and the limited access to colonoscopy should be used to assist patients and clinicians in the clinical decision-making process.
