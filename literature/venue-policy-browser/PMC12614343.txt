## ABSTRACT
Chronic back pain (CBP) is a widespread public health issue. There is a lack of community-based care options for Indigenous Peoples with CBP. A virtual care clinic co-designed with community was implemented in the Cree Community of Pelican Narrows using remote presence (RPR) technology. Methods We used a community-based participatory action framework and pre-post design to evaluate this intervention. Pain, quality of life and experience outcomes were measured. An assessment was provided by a local nurse practitioner and a physical therapist joining over RPR. The physical therapist provided jp to four follow-up treatments per participant using RPR. Results Thirty-eight participants were assessed, and 78 follow-up treatments delivered. No significant differences between pre- and post- pain or quality of life were found. Thirteen patient participants and five health providers completed interviews. Patient themes included: (1) community and healthcare context, (2) community preferences for back pain care, and (3) experience with virtual back pain clinic. Health care provider themes included: (1) getting people to clinic, (2) experience with virtual back pain clinic, and (3) ways to enhance care. Conclusion Virtual CBP clinic enhanced access to therapy and was experienced positively. Participants suggested ways to address challenges, including a hybrid model of care.
KEYWORDS: Chronic back pain, telerehabilitation, physical therapy, virtual care, interprofessional team

## Introduction
Chronic back pain (CBP) is a prevalent public health condition and an economic burden in Canada [ ]. Low back pain is the leading cause of morbidity worldwide considering years lived with disability, compared to 289 other diseases and conditions [ ]. CBP not only inflicts substantial suffering on individuals but also exerts a considerable toll on healthcare systems and economies [ ]. In Canada, 22% of adults have CBP, with medical expenses estimated between $6 and $12 billion annually [ ]. Within rural and remote regions, residents are 30% more likely to report having CBP than are residents living in urban centers, with Indigenous people reporting disproportionally higher rates [ ]. Reduced access to appropriate health care is a proposed contributing factor to this greater proportion of people living in rural and remote locations [ , ]. As the population ages, musculoskeletal conditions such as CBP are likely to become more prominent contributors to the overall landscape of disability at the population level, both provincially and nationally. The associated costs, both in terms of medical expenses for the individual and health systems contribute to the gravity of CBP as a public health issue. CBP places a greater burden on individuals in the health system because of increased rates of primary physician care visits, diagnostic procedures, specialist consultations and opioid use [ ]. Addressing this widespread problem requires a comprehensive approach that encompasses prevention, effective management strategies and increased access to care to mitigate the societal impact of CBP.
The disparity of access to rehabilitation care in rural and remote areas of Saskatchewan and Canada remains a pressing concern [ ]. Residents in these areas often encounter challenges in accessing rehabilitation facilities and specialised care due to geographical distances, limited infrastructure and healthcare resource distribution [ , , ]. The scarcity of rehabilitation services results in delayed or insufficient treatment for individuals with various health conditions, including CBP, hindering their recovery and quality of life. Geographical barriers exacerbate existing healthcare inequalities, emphasising the need for targeted interventions and community-directed solutions to ensure equitable access to rehabilitation services [ ]. The absence of consistent healthcare providers contributes to the burden on patients, as a lack of established relationships and limited trust can impede timely and effective treatment.
Limited information exists on CBP in Indigenous populations in Canada. Research investigating CBP among Aboriginal people in Australia revealed that this condition can have significant disabling effects [ ]. The research emphasised the relevance of factors such as sex and gender, cultural responsibilities, and the profound impact of chronic back pain on emotional well-being as crucial considerations in healthcare for this population [ ]. Indigenous peoples in Canada have unique historical, cultural, psychosocial and environmental influences related to overall health and wellness that impact conditions such as CBP and their experiences accessing care for CBP. This presents unique challenges within indigenous communities, where access to care is intertwined with specific circumstances influenced by systemic racism, intergenerational trauma and social determinants of health. Systemic disparities contribute to limited healthcare resources, negatively impacting adequate access for indigenous people experiencing CBP. Therefore, working in partnership with indigenous communities to codesign, coimplement and coevaluate care, build upon communities’ unique strengths, culture, worldview, languages, practices and protocols to enhance health care service delivery and ensure culturally safe and relevant rehabilitation care in meaningful ways is of paramount importance.
A CBP management needs assessment conducted in the remote Saskatchewan Cree community of Pelican Narrows (population just over 2,000 in the community and over 4,000 in the surrounding area and located 512 km north of the urban tertiary health center of Saskatoon) revealed several contextual factors that significantly delayed the recovery process for people with CBP [ ]. These factors included a lack of prevention and educational resources, challenges in accessing care as a result of no physical therapy services available in the community resulting in expenses incurred and discomfort associated with travel, and the lack of social support and anxiety in having to leave the community and family [ ]. Additionally, prolonged wait times for specialised care and the absence of culturally safe and relevant care further impeded the recovery process [ ] and contributed to a feeling of culture shock, which described the disorientation of community members when seeking care in an unsafe and unfamiliar setting. These findings provide insight into the multifaceted nature of barriers to effective CBP care in the community, emphasising the need for comprehensive and culturally responsive, locally available interventions to address these barriers.
The lived experiences within communities and the historical impact of colonisation, intergenerational trauma, and systemic racism persistently shape health and well-being. The Calls to Action from the Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada, particularly Action 20, highlight the impact of colonial policies, such as residential schools, on health [ ]. It is imperative that health care providers “recognize the value of Aboriginal healing practices and use them in the treatment of Aboriginal patients in collaboration with Aboriginal healers and Elders, where requested by Aboriginal patients” [ ]. Addressing this issue requires collaboration among healthcare providers, policy makers and community stakeholders to develop strategies to increase the availability of culturally relevant rehabilitation care in rural and remote communities, in the languages and in the worldview of the community.
The integration of telerehabilitation in Canada, including Saskatchewan, holds significant potential in transforming healthcare delivery and addressing access to care inequities [ , ]. Telerehabilitation benefits from overcoming geographical barriers and providing individuals in rural and remote areas with access to rehabilitation services [ , ]. The anticipated impacts include improved convenience for patients, reduced travel-related psychological and financial burdens, and increased continuity of care [ , , ]. However, there is a financial burden associated with telerehabilitation, particularly concerning internet access and the availability of computers to connect virtually. These challenges must be navigated to reach the full potential of tele-rehabilitation. As technology continues to advance, the integration of telehealth solutions into rehabilitation practices can contribute to more equitable and timely healthcare delivery across the country [ ].
The purpose of this project was to implement and evaluate an interprofessional CBP clinic utilising remote presence robot (RBR) virtual technology in partnership with the northern Saskatchewan Cree community of Pelican Narrows. The three objectives for this project included the following: (1) strengthen and leverage existing relationships with community partners while actively engaging in meaningful ways throughout the project; (2) codevelop and implement with the community a person and community-centered model of back pain care that is culturally responsive; and (3) collaboratively identify a set of multidimensional metrics to evaluate the CBP hybrid clinic in a community-directed manner.

## Materials and methods
This research embraces the combined strengths of indigenous and Western knowledge, using a two-eyed seeing approach with the prioritisation of indigenous knowledge and an ethical space framework to ensure relationship building and trust [ , ]. The use of Western-based pain assessment tools and approaches for health service evaluation may not accurately reflect indigenous ways of expressing pain or may be the most appropriate way to measure the effectiveness of a management approach for CBP in the First Nation community [ ]. Accordingly, community experiences and perspectives informing this project evaluation are needed, and a community-based participatory action research approach (CBPAR) was utilised [ ]. Using CBPAR, community stakeholder and advisory perspectives were integrated at each stage of the implementation and evaluation phases, modifying the process and metrics throughout the project. Four phases of project activities were implemented to address the objectives. This project is part of a multi-year project of engagement and relationship with the community of Pelican Narrows and the Peter Ballantyne Cree Nation. The study was approved by the University of Saskatchewan Biomedical Ethics Board (protocol code Bio−1435 October 4, 2019).

## a. Phase 1
Project initiation and community engagement activities were prioritised to strengthen pre-established community relationships and create opportunities for community members to co-develop and refine a preliminary project plan. A meeting and discussion occurred with the health services board and local community member Elder Advisors to discuss community needs, provide information about the research and acquire community-level approval. Community members participated in informing and refining the proposed research questions, methods and approaches. The outcome of this phase was a Memorandum of Participation to advise future research activities.

## b. Phase 2
Community visits interspersed with phone calls and online meetings actively engaged Cree community members to collaboratively (1) design a person and community-centered model of care for CBP and (2) identify a set of multidimensional metrics relevant to the community. Engagement with a community advisory group through in-person circles and gatherings along with videoconference meetings produced a co-designed model of care for CBP using RPR that was responsive to the care needs of community members, integrate Cree perspectives, worldview and experiences in the process. The advisory group consisted of community members with lived experience of CBP, Elders, health care providers and managers.

## c. Phase 3
Findings from Phases 1 and 2 informed the implementation and evaluation of a team approach to CBP care using RPR technology as well as additional community support and services. Local community health care providers worked with urban-based physical therapists (PTs) using the RPR to provide care for participants with CBP. Although this was initially planned to be a hybrid clinic (RPR and in person), owing to the onset of the COVID−19 pandemic and provincial and community lockdowns, we proceeded with the CBP clinic by the RPR only. Physical therapy services are not covered in this community and are not funded under federal or provincial jurisdiction at this time, so intervention expenses were covered through a Saskatchewan Health Research Foundation research grant.

## Participants
Participants were recruited through posters in the local health facility, as well as word of mouth through physicians and nurse practitioners. Further information was provided by the researchers as requested. Participants were included if they were adults with back pain longer than three months and were able to provide consent in English or Cree. Informed consent was obtained from all participants involved in this study. The participants completed a team-based back pain assessment where an urban consulting PT joined a nurse practitioner and the participants in the remote Cree community, utilising RPR Up to four to five follow-up treatment sessions were provided by the PT connecting to the participant with the RPR in the community health centre. The intervention took place from February 2022 to June 2023.

## Outcome measures and quantitative data analysis
The participants completed a demographic questionnaire prior to participation, as did the Numeric Pain Rating Scale (NPRS) and Modified SF-6D before and after the intervention. The participants also completed a Community-Directed Pain Scale (CDPS), which has been published elsewhere [ ]. The NPRS is commonly used and contains a 0−10 scale. The CDPS was requested by an Elder and Knowledge Keeper for this CBP project to ensure a more descriptive way for participants to describe their pain to health providers (including Cree descriptors, faces and colors embedded in one scale). The CDPS was developed and evaluated with the Knowledge Keeper and Elder, and the results of the evaluation of the new scale are reported elsewhere by Zhang et al. [ ]. We worked with a Knowledge Keeper and Elder to co-create modifications on the SF-6D to ensure the relevance and appropriateness of the scale (Appendix A). The data were summarised using descriptive statistics. The Shapiro‒Wilk test was used to detect the normality of continuous variables. The means and standard deviations are reported for continuous variables that are normally distributed, whereas the medians and interquartile ranges are reported for continuous variables that are not normally distributed. Categorical variables are reported as numbers and percentages. The paired t-test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test were used to analyze continuous variables according to their distribution patterns. A p value of < 0.05 was considered significant. Analyses were performed using R-version 4.0.2 programming language for statistical computing.

## Qualitative analysis
While quantitative measures provided an overall descriptive picture of the participants, qualitative methods were utilised to obtain a deeper look at the experience of the participants. The interviews were conducted in person in the community or via a remote presence robot depending on the participant’s preference. Language interpreters were available but not requested by any of the participants. The interviews lasted between 30 and 60 min. The interview guides for patients and health providers are included in Appendices B and C. The interviews of 13 patient participants and five health care providers were recorded by researchers and transcribed by the Canadian Hub for Applied and Social Research, University of Saskatchewan. The research team followed an approach described by Vaismoradi et al. and Braun and Clarke [ , ]. NVIVO software was used for all qualitative analyses [ ]. Research students (KA and KA) reviewed all the transcripts to become familiar with the data. SL, BB, KA, KA and RS reviewed four transcripts and identified codes together to standardise the team approach. Researchers KA and KA independently utilised iterative thematic analysis to identify codes and themes within and connections between reported experiences. A collaborative review of themes and axial coding then occurred with SL, BB and RS [ ]. The team met on two additional occasions to form a consensus and finalise the themes. A community Elder and Knowledge Keeper were consulted on the themes and had opportunity to provide insight into the analysis.

## Results
A total of 38 participants were included in the study. Thirty-eight participants were assessed, and 78 follow-up treatments were delivered. The participants received a minimum of 0 and a maximum of 7 follow-up treatments. The mean number of follow-up treatments was 2.05. The intervals between the initial assessment and final treatment ranged between 3 and 40 weeks, with a mean of 19 weeks. The demographics of the 38 participants are shown in . ## Patient participant experiences
A total of 13 community member participants and 5 health care providers were included in semi-structured interviews following the completion of the RPR CBP treatment sessions. The participants included 6 males and 7 females, with a range of ages 32−65 years and a mean age of 51 years. Health providers included physicians, nurses and physical therapists who both reside in or live at a distance to the community. Owing to the small staff, further details are not provided to ensure the privacy and confidentiality of the participants. We identified three overarching themes from the participant interviews: (1) community and healthcare context: available pain resources, community safety, community support, social determinants of health and barriers to healthcare; (2) community preferences for back pain care: communication preferences, education, exercise programming, physical therapy delivery, resources and facilities, and traditional methods; and (3) experiences with the virtual back pain clinic: positive feedback, positive outcomes, suggestions for improvements and telehealth feedback.

## Community and healthcare context
Participants described that available pain resources in general for back pain included analgesics like Tylenol or NSAIDs like Ibuprofen. Most preferred alternative options if they were available, with one participant mentioning, “And I don’t like taking medication…I don’t believe in them”. The explanation was provided that medications numb the pain but don’t heal.
Community safety included stories about the inability to walk in the community due to wild dogs and wolves, gangs carrying machetes, and weather risks with snow and slippage. Community members were not able to go out for walks nor participate in physical activity because of concerns about dog packs and risks to their safety. One participant explained, “It wasn’t like this in [the community]. It wasn’t like this. It was safer. I walked around all over but now I can’t walk around”.
 Community support described diminished recreational resources such as a community centre or arena which contained treadmills and a gym that is no longer available owing to a lack of funding and safety concerns. One participant mentioned the need for community programs for youth, but explained there was no funding available to reintroduce closed programs. The participants also identified social determinants of health factors, including options for healthy food, that contribute to back pain as a chronic condition. In Saskatchewan, North China, food prices are extremely high, and fresh vegetable availability is limited seasonally. This barrier to nutrition will have an impact on wellness and the management of chronic conditions.
“You need food; your vegetables and water to get the muscles going, right? And if you don't have those then you’re dehydrated, you’re nauseated, you’re whatever health conditions you have like, say diabetes, you have to have your nutrients in. And if you lack any of those, then they become nauseated, you become dizzy; all those factor in”.
Participants identified barriers to healthcare, including finances, a lack of access to care and travel. The limited availability of services in rural communities leads to longer wait times and increased out-of-pocket costs. Participants have to take time off work to travel for care, leading to missing out on pay, and also have increased costs and time requirements to travel to larger city centres that have the facilities they require. This takes a mental and physical toll on people as one participant noted:
“Like for health-wise and mentally, emotionally. Because if I were to leave today, and say I had an appointment in the city tomorrow, that will make 12 hours of travel time. That would like be a burden on my health, like my physical health, emotional health, mental health because of the history of my health already. It's already like in pain just to think about it. Like to sit there for six hours and then find a parking spot, go find the venue, go find place to go eat, go back to the appointment, sit there and wait for me to be called, another few hours of sitting. It’s just chaotic”.
Similarly, a participant noted the lack of musculoskeletal specialists like PT in the community, leading to having to leave responsibilities such as housework and children at home, in order to travel to access these services. It is very difficult to travel for days with aggravating pain.
“I'm leaving responsibilities, my kid’s education, my kid’s appointments, my kid’s homework, the laundry, the housework, the cooking. A lot of it is with children and workwise too, we don't have – we're leaving our responsibilities at work to travel and to attend our appointments. A lot of that is like two day’s worth of going to see physio. Most of it is travel and most of it is, with health being number one, and then you're having to sit there and your body is not having enough, all the nutrients to move, to exercise in between, plus the weather conditions. I don’t know, it’s a lot when I have to go further”.

## Community preferences for back pain care
Communication preferences focused on the importance of the language for community members. Some members spoke the Cree TH dialect while some were English, with most being comfortable in either or using a translator if necessary. In the subtheme education, community members wanted preventative approaches and education surrounding proper biomechanics to protect the back from an earlier age, along with information on what to do when pain occurs.
In the exercise programming subtheme, participants commented on the feasibility of doing their prescribed exercises, with the possibility of group-based programs being an idea to help facilitate uptake. They felt that a walking program would help endorse daily physical activity with social aspects to help build a sense of community. Physical therapy delivery included appreciation for access to PT through the RPR:
“II enjoyed it because I came here and didn't have to travel anywhere so – I don't really like travelling because especially those little minivans they send me to a doctor in. It’s so hard on my back”.
Some participants expressed a preference for having someone to do hands-on work (like PT and massage) in person; however, the ease of access to having an option in town was still heavily favoured compared to traveling to another city for services. Importantly, with a history of colonisation, trust and relationship building is so critical in a healthcare encounter. In person components can increase the opportunity for health providers to build the therapeutic alliance and strengthen relationships with patients to ensure optimal person-centered care.
In the resources and facilities subtheme, participants mentioned what tools and facilities would benefit their health and treatments. The use of treadmills and walking tracks, along with dedicated areas to exercise safely, was mentioned, with a focus on indoor facilities that are protected from the elements and wildlife, as something that would encourage more activity. One participant mentioned an evening fitness program in a gym that would also provide education to help with their back:
“Yeah, something in the evenings, like maybe once a week just to come and do exercises, how to do exercises. How to keep your posture, how to pick up items. It's important for these other patients, to learn how to not just pick up items without bending their knees or how to walk”.
Traditional methods include knowledge of traditional healing and techniques to help with back pain management. Some participants used different natural medicines like rat root, along with tree gum to manage pain, and make hot teas with these traditional ingredients for self-management.

## Experience with virtual back pain clinic
The subtheme of positive feedback focused on participants’ appreciation for learning “how to do exercises properly” and general appreciation for having access to rehabilitation:
“It’s easier because I'm learning to be able to be careful and how I pick up things or how I'm sitting or how long I should be walking, how long I should be sitting. This is how I should be sitting, walking, picking up stuff, and I learned a lot from my appointments”.
Positive outcomes identified by participants included the effectiveness of the prescribed exercises in reducing pain levels and a subsequent reduction in the use of painkiller medication. Performing the exercises administered by the PT through the RPR helped increase “their ability to move around again”, increasing the amount of work that can be done in a day and reducing overall stiffness: “Well, the first time when I started doing it and the first time I started listening to that guy [the PT], he started showing me how to do my exercises and I’ve been doing them and then I haven’t been feeling the pain”.

## Another participant shared a similar outcome
“Then I told him [the PT], forgot his name, he showed me exercises which really helped a lot because I had a sore back that was pinching and, I think it was due to my knee that my back was hurting. So, he showed me some exercises, which I did, and it really helped a lot. Like a sometimes, whenever I have a pain, I'll do those exercises about three, four days and then the pain will be gone for quite a long time”.
Suggestions for improvement included comfort, convenience, exercise, frequency and more comprehensive care. The participants reported that they would be more comfortable conversing with PTs who are from the community and doing the exercises in a secluded space. The convenience of having access to a PT in town without having to travel was very well received.
“I had to wait, two, three months for physio appointment before. Now, I can just make a quick call to one of the girls. And then I’ll be in like in a week. If I had like excruciating back pain, I’d be able to get in and talk to a specialist, you know what I mean? It’s convenient”.
The participants also recommended increased opportunity for exercises. Many addressed their desire to have increased access to a PT, increasing their ability to see them multiple times and progress through their exercises. The participants appreciated the available frequency of treatments: a participant who had treated with the RPR three times stated, “That would be three trips out of the community for that same amount”.
The subtheme of telehealth feedback focused on participants' feedback with the robot. Telehealth feedback had two further subheadings: comfort and technical challenges. Under subheading comfort, the participants felt comfortable conversing with the PT via the robot and learning how to perform the exercises. Some did state a preference for having an in-person PT in the community, with one participant willing to travel outside of the community for an in-person appointment rather than over the RPR. This preference for travel contradicts other participants’ opinions, and it is important to note that to ensure patient-centerred care, options should be available to people seeking care that meet their individual needs. We have noted above that the importance of in person encounters for the building of relationships must be noted, and these components provide an argument for hybrid care (a blend of in person and virtual care) going forward. Under technical challenges, there were a few comments mentioning the issues with the robot not turning on or a drop in connection due to faulty internet.
“Very comfortable and very convenient. I didn’t have to travel like I said, I could just come in. I felt so at ease too like I was talking to a specialist that knew about my health, health-wise. I could relate to somebody who knew about my pain and my health and I’m so glad that somebody is out there listening that has knowledge to speak about the back pain physically. It’s interesting”.

## Healthcare provider experiences
Healthcare provider interviews revealed the following themes: (1) getting people to the ,clinic; (2) experience with virtual back pain clinic: improved clinical care, providers’ growth and development and telehealth feedback; and (3) ways to enhance care.

## Getting people to the clinic
Healthcare providers shared the challenge of scheduling and ensuring that clients, practitioners and administration knew who would be seen by whom and at what time. Miscommunication, bad weather, and lack of transportation were identified as challenges/barriers to meeting participants’ needs. A dedicated person for driving clients to and from the clinic was identified as a possible solution.

## Experience with virtual back pain clinic
Healthcare providers noted that improved clinical care included increased confidence to use the technology and communicate adequately. One provider felt the virtual back pain clinic increased participants’ access to PT and helped manage patient pain levels. Providers' growth and development summarised the learning that came with using the technology and working as part of a team. Providers had to adapt to the tools they had, become creative with how they delivered treatments, and become confident in their CBP assessments. Telehealth feedback identified challenges associated with operating the virtual clinic as well as with the user experience. Internet and connectivity issues occurred, which affected communication. At times, the RPR would reboot and have an audio or video lag making communication difficult, but this was not experienced by everyone. One provider stated that “at no point did it feel like the technology or teams was a barrier to be able to connect with or care for the client”.

## Ways to enhance care
Process recommendations included having the ability to print out the exercises for clients, and having a larger dedicated space for programs. One provider provided on their thoughts on future use of virtual care when asked if they would use the model again:
I find it very beneficial, I think I would take more the hybrid approach – depending on the clinician's level of experience and comfort with utilising solely virtual, because I know there's more formal education for clinicians to take. But even again, there's still an art to it, and if you don't have that ability to do any kind of hands-on or feel like how strong is that person compared to like left or right – say measuring hip strengths. So, you have to get creative on how else you can measure that and communicate that to a person.

## Discussion
In the province of Saskatchewan Canada, remote northern First Nation communities have limited access to rehabilitation care for CBP owing to geography, limited numbers of specialised providers, and the need for long-distance travel to available care [ ]. This project involved a collaboration among researchers at the University of Saskatchewan and community members, Elders and health providers in the remote Cree community of Pelican Narrows to co-develop, implement and a culturally responsive, community-centered approach to CBP care. To meet the research objectives, the process of implementation included: (1) collaboration with community members and health providers, Elders and a Knowledge Keeper at all stages of the project to strengthen relationships, as well as co-develop, implement and evaluate this process; (2) an urban scheduler who called patients to book appointments, build relationships and provide follow-up reminders to support care; (3) an urban PT who used an RPR to join patients located with the NP in the facility who had a previous history of rapport with patients; (4) researchers who provided support, coordination, planning, and the necessary research components through travel to community and virtual communication using the RPR; (5) modification of outcomes measures to ensure a more culturally responsive approach; and (6) local remote licensed practical nurses who coordinated all community logistics (scheduling, patient transport, availability of treatment room) aspects. We have not identified any other literature that outlines a first-nation community-directed process for the virtual management of CBP. This process resulted in enhanced relationships and trust among the team.
Although we found no significant differences in self-reported pain, function or quality of life in pre and post measures, there were rich findings gathered from semi-structured interviews with participants and health care providers. In this way, qualitative information offered a deeper look at the results of the intervention from the patients’ viewpoints.
From the qualitative analysis of semi-structured interviews with patient participants, we identified themes related to the community and healthcare context, community preferences for back pain care, and experiences with the virtual back pain clinic. Within the community, participants identified that limited health and recreation resources, as well as safety concerns limiting the ability to walk outside, contributed to their difficulties in managing CBP. Community members wanted the use of Cree in their care, preventative education and exercise programming, and the opportunity to engage in traditional methods of healing for CBP. They appreciated the opportunity to access PT through the RPR, not having to travel, and enjoyed learning exercises for self-management. They were comfortable with the RPR. They wanted more frequent opportunities for PT and would like a PT from their community, as well as a private space to do exercise.
Interviews with health providers highlighted the themes of getting people to the clinic, experience with virtual back pain clinic, and ways to enhance care. Providers felt that they needed to develop comfort using technology to manage CBP and noted that internet capacity at some times limited their experience. They recommended that a larger space is needed for PT programming and that a hybrid (a combination of RPR and in-person care) would be beneficial additions.
Although Canadian and Australian studies have shown that Indigenous peoples experience CBP in ways that can greatly affect their lives [ , ], there is a gap in evidence to demonstrate the effect of virtual care with Indigenous peoples who have CBP. In addition, few studies have described a community-directed approach to the implementation and evaluation of a new virtual care process for CBP management in Indigenous communities. In a previous case study, Lovo et al. reported that the use of the RPR for an assessment and follow-up management of back pain was feasible [ ]. Lovo et al. reported that the use of virtual care for CBP management in a rural non-Indigenous community resulted in patient satisfaction as well as objective gains in the range of lumbar spine range of motion and nerve mobility, and the experiences for patients that included enhanced access to specialised care for CBP [ ]. Remote presence and virtual technology are being increasingly implemented and evaluated in the management of chronic conditions, including CBP and other musculoskeletal conditions [ ]. However, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, no prior study has conducted a collaborative, community-directed intervention to co-develop and evaluate a virtual CBP clinic. The present approach prioritised relationship building and Indigenous knowledges to ensure a culturally responsive, person- and community-centered approach, as recommended by Reichert et al. (2023) and Moeke et al. (2024) [ , ]. Prioritisation of Indigenous knowledges, and inclusion of Elders and Knowledge Keepers in health system and research processes is understood to be a critical component of ethical research with Indigenous communities and addresses a Truth and Reconciliation Commission Call to Action 22 to understand Indigenous health practices and work together with Elders and Knowledge Keepers for the health of Indigenous peoples [ ].
The findings of this research support the findings of Crockett et al. (2024), who interviewed Saskatchewan patients with CBP from urban, rural and remote areas. The participants in that study identified the importance of back pain education and self-management strategies for their CBP [ ]. Crockett et al. (2024) also reported that Saskatchewan health providers felt that virtual opportunities to connect patients seeking care for CBP were important, and that available care in patients’ home communities would facilitate CBP management [ ].
This project had several limitations. A hybrid intervention (virtual care and some in-person treatments where the PT would travel to the community) was the original intent of this study. Unfortunately, owing to COVID-19 restrictions, this was not possible, so the entire intervention was delivered virtually. Moeke et al. (2024) recommended hybrid care for rehabilitation in Indigenous communities to ensure in person services in addition to virtual care for hands on care and problem solving, which are important for relationship building and trust [ ]. This recommendation is in keeping with our findings as well. Additionally, the logistics of scheduling are highly complex because of the remote nature of the intervention, as well as the fact that the research team, patients and PTs were in different locations. In addition, at times, due to a lack of transport or inclement weather, participants may not have reached the appointment. As a result of scheduling difficulties, there was also a reduced number of fully completed pre- and post-outcome measures than we had predicted. Research staff traveled to the remote location several times to facilitate data collection; however, the collection of pre- and post-quantitative data is challenging.
The relationship among research and community team members has strengthened and resulted in the development of future directions and goals for this research. First, the evaluation of the impact of hybrid care on the management of other chronic conditions is now occurring, and long-term evaluations of community-directed hybrid models of care for patients with CBP are being conducted. We have identified that additional remote research staff in future projects would facilitate data collection and reporting. Remote nursing staff are extremely busy in their roles, and it is not realistic to assume that this role can be covered within a clinical day. In addition to research assistant staff in remote communities, trained PT assistant staffing and support for community members in PT training may enhance comfort for patients and consistency in the provision of service. Finally, owing to desires for more frequent PT access and room to do exercise, an examination of options for expanded space for virtual and hybrid rehabilitation in remote communities must occur.

## Conclusion
This project demonstrated the ability to strengthen relationships with a remote Cree community to undertake the co-development, implementation and evaluation of a virtual PT and NP clinic for the management of CBP. The focus on collaboration and relationships, as well as guidance and direction from community Knowledge Keepers, Elders and members including the modification of outcome measures to better reflect their lived experience, have resulted in a more culturally responsive model of virtual care for CBP management. Although quantitative outcome measures for pain and quality of life did not significantly differ between those who responded pre and post intervention, qualitative data identified appreciation for the availability of CBP care in the community, less travel, and opportunity to learn exercises for self-management strategies. Both patient participants and health providers had suggestions for improving the experience, including a combination of virtual and in-person sessions (hybrid care). This work can guide best practice strategies and future interventions in CBP and other musculoskeletal condition management in remote Indigenous communities and has implications for virtual PT practice, education and policy development.
