## Background
Early detection of Tuberculosis (TB) is critical to interrupt transmission and improve treatment outcomes. Community pharmacies are often the first place people with symptoms seek care; therefore, they are well-positioned to assist in the early detection and referral of TB cases. This study aimed to evaluate the outcomes of a TB case detection and management educational intervention among community pharmacists (CPs) using Lagos State, Nigeria as a case study.

## Methods
A quasi-experimental, single-group pre-post training with supportive follow-up study was conducted among CPs who participated in a structured TB training program. Six months after the training with monthly follow-up and reminders during the six months period, data were collected using a structured questionnaire to assess CPs’ post-training involvement in TB case detection, referral practices, perceived practice-level effects, and implementation challenges. Mystery shopper visits were conducted in selected community pharmacies to corroborate self-reported practices. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 17 at a 5% level of significance.

## Results
A statistically significant, though weak, positive correlation was found between CPs’ involvement in TB case detection and management support (r = 0.223; p < 0.05). Of the participants, 112 (71.3%) indicated they would like to continue providing referral services, and 73 (46.5%) suggested that pharmacists who provide referral services should receive incentives.

## Conclusion
This study suggests that educational intervention among health care providers may have some beneficial effects in TB case detection and referral activities. With proper training, regulatory oversight, and logistical support; CPs’ engagement in tuberculosis case detection and management may be a key strategy to increasing early TB case detection and management rates in Nigeria. Future studies should employ a randomized controlled study design to assess the effectiveness and scalability of pharmacist-led TB referral interventions.

## Background
Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major global public health challenge and a leading cause of infectious disease-related morbidity and mortality [ ]. In 2024, an estimated 10.7 million individuals worldwide were afflicted with TB, with a substantial proportion occurring in low- and middle-income countries [ ]. Nigeria accounts for approximately 4.8% of global TB cases and an estimated 11% of all unreported cases, ranking sixth among the 30 high TB-burden countries worldwide and the highest in Africa [ , ]. 
According to the WHO, Nigeria is among the ‘20 + 10’ Tuberculosis (TB) high burden countries of the world, with a prevalence of 330/100,000 population. However, the annual case finding ranged between 90,000 and 100,000, implying a case notification rate of about 49/100,000, which is far below the estimated prevalence of 330/100,000 in the population in 2014 and the case detection rate of 15% [ , ]. Additionally, Nigeria faces a high prevalence of TB–HIV co-infection and multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB).
In recognition of the need to accelerate progress towards ending the TB epidemic by 2035, the World Health Organization (WHO), through its End TB Strategy, emphasized the importance of engaging a broader range of stakeholders from both the public and private sectors [ ]. Consequently, strategic interventions to lower the country’s TB burden by 2030, through strengthened TB case detection and notification, remains a priority for Nigeria’s Federal Ministry of Health, the WHO, and other partners [ ]. 
Nigeria continues to face significant gaps in case detection and notification, leading to delayed diagnoses and ongoing community transmission. Early identification and referral of individuals with symptoms suggestive of TB are critical to interrupt transmission and improve outcomes.
The private health sector accounts for 33% of health care facilities and provides medical care to 60% of Nigerians [ ]. In Nigeria, individuals with persistent cough or other TB-related symptoms frequently seek initial care from pharmacies due to convenience, reduced waiting times, lower costs, and perceived confidentiality [ , , ]. Community pharmacists (CPs) therefore represent an important but underutilized entry point for TB screening and referral within national TB control strategies. Despite this potential, TB-related practices in private pharmacies are often inconsistent with recommended guidelines. Evidence from low- and middle-income countries reported limited recognition of presumptive TB symptoms, inappropriate antibiotic dispensing, and weak referral linkages with public TB services [ ]. 
Opinions regarding the private sector’s ability to provide healthcare, especially to vulnerable populations, are divided. While an increasing proportion of the public thinks that governments can break their commitment to equitable healthcare by enlisting private enterprises’ aid in providing medical care, a raised issue is the uneven caliber of services rendered by the private sector [ ]. One well-known example is the widespread misdiagnosis and treatment of various ailments by several private healthcare practitioners, including infectious diseases like HIV and TB, which can spread to other individuals [ ]. According to Daftary et al. (2017) and Sullivan, et al. (2017), the most commonly cited barriers include a disjointed healthcare system for tuberculosis screening, diagnosis, and treatment; logistical problems; and a lack of knowledge about the illness [ , ]. 
Although training programs for pharmacists have been implemented in several settings, important scientific gaps remain. Most research studies focus on knowledge gains or immediate post-training outcomes, with limited documentation of how pharmacists translate training into routine practice over time. There is scarce evidence from high-burden urban settings such as Lagos State, Nigeria, on CPs’ real-world referral behaviors, implementation challenges, and practice-level experiences following training. Such information is essential for assessing the feasibility and sustainability of engaging CPs in TB control.
These gaps highlight the need for structured, targeted capacity-building interventions to align CPs’ practices with national TB control guidelines. Hence, a three-phase research program was conducted to strengthen TB-related capacity among CPs in Lagos State. The first phase was a cross-sectional baseline assessment [ ]. The second phase focused on the development and evaluation of a structured TB training program for the CPs [ ]. Building on these earlier phases, the present study represents the six-month follow-up phase and provides a descriptive assessment of post-training practices, triangulated with mystery shopper observations. Findings from this study are intended to inform policy and guide future implementation strategies for private-sector engagement in TB control in Nigeria.

## Methods

## Study design
This study employed a quasi-experimental, single-group pre-post design with a supportive observational validation component. The present study represents the third phase of a three-phase intervention program aimed at strengthening CPs’ engagement in TB control activities in Nigeria using Lagos State as a case study. Pharmacists were compared to themselves over time (baseline vs. six-month follow-up) to document changes in TB case detection, management, and referral practices. This approach was explicitly exploratory/descriptive: no control group was included, so the analysis focused on patterns of change rather than formal causal inference, consistent with programmatic implementation evaluations.

## Study settings
The study was conducted in Lagos State, located in south-west Nigeria. Lagos State is the smallest State in Nigeria by land mass, covering an area of 3,345 square kilometers [ ]. However, it is the most populous and economically active State in the country and hosts a large concentration of private healthcare providers, including CPs [ ]. These characteristics make Lagos State a relevant setting for evaluating private-sector engagement in TB control.

## Study population
Study participants were registered community pharmacists in Lagos who enrolled in the TB training program. In Phase 1 of the program, 201 CPs completed a pre-training (baseline) questionnaire [ ]. Of these, 191 participated in the Phase 2 TB training workshop [ ]. In Phase 3, 157 participants (78% of the original cohort) gave their consent and completed the post-training assessment at the end of the six-month follow-up period.

## Participant flow and attrition
At follow-up, only the 157 pharmacists who completed the post-intervention assessment were included in the paired analysis (follow-up rate: 78.1%). The main reasons for participants’ loss to follow-up were relocation or inability to be reached despite repeated contact attempts. To assess potential selection bias due to participant attrition, baseline characteristics of the 157 completers were compared with those of the 44 who were lost to follow-up; no significant differences were found in their socio-demographic characteristics or baseline TB involvement measures (p > 0.05).

## Description of the intervention
The intervention was performed in two parts. The first was a one-day in-person structured TB training workshop, designed to build capacity and standardize CPs’ roles in TB control in line with national guidelines. The workshop was conducted at a central venue (the Pharmaceutical Society of Nigeria’s auditorium in Lagos) and employed a mix of lectures, case studies, and group discussions. Resource persons who delivered the training were experts in TB management and were drawn from reputable organizations, namely: the Pharmacy Council of Nigeria, KNCV Tuberculosis Foundation, Howard University Global Health Initiative Nigeria, the Lagos State Ministry of Health, and the Lagos branch of the Association of Community Pharmacists of Nigeria. The training content and learning objectives included: refreshing participants’ knowledge of TB signs, symptoms, and control; clarifying CPs’ role in identifying presumptive TB cases; introducing standardized referral pathways to designated DOTS centers, documentation procedures, and collaboration with local TB focal persons. The training was complemented by the provision of Information, Education and Communication (IEC) materials; referral forms; and TB registers which were supplied by the Lagos State Ministry of Health and the KNCV Tuberculosis Foundation. Certificates of participation were issued to participants at the end of the workshop.
The second part of the intervention was a monthly follow-up support to reinforce training content and minimize loss to follow-up. For six months, trained research assistants contacted each participant monthly (via phone calls and WhatsApp messages). During those contact periods, the research assistants reemphasized the need for effective referral of suspected TB cases, responded to technical questions, and sought clarifications from the principal investigator when necessary. Additional materials (e.g., IECs and referral forms) were distributed upon request.

## Mystery shopper visits
To validate self-reported practices during the follow-up period, trained mystery shoppers conducted unannounced visits to a purposive sample of 20 participating community pharmacies. This subset of pharmacies was chosen to cover different geographic areas of Lagos (urban and peri-urban) and patient volumes (high and low). Research assistants were chosen as mystery clients and trained using a standardized symptom script suggestive of presumptive pulmonary TB (e.g., persistent cough for ≥ 2 weeks, fatigue, weight loss). During each mystery visit, mystery shoppers presented the same case scenario without explicitly requesting a test or referral. Encounters were documented immediately after visits using a structured checklist to record each pharmacist’s responses, including referral to DOTS center or alternative management practices. Appropriate referral was predefined as referral to a designated DOTS center for diagnostic testing. If the pharmacist instead dispensed medications or gave general advice without recommending a formal TB evaluation, the encounter was coded as non-referral.

## Data collection instruments
Data were collected using two structured questionnaires: one administered at baseline (pre-training) and the other six-month post-training. The pre-training questionnaire, which was adapted from similar studies and designed to assess CPs’ knowledge and involvement in TB case detection and management, was developed as part of the initial phase of this multi-phase study and published elsewhere [ , , ]. It comprised items covering key domains such as participants’ socio-demographic characteristics, knowledge of TB, knowledge of National Tuberculosis Program and treatment regimen, and TB case detection and management (see Appendix ).
The post-training questionnaire was developed as a modification of the pre-training questionnaire. It was designed to assess participants’ sociodemographic characteristics and post-training activities such as identification of presumptive TB cases, TB case management, and referral of suspected TB cases, number of positive cases, status of the community pharmacies with respect to DOTS centers, and their perceptions of the intervention and institutionalization of TB-related activities (see Appendix ). While the post-training questionnaire included additional items to capture post-intervention experiences, the core items used to compute the involvement scores were retained across both questionnaires in order to ensure comparability. Modifications were limited to minor wording adjustments for clarity and appropriateness; only these comparable items were included in the pre-post comparative analysis.
The contents of both questionnaires underwent expert review for content and face validity. Specifically, the instruments were reviewed by the training resource persons in line with their expectations of the study participants’ post-training practices. Their feedback informed refinement of item wording, clarity, and relevance prior to data collection. No pilot study was conducted. Responses to the TB practice items were later used to compute the outcome measures described below (see Outcome measures).

## Outcome measures
The two primary outcomes of interest were CPs’ self-reported Involvement in TB case detection and their Involvement in TB management support. Because both outcomes were defined a priori as behavioral constructs that cannot be measured using a single indicator or a psychometric scale, they were operationalized as composite scores derived from self-reported practice activities to reflect distinct areas of professional engagement targeted by the intervention (as detailed below).

Involvement in TB case detection: This domain measured CPs participation in early identification and referral of presumptive TB cases. Indicators included:

Self-reported identification of clients suspected of pulmonary TB (Item 9),
Actions taken for suspected cases, particularly referral to a health facility (Item 10),
Number of suspected TB patients referred following the training (Item 11).

Responses reflecting active engagement in case detection were assigned a score of 1, and 0 if otherwise. For the “Number of suspected TB patients referred” variable, responses were dichotomised to reflect meaningful participation (≥ 1 referral = 1; none = 0), consistent with the programme’s expectations that any referral represents an active involvement. Subsequently, item scores were summed; higher scores indicated greater participation in TB case detection and referral practices. The total score ranged from 0 (no detection activities reported) up to a maximum of 3 if all items were positive.

2.Involvement in TB management support: This domain measured pharmacists’ participation in activities supporting continuity of care after referral. Indicators included:

Follow-up of referred patients (Item 13a),
Mode of follow-up, selected at least one (Item 13b),

For Item 13a, responses were scored 1 if a referred patient was followed up, and 0 if not. For the mode of follow-up, responses were scored as 1 if at least one mode was used. Scores were then summed; higher values reflected greater contribution to treatment-support functions. The total score ranged from 0 (no management support reported) up to a maximum of 2 if all items were positive.

Furthermore, the secondary outcome measures included:

Referral productivity, assessed using the self-reported number of TB suspects referred (Item 11).
Follow-up practices, evaluated by reported mechanisms used to track referred patients (Item 13b).
Perceived practice impact, measured through multiple-response items describing professional, social, or economic effects of involvement (Item 16).
Barriers and facilitators to engagement (Items 17 and 18).
Sustainability indicators, including willingness to continue referral services and desire for further training or incentives (Items 19 to 21).

## Data analysis
Data was entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using SPSS version 17. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and practice patterns. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations. Involvement in TB case detection and Involvement in TB management support scores were treated as continuous variables for analysis. Because the same participants were evaluated at baseline and follow-up using comparable core items, paired-sample t-tests were used to compare pre- and post-training mean scores of the core items, thereby ensuring methodological appropriateness. Mean differences, t-values, and corresponding p-values were reported. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed). This approach was considered appropriate given the approximately symmetric distribution of summed scores and the moderate sample size (n = 157), which supports robustness of parametric testing under the central limit theorem. To aid interpretation of the magnitude of change, effect sizes (Cohen’s d for repeated measures) were calculated and interpreted using conventional thresholds (0.2 = small effect, 0.5 = moderate effect, 0.8 = large effect).
To examine the association between CPs’ involvement in TB case detection and their involvement in TB management support, Pearson’s correlation coefficients were computed. Correlation strength was interpreted conservatively (r < 0.30 = weak; 0.30–0.49 = moderate; ≥0.50 = strong), recognizing that statistically significant correlations may reflect small effect sizes.

## Results

## Participant flow
Of the 201 pharmacists assessed at baseline, 157 completed the evaluation (follow-up rate: 78.1%) and were included in the paired analysis (see Fig.  ).

 

## Socio-demographic characteristics of participants
Table  presents the socio-demographic and practice characteristics of the participants. As shown, the mean age of participants was 36.6 ± 5.23 years. Those aged 41 years and older accounted for the majority (45.9%), followed by those aged 36–40 years (19.7%). There were slightly more males (52.9%) than females (47.1%). Most participants were married (79%) and had a Bachelor of Pharmacy (B. Pharm) degree as their highest academic qualification (72.6%) coupled with 1–10 years in community pharmacy practice (56.7%).

 

## Community pharmacists’ practice of TB case detection, management, and post-referral outcomes following the training intervention
While most of the participants (93.6%) reported that their pharmacy was not a DOTS center, the majority (84.7%) reported they had been identifying suspected TB clients (see Table  ). Of these, only 7.5% reported treating such clients with antibiotics, while the majority (92.5%) referred suspected cases to healthcare facilities. Regarding referral volume, 73.2% (90 out of 123) of those who identified presumptive TB cases reported referring 1–3 cases, whereas, 18.7% reported referring 4 or more cases while 8.1% reported no referrals at all. Among those who referred (n = 123), 51.2% followed up on these clients mainly via phone calls (63.5%) and arranged appointments (25.4%) while 11.1% called the referral center directly.
Participating CPs reported referring a total of 303 presumptive tuberculosis cases to designated DOTS centers. Of these referrals, 18 cases (5.9%) were bacteriologically confirmed as having TB while the number of cases evaluated and confirmed negative for TB was undocumented, leaving 94.1% of cases with unknown TB outcomes. Thus, the proportion of confirmed cases represents the minimum observable confirmation rate and should not be interpreted as the diagnostic yield of pharmacist referrals.

 

## Mystery shopper assessment
Of the 20 CPs purposively sampled and visited by mystery shoppers, 80% appropriately referred mystery shoppers to designated DOTS centers without dispensing antibiotics or symptomatic treatments while 20.0% recommended cough syrups and/or antibiotics (see Fig.  ).

 

## Changes in community pharmacists’ involvement in TB case detection and management support after the intervention
Table  presents the outcome of the paired-samples t-tests evaluating CPs’ changes in involvement in TB case detection and management support following the training intervention. As shown, the mean involvement scores for TB case detection increased from 1.44 ± 0.83 (at baseline) to 1.57 ± 0.74. The mean difference was 0.13 (95% CI: 0.01–0.25, t = 2.086, p = 0.039). The observed change corresponded to a small effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.19). Similarly, the mean involvement scores for TB management support increased from 0.72 ± 0.45 (at baseline) to 0.83 ± 0.38. The mean difference was 0.11 (95% CI: 0.03–0.1, t = 2.579, p = 0.011). This observed change also represented a small effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.24).

 

## Association between community pharmacists’ involvement in TB case detection and management support
Pearson’s correlation analysis demonstrated a weak (but statistically significant) positive association between CPs’ involvement in TB case detection and their involvement in TB management support (r = 0.223, p < 0.05).

## Perceived effects of TB case detection and referral on community pharmacy practice
The most frequently reported benefit of CPs’ involvement in TB case detection and referral activities was improvement in professionalism (28.7%). This was followed by increased clientele (26.1%), and an increase in the public’s image of and trust in their pharmacy practice (24.2%) (See Fig.  ).

 

## Challenges encountered by community pharmacists during TB case detection and referral and how they were resolved
Table  presents the challenges encountered by CPs during TB case detection and referral, along with the strategies adopted to address them. The most frequently reported challenge was fear of stigma among patients following a potential positive TB diagnosis (18.5%). Other commonly reported challenges included patient self-denial and refusal to visit referral centers (17.8%), and poor follow-up mechanisms (15.3%). In response to these challenges, participants described several coping strategies. The most commonly adopted approach was improved patient counselling aimed at addressing fear, stigma, and misconceptions about TB (36.3%). Other strategies included direct communication with referral centers to ensure patients were attended to in accordance with established procedures (26.8%), and contacting the researcher or Local Government Area (LGA) TB focal person for guidance and support (19.7%).

 

## Institutionalization of TB case detection and management in community pharmacies
Table  summarizes participants’ perceptions regarding the sustainability of TB case detection and referral as routine community pharmacy functions. Although the majority (80.3%) reported no further training/workshop during the six-month follow-up period, 72% expressed a desire for additional training to help improve referral rates of presumptive TB cases. Furthermore, 71.3% indicated a willingness to continue providing TB referral services, and 46.5% supported the introduction of financial incentives for referral pharmacies.

 

## Discussion
This study reports findings from the third phase of a multi-phase intervention aimed at scaling up knowledge and strengthening CPs’ involvement in TB case detection and referral, using Lagos State, Nigeria as a case study. Unlike experimental evaluations designed to establish causality, this study focused on describing post-training practices, perceived practice-level effects, and implementation challenges encountered by trained CPs during routine service delivery. The findings provide insights into how CPs operationalized TB referral expectations following a structured training program and ongoing programmatic support.
Overall, a high proportion of participants reported identifying clients with pulmonary TB symptoms and referring them to designated health facilities. These self-reports were corroborated by mystery shoppers’ findings, in which most visits appropriately resulted in referral to DOTS centers. These observations fit with the fact that private pharmacies often serve as the first point of contact for people with TB symptoms in high-burden settings [ ]. Thus, establishing structured referral links from pharmacies to DOTS centers and involving CPs in community-based interventions (like TB mobilizations and information dissemination) may help identify cases that might be missed [ , ]. In this study, the high referral rate suggests that trained CPs may be able to understand and follow national TB referral pathways. Likewise, a rising corpus of studies supports CPs’ involvement in TB prevention and control [ , – ]. At the same time, inappropriate dispensing of antibiotics was observed in a minority of cases. This finding is consistent with prior studies which revealed that community pharmacies frequently dispensed antibiotics for respiratory symptoms in settings where oversight is limited [ , ]. This practice raises concerns for antimicrobial stewardship as unnecessary antibiotic use in patients with undiagnosed TB can promote antimicrobial resistance (including multidrug-resistant TB) [ ]. Thus, strengthened regulation and oversight – such as enforcing prescription-only policies and reinforcing pharmacy training – will be needed to ensure rational dispensing and adherence to TB referral guidelines.
The observed proportion of referred presumptive TB cases that tested positive for TB was relatively low. Since a substantial proportion of outcome data for referred cases was unavailable, this observed confirmatory rate reflects only the minimum observable proportion rather than the true diagnostic yield of CPs’ referrals. This limitation highlights challenges in feedback mechanisms between private pharmacies and national TB programs. However, similar studies have demonstrated that high referral volumes do not necessarily translate into high confirmation rates [ , ]. This is unsurprising given the non-specific nature of symptoms like cough. Nevertheless, an intervention study in Tanzania demonstrated that CPs-led referral of suspected TB cases resulted in higher bacteriologically confirmed TB rates [ ]. Thus, referral of presumptive cases remains a critical entry point for early diagnosis and contributes to strengthening TB surveillance systems, even when confirmatory yields are modest.
The observed increments in pre- and post-training comparisons of self-reported involvement scores in TB case detection and management support were statistically significant but small in magnitude, as reflected by their small effect sizes. These findings suggest incremental behavioral adjustments rather than substantial shifts in practice. Also, the observed correlation between case-detection and management support was weak, suggesting that engagement in one domain may or may not necessarily translate into comprehensive participation across the continuum of TB care. However, prior evidence indicate that targeted capacity-building interventions can enhance health workers’ engagement in disease control activities when appropriately supported [ , , , ]. 
Pharmacists reported several perceived benefits associated with their involvement in TB case detection and referral. Consistent with other CPs’ experiences, many participants reported improvements in professionalism, enhanced public trust, and increased client loyalty [ , ]. At the same time, nearly half of CPs expressed desire for financial incentives and nearly two-third expressed desire for further training to enhance referrals. These findings suggest that combining training with appropriate incentives (monetary or in-kind) can help support and sustain CPs’ active involvement in TB control.
Despite these positive observations, participants reported several implementation challenges, including patient-level barriers (such as fear of stigma and refusal to attend referral centers) as well as system-level barriers (such as poor follow-up mechanisms and operational constraints within public health facilities). The strategies adopted by CPs to address these challenges (e.g., improved patient counselling and direct communication with referral centers or TB focal persons) suggest that CPs can function as effective intermediaries between patients and healthcare systems.
Notably, some CPs incorrectly claimed their premises were DOTS centers. Official verification from TB focal persons, partner organizations, and the National TB Control Program confirmed that no community pharmacies are accredited in Lagos, revealing significant knowledge gaps. This finding highlights the need for stronger regulatory clarity and the incorporation of TB case-control into educational curricula. For example, the Pharmacy Council of Nigeria and TB focal persons could jointly provide training/certification so that CPs and students clearly understand that they are referral points, not treatment centers, and that TB medications require prescriptions.

## Study strengths and limitations
Remarkably, this project has strengthened linkages and collaborations between CPs and the local TB focal persons. It also represents an important health systems gain beyond individual-level training outcomes. Additionally, while the National TB program has not formally assigned a defined TB control role to CPs,
the study revealed that CPs can contribute meaningfully towards TB control. Thus, the institutionalization of TB case detection and referral as part of routine community pharmacy practice is feasible.
Despite these strengths, the study has several limitations that should be noted. First, the single-group pre–post design and reliance on cross-sectional follow-up data mean that the study procedures themselves (including the follow-up calls) may have influenced behavior, and no causal inference is possible. Second, the primary outcomes were based on CPs’ self-reports, which are subject to social desirability and recall bias, especially after a normative training intervention. Although we attempted to validate practices with mystery clients, those visits were limited to 20 pharmacies and may not capture all variations in practice. Third, about 22% of participants were lost to follow-up. We compared baseline characteristics of completers versus dropouts and found no statistically significant differences, but differential dropout (for example, if less-engaged pharmacists were more likely to drop out) could still bias the results. Fourth, there may have been a Hawthorne effect: CPs knew they had been trained and that their practices might be checked, which could temporarily increase adherence to desired behaviors. Finally, generalizability is limited: Lagos is an urban, high-burden setting, and participants volunteered for a training. Results may differ in rural areas or in settings with different health-seeking patterns. We also did not include other private providers (such as patent medicine vendors), so the findings strictly apply to licensed CPs.

## Conclusion
This study provides exploratory evidence that adds to the growing regional and international body of work supporting the strategic engagement of CPs in TB case detection and referral. While a single training intervention is unlikely to produce sustained behavioral change in isolation, the findings demonstrate that trained CPs can align their practices with national TB referral expectations, thereby contributing to the End TB strategy objectives. However, given the single-group pre-post design, reliance on self-reported measures (with partial validation through mystery client assessments), modest effect sizes, and the absence of a control group, these findings should be interpreted as indicative of feasibility and potential contribution rather than as evidence of causal effectiveness.
Importantly, the study aligns with key strategic priorities of Nigeria’s National Strategic Plan for Tuberculosis (NSP-TB), which emphasizes integrating TB screening, referral, and case-finding into the routine activities of non-TB service providers, including private-sector and community-based actors, to improve case notification at relatively low cost. The findings are also consistent with priority areas identified in the National Strategic Health Development Plan (NSHDP), which advocates for strengthened public–private collaboration in health service delivery.
By demonstrating the feasibility of engaging CPs in TB referral activities within routine practice settings, this study underscores the potential of pharmacists as accessible partners in TB control in high-burden settings. Integrating CPs into TB control efforts through structured training, sustained engagement, supportive supervision, and clear regulatory frameworks may strengthen health system responses to tuberculosis in Nigeria and other similar high-burden settings.

## Suggestions for future research
The high cost of research limited the study to CPs only. Future research employing controlled study designs, longer follow-up, and the inclusion of Patent and Proprietary Medicine Vendors (PPMVs) and other community stakeholders, who are more widely distributed across Nigeria’s local government areas, is warranted to further the effectiveness, scalability, and sustainability of pharmacist-led TB referral interventions and also understand why some CPs recommend antibiotics for presumptive TP clients.

Permission to conduct the study among community pharmacists was also granted by the Association of Community Pharmacists of Nigeria (ACPN), Lagos State Branch. All participants were briefed on the study’s objectives, the voluntary nature of their participation, and their right to withdraw at any stage without penalty. Participants’ voluntary attendance at the interventional training and endorsement of the informed consent (see Appendix ), confirmed their willingness to participate in the study. Data were anonymized to ensure participant confidentiality and privacy.
