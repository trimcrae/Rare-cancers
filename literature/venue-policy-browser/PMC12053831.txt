## Abstract
The way political identity serves as a foundation for political polarization in the United States permits elites to extend conflict rapidly to new issue areas. Further, the types of cognitive mechanisms and shortcuts used in the politically polarized information environment are similar to some of those used in risk perception. Consequently, political elites may easily create partisan risk positions, largely through politically focused social amplification of risk. The COVID‐19 pandemic provided a natural experiment for testing predictions about such risk politics. We asked questions about pandemic‐related views, behaviors, and policies at the outset of the pandemic in April 2020 and again in September 2020 via public opinion surveys. Our data and analyses focus primarily on a single state, with some analysis extended to four states. We begin by demonstrating strong linkages between political partisan identification on the one hand and support for co‐partisan elites, use of partisan information sources, and support for co‐partisan policies on the other hand. We then find evidence that pandemic risk positions correspond with partisan information sources and find support for a mechanism involving partisan‐tinted evaluation of elite cues. Partisan risk positions quickly became part of the larger polarized structure of political support and views. Finally, our evidence shows on the balance that partisan risk positions related to the pandemic coalesced and strengthened over time. Overall, while self‐identified Democrats consistently viewed the coronavirus as the primary threat, self‐identified Republicans quickly pivoted toward threats to their freedoms and to the economy.

Multiple mechanisms reinforce each other in creating these “echo chambers.” One mechanism is an affective response by which an individual associates liking and positive feelings with their co‐partisans and perhaps even stronger dislike and negative feelings with the “others” (Druckman & Levendusky, ; Iyengar et al., ). People search for information from elites and peers they like—people on their partisan “team.” Changes to the media environment have made finding confirmatory (Taber & Lodge, ) messages easier. Audience members can readily find media sources sharing their political predispositions and views (Prior, ; Stroud, ), an activity known as partisan selective exposure to information. In a relatively recent survey, 93% of those who said Fox News (a conservative source) was their main source for political and election news identified as Republican, while 93% of those choosing MSNBC (a liberal source) identified as Democratic (Grieco, ). These echo chambers in which ideas go unchallenged are also evident in social media (Cinelli et al., ; Jamieson & Cappella, ).
Consistent exposure to partisan messaging subsequently influences individual beliefs and attitudes (see Figure  ). Partisan information sources push people toward views that align with the respective foundational views of the political party. Boundedly rational explanations about the influence of partisan cues on mass opinion emphasize cognitive limitations and shortcuts. According to one line of thinking, mass cognition about complex policies typically uses the “peripheral” route, which involves shortcuts or heuristics rather than careful reasoning (Petty & Cacioppo, ). Source credibility or trust is one commonly cited heuristic (Zaller, ), though distrust also increases reliance on partisan predispositions (Ladd, ). Elite cues can provide guidance on important policy issues that translates directly into partisan policy support among the masses (Yeyati et al., ; Zaller, ), an effect that is even more pronounced in a polarized environment (Druckman et al., ). Affective response (Lodge & Taber, ) is another evaluative shortcut. Further, individuals do not accept information in uniform ways. People readily accept opinion‐confirming information and critically evaluate disconfirming evidence via “biased assimilation” (Lord et al., ). Two individuals exposed to the exact same information might interpret that information in different ways due to confirmation bias (Modgil et al., ). Finally, individuals engaging in “partisan motivated reasoning” are also taking positions consistent with partisan endorsements but may be expending more cognitive effort to do so (Bisgaard, ; Bolsen et al., ; Leeper & Slothuus, ). 

Even the different “boxes” (see Figure  ) that compose the set of standard partisan beliefs and attitudes can be simple. Cues in the information environment (in the form of brief spoken messages, symbols, pictures, etc.) can push people toward feelings and oversimplified views about situations and policies. “Frames” about problems can help with categorizing an issue (see Shanahan et al., ). Views of problems often overlap significantly with perceived threats. These views might also—separately or in conjunction—invoke standard elements of stories that we seem hardwired to accept as human beings. The Narrative Policy Framework provides theoretical grounding for thinking about these boxes (Jones et al., ). For example, heroes are characters often invoked in policy discussions. In the hands of skilled persuaders, uncomplicated views of threats and problems lead in straightforward ways to desired views of heroes and basic solutions. Despite dynamic interaction, these foundational views must retain some stability to serve their function well.
In review, we outline a relatively minimalist process involving identity politics, communication patterns, and cue taking. Upon establishing a political identity, an individual simply needs to locate their “team's” information sources and then follow the cues. They can quickly absorb messages from trusted/liked sources or interpret information in partisan‐consistent ways that avoid cognitive dissonance (Harmon‐Jones & Mills, ). This process leads to a set of individual attitudes and behaviors that are consistent with the mainstream of the political faction, manifesting in aggregate partisan agreement in public opinion and behavior.
To test the Figure  model in our data, we start with a dueling‐paths hypothesis that shows the extent of existing political polarization. Partisan identity serves as a foundation for a split in support for elites, selective exposure to information, and support for well‐established policy positions. We must establish that such polarization exists in our sample to proceed with additional tests. Voting for in‐party elites (i.e., co‐partisan elites) is quite strong in absolute terms in the United States, with increasing partisan voting in US presidential and congressional elections and lower levels of ticket splitting over time (Bartels, ; Bump, ; Weinschenk, ). Media distrust, which has been increasing (Gottfried & Liedke, ), contributes to more partisan voting (Ladd, ). Similar trends are visible for presidential approval (Bond & Fleisher, ; Donovan et al., ). Support for in‐party elites is often an important precursor to effective elite–mass cue transmission. Effective cue‐taking should benefit from a relatively uniform partisan information environment, which requires evidence of partisan selective exposure to information. Finally, evidence of public opinion polarization on standard issues helps establish that this process works generally. Accordingly, our dueling‐paths hypothesis about the centrality of political identity for political views and behaviors produces the three specific subhypotheses that follow.
H1A: Individuals support co‐partisan elites.
H1B: Individuals engage in partisan selective exposure to information.
H1C: Individuals support well‐established co‐partisan policy positions.

## Risk politics
We now examine the potential for assimilating individual risk positions into the partisan mainstream. The field of risk is a scientific and mathematical one, and the calculation of objective, technical risk can be complex. Consequently, politicization could prove difficult, especially in a specialized field such as public health. However, some branches of the literature on risk perception align remarkably well with the ideas and mechanisms presented in the preceding subsection on political polarization.
The largely negative nature of US politics during the present period of polarization matches well with the inherently negative nature of risk evaluation, which is about the likelihood of bad things happening. Negative advertising strongly influences affective polarization (Iyengar et al., ). Further, “negative partisanship” has been the key driving dynamic in partisan sorting (Abramowitz & Webster, ). The dislike and distrust that characterize affective polarization have become powerful forces in US politics (Iyengar et al., ). Claims about risk, often presented through fear appeals (Glassner, ; Tannenbaum et al., ), fit comfortably into narratives about out‐partisans being dumb, immoral, extreme, dangerous, and untrustworthy.
The well‐established field of risk studies spans multiple disciplines. Psychological approaches to risk perception date to the 1970s (see Slovic et al., ). Such studies have shown that people tend to use a variety of heuristics (i.e., cognitive shortcuts or rules of thumb) in perceiving risk. These heuristics include the cognitive availability of examples (Tversky & Kahneman, ), dread of a situation (Slovic et al., ), and the representativeness of events and avoidance of cognitive dissonance (see overview in Rohrmann & Renn, ). More recent psychological research has homed in on factors such as affective reactions, emotions, and worldviews in risk perception (Dickert et al., ; Magnan et al., ; Raile et al., ; Siegrist & Árvai, ; Slovic, ). Another broad approach has focused on risk perception as an element or outcome of cultural and social processes (Beck, ; Douglas, ; Giddens, ; Johnson & Swedlow, ; Lupton, ). The social amplification of risk framework (SARF) integrated many of these ideas about risk perception (Kasperson & Kasperson, ; Kasperson et al., , ; Pidgeon et al., ). SARF authors define it as “[T]he phenomenon by which information processes, institutional structures, social‐group behavior, and individual responses shape the social experience of risk, thereby contributing to risk consequences” (Kasperson et al., , p. 181).
Risk communication is another important element of SARF. Risk communication research considers the processing of risk information and the formation of risk perceptions, both of which inform risk decisions (Covello, ). Research on mental models of risk (Svenson, ) finds that people's ability to process risk information under stress is greatly reduced. Especially given the increase in information available via digital and social media, people are likely to use heuristics to interpret information and make decisions, thereby creating an opening for politicizing risk. Individuals may assess the trustworthiness of information through heuristics such as confidence in the person or entity communicating about risk (Siegrist, ).
Given the significant overlap in processes and mechanisms, we propose that certain risk positions can fit rather seamlessly into the polarized political structure in the United States. We define “partisan risk positions” as beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors that reveal a partisan‐consistent risk perception or risk evaluation related to a particular object or phenomenon. The idea of political identity being a strong factor in risk perception is relatively new.
We further suggest that risk positions enter polarized politics through “conflict extension,” a process by which elites pull new policies and values into the existing unidimensional (i.e., left–right, liberal–conservative, or Democratic–Republican) structure (Bawn et al., ; Hare, ; Layman et al., ). Only specific issues targeted for conflict extension by political elites such as elected officials, media figures, and activists are likely to be folded into the polarized structure. Climate change is a prominent example of such “politicization of risk” (Linde, ). We propose that polarization permits risk politics to become a more significant set of activities in which elites attempt to create partisan risk positions, largely through politically focused social amplification of risk. We define “risk politics” as political, social, and informational processes designed to generate and support partisan risk positions. We move now to the case of the COVID‐19 pandemic.

2.3

## Conflict extension and the COVID‐19 pandemic
The COVID‐19 pandemic provided a unique opportunity to observe and test risk politics in the polarized political environment. The COVID‐19 pandemic was a novel political issue, and political parties had not previously defined their positions clearly. Earlier pandemic‐type scares (e.g., swine flu in 2009, Ebola in 2014, Zika virus in 2015) briefly became high‐attention issues but did not get folded into the polarized political structure. The COVID‐19 pandemic featured meaningful risk evaluations with potentially severe consequences, including long‐term illness and death. Further, the COVID‐19 pandemic required citizens to engage directly with policy issues that included stay‐at‐home orders, vaccinations, and wearing face coverings in public.
The information environment around COVID‐19 was dynamic throughout 2020 in the United States and was a frequent topic of research and analysis. Given that our two times of data collection were April and September 2020, we focus on research around and between these two time periods. The first confirmed case of COVID‐19 in the United States was in mid‐January 2020, and President Trump declared a national emergency in mid‐March. Early 2020 featured relatively high trust in media coverage of COVID‐19 (Newman et al., ), as well as a more universal focus on public health and prevention on Twitter (Jiang et al., ). However, the seeds of politicization and polarization were also evident in the January–April 2020 timeframe. All the highly influential Twitter users during this period were partisan, with right‐leaning influencers being more active (Jiang et al., ), and Twitter users were framing COVID‐19 as a core political issue (Jiang et al., ). Early 2020 was a time of greater trust and lower polarization, but efforts at conflict extension had begun.
A growing split in coverage between Republican and Democratic information sources was evident throughout 2020. While newspapers politicized and polarized almost immediately, television network news coverage did not show such ideological tendencies early (Hart et al., ). Overall, right‐leaning outlets featured lower scientific quality in their reporting and tended to downplay the pandemic (Mach et al., ). Most scientifically inaccurate information in the March–May period was associated with President Trump (Evanega et al., ). Much of the research focused specifically on Fox News’ coverage of COVID‐19 given the network's dominance and importance in the conservative media environment. Fox News’ pandemic coverage dropped off relatively quickly, going from 95% of all coverage in March–April to 56% in May 2020 (Savillo & Power, ). When Fox News did cover the pandemic thereafter, the coverage tended to include scientifically inaccurate information. Such information appeared 253 times in a single week in July 2020, with a corresponding push to make masks a cultural flashpoint (Media Matters Staff, ). Similarly, 1001 Fox News segments from September–November 2020 undermined public health recommendations (Media Matters Staff, ).
Significant evidence substantiates the idea that the differing information environments produced differences in COVID‐19 views and reported behaviors. Cues from political elites produced affective polarization in the United States and in other countries (Flores et al., ). Moreover, the evidence shows that those consuming right‐wing messaging were less worried about the pandemic and were more willing to engage in behaviors deemed risky by public health experts. Early on, Fox News viewers were more likely to have incorrect information (Jurkowitz, ) and were more concerned about running out of money and less concerned about acquiring COVID‐19 (Bruine de Bruin et al., ). Further, they were less likely to engage in preventive behaviors and more likely to behave in risky ways (Zhao et al., ). We hypothesize that partisan cues in the information environment result in similar risk position splits in our data. We further propose one causal mechanism by offering that partisan elite cues are interpreted through partisan identity to produce differing risk positions. In other words, political identity moderates how the message influences an individual's views or behaviors.
H2A: Partisan selective exposure to information predicts risk positions on pandemic‐related issues.
H2B: Partisan identity moderates the processing of elite cues, leading to differing risk positions on pandemic‐related issues.

The next set of hypotheses proposes that COVID‐19 risk positions became integrated with the other elements of political polarization shown in Figure  . This is a desired outcome of the conflict extension efforts implemented via cue giving and other methods. Elements of the existing partisan polarized structure correspondingly become predictors for risk positions. Research on linkages to support for co‐partisan elites and to co‐partisan policy positions is limited, though some research has found that support for President Donald Trump was a larger driver of attitudes toward COVID‐19 than partisanship itself (Kaushal et al., ; Relihan et al., ). Conversely, the literature supplies significant support for the third subhypothesis on partisan identity, which links the initial and final pieces in our model. Partisanship (and its proxies) influenced people's concern about the virus (Ruisch et al., ), behaviors in response to governors’ recommendations (Grossman et al., ), engagement in social distancing and other behaviors (Allcott et al., ; Douglas & Sutton, ; Kerr et al., ), willingness to get vaccinated (Funk & Tyson, ), attitudes toward relevant policies (Gadarian et al., ; Rodriguez et al., ), actual vaccination rates (Kates et al., 2021), and eventual death rates (Wallace et al., ).
H3A: Support for co‐partisan elites predicts risk positions on pandemic‐related issues.
H3B: Support for co‐partisan policy positions predicts risk positions on pandemic‐related issues.
H3C: Partisan identity produces differing risk positions for pandemic‐related issues.

Another desirable outcome of conflict extension would be increased integration of the new, important issue into the existing polarized infrastructure over time as the cues continued. The messages from media and peers should influence more people over time. Examination of a large national sample found evidence of increasing polarization during roughly the same timeframe as our study (Relihan et al., ).
H4: The influence of partisan identity on risk positions for pandemic‐related issues increases over time.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS
The COVID‐19 pandemic constituted a natural stimulus, introducing a new, highly salient, major issue to the US public that quickly became part of conflict extension efforts. This natural experiment allows for melding internal validity and external validity through survey methods. Further, the study has high ecological validity because the pandemic and associated risk behaviors and policies were highly relevant to people's everyday lives. Surveys in April and September 2020 provide the data for hypothesis testing.
Nearly all the testing uses Montana as a case for multiple reasons. First, data availability allows for testing multiple pieces of our proposed model at once. Second, the authors understand nuances in the state, which is important given our efforts in risk theory building and initial testing. Third, Montana is similar to other vast swaths of the United States in which large political changes may be relatively easy to achieve due to a lack of heterogeneity. Montana provides a cleaner test of the model that “controls” for other potential influences. Fourth, places such as Montana have a disproportionate influence on national politics due to institutional structures. Fifth, given divisions in the country presently, drawing conclusions from “representative” national samples can be problematic. Those samples are valuable, but they are likely obscuring important variation. In some limited cases, we expand our analysis to four Western US states in which these arguments largely hold, as well.
We administered the first survey, the Western States Coronavirus Survey, online from April 10–27, 2020, with residents of Montana, North Dakota, Utah, and Colorado. The purpose of the survey was to assess public attitudes and self‐reported behaviors related to the pandemic. We recruited representative samples from the Qualtrics online probability panel. The 20‐minute questionnaire included pandemic items in addition to standard political and demographic items. When possible, we used established language from the American National Election Study, Pew, Gallup, and the General Social Survey. The survey produced 2220 responses, with 503 from Colorado, 738 from Montana, 481 from North Dakota, and 498 from Utah. We generated “raked” weights through iterative proportional fitting based on age, gender, education, race, ethnicity, and urban versus rural residence. However, for Montana and Colorado, we also had weighted subsamples for registered and likely voters. The variables used in weighting these subsamples are respondent age, gender, education level, race, and 2016 presidential vote choice. This survey also included a brief messaging experiment. A respondent randomly received one of three messages about mask wearing: one with no quote from President Trump, one with a Trump quote that was dismissive of wearing masks, and one with a Trump quote that was supportive of wearing masks. Respondents were then asked about their likelihood of starting to wear a mask.
The second survey, the Treasure State 2020 Pre‐Election Poll, was administered by mail from September 14 to October 2, 2020. The original population for the survey was all registered and active Montana voters. The purpose of this survey was to assess pre‐election opinions and attitudes that might be relevant to the election, including replication of certain Western States pandemic items and the addition of some new pandemic items. Again, item language mirrored established language from prominent sources. The survey produced a 20% response rate, with 1789 responses out of 8836 deliverable addresses selected randomly from the state voter file. Post hoc filtering for people who actually voted in the 2020 election reduced the number of responses slightly to 1765. The raked weights for this survey incorporate age, education, gender, media market, self‐reported 2020 presidential vote choice, and self‐reported vote on the marijuana ballot measure. The weight compositions differed slightly due to more information being available for weighting with this second survey.
We use multiple statistical methods to test the hypotheses. The primary statistical methods used in testing all three parts of H1 are difference in proportions tests and Phi coefficients. The first hypothesis deals with linkages between partisan identity and support for co‐partisan elites, partisan selective exposure to information, and support for co‐partisan policy positions. Supplements and in the Supporting Information supply information about the variables used in testing the hypotheses. We have six indicators for each wave for H1A, four indicators for each wave for H1B, and three indicators for each wave for H1C (see Table  ) for Montana voters. We look first at partisan splits (Republican vs. Democratic) in views and self‐reported behaviors, with corresponding Z‐tests. The difference in proportions is the difference in likelihood that a given Republican individual would hold a view as opposed to a given Democratic individual. The null hypothesis is that the two proportions are equal in the population. Large Z‐scores and small p‐values supply strong evidence that the two proportions are statistically distinguishable (i.e., Republicans and Democrats are different).
We then present the Phi coefficient as a measure of the relationship between a given variable and political partisanship. Phi is appropriate for proportions but is equivalent to Cramer's V and Pearson's r in the case of relationships between binary variables such as those being used here. This number summarizes how much views and behaviors split along a two‐party line. We use the same methods in evaluating H3C about partisan identity producing different risk positions for pandemic‐related issues among Montana voters (see Table  ), with 11 indicators for April and seven for September.
H2A proposes that partisan selective exposure to information predicts risk positions on pandemic‐related issues. Here, we use binary logistic regressions on our indicators of risk positions on pandemic‐related issues (see Table  ). This form of nonlinear regression is the appropriate method given the binary (0–1) nature of the dependent variables. For the sake of simplicity, our independent variables are indexes built from multiple indicators as described in Supplement in the Supporting Information.
H2B more closely examines a causal mechanism, proposing that partisan identity moderates the processing of elite cues. The April survey included an experiment in which President Trump gave different cues about wearing masks (see Supplement in the Supporting Information for language). After random assignment, the control group received only neutral information about the recent recommendation from the Center for Disease Control. The “dismissive” condition group received the neutral information plus language attributed to President Trump, based on real‐life language he used, dismissive of mask wearing. The “supportive” condition group again received the neutral information plus language attributed to President Trump that emphasized the importance of mask wearing. Following the experiment, research participants answered multiple questions about mask wearing, including one about the likelihood they would personally start wearing a cloth face mask in public. Participants then received a debriefing explaining the experimental manipulation. We use ordinary least squares to regress on the likelihood of mask wearing. As this dependent variable is not technically continuous, we also evaluate using ordered logit for robustness purposes. Ordered logit is a nonlinear regression model appropriate for ordinal‐level dependent variables. For simplicity and clarity, we evaluate three different specifications (see Table  ). The experimental condition effects and interaction terms (see Fairchild & MacKinnon, ) become essential for assessing the partisan differences and the difference in differences (i.e., the moderating effects).
The third hypothesis predicts that risk positions on pandemic‐related issues become part of the existing partisan cognitive schema. This means such risk positions become predictable based on support for co‐partisan elites (H3A), support for co‐partisan policy positions (H3B), and ultimately partisan identity (H3C). Again, we use binary logistic regressions on our indicators of risk positions on pandemic‐related issues for H3A and H3B (see Table  ), while we use difference in proportions tests and Phi coefficients for H3C (Table  ).
Evaluation of H4, about an increasing role of partisan identity in these risk positions over time, requires different approaches. The strongest test is whether the relationship between partisan identity and the different risk position indicators becomes stronger in a statistically significant sense over time. This means evaluating the difference in differences between the April and September timeframes (i.e., whether the partisan split was larger in September). We do this through a logistic regression with the following specification:

logp/1−p=β0+β1∗Party+β2∗Wave+β3∗Party∗Wave+ε,
in which the statistical significance of β3 becomes the test of the difference in differences for the binary independent variables (Table  ; see details in Supplement in the Supporting Information). We use a different and simpler approach in Table  to assess H4 by evaluating the difference in differences for proportions (see the notes for Table  ).

## RESULTS
The first hypothesis addresses the idea of dueling paths. Table  displays the results of empirical testing of this hypothesis. We note that all 26 partisan splits in Table  are in the expected direction and are statistically significant. The very large Z‐scores and very small p‐values in Table  are a result of the clearly polarized results. Hypothesis H1A proposes support for co‐partisan elites based on party identification. Republicans were much more likely to support and approve of their own candidates, and the same was true of Democrats. The splits are dramatic, as further evidenced by Phi values typically in the 0.70 to 0.90 range. Notably, these relationships were slightly stronger in September than in April, which is not surprising given that 2020 was a general election year and given the politicization of the pandemic. The clear exception to the overall patterns is approval of Montana Governor Steve Bullock in April. Early in the pandemic, his support was high and more uniform across parties, likely due to a rally‐around‐the‐flag effect. Hypothesis H1B proposes partisan selective exposure to information. The middle section of Table  supports the idea that Democrats and Republicans have their own information sources. Republicans are much more likely to use Fox News and conservative talk radio as information sources, while Democrats are much more likely to use MSNBC and CNN. Hypothesis H1C proposes that individuals support well‐established co‐partisan policy positions. The specific variables differed across periods, but the partisan splits are pronounced. Overall, we find very strong support for the idea of dueling paths, with partisan identity sorting elites, information sources, and policy positions into clear, separate paths.
 H2A proposes that partisan selective exposure to information predicts risk positions on pandemic‐related issues. Table  displays the results of logistic regressions for six dependent variables in April and six in September, with four of these the same across the periods. Multiple risk positions at each time were predicted by partisan selective exposure to information, supporting H2A. The second part of this hypothesis, H2B, proposes that partisan identity moderates the processing of elite cues, leading to differing risk positions on pandemic‐related issues. Table  displays the results from the survey experiment. Most importantly, Republicans receiving the dismissive mask message scored lower on the scale of likely mask wearing than did control Republicans (−0.349). The gap between Republicans receiving supportive and dismissive messages was even larger (0.609). Interestingly, Democrats who received the supportive message from President Trump scored considerably lower on mask wearing (−0.326) than did control Democrats. We note that ordered logit results are substantively identical. The survey was in the field shortly after the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention initially recommended mask wearing.
 The third hypothesis proposes that other elements in these partisan polarized structures became predictors for risk positions on pandemic issues. Returning to Table  , worry about catching COVID‐19 and about an economic depression were not clearly part of the partisan structure in April. The other risk positions largely were predicted by support for co‐partisan elites (H3A via conservative elite index) and support for co‐partisan policy positions (H3B via conservative policy index) in the expected directions, suggesting some early integration into the polarized structure. In September, all issues except worry about economic depression are part of the polarized partisan structure. The new issues of protecting the economy and supporting statewide mask orders are especially well integrated. Integration with established policy positions appears to be relatively more difficult. The last subhypothesis (H3C) proposes that partisan identity can ultimately predict differing risk positions for pandemic‐related issues. Table  shows the results of tests of this hypothesis, with all 18 relationships in the expected direction (though worry about economic depression is debatable). While some partisan differences are evident in April, the splits and Phis are smaller than those observed in Table  . The Phis range from 0.08 to 0.38 for April. These smaller values are largely due to a sizable proportion of Republicans supporting public health policies and engaging in preventative behaviors in April. The September partisan relationships are stronger, with Phis ranging from 0.12 to 0.63, though mostly in the 0.29 to 0.63 range. Some of the partisan splits in September, such as the need to protect the economy and support for statewide mask orders, are especially stark.
 The final hypothesis, H4, proposes that the influence of partisan identity on risk positions for pandemic‐related issues increases over time. We see some solidification of partisan polarization between April and September, providing supportive evidence for the idea of conflict extension over time. We test this hypothesis in two ways. Most directly, the bottom half of Table  shows statistically significant differences in differences as bolded results. We calculated these via a merged data set and interaction terms, as previously described. These results are less conclusive, particularly since Republicans became relatively less concerned about economic depression and since Democratic intention to get vaccinated dropped relatively more. However, worry about catching COVID‐19 became more predictable based on co‐partisan elite support over time.
We also test this hypothesis as shown in Table  . The median Phi across all pandemic variables went from 0.24 in April to 0.39 in September, supporting H4. The difference‐in‐difference tests provide even stronger evidence for the increasingly partisan nature of pandemic views and behaviors. As shown in Table  , the partisan gaps for all four pandemic variables that appeared in both waves changed in statistically significant ways between April and September (see the asterisks). However, the gap for the intention to get vaccinated actually got smaller in a statistically significant way. Figure  shows how conflict extension sorted the COVID‐19 pandemic into preexisting boxes for each political party. While Democrats still saw the coronavirus as a public health issue and as the core threat in September, Republicans had shifted in the direction of focusing more intently on protecting the economy and threats to personal liberties. In short, the pandemic became politicized and polarized in the expected way, and this process solidified between April and September.

## DISCUSSION
We aimed to begin answering the following general question: How do political identity and information sources facilitate the incorporation of risk positions into a preexisting polarized political structure? The current, highly polarized political environment in the United States can worsen gaps in risk perception, can create dangerous situations for individuals and communities, and can make effective governance more difficult. Understanding how conflict extension incorporates risk positions is crucial. Consequently, we build on established bodies of literature about identity politics, political polarization, and risk perception and social amplification to propose and test ideas about such conflict extension.
To test these ideas, we leveraged the natural experiment provided by the COVID‐19 pandemic as well as two waves of public opinion data. We focused most analyses on a representative sample of Montana voters. Again, we think this approach has significant advantages for theory development and initial testing. Overall, we find that political partisans in our sample generally are well polarized in their support for co‐partisan elites, partisan selective exposure to information, and support for well‐established co‐partisan policy positions. Further, pandemic risk positions correspond with partisan information sources, and a mechanism involving partisan‐tinted evaluation of elite cues finds support. Pandemic risk positions also quickly became part of the preexisting partisan cognitive schemas, with partisan identity ultimately predicting these risk positions. Finally, the evidence is less uniform, but we find increased linkages among the polarization elements between April and September 2020. In short, the data align well with our proposed ideas about conflict extension during the COVID‐19 pandemic.
Some limited results do not fit as cleanly with our theory, while other results were not directly predicted. In terms of anomalous results, vaccine intention and worry about economic depression require further explanation. These variable values dropped for both Republicans and Democrats between the two waves, but vaccine intention dropped more for Democrats and worry about economic depression more for Republicans. Our data (not presented) also show that Democrats were worried about vaccines in September due to vaccine safety concerns (cited by 66% of Democrats vs. 28% of Republicans). At that point in time, Democrats were quite worried that vaccines would “not be proven safe” before deployment. The economic depression drop for Republicans does not immediately make sense given their relative emphasis on protecting the economy. We suspect they were less worried about depression because they thought their preferred elites and policies would emphasize addressing economic concerns. Finally, the lesser integration of pandemic risk positions with established partisan policy positions is worth examination. One would expect integration with policy to be more difficult because policies are more complex and the information environment provides fewer direct clues. Integration would involve a lag, as the broader population is less ideological and attentive.
Our study features certain limitations and therefore presents opportunities for future work. First, certain evidence may be circumstantial in that it is not linked directly or through experimental methods. In other words, we are not testing all the pieces of the theoretical model we have presented. While we think the volume of circumstantial evidence is meaningful (and existing studies fill in many gaps), opportunities exist for making more direct causal connections and for elucidating specific causal mechanisms. Second, our study features better external than internal validity, though the experiment helps. Additional experiments would bolster claims. Third, the Montana sample used for most of the analyses is not a nationally representative one. Despite the strengths of the sample explained earlier, conducting studies in other locations in the United States and beyond should prove useful in filling out the picture. Fourth, our study time period was not long enough to capture all the ebbs and flows of conflict extension for this issue area. Expansion of the time period might prove illuminating. Did additional time increase polarization or has polarization decreased as issue attention has faded? Fifth, the slightly different survey methods used between the two waves may raise some concerns about interwave comparability. However, we have minimized those differences and conducted robustness checks whenever possible. Finally, extending the idea of risk politics to other issue areas, likely with longer time horizons, should add to understanding of the limitations of risk politicization.
We have emphasized that this study represents an initial step in understanding how political conflict extension incorporates risk positions and risk amplification, though our study builds on well‐established theory such as SARF. Many of the elements of our study fit well with SARF, including the strong role of communication via “amplification stations,” the selective processing of signals, and the connections between the signals and risk perceptions and behaviors (Kasperson et al., ). However, we also venture into less‐developed SARF territory, including the incorporation of political polarization, the mechanisms for amplification of risk in part of the population while the same risk is effectively being attenuated in another part, and the idea of conflict extension aligning risk perceptions across issues. Moreover, our study generates additional questions (including those discussed in the previous paragraph). Can we identify or predict which issues are likely to become part of this process? Can other major issues influence this process? Do politicization and political polarization worsen or improve alignment between risk perception and related views and behaviors? As for the latter, polarization makes position‐taking easier and more consistent, which would suggest better alignment in some respects—including associated risk behaviors. However, polarization might also blind individuals to legitimate risks in harmful ways. Further, the simplicity of polarization might allow for expansion of the carrying capacity of the public agenda, meaning citizens can pay attention to more issues because the cognitive load is simplified. Is this happening? To the extent these trends and processes constitute problems, we need to understand them to deal with them effectively.
