## Background
In recent years, the Chinese government has increased its support for the development of community-based psychiatric rehabilitation (CBPR) services and initiated pilot programs for government purchasing of CBPR services from non-governmental organizations (NGOs) in order to address the inadequacy of the government’s own supply capacity of CBPR services. This study investigated how the NGOs in Shanghai perceived the challenges and opportunities for participating in the provision of government-purchased CBPR services.

## Methods
A qualitative descriptive approach was employed in this study. The local NGOs’ program directors who had been responsible for the provision of government-purchased CBPR services in Shanghai were interviewed regarding their perceptions of the challenges and opportunities. Interview transcripts were coded and analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis method.

## Results
A total of 19 NGOs’ program directors were interviewed. The challenges perceived by them for NGOs to participate in the provision of government-purchased CBPR services included difficulty with engaging individuals with mental illness and their families, lack of resources, lack of knowledge and skills, inadequate collaborations with other stakeholders, and limitations of services. The opportunities could be grouped into external and internal ones. External opportunities included China’s favorable policy environment, Shanghai’s developed economic environment, and increased public awareness of mental health, while internal opportunities included NGOs’ greater flexibility, high team cohesion, stronger focus on the holistic needs of clients, better resource integration capabilities, and ability to provide long-term follow-up and support for clients.

## Conclusions
Based on the findings, recommendations for future actions are provided in the areas of research, public awareness, funding, training, referrals, collaboration, and service designing to improve the mechanism of government purchasing of CBPR services from NGOs in China.

## Background
Community-based psychiatric rehabilitation (CBPR) is an essential component of mental health care that aims to support individuals with mental illness to live a satisfying, hopeful, and contributing life in their communities [ ]. However, in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), the provision of CBPR services for individuals with mental illness remains extremely limited. Although the evidence supports the feasibility and effectiveness of CBPR services for individuals with mental illness in LMICs [ ], the vast majority of individuals with mental illness in LMICs still do not have access to CBPR services. According to the second China National Disability Survey conducted in 2006, it was found that only 1.0% of Chinese adults with a mental disability had ever used any rehabilitation service [ ]. A more recent survey conducted in rural communities in Guangxi Province, China, in 2016 showed that the utilization rate of rehabilitation services in patients with psychotic disorders was still as low as 17.0% [ ]. These two surveys didn’t differentiate rehabilitation services provided in hospitals or in community settings. Therefore, it can be speculated that the coverage of CBPR services in China may be even lower.

Recognizing the importance of, as well as the low coverage of, CBPR services, the Chinese government has placed particular emphasis on CBPR services in recent years, with the Ministry of Civil Affairs primarily held accountable for the development of CBPR services in China [ ]. In 2013, the China’s Mental Health Law entered into effect, and it explicitly designated CBPR services as a crucial component of China’s mental health care system. In 2017, the Ministry of Civil Affairs issued the “Opinions on Accelerating the Development of Community-based Psychiatric Rehabilitation”, setting the target that "by 2025, more than 80% of cities in China shall provide CBPR services, and in cities providing CBPR services, more than 60% of home-based individuals with mental illness shall receive CBPR services.” [ ] In 2020, the Ministry of Civil Affairs further developed the “Standards for Community-based Psychiatric Rehabilitation Services” which provided detailed guidelines for the implementation of CBPR services [ ]. In 2021, the Integrated Actions for Psychiatric Rehabilitation, a three-year national action plan for the development of CBPR services, was initiated with the aim of establishing a well-structured CBPR service system in China [ ].
While the government has ambitious goals for expanding CBPR services, the supply capacity of CBPR services by the government itself is extremely limited. Therefore, the Chinese government has introduced the mechanism of government service purchasing by contracting with and providing funding for non-governmental organizations (NGOs) to provide CBPR services for those in need. In December 2020, the Ministry of Civil Affairs issued the “Guiding Opinions for Promoting Government Purchasing of Community-based Psychiatric Rehabilitation Services”, which set the target that “by 2025, a relatively complete government purchasing system for CBPR services shall be established in China.” [ ] Since then, a variety of cities in China, including Shanghai, have initiated pilot programs for government purchasing of CBPR services, with a group of NGOs having participated in the provision of CBPR services purchased by the government. However, it has been said that, during the provision of CBPR services, the NGOs have encountered several challenges, such as the lack of training on CBPR services, and the lack of coordination with medical institutions and other potential partners.
One recently published review of the grey literature on CBPR in LMICs reported that, among the 23 included CBPR programs from 19 LMICs, the most frequently reported challenge was a lack of resources, including financial and human resources, while the second most common challenge was stigma from the community or family members [ ]. In addition, accessible transport, logistical challenges, high levels of poverty, unrealistic expectations held by clients, stakeholder engagement, and appropriateness of training content and materials were also among the challenges faced by CBPR programs in LMICs [ ]. Another review of psychosocial interventions for schizophrenia in LMCIs found that, while preliminary evidence suggested that community-based psychosocial interventions for schizophrenia may be acceptable in LMICs, significant barriers to their feasibility and acceptance remained, including the fear of stigma associated with the disclosure of diagnosis, lack of appreciation of intervention benefits, the unavailability of caregivers, and resource constraints [ ].
Till now, it has been poorly studied how the NGOs in China perceive the challenges, as well as the opportunities, for participating in the provision of government-purchased CBPR services. Therefore, this study employed a qualitative descriptive approach to investigate, from the perspective of NGOs in Shanghai, the challenges and opportunities for participating in the provision of government-purchased CBPR services, hoping to provide insights for further improving the government purchasing mechanism of CBPR services.

## Methods

## Study design
This study employed a qualitative descriptive approach to investigate the local NGOs’ perceptions of the challenges and opportunities for participating in the provision of government-purchased CBPR services in Shanghai, China. The qualitative descriptive approach is a research method designed to offer a straightforward, detailed account of the participants’ perspectives [ ]. This approach is typically rooted in a naturalistic paradigm and focuses on providing close to the surface meaning of the collected data, with a minimal degree of data interpretation [ ]. Therefore, it is particularly useful to the present study which aims to capture the participants’ views in a clear and accessible manner.

## Setting and participants
Shanghai, located on the eastern coast of China, is one of the country’s most economically developed and urbanized cities. As of 2022, Shanghai had a permanent population of 24.76 million, with an urbanization rate (the proportion of urban population) of 89.3%, an aging rate (the proportion of people aged 65 and over) of 18.7%, and a life expectancy at birth of 83.2 years. In 2022, Shanghai’s annual Gross Domestic Product (GDP) ranked first among cities in China, reaching 4.47 trillion yuan (approximately 0.66 trillion USD). Shanghai is also one of the cities in China with the richest mental health resources. As of 2020, Shanghai had 5.00 psychiatrists per 10,000 people, ranking third nationwide [ ]. Shanghai is the first city in China to establish a three-tier model of mental health service delivery, the so-called “Shanghai Model” [ , ], which is composed of one municipal mental health center, seventeen district-level mental health centers, and more than two hundred community health centers which are equipped with full-time or part-time community mental health workers [ ]. Since its inception in 1980s, the “Shanghai Model” had been replicated in a plenty of cities in China and acted as a source of inspiration for the later-established National Serious Mental Illness Management and Treatment Program (the “686 Program”). Initiated in 2004, the “686 Program” aims to develop a nationwide mental health services system which integrates hospital-based and community-based services, with a particular focus on delivering community-based mental health services for people with severe mental illness [ ]. Due to limitations in resources and capabilities, the services provided by the “686 Program” have been restricted to outpatient follow-up, family visits, free medication, and financial assistance [ ]. Therefore, the development of government-purchased CBPR services in recent years can be seen as an important supplement to the “686 Program”. In 2021, the Shanghai Municipal Bureau of Civil Affairs initiated the pilot program for government purchasing of CBPR services at a citywide level and, through its district-level branches, contracted with local NGOs to provide CBPR services for people with mental illness within each district. With the assistance of the Shanghai Municipal Bureau of Civil Affairs, we invited these NGOs’ program directors who had been responsible for the provision of government-purchased CBPR services to participate in the current study. Each NGO should have only one participant and this person should have been directly in charge of the provision of government-purchased CBPR services within their organization. At the time this study was conducted, the participants should have already been involved in the provision of government-purchased CBPR services for at least one year. A purposive sampling method was used to recruit the participants in the study.

## Data collection
Data were collected by a team of experienced and trained qualitative interviewers from August–September 2022 using a semi-structured interview guide (see Supplementary Material 1) that had been field-tested with two local NGOs’ program directors. The interviews with these two directors were also included in the subsequent analysis, given that only minor changes were made to the interview guide after field-testing. All interviews were conducted online using a virtual conference platform. The participants were first asked to give a detailed introduction of the CBPR services their organizations had been providing. Then, the participants’ understandings of CBPR, including its purposes and components, were probed. Last, the participants were asked to share their perceptions of the challenges and opportunities for participating in the provision of government-purchased CBPR services in Shanghai. On average, the interviews lasted 60 min each. All interview sessions were audio recorded and transcribed and returned to the participants for confirmation of accuracy. All the participants and their organizations were assigned pseudonyms, and identifying information was removed to ensure anonymity. The recruitment and interviewing of the participants continued until the research team deemed no new information was being provided after careful reading and re-reading of the transcripts, and data saturation had been achieved.

## Data analysis
De-identified transcripts were analyzed using a qualitative thematic analysis approach, following Braun and Clarke’s principles and phases [ ]. One researcher first reviewed all the transcripts, noting connections and preliminary themes across the data, discarding those that were not pervasive. A code manual was then generated, including detailed definitions and explanations of each code. To reinforce code reliability, the code manual was tested by two researchers separately coding several transcript excerpts, discussing inconsistencies or points of confusion or repetition, revising the manual accordingly. This process was repeated with two complete transcripts. The draft manual was then vetted through the whole research team. Once a reliable code manual was established, all the transcripts were coded by one researcher using the manual. The codes were ultimately grouped into several themes and sub-themes under the category of challenges and opportunities perceived by the local NGOs for participating in the provision of government-purchased CBPR services. The detailed descriptions of the themes and sub-themes in the final manual were shown below. All the analysis was conducted using NVivo software (version 12).

## Ethical considerations
All study procedures were reviewed and approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of the School of Public Health of Fudan University (IRB No. 2021–09–0916). Before being interviewed, the participants were given a written explanation, as well as a verbal one, about the background, purpose, and procedure of the study in advance. They were assured that the interviews would not be used for any other purpose, and both their personal identities, as well as their organizations’ names, would remain anonymous in all reporting of the study. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all the participants before data collection.

## Reflexivity
As a team, we discussed our prior beliefs and experiences in early discussions and during analysis to reflect on how these may influence our analysis. The research team included two psychiatrists specializing in psychiatric rehabilitation, three public health physicians specializing in community-based mental health services, one mental health social worker, and three public health researchers who studied mental health policies and systems. The authors worked either in hospitals or in academia. No author had a first-hand experience in the provision of government-purchased CBPR services in Shanghai. However, examining local NGOs’ perspectives on the provision of government-purchased CBPR services lies at the intersection of all the authors’ interests, with the purpose of offering essential support to these organizations and ultimately improving the existing community-based mental health services system in Shanghai. In this study, we maintained a curious, open, and non-judgmental attitude toward the participants’ perspectives. All the authors had experience in collecting and analyzing qualitative data and had collaborated for years on projects about Shanghai’s community-based mental health services system.

## Results
Ultimately, a total of 19 NGOs’ program directors who had been responsible for the provision of government-purchased CBPR services in Shanghai were interviewed. Table presents the basic information of the participants. Among them, fourteen participants were female and five were male. Their median age was 38 years (IQR 35–45 years), and their median length of involvement in CBPR services was 3 years (IQR 1–7 years). Most participants (n = 12) had a primary professional background in social work, while the remaining had primary backgrounds in such fields as psychological counseling (n = 2), business management (n = 2), psychiatry (n = 1), and others (n = 2). Most participants’ NGOs (n = 15) contracted with only one district of Shanghai to provide government-purchased CBPR services, while four participants’ NGOs contracted with more than one district. The CBPR services that the participants were taking charge of were distributed in all the sixteen districts of Shanghai (Fig.  ). Based on 177 codes extracted from the transcripts, the study revealed 5 themes consisting of 15 sub-themes in the category of challenges and 2 themes consisting of 8 sub-themes in the category of opportunities (Fig.  ).
 

## Challenges
When providing government-purchased CBPR services, the NGOs were faced with a lot of challenges, including difficulty with engaging individuals with mental illness and their families, lack of resources, lack of knowledge and skills, inadequate collaborations with other stakeholders, and limitations of services.

## Difficulty with engaging individuals with mental illness and their families
Difficulty with engaging individuals with mental illness and their families was a common challenge encountered by the NGOs when providing government-purchased CBPR services. Considering that the “686 Program” had already established a community-based mental health services delivery system, it was conceivable that the NGOs that participated in the provision of CBPR services could leverage this system to identify their target clients rather than starting afresh. Therefore, the individuals with mental illness registered in the “686 Program” largely became the first group of clients that the NGOs attempted to reach. Usually, the NGOs would receive a list of registered individuals in the “686 Program” from the local governments at the community level and then go door-to-door to ask about their willingness to receive CBPR services. However, it was not uncommon that individuals with mental illness and their families would refuse the services provided by the NGOs. The reasons were at least three-fold.

## Low acceptance of NGOs
First, public acceptance of NGOs was currently low. In Shanghai, psychiatric hospitals remained the primary providers of mental health services, with NGOs only beginning to be involved in the system. The general public, including individuals with mental illness and their families, were not well-informed about the nature of NGOs and their services, which led to the professionalism of NGOs often being subject to scrutiny.(Interviewee No. 1) Public acceptance of NGOs is relatively low. When we started our services around 2012–2013, not many people knew about us. If people don’t know what your organization does, their perception of your organization is often off, which makes it hard for us to get our work done smoothly.
(Interviewee No. 13) When we set out to provide our services, we’re often met with questions from our target clients about who we are, what kind of organization we are from, and why they should trust us. People frequently have doubts about us and may even think we are scammers.

To address this challenge, many NGOs chose to collaborate with local community personnel who were more familiar to the target clients. The NGOs would often ask these community personnel to introduce them to the clients, trying to ease doubts among the clients. However, if support from local community personnel was limited, it could be challenging for the NGOs to engage individuals with mental illness and their families in their services.(Interviewee No. 16) At the very beginning, we tried to approach our clients by ourselves. However, many people were reluctant. They questioned our intentions. They were unwilling to let us in. This issue persisted until we invited local community personnel to work with us. After that, this issue was resolved very quickly.

## Self-stigma of individuals with mental illness and their families
Self-stigma of individuals with mental illness and their families was the second factor that the program directors mentioned that could lead to difficulty with engaging them in CBPR services. Individuals with mental illness and their families often preferred to keep the presence of mental illness private. They were generally unwilling to go out and interact with others, nor did they wish to receive visitors, even when such visits were intended to provide services.(Interviewee No. 6) [Individuals with mental illness] may have self-stigma and be hesitant to let others know that they have mental illness. In some cases, even their own relatives might be unaware of their illness. This makes it easy for strangers like us to be turned away.
(Interviewee No. 8) Some families are unwilling to let us interact with them, because being associated with someone with mental illness is a stigma.
(Interviewee No.10) In rural areas, where everyone lives close, if you go there, a lot of people will gather around. Patients are very reluctant to encounter such circumstances, and I can also sense the shame and embarrassment their family members feel under such circumstances.

## Inconvenient transportation
Some NGOs provided CBPR services in rural areas. However, in these areas, individuals with mental illness lived far apart, and transportation was not convenient. This created difficulties for NGO staff in conducting home visits, as well as for individuals with mental illness in traveling to NGO service sites to receive services.(Interviewee No. 10) Our catchment area is not very accessible. The distribution of our clients is very vast. In rural areas, going door-to-door for home visits is very challenging because vehicles cannot reach there. Public transportation doesn’t cover some locations. Rural roads are often quite narrow. It is difficult for us to engage our clients.

## Lack of resources
Lack of resources was mentioned by many program directors when speaking of the challenges for NGOs to participate in the provision of government-purchased CBPR services. It included three subthemes, that is lack of money, lack of staff, and lack of space.

## Lack of money
Insufficient funding was a challenge reported by most program directors. This challenge led to many NGOs being unable to expand their service coverage, to provide more individualized services, and to recruit and retain an adequate number of service personnel.(Interviewee No. 5) To be honest, our only major challenge right now is the lack of funding. We’re also wondering how we’ll make it through this year.
(Interviewee No. 6) The biggest challenge is definitely the financial challenge because our budget is quite limited. We want to provide more services to our clients, but due to budget constraints, our ability to do so is limited. We can only work with a small number of clients, maybe just five families, because we need to ensure a certain level of service quality. This is one of our biggest challenges.
(Interviewee No. 17) Due to limited funding, we can only run large group sessions that are not tailored to individual needs. Each client is different, and you should consider which clients are suitable for our services. The time of participation for each client should also be individualized. However, due to limited funding, we can only offer some basic courses, which leaves the overall effectiveness of our services in question.

The lack of funding was partly due to the scarcity of funding sources. Currently, the NGOs that participated in the provision of CBPR services heavily relied on the government funding, with fewer alternative funding sources. However, the amount of funding that could be obtained via the mechanism of government service purchasing might be unstable.(Interviewee No. 1) Our funding is entirely obtained from the government. If the government funding were cut, our services would be unable to continue. Our disadvantage is that we are too highly dependent on the government funding.

## Lack of staff
The insufficiency of human resources was also a challenge highlighted by many program directors. The NGOs were faced with a challenge of staff recruitment as well as a challenge of staff retention. The program directors mentioned several factors that could lead to the insufficiency of human resources, including the limited funding, the lack of career development opportunities, and the heavy workload.(Interviewee No. 1) Our staff turnover rate is high. Even when we manage to hire new staff, they often leave quickly due to low salaries, limited career growth, and other similar reasons.
(Interviewee No. 2) Our organization has a high turnover rate. On one hand, the salary is low. On the other hand, the workload is heavy. The employee might work for a single year. However, in the next year, they might leave because they feel that they cannot support themselves with this job. Sometimes, they even feel their clients live better than they do.
(Interviewee No. 12) It’s all because of insufficient funding that we can only hire a small number of people.

## Lack of space
Most program directors reported that their NGOs did not own service venues themselves. Some NGOs managed to access free venues by coordinating with local governments but thought it would be better to have their own dedicated venues. Some NGOs were still struggling with securing service venues.(Interviewee No. 7) Our service venue is obtained by coordinating with the local government. It would be helpful if we could have a dedicated venue in the community so that our staff could have a regular presence there. Such a regular presence would make it more convenient for us to serve our clients.
(Interviewee No. 10) As an NGO, you typically don’t have many service venues of your own. Even if you have a dedicated venue, it’s usually at your main office, but your clients may be located far away.

## Lack of knowledge and skills
Many program directors reported that their staff lacked sufficient knowledge and skills for providing CBPR services. In Shanghai, very few NGOs were specifically dedicated to the provision of CBPR services. Therefore, the government relaxed its entry requirements for NGOs to participate in the provision of CBPR services during the bidding stage. In addition to NGOs specifically dedicated to CBPR services, the government also allowed other NGOs engaged in related work, e.g., disability services and elder care, to participate in the bidding for the provision of government-purchased CBPR services. However, in the process of service provision, these NGOs often found that their staff lacked sufficient knowledge and skills, as well as necessary training and supervision.(Interviewee No. 17) The biggest challenge is definitely the lack of expertise. We not only lack medical knowledge but also lack skills in rehabilitation.
(Interviewee No. 14) The Bureau of Civil Affairs has issued a document. In the document, they hope us to provide medication management, relapse prevention, physical health management, social skills training, and vocational rehabilitation for our clients. However, all these services are beyond our professional knowledge and skills. We exactly lack the expertise to provide them.

## Inadequate collaborations with other stakeholders
Many program directors emphasized that the provision of government-purchased CBPR services required not only the involvement of their NGOs but also that of other stakeholders. Specifically, they noted that they were eager to establish collaborations with local governments, with medical institutions, and among different NGOs. However, such collaborations were currently inadequate.

## Inadequate collaborations with local governments
The inadequacy of collaborations between NGOs and local governments was mainly reflected in the lack of smooth information sharing. As noted earlier, when the NGOs started to provide services in the communities, they would receive a list of target clients from the local government. However, these lists usually did not include necessary information about the clients, such as medication details. Therefore, NGOs had to spend significant time visiting each of their clients to gather this information.(Interviewee No. 7) We hope to have as much detailed information about our clients as possible. Currently, [the local government] only provides us with a list, and all specific information about our clients has to be gathered by us through interviews. I understand that it‘s already quite good to receive a list from the local government. But we’d like to know additional information about our clients beforehand, such as their medication adherence. In fact, the more we know, the smoother our services can be.
(Interviewee No. 11) When we start providing services, we only have access to some very basic information about our clients. To obtain more detailed information, such as our clients’ personalities and medications, we had to coordinate with the local government, requesting information client by client. However, the contract timeline [for government service purchasing] is very short, making it nearly impossible for us to gain a comprehensive understanding of each of our clients.

The level of emphasis placed upon CBPR services varied among local governments. Several program directors noted that local government officials may sometimes hold prejudice against individuals with mental illness, which further complicated collaboration efforts.(Interviewee No. 8) When we provide services, some local governments demonstrate relatively low levels of collaboration… Sometimes, local government officials are a little afraid of individuals with mental illness and may even show resistant attitudes toward them.

## Inadequate collaborations with medical institutions
Many program directors expressed a desire to collaborate with medical institutions to receive expert guidance on CBPR services. Additionally, they hoped for more collaborations with medical institutions in service coordination, enabling mutual referrals of individuals with mental illness between NGOs and medical institutions. However, such collaborations remained limited at present.(Interviewee No. 12) We know some psychiatrists personally, and they can provide us with some support. However, formal collaboration with medical institutions is quite lacking, and there is no established mechanism for mutual referrals.
(Interviewee No. 15) I believe there’s a need to strengthen service coordination between NGOs and medical institutions. Right now, the connection isn’t close enough. Although the government has linked us with a medical institution, both sides remain somewhat passive in the collaboration.

## Inadequate collaborations among different NGOs
Some program directors emphasized the need for more collaborations among different NGOs. They thought that each NGO had its own strengths and limitations in delivering CBPR services, and only through inter-organizational collaborations can a comprehensive CBPR services network be established. However, collaborations among NGOs remained limited. Some directors noted that the current mechanism of government service purchasing required NGOs to compete, and only one NGO would be awarded the contract. This mechanism might discourage collaborations among NGOs.(Interviewee No. 3) There is no collaboration among NGOs. Often, say, there are only four contracts available, but eight organizations competing for them. How can we imagine collaboration among different NGOs?
(Interviewee No. 2) The government tends to delegate all services within one community to a single NGO. I think different NGOs should collaborate, each focusing on their areas of expertise. The government could allow multiple NGOs to submit joint bids, and one community doesn’t have to be served by only one NGO.

## Limitations of services
Program directors also spoke of certain limitations of services their NGOs were providing, including limited coverage, poor continuity, difficulty with effectiveness evaluation, rigidity of service designing, and ideological conflicts with the government.

## Limited coverage
Several program directors noted that the coverage of CBPR services provided by their organizations was quite limited. First, the service purchasing contract signed between NGOs and local governments often specified that the service coverage was limited to the individuals residing within the jurisdiction of that government. Second, as mentioned before, the clients of government-purchased CBPR services were often identified from the list of individuals registered in the “686 Program”, and the NGOs could not accept referrals from medical institutions or self-referrals from individuals in need. Third, even among the individuals registered in the “686 Program”, many were unwilling to utilize the CBPR services provided by the NGOs.(Interviewee No. 1) We can only provide services for those living within our designated catchment area. Many people called us seeking help, but if they’re living outside our catchment area, we cannot assist them. If we were to provide services for them, the local government would not acknowledge it.
(Interviewee No. 19) Why can’t our services be provided to those who are not registered [in the “686 Program”]? There’s no need to distinguish between registered and non-registered individuals. Those who are recently discharged [from inpatient psychiatric care] and those who lack access to rehabilitation services—why can’t they receive our services?
(Interviewee No. 17) The individuals with mental illness listed by the local government often have little interest in receiving rehabilitation services. It would be much better if mental health centers or primary health care facilities could refer individuals who are willing to participate in our services to our organization.

## Poor continuity
Under the current mechanism of government service purchasing, the contracts signed between NGOs and local governments often had a fixed term such as one year for the provision of CBPR services. Once the contracts expired and were not renewed, the NGOs could no longer continue providing services for their clients. Some program directors expressed their concern that poor service continuity might undermine the effectiveness of their services.(Interviewee No. 18) If the contract only has a one-year term, then you can only provide services for that one year. We believe that for community-based psychiatric rehabilitation services, a one-year contract won’t allow enough time to achieve significant changes in our clients. Some services require a longer duration to see effects. I think, rehabilitation services need to be continuous.

## Difficulty with effectiveness evaluation
While the government emphasized the effectiveness of CBPR services, there was still a lack of standardized methods to assess effectiveness. Some program directors complained that, in practice, effectiveness evaluation often became a mere record of activities conducted by the NGOs, which they believed could not demonstrate the real effectiveness of their services.(Interviewee No. 13) There is currently an emphasis on service effectiveness. In my view, if we really want to assess service effectiveness, we need to look at whether the organization is delivering professional services and whether the clients recognize the services provided by the organization. More ideally, we need to conduct pre- and post-evaluations. However, the society focuses more on the records of activities conducted by the organization, like photos, summaries, and so on. I don’t think these records can demonstrate the real effectiveness. They can only show that you have completed the tasks.

## Rigidity of service designing
Program directors noted that once the service purchasing contracts were signed between NGOs and local governments, the specifics of service designing were fixed, including the form, content, and frequency of services, based on which the eventual performance of NGOs in delivering services would be evaluated by local governments. Therefore, there was little room for NGOs to make adjustments to service designing in order to accommodate the particular needs of their clients.(Interviewee No. 11) We need to adjust our services based on the clients’ particular needs. However, if we make too many adjustments, our services might fail to meet the standards during the audit.

## Ideological conflicts with the government
Some program directors mentioned the presence of ideological conflicts with the government regarding the provision of CBPR services. For example, the government may require NGOs to submit service records to access performance, which actually conflicted with the confidentiality requirements associated with mental health service provision.(Interviewee No. 5) We strictly adhere to the confidentiality principles of mental health counseling and prefer not to provide the government with our service records. While the government seeks to monitor our progress, it’s impractical for us to take photos while providing services.

Faced with ideological conflicts with the government, many program directors expressed their frustration. On one hand, they needed to comply with the requirements set by the government, as their services were funded by the government. On the other hand, they wanted to maintain their autonomy and independence. As a result, they often felt torn between these conflicting demands.(Interviewee No. 3) We have a quite delicate relationship with the government. Should we follow the requirements set by the government or adhere to our own professional ethics? Most of the time, we have to choose the former, since this is the only way for us to secure government funding and keep services running. For small NGOs like ours, it’s challenging to maintain true autonomy and independence. We can only strive to uphold our principles as much as possible within acceptable limits.

## Opportunities
Despite the above challenges, program directors also highlighted many opportunities for NGOs in Shanghai to participate in the provision of government-purchased CBPR services, which could be grouped into external and internal ones.

## External opportunities
External opportunities referred to the favorable conditions in the external environment that NGOs could leverage to reinforce its participation in the provision of government-purchased CBPR services. These external opportunities included China’s favorable policy environment, Shanghai’s developed economic environment, and increased public awareness of mental health.

## China’s favorable policy environment
In recent years, the Chinese government had placed increasing emphasis on CBPR services for individuals with mental illness and formulated a series of favorable policies for the development of CBPR services. Moreover, the Chinese government particularly encouraged NGOs to participate in the provision of CBPR services for individuals with mental illness.(Interviewee No. 12) In the past few years, the Bureau of Civil Affairs has issued several policies to promote the development of community-based psychiatric rehabilitation services. I think these policies have provided us with great opportunities.

## Shanghai’s developed economic environment
In addition to the favorable policy environment at the national level, several program directors mentioned that Shanghai’s developed economic environment also presented an opportunity for their NGOs to participate in the provision of CBPR services.(Interviewee No. 1) Shanghai is an international metropolis with developed economic conditions. Compared to the central and western regions [of China], Shanghai has a higher level of acceptance for community services. I think it also presents an opportunity for NGOs to participate in the delivery of community-based psychiatric rehabilitation services.

## Increased public awareness of mental health
Some program directors mentioned that public awareness of mental health had been increasing in recent years. Particularly since the COVID-19 pandemic, Chinese people had become more aware of the importance of mental health, which also created opportunities for the development of CBPR services for individuals with mental illness.(Interviewee No. 1) During the pandemic, there was a significant increase in the demand for psychiatric rehabilitation services. I believe that both the government and the public are gradually becoming more aware of the importance of psychiatric rehabilitation.
(Interviewee No. 8) As people’s education levels rise, there is an increasing focus on mental health and wellbeing. So, I think the prospects for the development of psychiatric rehabilitation should be promising, and the demand [for psychiatric rehabilitation services] is quite significant.

## Internal opportunities
Internal opportunities referred to the NGOs’ inherent strengths in providing CBPR services compared to other service providers such as medical institutions. Program directors highlighted a variety of strengths possessed by NGOs, including greater flexibility, high team cohesion, stronger focus on the holistic needs of clients, better resource integration capabilities, and ability to provide long-term follow-up and support for clients.

## NGOs’ greater flexibility
Several program directors believed that compared to government-owned institutions, NGOs were more flexible in delivering CBPR services to individuals with mental illness. Because of their smaller scale, NGOs often enjoyed more efficient internal communication and decision-making processes, facing fewer bureaucratic hurdles.(Interviewee No. 1) NGOs do not have as many bureaucratic procedures as government-owned institutions. Our procedures are simpler. Communication across different levels is more convenient. Therefore, we can get things done more quickly.

## NGOs’ high team cohesion
A few program directors noted that most employees in their NGOs joined the nonprofit sector out of passion, which contributed to a strong sense of team cohesion.(Interviewee No. 1) Our team members are like-minded. They are all passionate about charity work, and share a common understanding of psychiatric rehabilitation. So, when we work on something, we all work together towards a common goal.

## NGOs’ stronger focus on the holistic needs of clients
Many program directors pointed out that, unlike medical institutions which paid more attention to symptom control, NGOs showed a stronger focus on the holistic needs of clients. With person-centeredness at the core of service philosophy, NGOs aimed to support the recovery of individuals with mental illness and assist them to re-integrate into society. Some directors further noted that this holistic approach enabled NGOs to build closer relationships with their clients.(Interviewee No. 2) When providing services, we may address a broader range of needs than medical institutions typically do, as they often focus primarily on symptom control. Instead, we take a holistic approach to the needs of individuals with mental illness and also extend support to their families.
(Interviewee No. 8) Our services are person-centered. Unlike medical institutions which focus more on symptom management, we support the recovery of individuals with menta illness, paying attention to their physical, mental, and spiritual well-being, and assisting them to re-integrate into society.
(Interviewee No. 12) We focus more on addressing the needs of our clients when providing services, which makes it easier for us to establish closer relationships with them.

## NGOs’ better resource integration capabilities
Better resource integration capabilities were highlighted by program directors as another strength possessed by NGOs. As NGOs often partnered with a variety of organizations, they were well positioned to integrate a range of resources, which allowed them to connect clients with appropriate supports.(Interviewee No. 14) We are an extension of the government’s arm, serving as a bridge between the government and vulnerable groups. With the support of multiple partner organizations, our ability to integrate various social resources is quite prominent.
(Interviewee No. 15) Our NGO can mobilize a broader range of resources. Resource integration is a significant role we play. We can connect clients with hospitals, communities, workplaces, schools, peer groups, and relatives and friends.

## NGOs’ ability to provide long-term follow-up and support for clients
Compared to medical institutions, NGOs were deeply rooted in the community and operated with greater flexibility, which provided them with more opportunities to provide long-term follow-up and support for their clients.(Interviewee No. 1) We are able to conduct long-term follow-up, which may be challenging for medical institutions. Medical staff often have extensive administrative work to handle, while we focus solely on providing services. Additionally, it is easier for clients to contact us directly than to reach a medical institution, where they would need to schedule appointments in advance. Since our NGO is based locally, we can provide our contact information to our clients and they can reach us directly. Therefore, compared to medical institutions, we are better positioned to offer long-term follow-up and support for our clients.

## Discussion
The past few years have witnessed the initial attempts by the Chinese government to expand the coverage of CBPR services, thereby achieving the goal of ensuring “universal access to rehabilitation services” [ ]. Through the mechanism of government service purchasing, the Chinese government has supported, nurtured, and mobilized a batch of local community-based NGOs in providing CBPR services and thus made up for the insufficient supply capacity of the government itself in providing CBPR services. For NGOs in China, while there exist numerous opportunities, they have also encountered various challenges during the past few years of participating in the provision of CBPR services purchased by the government. This study is, to the best of our knowledge, the first of its kind in China that investigated the NGOs’ perceptions of the challenges and opportunities for participating in the provision of government-purchased CBPR services.
China’s favorable policy environment has been proved to be an important opportunity for NGOs to participate in the provision of CBPR services in recent years. For a long time, China’s mental health service system has been dominated by hospitals. Instead of adopting an aggressive strategy of deinstitutionalization, China has been taking a gradual approach to transfer mental health services from hospitals to community settings, with the aim to build an integrated mental health service system that incorporates both hospital and community services. The well-known “686 Program” initiated in 2004 is an important step taken by China in scaling up community mental health services throughout the country. However, due to limited resources, the initial efforts of the “686 Program” were focused largely on patient identification and registration, free medication and regular follow-up in the community, management of community emergencies, and free emergency hospitalization [ ]. In recent years, there has been an increasing call in Chinese society to further emphasizing the importance of rehabilitation in community mental health services, with the hope that community mental health services can truly support every and each individual with mental illness in China to achieve recovery within the community [ – ]. In this context, the Chinese government has issued a series of policy documents to support the development of CBPR services for individuals with mental illness [ – ]. On the other hand, the Chinese government has vigorously promoted the purchasing of public services from NGOs in recent years in order to enhance its supply capacity of public services, as reflected in the “Guiding Opinions on Government Purchase of Services from Social Forces” issued by the State Council in 2013 [ ]. Government purchasing of CBPR services from NGOs is exactly one such example. All these policies have paved the way for NGOs to participate in the provision of CBPR services on a large scale.
However, it seems, from the findings of this study, that the public has not been ready to fully embrace the services provided by NGOs, which, to a considerable extent, hinders the access of NGOs to potential clients. Even though the services provided by NGOs are free of charge, individuals with mental illness and their families may still refuse to utilize these services. Distrust of service providers, self-stigma, inaccessibility of the services, unawareness of the needs for rehabilitation, and previous experiences of mistreatment within the services are among the factors that may influence utilization of mental health services [ – ]. In one systematic review, Brooke-Sumner et al. [ ] found that lack of appreciation of intervention benefits and fear of stigma linked with the disclosure of diagnosis were two major barriers to acceptability of psychosocial interventions for schizophrenia in LMICs. For example, in the COPSI (Community Care for People with Schizophrenia in India) study in India, nearly one third families who were referred to the piloting of the community-based psychosocial intervention for schizophrenia declined to participate because they were “not interested” or did not think the intervention would be “useful” [ ]. Internalized stigma is prevalent among individuals with mental illness and their families in China [ , ], which may also decrease their willingness to utilize community-based services. In a community-based psychoeducational program for schizophrenia in China, Ran et al. [ ] found that 22.6% of individuals who refused to participate in the program were afraid of social discrimination in their local community. Likewise, in another family-based intervention for schizophrenia in China, Xiong et al. reported that 32% of families never attended family group meetings because of fear of exposure of the presence of mental illness in their families [ ]. In Butura et al.’s review, stigma was cited as the second most common challenge to implementation of CBPR services in LMICs [ ]. However, to the best of our knowledge, there is still no study that investigates the attitudes toward government-purchased CBPR services among community-dwelling individuals with mental illness and their families in China. Future studies are needed to further inquire into the reasons why individuals with mental illness and their families in China may refuse to utilize CBPR services provided by NGOs.
In this study, it was also found that, during the provision of government-purchased CBPR services, NGOs in Shanghai were constrained by the lack of resources, knowledge and skills, and inter-institutional collaborations. Nowadays, government service purchasing was often the main or, in some cases, even the only fundraising source for many NGOs in this study, which made them vulnerable to financial difficulties. Furthermore, insufficient funding restricted the NGOs from expanding investments in manpower and infrastructure. Worldwide, lack of resources was reported as the most common challenge to implementation CBPR services in LMICs [ ]. In Butura et al.’s review, 7 out of the 12 programs that reported challenges to implementation mentioned the lack of resources, including financial resources, human resources, and medications [ ]. In China, approximately 58% of health resources are concentrated at tertiary hospitals, while only 18% at primary healthcare facilities [ ]. With the departments of civil affairs beginning to invest in CBPR services, it is promising that the coming years will witness an increase in mental health resources at the community level.
Currently, the majority of personnel providing CBPR services in China are mental health social workers (MHSWs). China’s Integrated Actions for Psychiatric Rehabilitation proposed to vigorously cultivate a cadre of MHSWs to address the shortage of human resources in CBPR services, with psychiatrists, rehabilitation therapists, psychotherapists, public health physicians, nurses, and other professional and technical personnel serving as supportive roles for MHSWs [ ]. However, training on CBPR is largely lacking in the current undergraduate and graduate education of social workers in China, and a postgraduate education system specifically for MHSWs participating in the provision of CBPR services has yet to be established. As a result, numerous NGO program directors have acknowledged some apprehension about their staff’s capacity to deliver CBPR services and expressed a strong interest in increased training and supervision in this area. Staff capacity is often a barrier to implementation of evidence-based psychosocial interventions for individuals with mental health conditions and their families in LMICs [ – ]. Several training programs, such as the WHO/UNICEF Ensuring Quality in Psychosocial and Mental Health Care (EQUIP) project [ ], have been developed to build psychosocial intervention capacity among mental health workers in LMICs. In high-income countries (HICs), there also exist numerous well-known training programs for psychiatric rehabilitation [ ], such as the training program at the Boston University Center for Psychiatric Rehabilitation [ ]. Future efforts are needed to design a culturally appropriate training program to enhance the capacity of NGO staff in delivering CBPR services in China.
NGO program directors also expressed their concern for the lack of close collaborations between their NGOs and other stakeholders, including local governments, medical institutions, and other NGOs. In other countries, difficulty engaging with government officials and policymakers was reported as a challenge by the Amaudo program, a mental health rehabilitation program for homeless people with mental illness in Nigeria [ ]. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends multisectoral and multistakeholder actions to provide mental health care and support in a coordinated, comprehensive, continuous, and integrated way [ , ]. The WHO’s community-based rehabilitation matrix also highlights a multicomponent approach to community-based rehabilitation [ ] which further warrants good collaborations between different institutions that participate in the provision of CBPR services. However, in China, the collaboration channels between different stakeholders in the provision of CBPR services are yet to be set up.
In addition, several limitations of government-purchased CBPR services were noted in this study. When the government contracted with NGOs to deliver CBPR services, it often specified the types, forms, volumes, times, and performance measurements of services that the NGOs need to provide. As a result, the NGOs had limited flexibility to expand service coverage or to adjust services to meet the needs of individual clients. In some circumstances, the NGOs may even encounter conflicts between their ideas and those of the government, and the autonomy of NGOs was often sacrificed to meet the government’s expectations for CBPR services [ , ].
This study has several strengths. First, it is the first study in Shanghai and in China to evaluate the NGOs’ perceptions of the challenges and opportunities for participating in the provision of government-purchased CBPR services. Considering that few studies have examined the feasibility and acceptability to implementation of CBPR services in the mechanism of government service purchasing in China, this study provides important and well-times information for the actions that need to be taken in the future to improve this nascent mechanism. Second, it included 19 NGOs that covered all the sixteen districts of Shanghai. Therefore, the findings of this study can represent both the challenges and opportunities for NGOs to provide government-purchased CBPR services in urban settings of Shanghai and those in rural ones. Third, this study employed a qualitative descriptive approach to capture as detailed accounts of NGO program directors about the provision of CBPR services as possible and report them in a clear, unembellished manner, which makes the findings of this study accessible to a broad audience.
However, there are also some limitations in this study that cannot be overlooked. First, this study was conducted only in one city. While Shanghai is one of the earliest cities in the country that initiate the pilot program of government purchasing of CBPR services, it still cannot represent the entire country. Compared with NGOs in other regions of China, it was evident that NGOs in Shanghai had some unique opportunities in the provision of CBPR services, including Shanghai’s developed economic environment, higher levels of mental health literacy among the general public, and richer mental health resources. As more cities in China are setting about scaling up CBPR services within the framework of government service purchasing, future studies are needed to investigate the experiences of NGOs in other cities regarding the provision of government-purchased CBPR services. Second, this study only interviewed the included NGOs’ program directors and did not interview the frontline service staff. Therefore, we don’t know how the frontline service staff perceive the challenges and opportunities in the provision of CBPR services purchased by the government. The perceptual differences between managers and employees are commonly seen in the service industry [ , ]. In future, it is essential to broaden the scope of research to include frontline service staff, focusing on their experiences in delivering government-purchased CBPR services. Third, China’s efforts to expand CBPR services through the mechanism of government service purchasing are still at the early stage. Therefore, the findings of this study may only apply to the current stage of China’s development in government-purchased CBPR services. As China’s CBPR service system continues to evolve, the challenges and opportunities for NGOs to participate in the provision of CBPR services may change accordingly. Follow-up studies are needed to inquire into such changes and put forward appropriate recommendations for the development of CBPR services at different stages.

## Conclusions
In recent years, the Chinese government has increased its support for the development of CBPR services and initiated pilot programs for government purchasing of CBPR services from NGOs. Despite the opportunities, the NGOs are also faced with many challenges in participating in the provision of government-purchased CBPR services. Using the NGOs in Shanghai as a case, this study elucidates, for the first time, the NGOs’ perceptions of the challenges and opportunities for participating in the provision of government-purchased CBPR services in China. The findings reveal that the NGOs encountered multiple challenges and opportunities. As Shanghai is one of the earliest cities in China that initiated the pilot program of government purchasing of CBPR services, the data collected here can serve as an important benchmark for us to further examine the experiences of NGOs in other cities regarding the provision of government-purchased CBPR services. Moreover, China’s experience in scaling up CBPR services through the mechanism of government service purchasing may be useful for other LMICs to reflect upon and improve their own CBPR service systems.

## Recommendations for future actions
Based on the findings of this study, several recommendations for improving the mechanism of government purchasing of CBPR services from NGOs in China are given below. First, this study indicates that public acceptance of NGOs remains low in China. Therefore, it is necessary for the government to amplify its efforts in publicizing NGOs and their services in order to make individuals with mental illness and their families convinced of the effects of CBPR services provided by NGOs. Second, as resource constraints are cited as a major challenge for NGOs to participate in the provision of CBPR services, government funding for CBPR services should be increased. Additionally, it is essential for NGOs to strengthen their fundraising capacities and diversify their fundraising sources to enhance financial resilience. Third, to enhance the service capabilities of NGO personnel, the government should support the establishment of professional training programs for CBPR services, with continuous guidance and supervision provided for NGO personnel. Forth, given the current limited coverage of CBPR services, a mutual referral mechanism between NGOs and medical institutions should be developed, allowing NGOs to accept referrals from medical institutions. Self-referrals from individuals in need should also be sanctioned. Fifth, considering the unique strengths of different NGOs in delivering CBPR services, a comprehensive CBPR service network should be created to foster collaboration among NGOs. This network would allow a broad range of CBPR services, such as supported education, supported employment, social skills training, and family education, to be offered. The government could facilitate this collaboration by establishing a platform where NGOs can exchange resources. Finally, to ensure that NGOs can adapt services based on clients’ individual needs, government service purchasing contracts should be formulated with some flexibility in service designing. This flexibility would support more tailored, effective service delivery.
