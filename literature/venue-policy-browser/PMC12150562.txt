## Abstract

## Background
This study evaluated the efficiency and effectiveness of Community-Based Voluntary Counseling and Testing (CBVCT) for HIV in Turin as part of the Fast-Track Cities initiative. It aimed to understand demographic characteristics and risk behaviors to identify factors associated with routine testing. The findings can help to better tailor interventions for reducing HIV transmission and improving public health outcomes.

## Methods
A cross-sectional design was employed, utilizing anonymous questionnaires administered throughout 2022. Participants were individuals attending rapid serological testing sessions for HIV organized by third-sector associations in Turin, Northern Italy. The sample comprised 795 participants who attended HIV testing sessions. Data were collected via paper-based questionnaires (n = 495), later digitized for analysis. Descriptive statistics, univariate, and logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine socio-demographic characteristics, risk behaviors, and testing outcomes. Cost-effectiveness was evaluated using a Markov model stratifying patients by CD4 + levels to assess QALYs and direct healthcare costs.

## Results
The study achieved a high HIV testing rate of 99.9%, with a positivity rate of 0.5%. All individuals with reactive tests were successfully linked to care. Syphilis testing had a lower uptake (76.7%) and identified a positivity rate of 0.7%. Significant demographic and behavioral differences were found: previously tested participants are more likely to be older (p < 0.001, OR = 1.15) than first-time testers, more likely to be MSM (Men who have Sex with Men) than MSF (Men who have Sex with Female) and FSM (Female who has Sex with Male) (p < 0.001, OR = 0.132; p < 0.001, OR = 0.183); and are more likely to have had a history of sexually transmitted infections (STIs)(p = 0.003, OR = 0.128). Immediate HIV diagnosis and access to antiviral therapy reduce healthcare costs by €3,841 per patient, with total savings of €23,808 due to lower treatment expenses before immunodeficiency onset.

## Conclusions
CBVCT programs in Turin demonstrated high feasibility and effectiveness in engaging the target population and ensuring linkage to care. However, syphilis testing requires greater integration. The findings highlight the need for targeted interventions to address disparities in testing behaviors and reinforce the economic sustainability of early HIV diagnosis and treatment.

## Introduction
Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) and its progression to Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS) remain significant public health challenges, primarily due to the virus’s impact on the immune system by destroying CD4 + T cells [ , ]. HIV transmission occurs through infected body fluids, necessitating preventive measures and effective treatments like antiretroviral therapy (ART) [ , ]. Untreated HIV advances to AIDS, characterized by a CD4 count below 200 cells/mm³ or severe clinical conditions defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) [ , ].
Globally, HIV affects 38.4 million people, with 1.5 million new infections and 650,000 AIDS-related deaths in 2021. In the WHO European Region, over 2.3 million individuals live with HIV, with about 107,000 new diagnoses in 2021 [ , ]. Italy faces a significant burden with an estimated 110,000 to 150,000 people living with HIV (PLWH), and a demographic shift in new diagnoses towards older age groups and immigrants. Challenges persist in equitable access to care despite treatment advancements [ – ]. 
In Piedmont, Northern Italy, there were 161 new HIV diagnoses in 2022, aligning with the national incidence rate of 3.4 cases per 100,000 inhabitants [ , ]. While new diagnoses have declined since 2009, unprotected sexual intercourse remains the primary transmission mode, especially among men who have sex with men (MSM). Late diagnosis rates are concerning, especially among young people aged 25 to 34 years [ , , ]. Syphilis also poses a public health challenge in Piedmont, with 346 cases reported in 2022 and an incidence rate of 8.2 per 100,000 population. The overlap between HIV and syphilis diagnoses and risk factors highlights the need for comprehensive sexual health strategies [ ]. 
The administration of HIV testing has historically occurred predominantly within healthcare facilities, such as hospitals. However, recent studies have explored a community-based approach, examining the impact of a service that is readily accessible and actively monitors its users, as opposed to the more traditional model of passive user-initiated contact with the service [ – ]. 
The Regional Prevention Plan (PRP) 2020–2025 of Piedmont underscores the importance of early detection and intervention to reduce HIV transmission, particularly among key populations [ ]. Objective MO6-20 aims to enhance parallel strategies based on the community-based voluntary counseling and testing (CBVCT) model - that promote HIV testing and counseling through trained professionals in non-healthcare settings. To achieve this, the PRP outlines strategic intervention lines such as MO6LSs, which focuses on offering STI screening to target populations, and MO6LSw, which includes pharmacological prevention strategies such as Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP), treatment as prevention (TasP), and maternal-fetal transmission prevention therapy [ ]. PrEP is a key biomedical prevention tool that significantly reduces HIV acquisition risk among high-risk individuals [ , ]. In May 2023, the Italian Medicines Agency approved PrEP reimbursability for high-risk adults and adolescents, facilitating broader access. Infectious disease specialists prescribe PrEP under strict criteria, with distribution limited to hospital pharmacies [ ]. The integration of PrEP into prevention frameworks aligns with CBVCT strategies, ensuring that individuals at risk receive comprehensive preventive care, thereby improving health outcomes and reducing transmission rates [ ]. The Regional Plan for Prevention 2020–2025 of Piedmont emphasizes early detection and intervention, particularly among key populations [ ]. CBVCT models are promoted to reduce transmission risks and improve outcomes through early treatment initiation [ , , ].
A pivotal component of the global response to HIV is the Fast-Track Cities initiative, a global partnership aiming to end AIDS as a public health threat by 2030. Turin is one of the 310 cities that signed the Paris Agreement committing to achieve the 95-95-95 objectives: ensuring that 95% of PLWH know their status, 95% of those diagnosed are on ART, and 95% of those on ART achieve viral suppression. Community-based approaches are crucial in reaching underserved populations, enhancing accessibility, fostering early diagnosis and treatment, and addressing HIV-related stigma and discrimination [ – ]. The Fast-Track Cities initiative establishes a framework for network collaboration between participating cities and the local associations that focus on rapid HIV testing, prevention, fight against stigma, and liaising with health organizations when cases of reactive tests are detected [ ]. This initiative is particularly significant as it underscores the need to assess both the efficiency of resources utilized by entities outside of public organizations (such as municipalities and healthcare institutions) and the effectiveness of the outcomes achieved [ ]. The resources are integrated through collaboration agreements, which result from co-programming and co-planning in many European contexts [ ]. The involvement of third-sector organizations (non-profit) in the provision of primary services, such as healthcare diagnostics, arose following the challenges posed by the COVID-19 pandemic, which also reduced the accessibility of services, including HIV testing [ , ]. Furthermore, third sector organizations have the opportunity to be closer to citizens and respond to their needs more effectively [ ]. However, studies to date have not sufficiently examined the economic sustainability of these initiatives as a balance between efficiency and effectiveness.
Recent studies have emphasized the effectiveness of repeated HIV testing for high-risk groups, especially when combined with one-time testing of the general population [ ]. This approach is crucial for the early detection and treatment of HIV and can result in a substantial decrease in HIV transmission rates [ , , ]. Both standard and rapid testing have been demonstrated to be cost-effective [ ]. Community and outreach settings in Italy have successfully used rapid oral fluid testing to reach people who have never been tested, leading to new HIV diagnoses [ ]. However, there is a lack of evidence in the literature regarding the use of rapid serological tests for HIV diagnosis.
A study from Marcellusi et al. [ ] calculated the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) for Early Treatment HIV comparing it with the Standard of Care (SoC). The model demonstrated that early treatment appeared to be the most cost-effective option compared to SoC with an ICER per quality-adjusted life years (QALY) gained ranging from €6,000 to €18,000. Interventions aimed at reducing diagnostic delays are highly beneficial, increasing the number of patients in early treatment scenarios, improving both the number of QALYs earned and resources saved. The treatment of patients that have CD4 ≥ 500 has an average cost of € 2,502, while patients with CD4 count < 200 have an average cost of €6,343. The annual cost of treating opportunistic infections was estimated at €2,111 per patient [ – ].
The primary aim of this study was to assess the efficiency and effectiveness of Community-Based Voluntary Counseling and Testing (CBVCT) for HIV in Turin, conducted as part of the Fast-Track Cities initiative. The study also sought to analyze demographic characteristics and risk factors, both overall and according to the time elapsed since the last HIV test, in order to identify factors associated with routine testing. The investigation also encompassed an assessment of the initiative’s cost-effectiveness, with the objective of ascertaining its sustainability over the medium to long term. It is anticipated that the findings of this study will provide invaluable insights from a public health perspective, thereby facilitating the optimization of interventions, the reduction of HIV transmission, and the enhancement of health outcomes.

## Methods

## Study design
This study utilized a cross-sectional design to evaluate the efficiency and effectiveness of CBVCT for HIV in Turin as part of the Fast-Track Cities initiative. Efficiency concerns the relationship between the quantity of resources used and the quantity of results obtained. In this study, effectiveness refers to the extent to which the service achieves its intended qualitative and quantitative outcomes, meaning how well the testing program meets predefined standards in terms of accessibility, diagnostic capability, and linkage to care [ ]. Additionally, effectiveness is closely linked to the economic sustainability of the intervention, assessed through a cost-effectiveness analysis. This aspect allows us to determine whether the resources used generate significant health benefits, justifying the investment in terms of early diagnosis and future healthcare cost reduction [ , ]. 
Efficiency and effectiveness were analyzed to determine sustainability from a financial-economic point of view, understood as a balance between the two criteria [ ]. Subsequently, the analysis focuses on the effectiveness of the testing and information activity based on the previous evidence provided by the literature [ ]. Furthermore, the statistical analysis of the sample of users provides clear evidence on the effectiveness and efficiency of the action, alongside other studies conducted on HIV fast track cities in Italy considering the sustainability of the approach [ , – ].
The study by Marcellusi et al. provides the elements necessary to identify the savings costs and cost-efficiency and cost-effectiveness associated with each HIV + patient identified on the basis of the Italian context in the pandemic period [ ]. The methodology used to assess costs associated with HIV patients based on CD4 + levels involved the adaptation of a Markov model from existing literature. The simulations considered continuous treatment for already diagnosed patients and immediate initiation of therapy for newly diagnosed patients. The analysis was performed on a population stratified by CD4 + levels and used both diagnosed and undiagnosed patients from surveillance data. Costs and outcomes were discounted at 3% annually, using a 10-year time horizon, and outcomes included QALYs and direct healthcare costs​. The approach is more innovative than those proposed and more widespread according to the analysis of the literature aimed at analyzing cost-effectiveness although QALY has already been adopted in the past [ ].
Furthermore, the study aimed to analyze demographic and behavioral factors influencing testing behaviors. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) for cross-sectional studies was used to report the study [ ]. The STROBE checklist is available as Supplementary Material .

## Participants
Participants were people over 18 years of age, not known to be HIV positive, who attended HIV testing sessions organized by third sector associations in Turin, Northern Italy, from 28 th of January to 30 th of December 2022. The study included people from different socio-demographic backgrounds and sexual orientations.

## Data collection and tools
Data were collected through anonymous, paper-based questionnaires administered during testing sessions. The questionnaire was developed based on guidelines provided by COBATEST [ ], and is provided as Supplementary Material . The questionnaire unifies the sections data collection form, PrEP/Chemsex module, Other STIs module present at the link https://cobatest.org/en/tool/. The survey covered socio-demographic information, reasons for HIV testing, and risk behaviors. These variables are explained in depth in the “measures” section. Participation was voluntary, anonymous, and unpaid. Each participant adhered by signing the informed consent and privacy policy. Data were uploaded to the COBATEST platform and subsequently downloaded anonymously for analysis. Therefore, this study does not require approval by a bioethics committee. All procedures adhered to the principles of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent revisions.
The economic-financial report of the initiative used in the co-planning phase by the City of Turin supported the analysis of cost-efficiency and cost-effectiveness. One of the authors’ direct collections of information and involvement in the co-design, co-planning, and execution phases of the Turin Fast Track City project guaranteed the truthfulness of the economic information shared with third-sector partners. The data is also present in the public co-planning and co-design documents signed by the City with Third Sector Bodies. The economic and financial elements associated with the number of reactivities confirmed and recorded by the questionnaire supports the analysis and deduction in terms of cost-effectiveness and cost-efficiency.

## Setting and project organization
The project was implemented through the collaboration of various third-sector associations, enabling the organization of Community-Based Voluntary Counseling and Testing (CBVCT) activities. These activities were conducted at two fixed locations: one permanently at Casa Arcobaleno ODV and another rotating among participating associations (Abele Onlus Group, Italian Red Cross - Turin Committee, Turin Section Anlaids, Lila Piemonte, Giobbe Association, and Arcobaleno AIDS). Additionally, a testing station was set up at the Torino Pride event in June 2022. At each location, trained counselors provided pre-test counseling to all consenting participants, covering essential information about HIV/AIDS, testing procedures, confidentiality, and result reliability. Three types of rapid tests were utilized: Alere Determine™ HIV-1/2 and Alere Syphilis Determine™, providing results in approximately 15–30 min [ , ], and INSTI-Multiplex HIV ½ Syphilis Ab test, used specifically at the Torino Pride event, capable of yielding reactive results within one minute [ ].
Post-test counseling was provided to all participants, and in cases of positive results, patients were referred for confirmatory serological testing and linked to care. Participants also completed a paper-based questionnaire, which volunteers later uploaded to the COBATEST platform for analysis. Further details on the event organization can be found in the referenced articles [ , ].

## Economical measures
In light of the €20,000 funding provided by the Piedmont Region and the Italian Ministry of Labor and Social Policies, the total cost amounted to €25,067, incorporating a 20.2% co-financing by third sector organizations (associations). The expenditure breakdown includes rapid HIV and Syphilis tests administration (59.8% of the total costs), first aid medical supplies (7.3% of the total costs), website and promotional activities (10.0% of the total costs), biological waste disposal services (12.6% of the total costs), planning activities and administrative management (5.0% of the total costs), and insurance for volunteer protection (5.3% of the total costs). The project utilized three association offices (Casa Arcobaleno, Croce Rossa, and Gruppo Abele) and involved 20 volunteers, adhering to national norms (Italian Ministry of Health, 2021), nine doctors, four nurses, and four counselors. It also covered consumables and an agreement with the City of Turin and the Regional Health System for communication about the availability of rapid tests in an extra-hospital setting whenever reactivity is detected.

## Sample size calculation
Considering the population of Turin (N = 886,837 inhabitants), a margin of error of 5%, a confidence level of 95%, and a standard deviation of 50%, the required sample size was calculated to be 384 participants. For this purpose, the Rao Soft sample size calculator was used [ ].

## Statistical analysis
Descriptive statistics were used to summarize process and outcome measures, socio-demographic characteristics, reasons why participants underwent HIV testing, risk behaviors and risk factors.
Based on the literature [ , ], several process and outcome measures were identified to evaluate the project’s effectiveness:

Coverage: Number of people approached/total number of service users.
Testing Rate: Number of tested people/total number of approached people.
Positivity Rate: Number of people with reactive tests/total number of tested people.
Linkage to Care: Number of people attending the referral clinical center for confirmatory testing and initial visit/number of people with reactive tests.

The precise calculation of the project’s coverage rate (i.e., the ratio of individuals approached to the total number of service users) is not feasible due to several factors. The broad population focus of the initiative makes it difficult to establish a fixed number of service users. The project’s activities were conducted both at fixed locations and during large-scale events, such as Torino Pride, where logistical challenges inherent to the context made it impractical to record every individual interaction. Furthermore, the absence of data on the proportion of at-risk individuals within the Turin population adds another layer of complexity, rendering it impossible to accurately estimate the coverage rate.

## Measures
The socio-demographic variables included were gender, age (median), nationality, sexual orientation, and geographic area. Additionally, participants’ reasons for HIV testing were examined, categorized as either risk exposure or routine screening. The study also investigated various risk behaviors and factors, including condom use during the last sexual intercourse, receipt of payment for sex (sex worker), history of sexually transmitted infections (STIs), instances of unprotected sex with HIV-positive individuals, awareness of partners’ serological status, unprotected sex with men who have sex with men (MSM), engagement in chemsex, and awareness about pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP).
Differences among individuals undergoing HIV testing for the first time and those tested within different time frames (less than 12 months, and more than 12 months prior) were evaluated using Pearson’s chi-squared test, Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test, and Fisher’s exact test, when appropriate. The decision to categorize within 12 months and between 12 and 24 months allows for the granularity of the analysis to be enhanced, as has been demonstrated in other studies in the literature [ ]. For this analysis, the pairwise deletion method was employed.
Descriptive statistics, univariate and logistic regression of previous and first-time tested individuals were performed to ascertain whether individuals undergoing HIV testing for the first time exhibited significant differences with respect to key variables identified by the research team based on research hypotheses and the scientific literature on the subject, when compared to those who had previously been tested. For this analysis, the listwise deletion method was employed.
A two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was performed using the statistical software Jamovi [ ].
In order to calculate the cost-effectiveness of the initiative, we considered data provided by Marcellusi et al. on yearly cost for treatment of HIV, which we referenced in the introduction of this study [ ].

## Results

## HIV and syphilis Testing - process and outcome measures
A total of 795 HIV tests were administered. Questionnaires were distributed to a sample of 452 participants, of whom 451 individuals underwent HIV testing, yielding a testing rate of 99.9%. The positivity rate, defined as the number of individuals with reactive tests divided by the total number of individuals tested, was 0.5%. Subsequent confirmatory testing validated these results, with all 4 cases (100%) successfully linked to care.
Syphilis tests were administered to 610 participants (76.7%), among whom only 4 cases tested reactive (0.7%). The number of participants that undergo the syphilis tests is lower than the HIV tests since the main focus of the activity was the HIV screening and the Syphilis tests were not available at every venue and on every occasion. Moreover, the difference between the number of HIV tests and Syphilis tests administered was already foreseen by the project since the beginning. Further details are shown in Fig.  .
 

## Cost-effectiveness of the project
Marcellusi et al. updated the costs to 2020 and assessed the expenses associated with the average annual treatment cost during the pandemic in Italy [ ]. They evaluated the costs for the healthcare system related to CD4 levels and the productivity loss linked to HIV + pathology. Using epidemiological data, described models, and the reference sample, they estimated the savings in Italy due to access to therapies and the reduction in average treatment costs before immunodeficiency onset. Immediate diagnosis ensures access to antiviral therapies, which increases CD4 counts and reduces associated costs by €3,841 each [ , ]. The expenditure reduction is calculated as the difference between costs for CD4 < 200 (€6,343) and CD4 ≥ 500 (€2,502). For four patients under healthcare system management, the savings amount to €8,444 plus €15,364, resulting in total savings of €23,808 (the average reduction in prospective treatment costs compared to the timeframe with antiviral drug assumption).

## Analysis of socio-demographic characteristics and risk behaviors, overall and according to the time from the last HIV test
Through descriptive statistics and univariate analysis, notable disparities have been identified (p < 0.05) between individuals undergoing HIV testing for the first time and those tested within varying time intervals (less than 12 months versus more than 12 months prior). Significant differences were found across gender, median age, sexual orientation, history of sexually transmitted infection (STI) diagnosis, recent sexual activity with MSM within the last 12 months, and awareness of PreP (p < 0.001).
Regarding gender distribution, a significant difference was observed among the groups (p < 0.001): males comprised the majority of the sample across all categories, with the highest proportion observed among those who had been tested less then 12 months before. Age median also varied significantly across the groups (p < 0.001): participants who had undergone testing within the last 12 months had a higher median age (31 years, IQR: 27–40). No significant difference was found in nationality distribution among the groups. In the sample investigated, 400 participants were Italian (89.3%). Significant differences were observed in sexual orientation distribution (p < 0.001): the majority of MSM were found among those tested within the last 12 months, while heterosexual women (FSM) were more prevalent among those who had never been tested or had an unknown testing history. Geographically, no significant difference was observed among the groups. Most of the participants were from northern Italy (81.1%), where the project was conducted.
Considering the reasons participants got tested for HIV, no significant differences were observed between the groups in the percentage of participants who underwent testing for risk exposure (40.9%) or screening purposes (85.8%). The subsequent chapter and Table  present a comprehensive account of the reasons behind the participants’ decision to undergo HIV testing, as well as the sources of information that influenced their decision to participate in the project.
 About the risk behaviors and risk factors, a statistically significant difference was observed between the groups in the proportion of participants who had previously received a diagnosis of a STI (p < 0.001): those tested within the previous 12 months exhibited a higher prevalence of STI diagnosis compared to other groups. Furthermore, significant differences were observed in the proportion of participants reporting unprotected sex with MSM (p < 0.001), which represented 40.8% of the sample. A significant difference was observed in those who had heard about pre-exposure prophylaxis (PreP) (p < 0.001). The study found no significant differences in the prevalence of condom use, receipt of payment for sex, unprotected sex with HIV-positive individuals, knowledge of the serological status of the partner, and engagement in chemsex across the groups. ## Reasons why participants underwent HIV testing and the sources of their awareness of the project
Among the cohort subjected to testing, 185 participants (40.9%) cited exposure-related concerns as their primary motivation: notably, the predominant factors driving this motivation included engagement in unprotected oral sex (61.6%), followed by unprotected vaginal (43.8%) and anal intercourse (36.2%). For 388 participants (85.5%), the impetus for testing stemmed from control and screening objectives. Particularly noteworthy were motivations such as routine health monitoring (62.1%) and a desire to ascertain personal health status (44.8%). A minority opted for testing due to either a previous test falling within the window period (2%) or the emergence of clinical symptoms (0.4%). With regard to the decision to participate in the testing program at the centers involved in the Turin Fast Track City project, the participants indicated that they had become aware of the project through printed materials (33.2%) and online sources (28.3%). Additionally, 80 (17.7%) individuals indicated that they were already familiar with the project because they had previously participated in it. Further details can be found in Table  .

## Logistic regression results of previous and first-time tested individuals
The logistic regression analysis offers a comprehensive insight into the underlying factors and discrepancies between individuals undergoing HIV testing for the first time and those who have been tested previously. People who had been previously tested were older (OR = 1.15; p < 0.001), less likely to identify as heterosexual, both MSF (OR = 0.132, p < 0.001) and FSM (0.183, p < 0.001), and less likely to report no STI diagnosis in the last 12 months (OR = 0.128, p = 0.003) compared to those tested for the first time. In contrast, people testing for the first time were younger, less likely to identify as MSM, and less likely to have a history of STI diagnosis.
No significant differences were observed between first-time and repeated testers with respect to nationality, reasons for HIV testing, or condom use during their last sexual intercourse. These findings provide a clearer profile of first-time testers compared to those who have previously undergone testing. ## Discussion
The present study assessed the processes and outcomes of HIV and syphilis testing among a sample of 795 participants, alongside the analysis of demographic and behavioral factors influencing HIV testing behaviors among the 452 subjects who responded to the questionnaire. The high testing rate (99.9%) observed in our sample provides evidence of the feasibility and acceptability of CBVCT programs. Four cases of HIV were diagnosed, resulting in a positivity rate of 0.5%. This finding is consistent with the results of other studies on the same subject at the Italian national level, such as the study by Scognamiglio et al., in which an HIV rapid test was performed on oral fluid [ ]. The same efficiency and effectiveness criteria adopted for rapid testing on oral fluid can also be confirmed for rapid blood testing.
All individuals who tested positive were successfully linked to care (100%). This represents a crucial aspect of the Fast-Track Cities initiative, which demonstrates the efficacy of a comprehensive continuum of care following a diagnosis, as well as the value of the bridge between the association network and the NHS.

The highlighted results indicate that the CBVCT project is cost-effective. Furthermore, it should be noted that the actual savings extend well beyond the mere early diagnosis of HIV. Indeed, the value of the community-based project is enhanced by its crucial role in disseminating information about risk factors of infections, encouraging healthy behaviors, increasing awareness of personal serological status, and preventing transmission [ ].
In contrast, the testing rate for syphilis was significantly lower (76.7%), which may suggest the need for increased awareness and availability of syphilis testing. As previously stated in the introduction, the number of participants who underwent syphilis testing was lower than the number of individuals who underwent HIV testing. This was due to the fact that the primary objective of the activity was to screen for HIV, and syphilis tests were not available at every location and on every occasion. Despite the lower number of syphilis tests performed, four positive cases were detected. The overlap of risk factors and behaviors among these infections calls for a comprehensive approach to STI prevention and management.
The project’s implementation during a period of pandemic-related service disruptions amplified its importance. Many traditional testing services were either closed or undergoing reorganization, making the community-based approach a vital alternative. The high engagement rates observed in our study reflect the project’s ability to bridge service gaps and ensure continued access to testing.
The analysis revealed significant demographic and behavioral differences among participants, particularly between those undergoing HIV testing for the first time and those with prior testing history. Men, particularly those who identified as MSM, were more likely to have tested for HIV within the last 12 months, which aligns with regional epidemiological trends, highlighting the disproportionate impact of HIV on MSM populations. The logistic regression confirmed this result, indicating that MSM are more likely to have been previously tested, while heterosexual men (MSF) and women (FSM) are more likely to be first-time testers. This trend may be influenced by heightened awareness of HIV risks and prevention measures within the MSM community, as well as targeted health promotion campaigns. However, the prevalence of heterosexual man and women who had never been tested or had an unknown testing history indicates a potential gap in reaching this demographic, highlighting a need for tailored interventions to promote testing in diverse populations.
The higher median age among recently tested individuals (31 years) suggests that older individuals in the sample are more engaged in regular testing, which could be attributed to increased risk perception or more regular interactions with healthcare services. The significant difference in median age suggests that younger individuals are more likely to be first-time testers. The logistic regression result indicates that with each additional year of age, the odds of being a first-time tester decrease by 15% (OR = 1.15, 95% CI = 1.097–1.206). This finding may be indicative of an increase in awareness and testing habits among older individuals. In light of the documented high incidence of HIV among young people in the international literature and the confirmed surveillance data in Italy (6.5 new cases per 100,000 people between the ages of 25 and 29), this demographic group represents a crucial target for HIV prevention efforts [ , ].
The literature also indicates that infection rates are high among foreign individuals, accounting for 31.2% of new diagnoses in Italy, according to the most recent surveillance data [ ]. Sex-workers and injection drug use are also important risk factors for the spread of HIV infection [ ]. However, in our sample, foreign individuals, sex workers, and IDU users represent a residual share, so it is not possible to provide further specific observations regarding these population subgroups.
The analysis indicated that a greater number of participants were motivated to test for routine screening purposes rather than perceived risk exposure. This finding suggests that ongoing engagement in regular health monitoring is a significant driver of testing behavior, as also shown in other studies on the subject [ ]. Participants with a history of STI diagnoses were more likely to test frequently, highlighting a proactive approach to sexual health among this group.
The significant proportion of participants reporting unprotected sex further underscores the necessity for continuous education and prevention efforts, particularly around safe sex practices and the availability of PrEP. Individuals who have been tested within the last year are more familiar with the service and have a greater awareness of PrEP. However, there remains a significant segment of the population who are not aware of this preventive measure, as evidenced by scientific literature on the subject [ , ]. This gap suggests that educational campaigns, such as those promoted within the Fast Track Cities project, are essential to improve risk perception and PrEP awareness and uptake, especially among high-risk groups.
The study demonstrates that the collaboration between public entities, such as municipalities and healthcare service, initially supported by funding from the Ministry, has achieved financial and economic efficiency. The allocated funding was effectively distributed to meet the expected outcomes, including the number of tests administered, the identification of HIV-positive individuals, and the sensitization of users during counseling sessions. A cost-efficiency analysis highlights the capacity to cover public funding of €20,000, along with economic savings of €23,808 generated for healthcare organizations. The creation of the website, a one-time expense, does not pose a long-term financial burden on the continued implementation of Turin’s Fast-Track City activities. In this regard, cost-effectiveness is also maintained due to the co-financing provided by participating associations. The evidence of financial and economic efficiency suggests the potential for medium-term sustainability of the network. However, it is important to acknowledge that the study by Marcellusi et al. was critical in estimating economic savings and the capacity for financial coverage. The application of the Markov model, quality-adjusted life years (QALY), and direct cost analysis in other contexts is feasible, but it requires careful consideration of the specific healthcare system and context. Nonetheless, the study demonstrates its value in supporting cost-efficiency analyses.

## Implications for public health
The findings from this study have several implications for public health strategies aimed at HIV and syphilis prevention. First, the high HIV testing rate is commendable and should be sustained through ongoing community engagement and outreach programs. Public health initiatives should aim to integrate syphilis testing with HIV testing services to enhance overall sexual health outcomes.
Moreover, targeted interventions are required to address the disparities in testing behaviors observed across different demographic groups. Given the lack of knowledge and awareness regarding STI, as also evidenced by Viola et al., there is an urgent need for more comprehensive educational campaigns that emphasize the importance of regular testing and preventive measures for avoiding infection. Such campaigns should be widely disseminated to ensure that the general public has access to accurate information and is encouraged to take appropriate measures to protect their health [ ].
The study demonstrates the economic sustainability of community-based HIV prevention projects with NHS savings justifying integrated HIV prevention and diagnosis policies.

## Limitations and future research
This study has several limitations that could impact the interpretation of its findings. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between demographic and behavioral factors and testing behaviors. Longitudinal studies would provide a more comprehensive understanding of how these factors influence testing behaviors over time and help identify effective interventions.
There are several potential biases to consider. Selection bias may have occurred as participants were recruited from specific CBVCT locations and events such as Torino Pride, which might not represent the broader population of Turin, particularly those who do not attend such events or access these specific services. However, it should be considered that the initiative is aimed at reaching high-risk groups. Response bias is also a concern, given the reliance on self-reported questionnaires. Participants may provide socially desirable answers rather than truthful responses, especially concerning sensitive topics like sexual behavior and drug use. To try to limit the effect of this bias, the questionnaires were collected anonymously. Recall bias could affect the accuracy of past behaviors reported by participants; using a narrow time frame of 12 months may help reduce this bias. Additionally, volunteer bias might be present, as individuals who chose to participate in the study may differ systematically from those who did not, potentially being more health-conscious or more aware of HIV and STI risks, thus skewing the results.
Additionally, this study lacked comprehensive data on participants’ lifetime risk behaviors. The information collected was limited to the past 12 months, as it was primarily intended to support immediate counseling needs [ ]. Expanding the scope of data collection to include lifetime risk behaviors would offer a more complete picture of the participants’ health profiles and risk factors.
Addressing these limitations in future research could enhance the validity and applicability of findings, ultimately contributing to more effective public health strategies for HIV and STI prevention.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, the project is efficient in terms of process and outcome measures due to a high testing rate, a positivity rate in line with other projects of this type, and a linkage to care of 100% positive cases. The community-based approach of Turin Fast Track Cities thus represents an excellent initiative to engage the target population, enhance accessibility, foster early diagnosis and treatment, and disseminate knowledge for the purpose of fostering health education on risk factors and prevention for HIV. The study identified significant disparities in testing behaviors and awareness of preventive measures. Addressing these gaps through targeted public health interventions is crucial to improving sexual health outcomes and reducing the burden of HIV and syphilis in the population. The study demonstrates cost-efficiency based on correct use of resources to answer the citizen request needs. Cost-efficiency is proven by the coverage of costs producing economic savings to obtain an optimal result in terms of the number of users tested and the identification of HIV + subjects. The study proposes and offers tools for measuring the efficiency and effectiveness of community-based HIV prevention activities carried out by the Fast Track City network. The study is part of the debate regarding the cost-effectiveness and cost-efficiency of tests and the measurement in development countries of the effectiveness of the results linked to rapid HIV test [ ]. Nonetheless, further comparisons in other contexts and different healthcare systems are needed to highlight economic sustainability by replicating the adopted methodology or testing new measurement approaches. The statistical metrics and data collection adopted thanks to the COBATEST tools confirm the ability to analyze the result and provide comparison metrics in future studies with other contexts participating in Fast Track City [ – ].
