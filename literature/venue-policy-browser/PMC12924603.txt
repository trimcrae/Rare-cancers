## Abstract

## Background
Breast cancer is the most diagnosed cancer among women in the United States. While rates of advanced-stage breast cancer have declined overall, incidence is rising among Korean American women. Advanced-stage breast cancer is more frequent among Korean women compared to other Asian ethnic groups, largely attributed to low adherence to breast cancer screening. Church settings offer important opportunities for education as most Korean American women over age 50 regularly attend church services. The objective of this trial is to evaluate the efficacy of a culturally tailored intervention Faith in Action! on breast cancer screening adherence rates among Korean American women.

## Methods
A parallel cluster randomized trial with staggered roll-out was designed to evaluate the efficacy of the Faith in Action! intervention, a church-based small group education and lay health navigator program, delivered by trained church members, on breast cancer screening among Korean American women (NCT05298605). The study was designed and will be conducted in partnership with our center’s Community Outreach and Engagement (COE), which formed a Community Advisory Board. Twenty-four total Korean churches in Los Angeles will be randomized within blocks of 8 churches to either intervention or waitlist control conditions in a 1:1 ratio. Using a train-the-trainer approach, lay navigators from the Korean Church community nominated by church leadership will be trained by COE and certified to deliver the Faith in Action! intervention to study participants over a 6-month program period. Participants at churches randomized to the waitlist control group receive an educational presentation on physical activity and nutrition and, after 6-month follow-up, are invited to receive the Faith in Action! intervention. Eligible participants include women, ages 45–75 years, who attend a participating church in Los Angeles County, do not have a breast cancer diagnosis, and did not receive a mammogram in the last 2 years. The primary outcome is adherence to breast cancer screening guidelines within 6 months from the end of the intervention. Secondary outcomes include adherence to breast cancer screening within 1 year and knowledge on breast cancer screening.

## Discussion
This study addresses a current need for developing and testing culturally tailored interventions to increase breast cancer screening adherence among Korean American women. Leveraging faith-based organizations and delivering the intervention through trained lay health navigators, if found to be effective, has the potential to provide a scalable and sustainable strategy for increasing screening rates and ultimately improve health and well-being as well as reduce cancer-related disparities in this population.

## Introduction

## Background and rationale {6a}
Breast cancer remains the most commonly diagnosed cancer among women in the United States. Although female breast cancer trends have been declining or stable among women in the United States, breast cancer rates have increased among Korean immigrant women, who continue to have the highest breast cancer mortality rates. One notable modifiable risk factor is that Korean women have the lowest screening rates across all racial and ethnic groups [ , ]. Despite increases in mammogram utilization over the past two decades, disparities in screening practices still exist among Asian Americans [ ]. Use of mammography in women over 40 years of age in the United States was reported to be 59.7% (SE: 2.8) in Asian Americans, compared with either Whites (65.3%, SE: 0.7) or African Americans (69.8%, SE: 1.5) [ , ]. Screening rates are even lower in California, based on results from the 2016 California Health Interview Survey, where only 52% of eligible Korean women had a mammography in the past 2 years [ ]. Los Angeles County has the largest Korean American population in the USA, with a total of 1.4 million Asian Americans and approximately 226,000 Korean individuals [ ]. The risk for breast cancer among Korean women living in Los Angeles continues to climb alongside the national trend, for both foreign and US-born women [ ]. The rate of invasive breast cancer among Korean women in Los Angeles County has also increased dramatically from 1976 to 2012 and is now similar to rates seen in Chinese and South Asian women [ ]. Further, proportionately more breast cancers expressed HER2 relative to HR+/HER2− in Korean women, and HER+ breast cancers tend to grow more quickly, spread more aggressively, present more often as high-grade disease, and have an increased risk for death [ , ]. Geographic disparities are also observed within the Korean American community; the proportion of breast cancers diagnosed at advanced stages is greater in Los Angeles’s Koreatown neighborhood than in surrounding areas.
Reasons for low screening utilization include socioeconomic and geographic disparities, cultural barriers, beliefs, trust, lack of education, and culturally tailored communication on cancer risk and the importance of screening [ , ]. Koreans in Koreatown, the cultural hub for Korean Americans living in Southern California and the most densely populated area in Los Angeles County, have reported more limited English proficiency, lower household income, and lower education levels compared to other Korean Americans in California [ , ]. Disparities also exist in health literacy among foreign-born Korean Americans, and despite the high number of Koreans who own cell phones, Korean elderly living in the USA have shown low health literacy and limited access to credible medical information [ – ]. 
There is a current need for culturally tailored interventions to increase breast cancer screening adherence among Korean American women. Interventions among Korean women that include education and information about free or low-cost mammograms have yielded positive results [ – ]. In other disease sites, churches have been shown to be successful venues for community interventions aiming to increase cervical and colorectal cancer screening among Korean men and women [ , , ]. RCTs involving Korean churches have also been performed to affect hepatitis B virus screening and vaccination and mammography screening, with significant increases in screening behavior in each trial [ , , , ]. 
However, to date, no studies have evaluated the efficacy and implementation of culturally tailored interventions to increase breast cancer screening adherence in Korean women in faith-based settings, and none had been delivered by peer agents. Thus, building on our previous work implemented by our center’s COE, which developed strong collaborations with stakeholders and faith leaders in the Korean community, we designed a cluster randomized trial to evaluate a culturally adapted curriculum and training or expert school titled Faith in Action! developed by our team, and its impact on breast cancer screening in Korean women recruited from churches in Los Angeles County.

## Objectives {7}
The primary objective of the trial is to evaluate the efficacy of the Faith in Action! intervention on breast cancer screening adherence rates among Korean American women. Secondary objectives include determining factors associated with adherence to screening (demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, behavioral and cultural, and anthropologic factors), assessing the knowledge and barriers surrounding cancer screening, and evaluating program acceptability through qualitative key informant interviews.

## Trial design {8}
A parallel cluster randomized trial (CRT) with staggered roll-out will be conducted to evaluate the efficacy of the Faith in Action! intervention, delivered by church navigators, on breast cancer screening rates among Korean American women. This will involve three blocks of 4–8 churches each (total 20–24 churches) randomized to either the intervention or waitlist control (Fig.  ). Churches randomized to the waitlist control group will receive an educational presentation on physical activity and nutrition. This design will enable all churches to eventually receive the intervention and distribute the training effort across time (e.g., training navigators and administering the expert learning program) and churches randomized to waitlist control will still receive the intervention. The trial was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov on March 17, 2022 (NCT05298605).
 

## Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

## Study setting {9}
The study will be based in Los Angeles County, which has the largest ethnic Korean population outside of the Korean Peninsula. Subjects will be recruited from participating Korean churches in Los Angeles County. The community of interest is the Korean American community, which has an increasing incidence of breast cancer, low adherence to breast cancer screening guidelines, and a higher proportion of later-stage breast cancer at diagnosis than other racial/ethnic groups. The intervention we propose leverages the Korean culture’s reverence and engagement with “schools” at which they learn from experts. The venue for the intervention will be churches because weekly church attendance is very high in the Korean American community, and other research cited above demonstrates that church-based interventions are effective. This study will be carried out fully in partnership with the Los Angeles Onnuri Church, which serves as the central hub for recruitment, delivery, and coordination of activities. The church’s existing infrastructure and trusted relationships within the community were integral to program implementation.

## Eligibility criteria {10}
Eligibility criteria for participating navigators and participants are summarized in Tables and , respectively. Eligible participants for the trial identify as women of Korean descent between the ages of 45–75 years, currently attending one of the participating Korean churches in Los Angeles, who do not currently have a breast cancer diagnosis, are eligible for, but non-adherent to cancer screening (did not receive a mammogram in the last 2 years based on self-report), speak Korean and/or English, and are willing to comply with protocol procedures including participation in the presentation and navigation. Any person who does not meet the inclusion criteria will be excluded from the study (see Tables  and ).

 

## Who will take informed consent? {26a}
Only certified study coordinators who are bilingual in Korean and English with in-depth knowledge about the project will obtain consent from potential trial participants:

Navigator consent: In collaboration with the Faith leaders at each of the Churches, potential navigators who meet the eligibility criteria and are willing to participate in the study will be identified. Prior to attending training sessions and participating in key informant interviews, navigators will provide written informed consent. Navigators will then be contacted by the research team initially via email or phone to explain the purpose of the interviews and coordinate interview participation. Participants will be provided with a study consent form via email or in person and will provide signed informed consent.
Participant consent: All participants will complete informed consent in their preferred language (English or Korean), prior to participation in any research activities. During the screening process, each participant will receive a HIPAA agreement on sharing information with the research team and will be asked to complete a short pre-study screener to assess eligibility criteria (age, gender identification, race/ethnicity, breast cancer screening history). An information sheet will describe the proposed research and provide information on ways to participate in the study, including preferred communication methods. Participants are given ample time to review the materials and have any questions answered before returning a signed consent form to participate in the study.

## Interventions

## Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}
A waitlist control group will serve as the comparator for both ethical and practical reasons, as it ensures that all participants, regardless of group assignment, will ultimately have access to the intervention, and can improve recruitment and retention efforts, especially in those randomized to the control condition. Churches randomized to a waitlist control group will receive a presentation on physical activity and nutrition and a letter notifying them of the anticipated date for when they will receive Faith in Action! Breast cancer education; this date will correspond to the completion of the 6-month intervention period in the paired experimental group. To minimize potential bias or potential crossover, participants and navigators from other churches will not be aware of start dates of other groups or the order in which they will receive the intervention. At the end of the study, the waitlist control group participants will receive the Faith in Action! intervention and control group navigators have the option to participate in the same training as for the navigators who were randomly assigned to churches in the intervention group.
The provision of standard educational content and a presentation on the importance of physical activity and nutrition was selected as an active control, as it reflects general health promotion practices, allows participants to attend a similar workshop (designed to be the same format and style as the intervention at the beginning of the study), but does not include the tailored content or community-specific engagement strategies central to the intervention (i.e., cancer screening practices). The content for education on the benefits of physical activity and nutrition was developed from the UCLA Korean Healthy Life Project, overseen by the co-investigator on this study (Herrmann). This will allow for an appropriate assessment of the study intervention’s specific impact on the primary outcome (breast cancer screening uptake) and reduce potential risk for contamination or drop-out.

## Intervention description {11a}
The intervention consists of two main components: navigator training and participant navigation. A train-the-trainer approach [ ] will be used to train lay health navigators identified by leaders from the participating Korean churches. Navigator training includes two 6-h periods of education followed by four 2-h meetings (total 20 h for 6 months), trained by leaders and members of the Cedars-Sinai Cancer Center’s Community Outreach and Engagement (COE). The lay navigators will be trained in several areas concerning cancer education, including risk factors, screening guidelines, diagnosis, treatment, navigation, and community resources. This curriculum aligns with and is drawn from the Cancer 101 curriculum developed by the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Research Center and the Center for Community Health Promotion, and in part, the George Washington Cancer Institute Core Competencies for Non-Clinically Licensed Patient Navigators [ ]. The curriculum will also include a Human Research Protections section explaining the processes of obtaining consent and randomization (even though these roles are carried out by the research team), so navigators understand that the study is following the recommended best practices in research ethics. The navigators will then recruit and screen participants into the study, deliver a workshop and presentation to participants randomized to the Faith in Action! study group, and provide continued navigation throughout the 6-month study duration, including the identification of individual barriers to receiving care and coordinate with the study team for follow-up care, contacting patients and eliciting information regarding the barriers to completion of recommended care, and developing an individualized and culturally tailored plan to address the barriers that are identified.

## Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}
Participants may discontinue their participation in the study at any time for any reason. As this is a behavioral intervention, the study interventions will not be modified once allocated.

## Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}
The intervention we propose leverages the Korean culture’s reverence and engagement with expert schools delivered in churches because weekly church attendance is high in the Korean American community, and other research cited above demonstrates that church-based interventions are effective. Trusted lay health navigators are central to the intervention and will play a key role in supporting participants and enhancing participant engagement and retention throughout the study. Navigation has been shown to effectively facilitate communication between patients and providers and improve healthcare retention, particularly among populations from underserved communities. Building on this approach, we will continue to engage our COE, Community Advisory Board (CAB), and other community stakeholders in outreach and communication efforts throughout the study.

## Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}
There will be no relevant concomitant care or interventions that are prohibited during the trial. As this study is being conducted in a community-based setting, our aim is to preserve the real-world applicability and generalizability of the intervention. Therefore, participants will not be restricted from accessing other concomitant medical care or health-related interventions (e.g., medical visits, participation in other health promotion activities, or receiving care related to chronic conditions, and mental health). Given that individuals may engage in multiple forms of care simultaneously in such settings, excluding these would not reflect actual conditions in which the intervention might ultimately be delivered or adopted. All conditions and concomitant care are being documented and adjusted for throughout the study.

## Provisions for post-trial care {30}
The waitlist control group will receive Faith in Action! intervention at the end of the study and control group navigators will have the option to participate in the same training as for the navigators who were randomly assigned to churches in the intervention group. For participants who receive abnormal mammography results, follow-up contacts will be made available and follow-up surveys will be administered to capture follow-up care, diagnosis, treatment decisions made, and any barriers experienced in the continuum of care.
Provision of clinical breast exams, mammograms, diagnostic workup, and treatment services for Korean women. We work closely with two local health care providers and the State’s free breast cancer program: Korean Health, Education, Information & Research Center (Kheir Clinic), Koryo Health Foundation, and California’s Every Woman Counts program to actively support navigators trained by Faith in Action! to navigate eligible women into breast cancer screening programs. These programs are already incorporated into the trainer curriculum and provide a seamless system of care for low-income and uninsured Korean community members from screening to diagnosis and treatment services. These partners will actively participate in all the training to provide details about how women can access each of their respective clinics and programs. Access to all Faith in Action! materials and information will remain available to both navigators and participants after completion of the trial, with navigation continuing at the participating churches and its navigators.

## Outcomes {12}
The primary outcome is percent change in adherence to breast cancer screening guidelines within 6 months from the end of the program. Secondary outcomes include adherence to breast cancer screening guidelines within 1 year, knowledge and barriers associated with lack of adherence to breast cancer screening, intervention acceptability (at the participant and navigator level), and global health. To identify factors associated with screening adherence, participant demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, behavioral, and cultural factors will be collected at baseline. For participants who receive abnormal mammography results, follow-up contacts will be made available and follow-up surveys will be administered to capture follow-up care, diagnosis, treatment decisions made, and any barriers experienced in the continuum of care. Outcomes are assessed via self-report in patient surveys administered electronically or on paper, verified in the participant’s medical chart (for the primary outcome), and obtained through qualitative key-informant interviews. Through semi-structured interviews, participants will be asked about their overall program experience, experience with the navigators, barriers or challenges to participation, barriers and facilitators to obtaining a mammogram, and reasons why/why not for screening, which elements were the most or least helpful part of the intervention and navigation, and any additional feedback and recommendations including optimal format for working with navigators. Navigators will also participate in an interview, where they will be asked about the most and least useful components of the navigation training sessions, recommendations for additional training elements and format, and feedback on specific curriculum elements.

## Sample size {14}
The main hypothesis to be tested is that breast cancer screening rates will be higher among subjects that have participated in the program when compared with subjects who have not within 6 months from the implementation of the program. Under the proposed staggered parallel cluster randomized design, we consider a sample of up to 24 churches (minimum 18) divided into 2 groups (intervention or waitlist control) with 3 time periods/blocks (including baseline) and 4 churches each with an average of 7 members (SD: 3.4) per class for churches with < 500 members, and 13 members (SD: 4.2) per class for churches with ≥ 500 members, with coefficients of variation of 0.52 and 0.32, respectively, and accounting for unequal cluster sizes. The minimum detectable screening rate difference ranges are 11.4–13.8%, 14.2–16.9%, and 17.4–20.3% for ICC values of 0.01, 0.05, and 0.10, respectively, with 80% of power at the 5% significance level using one-sided Cochran-Mantel Haenszel Test for an estimated sample size of 200 (100 individuals per group). Therefore, we will declare the program effective if the breast cancer screening rate after 6 months of program implementation for the intervention group is at least 23.8%, 26.9%, and 30.3% for intra-cluster correlation (ICC) values of 0.01, 0.05, and 0.10, and a control screening rate of 10%.

## Recruitment {15}
In collaboration with the Community Outreach and Engagement (COE) team and partnership with the Korean Faith organization leadership, including the co-PI (LA Onnuri Church), recruitment will occur across Korean churches in Los Angeles. Navigators will be referred by Pastors and Church Elders at their discretion. Navigator Recruitment: Churches that have a health ministry or elders will refer individuals who feel comfortable in a leadership position, are involved with the church group projects or are group leaders, and are active in the church member network. Church navigators will then assist with the recruitment of potential individuals into the study from the participating churches and the Korean community. Each church will have 1–2 trained navigators; 2 navigators for larger churches, who will recruit participants, deliver the intervention and be responsible for tracking participant attendance, screening reminders, mammography appointment reminders, and administering the baseline and study questionnaires. 
Participant recruitment: Recruitment efforts include a community information session, hosted by the research team for Church leadership, the distribution of culturally tailored study flyers and information sheets, and through word of mouth via Church members, friends, and family members. If a participant is eligible, navigators will distribute information sheets and confirm their attendance at the workshop. Navigators will keep track of the number of people approached/interested and how many are eligible in a log.
Each participant will receive a HIPAA agreement on sharing information with the research team and will be asked to complete a very short pre-study screener to assess eligibility criteria (age, gender identification, race/ethnicity, breast cancer screening history). Eligibility will be confirmed by study staff prior to enrollment. An information sheet will describe the proposed research and provide information on ways to participate in the study, including preferred communication methods.

## Assignment of interventions: allocation

## Sequence generation {16a}
The allocation sequence was generated using computer-generated random numbers by the senior biostatistician using block randomization (unequal block sizes) and stratified by Church membership size (less than 500 vs. greater than or equal to 500 members).

## Concealment mechanism {16b}
The allocation sequence is stored separately in a secure and private file and will remain concealed from all study investigators and staff. Three groups of 8 churches will be randomized to one of three Faith in Action! Expert School start dates.

## Implementation {16c}
Upon randomization, churches belonging to each group will be provided with the start date of the program in an opaque envelope (dropped off in person with follow up by phone and email) as to which group and sequence they belong to. Only the COE lead and coordinator will be made aware of the assignments, as they are responsible for facilitating the scheduling and navigator training. All other research investigators and staff will be blinded to the assignments.

## Assignment of interventions: blinding

## Who will be blinded {17a}
Participants and navigators will not be blinded due to the nature of the intervention and requirement for engagement with members of the COE team as part of the intervention (e.g., communication with participating Churches and leadership, navigator training). However, study investigators, research staff involved in data collection, outcome assessment, and analysis will be unaware of the allocation sequence. Data managers and the biostatisticians performing the statistical analysis will also be blinded to group assignments.

## Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}
Unblinding may be permissible should a Church drop-out or require to be replaced during the study. As participants and navigators are not blinded to the intervention, there will be no procedures for unblinding at the participant level.

## Data collection and management

## Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}
All data will be entered and managed in a study-specific REDCap database. Data will be collected through questionnaires, completed electronically via REDCap or on paper per the participant’s preference. Questionnaires will be administered at baseline prior to crossover to intervention in each group, and at 6 months post-initiation of the program to conduct end-of-study assessments. Participants will have 6 months’ follow-up time from completion of the program. Post-intervention questionnaires will be administered at 1 month and 2 months post-intervention and at 6 and 12 months post-intervention using REDCap surveys or paper questionnaires, if preferred. Surveys including demographics, medical history, and healthcare access were adapted from existing validated surveys, when applicable, and translated by a certified translator. Surveys were then verified and tested for cultural relevance and appropriateness by the COE Korean lead and Korean speaking study coordinators and data managers. Validated surveys including NIH PROMIS® and the breast cancer screening beliefs surveys will be available in Korean and will be administered and scored in accordance with available standardized protocols and scoring manuals. Medical records as relevant to breast cancer screening will be requested and recorded by trained study coordinators and will include data obtained through the Every Woman Counts program—a California state program that provides free breast and cervical cancer screening and diagnostic services to underserved populations who meet specific eligibility criteria [ ]—and charts from participating Federally Qualified Health Centers (FQHCs) that offer mammography, upon medical release. Requested information will include the type, date, and location of breast cancer screening; relevant medical history; and screening test results. Test results will be confirmed through physician documentation when available, supplementing self-reported data from participants. Screening adherence, follow-up care, and any reported barriers to care will also be recorded. Diagnosis, treatment, and long-term follow-up will fall outside the scope of the current study but may be explored in a future ancillary investigation. For qualitative interviews, deidentified audio recordings will be stored in a secure study folder along with interview transcripts for qualitative analysis. ## Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {18b}
To promote participant retention, we will incorporate multiple evidence-based strategies grounded in our prior work and community engagement experience. By leveraging our established partnerships with community organizations and the COE team, we will implement a structured communication plan that includes regularly scheduled meetings with navigators, standardized scripts and outreach materials for participant engagement, and ongoing cultural competency training for study staff. These strategies will help build trust with participants and ensure clear, consistent communication among the study team, navigators, and participants throughout the study. Communication will be provided in the participant’s preferred language, and reminder phone calls or text messages will be used to support assessment completion and appointment attendance. To address logistical barriers, we will offer remote interactions (e.g., video conferencing, phone calls) whenever possible, provide support through navigators to identify transportation options, and reimburse participants for parking expenses. We will also schedule workshops and training sessions during non-peak hours to accommodate participant availability. Additional strategies to enhance retention will include offering gift card incentives for study participation as well as compensation for time for the study navigators. Research updates will be shared with the participants and navigators to keep participants informed and engaged with study progress, as well as hosting culturally relevant events and educational programming tailored to Korean participants and their families. All research staff and navigators will be thoroughly trained, possess extensive experience working within the community, and demonstrate cultural competence and fluency in the participants’ language to ensure clear and respectful communication throughout the study.

## Data management {19}
All collected data will be entered and managed in a study-specific, HIPAA-compliant, REDCap database, with built-in data validation checks (e.g., range checks, required fields) to promote data quality. Data entry will be performed by trained study personnel, and routine quality control procedures will be conducted to ensure data accuracy and completeness. Completed hard copy surveys will be stored in a locked file cabinet and entered into a 21 CFR Part 11 compliant, HIPAA compliant REDCap database. Interview audio will be recorded using digital recording devices (without any Internet or Wi-Fi capabilities) or via Zoom recording and stored in a password-protected HIPAA compliant Box folder only accessible to the research team. Hard copy and electronic survey completion options will be available. The study staff will be responsible for data processing in accordance with procedural documentation. Database lock will occur once quality assurance procedures have been completed.

Any further identifiable information that may emerge will be removed by the research team prior to review by any other researchers or any presentation of data and findings.

Any further identifiable information that may emerge will be removed by the research team prior to review by any other researchers or any presentation of data and findings.

## Statistical methods

## Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}
Intention-to-treat analysis (ITT) will be employed wherein churches will be analyzed according to their randomized group. Descriptive analyses will be calculated at baseline and follow-up for all variables of interest at the individual and church level. As the primary analysis, the ratio of completion rate will also be evaluated using the Poisson Regression model with robust standard errors. Mixed effects models will be fitted with adherence to screening after 6 months, as well as 1 year as a secondary analysis, with the response variable and study group (Faith in Action! vs. control), randomization period (randomization block), and church size (stratification variable) as fixed effects. Random effects will describe the correlation of participants within the same church. Adjusting for randomization period is important as there may also be underlying temporal effects, although seasonal effects are expected to be low in LAC. We will also explore interactions between randomization period and group as covariates adjusted for participant age, education level, time spent in the United States, marital status, employment status, comorbidities, behaviors (smoking, drinking) as a secondary analysis. We will explore subgroup analyses by church denomination (e.g., Presbyterian, Methodist, Catholic, Baptist/nondenominational, other) at the church level and insurance status and family history of cancer yes/no among individuals. In the case of missing data, we will perform multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) adjusted for patient characteristics as listed above, assuming the missing data mechanism is missing at random. If data is not missing at random, we will consider a conservative analysis, where missing data will be considered as not completed screening. A modified ITT may also be employed, excluding those with missing outcome assessment.
We will conduct a qualitative analysis of interview transcripts (4.4) to identify key themes and concepts surrounding cultural adaptability of the curriculum. Information will be organized using codes by independent investigators and grouped into similar ideas using qualitative software (e.g., NVivo). Results from the qualitative analysis will be presented and discussed with the CAB over two iterative meetings following a modified Delphi approach for curriculum adaptation and obtaining consensus on the preferred content and adapted content to include in the curriculum. Participant interview data will be further analyzed using qualitative methods described above to identify key themes and concepts as well as facilitators and barriers to screening.

## Interim analyses {21b}
Not applicable: No interim analyses or stopping guidelines were planned or conducted during the trial.

## Methods for additional analyses (e.g., subgroup analyses) {20b}
The analyses as described above will be repeated and adjusted for covariates including participant age, education level, time spent in the United States, marital status, employment status, comorbidities, behaviors (smoking, drinking) as a secondary analysis. Planned subgroup analyses will be conducted including analyses at the church level by individual church and denomination (e.g., Presbyterian, Methodist, Catholic, Baptist/nondenominational, other) as well as at the individual level including insurance status and family history of cancer (yes/no) among individuals.

## Methods in analysis to handle protocol non-adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}
Maximum efforts will be made to avoid missing data (see Sects. 11c, 18b). In the case of missing data, we will explore methods for imputation and assessment of missingness to evaluate the impact of data loss. Data patterns for missing variables will be examined using the method proposed by Little [ ]. If the data are found to be missing not at random (MNAR), missing values will be imputed with values drawn from a fully conditional specification using the multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE) algorithm [ , ], with the number of imputed datasets chosen such that the maximum loss of efficiency is 5%. A sensitivity analysis will be performed to evaluate the robustness of the MAR assumption and departures towards the MNAR assumption using pattern-mixture models [ ]. A modified ITT may also be employed, excluding those with missing outcome assessment. Reporting will follow the Ottawa Statement and CONSORT extension for CRT designs [ , ].

## Dissemination plans {31a}
In collaboration with the Korean Community partners overseen by the co-PIs, study updates and findings will be disseminated to the participants and community throughout the study. The sustainability of the program is reinforced by the ongoing commitment of the Los Angeles Onnuri Church to maintain its delivery post-study. This will allow the program to remain accessible to the community and may serve as a model for similar faith-based partnerships. The dissemination plan includes in-person presentations by the research team to faith and community organizations, providers, and language schools. Research findings will also be disseminated internally through seminars and faculty meetings at Cedars-Sinai, through National and International Conferences, as well as journal publications.
Final findings of the study will be disseminated to Korean faith and community leaders at a forum to be held at Cedars-Sinai at completion of the study. There are several large Federally Qualified Health Centers that serve Korean Americans in Southern California, and we will disseminate the findings to these clinics as well (Kheir Center, Koryo Health Foundation, and others). This will follow a culturally tailored model for dissemination and implementation for the target population and is likely to increase the impact of the study and help sustain effective components of the intervention in the community. Any data dissemination material will be presented to the Community Advisory Board for input and approval. This will ensure that any culturally sensitive material is appropriately managed. Furthermore, data will be de-identified to ensure confidentiality among the community members and the public at large.

## Discussion
Building on formative research and longstanding partnerships within the Los Angeles Korean Community, we propose a cluster randomized trial to evaluate a culturally tailored, faith-based navigation intervention to increase breast cancer screening among Korean American women—addressing a critical and unmet need in this population. Significant disparities continue to persist among Korean American women, who report some of the lowest rates of screening adherence in the United States. While evidence-based guidelines exist to promote screening uptake, several cultural, language, and structural barriers exist and contribute to lower rates of screening in the Korean community. Partnering with the Community Outreach and Engagement team (COE), including Korean community experts and Korean-speaking staff, we established partnerships, built trust, and adapted evidence-based cancer screening material to create a culturally relevant intervention. This approach not only leverages the trusted role of churches in the Korean American community but also provides a potentially sustainable model for delivering preventive care education and navigation beyond the trial setting. The trial is adequately powered to assess the effectiveness of the intervention compared to a waitlist control as well as evaluate the acceptability of the intervention and factors associated with the success of the intervention. Through qualitative interviews, we will also gain a deeper understanding of the role of lay navigators and how they can be used to overcome barriers to screening. We expect that this intervention will be effective and that the screening rates in the group randomized to the Faith in Action! intervention group will be higher than those in the waitlist control group. A notable strength of this study includes the partnership with the Los Angeles Onnuri Church, which plans to sustain the program after the study concludes. By embedding the intervention into its regular health and outreach activities, the church ensures continued access for community members. Should this intervention be shown to be effective, it has the potential to offer a scalable and sustainable approach to increasing breast cancer screening and reducing the burden of breast cancer among Korean American women. By training church-delegated lay health navigators, we will empower the community to disseminate cancer prevention interventions in a self-sustaining manner. Increasing awareness of the benefits of breast cancer screening may also help to improve the uptake of other preventive services in the Korean community. To our knowledge, Faith in Action! is the first culturally tailored, faith-based navigation intervention designed specifically for Korean Americans, with the goal of building capacity within the Korean faith community and serving as a model for broader cancer prevention and control initiatives. Finally, the findings from this study can inform the development of future community-based health programs and guide policy efforts focused on advancing equity in cancer prevention across diverse populations.
