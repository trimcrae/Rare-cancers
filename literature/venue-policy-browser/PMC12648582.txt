We introduce CAFE, a novel dataset comprising approximately 37 h of spontaneous, in vivo human–human conversations among 100+ speakers across Algeria. The dialogues cover diverse everyday topics such as sports, science, and technology, and exhibit a rich range of natural conversational phenomena, including explicit code-switching, overlapping speech, non-lexical vocalizations (e.g., laughter, fillers, ambient noise), and dialectal variation reflecting Algeria’s sociolinguistic landscape. To support controlled experiments, CAFE-small includes two curated subsets: (i) CAFE-small-clean (2 h 18 m): Contains utterances with no overlapping speech. (ii) CAFE-small-overlap (17 m): Contains 23 files with overlap segments and timestamps. The dataset also provides rich metadata, including audio chunk IDs, dialect labels, and both raw and linguistically processed transcripts. CAFE offers a valuable resource for advancing ASR, dialect identification, and sociolinguistic analysis in multilingual and low-resource settings.

## Value of the Data

•Addresses a critical gap in Algerian speech resources. CAFE is one of the first public datasets to capture real world, spontaneous code-switching between Algerian Arabic, French, and English. Existing resources such as ALGSAD [ ] and KALAM'DZ [ ] either focus on read or monolingual speech and lack explicit code-switching. CAFE, in contrast, offers high-quality human annotations, Arabic script for Algerian dialect, and Latin script for French and English.
•Supports research on real-world speech variability and multimodal interaction.
The dataset captures authentic conversational dynamics, including overlapping speech, hesitations, repetitions, pronunciation variability, and self-corrections. Processed audio chunks maintain contextual integrity, with durations ranging from 15 to 120 s and segmentation based on natural silence boundaries. Since the recordings originate from online podcast content and involve a variety of speaker devices, the dataset reflects diverse acoustic environments. This makes it valuable for studying prosody, speaker behaviour, training robust ASR systems, and advancing voice-to-voice multimodal large language models (LLMs) that must handle varying input quality.
•Enables fine-grained dialect and code-switching analysis. Each audio chunk includes both raw and linguistically enriched transcriptions, as well as dialect-level labels following the ZAEBUC guidelines [ ]. This makes the dataset suitable for training and evaluating Multilingual ASR systems, code-switch detection and analysis, language identification, and dialect classification models.
•Supports a wide range of downstream tasks and user needs. CAFE is released in two tiers: CAFE-small, a human-annotated subset ideal for benchmarking and detailed linguistic analysis, and CAFE-large, a pseudo-label set that can be reused in tasks such as semi-supervised learning, transfer learning, and cross-lingual adaptation in both academia and industry. The dataset also include segmentation indicating overlapping speech instances.

## Background
Algerian Arabic (Darja) remains significantly underrepresented in speech technology research, despite its widespread use across North Africa. Shaped by diverse historical and sociolinguistic influences, the dialect incorporates elements from Berber, French, Spanish, and Turkish [ ]. Morphologically, Darja differs from Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) through the omission of case endings and simplified sentence structures, while its lexicon includes numerous French and English borrowings with localized phonological patterns [ ].
Several studies have recently investigated ASR for code-switching in different language pairs [ , ], including Mandarin–English [ , ], Chinese–English [ ], Frisian–Dutch [ ], and English–Spanish [ ]. While these efforts have expanded resources for a range of language pairs, datasets for spontaneous Algerian Arabic–French–English code-switching remain scarce. Existing resources such as MGB-3 ADI-5 [ ], KALAM'DZ [ ], and ALGSAD [ ] primarily address dialect identification or read speech, without detailed code-switch annotations. Other code-switching corpora like ZAEBUC-Spoken [ ], QASR [ ], and ArzEn [ ] include MSA-English or dialect-English combinations, yet none specifically target the Algerian dialectal context.
To address this gap, the CAFE dataset was compiled using audio extracted from public podcast recordings, featuring tri-lingual speech in Algerian Arabic, French, and English. Transcriptions were generated through Whisper-based pseudo-labeling [ ] and manually edited. This dataset complements the original research article [ ] by documenting and releasing the data for broader accessibility and reproducibility.

## Data Description
The CAFE dataset comprises approximately 37 h of spontaneous multilingual speech collected from podcast recordings and is structured into two main subsets: CAFE-small and CAFE-large. Both subsets capture rich conversational phenomena such as code-switching, overlapping speech, repetitions, hesitations, non-lexical vocalizations (e.g., laughter, fillers, ambient noise), and dialectal variation, making them valuable for real-world ASR training and evaluation.•CAFE-SMALL: The CAFE-small subset consists of 170 audio files totalling 2 h and 36 min, all of which are fully human-annotated. Each file is accompanied by both raw transcriptions and linguistically enriched annotations based on the ZAEBUC-Spoken guidelines [ ]. The annotations include:∘Explicit code-switching points across Algerian Arabic, French, and English.
∘Non-lexical events marked with tags such as [laugh], [noise], and [breath].
∘Dialectal labels ranging from L0 to L4 representing varying levels of dialect specificity.

From this set, two derived subsets are provided to support different experimental conditions:∘CAFE-small-clean: A curated subset containing 147 audio files (2 h 18 m) with no overlapping speech, ideal for clean ASR benchmarking.
∘CAFE-small-overlap: This subset includes 23 audio files (17 m) that contain explicit overlapping speech segments. Precise time-stamped annotations mark the start and end of overlaps. Additionally, a processed version is provided, where overlapping regions are automatically excised, and adjacent segments are concatenated in both the audio and transcription, enabling ablation studies or alignment-sensitive tasks.

•CAFE-large: The CAFE-large subset comprises 3588 audio files totalling approximately 34 h and 35 min. This larger portion of the corpus was automatically transcribed using an ASR pipeline built on Whisper [ ], enhanced with domain-adapted acoustic pre-processing [ ]. While not manually verified, these pseudo-labeled transcripts offer substantial coverage and are suitable for pretraining, semi-supervised learning, and domain adaptation tasks.

## Audio segmentation
Audio segmentation was performed using the split_on_silence method from the PyDub library [ ]. In the first pass, segments were extracted using a minimum silence duration of 1 s and a silence threshold of −45 dB, aiming to produce chunks between 25 and 60 s in length. If this initial pass failed to yield sufficiently long or coherent segments, a secondary pass was applied with a broader duration range of 15 to 120 s ( ). 
This two-stage segmentation strategy allows the generation of up to 10 segments per original podcast episode, ensuring both contextual completeness and suitability for downstream processing, such as ASR model training and evaluation.

3.3

## Annotation and metadata
In the CAFE-small subset, non-lexical events were annotated according to the ZAEBUC-Spoken guidelines [ ]. Transcription follows a script-based convention: Algerian Darja and Modern Standard Arabic are written in Arabic script, while French and English segments are rendered in Latin script. Numerals are retained in numeric form for clarity, and each audio chunk is tagged with a dialect intensity level (L0 to L4).
Code-switching metrics, including Code-Switching According to a Reference language (CESAR) and Code-Mixing Index (CMI), were computed at the utterance level (see corresponding equations below in the Code-Switching Metrics section). The average CMI across CAFE-small is 0.254. Distributions of these metrics are presented in and .

## Experimental Design, Materials, and Methods
4.1

## Dataset collection
To capture spontaneous speech in the Algerian context, we sourced audio from public podcast recordings rather than controlled read-speech sessions. Specifically, audio content was extracted from the Gusra Podcast YouTube channel, and subsequently segmented using the procedure described in the entitled Section “Audio Segmentation”. This process yielded approximately 37 h of segmented audio with sampling rate of 16 kHz, 16-bit PCM WAV (mono) format to ensure consistency across all files.
Non-lexical vocalizations (e.g., laughter, coughing, fillers such as “mhm,” “umm”) were annotated in CAFE-small following the ZAEBUC-Spoken guidelines [ ] as illustrated in , to reflect their relevance in ASR system performance.

4.2

## Data processing
Our processing pipeline (see ) segments raw audio into chunks that balance contextual integrity with computational tractability (refer to the section entitled Audio Segmentation for more details). 

4.3

## Pseudo-labeling with whisper-based framework
We employed our pseudo-labeling framework introduced in [ ], which utilizes the Whisper large-v3 model [ ] to generate initial transcriptions for both subsets of the dataset:•CAFE-small: Transcriptions were generated using Whisper with default decoding parameters. These initial transcripts served as a basis for manual correction, benchmarking, and further refinement of the labeling pipeline.
•CAFE-large: Informed by the findings on CAFE-small, a tailored pipeline was developed that integrates Whisper with a pyannote-based speaker diarization model (pyannote/speaker-diarization-3.1) [ , ], as described in [ ]. Diarization played a crucial role in identifying speech segments in the audio chunks, particularly at the beginning of each segment, where Whisper expects the first token of speech to appear within the first second of audio input; when initial silence or background noise is present, it often leads to hallucinated or misaligned transcriptions, and given Whisper’s autoregressive decoding, such early errors can propagate to subsequent predictions. To mitigate this, our pipeline removes non-speech content at the start of segments if the initial silence exceeds 0.45 s, ensuring that Whisper receives clean audio files. The resulting pseudo-labels are available internally to our team for manual verification via our web platform illustrated in (see for CAFE-large dataset details). 

4.4

## Data annotation
Manual Transcription of Raw Text.
The manual transcription and linguistic annotation of the CAFE-small subset were carried out by two native Algerian Arabic speakers fluent in French and English, all with prior expertise in NLP and ASR. Prior to annotation, team members underwent a structured training program covering the ZAEBUC-Spoken guidelines [ ], including the handling of code-switching boundaries, non-lexical events, and dialect intensity labeling. In addition, annotators received tutorials on using our custom web-based platform to carry out transcription and quality assurance tasks.
The annotation process followed a three-stage pipeline: (1) Whisper-generated pseudo-labels served as initial drafts; (2) annotators post-edited transcriptions to correct it and reflect spoken content accurately; (3) a dedicated QA reviewer verified each transcription for accuracy and adherence to the ZAEBUC standards. Segments failing QA were returned for further revision. This iterative process ensured consistency and high-quality annotations across the dataset. Each audio file was assigned to a single annotator; therefore, no inter-annotator agreement (IAA) was computed. Instead, annotation consistency was ensured through the dedicated QA review stage.
Transcriptions adhere strictly to spoken content, where words were transcribed exactly as spoken, using:•Arabic script for Algerian dialect and MSA.
•Latin script for French and English.
•No punctuation or normalization of background noise.

## ZAEBUC-Spoken guidelines
In a parallel annotation track, CAFE-small was transcribed following the ZAEBUC-Spoken corpus standards [ ], which prescribe uniform treatment of punctuation, numerals, background sounds, mispronunciations, and code-switch formatting. illustrates examples.

4.6

## Overlapping speech subset
Overlapped speech regions were identified using the pyannote overlapped-speech-detection model [ , ]. For the 23 files containing overlap, only the audio portions classified as overlapping were removed from the original segments, and the corresponding transcribed words within those regions were removed to maintain alignment, as clarified in the next section. This process resulted the cafe-small-overlap subset. All processing preserved the original audio fidelity (16 kHz sampling rate, 16-bit PCM mono format). This procedure was motivated by two goals: (a) to examine how ASR models handles overlapping speech; (b) to prepare a curated subset with annotated overlapping regions for evaluating and benchmarking our pseudo-labeling framework under realistic, challenging conditions.

4.7

## Linguistic processing
Following acoustic cleaning, corresponding transcription files were updated to remove any tokens aligned with the excised overlapping portions or unintelligible speech fragments. This step ensures that the remaining audio and transcripts remain tightly aligned for accurate downstream analysis and model evaluation.
Why Overlapping Speech in CAFE Is Critical for ASR Evaluation
While the acoustic and linguistic processing helped align the overlapping subset, this task highlights the inherent challenges posed by realistic, conversational data such as that found in our dataset (CAFE). Unlike scripted or read speech, spontaneous podcast interactions often include natural interruptions, where Speaker A cuts in while Speaker B is still articulating a word. When overlapping regions are automatically detected and removed, this frequently results in truncated or incomplete words. Even when partially spoken words are retained during transcription for alignment purposes, such artifacts still lead to degraded ASR performance. As noted in [ ], this reflects the complexity of handling overlap in real world, multi-speaker scenarios and underscores the value of CAFE in evaluating the robustness of modern ASR systems under such conditions.

## Limitations
The gender distribution in CAFE-small (4F/31 M) and CAFE-large (18F/70 M) reflects the speaker composition of the source podcast, which includes predominantly male guests. While this results in limited gender balance, it faithfully represents the characteristics of the original material. Researchers are encouraged to complement this dataset with more gender-balanced resources when developing inclusive ASR systems.
