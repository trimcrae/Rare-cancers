## Abstract

## Purpose
Spouses often assume the responsibility of caring for their partners in advanced age, given their intimate relationships. However, less attention has been given to understanding their unique needs during caregiving. This study aims to explore the spouses’ experience of caring for a partner with frailty in older couples.

## Method
An exploratory qualitative study was conducted through focus group interviews. The interviews were transcribed verbatim and analysed using qualitative content analysis.

## Results
A total of 23 participants were interviewed in three focus groups. Their average age was 68.4 (SD 7.6) years, and the average duration of marriage or cohabitation was 43.2 (SD 13.4) years. Most of them were female (69.6%) and had a primary school education or lower (60.9%). Three categories were identified, namely, impacts of caregiving responsibilities, caregiving resources and coping strategies.

## Conclusion
This qualitative study reveals that the caregiving experiences perceived by spousal caregivers are influenced by the availability of caregiving resources and appropriateness of individual coping skills for counteracting the impacts brought by the caregiving responsibilities for partners with frailty. It points to the need for multidimensional interventions that offer both psychological and practical support to spousal caregivers.

## Introduction
Spouses often take on the primary caregiving role when their partners experience a health decline (AARP and National Alliance for Caregiving, ). Globally, evolving nuclear family structures and a decline in intergenerational co-residence have increased the reliance on spouses as the primary source of support in later life, given an ageing population (Ministry of Health ; National Bureau of Statistics, ; United Nations, ). Living with the care recipient frequently blurs the boundaries between caregiving and personal life, significantly expanding their responsibilities and engagement (Riekkola Carabante et al., ). Hence, compared with adult children, spouses often report greater responsibilities in the caregiving roles due to their intimacy and proximity (Dang et al., ).
Our systematic review found that current psychoeducation interventions fail to improve the caregiving burden, depressive symptoms, and anxiety faced by spousal caregivers of partners with frailty, and positive emotions were understudied (Zhang et al., ). The experiences of spousal caregivers for specific diseases, such as dementia, stroke or cancer, were explored (Hirayama et al., ; Macdonald et al., ; Zhu et al., ), but the findings may not apply to spousal caregiving for partners with frailty. The trajectory of frailty is distinct from these disease-specific conditions. Frailty is a multifactorial and progressive geriatric syndrome rather than a discrete diagnosis, characterised by cumulative declines across physical, psychological, and social domains (Hoogendijk et al., ). Its impacts are often less visible and less formally recognised, yet they necessitate prolonged, wide-ranging caregiving support that may fluctuate over time (Fernández-Carnero et al., ). This ambiguity can complicate spousal caregivers’ understanding of their role and limit access to condition-specific resources.
The complexity of this role is further compounded when both partners are of an advanced age. Being of a similar age to their partners, spousal caregivers also experience ageing-related health decline, leading to a tension between self-care and caregiving roles (Cai et al., ). Tasks such as assisting with personal care and activities of daily living are physically demanding and increase the risk of injury and accidents (Sabatini et al., ). Third, many spousal caregivers also need to juggle their caregiving role alongside caregiving responsibilities for other younger family members, such as adult children and grandchildren (Tolhurst et al., ).
Despite the challenges of spousal caregiving for partners with frailty, studies revealed that the caregiving experience is not entirely negative. While negative emotions, such as depressive symptoms and a sense of social isolation, were noted (Li et al., ; Zhao et al., ), positive experiences were also reported. Spousal caregivers may derive a sense of personal growth and self-worth from supporting their partners when they are in need (Autio et al., ; Chow et al., ). Many spouses feel a strong sense of obligation rooted in marital commitment and cultural norms, and thus prioritise their partner’s needs above their own well-being (Wang, ). The coexistence of positive and negative emotions in spousal caregiving highlights its multifaceted and paradoxical nature. An in-depth, contextualised understanding is therefore needed to inform the development of targeted and effective support strategies. This study aims to explore the experiences of taking care of partners who were living with frailty in the Chinese community.

2.

## Methods
2.1.

## Design
An exploratory qualitative design was employed to facilitate open-ended enquiry and inductive understanding of a phenomenon that remains under-explored (Tomaszewski et al., ). This approach was considered appropriate for examining how spousal caregivers make sense of their caregiving roles, challenges, and perceived needs in the context of frailty. The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist (Tong et al., ).
Face-to-face focus group interviews were conducted. This method was selected to capture shared meanings, collective norms, and socially constructed understandings of caregiving within a cultural context where family roles and marital obligations are often negotiated relationally (Guest et al., ). The group environment helps normalise sensitive experiences, such as familial conflict, allowing participants to disclose feelings they might otherwise perceive as stigmatising in an individual interview (Ning et al., ). Interaction among participants enabled comparison, validation, and elaboration of experiences, which facilitated richer discussion (Gill & Baillie, ).

2.2.

## Setting and participants
Data was collected from July to August 2024 at a community healthcare centre in Taizhou (China). This setting was selected as it functions as the primary hub for geriatric care within a catchment area of over 130,000 residents, offering the direct access to the target population. The centre provides integrated healthcare services specifically for individuals with frailty, including specialist outpatient care and rehabilitation therapy; it represents the interface for older adults managing this condition within the region.
Participants were recruited using a purposive sampling strategy to capture a spectrum of spousal caregiving experiences. To maximise variation in the sample, participants with diverse demographic dimensions in terms of age, sex, education level, economic status, the severity of the partner’s frailty and the duration of care provided, were included to reflect the heterogeneity of the study population. The inclusion criteria were: (1) living with their partner who is aged 60 years or older with frailty, which is defined as 4 points or above in the Groningen Frailty Indicator (GFI) (Huang et al., ). The GFI was selected for its established reliability and validity as a multidimensional instrument assessing physical, cognitive, social, and psychological domains in community-dwelling populations (Cronbach’s α = 0.87); (2) taking on the main responsibility for their partner’s daily needs (Yan, ). The responsibility included, but was not limited to, assistance with daily care tasks (e.g., bathing, dressing), care coordination and emotional support; and (3) fluent in Mandarin or a local dialect. Exclusion criteria were individuals who: (1) exhibit cognitive impairment, indicated by a score below 7 on the Abbreviated Mental Test (Hernandez et al., ); or (2) self-report a diagnosis of a mental health problem (e.g., schizophrenia, depression) that would hinder their ability to participate in the study.

2.3.

## Procedure
Staff of the community healthcare centre assisted in promoting the study to their clients. For individuals who expressed interest, the first author conducted an eligibility screening in person. During this process, the first author provided a detailed explanation of the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, and benefits. Participants were given an information sheet and the opportunity to ask questions before providing written informed consent. A schedule with several potential dates and times for the focus group interview was then offered, from which participants could select the session that best suited their availability.
Each focus group consisted of 6 to 12 participants, according to recommendations for group interviews (Khan & Abedin, ). The sessions were conducted in a private meeting room at the community healthcare centre to ensure confidentiality. A semi-structured format was adopted, guided by an interview guide (see Supplementary File) that included open-ended questions designed to explore the perceived impact of caregiving and the adaptation process. The first author, a PhD candidate with experience in qualitative research and geriatric nursing, moderated the sessions.
To ensure psychological safety, the moderator established ground rules at the commencement of each session, emphasising the necessity of confidentiality and mutual respect for differing opinions. To mitigate inhibition and conformity bias, the moderator employed active facilitation techniques (O. Nyumba et al., ). This involved inviting quieter participants to share their views and validating dissenting opinions to demonstrate that consensus was not required. Furthermore, the moderator managed group dynamics by gently redirecting dominant voices to ensure all participants had the opportunity to contribute.
Although the interview guide comprised pre-defined questions, the semi-structured design allowed the interviewer the flexibility to utilise follow-up prompts. These prompts were employed to seek clarification or encourage elaboration on participant responses, facilitating the exploration of nuances within the approved scope of the study without deviating from the original protocol (Knott et al., ). A research assistant helped with note-taking during the interviews because the field notes offer contextual information such as non-verbal communication (e.g., gestures, nodding, emotional tone) and group dynamics (Davies et al., ).
Data collection and analysis were conducted iteratively to allow for the continuous monitoring of saturation. Data saturation was operationalised as the point at which no new codes, categories, or conceptual insights emerged from successive focus group interviews, and when additional data did not prompt modification of the existing coding framework (Saunders et al., ). After each focus group, the research team conducted preliminary coding and analytic discussions to assess the emergence of new concepts. When the stability and adequacy of the existing coding structure are confirmed, it is deemed to have reached a state of saturation. Decisions regarding saturation were documented through team meeting notes and iterative revisions of the coding framework.

2.4.

## Data analysis
All focus group interviews were audio recorded with participants’ consent and transcribed verbatim to facilitate qualitative content analysis, a method that allows for the systematic coding and categorisation of large volumes of textual data to identify patterns and meaning (Kleinheksel et al., ). NVivo version 12 was used to facilitate data management. After each focus group interview, the first and second authors, both with qualitative research experience, read the interview transcripts and code meaningful units independently. They had regular meetings with the last author, who is an experienced qualitative researcher, to discuss the data collection and analysis process. This process allowed for the adjustment of the interview guide; for instance, new prompts were added to explore unexpected topics, such as conflicts with adult children over care decisions, that arose in interviews to facilitate in-depth understanding for the enquiry. The research team had meetings to review the coding scheme. Related codes were grouped into subcategories, which were further combined into broader categories. This dialogue supported the refinement of the coding structure, including the merging and renaming of codes, with the final categorisation system being reached through team consensus.

2.5.

## Trustworthiness
The trustworthiness of the qualitative study was ensured through credibility, reflexivity, transferability, dependability, confirmability transferability and authenticity (Johnson & Rasulova, ; Morse, ; Olmos-Vega et al., ).
Credibility was enhanced through several strategies. The transcripts were cross-checked against the audio recordings to ensure accuracy by the first author. Investigator triangulation was achieved through regular meetings among all authors to discuss emerging codes, review raw data, and collaboratively develop preliminary categories. This deliberation process can avoid presumption and bias from a single researcher’s perspective. Peer debriefing was conducted by presenting the preliminary analysis to researchers not involved in the study, who provided alternative interpretations and challenged analytic decisions.
To practice reflexivity, the first author, who conducted the focus group interviews and led the data analysis, carefully considered the influence of their own background throughout the research process. With prior training and experience in qualitative methods and gerontological research, the first author’s familiarity with ageing and caregiving issues facilitated rapport-building with participants. Nevertheless, this background had the potential to shape expectations and interpretations during data collection and analysis. Within the cultural context of the Chinese community, researchers are often perceived as authority figures, which can hinder participants from disclosing sensitive family matters. To mitigate this power imbalance, the first author deliberately adopted a learner’s stance during interviews, emphasising that participants were the experts on their own experience and that there were no right or wrong answers. To address potential bias, the first author maintained a reflexive journal documenting emotional responses, presumptions, and immediate impressions of the data. These reflexive notes served as a basis for peer debriefing during research team meetings, where initial interpretations were critically examined. This process helped ensure that the final themes were firmly grounded in participants’ narratives rather than the researcher’s preconceived assumptions or theoretical expectations.
Transferability was facilitated by providing rich and detailed descriptions of the research setting, participants’ demographic characteristics, and the socio-cultural context of spousal caregiving for partners living with frailty in the Chinese community, enabling readers to assess the applicability of the findings to other contexts.
Dependability and confirmability were supported through the maintenance of a comprehensive audit trail, including verbatim transcripts, field notes, coding records, reflexive journals, and documentation of analytic decisions. This transparent record allows the research process to be scrutinised and ensures that findings are grounded in the data.
Authenticity was ensured by presenting accounts of participants’ experiences, reflecting both shared patterns and divergent perspectives. The use of verbatim quotations throughout the findings anchors the analysis in participants’ own words and preserves the complexity of their own experiences.

2.6.

## Ethical considerations
The study was approved by the Survey and Behaviour Research Ethics Committee of The Chinese University of Hong Kong (reference number: SBRE-23-0126) and was authorised by the manager of the community healthcare centre. Written consent was obtained from each participant before data collection. The information sheet outlining the study’s nature and purpose was provided to each participant before the interview. Participants were informed that the interviews would be recorded, but anonymity would be maintained, and they could choose not to respond to any question or withdraw at any time without affecting any services they or their partners receive from the community healthcare centre. The first author guided the interviews with sensitivity and respect for confidentiality (Westland et al., ).

## Results
3.1.

## Participant description
A total of three focus group interviews were conducted with 23 spousal caregivers, ranging from 6 to 9 participants per group. The interviews ranged from 63 to 77 minutes, with a mean duration of 69 minutes. The average age of the participants was 68.4 years (SD = 7.6; range = 56–80). On average, participants had been married or cohabiting for 43.2 years (SD = 13.4; range = 1–60) and had provided personal care to their spouse for 6.1 years (SD = 3.3; range = 1–15). Most participants were women (69.6%) and had completed primary education or less (60.9%). Detailed demographic information is provided in .

 3.2.1.

## Impacts of caregiving responsibilities
From the participants’ sharing, caregiving for their spouses was described as relentless. The nature of caregiving is multifaceted, encompassing assistance with daily activities and medical care, and also providing emotional support and companionship. The high-demand and intensity of caregiving led to both physical and emotional exhaustion, as described below.
Physical exhaustion. Participants commonly shared that the caregiving responsibilities for their spouses led to severe and persistent fatigue. This exhaustion was often attributed to the cumulative strain of domestic and care-related duties, which they felt was almost entirely on their shoulders. These caregiving demands intensified when their spouses experienced health deterioration, requiring constant vigilance. As a participant shared,
‘You [need to] look after him at night, you could not sleep all night. [When] His mouth gets dry, you have to use a cotton swab to moisten his lips constantly.’ (FG3)
Many participants linked the decline in their own health, citing both the onset of new conditions and the worsening of existing ones, with their caregiving role. One participant described,
‘I myself have high blood lipids, high blood pressure, a tumour… [after caring for my spouse] my back hurts, my arms are sore… anyway, it’s just so tiring.’ (FG1)
Emotional exhaustion. In addition to physical exhaustion, participants also shared that they were emotionally overwhelmed. They reported a range of emotional strains, including worries about current caregiving demands and fears about the future, resulting in emotional exhaustion. This emotional burden was heightened by anxiety over their spouses’ deteriorating health and the fear of losing them. One participant shared,
‘At that time, he worried himself, afraid he wouldn’t make it back… I was worried sick!’ (FG3)
Feelings of loneliness further intensified the emotional strain, particularly when their spouses were hospitalised. The prolonged time of accompanying their spouses in the hospital often deprived caregivers of their usual social networks, leaving them feeling alienated and unsupported. As one participant described:
‘The feeling of being a stranger in a big city… just staying in the ward. There’s no one to talk to.’ (FG1)
Besides, participants noted that their emotional states were closely intertwined with those of their spouses. Participants described a loss of emotional independence, where their mood was affected by their spouses’ condition or temperament. As one participant explained,
‘When he’s in a bad mood, you’re in a bad mood too… he would not give you a pleasant look. He just made things very difficult for you.’ (FG1)

3.2.2.

## Caregiving resources
Participants identified different internal and external resources required for sustaining their caregiving roles. These resources are categorised into three types, namely relational resources, pragmatic resources and informational resources, as described below.
Relational resources. Many participants cited their deeply held beliefs about the marital relationship as a source of emotional strength. They viewed caregiving as an inherent moral responsibility of a spouse rooted in cultural values. These relational resources provided both emotional sustenance and a sense of purpose. The caregiving obligation was framed as an expression of love, reciprocity and lifelong companionship, but not a burden.
One participant shared,
‘It’s also a responsibility… You can’t be irresponsible, how could you call yourself a Chinese person then!’ (FG1)
Another participant shared that,
‘A couple, of course, you have to help each other. You can’t have complaints... As the old folks say, boyhood sweethearts become lifelong companions. Now that he’s old and like this, he needs companionship.’ (FG3)
For some, facing illness together deepened the marital bond, transforming hardship into a reaffirmation of intimacy. One participant noted that,
‘Only in critical moments, when you encounter special, major events, can you truly test the feelings between people… This relationship, on the contrary, feels like it has become closer and closer.’ (FG2)
Besides, the relationship with adult children was also regarded as an important source of emotional and moral support. Even when children were unable to provide direct care, participants expressed understanding and empathy toward their competing responsibilities rather than resentment. One participant told,
‘The children are all working, they have no time… They have their own families. Actually, they have tried their best, but it is only their best.’ (FG1)
Pragmatic resources. Many participants viewed practical help, for example, financial assistance and access to professional care, as an important resource. However, many encountered barriers in obtaining these resources. While some received financial support from their children, it was insufficient to cover the costs of formal professional care services.
Even among those without financial constraints, formal care was perceived as inaccessible or inadequate. Several participants expressed dissatisfaction with paid caregivers, not because of their technical skills, but due to a perceived lack of compassion and emotional engagement in their work. One participant shared that,
‘Even if you hire a domestic helper, no one is as sincere and dedicated [as a family member] in serving you… She’s just doing the job for money… She might feed my wife, but she doesn’t talk to her or comfort her. It feels… cold [apathy].’ (FG1)
Information resources. Many participants expressed a strong need for information to prepare them for the caregiving tasks. However, access to such resources was limited. While some were unaware of available training programmes in the community healthcare centre, others found it difficult to attend in-person training activities amidst their caregiving tasks. As one participant explained,
‘Looking at my phone is convenient. If you ask me to go to some community healthcare centres, how would I have the time?’ (FG1)

3.2.3.

## Coping strategies
In response to the caregiving challenges, participants adopted a range of coping strategies. These strategies can be categorised into proactive skill acquisition, active emotional regulation and passive coping.
Proactive skill acquisition. To counter feelings of helplessness and enhance their ability to manage daily caregiving tasks, many participants actively sought to acquire new skills and knowledge. This process helped them build caregiving competence and gain a sense of control. One participant explained that she learned through observation, having previously hired a paid caregiver as a way to learn practical skills:
‘I just wanted to learn. I observed how she did the washing and cleaning… I knew just by watching how she performed.’ (FG1)
Others relied on online resources to obtain information and clarify caregiving-related enquiries. One participant told,
‘We usually check on Baidu [a popular search engine in China]. For anything we don’t know, like what can be eaten and what cannot.’ (FG1)
Active emotional regulation. Apart from practical caregiving strategies, some participants developed ways to manage their emotional responses by reframing their perspectives. They acknowledged that while the caregiving situations might remain unchanged, their attitudes could influence how they coped. As one participant explained,
‘Worrying is useless! How can worrying help? It’s useless! You must treat it seriously, change your own perspective, change your own strategies.’ (FG1)
Others sought emotional support by confiding in their family members. Sharing their feelings openly served as a means of emotional release. One participant said that,
‘For me, I just make phone calls to family. Talk about what’s in my heart… and I feel a bit better.’ (FG1)
Passive emotional coping. Some participants, recognising the unchangeable nature of their circumstances, chose to endure the distress silently. However, the participants’ sharing suggested that prolonged endurance is sometimes associated with a sense of fatalism and a sense of powerlessness. One participant shared that,
‘Just accept fate... recognise the unchangeable nature. We are old, just live one day at a time.’ (FG3)

4.

## Discussion
This qualitative study offers an in-depth exploration of the experiences of spouses caring for their older partners with frailty. Our findings reveal that spousal caregivers are often physically and emotionally drained due to the prolonged and intensified demands of caregiving. However, these experiences can be shaped by the availability of caregiving resources within their context and also the coping strategies they employ. While spousal caregivers tried to sustain their caregiving responsibilities using various resources, not all were readily available, and the effects of coping strategies varied.
Consistent with prior research, participants described physical and emotional exhaustion associated with prolonged caregiving (Søvde et al., ). Frailty, as a dynamic and unpredictable syndrome, requires ongoing monitoring and adjustment rather than task-specific care, thereby intensifying caregivers’ vigilance and emotional labour (Guo et al., ). Unlike caregiving for defined disease trajectories, the gradual and ambiguous progression of frailty appeared to blur distinctions between normal ageing and illness, increasing uncertainty and cumulative strain.
Subtle differences in caregiving experiences between spousal caregivers and other family members were noted. Spousal caregivers framed their caregiving role as a moral and relational obligation rooted in marital commitment, reflecting Confucian-influenced values of loyalty, endurance, and family responsibility common in Chinese cultural contexts (Lu, ). This sense of duty functioned as both a sustaining force and a constraint. While it motivated spousal caregivers to persist despite adversity, it also limited their willingness to seek respite or prioritise self-care. This aligns with research on role identity in long-term partnerships, which suggests that strong spousal identification can intensify emotional interdependence and reduce perceived legitimacy of individual needs (Sels et al., ).
Structural and systemic factors further compounded caregiving challenges. Participants’ accounts of limited access to affordable, trustworthy, and flexible support services reflect broader gaps in China’s long-term care infrastructure. Although long-term care insurance (LTCI) schemes have expanded, their focus remains largely on individuals with high dependency or institutional care needs (Feng et al., ), leaving spousal caregivers of community-dwelling older adults with frailty insufficiently supported.
In response to a high-stress yet limited support situation, spousal caregivers employed a range of coping mechanisms that align with the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping (Lazarus & Folkman, ). Spousal caregivers’ efforts to acquire caregiving skills and seek information demonstrate problem-focused coping aimed at enhancing perceived control and competence. However, reliance on informal online sources also introduced risks related to misinformation and fraud. Emotion-focused coping strategies revealed variation. Active forms of emotional regulation, such as acceptance and cognitive reframing, appeared to support psychological adjustment, whereas passive strategies characterised by endurance or fatalism reflected diminished agency and potential vulnerability to burnout (Schell et al., ).
4.1.

## Implications for practice, education, and policy
Practice. The findings suggest that spousal caregivers’ challenges are dynamic and shaped by both available resources and coping capacities. In current Chinese primary care practice, assessment typically focuses on the older person, while caregivers’ needs remain largely informal and unrecognised. Routine caregiver assessment should therefore be integrated into primary care services, with attention to spousal caregivers’ physical strain (e.g., sleep disturbance), emotional well-being (e.g., stress), caregiving competence (e.g., recognising frailty-related deterioration), and coping patterns. Targeted interventions could include skills-based training focused on frailty care needs, such as fall prevention strategies and stress-management techniques. Facilitated access to respite and self-care resources is also essential, as spousal caregivers often remain deeply engaged in caregiving while deprioritising their own needs.
Education. Given participants’ reliance on online information-seeking, structured psychoeducation programmes are warranted to provide accessible, evidence-based guidance tailored to frailty care. Such programmes could address understanding frailty trajectories and uncertainty, recognising warning signs that require professional input, navigating community services, and legitimising spousal caregivers’ own self-care needs. Collaboration between community health centres and trusted digital health platforms may reduce exposure to misinformation while improving reach and acceptability among older spousal caregivers.
Policy. Current LTCI schemes largely prioritise individuals with severe disability (low Barthel Index scores), whereas older adults with mild-to-moderate frailty often fall outside eligibility thresholds. Policy expansion could therefore include: (1) coverage for home-based supportive services for community-dwelling older adults with frailty; (2) accessible and affordable formal respite care options for family caregivers; and (3) reimbursement for caregiver education and training.

4.2.

## Limitations and recommendations for future research
This study has limitations. First, participants were recruited from a single community healthcare centre, which may limit generalisability to spousal caregivers in other regions or service contexts. Second, voluntary participation may also have resulted in the overrepresentation of spousal caregivers who were more willing or able to articulate their experiences. Third, while we explored the caregiving dynamic, data were obtained only from the spousal caregiver’s perspective.
Future multi-site studies across diverse urban and rural settings are needed to examine how caregiving experiences and access to resources vary under different service infrastructures and policy contexts. While incorporating the voice of the care-receiver would enrich understanding of the dyadic experience, expecting mutual engagement in research may be unrealistic given the cognitive and physical limitations associated with frailty. Therefore, future interventions should prioritise caregiver-focused support systems that acknowledge this asymmetry in the caregiving relationship. A co-design approach with spousal caregivers should be adopted when developing psychoeducation interventions to ensure their experiences, practical needs, and values are meaningfully integrated into programme design.

5.

## Conclusion
This study explores the experiences of spouses caring for older partners with frailty through a qualitative focus group design. The analysis revealed that whilst spousal caregivers are sustained by a profound sense of marital duty and cultural responsibility, this intrinsic motivation is continually tested by intense physical and emotional demands. Furthermore, the findings demonstrated that external support systems are inaccessible or poorly aligned with the specific needs of spouses. Future research and practice must prioritise the development of flexible, reliable, and empathetic support services that acknowledge these challenges.
