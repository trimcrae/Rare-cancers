## Abstract

## Background
Although often used to inform clinical trials and public health campaigns, academic–community partnerships have been less frequently used in the context of large, national epidemiologic studies. These partnerships could enhance key aspects of the research process if appropriately leveraged. We aimed to elicit perspectives toward the formation of a participant advisory board in the REGARDS (Reasons for Geographic and Racial Differences in Stroke) study, a national prospective cohort study that enrolled >30 000 Black and White community‐dwelling adults aged ≥45 years from 48 states from 2003 to 2007 with ongoing follow‐up.

## Methods
For this qualitative study, we used a convenience sampling approach to recruit participants. We conducted semistructured virtual interviews between May 2023 and July 2023. The Cargo and Mercer framework on academic–community partnerships in health equity research informed the interview guide and thematic analysis.

## Results
The 18 participants had a median age of 69 years, 67% were women, 39% were Black, and 78% had a college degree or higher. Four key themes emerged: (1) trust, diversity, mutual respect, and accountability; (2) sharing the purpose and scope of research and dissemination of science; (3) engagement, formalization, and maintenance; (4) social and environmental justice and translating knowledge into action.

## Conclusions
Participants in this large epidemiologic cohort were interested in participating in a participant advisory board and saw its benefits. Our findings can inform the formation of the REGARDS study participant advisory board, including how it should be structured and guided. This has implications for other observational cohorts interested in including participants in an advisory capacity.

## Research Perspective

## What Is New?

This study raises the question of how academic–community partnerships can be effectively implemented in large national epidemiologic studies to enhance participant engagement, trust, and diversity. It also highlights the role of social and environmental justice in shaping these partnerships and promoting meaningful contributions from diverse groups.
A key question emerging from this research is how to structure and sustain participant advisory boards to ensure their long‐term engagement and impact in large, long‐term cohort studies such as REGARDS (Reasons for Geographic and Racial Differences in Stroke).

## What Question Should Be Addressed Next?

Future research should focus on best practices for integrating participant advisory boards into study design, ensuring effective dissemination of findings and translating knowledge into action to improve inclusivity, accountability, and public health outcomes.

Over the past decade, there has been an increased appreciation and enthusiasm for academic–community partnerships as a critical component of impactful efforts to advance health equity. These partnerships aim to bridge what has been a historically large divide between academic institutions conducting research, community members, and research participants. , Formal academic–community partnerships have the potential to offer a venue for perspectives from diverse communities to inform and shape the design and conduct of scientific studies, ultimately enhancing direct benefits for both parties (academics and community members/study participants). , 

Although frequently used in public health initiatives and clinical trials, and more recently encouraged by funding agencies, , academic–community partnerships have been less frequently seen in the context of large, epidemiologic studies. For example, the All of Us Research Study, a national cohort study, conducts community outreach using existing local community advisory boards to recruit participants and disseminate findings. Other cohort studies have seldom established a formal process to engage community members as collaborators and co‐owners of the research. Lack of community engagement has multiple disadvantages. First, scientists may lack the perspectives of the people they are aiming to understand, as well as the best ways to translate their findings into actionable, high‐impact results. Second, without opportunities to contribute their perspectives, participants and community members may not feel fully empowered or connected to the study or its findings, limiting robust adoption of new clinical strategies. Notably, the JHS (Jackson Heart Study), a large, single‐site, community‐based epidemiologic investigation of environmental and genetic factors associated with cardiovascular disease among Black people, transformed their community‐focused unit, which was established in 1999, into a community engagement center between 2013 and 2018 to address this issue; the goal of the center is to ensure that data are put into action for participants, key stakeholders, and policy makers in Mississippi. 

To improve the rigor and relevance of research findings, the REGARDS (Reasons for Geographic and Racial Differences in Stroke) study, an ongoing, prospective, population‐based cohort of Black and White adults in the United States, intends to establish a participant advisory board (PAB). Our study was informed by the Cargo and Mercer conceptual framework, which has been used to guide academic–community partnerships that pertain to health equity research. This framework provides a model for developing, maintaining, and evaluating a PAB for intermediate and long‐term outcomes. Guided by the principles of community engagement, a PAB can provide REGARDS with an infrastructure and vehicle to formalize partnerships between participants and researchers. Specifically, the PAB can engage participants in key aspects of the research process and work to ensure that results are disseminated widely and impact participants, communities, and policymakers.
To inform the formation of a PAB for the REGARDS study, we conducted a qualitative study with current active REGARDS participants. The overall goal was to elicit their perspectives toward the concept of forming a PAB, including its mission and the proposed activities in which members would contribute, and to elicit feedback on how to structure the PAB to best meet their preferences and the overall goals of REGARDS.

## METHODS

## Data Availability Statement
The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

## Guiding Conceptual Framework
This study was informed by the Cargo and Mercer conceptual framework, which has been used to guide academic–community partnerships that pertain to health equity research. This framework provides a model for developing, maintaining, and evaluating a PAB for intermediate and long‐term outcomes. The framework guides 5 main questions that researchers and their partners should consider when developing and maintaining a PAB: (1) What are the values or drivers behind the research? (2) Who should participate in the research and how? (3) How are partnerships initiated, and how do they evolve? (4) What are core elements of community‐partnered work? (5) What is the added value of community‐partnered work in each of the research phases? These questions are centered along the framework's (1) core elements, (2) partnership process domains, and (3) core values. This framework informed the interview topic guide and the data analysis.

## Study Setting, Design, and Participants
The REGARDS study is a population‐based cohort of 30 239 non‐Hispanic Black and White adults aged ≥45 years from the 48 contiguous US states and the District of Columbia who were enrolled between 2003 and 2007 with ongoing follow‐up. Black Americans and individuals living in the southeastern United States were oversampled, 2 groups with higher stroke mortality than their comparison groups, because the study was designed to elucidate reasons for racial and geographic differences in stroke mortality. Race was self‐reported, and only English‐speaking non‐Hispanic Black and White individuals were eligible. There were minimal inclusion/exclusion criteria. The study details have been published elsewhere. Briefly, baseline data collection included a computer‐assisted telephone interview on sociodemographics, medical history, and health status. An in‐home examination by trained staff followed standardized, quality‐controlled protocols to collect fasting blood and urine samples, ECGs, blood pressure, anthropometric measures, and data on medications via pill bottle review. Living participants or their next of kin are telephoned every 6 months with retrieval of medical records for reported hospitalizations. For these analyses, the age variable is based on age at interview. Race, sex, income, education, and region variables are based on the participant's baseline visit. Race included non‐Hispanic White and non‐Hispanic Black participants with female and male sex. Income categories were <$20 000, $20 000 to $34 000, $35 000 to $74 000, ≥$75 000, and refused. Education included high school graduates, some college, and college graduates and above. The region variable includes the stroke belt (Alabama, Arkansas, Georgia, Louisiana, Mississippi, North Carolina, South Carolina, and Tennessee), buckle (the coastal plain of Georgia, North Carolina, and South Carolina), and nonbelt regions (other regions in the continental United States), which are regions with excess stroke mortality. Additionally, the buckle region is the area of the coastal Piedmont region that has the highest rates of stroke mortality in the country, with one‐third of the participants recruited from this region. The REGARDS study has been approved by the institutional review boards of the participating centers, and all participants provided written informed consent.
This qualitative study was conducted virtually between May 2023 through July 2023. Because this qualitative study's overall goal was to inform the formation and structure of a PAB, we recruited a sample of REGARDS participants interested in potentially joining a PAB and who represented the overall study sample. To complete the sample, we initially used a convenience sampling approach that involved advertising the concept of PAB formation to each active REGARDS member via the regular newsletter. Participants who expressed interest after reading the newsletter advertisement contacted REGARDS leadership, who then passed that information to the study team. Participants who expressed interest were then contacted about the study; a study team member (M.S.B.) explained the study, shared reasons for doing this research, and participants who remained interested provided verbal consent. There were 2 participants who refused secondary to health‐related condition and transitioning to hospice care. Based on initial responses, we then applied a stratified sampling approach to ensure that those who agreed to participate were diverse with respect to sex (men versus women) and race (White versus Black).
In collaboration with the REGARDS parent study team, 1 investigator (M.S.B.) contacted participants by phone using a standardized script to assess interest and eligibility. To be eligible, participants had to be able to participate in a 1‐time 30‐ to 60‐minute telephone or Zoom‐based interview. Participants provided verbal informed consent, including permission to record the interview and to publish deidentified excerpts from the interview. No interviews were repeated, and notes were completed after each interview. All participants received a $25 gift card for their time. The study was approved by the University of Alabama at Birmingham Institutional Review Board.
This study was conducted in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research reporting guidelines (File  ).

## Data Collection and Interview Guide
Interviews were conducted by a lead researcher trained in qualitative research methods (M.S.B.). The interview process was overseen by 2 female investigators with expertise in community‐based qualitative research (M.R.S. and D.H.). The interviewer and other lead researchers, along with a senior researcher, were debriefed after every 5 interviews. Interviews were conducted virtually using Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act‐compliant Zoom call software, which allows participants to dial in via telephone or the internet. This strategy has been feasible during the COVID‐19 pandemic and in a geographically diverse sample. 

A semistructured topic guide was used for the interviews. The topic guide was informed by the Cargo and Mercer conceptual framework described above, prior research conducted by members of our team, informal discussions with REGARDS study leadership, and prior studies on academic–community partnership formation. Interview questions broadly focused on participant's perspectives toward: (1) forming a PAB; (2) the PAB mission; (3) the proposed activities for PAB members, which include: (1) providing feedback on selected (targeted) REGARDS study proposals; (2) interpreting study results in the context of their lives; (3) helping to translate results for lay audiences, stakeholders, and policy makers; (4) disseminating findings broadly; and (5) providing input as to their priorities for health‐related research. Some examples of the questions include: (1) Can you tell us about your experience as a study member of REGARDS? (2) What do you think about this mission and these goals? (3) What do you think of the proposed activities? (4) How much time would you be willing to spend as a member? (5) From your perspective, what is the best way to know if the board is successful? How would you define that? Each interview was recorded and professionally transcribed.

## Statistical Analysis
Participant characteristics were analyzed quantitatively with descriptive statistics. Data from each interview were managed using NVivo software, including organizing transcripts and conducting the coding process. Transcripts were not returned to participants for correction, and participants did not provide feedback on the current findings. We followed a thematic analysis approach to iteratively develop a codebook that included framework‐driven, a priori codes (Cargo and Mercer) and emergent, inductive codes when new concepts emerged, because this approach has been used in other community‐based participatory research in measuring success of collaborations. , , Two researchers (M.S.B. and D.H.) independently reviewed the first 3 transcripts and created codes guided by the framework. They met to reconcile the codes with disagreements resolved through peer debriefing, guided by a third researcher (M.R.S). The codebook was then reapplied to the initial and subsequent transcripts, pausing at every fifth transcript to review, reconcile, and consolidate codes as a team. Through discussion, codes were then categorized into themes and subthemes (categories). The broader investigative team with expertise in the REGARDS study; community engaged research; health equity; and cardiovascular, epidemiology, and stroke health reviewed and refined themes further. Interviews continued until saturation was reached, defined as the point at which no new themes emerged. Finally, we compared our themes to the Cargo and Mercer framework and looked for similarities and differences.

## RESULTS

## Participant Characteristics
A total of 18 REGARDS participants were interviewed. More than half identified as non‐Hispanic White (61%), and two‐thirds were women (67%). Participants' ages ranged from 65 to 72 years, with a median age of 69 years. The majority reported having an annual household income of ≥$75 000, holding a college degree or higher, and living in the non‐stroke belt regions of the United States. Participants resided in California, Colorado, Connecticut, Georgia, Illinois, Louisiana, Mississippi, North Carolina, Pennsylvania, South Carolina, and Tennessee. ## Theme 1

## 
PAB Should Be Formed and Function With a Core Set of Principles
Participants endorsed that 4 principles are central to the PAB's formation and functioning in the REGARDS Study: (1) diversity, (2) mutual respect, (3) accountability, and (4) trust.
Participants expressed the importance of diversity related to race, sex, and age in forming a PAB and in the overall relationship between academics (research scientists) and nonacademics (research participants or community members). Many explained that a diverse PAB, alongside study questions that include a diverse sample of participants, was critical to generate meaningful studies and for disseminating findings to the public. One participant remarked,
“Well, if you've got literature that is going to be made public, I'd like to see it ‘cause I wanna see some diversity in there’.”
Another participant explained that diversity and inclusion needed to be reflected across the PAB membership,
“But I do think some serious effort at inclusion, is really, really important, and it has to be visible. When you get on the screen here, I don't want to see a bunch of skinny old white guys.”
Although this comment is directed toward gender and race, other participants reflected on regional and age diversity.
In addition to diversity, the importance of mutual respect between PAB members and researchers was highlighted as a key factor for building relationships grounded in mutual respect and appreciation for each other's unique contributions. For example, 1 participant explained,
“… you have to treat each person with the respect they deserve…and their method and style.”
Participants expressed that with mutual respect, more could be accomplished. One commented,
“… if we know what the researchers are doing and they share that information with us and then we can give them ideas, I believe…and help them to…get the information they want to get to the people better….”
The principle of accountability emerged as important as well, particularly the concept that PAB members and researchers are together with the responsibility for ensuring that the purpose of the PAB and the established goals are being upheld.
“… you would need to have some sort of feedback, then, from the participants to see whether it's successful. And you might even have some external goals where it's like things about some measures of how well you communicate or that kind of stuff.”
Finally, trust in science, as well as the products the PAB is receiving and disseminating, were important because of the benefits it can have during encounters with health care systems. One participant stated,
“I trust science in a lot of areas…the doctors, they do a good job of treating patients. They do the best they can, and it takes science to do that. I benefited from science in many areas.”
Another participant said,
“…I [want to] trust information that is presented that I can read and study.”
Overall, trust was described as a balance between status, privilege, and cultural humility between academic and nonacademic partners.

## Theme 2

## 
PAB Can Inform and Disseminate Research
Participants revealed that sharing the purpose of research and disseminating research findings were 2 principles integral to community‐partnered research.
Participants spoke about the importance of being well‐informed about the PAB and REGARDS study questions. Additionally, they believed it was important that they be notified when the scope of research changes or when new research questions andancillary studies begin and end. They felt this would allow members to better inform their communities. One participant stated,
“It's important that we be kept up to date… so that if we have a family member or friend who has a particular illness, maybe we can say, ‘…there is a group that's workin’ on research for that particular illness.…”
Participants expressed areas of research they would like to have more understanding of including conditions that affected their friends and families as well as chronic conditions related to aging. For example, a participant shared,
“From what I've read, the research is more on what groups are more inclined to get strokes.… I would like to see information and research in that direction as to how to educate people, encourage healthy eating, up the exercise.”
These statements supported the need for clear and bidirectional communication between researchers and PAB members on study goals and status. One participant highlighted their own desire to stay abreast of research and how this could be mobilized to better serve the community. They stated,
“…information is so rapidly changing all the time…we just have to keep doing the best we can to keep up with it and to share as much as we can, you know, with everyone…so that we all can benefit.”

## Theme 3

## Formal Process Is Needed to Form and Maintain the PAB

Participants shared that engagement, formalization, and maintenance are integral to the formal process of establishing a PAB in an academic setting.
In terms of engagement, participants discussed the frequency and method in which they would like to be engaged in the PAB. They were open to a mix of in‐person and virtual meetings. One of the participants responded,
“I do think that 1 in‐person meeting is helpful.…in a Zoom meeting, I get only 1 view of you. In a personal meeting, I get different body language….”
With respect to frequency of meetings, 1 participant noted,
“…I don't mind scheduling this [Zoom] meeting and dedicating the time for it. Um, I'm not sure I would want to spend this much time on a weekly basis.”
Another participant, however, shared concerns about virtual communication, particularly with those who do not have access to the internet or sufficient health literacy. They stated,
“…The computer I'm on was given to me by the County of Los Angeles to expose me to iPads. I would have never purchased an iPad. Economically or whatever. I used to go to the library.”
Additional statements from participants highlighted the importance of establishing formal processes early. These involved structuring the PAB, as well as reviewing terms for compensation and feedback. For example, a participant explained,
“I think you have to think through the financial incentives. Is that the only reason [to do this], and that's probably not a very good incentive, at our age.”
Overall, participants believed that the formalization of PAB should involve a strategic mission, with an organizational structure that monitors productivity to maximize the collaborative efforts of the academic and nonacademic partners.
In regard to the maintenance of a PAB, participants expressed the need for continuous feedback and flexibility to foster continuous check‐ins and frequent discussions on areas of improvement between the board and researchers. A participant stated,
“Yeah, so, but that's up to y'all if you have good feedback loops, you'll figure that out after the first couple of meetings.”
Another participant revealed,
“…your board should be flexible enough to recognize, are we missing points, hitting points, getting, gathering enough information. I wanna be able to change when the people change. Because we're into a whole new world….”

## Theme 4

## Certain Key Values Drive the PAB and Its Activities
Participants endorsed 3 key values that they felt would drive the PAB and would inspire participants to be involved. These included: self‐motivation, social and environmental justice, and translating knowledge into action.
Participants reported that motivation, specifically underlying self‐motivation or the why behind someone getting involved, would be a key driver to form and maintain a PAB. A participant shared,
“I mean I'm doing this interview today because I think I can contribute some of my experiences. I would like to see it continue.”
For others, different factors motivated them, including finding common ground with others, socializing, and a desire to be involved in research and specifically the research processes. One participant shared,
“Um, well I like to interact with people…they may not be at all like me. But, you know, we found a common interest that, you know, ‘Hey, we're both interested in, you know, strokes, and how to avoid (that)’.…”
Another key value that arose was social and environmental justice. Particularly the notion that health care in the United States needs to improve for all, and that a PAB could make a difference. One participant reflected on the US health care system compared with others and shared,
“…their health care, in some ways, is better than ours, because it's better distributed, you know. The poor, this country, a lot of poor people don't have any access….”
Another participant stated,
“The disparity is there, and that our community dies at a disproportionate rate to others that are non‐African American…and so what I trust is my own desire to make certain that I'm not being overlooked.”
These participants expressed how equitable access to quality care is important for all and should be a goal of research in hopes of improving people's health status.
Last, participants supported the need to translate knowledge into action to reduce the impact of health disparities, specifically related to the current REGARDS study in addressing prevention of stroke. One participant shared,
“I was recently put on blood pressure medication, and I don't, I think I might've not bothered with that except that the whole point of [REGARDS] it is to prevent stroke….”
Echoing these same sentiments, another participant stated,
“And I think that a part of that is the data can help to, you know, influence policy….”
In the partnership process, participants spoke about how the PAB can support investigators by improving awareness and providing information about public service programs or other services available to local communities. In doing so, they reflected that the PAB and investigators would have a chance to positively impact health disparities across the United States.

## DISCUSSION
In this qualitative study with current REGARDS participants, we found that there was an eagerness to form a PAB, and it should be formed and function with a core set of principles and key values. Forming and maintaining it requires a formal process, which should be agreed upon by researchers and participants together, but once formed, that PAB can serve to both inform and help disseminate research. Notably, participants emphasized that the PAB could benefit not just the members of the PAB, but the people and communities it represents. Although our findings suggest that participants would welcome a PAB now, they can also inform other epidemiological or observational cohorts to implement a PAB from the start, allowing researchers to periodically evaluate its functioning using validated scales like the Research Engagement Survey Tool. 

The Cargo and Mercer conceptual framework, widely used in guiding academic–community partnered advisory boards, , , , serves as a robust foundation for our study. However, it is noteworthy that our findings diverged in some key areas. For example, REGARDS participants emphasized diversity, both in terms of the PAB's composition but also in the topics that researchers might want to pursue for study. This is not surprising, given its biracial composition, but diversity is also important and should be a key factor that is considered for PAB formation. Additionally, participants in our study emphasized other key values to guide the PAB, including self‐motivation, a commitment to social and environmental justice, and the importance of translating knowledge into action. These concepts were unique to our study, and as such resulted in a modified framework to guide the PAB in REGARDS.
Additionally, participants highlighted the complex nature of compensating advisory board members. Although financial incentives are often a primary consideration for recruitment and retention, the participants' reflections suggest that, at older ages, such incentives may hold less appeal. Existing literature supports this perspective, indicating that financial rewards are not always the most effective motivator for advisory board members. For example, research on advisory boards shows that members often value intrinsic rewards such as professional recognition, opportunities for personal growth, and the ability to influence strategic decisions over monetary compensation. Additionally, studies indicate that advisory board members may prioritize nonfinancial incentives, such as flexible time commitments, the chance to mentor others, or engage in meaningful projects that align with their values. , These findings suggest that although financial compensation can be an important factor, it is essential for the REGARDS staff to also consider nonmonetary motivations when recruiting advisory board members, particularly as priorities evolve.
Other large cohorts, such as the All of Us Research Study, use established PABs across the nation to elicit community feedback on their research initiatives, as well as assist with participant recruitment and dissemination efforts. Incorporating patient and consumer engagement is crucial for optimizing knowledge mobilization, because it ensures that the dissemination process is more relevant, accessible, and impactful. Recent studies have demonstrated that active involvement of patients in knowledge translation efforts can enhance the uptake and application of research findings, leading to better health outcomes and more informed decision‐making. , , Other epidemiologic cohorts have community advisory boards that maintain newsletters to disseminate findings, but this activity alone seems to be inadequate based on our participants' feedback. To our knowledge, only a few other epidemiological cohorts have established their own PABs that appear to have similar functions as the one suggested by our findings. For example, the JHS transformed its community mobilization office into the CHAN (Community Health Advisory Network) using the collaborative community science model and the collaborative citizen science approach to guide its development and activities. , , Within the JHS CHAN, participants and community members have the opportunity to serve as both a coinvestigator throughout the research process and as cardiovascular health educators to their peers and neighbors. Although similar lessons from the JHS CHAN can be applied to our cohort, it is important to note that the JHS is limited to the Jackson area within the state of Mississippi, whereas REGARDS draws its participants from 48 states. This could pose a challenge to establishing collaborations and relationship building among REGARDS participants, especially among those residing in different geographical regions across the United States.
Another unique aspect of the REGARDS cohort that may impact the PAB is how participants were recruited and the duration of their participation. Approximately 20 years ago, the REGARDS study recruited >30 000 individuals via mail and telephone to participate in their longitudinal study, rather than in‐person recruitment. Since initial recruitment, approximately one‐third of the REGARDS cohort has died. However, study staff have been able to maintain contact with over half the remaining population via telephone, demonstrating a high level of engagement over time. We were surprised by the degree to which people had similar beliefs that could be related to similarity in demographics or their high level of engagement/long‐term participation in the REGARDS study. Additionally, this is a limitation of the design of the study with those who agreed to participate and were included to speak about engagement and thus may be a biased sample. This may serve the cohort well in terms of participation in the PAB and the ability to achieve its goals. However, it is important to consider how cohort differences (ie, working adults) would impact engagement in the PAB. Finally, it should be noted, however, that many participants are now older adults, and therefore additional thought as to how to engage participants who require caregiver assistance (or their caregivers themselves) will be needed.

## Implications
The establishment of a PAB from a longitudinal epidemiologic cohort holds significant promise for the future of research, particularly in fostering robust nonacademic and academic partnerships. , , The collaborative efforts between researchers and PAB members can lead to more relevant and impactful research outcomes. Also, the PAB can serve as a critical link to the community, ensuring that research findings are disseminated in an accessible and beneficial manner to all stakeholders, including policymakers. This inclusive approach not only enhances the quality of research but also strengthens the overall impact on public health and well‐being.

## Strengths and Limitations
The study has key strengths including the diversity of the cohort by race, sex, education, and region, and our use of rigorous qualitative research methods and frameworks. However, it is important to acknowledge certain limitations. The sample was drawn from the remaining REGARDS study population who were identified using a convenience sampling methodology; therefore, it may not represent the current cohort or a broader population of interest. Although we attempted purposive sampling, those who ultimately agreed to participate were self‐selected due to their interest in the concept of an advisory committee. As such, we cannot infer that our findings apply to those who did not participate in this qualitative study. Additionally, the REGARDS study inclusion criteria required that individuals were English‐speaking non‐Hispanic Black and White participants; therefore, only non‐Hispanic Black and White adults were included in this study, which impacts the generalizability of our findings. Second, our questions were generated from an existing framework that may have limited the scope of the topics we were able to assess. However, we built and expanded on the existing framework using insights from our study participants.

## CONCLUSIONS
The data collected from this qualitative study can directly inform the formation of the PAB in the REGARDS study, including the activities in which it would focus and the ways in which it would be formalized, function, governed, and potentially evaluated. This user‐driven, participant‐informed approach helps to ensure that the PAB is designed to directly meet the needs of study participants and results in products that can have direct and powerful influences on their health and that of their communities. Finally, because forming a PAB has rarely been done for national epidemiological cohort studies, formally investigating participants' attitudes toward shaping it is critical and will advance the science of the field, serving as a model for other observation cohorts interested in doing this in the future.
