## Abstract
In China, the escalating repression of gender and sexual diversity has given rise to ‘queer necropolitics’. ‘Queer necropolitics’ – a concept we build on – refers to the hegemonic ability to determine who is allowed to suffer, survive, or thrive. We examine China’s sustained re-enforcement of traditional gender norms, which has rendered queer communities non-existent from official recognition. In this context, we aim to understand how China’s state queer necropolitics has shaped queer organising in the country. From 2021 to 2022, we conducted 26 semi-structured interviews with a diverse cohort of self-identified LGBTIQ+ members and activists across 12 provinces in China. We find that the interplay of legal invisibility and politicised, moralised visibility has placed queer communities under hegemonic governance techniques of suppression. The paper argues that China’s queer necropolitics has established invisible activism. The juxtaposition of invisibility with activism denotes the quiet, implicit, and everyday embodied acts of communal care among queer communities as a form of resistance. Building on the metaphor of ‘glowing fireflies’ shared by a research participant, invisible activism presents a more sustainable approach than conventional rights-based activism. The paper expands our understanding of politically antagonistic practices, acknowledging the diversity and complexity embedded within queer activism.
Keywords: China, criminalisation, gender governance, invisible activism, LGBTIQ+, queer necropolitics, re-criminalisation, surveillance

## Introduction
In China, queer activism has long navigated the grey zone of (in)visibility, complicated by moral panics and political ideology. The trend in LGBTIQ+
 
 control and suppression has re-emerged with force under the Xi Jinping administration. National initiatives that include laws and regulations indirectly constraining LGBTIQ + activism ( ), along with repressive measures such as online censorship, crackdowns on LGBTIQ + organisations and the detention of protestors ( ), have now rendered queer communities simultaneously invisible and covertly restricted. Faced with state-sponsored pushes for traditional gender roles and surveillance of queer communities, LGBTIQ+ groups are being forced to shut down, their activities systematically outlawed. These strategies have had a big impact on queer organising, monitored and recorded by scholars in the field (see, e.g. ; ; ). This article contributes to this growing body of scholarship by focussing on China’s hegemonic practices in governing queer populations under the Xi Jinping administration, providing a China-focused perspective on queer necropolitics.
China’s queer necropolitics has emerged from its networks of control that determine which bodies and populations are allowed to survive or thrive. Extended from Foucauldian biopolitics, the notion of necropolitics encompasses the state power dynamics to decide who lives and whose existence is ‘illegitimate’ or undesired. pin down the concept of necropolitics to queer life and examine the interplay of gender, sexuality, violence, and precarity. They interrogate that the incorporation of gender and sexual diversities in the hegemonic apparatuses – in the contemporary regimes marked by racism, neo/colonialism, the war on terror, incarceration, border of enforcement, and neoliberalism – has in fact accelerated premature death for individuals deemed unassimilable within liberal regimes of rights and representation, rendering them disposable. further argues that liberal queer politics essentially projects a form of white (heteronormative) futurity rendering sexual and racial minorities invisible or ‘socially dead’. We follow the proposition of queer necropolitics with a focus on authoritarian China where queer communities are legally invisible, morally scrutinised, and socio-politically harmed under the hegemonic governance techniques. This is our theoretical point of departure. In the context of China’s queer activism and adopting the theoretical concept of queer necropolitics, the article asks: How have China’s hegemonic governance techniques shaped queer organising in the country?
The literature review will contextualise queer organising in China, including their previous and current forms of existence, activism, and the recent suppressive actions of the state. The analysis uncovers three main findings. First, queer communities in China exist in a state of legal invisibility but face significant politicisation and moral scrutiny due to binary gender norms and heteronormativity. Second, an arbitrary space of re-criminalisation has emerged from the gap between legal invisibility and moral visibility, characterised by targeted surveillance and suppression of queer advocacy, further constituting ‘queer necropolitics’ with ‘Chinese characteristics’. In China, queer necropolitics manifests through the absence of legal protection and the discursive unspoken-ness surrounding queer communities. This lack of recognition creates significant precarity and uncertainty in queer lives, which are further exploited by law enforcement bodies at their explanations to systematically and arbitrarily jeopardise Chinese queer organising. Third, the paper delineates how, in response to China’s queer necropolitics, queer communities engage in ‘invisible activism’. This form of micro activism, represented by the metaphor of ‘glowing fireflies’, underscores the significance of individual existence and survival in fostering solidarity and communal preservation amidst the challenges posed by queer necropolitics.
This paper broadens the notion of ‘queer necropolitics’ by examining the legal frameworks and official discourses that address and neglect Chinese queer communities. While the established literature on queer necropolitics has focused on what has been stated and advocated in policy, we probe into how legal invisibility of the community has engendered precarity and uncertainty of queer life. It is important to recognise the omissions in laws and how legal invisibility can be exploited by law enforcement bodies to arbitrarily and systematically target, suppress, and even recriminalize queer organising and political minorities. Based on that, the paper introduces the term ‘invisible activism’ – a strategy used in queer organising to address uncertain state repercussions. The apparent oxymoron of the term emphasises the nuanced nature of activism that operates within spaces of everyday life, inviting exploration into the complexities of visibility and invisibility in political activism. By mobilising the concepts of ‘queer necropolitics’ and ‘invisible activism’, this paper deepens the understanding of queer policies and communities in China. We stress the importance of avoiding oversimplified conceptualisations of the Chinese state’s oppressive approach, acknowledging the complexities beyond binary notions of repression versus visibility, or oppression versus rights. The conceptual establishments can be relevant for understanding queer communities in the Global South, where despite tensions between visibility and invisibility, queer life endures.

## Queer organising in China
The issue of China’s LGBTIQ+ organising has received increasing academic attention. In the 1990s, the United Nations’ 1995 Fourth World Conference on Women in Beijing sparked a wave of feminist and queer organising. Since then, new-found organisations, many of which relied on foreign funding, prioritised politically driven programs over community-centred ones ( ), a priority that we will show to be no longer viable in China. Since then, LGBTIQ+ organising has grown and adapted to changes in political climates, ranging from the thawing conditions of Hu Jintao’s (2003–2013) leadership, which allowed a spectrum of community-based organising to grow – from hobby-based groups to social awareness activism and even rights-based advocacy groups – to the current re-freeze of civil society space in Xi Jinping’s China.
Less influenced by Christian morality that has historically bolstered homophobic sentiments, Chinese queer culture is uniquely complicated by the long queer history in the ancient Han-Chinese culture, China’s approach to Westernisation and modernisation since the late Qing dynasty, and the political directives of the Communist Party of China (hereafter CPC). During the Cultural Revolution (1969–1979), the Communist Party of China (the CPC) targeted homosexuality as a ‘non-socialist element’ ( ), denying China’s longstanding history of queerness or non-heteronormative practices ( ). Concurrently, the re-translation of classical Chinese literature during the time introduced gendered pronouns, previously absent ( : 36). Queerness has become a subject of moral apprehension and political ideology ( ).
The criminalisation of homosexuality occurred under Deng Xiaoping’s governance being classified under the designation of ‘hooliganism’ (liumang zui) ( ). In 1997, the crime of hooliganism was abolished, and a legal provision not to prosecute any crimes that were expressly designated as such implied the secondary meaning of decriminalising homosexuality ( ). While implied decriminalisation marked progress, it did not lead to the legal acknowledgement or safeguarding of LGBTIQ+ communities and their relationships, nor does it advocate for gender equity. Despite the legal change, queer communities in China have continued to face social stigma and moral scrutiny, frequently linked to prejudices around AIDS and illicit drug use ( ).
In China, queer activism often entails a strategic negotiation between online and offline domains to navigate around punitive measures enforced by authorities, resembling a guerrilla approach. The internet as a counter-space for LGBTIQ+ communities once provided avenues for gender and sexual minorities to express their voices and build communities ( ). Since the 1990s, at the early development of the Chinese internet, the official stance towards online LGBTIQ+ communities was characterised by a policy of ‘no encouraging, no discouraging, and no promoting’ ( ). In the early 2000s, the internet became a space for queer individuals to navigate life within an authoritarian, heteropatriarchal society. During the early 2000s, the space of expression provided a brief window for the flourishing of queer advocacy and activism ( ).
Since the 2010s, China’s civil society has faced intensified scrutiny under Xi Jinping’s administration, leading to significant restrictions on freedom of expression and activism both online and offline. The LGBTIQ+ community has been equally affected. Physical activities are now tightly monitored and controlled. For example, in 2021, Shanghai’s longstanding LGBTIQ+ pride festival was abruptly cancelled with organisers citing safety concerns ( ), much like the closure of several queer organisations that have been forced to close since then, including LGBT Rights Advocacy China and the Beijing LGBT Centre ( ). In the online world, the Chinese government’s aspiration for more control over the tech industry has led to a closer alignment of technologies with Beijing’s political ideologies ( ). In 2021, China’s primary digital platform WeChat adopted a more assertive stance against LGBTIQ+ communities, resulting in the suspension and closure of numerous WeChat public accounts operated by LGBTIQ+ university students without prior notice or specific reasons ( ). Although, content related to LGBTIQ+ communities is not explicitly banned, shadow-banning or internet traffic restrictions do occur. Platform regulations exhibit ambiguity, frequently entangled with oversight of online pornography, sexual violence, or other content deemed obscene by the Chinese state ( ). The online censorship targeting queer communities bears resemblance to the categorisation of homosexuality as ‘hooliganism’ during Deng Xiaoping’s administration, contradicting China’s diplomatic claims to the protection of human rights and gender equity ( ).
Under Xi Jinping’s administration, there has also been a notable rise in state-supported discourse that suppresses queerness by reinforcing binary gender norms and moral standards. This heteronormative framework has gained legitimacy through the political discourse of reviving Confucianism – under the CPC’s interpretation – to emphasise national interests rooted in heterosexual familialism ( ). Expressions of non-heterosexual, non-familial, and non-monogamous relationships have therefore been excluded from the Chinese societal norms and deemed morally unacceptable. There are exceptions. Individuals, such as Jin Xing, prominent trans figure in China’s mainstream culture have been seen as primarily performative in her gender representation, yet they have inadvertently reinforced heterosexual normativity and patriarchy in her TV shows ( ). Xing’s case further demonstrates that queer communities in China can only find visibility by aligning with hegemonic norms. In response, queer communities have turned to songs and filmmaking as alternative forums to advocate for rights and form communal or transnational bonds. This ‘soft activism’ through performing arts, culture, and media ( ) includes practices like hashtag activism ( ) or emphasising living a ‘caring activist life’ as a form of resistance ( : 480). In a different vein, illustrate how Chinese straight parents have increasingly joined grassroots organisations to advocate for gay rights, leveraging the cultural capital of parenthood to foster a familial model of LGBTIQ+ activism.
Furthermore, the CPC and state media have depicted human rights issues as ideological attacks by malicious Western forces aiming to destabilise China ( ). In Chinese politics, masculinity and femininity hold sway; queerness, however, is often deemed ‘non-Chinese’, associated with foreign influence coming from the West or the neighbouring Eastern Asian countries, and consequently subjected to criticism. Self-proclaimed nationalist critics often tie China’s feminist discourse and LGBTIQ+ movements to Western influence and attribute effeminate male images in mass media, such as ‘little fresh meat’ (xiao xian rou) or Otokonoko (wei niang), to the influence of Korean and Japanese pop culture ( ; ) In some more extreme examples, LGBTIQ+ people have been linked to state security concerns and foreign espionage. Online, Chinese cyber-nationalism, which aims to combat ‘foreign forces’ instead of opposing ‘gender ideology’, underscores that anti-gender sentiments are not just ideological conflicts but also power struggles ( ). For queer communities, overt forms of activism like protests, public gatherings, and poster displays are now risky, often leading to everyday scrutiny, legal repercussions, and harassment from local law enforcement bodies which we understand as ‘queer necropolitics’.

## Queer necropolitics
Scholars have conceptualised China’s approach to LGBTIQ+ organising as ‘systematic queerphobia’ ( ; ; ). While the sociological approach to ‘phobia’ describes macro-level fear, ‘queerphobia’ encompasses a cultural sensibility viewing discrimination and oppression as stemming from a psychological complex or mental illness. Understanding the Chinese state as instilling ‘fear’ among queer communities oversimplifies the situation by reducing it to binary dynamics of suppression and resistance. Instead, the state has consistently pursued top-down corrective approaches, such as promoting heteropatriarchal family values to ‘harmonise’ the queer community, prioritising proactive measures over defensive ones. The current systems of control and suppression extend beyond ‘systematic queerphobia’ and towards ‘queer necropolitics’.
The employment of ‘queer necropolitics’ ( ), extended from Foucauldian ‘necropolitics’ and ‘biopolitics’, offers a nuanced understanding of the motivations behind everyday suppression and oppressive tactics and their intended consequences beyond mere censorship or silencing queer activism. In China, state violence against queer communities is often presented as a solution to public anxieties regarding non-heteronormativity. Social control is positioned as a remedy for the uncertainties and vulnerabilities of the contemporary neoliberal political order to promise security and safety to those who embrace and adhere to such suppressive measures.
The idea of ‘necropolitics’ – and the other side of the same coin ‘necropower’ – is rooted in Foucauldian biopolitics. It pertains to a form of power that structurally designs ‘everyday death worlds’ ( ) involving decisions about who should live (i.e. necropower), and, by extension, who merits social welfare and protection, versus who is deemed as ‘illegitimate’ or ‘illegal’ and thereby targeted for death (i.e. necropolitics). The idea of ‘everyday death’ encompasses a range of contexts, from overt instances such as war, torture, or imperial invasion to more subtle forms of violence normalised in gender, race, and class dynamics. In this way, death extends beyond literal physical demise to involve social, political, and civil death – the social relationships marked by decay, neglect, and suffering resulting from prolonged exposure to violence and deprivation. Achille proposal of necropolitics centralises the death in subaltern groups who experience the symbiotic co-presence of life and death as state and corporate entities allocate social resources to favour the privileged (e.g. the rich, the abled, desired citizens) while disadvantaging the non-privileged (e.g. the poor, the disabled, non-citizens).
Scholars have associated necropolitics with queer life. Sarah explores ‘queer investments in punishment’, showing how progressive movements in North America’s carceral states have rebranded policing, incarceration, and laws once linked to repression as symbols of sexual justice through discursive, affective, labour, and commercial measures. More broadly, collect a range of scholarships to construct the notion of ‘queer necropolitics’ which illuminate and connect forms of killing and ‘letting die’, ranging from spectacular to mundane, within the dichotomy of repression versus visibility. They argue that the incorporation of gender and sexual diversity under hegemonic governance accelerates premature death for individuals who are deemed unassimilable within liberal regimes of rights and representation, thus rendering them disposable. This is particularly true within hegemonic orders marked by racism, neo/colonialism, the war on terror, incarceration, border enforcement, and neoliberalism. Lamble and colleagues (2017) further emphasise the necessity for critical insights into how society establishes and fosters forms of deadly inclusion and exclusion for those experiencing ‘competing marginalities’ ( , disadvantaged by systems of oppression (i.e. capitalism, imperialism, and patriarchy).
In this article, we use queer necropolitics as a framework to explore the various forms of suppression and their consequences, particularly in the context of queer activism in China. Using this framework, we highlight how suppression impacts the everyday existence, visibility, and protection of queer communities. This approach allows us to connect the broader implications of necropolitics with the specific challenges faced by queer community and activist groups in China.

## Methodology
From December 2021 to March 2022, the lead author conducted semi-structured, qualitative interviews with 26 participants from 12 provinces in China. These participants self-identified as members of LGBTIQ+ communities, with all the participants actively engaged as organisers. The research timeline provided insights into participants’ daily experiences of queer organising amid China’s post-pandemic reconstruction, particularly considering the growing integration of digital and automated surveillance technologies into the organisation of everyday life.
The recruitment process, akin to existing studies on China’s queer communities, initially drew from the lead author’s personal networks and subsequently expanded through snowball sampling. We acknowledge the potential limitation of this approach, which often results in a homogeneous participant cohort predominantly consisting of middle-class gay men or lesbian women based in metropolitan areas in China ( ). We were careful not to over-emphasise socially advantaged queer members and overlook those outside middle-class, metropolitan networks. To address this potential imbalance, the research design was intentionally crafted to diversify the participant pool (see ). The lead researcher actively sought to include LGBTIQ+ individuals from different social stratifications across various regions, mitigating the risks of homogeneity and ensuring inclusive representations of the LGBTIQ+ community.
 The research included participants across 12 provinces/regions in China. There were 13 participants coming from metropolitan cities and 13 of them from smaller-sized cities. When asked to identify themselves on the LGBTIQ+ spectrum, 10 participants noted their most salient identity as trans and/or gender diverse, 9 as cisgender queer men, 6 as cisgender queer women, and one person identified themselves as a cisgender heterosexual ally.
Given the sensitivity of the project, the lead researcher took additional precautions during the fieldwork. Priority was placed on safeguarding participants’ safety and fostering a secure and inclusive environment for sharing. Recognising that participants may feel uneasy about being present on Chinese social media platforms alongside the researcher, all interviews were conducted via encrypted communication platforms like Signal and Telegram. Recruited participants had adopted Virtual Private Networks (VPNs) to circumvent China’s Great Firewall and gain access to the encrypted platforms outside the context of this research. Individuals without access to VPNs due to financial constraints, limited digital literacy, or security concerns were unable to engage in the study. As an additional precaution, all research-related communication ceased after the conclusion of the fieldwork to minimise the risk of participant re-identification. Participants who requested to review the research deliverables were advised to use encrypted channels for any subsequent communication. We acknowledge that VPNs posed barriers to research participation but were pertinent to ensuring the safety and confidentiality of the participants.
The interviews used a semi-structured format focussing on digital surveillance and activism practices amid the LGBTIQ+ communities. First, the first author who collected the data read out the information sheet and consent form before answering any questions that the interviewee had and seeking out verbal consent. Verbal consent was transcribed. This strategy ensured minimal exchange of signed documents that may leave digital traces of the research project and endanger the interviewees. The interview started with basic questions about the interviewees’ experience in LGBTIQ+ activism and community building. It then moved to their experiences during the COVID-19 lockdowns and organisational changes. While the focus was not on the lockdowns, the first three interviews highlighted changes in activism and community organising, thus leading to adjustments in the interview guide. Open questions (e.g. ‘Could you describe how, if at all, your work has changed before, during, and after the COVID-19 lockdowns?’) and prompts (e.g. ‘If you are comfortable to do so, please tell me more about your encounters with public security.’) were used to avoid suggesting a negative or positive stance of the interviewer.
We used NVivo-assisted reflexive thematic analysis ( ). The researchers familiarised themselves with the data, completed an initial round of coding, generated initial themes based on the codes, reviewed and developed conceptual themes, refined, defined, and named themes, and drafted the paper ( : 39). The research team discussed the latent meaning behind these themes and connections between codes to ensure coding validity. To ensure accurate Chinese-English translation, the research team discussed and cross-checked uncertain areas. In the paper, we anonymise our participants’ identities, including names, organisations, and cities of residence and use pseudonyms when referring to the participants. Direct quotes from interviews conducted in Mandarin are translated into English and generalised to prevent any potential re-identification of participants through specific terms or phrases. Where participants chose to speak English in the interview, the quotes included do not correct their language.

## Findings
As our discussion section will elaborate, resistance persists, albeit reshaped, urging a more complicated understanding of queer activism in China that scrutinises how anti-queerness is increasingly assimilated into hegemonic structures, potentially erasing the existence of queer communities.

## LGBTIQ+ communities: The moralised and politically invisible subjects
LGBTIQ+ communities in China exist as invisible subjects within the nation’s legislative construction, yet their online and offline presence is subject to continual moral scrutiny. Straddling the realms of legal invisibility and moralised visibility, participants expressed feelings of unease and precarity amidst China’s deliberate neglect of LGBTIQ+ rights and its construction of gender norms that label gender diversity as deviant from societal norms and values. This is the first layer of China’s queer necropolitics, broadly framing LGBTIQIA+ organising as undesirable by the state.
In China’s mainstream discourses, issues with LGBTIQ+ rights are largely under-represented and thus queer communities are barely protected in the country’s legal framework or public announcements. Participant Xiaofang, who is a gay activist, discussed:
There is a noticeable reluctance to openly discuss or release official statements directly addressing the LGBT community and its associated policies.
The reasons for this political avoidance are likely to be multifaceted. At the core it is an ecosystem of cultural, political, social, and digital norms that are set in place to distance LGBTIQ+ topics from the broader society: ‘the reasons are very complicated, and it [the political sensitivity of LGBTIQ+ topics] cannot be explained by one or two reasons’ (Xiaoling, lesbian activist). These sustained political strategies of avoidance introduced uncertainty:
At some level, for example, women are half the sky, right? The equal rights of women compared with men, is … absolutely right, you can always use that. But then if you ask for more, hmm! Maybe there’s danger … Those movements that can mobilise people… people in power might think: maybe that’s not right because that will create potential connections to social instability. (Momo, gay activist)
Such political avoidance demonstrates that queerness is perceived as an unspeakable social taboo. Such avoidance effectively precludes any constructive dialogue among the public, the government, and LGBTIQ+ communities. The legislative invisibility of LGBTIQ+ communities results in the government’s suppressive measures manifesting ambiguously, spanning from censorship to detention. Legal obscurity has consequently led to LGBTIQ+ communities being addressed indifferently, subjected to punishment or silencing, alongside other social and political matters or controversial public figures. Another participant commented:
[In 2021], the original [government] phrasing is to improve the online environment for underage and teenager and college students. And then [the authorities] first they banned a lot of idol programmes, then they banned… we call it a ‘sissy ban
 
’. And later, they [the government] ban other… famous celebrity who avoided taxes, who had bad habit, who had divorced. So, it’s … a very huge, massive crackdown on the current society, on the kind of people that don’t follow traditional moral standards. (Fanfan, gay activist)
The ‘sissy ban’ resulted in the banning of numerous livestreaming accounts, in particular negatively affecting gay men and trans women. Speaking to the trans experience, An’ni, who has been a trans activist for almost a decade, commented on the legal invisibility of trans communities:
With restricted access to obtaining a formal university degree and healthcare that would subsequently allow a formal change [of the gender marker] on the national identity card, obtaining legal employment is an issue, leaving many to seek lowest-paid or precarious jobs like delivery work and livestreaming. (An’ni, trans activist)
A cis-gay activist Jin Qiang who has provided undercover HIV testing services to trans women for 5 years further noted that yet others – especially trans women – are pushed into sex work through structural inequities. These concerns are supported by a 2016 national survey conducted by the United Nations Development Programme, which surveyed over 1100 trans people in China, out of a total of 28,454 respondents ( ). The survey found that trans people faced more challenges accessing healthcare and social services than cisgender LGBIQ+ individuals and were more likely to have their academic performance affected by their identity. Over 20% had some college education but no degree. Trans-specific health care and legal issues, lack of anti-discrimination laws in employment and education and mental health have also been confirmed by later qualitative community-based participatory research with 21 trans activists from across China ( ). This research project confirms that these large structural inequity issues are continuing for trans community members.
According to the participants, for China’s ruling power, queerness was more than an issue of gender or sexuality. This is evident with the public acceptability of figures such as Jin Xing, who conforms with heteronormativity. While queerness occupies the space of legal invisibility, the state may choose to ‘queer-ise’ certain subjects, as the original meaning of queer suggests, by making them suspicious, odd, strange, or problematising them. Gender reassignment is medicalised and therefore accepted, providing the individual conforms with their new gender identity and does not continue to identify as queer. For example, cisgender men or trans women who appear ‘unmasculine’, along with the public figures conducting tax evasion, may be viewed as perverse to contrast the image of the ‘normal’ Chinese citizen in society. Participants felt the state curated their identities based on narrow Party-state definitions, creating a narrative that justifies covert re-criminalisation.

## Covert re-criminalisation of LGBTIQ+ communities
The national-level narratives set space for queer necropolitics via policymaking, which in turn creates on-the-ground containment mechanisms of queer groups. According to the participants, the containment strategies range from covert monitoring and censorship of LGBTIQ+ groups and individuals, co-optation strategies (e.g. police officers visiting LGBTIQ+ organisers to explain new laws and regulations), to more coercive confrontation such as invitations to ‘drink tea’ with the police. The most severe means of coercion were run-ins with state security authorities, that can escalate to extensive interrogations (the longest reported lasted 10 h; n = 3), interception of hard drives, laptops, and phones to research LGBTIQ+ group networks (n = 3), and even wiretapping of houses (n = 1). The findings suggest that there is a structural move away from the ‘not encouraging, not discouraging, and not promoting’ that has termed a ‘liminal grey area’, and towards covert re-criminalisation of LGBTIQ+ groups via making a wide range of activities that LGBTIQ+ groups typically undertake illegal.
Li Lin, a pansexual woman participant with just under a decade of LGBTIQ+ organising experience, shared her experiences of organising events only to have the public security actors repeatedly cancel them. She termed this censorship strategy ‘slow killing’:
It’s so exhausting. I think it’s like slow killing. They don’t kill you directly but they just exhaust you until you have to stop working on your own … ‘We’ll let you survive but we don’t let you do anything so you just have to stop on your own’.
Chinese LGBTIQ+ communities’ vulnerability to state governance hinges significantly on their registration status and geographic location. According to three research participants, formally registered organisations faced more bureaucratic scrutiny in their daily reporting work but were less frequently harassed by the police and did not report run-ins with state security organs. Disguising LGBTIQ+ censorship under a blanket of law and regulations has been a hallmark strategy of the Xi Jinping administration. One participant succinctly explained the dynamics of covert discrimination through legal requirements posed for LGBTIQ+ communities:
China does not have a so-called discriminatory policy against the LGBT community. But actually there are many such doors that are closed [to the LGBTIQ+ community]. From the provisions of the written law, or from the policy, they do not say that you can’t do [LGBTIQ+ community activities], But they tell you to register your organisation. If you want to raise funds, you must meet the regulations. They give you a legal condition. … But in fact, in mainland China, only some non-profit groups that HIV testing and intervention in the LGBTIQ+ communities and can register with the civil affairs department. Other than them, things like doing advocacy, they are actually impossible for you to apply for registration as a non-profit organisation. (Zhang Tie, gay activist)
Among 26 research participants, only three of them worked for LGBTIQ+ organisations with government-issued certifications. None of the trans-led groups that we talked to had a formal registration status. Some trans activists ran activities in larger LGBTIQ+ organisations with a formal registration status. Maintaining government registrations posed challenges for the majority of LGBTIQ+ activists. Without legal certification, LGBTIQ+ organisations primarily operated within a legal grey zone, opening both the staff and volunteers for sustained public security surveillance and harassment ( ).
From the Chinese government’s perspective, the perceived risk of the LGBTIQ+ movement is evaluated based on the geographical location of LGBTIQ+ members, which is further linked to the movement’s international visibility and influence. In big cities like Beijing, Shanghai, or Guangzhou, LGBTIQ+ communities often faced regular police surveillance. Interviewees noted that local police were particularly concerned about external political interference with the local LGBTIQ+ communities. These cities, often home to international embassies and extensive local and foreign volunteer networks, are seen by police as potential sources of social movements that could disrupt China’s political stability. 
As formal organisational registration is inaccessible for most of the queer groups, public security agencies exploit numerous laws and regulations to pin illegal activities on LGBTIQ+ groups. As discussed above, LGBTIQ+ organisations that would like to register as formal organisations are informally blocked from doing so. The swathe of laws and regulations rationalises public security interventions as expected reactions to illegal activities. Among some of the most common ones were police interrogation of LGBTIQ+ about receiving funding or interview requests from foreign media (n = 1), direct harassment from the police and public security (n = 3), encouraging censorship of feminist and LGBTIQ+ topics online (n = 1). In some cases, even a formal registration status was not sufficient to protect queer groups and rather served as a mechanism of surveillance, rather than government support or protection. A genderqueer activist Peipei revealed:
Well, during our last participation in a meeting organised by an AIDS prevention group, several universities in the area collaborated. It’s worth noting that this AIDS prevention organisation is officially registered and operates within the bounds of the law. … Following the meeting, approximately a week or two later, all attendees received inquiries from their university counsellors, probing whether they had taken part in any meetings. To our surprise, the counsellors seemed well-informed about the specifics of our gathering: the venue where the event took place and the number of participants. … The university offered no clear explanation, and it appeared they were unaware of the meeting until alerted by the police.
Following a direct police harassment event, LGBTIQ+ organisations expected to be under close surveillance. Five activists from five different groups reported police officers being assigned to observe their group and keeping in close contact with the same police officer over multiple years. The participant Zhang You, who is a gay activist, recalled a time in 2021 when nationwide activism activities were disrupted within one day. Zhang revealed:
The police had already arrested me, which means that they would be paying close attention to me…, I would be closely watched now. I am sure that at least for a short period of time, I would not be able to do anything else.
Online censorship applies to silencing pro-queer voices in an ambiguous way without pointing to specific guidelines. Like many feminist groups, queer communities active online employ strategies of self-censorship ( ), as well as ‘digital masquerading’ ( ) and satire ( ) to sustain their online presence. Internet censorship is not equally distributed. Our respondents reported that those who perpetrate harm towards queer communities can often evade platform censorship despite being reported for violating online community guidelines. Participant Liang Bo summarised how online censorship is arbitrarily and unequally applied. LGBTIQ+ social media posts often face stricter scrutiny compared to those supporting extreme nationalism. Liang states:
If you’re a feminist activist or an LGBT activist, …[and] you speak a lot on rights-based issues, you will be blocked. If you’re a nationalist and you have an individual or a public account, … no matter what kind of hate speech, no matter what kind of … argument you have you have incited, you won’t be blocked. … simply speaking, the authority doesn’t like any kind of … grassroots movement on gender. (Liang Bo, gay activist)
According to participant Liang, online national populists played a role in reinforcing what Lisa Duggan terms ‘homonormativity’ ( ). Homonormativity describes a politics that does not challenge dominant heteronormative assumptions and institutions; instead, it supports and perpetuates them. The banning of pro-LGBTIQ+ discourses has exacerbated the impact of the widespread moral anti-LGBTIQ+ examination of queerness. Anti-queer sentiments are frequently intertwined with expressions of Chinese patriotism. Specifically, four participants described encountering online attacks and trolling from extreme nationalist groups who endorse the national binary gender construction.
The legal invisibility and moral visibility of queer communities create a space where re-criminalisation can occur. Since the 2010s, the Party-state has gradually introduced laws, policies, and proposals to restrict activities like fundraising, campus activism, and social media publication ( ). While these measures do not explicitly target LGBTIQ+ communities, they threaten queer activism by empowering the government to restrict these populations. The findings in this section suggest that the treatment of LGBTIQ+ groups is shifting from covert and gradual ‘illegalisation’ ( ) towards institutionalised criminalisation, especially when foreign individuals or organisations are involved.

## Invisible activism: de-formalisation, survival, and communal care
At the group level, queer groups applied a variety of strategies to continue their activities, oftentimes reporting engagement in what we term invisible activism. Invisible activism prioritises communal care and solidarity, intentionally avoiding overt advocacy or explicit keywords online that may direct attention to queer communities. This section elucidates how queer organisers exert agency and resistance in the face of restrictive gender governance and institutional perspectives of LGBTIQ+ groups in China.
During the interview, our participant Zhang Tie, who is a gay activist, discussed how he had been negotiating this movements under China’s heteronormative gender governance:
In China, there is a saying that goes: ‘There are policies at the top, and countermeasures at the bottom’. It means that you have your policies that I will not openly confront. I will work around to find another method, find my own living space.
While the scope of politically sensitive keywords is contingent on the contextually defined social values by the Chinese Party-state ( ), research participants anticipated which keywords might be subjected to censorship. Particularly when working online, LGBTIQ+ members use double-speak ( ), to render sensitive discussions invisible and circumvent platform censorship. Queer participants shared several examples of such double-speak, such as the term ‘homosexuality’ being replaced by the rainbow emoji or the term ‘tong xun lu’ (meaning ‘address book’), which shares an abbreviation of ‘txl’ with the Chinese term for homosexuality – ‘tong xing lian’.
Research participants revealed that taking screenshots is a common tactic within the community to preserve and recirculate online discussions. Participant Peipei stated:
Well, this is quite common in China, and everyone is used to it, that is, some sensitive topics will be deleted on this kind of public platform. We’re pretty used to it now. […] When a sensitive article is sent out, we must read it as soon as it is sent out, and then take a screenshot to save it, because it will be gone soon. (Peipei, genderqueer activist)
The cat-and-mouse practices of censorship avoidance are constantly evolving. Participant Zhang Tie further explained during the interview:
If we run into some obstacles, maybe we just adjust. For example, if we want to post on Weibo or WeChat, if some words fail, then we can change another word, for example, if the word ‘homosexuality’ fails, then we don’t use this phrase (Zhang Tie, gay activist).
It is nearly impossible for queer communities to formalise their organisations or organise collective activism on a large scale. Consequently, LGBTIQ+ communities have leaned towards de-formalising their approaches to activism, integrating everyday resistance to China’s queer necropolitics through providing communal care. Communal care serves as a collective survival strategy, aiding in the preservation of the community while remaining outside the government’s scrutiny. Five participants reported a wide range of ‘non-political’ activities planned, many of which were not labelled under the term ‘LGBTIQ+’ and simply facilitated communal activities, such as hiking, game or movie nights. Participant Momo has been advocating for LGBTIQ+ rights for over a decade. He explained the changed format of LGBTIQ+ activism in China:
Strict activism is gone, that period has gone. […] If you raise your flag, probably you will drag us down in the water. We should really act and think strategically – what we need to change, sustainably. (Momo, gay activist)
Trans community organisers similarly stressed the importance of quietly supporting their communities. Among the activities that trans community organisers prioritised was a focus on mental health via support groups and hotlines. Our participant Lingling, who is a trans activist, reported her determination to continue trans-specific mental health support services despite the frequent public security calls, visits, and interrogations. Another trans activist, Yue, chose to create booklets and run online workshops to support those community members who undergo gender-affirming surgeries and use hormone replacement medications. These tactics contribute to the body of literature that has evidenced the visibility and survival practices of trans communities online amid anti-trans sentiments both off- and online, including harnessing empathy through sharing lived experiences online ( ).
Through organised communal activities, queer communities engage in ‘invisible activism’, which involves making and preserving connections through the embeddedness of queer members and community leaders and through small, everyday acts of social networking. Although often presented as apolitical, invisible activism is inherently composed of acts of sustained, everyday resistance against China’s queer necropolitics, which perpetuates a world where everyday gender-based violence is normalised. Invisible activism involves small actions that might not directly influence politics or be known publicly beyond the community. Yet, these actions build social bonds that strengthen community resilience and could lead to future social change. These findings speak to published literature that notes ‘the limits of a protest-based, politically contentious model of social change’ ( : 5).
When asked about the future of China’s LGBTIQ+ activism, participant Xiaoling concluded:
If everyone is like a firefly, we can continuously glow and attract other fireflies to glow together. (Xiaoling, lesbian activist)
In the participant’s statement, the metaphor of being collectively ‘glowing fireflies’ coincides with Lauren notion of ‘solidarity’ which stems from the collective effort for survival that increasingly consumes people’s lives. The potential scalability of such solidarity could once again bring LGBTIQ+ communities into visibility, making them susceptible to systematic suppression. Narratives in China’s domestic LGBTIQ+ communities, especially among those unlikely to emigrate, convey a positive vision of the future. As participant Zhang Tie concluded:
We survived the Cultural Revolution, we will also survive this. (Zhang Tie, gay activist)
Contemporary China is different to the tumultuous era of the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976) characterised by notable transformation in its political, economic, and technological spheres. Xi’s governing approach diverges from that of Mao’s era. Rather than pursuing radical societal restructuring, China’s avoidance of discussing queer matters has demonstrated that the current CPC focuses on minimising disruptions and ensuring its enduring influence in the collective consciousness of the Chinese population. Zhang Tie conveyed a sense of optimism when comparing China’s current heteronormative gender construction to the era of the Cultural Revolution, suggesting a belief that a less necropolitical future may be forthcoming. We also question what transpires when these promises diminish under China’s queer necropolitics. The optimistic promise of a brighter future for LGBTIQ+ communities under China’s queer necropolitics turns ‘cruel’ when activism can only exist in an invisible form, and any visibility can jeopardise the survival of LGBTIQ+ members.

## Discussion
The conceptual contribution of this paper is twofold. First, we extend the concept of ‘queer necropolitics’ to include an authoritarian state’s necropolitical approach towards queer communities through legal invisibility and ambiguity. The state can exploit legal invisibility to undermine queer communities and activism whenever their existence does not align with state interests. Second, we introduce the term ‘invisible activism’ to conceptualise Chinese queer activism under China’s queer necropolitics.
This discussion further develops the concept of queer necropolitics by discussing the (in)visible governance of queer activism under Xi Jinping’s grand national ‘China dream’ politico-economic plan for the duration of Xi’s time in office. In other countries, queer necropolitics are evident in explicit policies that name LGBTIQ+ communities (e.g. through pinkwashing or expanding the carceral state under the guise of protecting queer people, see, e.g. ). China’s approach differs significantly: policymakers there invisibilise queer communities, never acknowledging them publicly. Meanwhile, queer groups face increasing scrutiny under numerous laws and regulations that restrict their activities indirectly, a process described by our participant Li Ling as ‘slow killing’.
This position legitimises the state to engage in suppressing the online and offline visibility of queer communities and their activism. Such queer necropolitical strategies are met with counter-strategies of silent, invisible, ‘unlabelled’ activism. While facing systematic queerphobia (i.e. digital censorship, meetings with police and even state security authorities), LGBTIQ+ activism is neither silenced nor loud. Navigating between legal invisibility (i.e. avoidance) and moral visibility (i.e. interrogation), LGBTIQ+ communities in China engage in what we conceptualise as invisible activism. Invisible activism is a survival technique to take care of themselves and community members. Invisible activism, in part, responds to the platform censorship and systemic suppression that erase any speech or activities online and offline that support gender equity. Additionally, it characterises the form of LGBTIQ+ activism in China that is not loud or visible but is manifested by invisibility, optimism, and the politics of care ( ).
The term does not imply the absence of LGBTIQ+ activism in China. Rather, our conceptual proposal for invisible activism recognises a range of activities, particularly those that do not or cannot, in the context of China’s LGBTIQ+ activism, directly target changes in the political economy but still contribute to various aspects of social life as political actions ( ; ). Given the heightened and contested experience of safety in public space for marginalised minority groups, further examination and conceptualisation of non-dominant forms of activism can be particularly important. In an example of everyday resistance, patrons of the beloved lala (women-loving-women) bar Roxie in Shanghai hung their bras from the ceiling pipes during a final party before its closure in June 2024 (see ).
 We show how instead of resisting institutionalised boundaries such as laws – and, indeed, increasingly bound by them – activists have adopted quiet counter-strategies of communal care have become survival strategies for LGBTIQ+ groups in China. These strategies are not loud or visible but are rather manifested by invisibility, optimism, and the politics of care ( ). These strategies in themselves challenge the norms of rights-based activism praxis propagated by Western liberal nations ( ). Survival by embracing internalised censorship and adjusted behaviours is now recognised by LGBTIQ+ organisers as the most fool-proof way to resistance. As one of our more senior research participants Zhang Tie proclaimed, ‘it was worse during the Cultural Revolution’.
Our analysis of queer necropolitics in China underscores its unique aspects characterised by the political avoidance of queerness and the legal invisibility of queer subjects. We have identified that China’s queer necropolitics results from the escalation of systematic queerphobia at both institutional and individual levels, amid the absence of government guidelines or legislation. The legal invisibility of queer communities creates a regulatory grey area, allowing public and state security actors, as identified by research participants, to interpret and target the community based on societal and individual prejudices, leading to surveillance, interrogation, and penalisation of queer individuals. The involvement of state security investigations is primarily triggered by the involvement of foreign funding and foreign activists, indicating that the national focus is on eliminating ‘hostile foreign forces’ ( ). In navigating China’s queer necropolitics, visible antagonism against state actions is impractical and can further induce harm to queer members. To mitigate actions of queer necropolitics, queer communities mobilise what we term invisible activism.
The proposal of invisible activism aligns with intellectual trajectories of minority feminist scholars to acknowledge diverse forms of activism within socially, culturally, and politically marginalised groups. This concept is also in conversation with what Jing called ‘nonconfrontational activism’ – a means for NGOs and other actors of networked activism to explore the bending, not breaking, of rules, and Hongwei conceptualisation of ‘soft activism’ – bonding through communal and even transnational networks. The apparent oxymoron of the term offers an opportunity to further question the perceived dichotomy between visibility and invisibility, and between overt and covert activism. We emphasise the need for scholarly attention to alternative forms of activism under systematic suppression, contested safety, and sustained stress. This diversified conceptualisation becomes crucial, particularly considering that the increasing use of automation and AI in governance, both within authoritarian regimes and Western democracies, will significantly impact the landscape of queer activism.

## Conclusion
In China, queer communities exist within a complex framework shaped by legislation, morality, national gender governance, and geopolitics. This paper sought to address the question: How have China’s hegemonic governance techniques shaped queer organising in the country? We have identified that LGBTIQ+ communities in China operate as legally invisible subjects, as they are not openly discussed in official or public discourse. This legal invisibility, coupled with the government’s crackdown on civil society has legitimised China’s approach to queer necropolitics, namely, state resources are channelled to undermine queer activism, leading to the re-criminalisation of LGBTIQ+ organisations and moral scrutiny of their online visibility. While activism from traditional accounts faces suppression, queer activists, understanding themselves as ‘glowing fireflies’, engage in what we term ‘invisible activism’ to continue advocating for gender equity while prioritising self-survival and protection. Invisible activism operates quietly, implicitly, and less overtly while embodying political and antagonistic significance. It is manifested through everyday practices of communal care, where activists care for each other, remaining optimistic about their future of activism under China’s queer necropolitics. This optimism, while valuable, turns ‘cruel’ when activism can only exist in an invisible form, and any visibility poses a threat to the survival of LGBTIQ+ members. Like the soft glow of a firefly, communities form bonds through survival.
