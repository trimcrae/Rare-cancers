## Background:
The introduction of rentable electric scooters (e-scooters) has been associated with injury presentations to emergency departments (EDs). Our objective was to determine the incidence and severity of injuries from rentable e-scooters among adults presenting to EDs in a northern urban region.

## Methods:
Adults presenting to all Edmonton EDs with injuries related to rentable e-scooters during 3 summers (2019–2021) were eligible for inclusion. We identified e-scooter charts using multiple sources: administrative data, trauma registry, and text-based triage searching. Two independent reviewers assessed each patient for study inclusion; disagreements were resolved by content experts. Trained researchers performed data extraction and descriptive statistical analysis.

## Results:
We included 759 e-scooter-related injury presentations. The median age was 28 years, males and females were almost equally represented, 20% presented by ambulance, and 14% were triaged as urgent. Most patients had multiple injuries (62%), with fractures (32%) and head injuries (17%) being common. Helmet use was infrequent (2%) and concurrent substance use was prevalent (26%). Admission to hospital was uncommon (5.5%); however, 30% of patients presenting to an ED with an e-scooter injury required further follow-up, with 9% undergoing surgery within 30 days of their index visit.

## Conclusion:
Injuries related to rentable e-scooters are increasingly common. Most injured patients have multiple injuries and require investigations, and a third require further management. These injuries represent substantial burdens to patients and the health care system in Canada. Injury prevention strategies should be considered to reduce injuries.

## Contexte:
L’arrivée sur les routes des trottinettes électriques de location (e-scooters) a été associée à des blessures nécessitant une visite aux urgences. Notre objectif était de déterminer l’incidence et la gravité des blessures occasionnées par ces trottinettes chez les adultes se présentant dans les services d’urgence dans une région urbaine nordique.

## Méthodes:
Nous avons évalué l’admissibilité à l’étude de toutes les personnes s’étant présentées dans un service d’urgence d’Edmonton en raison d’une blessure liée à l’utilisation de trottinettes électriques de location durant 3 étés (2019–2021). Nous avons utilisé de multiples sources pour repérer les cas liés aux trottinettes : données administratives, registre de traumatologie et recherches textuelles dans les dossiers de triage. Deux membres de l’équipe ont évalué indépendamment chaque patient et patiente pour déterminer son admissibilité à l’étude; les désaccords ont été résolus par des expertes et experts. L’extraction de données et l’analyse statistique descriptive ont été effectuées par du personnel de recherche compétent.

## Résultats:
Nous avons retenu 759 visites aux urgences pour des blessures liées à l’utilisation d’une trottinette électrique. L’échantillon retenu présentait les caractéristiques suivantes : âge médian de 28 ans, répartition presque égale entre les sexes, arrivée par ambulance dans 20 % des cas, 14 % des cas classés comme urgents au triage. La plupart avaient subi de multiples blessures (62 %), et les fractures (32 %) et les traumatismes crâniens (17 %) étaient fréquents. Le port du casque a été rarement rapporté (2 %), et l’usage de substances psychoactives était prévalent (26 %). Peu de cas ont nécessité une hospitalisation (5,5 %), mais 30 % des personnes incluses dans l’échantillon ont eu besoin d’un suivi, et 9 % ont subi une intervention chirurgicale dans les 30 jours suivant leur visite initiale.

## Conclusion:
Les blessures associées à l’utilisation de trottinettes électriques de location sont de plus en plus courantes. Dans la plupart des cas, les personnes ont subi des blessures multiples nécessitant des tests supplémentaires, et le tiers d’entre elles auront besoin d’un suivi. Ces blessures représentent un fardeau considérable, tant pour la patientèle que pour le système de santé canadien. Des stratégies de prévention des blessures devraient donc être envisagées pour réduire leur prévalence.

The rapid adoption of e-scooters is related in part to their accessibility, ease of use, efficiency, and trivial environmental impact. Many large cities struggle with traffic congestion and parking, and in addition to cycling and walking, e-scooters represent an appealing approach to address these issues. 
Unfortunately, the introduction of rentable e-scooters has been associated with an increase of e-scooter-related injuries. Published literature reviews have documented e-scooter injuries in numerous cities and various countries; , however, limited data are available regarding injuries from rentable e-scooters in Canada. – Canadian transportation policy-makers, injury prevention specialists, health care providers, and other key partners require Canadian data on injuries related to rentable e-scooters to optimize injury prevention and public safety.
Our primary objective was to determine the incidence of injures related to rentable e-scooters among adults presenting to Edmonton emergency departments (EDs) between 2019 and 2021. Secondary objectives were to describe patient and accident characteristics of e-scooter injuries among adults.

## Methods

## Location
Edmonton is the capital of Alberta, located in northern Alberta, and has a population of about 1 million people. Harsh winter weather (e.g., extreme cold, snow accumulations, icy road conditions) preclude e-scooter use in the winter. Most e-scooter use in Edmonton involves rentals, as the use of privately owned electric scooters on city property is prohibited. 

## Patient identification
All adult (age ≥ 18 yr) patients presenting to 1 of the 7 EDs in Edmonton and surrounding areas with injuries related to rentable e-scooters for 3 summers were eligible for study inclusion. We excluded the Stollery Children’s Hospital, which sees only children up to age 17 years, and several small-volume EDs in the Edmonton region (e.g., Devon, Leduc, Westview), where e-scooter rental contracts were not operational at the time of the study.
The City of Edmonton began its rentable e-scooter pilot on Aug. 16, 2019. In Edmonton, there are no e-scooter helmet bylaws, e-scooter vehicle speeds are limited to 20 km/h, and it is unlawful to ride on city sidewalks. Study enrolment occurred over 3 summer seasons: Aug. 16 to Sept. 30, 2019; Apr. 30 to Sept. 30, 2020; and Apr. 30 to Nov. 15, 2021. These periods changed annually as e-scooter availability increased.
We identified participants retrospectively using multiple techniques. We obtained sports and recreation codes from the National Ambulatory Care Reporting System (NACRS), searched the local trauma registry, and applied automated text parsing algorithms to unstructured ED triage notes for terms related to e-scooters. Participants were also enrolled prospectively in 2021, when ED clinicians were encouraged to enrol patients using a faxable enrolment form. Patients younger than 18 years, patients with injuries related to nonrentable e-scooters, and patients who were injured using a mobility aid scooter were excluded from the study. We anonymized all charts identified for inclusion using unique patient identifiers and removed duplicate records. Once duplicates were removed, 2 independent reviewers assessed all charts for study inclusion and any disagreements were resolved by content experts.

## Data extraction
We extracted patient demographics (e.g., age, sex), presentation information (e.g., time of day, day of week, arrival), level on Canadian Triage and Acuity Scale (CTAS), causal factors (e.g., helmet use, substance use, road conditions), ED investigations (e.g., imaging, laboratory testing, procedures), and length of stay (LOS). In Canada, CTAS is a valid and reliable 5-level tool designed to assess initial acuity at ED presentation and is classified as Level I (Resuscitation), Level II (Urgent), Level III (Emergent), Level IV (Less urgent), and Level V (Non-urgent). Injury severity was measured in multiple ways, including CTAS level, hospital admission, Injury Severity Score (ISS) for trauma patients, and procedures (inpatient and outpatient). We selected 2 control groups — of patients aged 18 years and older presenting to Edmonton-area EDs with bicycle injuries and without e-scooter-related injuries — from the NACRS database during the study period, to facilitate determining demographic differences between these populations.

## Outcomes
The study’s primary outcomes, the incidence and severity of injuries to adults from rentable e-scooters, were determined by the number of index ED visits for e-scooter injuries. In addition, secondary outcomes included 30-day mortality, hospital admission, surgery, subsequent ED visits, and other organized outpatient follow-up (e.g., cast clinic appointment).

## Statistical analysis
Data extraction was performed by trained research team members and recorded using the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) program (V9.1.0; Vanderbilt University). Statistical analysis was performed by a trained data analyst using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute). Analyses were mainly descriptive. Categorical variables were described with frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were summarized with means and standard deviations, or medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), as appropriate. To account for variable study periods, we compared the same time frame, Apr. 30 to Sept. 30, across 2 years and calculated the rates per week and per month. We used the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) cohort checklist when writing our report. 

## Ethics approval
The study received ethics approval from the University of Alberta Research Ethics Board (PRO00108506). Owing to the retrospective nature of the study and the large study sample size, written informed consent was not required. Operational and administrative approvals (PRJ38052) were obtained from Alberta Health Services and Covenant Health, and a data-sharing agreement was formally executed.

## Results
We assessed a total of 1014 ED visits for study inclusion. After 2 independent reviewers removed duplicates and applied inclusion and exclusion criteria, we identified 759 ED visits to be an index ED presentation for an injury related to a rentable e-scooter during the study period ( ). The trauma registry identified 3 patients who had an ISS of 12 or more (scores of 14, 21, 24), which were all identified through other data sources. The median age of patients presenting to Edmonton-area EDs with an injury related to a rentable e-scooter was 28 (IQR 23–35) years, and both sexes were represented almost equally: 49.1% of patients were male and 50.9% female ( ). Almost 20% of patients were transported to the hospital by ambulance and 14.2% had a severe triage assessment, as reflected in CTAS scores of 1 or 2.
Direct admission to hospital was uncommon (5.5%); however, almost a third of patients (30.9%) required admission or follow-up within 30 days of their initial ED presentation for an e-scooter injury ( and ). This included 9.1% of patients requiring surgery within 30 days of their primary visit to an ED with an e-scooter injury, 10.3% of patients re-presenting to an ED, and another 9.7% of patients requiring a formal outpatient follow-up visit. No deaths were documented during the study period.
Compared with cycling-injured patients and the general population of patients presenting to the study EDs during the study period, the e-scooter-injured patients were younger ( ). The almost equal distribution of injuries among males and females is notable, as males were more heavily represented when assessing injury presentations sustained during other sport and recreational activities during the study period ( ).
The number of ED visits increased each summer of the study period ( ); however, the study periods were also longer each year. The mean daily number of injuries (8.8/wk in 2019, 13.9/wk in 2020, 17.6/wk in 2021) and the median monthly number of injuries (26.5/mo in 2019, 58/mo in 2020, 81/mo in 2021) increased annually over the 3 study years. Most patients injured by an e-scooter were driving the device (96.7%), rather than being a pedestrian bystander (1.2%) or additional rider (0.5%; ). Helmet use was rare (2.4%), and concomitant substance use was common (26.4%). Patients injured by e-scooters presented equally to community EDs (50.7%) and specialized trauma centres (49.3%). Most patients underwent investigations during their ED visit (76.3%); however, only a minority of patients required a consulting service while in the ED (11.1%; ).
Most patients presenting to an ED with an e-scooter injury were diagnosed with multiple injuries (61.5%) ( ). Although superficial injuries were the most common (70.4%), it is important to note that 31.9% of patients presenting to an ED with an e-scooter injury were treated for a fracture and 17% were diagnosed with a mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI). Median LOS in the ED for all patients was 244.5 (IQR 157–373.5) minutes (4.1 h). Admission to the hospital from the ED was uncommon (42/759), with 66.7% of those admitted undergoing inpatient surgery. Patients had notable 30-day follow-up outcomes, including 5.4% undergoing outpatient surgery, 10.3% returning to the ED, and 9.7% following up at a formal clinic (e.g., cast clinic; ).

## Discussion
In this multicentre retrospective chart review study, we identified 759 unique ED visits as presentations for an e-scooter injury during the 3-summer study period. The injured patients were young, sex distribution was almost equal, and ambulance use was common. Injury burden was substantial, with 32% of patients diagnosed with a fracture and 17% of patients documented to have an mTBI or concussion. Investigations were common, as were minor procedures (e.g., laceration repair, fracture and dislocation reductions, cast application). Finally, LOS was prolonged and contributed to ED crowding concerns; however, admissions were uncommon (about 5%). Overall, the impact of these injuries on patients and the overburdened health care system cannot be overstated.
Some unique features of this study require additional discussion. First, compared with other injuries resulting from recreational activities (e.g., motorcycling, cycling, snowmobiling) where males predominate, the sex distribution among people with e-scooter injuries was similar. Because rental e-scooter speed and location are regulated in Edmonton, perhaps some male risk-taking seen in other activities is tempered while using an e-scooter. For example, in addition to existing vehicle speed control, limiting riding (using skill-testing questions) during high-risk times (at night) and location regulation (in congested areas) could reduce injury risk. Second, 17% of injured patients had an mTBI or concussion; however, known helmet use was uncommon (about 2%). Given the disability associated with cases that are classified as “mild,” this seems like an important focus for injury prevention. Some e-scooter rentals include a helmet, and this approach warrants consideration and further research. Known concomitant substance use was 26%, and prohibiting e-scooter use while intoxicated could be an ideal target for an injury prevention strategy. Finally, almost a third of patients required additional follow-up, including 9% of patients undergoing surgery within 30 days of their initial presentation. This is an important outcome, and its collection should be encouraged in future research.
Determining the frequency, severity, and characteristics of e-scooter injuries among patients presenting to hospital EDs is a critical step in the creation and implementation of injury prevention strategies. Each urban centre has unique rules and regulations, built environments, and climate conditions. For example, Edmonton represents a medium-sized Canadian city with a short summer e-scooter season, owing to its extreme winter weather. Rental scooters predominate in this community; however, despite it currently being illegal to operate personal e-scooters on city property, the practice is becoming more common. 
Two studies have documented the increasing incidence of e-scooter injuries, , but neither recent literature review included a Canadian study. Sheikh and colleagues studied e-scooter injuries presenting to Calgary EDs and urgent care centres. A recent Canadian case report described an injured pedestrian struck by an e-scooter. To our knowledge, ours is the first Canadian study with a robust and comprehensive approach to patient enrolment with the aim of capturing as many eligible patients as possible. Furthermore, this Canadian study is unique as it reviewed every potentially eligible patient chart to ensure patient eligibility and provide in-depth details regarding the events and outcomes.
E-scooter injury studies performed in other countries also document low helmet use – and frequent concomitant impairment resulting from substance use. These international studies also report high ambulance usage , and a similarly large volume of patients requiring surgery for their e-scooter injuries. , , In keeping with the published literature, our study also documents limb fractures and head injuries as common injuries associated with e-scooter trauma. , E-scooter-related injury characteristics seen in international municipalities are similar to the patterns documented in this study in Canada.
Future research to assess the incidence and severity of injuries in both adult and pediatric riders is urgently needed. The infrequent use of personal protective equipment, such as helmets, and the high rates of concomitant substance use associated with e-scooter injuries are prime targets for injury prevention strategies. Even though advertising imagery can influence rider behaviours, research involving official social media accounts of popular e-scooter rental companies seldom showed e-scooters being used with protective gear, such as helmets. Studying the implementation of injury prevention strategies and their effects on e-scooter use and injury rates will be imperative given the growing popularity of these and similar micromobility vehicles.

## Limitations
First, our study focused exclusively on e-scooter injuries among adults in an urban community; therefore, pediatric injuries were excluded. Because e-scooter rentals are restricted to adults, Canadian research indicates that the incidence of e-scooter injuries in children is far lower. For example, a study of 11 pediatric and 9 adult or mixed Canadian EDs affiliated with the Canadian Hospitals Injury Reporting and Prevention Program over 8 summer seasons identified only 523 cases related to motorized scooter injuries. Second, our catchment population was limited to a single municipality, and we recognize that patterns likely vary based on multiple factors. Third, e-scooter injuries could have been underestimated owing to missing triage text or inadequate chart coding; however, we used multiple sources in efforts to mitigate this problem. At the time of the study, NACRS did not have a specific e-scooter injury code; that has changed recently, which should permit interprovincial comparisons in the future. Fourth, the study period encompassed the summers when Edmonton was under numerous restrictions related to COVID-19. It is conceivable that COVID-19-related curfews, venue restrictions, and gathering limitations may have affected citizen behaviours and led to a decrease in e-scooter usage and likely associated injuries. Continued surveillance is necessary to understand use and injury patterns. Fifth, there are no universal substance screening protocols in Edmonton EDs; therefore, we may be underreporting substance use in our study population as we were relying on clinician discretion to order blood ethanol levels or document reported or suspected substance use in the chart. Sixth, we did not calculate ISS for all patients; however, we used multiple measures of severity to describe the cohort. Finally, the study relied on retrospective data collection, resulting in a considerable amount of missing data (especially with respect to helmet use). An attempt was made to obtain more detailed prospective data from local EDs using a study form, but the proportion of injured patients captured was low in these high-volume EDs.

## Conclusion
E-scooter-related injuries among adults are increasingly common in EDs in this northern urban centre. The injured patients are young and almost equally male and female, helmet use is infrequent, and concurrent substance use is common. Most patients have multiple injuries, with a third of patients requiring further management, including 9% needing surgery within 30 days of their ED presentation. These injuries represent a substantial burden to patients and urban Canadian health care systems. Legislative changes (e.g., mandatory helmet laws, further speed control, substance use regulations) and other injury-prevention strategies should be considered to reduce these injuries.
