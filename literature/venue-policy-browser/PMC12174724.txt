## Abstract
The meaningful participation of young people from marginalized ethnic backgrounds in civic processes is central to the social cohesion of increasingly diverse liberal democracies, but their participation is compromised by a range of barriers resulting in decision‐making that is disconnected from their lives. To address participation barriers, a group of young people from marginalized ethnic migrant backgrounds joined a team of researchers, social innovators, and community leaders to co‐design and pilot an innovation to increase youth participation in policymaking. Delivered in phases over an 18‐month period, the project followed an approach that combined social innovation and evaluation methods. The process revealed factors that help and hinder young people from marginalized ethnic backgrounds' genuine engagement in partnerships with adults. The project learnings informed principle‐based guidelines for marginalized young people's civic engagement, which are offered to policymakers, practitioners, and researchers interested in inclusive youth participation.
Keywords: civic engagement, marginalized youth, social innovation

## INTRODUCTION
Liberal democracies are grappling with global challenges of maintaining social cohesion amid rapid and polarizing changes propelled by political, technological, social, environmental, and economic forces (Vertovec,  ). Although the intersecting influences are complex, at its core, social cohesion relies on vertical trust that governing institutions will be accountable and make decisions for the greater public good and horizontal trust and cooperation among the diverse groups and individuals that comprise society (Putnam,  ). Collective forms of civic engagement enable individuals to build bonding social capital through shared aspirations and commitment to address issues of public concern. The bonding social capital developed through such opportunities is a critical mechanism for developing the trust and collective efficacy required for social cohesion (Collins et al.,  ).
Globally, young migrants from marginalized ethnic groups are at the center of public debates about multiculturalism and immigration, and are frequently positioned as threats to social cohesion, mainly due to discourses that presume their alienation from society due to failures to integrate (Reynolds & Zontini,  ). Ironically, research finds that migrant youth from marginalized ethnic backgrounds are instead often relied on to support the integration of older family members within host countries because of their faster acquisition of the dominant language and a growing ability to navigate the different worlds of home and mainstream society (Mackay,  ; Nunn et al.,  ). The contradictory positioning of young people from marginalized ethnic backgrounds as both threats to and enablers of social cohesion calls for a better understanding of their and their families' needs. Yet young people from marginalized ethnic migrant backgrounds rarely have a seat at the table where discussions about these complex issues occur, and decision‐making on civic matters that affect them is largely disconnected from their lived experiences (United Nations,  ). There is a clear need for solutions to increase their collective engagement with policymaking.
In 2022, the New Zealand Government developed an explicit social cohesion strategy that aims to produce outcomes whereby all people, including marginalized ethnic groups, have a sense of belonging and inclusion; experience equity and respect for their own and others' social identities; are able and willing to participate in multiple domains of life; and trust institutions and other people to ensure that their needs will be met and they will be treated fairly (Ministry of Social Development,  ). In part, the strategy was driven by the recognition that multiple and convoluted factors contribute to the social exclusion and marginalization of some migrant ethnic groups, including refugees, as demonstrated in overseas (Bronstein,  ; Dimitrova et al.,  ; Goodwin et al., ) and New Zealand research (Kate et al.,  ). One action associated with the strategy was to commission a co‐design project with youth to address the barriers that impede young people from marginalized ethnic migrant and refugee backgrounds' participation in policy development. This article describes the project process and discusses the successes and challenges encountered on this journey of co‐creation. Informed by the project insights, this article also presents a set of principle‐based guidelines that extend current understandings of what it takes to meaningfully engage young people from marginalized ethnic backgrounds in civic processes and better ensure their lived experiences are central to shaping inclusive and just policies.

## Civic engagement and young people from marginalized ethnic migrant backgrounds
Concerningly, a global decline in conventional political participation is particularly apparent among young people from marginalized ethnic migrant backgrounds (Pachi & Barrett,  ). The “civic engagement gap” (Phan & Kloos,  , p. 59) between marginalized ethnic migrant groups and those identifying with the dominant culture has led to stipulation from researchers that lack of trust, sense of belonging, and self‐efficacy underlies disengagement. However, Phan and Kloos argue that the “gap” is due to different understandings and conceptualizations of civic engagement which, in the literature to date, have been predominantly based on White, Western, and adult views. The authors argue that a broad, ecological view of civic engagement, considering systemic and structural barriers, as well as attitudes and behaviors, would better explain the differing civic engagement patterns between young people from marginalized ethnic migrant backgrounds and those identifying with the dominant culture. A stronger focus on the lived experience perspectives of young people from marginalized ethnic migrant backgrounds may point to new avenues for systemic intervention that produce more empowering conditions for those young people, as opposed to the individualized, victim‐blaming solutions commonly put forward (Phan & Kloos,  ).

## Participation barriers for young ethnic migrants
Systemic intervention to address the youth civic engagement gap cannot overlook the barriers to participation created by the profound loss of social and cultural capital commonly experienced by recently arrived migrants from marginalized ethnic backgrounds. Language proficiency challenges and difficulties getting existing work experience and qualifications recognized compromise opportunities for economic mobility. Often, this results in under‐employment and settlement in low‐income neighborhoods that have long‐term effects on family stability and security and contribute to entrenched inequality (Goodwin et al., ; Kate et al.,  ). Communication difficulties, lack of culturally responsive services, general mistrust of government authorities, and firsthand experiences of racism and discrimination contribute to fear of speaking out, secrecy, and defensiveness within marginalized ethnic communities. These factors, combined with concomitant information marginalization, can curtail individuals' agency to seek support, extend social connections, and participate in civic activities (Bronstein,  ).
Migration challenges also have different complexities for young people, many of whom face immense pressure to honor the sacrifices their parents made to provide their families with better opportunities or a safer life in a new country. This can lead to young people needing to act as go‐betweens to communicate on behalf of older family members, negotiate for their families' needs, and secure work to contribute financially (Mackay,  ; Nunn et al.,  ). New family responsibilities, confronting and confusing cultural value clashes, and the additional time required to learn a new language and to make up for educational disruptions caused by the resettlement process can limit time for socialization and contribute to isolation and mental health concerns (Kate et al.,  ; Mackay,  ).
Civic engagement opportunities can confer a wide range of developmental and well‐being benefits, possibly mitigating isolation and mental health risks, if young people are effectively supported to participate (Zeldin et al.,  ). Civic engagement opportunities grow knowledge and skills that are transferable to education and employment contexts. They can also be sites for networking with influential adults that offer bridging capital for vertical mobility (Zeldin et al.,  ). In bestowing personal resources for social and economic mobility, the provision of civic participation opportunities to young people who are marginalized due to systemic barriers and stigmatized identities can support equity aims. Targeted involvement of marginalized young people also increases the likelihood that their lived experiences are considered within mainstream decision‐making structures. Accordingly, the civic engagement of young people from marginalized ethnic migrant backgrounds offers avenues to increase their sense of belonging, which can drive upstream effects for the other family members and, ultimately, contribute to increased national social cohesion.
Unfortunately, the representation of diverse lived experiences in such opportunities remains a consistent hurdle in the youth participation field (Augsberger et al.,  ; McMellon & Tisdall,  ). Commonly deemed too hard to reach, “seldom heard” (Kelleher et al.,  , p. 1), groups of young people, including those from marginalized ethnic migrant backgrounds, are often excluded from opportunities to share their perspectives, have influence, and gain valuable skills and connections to advance their position in society (Augsberger et al.,  ; Kelleher et al.,  ). Time and resource constrained participation initiatives conceptualized by adults and implemented within rigid institutional structures tend to favor older young people who are visible, vocal, and already succeeding in those systems (Augsberger et al.,  ; Kelleher et al.,  ). In addition, it is rare that adult decision‐makers are held to account by demonstrating how youth input was considered. So when young people do participate, they can end up feeling tokenized and further alienated from future participation opportunities (Kelleher et al.,  ; McMellon & Tisdall,  ). Traditional youth participation initiatives can thus inadvertently reinforce inequality (Augsberger et al.,  ) and drive growing mistrust among marginalized young people that threatens their civic engagement.

## Adultism as a root cause of youth alienation
Adultism (i.e., the implicit or overt undermining attitudes and beliefs that adults hold towards young people's capabilities and vulnerabilities) contributes to barriers young people face in decision‐making processes and limits young people's participation and agency (Augsberger et al.,  ; Kennedy et al.,  ). Adultism often has racialized dimensions, leading to greater control of young people from marginalized ethnic groups (Kennedy et al.,  ). Mistrust of adult authority is also more likely to be higher among young people who feel marginalized by current systems or come from countries with visible corruption. Fear of speaking up and suspicion about hidden agendas can impede young people from marginalized ethnic migrant backgrounds' authentic participation (Mackay,  ). Additionally, the espoused values of youth participation in liberal democracies that promote a more equal footing between young people and adults (and among youth of different genders) can clash with cultural values based on patriarchal hierarchies, which may create conflict within the young person's home life (Holm,  ; Mackay,  ). Language barriers and immediate family, education, and financial pressures further limit the time and energy new migrant and refugee young people from marginalized ethnic backgrounds can devote to voicing their experiences and perspectives on issues affecting their collective well‐being (Mackay,  ). There is a need for civic engagement opportunities that involve young people from marginalized ethnic migrant backgrounds in co‐creating solutions to better address these barriers and provide benefits that address their personal and familial needs. However, the approach to the co‐creation process itself must also address their needs.

## Methods to increase public participation in addressing wicked problems
Community‐Based Participatory Research (CBPR) is a methodology that offers process‐focused guidance on how to engage community members, stakeholders, and researchers in all stages of a collaborative project to leverage co‐learning and mutual ownership of the research process (Minkler & Wallerstein,  ). The methods utilized in CBPR can vary widely, but where the shared goals of such projects focus on the co‐creation of novel solutions to address social needs, they may also involve social innovation (Mulgan,  ). Social innovation and CBPR overlap in their focus on collaboration, valuing stakeholder and community knowledge, shared decision‐making, and ownership. However, social innovation has an explicit commitment to design new services, products, or policies that aim to foster sustainable change in complex systems. In leveraging approaches from the domains of design, product development, and service delivery optimization, social innovation provides methodologies that take valuable CBPR insights into the co‐creation of novel and compelling solutions (Zivkovic,  ). Critically, both approaches are, by definition, only effective if they engage the community and other stakeholders.
Unfortunately, such engagement can be challenging when the broader context positions the goal of a project as an impossible or wicked problem. Perception of a problem as “wicked” can lead to “a sense of overwhelm and stuckness” (Rittel & Webber,  , p. 14), which may prevent or demotivate community participation and thwart the transformative potential of these approaches. By drawing on systems‐ and design‐thinking approaches to identify feasible solutions to specific parts of complex problems that can produce meaningful change (Brown & Wyatt,  ), social innovation methods can foster greater confidence from community members and other stakeholders that their engagement is a productive use of their time and resources, and that change, even in a “stuck” system, is indeed possible.
Reflection is fundamental to both approaches and the development of new initiatives benefits from having evaluation built in from the outset. For example, evaluability assessment utilizes the theory of change to interrogate the design or implementation of an initiative to identify improvements before resources are committed (Trevisan & Walser,  ). Developmental evaluation provides another methodology to facilitate evaluative reasoning when making design decisions in contexts of complexity (Patton,  ). Despite calls for greater intersection between the fields of evaluation, innovation, and design (Adams & Nash,  ), few examples of integrated approaches exist. The current project provides such an example that is also grounded in youth–adult partnership (Y‐AP) and a collective civic engagement opportunity.

## THE INNOVATION PROJECT
The New Zealand Ministry of Youth Development commissioned and collaborated with academic researchers and Innovation Unit to co‐design a solution aimed at increasing the participation of young people from marginalized ethnic migrant and refugee backgrounds in policymaking. The project followed a phased approach that combined social innovation and evaluation streams. In the initial discovery phase, led by Innovation Unit, community consultations and interviews were conducted to gather insights that informed the subsequent co‐design and evaluation processes. While these early activities sit outside this article's scope, they played a crucial role in shaping the project's direction (see Deane et al.,  ). The primary goal across all phases was to ensure that young people from marginalized ethnic backgrounds participated as genuine decision‐making partners throughout the process, alongside adult government representatives, community leaders, social innovators, and academic researchers. Table  provides an overview of the project phases and the participant roles and responsibilities at each step.
 

## Phase 1: Co‐design and theory‐building
The first phase was coordinated by the Project Team, which included six academic researchers and four individuals from Innovation Unit (seven women and three men, three who were youth under 25 years of age and five who were migrants from marginalized ethnic communities, with two identifying as refugees). The primary aim of the first phase was to assemble a Co‐Design Team comprising young people from marginalized ethnic migrant backgrounds, government representatives, community leaders, and Project Team members to collaboratively generate a solution, accompanied by a theory of change, over a series of four workshops. The aspiration was to intentionally structure the Co‐Design team to minimize power imbalances, with a youth‐to‐adult ratio of 3:1.
The finalized co‐design team comprised 14 young people (10 young women, 4 young men) aged 17–24 years (M = 20.92, SD = 2.21), representing ethnicities from East Asia (4), India (4), the Middle East (3), Africa (2), and Latin America (1). Twelve were born overseas (four of whom identified as refugees), and two were born in New Zealand. One community leader (a woman) and two government representatives (both men) from marginalized ethnic communities were also involved. The Co‐Design Team worked collaboratively during these workshops with support from the Project Team to identify the challenges that young people from marginalized ethnic migrant backgrounds face in engaging with policy processes, and innovate strategies to enhance their participation.
Participants who expressed interest but could not commit to the full series of workshops were invited to join the Challenge Team, which provided critical feedback on the emerging innovation and enabled a flexible structure that allowed for a broader range of perspectives to influence the innovation process. Challenge Team participants included 15 women and 5 men aged 14–70 (M = 32.95, SD = 15.50), with 33% under 25 and representing various ethnicities from East Asia, the Indian subcontinent, Africa, the Middle East, Latin America, Continental Europe, and New Zealand. Workshops were designed to be inclusive and participatory, with compensation provided to acknowledge participants' contributions (Co‐Design participants received a $50 voucher per workshop, and Challenge Team participants received a $20 voucher). Participants 16 years and older provided informed consent to participate. Those under 16 years old assented and provided parental consent. The project received approval from the University of Auckland Human Participants Ethics Committee to utilize recordings and outputs of the workshops and survey data for research purposes.
The Project Team, including two youth members who led facilitation, collaboratively designed and conducted four online co‐design workshops. Utilizing Miro digital collaboration software, the workshops combined presentations, interactive activities, and breakout discussions to support individual reflection and group‐based participation. Workshop 1 focused on relationship‐building and introducing participants to the project goals and co‐design principles. Key insights from the Discovery Phase were shared to guide subsequent discussions (see Deane et al.,  ). Workshop 2 centered on ideation, where participants engaged in brainstorming and worked in small groups to generate and prioritize solutions linked to the discovery insights. Ideas were evaluated through a collective voting process, leading to the initial development of prototypes. Workshop 3 introduced the concept of a theory of change and participants, comprising four small teams, refined their prototypes in relation to the desired outcomes and mechanisms of change. These teams were supported by an evaluator who was an academic member of the project team. The prototypes were narrowed down to two innovations: (1) relationship‐building events for young people from marginalized ethnic migrant backgrounds and policymakers, and (2) training for policymakers with performance incentives. The two teams then developed pitches of these innovations for the Challenge Team. The Challenge team provided feedback on these prototypes, with relationship‐building events receiving the most support for pilot implementation. The fourth workshop began with a presentation on the emerging theory of change, outlining the presumed mediators and moderators of the desired outcomes (see Deane et al.,  ). Participants reviewed and refined this theory through group discussions in breakout rooms, focusing on strategies to address potential challenges. A preliminary implementation plan for the selected prototype was developed, including a partnership with Inclusive Aotearoa Collective Tāhono, a community organisation, to support event logistics and recruitment. The embedded evaluator also presented an evaluation questions menu derived from the theory of change, with participants prioritizing formative evaluation questions related to event accessibility, participant diversity, and effectiveness in increasing understanding between young people and government representatives.

## Phase 2: Implementation planning and logic analysis
Following Workshop 4, participants were invited to join the Implementation Team, which was tasked with turning the prototype into actionable plans for piloting. This team was composed of members from the Co‐Design and Challenge Teams, staff from Inclusive Aotearoa Collective Tāhono, and Innovation Unit. This diverse group of stakeholders, including young people, social innovators, and community leaders, met regularly to coordinate event planning, recruitment, and logistics.
A youth‐led arm of the Implementation Team, called the “Fun Committee” was formed to spearhead the design and facilitation of the pilot events. This 8‐person committee, supported by a staff member from Innovation Unit, met weekly and took charge of crafting the event content and ensuring activities were engaging and relevant to the target audience. Their work was reviewed and refined in collaboration with the adult members of the Implementation Team, who handled operational details like marketing and venue logistics.
To ensure the proposed innovation was grounded in evidence, a logic analysis (see Brousselle & Champagne,  ) was conducted as an evaluation approach to validate the theory of change and identify useful modifications and potential risks through a review of existing literature on youth development and participation (findings available in Deane et al.,  ). These insights were presented to the Implementation Team to guide risk mitigation strategies and refine the approach before committing resources to the pilot phase.

## Phase 3: Testing and evaluative feedback
The Implementation Team tested the innovation through three pilot events designed to foster dialog between ethnic youth and policymakers. Each event aimed to build understanding and promote civic engagement, with minor, iterative modifications made to improve attendance and engagement. The first event, held in person in an urban location, centered discussions on national identity, climate change, and poverty, highlighting intergenerational perspectives. The second event, conducted online, focused on dispelling myths that hinder communication. The third event was in person in a suburban location and aimed to accommodate school schedules and centered on discussions about positive social change and civic engagement.
In total, 52 people participated in the three events, including 26 young people from marginalized ethnic migrant backgrounds and 26 adults. Of the youth participants, seven were members of the Fun Committee who also facilitated activities. Attendees included individuals who had been involved in earlier project phases as well as new government representatives and community members. An anonymous formative evaluation questionnaire was conducted using a questionnaire informed by the interests of the project participants focused on event accessibility, participant demographics, and the effectiveness of the events. Twenty participants responded (38.46% response rate, of which 65% were from young people). Responses indicated the events largely achieved their aim of bringing together ethnic young people and policymakers to build relationships and share perspectives in a fun, safe, and welcoming space. However, challenges included balancing contributions between young people and adults and addressing insensitive questions.

## Phase 4: Project synthesis and development of the framework
In the final phase, the Project Team synthesized all project findings to produce a summary report with a framework for involving young people from marginalized ethnic migrant backgrounds in policymaking (available in Deane et al.,  ). This framework integrated insights from the event series innovation, the youth‐led Fun Committee, and the logic analysis. The intent was to create a practical and adaptable model for future initiatives aimed at increasing youth participation in policy development. The project process and findings were presented to all interested stakeholders.

## FINDINGS
Throughout the project, participants were encouraged to provide anonymous feedback via Miro boards and surveys for project refinement and future consideration. In addition, the Project Team debriefed regularly to reflect on the emerging successes and challenges, including how well genuine and diverse youth participation was being supported. These reflections, combined with participant and logic analysis insights, resulted in several key themes, which are summarized next. Participant quotes are included to provide context, with roles and ages (for youth) noted.

## The need for and value of authentic Y‐APs
The majority of the young ethnic co‐designers expressed motivation to be involved in the project because there are few opportunities for the perspectives of young people from marginalized ethnic migrant backgrounds to be heard. Several young co‐designers conveyed that they wanted a different future to what they had experienced for young people from their ethnic communities. They were quick to confirm the insights generated from the Discovery Phase resonated with their experiences of both having the desire to have their voices heard and be part of creating positive change for young people within their ethnic communities, while at the same time confronting numerous barriers, including exhaustion that comes from fighting to be seen as more than a migrant. “Ethnic people are so tired! AMEN,” read one anonymous Co‐Designer comment on a Miro Board in response to the presentation of Discovery insights. An 18‐year‐old female Co‐Designer also shared, “I kind of realized … in high school … that people can get involved in decision‐making processes but it's just really out of reach for a lot of ethnic young people, and a lot of our voices are not heard.”
As the project progressed, the Project Team observed high engagement when conditions for authentic youth participation and Y‐AP in decision‐making were cultivated. Conditions included adults stepping in to support young people to participate when needed and in a manner they found comfortable. “I appreciated how you [Project Team Members] helped to facilitate our ideas … put less pressure onto us to talk/write, etc., which takes a lot of energy” was another anonymous comment on a Miro board. Meaningful connection and perspective‐sharing between young people and adults in varied but influential community and government roles also enhanced engagement. For example, all were energized by the Challenge Team session, where the young co‐designers stepped into a high‐pressure situation to pitch their prototype ideas and subsequently felt listened to, respected, and received highly constructive feedback from powerful people in government and community. A 23‐year‐old Co‐Design Team member who also works in government shared:
I loved how there was various people engaged. We had people who like do this as a consultant, and an analyst themself, and people from the community and those were very valuable to have. I think some key themes that came across to me were the same ones that we shared. We don't want this to be tokenistic, we don't want this to be just another check box…
An adult Project Team member also noted “having [a Senior Politician] there really helped to elevate the significance of the project.”

## Addressing unequal power dynamics through peer support and reflective spaces
Creating an enabling environment for Y‐APs was an intentional and ongoing consideration in Project Team debriefs. Despite this, unequal power dynamics among younger and older, more educated youth and adults stifled the participation of young people in more minoritized positions. As one adult project member noted,
…the engagement from our young people is getting tunneled down to the ones who are most engaged, the most active … it's the “star kids” … How do we make sure, you know, no young person is left behind? What are the extra efforts we need to undertake to make sure that happens? … it still ends up being male dominated so often…
Examples of adultism were also commonly observed. The Implementation Team realized early on in its establishment that the younger ethnic members of the group needed a separate youth‐focused space that was less formal than full Implementation Team meetings focused on the event logistics because those meetings were rushed, focused on contracted deliverables, and driven by adult agendas. Feedback passed on through one adult member who was well connected to the youth co‐designers indicated that the young people did not feel comfortable speaking up under those conditions. Further, there was limited time to provide capability building for the adults needing to upskill in participatory facilitation processes. Creating a separate youth‐led branch provided a solution to maintain a Y‐AP that enabled the young people to have greater influence.
At other times in the project, the opposite occurred, and adults deferred all decision‐making to young people without offering useful input and expertise that would have benefited young people. In response to one of the test events, an anonymous evaluation survey respondent noted, “Adults couldn't provide their ideas and didn't have the opportunity to take things further.” An event attendee also remarked in the evaluation survey that “some questions and things said felt insensitive and disheartening,” suggesting there is a risk of harm when individuals involved in partnership activities are not equipped with skills and knowledge to support safe, strengths‐based, and responsive engagement.
However, we had success in leveraging the skills of the youth facilitators on the Project Team, who shared lived experiences of migration with other youth members. Having peers who effectively modeled youth leadership and proactively reached out to support the less vocal young members, as well as the Project team's agility in making changes to discussion groups to redress unequal power dynamics, proved to be influential. As one youth facilitator noted:
I was really happy with my break out room … I popped in and was like “Hey guys, what's up?” And they were like, “Ok [Speaker name], we don't know what [academic evaluator] just said. She spoke so fast, like we were listening, but it was a lot so can you just help us out for a second?” And I felt really happy that we had developed the space where they could say that and not feel weird or awkward…

## The challenge of involving seldom heard young people
The project approach was effective in drawing participation from early to late adolescents in the Fun Committee and event series, and late adolescents and emerging adults throughout the project phases. In addition, the youth participants identified with a range of marginalized ethnic communities and the majority were born outside of New Zealand. Few were new migrants, however, and a limited number of participants migrated to New Zealand as refugees. A young Co‐Design Team member anonymously reflected on a Miro board, “I wonder if we are an accurate representation of the ethnic young people community?” Similarly, an anonymous survey respondent noted, “…the people that attended were not very diverse, I was expecting people from other parts of Auckland and the country, but the hui [gathering] hadn't reached other areas.” Concern about needing more diverse participation was a theme throughout the project.

## The consequential effects of time and resourcing
We noted levels of energy deflated and frustrations were expressed when parts of the process felt rushed, as one Co‐Designer Team member anonymously commented, “I wish we had more time to co‐design the prototypes. I found that it was a bit rushed and therefore didn't allow for nuance (which is key) to come across.” In commenting on how we might address the lack of confidence or cultural safety potentially restricting the contributions of some younger ethnic women co‐designers, compared to their more vocal male peers, an adult Project Team member noted, “it's usually a long‐term thing too, it's not something that we can do in one workshop or two.”
With regard to resourcing to provide appropriate and equitable compensation for both youth and adults, young people and adults received the same honorarium amounts and participated outside of normal business and school hours during the design phase. Fun Committee members were employed on casual contracts and remunerated for their time. Nevertheless, one young Co‐Design Team member lamented the fact that the workshops required time off part‐time work over several weekends and implied that, overall, participation was a financial cost. The benefits of youth participation opportunities need to outweigh the costs for young people who are grappling with financial and other pressures.

## DISCUSSION
In this article, we argue that national social cohesion relies on the meaningful engagement of young people in civic processes and, as affirmed by others (Zeldin et al.,  ), youth civic engagement requires Y‐APs where adults make space for young people at decision‐making tables and take on board the lived experience expertise they bring. Unfortunately, conventional approaches to civic engagement frequently fail to engage young people from marginalized ethnic migrant backgrounds. To redress the systemic exclusion of young people from marginalized ethnic migrant backgrounds in New Zealand policymaking, a Project Team was commissioned by the New Zealand Government to innovate a solution in partnership with young people from marginalized ethnic migrant backgrounds that was grounded in their lived experiences and supported by existing evidence. The approach we undertook was based on an integration of social innovation and evaluation methods, and the project process generated valuable learnings about effectively partnering with young people from marginalized ethnic migrant backgrounds that have been translated into a set of principle‐based guidelines (see Table  ). The guidelines are likely to have broad applicability for the global youth participation field, thus we offer them to others interested in designing inclusive participation opportunities for marginalized young people.
 The following discussion summarizes the advantages of the integrative innovation and evaluation approach we followed in terms of how it facilitates civic engagement in the face of “wicked” problems. It also highlights three key practice implications based on the limitations of our approach and relevant research literature. We contend that consideration of these implications is fundamental to the meaningful inclusion of marginalized young people in collective civic engagement opportunities.

## Collective civic engagement through integrative innovation and evaluation methods
Contemporary models of public governance recognize that government policy and service delivery are more effective when policymakers and professionals draw on the lived experience expertise of service users and other members of the public and involve them in decision‐making throughout the design, implementation, and evaluation process (Cullingworth et al.,  ). This type of public service co‐production (or co‐creation) requires negotiation of complex relational dynamics to dismantle the social hierarchies that create inherent power differences between the actors within such partnerships; however, the mechanisms through which this can be achieved are not well understood because process‐focused descriptions have been lacking (Cullingworth et al.,  ). Initiatives that involve collaboration with marginalized community groups, in particular, require processes that enable policymakers and professionals to connect empathically with their lived experience expertise and cultivate the collective efficacy required to motivate sustained engagement.
Unlike traditional research and development approaches that rely on distant “expert” sources, social innovation involves methods that draw on design‐thinking and explicitly build in techniques to facilitate empathy with the affected users' lived experiences (Adams & Nash,  ). Social innovation also provides methods and interlocking sets of processes to effect larger change by focusing on one aspect of a complex or wicked problem, mitigating perceptions that the problem is insurmountable. In this way, social innovation can uncover practical intervention pathways that foster confidence and engagement from community members and other stakeholders (Zivkovic,  ). The redistribution of power among co‐designers facilitates ownership of the ideas and continued investment, as we saw with the young people who stayed committed through the implementation and testing phases of this project.
Due to the facilitative design process, project constraints, challenges, and limitations surfaced quickly. Although the time constraints were frustrating for participants, design‐thinking emphasizes the need to hold ideas lightly, to value learnings from 'failing' quickly, and to continue iterating in a rapid and agile way (Adams & Nash,  ). Integration of evaluation methods and evaluative reasoning supported critical reflection and the use of evidence to identify adaptations and enhancements to address the limitations of the prototyping and pilot‐testing phases. The phased and integrated project structure is shared in hopes that it will benefit others working in the youth participation space (see Table  ). Nevertheless, we also encountered limitations that could be better mitigated in the future.

## The need for genuine buy‐in from young people and adult decision‐makers
Participatory approaches are only effective insofar as they genuinely engage those who are affected by the problem under investigation; however, the effectiveness of such approaches is also contingent on engagement from those who are better positioned to affect the problem. Despite buy‐in from a group of young people who stayed committed throughout the current project, conditions for participation, ownership, and implementation may be different for different stakeholders. Individual sense of ownership will not be sufficient to support implementation if there is no buy‐in from those with ultimate decision‐making ability. Cullingworth et al.'s ( ) examination of three co‐production case studies involving young people with offending histories in a public service partnership to design a youth crime reduction intervention emphasized the essential role of public service professionals in managing and coordinating the co‐production process. Augsberger et al. ( ) also found that regular contact with influential decision‐makers increased the perceived sense of resonance the young people's ideas had with local government in one youth participation initiative.
Although rapid testing during the prototyping and pilot phases can surface useful insights, youth participation projects that are rushed and lack capable staff members because of insufficient time frames and poor resourcing can compromise the engagement and psychological safety of youth participants, the wellbeing of staff (Holm,  ), and the success of the project (Goodwin et al.,  ). The resourcing requirements for authentic co‐designed Y‐AP projects are likely to be larger than traditional youth participation opportunities because building the necessary capabilities in both adults and young people requires regular contact over an extended period of time (Holm,  ). Remunerating youth for their time and expertise to be involved in projects where adults are participating as part of an employment role is another noted feature of successful Y‐APs (Goodwin et al.,  ; Holm,  ). Finally, if young people have invested heavily in the innovation process and developed a sense of ownership and hope that the innovation will have a genuine impact, resourcing that does not extend beyond the pilot tokenizes young people's efforts and may diminish the sense of empowerment gained through the process.

## Responding to adultism
The low participation of policymakers in this project was likely due to workload pressures, other priorities, and scheduling of events outside of business hours. In one case example of the youth crime prevention co‐production initiative in Glasgow, Cullingworth et al. ( ) also discovered that the competing priorities of public service professionals, who were accustomed to working within rigid public service delivery models, compromised the engagement of youth members because the professionals struggled to embrace the flexible and power‐flattening philosophy of co‐production. Institutional systems and settings that prevent the meaningful involvement of young people are an expression of adultism (Augsberger et al.,  ). Further, young people should not be expected to volunteer their limited time outside of other important commitments, while adults get paid to participate as part of their role during business hours.
Adultism is evident when adults overtake control, often in fear of project failure; however, it is also seen in feigned “age‐blindness” (Hall,  , p. 1301) that sees adults leaving young people to lead with limited support that ultimately sabotages the endeavor's success. Y‐APs require input from both sides of the partnership and have been found to work best when adults engage in a scaffold‐and‐fade approach that provides structure and direction when needed, then fades away when it is not (Hall,  ). Capacity sharing, rather than capacity building, recognizes that everyone has something to offer. If adults with relevant expertise are not active in the process, then this leaves young people addressing complex issues on their own. Effective Y‐APs, therefore, involve mutual input, scaffolding of skills, co‐governance, and co‐facilitation (Augsberger et al.,  ; Zeldin et al.,  ). The importance of carefully cultivating the partnership cannot be stressed enough because Y‐APs are “an active ingredient” (Zeldin et al.,  , p. 393) of youth civic engagement.
Addressing adultism also requires self‐reflective processing and intentional efforts to build awareness and regulation strategies (Augsberger et al.,  ; Holm, ). Separate reflective spaces for young people and adults, facilitated by peer mentors, can foster safe communication of shared reflections. Moreover, it is not uncommon for adults to lack the capabilities to partner safely and effectively with youth (Goodwin et al.,  ), thus, mentoring and training opportunities must focus on both young people and adults (Holm,  ). Shared lived experiences are not enough to equip adults with the requisite knowledge and skills related to positive youth development, participatory approaches, trauma‐informed practices, and cultural responsiveness (Holm,  ).

## Empowering young ethnic migrants through inclusive engagement
The project approach to empowering young people from marginalized ethnic migrant backgrounds by giving them direct and meaningful involvement in the co‐creation of an innovation to better meet their needs demonstrates how targeted efforts can dismantle barriers and amplifying seldom heard voices and perspectives. However, the importance of additional outreach and support is paramount to ensure that marginalized young people who are also new to a host country have access to the resources and mentorship required to overcome participation barriers and sustain their engagement. Peers from similar cultural backgrounds or with shared lived experienced have been used successfully to bridge access to participation opportunities for marginalized groups of young people who adults have traditionally struggled to engage (Bronstein,  ; Holm,  ). Research also shows that proactive outreach and ongoing support from a “buddy” or ally aids marginalized young people in overcoming access barriers (Holm,  ). This is a feature that should be built into future youth participation initiatives because diverse involvement from young people with varied demographic characteristics, academic skills, and complex lived experiences has consistently been found to be lacking in youth participation initiatives (Kelleher et al.,  ). Lessons learned through this project underscore the need for adults to create empowering conditions with youth to increase the likelihood that opportunities for youth voices to be heard are equitable.

## CONCLUSION
Grounded in the lived experiences of young people from marginalized ethnic migrant backgrounds and enhanced with evidence from prior research, the principle‐based guidelines and phased, partnership‐focused project structure offer promising direction for creating inclusive and meaningful conditions for youth participation. The focus of this project was on the participation of young people from marginalized ethnic migrant and refugee backgrounds; however, the principles and strategies articulated in this article are applicable to other seldom heard groups of young people and different forms of civic participation. We encourage broad uptake of the phased‐process and guidelines by policymakers, practitioners, or researchers who are involved in designing solutions to improve the involvement of marginalized young people in civic processes. Adult decision‐makers need to move beyond giving space for young people's voices and engendering hope for change. Opportunities for civic engagement need to be more equitable, with a range of perspectives recognized and respected, and young people need to see that their voices have resonance beyond tokenistic echo chambers to feel part of a cohesive democracy.
