## Background
Women and children are more severely affected during humanitarian crises, and their vulnerabilities affect their oral health practices. This study aimed to assess the associations between the reported early childhood caries (ECC) preventive practices and maternal education, knowledge of dental caries prevention, and displacement-related variables.

## Methods
This cross-sectional study was a secondary analysis of the data of a convenience sample of 625 mother-child dyads who identified as internally displaced persons (IDPs), refugees, and migrants in five Nigerian states—Borno, Taraba, Yobe, Cross River, and Benue – in December 2024. Children had to be younger than 6 years to be included in the data. Data extracted included study independent variable (displacement status -IDPs, refugees, migrants), dependent variables (children’s daily tooth brushing frequency, fluoridated toothpaste use, and daily consumption of refined carbohydrates in between meals), and study confounders (maternal education level -no formal education, Quranic/primary, secondary, tertiary- maternal age at last birthday, and maternal dental caries prevention knowledge). No clinical dental examinations were conducted. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to determine the associations between the dependent and independent variables after adjusting for confounders.

## Results
Of the 625 participants, 274 (43.8%) were children of IDPs, 120 (19.2%) were children of migrants, and 231 (37.0%) were children of refugees. The age of mothers ranged from 18 to 60, with a mean (standard deviation) of 30.8 (7.8) years. Furthermore, 81 (13.0%) reported brushing with a toothbrush at least twice daily, and 195 (31.2%) reported using fluoridated toothpaste. Compared to children of refugees, children of migrants had significantly lower odds of brushing their teeth at least twice daily (AOR = 0.391; 95% CI: 0.172–0.888; p = 0.025), while children of IDPs had significantly higher odds of using fluoride toothpaste regularly (AOR = 1.909; 95% CI: 1.218–2.990; p = 0.005). In addition, children of IDPs and migrants showed non-significantly higher odds of consuming refined carbohydrates in-between-meals less than three times when compared to refugees.

## Conclusion
This study highlights the multifaceted and interconnected factors affecting reported ECC prevention practices among displaced Nigerian children. To gain a deeper understanding of these dynamics, future research should employ longitudinal and mixed-method approaches that can better inform the development of evidence-based and culturally responsive policies and programmes.

## Introduction
Dental caries, particularly early childhood caries (ECC), is a pervasive global public health challenge, disproportionately affecting socioeconomically disadvantaged and displaced populations [ ]. It is defined as a cavitated or non-cavitated dental lesion affecting the primary teeth of children younger than 72 months of age [ ] and affects over 530 million children [ ]. Between 1990 and 2019, there was an 11.74% increase in new cases of untreated dental caries in primary teeth; the number of prevalent cases rose by 5.89% to 0.52 billion, and the years lived with disability (YLDs) increased by 6.03%, totaling 0.20 million [ ]. The contributions of the growing global humanitarian crisis [ ] to these figures remain unknown.
In Nigeria, over 70% of dental caries in children under 6 remain untreated [ ]. This is attributable to a combination of factors such as limited access to dental care [ ], poor access to and uptake of dental insurance [ , ], low household income [ ], and lower levels of parental education [ ]. These factors may contribute to poor oral hygiene [ ] and inappropriate feeding practices [ – ] that contribute to the high risk of ECC among children in the country.
Children affected by a humanitarian crisis are particularly vulnerable to ECC. The crisis exacerbates existing inequalities by disrupting access to basic healthcare services, including oral health care [ ], poor strengthening of oral health systems in crisis regions [ , ], and intensifying poverty, food insecurity, and forced displacement in Nigeria [ , ]. In these contexts, families may prioritize immediate survival needs over preventive dental care [ ].
Forcibly displaced persons (Internally displaced persons (IDPs) and refugees) and migrants, being products of humanitarian crises, are vulnerable to ECC [ , ]. In Nigeria, over 3 million people are internally displaced due to insurgency and climate crises [ ]. The country has the largest number of displaced communities in Africa and the 3rd largest in the world, with 3.3 million displaced persons in Nigeria [ ]. It is therefore possible that the burden of ECC is high among the population in Nigeria.
In a context like Nigeria, where access to oral health care is poor, ECC management for children of IDPs, refugees, and migrants may need to be heavily focused on modifiable dental caries risk factors such as twice-daily tooth brushing with fluoridated toothpaste [ ], reduced consumption of refined carbohydrates [ ], and timely dental visits [ ]. However, these practices are critically shaped by caregivers’ knowledge of dental caries prevention [ ] among other factors.
A mother’s awareness of dental caries prevention strategies and educational status directly influences her capacity to adopt evidence-based practices, such as supervising tooth brushing or limiting the intake of sugary snacks [ ]. While studies have examined the intersections between maternal sociodemographic factors and the risk for ECC of children [ ], few, if any, have examined how displacement status intersects with maternal sociodemographic factors to shape oral health practices of these children.
This study tries to address this knowledge gap. We investigated ECC-related preventive practices—tooth brushing frequency, fluoride toothpaste use, refined carbohydrate consumption, and dental service utilization—among children from IDP, migrant, and refugee communities in Nigeria. The specific objective was to determine the associations between the reported ECC preventive practices and maternal education, knowledge of dental caries prevention, and displacement-related variables.

## Methods

## Ethical consideration
Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institute of Public Health (IPHOAU/12/2408) and the State Ministries of Health in Benue (MOH/STA/204/260), Borno (190/2023), Cross River (CRS/MH/HREC/023/Vol 1/280 and CRS/MH/HREC/2024/Vol 2/022), Taraba (TRSHREC/2023/APP/034 and TRSHREC/2024/APP/034), and Yobe (MOH/GEN/747/Vol 1). The study followed established ethical standards, including obtaining written informed consent and ensuring participant confidentiality and anonymity. It also promoted inclusive participation by ensuring diverse gender representation. The reporting of the study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [ ]. The primary study did not obtain consent for data sharing. Data will be made available to other researchers who apply for access using a special deposit agreement in line with the requirements of the ethics committee of records.

## Study design and participants
This cross-sectional study was a secondary analysis of data derived from a broader cross-sectional study conducted in five Nigerian states—Borno, Taraba, Yobe, Cross River, and Benue—selected for their high populations of refugees, internally displaced persons (IDPs), and migrants. The parent study evaluated HIV-related service needs among these groups [ , ].

## Study locations
Recruitment sites were determined through state-level stakeholder consultations, prioritizing population density, accessibility, and safety. Data collection occurred in designated camps and settlements across the five states: Borno State at multiple IDP/refugee/migrant camps located in Jere, Maiduguri, and Konduga Local Government Areas; Taraba State at Bali Camp, Gembu Settlement, Kuffia Jalingo located in Bali, Sarduna, and Adro-Kola Local Govement Area; Yobe State at IDP, migrant, and refugee sites located at Geidam, Bada, and Damaturu Local Government Areas; Cross River State at Adagom 1, Adagom 3, and Ukende refugee settlements located at Ogoja Local Government Area; and Benue State at Ikyogen Settlement, Mega, Daudu, and Abagena IDP Camps located at Kwande and Guma Local Government Areas;

## Study population
The data of mother-child dyads of refugees, IDPs, and migrants were extracted for this study. The primary study received informed consent from all mothers for themselves and their children. Only mothers of children and children who were younger than 6 years, and those who gave consent for their (mother and child), participated in the study. Individuals with severe health impairments were excluded from the study.

## Questionnaire development and field testing
For the primary study, data were collected using a structured questionnaire that had been used for similar studies conducted in Nigeria. The survey was administered in English, Yoruba, Hausa, and Ibibio. Local leaders provided real-time translation for other dialects. Translations were performed by native speakers, back-translated for accuracy, and refined during pilot testing. Cultural relevance was ensured through stakeholder consultations and pilot testing of the data collection tool between January 29 and February 2, 2024. Details on the questionnaire development and validation process had been reported in the primary study [ , ].

## Sample size
The data of 625 children aged 0–5 years were extracted from the database. Using Leslie Kish’s formula, the estimated sample size to determine the proportion of children with ECC with an ECC prevalence of 17% is 217 participants [ ]. After Adjustments for design effect (clustering) and non-response rates (10–20%), the sample size increased to 271. The data of 625 children is considered adequate for this study.

## Sampling and recruitment
Non-probability purposive sampling methods—venue-based and snowball sampling—were employed for the recruitment of study participants in the primary study. Recruitment was facilitated by community leaders and peer networks. Participants received referral coupons to engage peers, enhancing outreach to marginalized groups. Mothers with children younger than 6 years were encouraged to refer their peers for the study.
Community leaders supported the recruitment of IDPs, refugees, and migrants, respectively, by identifying trusted individuals within their communities who served as seeds for the snowballing process, providing access to hard-to-reach populations, and offering culturally appropriate guidance to ensure informed participation. Their involvement helped to build trust and mobility barriers and increase participation among underrepresented groups who may otherwise be excluded from conventional recruitment strategies. Data was collected in December 2024.

## Data collection
Data collection for the primary study was preceded by community sensitization meetings and site-level training for community leaders. The community sensitisation program was implemented by the community leaders identified for each of the target populations – IDPs, refugees, and migrants. The community leaders were involved in making inputs into the study protocols. In addition, they were trained on the objectives and the methodology for the study after the study received ethics approval. The trained community leaders then conducted community sensitisation about the study before the commencement of data collection.
The structured survey tool was developed by adapting validated instruments from two key sources: the Nigeria Demographic Health Survey and the community HIV service delivery survey used for collecting Community-Led Monitoring data. The tool also incorporated a previously utilized oral health survey questionnaire employed in Nigerian community-level studies. The combined instrument comprehensively addressed health and HIV service access, essential supply availability, barriers to care, and experiences of human rights violations. The questionnaire was field-tested and refined through stakeholder consultations held between January 29 and February 2, 2024, to ensure cultural relevance and contextual appropriateness. Following adjustments based on this feedback, the tool was uploaded to an online platform and pilot-tested with community members to confirm its sensitivity and applicability before deployment [ , ].
Data was collected by trained field teams using encrypted tablet/phone-assisted self-interviews or in-person interviews using an online platform. Mothers provided the information for themselves and their children after providing written informed consent. Data collection took place at a site the mother found convenient for the exercise. No clinical data were collected. Secured data transmission and storage protocols were followed. Supplemental File 1 provides the details of the study questionnaire.

## Study variables

## Independent variable

## Displacement status
(IDPs, refugees, migrants), self-identified and verified by community leaders. Definitions aligned with standards used in Nigeria [ ].

## Dependent variables

## Oral health practices
The study collected data on the children’s daily tooth brushing frequency, daily use of fluoridated toothpaste, and daily frequency of consumption of refined carbohydrates in between meals reported by the caregiver. Responses ranged from 4 to 7 options per item. The responses were dichotomised using predefined thresholds: brushing ≥ twice a day, fluoridated toothpaste use “always”/“quite often”, and daily consumption of refined carbohydrates in-between meals ≤ three times per day [ ].

## Confounders

## Maternal demographic profile
Maternal education level (no formal education, Quranic/primary, secondary, tertiary), and maternal age at last birthday.

## Maternal dental caries prevention knowledge
This study collected data on maternal knowledge of dental caries prevention using responses to eight Likert-scale statements (1 = “strongly disagree” to 5 = “strongly agree”). Topics included fluoridation efficacy, fluoride toothpaste use, sugar consumption frequency, sealants, post-brushing rinsing, brushing habits, and dental visits. “Strongly agree”/“agree” responses were deemed correct. Each statement scored 1–5 (non-responses = 1), yielding total scores of 8–40. Mothers scoring ≥ 21 were classified as having good oral health knowledge; scores ≤ 20 indicated poor knowledge [ ].

## Statistical analysis
Descriptive statistics summarized categorical variables (frequencies, percentages) and age (mean ± SD). Multivariate logistic regression estimated adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% CIs for associations between the dependent and independent variables, adjusting for confounders. Model adequacy was confirmed via Hosmer-Lemeshow tests (p > 0.05) and variance inflation factors (< 2.0).
The level of missing data was low, with 3.7% missing for children’s toothbrushing frequency, 4.4% for fluoridated toothpaste use, and 4.1% for between-meal carbohydrate consumption. Little’s test for MCAR was not significant (p = 0.270), indicating the data were missing completely at random; therefore, a complete-case analysis was employed as the primary method. Analyses used Stata 18.0, with significance at p < 0.05 and non-overlapping 95% CIs. Results were stratified by displacement status to highlight disparities.

## Results
Of the 625 participants included in the study, 274 (43.8%) were children of IDPs, 120 (19.2%) were children of migrants, and 231 (37.0%) were children of refugees. The age of mothers ranged from 18 to 60, with a mean (standard deviation) of 30.8 (7.8) years. Furthermore, 81 (13.0%) reported brushing with a toothbrush at least twice daily, and 195 (31.2%) reported using fluoridated toothpaste. However, only 9 (1.4%) consumed refined carbohydrates in between meals three times a day or more.
As shown in Table , among the target populations, children of migrants had the lowest proportion of frequent tooth brushing (n = 9; 7.5%), while children of refugees showed the lowest proportion of use of fluoridated toothpaste (n = 64, 27.7%). Children of refugees reported the highest proportion of tooth brushing twice a day or more (n = 39, 16.9%), while children of IDPs had the highest proportion of use of fluoridated toothpaste (n = 92, 33.6%).
Maternal education appeared to influence practices: more children of mothers with tertiary education brushed their teeth twice a day or more, used fluoridated toothpaste, and consumed refined carbohydrates in-between meals less than three times a day.
Children with mothers who had good knowledge of dental caries prevention reported slightly lower rates of tooth brushing twice a day or more (11.7% vs. 14.7%), compared to children of mothers with poor knowledge. On the other hand, slightly lower rates of using fluoridated toothpaste (30.4% vs. 32.3%), and slightly higher rates consumed refined carbohydrates in-between meals less than three times a day (98.6% vs. 98.5%) compared to those with poor knowledge (Table ).

 

Table  shows that the multivariate logistic regression analysis identified significant associations between displacement status and oral health practices among children. Compared to children of refugees, children of migrants had significantly lower odds of brushing their teeth at least twice daily (AOR = 0.391; 95% CI: 0.172–0.888; p = 0.025), while children of IDPs had significantly higher odds of using fluoride toothpaste regularly (AOR = 1.909; 95% CI: 1.218–2.990; p = 0.005). In addition, although children of IDPs and migrants had higher odds of consuming refined carbohydrates in-between-meals less than three times when compared to refugees, the difference was not significant.

 

## Discussion
The current study explored preventive practices for ECC among displaced populations in Nigeria, providing important insights into how displacement status, maternal factors, and structural challenges interact to shape oral health practices in humanitarian settings. And a key finding was the significant variation in oral hygiene practices across displacement groups. Children of IDPs had significantly higher odds of using fluoridated toothpastes when compared to refugees. In addition, children of migrants had significantly lower odds of brushing their teeth at least twice daily when compared to refugees. Refined carbohydrate intake was, however, low across the sample, with less than 4 in 100 children consuming refined carbohydrate in-between meals more than three times a day.
The current study provides the first insight into the dental caries prevention practices of forcefully displaced children and children of migrants in Nigeria, thereby addressing a critical evidence gap of populations often excluded from oral health research. In addition, the robust and geographically diverse sampling ensured representation of varied displacement contexts, improving both the statistical power and the generalizability of the findings within similar humanitarian settings.
Despite providing rich insights, the study has several limitations. The use of venue-based and snowball sampling methods may have biased the sample towards more accessible or engaged individuals, excluding marginalized subgroups with potentially worse oral health practices. In addition, self-reported data are subject to social desirability bias, which may have led to overestimation of optimal practices. The cross-sectional design also prevents the establishment of causal relationships. Moreover, several potential confounders, such as access to oral hygiene products, cultural beliefs, or camp sanitation, were not measured, which may influence the observed associations. Nonetheless, the study’s findings remain relevant and robust within their context.
First, the study’s findings underscore how forced displacement acts as a structural driver of oral health disparities, particularly among children of refugees in Nigeria who demonstrated the poorest ECC prevention practices. This finding suggests that migrant children may face distinct structural or social barriers. Several theoretical frameworks offer valuable insights into the mechanisms through which displacement compounds oral health inequities. Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems Theory [ , ] provides a useful structure for understanding the layered impacts of displacement on oral health practices. In this context, refugee status can be viewed as an exosystemic and macrosystemic disruption. The exosystem encompasses the institutional and infrastructural environment, such as humanitarian aid, camp conditions, and access to health services, which are often weakened or chaotic in refugee contexts [ ]. The macrosystem includes larger socio-political and economic structures, such as systemic poverty, marginalization, and policy neglect [ ]. These layers interact to constrain caregivers’ capacity to practice and support optimal oral hygiene practices for their children, regardless of their knowledge or intent, as well as limit their access to the necessary resources (fluoridated toothpaste, health education, regular dental care) [ ]. These structural barriers are often invisible in clinical or behavioural models but are central to understanding the lived realities of displaced populations.
Furthermore, the lower prevalence of ECC preventive practices among refugee children may reflect a deprivation not only of material resources but of capabilities, the freedom and practical means to achieve health-promoting behaviours [ ]. For example, even if refugee mothers know the benefits of fluoride toothpaste, they may lack access or agency to procure it, thereby diminishing their real opportunity to act on that knowledge, possibly reflecting differential access to oral hygiene products between these groups. IDPs, while displaced, remain within national borders and may benefit from domestic humanitarian distributions and local market access more readily than refugees, who often depend on inconsistent international aid. Furthermore, refugee camps may face greater resource constraints and less integration with local health systems, limiting the regular supply of fluoride toothpaste. This contrasts with IDPs who, while also vulnerable, may face fewer constraints and retain greater autonomy or community integration because they remain within the borders of their own country, which can facilitate easier access to resources and social support networks [ , ]. Also, migrants often benefit from more established support networks, existing ties with host communities, and comparatively easier access to legal and social services than refugees [ , ]. However, despite these relative advantages in social integration, migrant children demonstrated significantly lower odds of brushing twice daily compared to refugees, a finding that suggests distinct barriers, such as irregular living conditions, economic pressures prioritizing survival over prevention, and less exposure to structured hygiene campaigns often implemented in refugee camp settings. These explanations are speculative and based on contextual observation. Future qualitative research is needed to explore the mechanisms behind these disparities.
Taken together, these theoretical frameworks suggest that oral health disparities among the children of refugees in Nigeria are not solely the result of individual behaviours or deficits in knowledge. Rather, they are the outcome of complex interactions between displacement, structural inequities, and constrained capabilities. Public health responses, therefore, need to move beyond information campaigns or behaviour change interventions and address the social and environmental conditions that undermine displaced families’ ability to practice preventive oral health. Integrating oral health into humanitarian aid packages, improving camp infrastructure, and empowering refugee women through education and resource access are essential strategies for redressing these inequities.
Second, we observed that consistently across displacement groups, there was a low frequency of daily consumption of refined carbohydrates in-between meals, a pattern that contrasts with global patterns of high sugar consumption among displaced persons when the consumption is not controlled [ , ]. This may be due to cultural dietary practices favoring traditional staples like grains and tubers over sugary foods [ , ], economic constraints reducing discretionary spending on non-essential items [ , ], and or the prioritized nutrient-dense staples over refined carbohydrates as humanitarian food aid in Nigerian camps inadvertently shaping dietary habits [ , ]. A study among migrants in Ghana, a country with a sociocultural context like Nigeria, found that sugar consumption among migrants was significantly lower than among non-migrants [ ]. Further research is warranted to explore how displacement-specific environments mediate dietary choices in low-resource settings.
Finally, the study generated actionable insights for oral health promotion in humanitarian settings. Findings support targeted interventions such as fluoride toothpaste distribution and community-based maternal education programs—strategies that are both feasible in low-resource contexts and aligned with Nigeria’s National Oral Health Policy [ ]. Moreover, by advancing knowledge on ECC prevention among vulnerable populations, the study contributes to Sustainable Development Goal 3 (good health and well-being) and aligns with the global agenda set by organizations like the WHO and UNHCR to improve health outcomes in crisis-affected populations. The current findings indicate there is a need for the integration of oral health promotion into displacement-related health interventions, with a dual focus on ensuring physical access to essential products and empowering caregivers through education.
In conclusion, this study underscores the complex and layered factors influencing ECC prevention in displaced Nigerian children. To improve oral health outcomes for children in humanitarian contexts, interventions must move beyond individual-level strategies and address the broader ecosystem shaping families’ daily realities. Future research should adopt longitudinal and mixed-method approaches to unpack these dynamics more deeply and inform evidence-based, culturally sensitive policy and program design.
