## Abstract

## Objective
To evaluate gender and geographic representation (within the USA) and assess financial conflicts of interest and industry payments among task force, panel and cross-cutting review group members of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition, text revision (DSM-5-TR).

## Design
Cross-sectional analysis.

## Setting
Open Payments database, USA; National Provider Identifier (NPI) registry, USA.

## Participants
The 116 physicians who served on a task force (n=5), panel (n=83) and cross-cutting review group (n=28) for the DSM-5-TR with information recorded in the Open Payments database and NPI registry.

## Main outcome measures
Demographic patterns among contributors to the DSM-5-TR, including gender and geographic location (USA) and type and extent of industry payments received by contributors to the DSM-5-TR during 2016–2019.

## Results
Women represented 30.2% (n=35) of all DSM-5-TR contributors. Men accounted for 70.8% (n=46) of the contributors receiving industry funding and received 91.8% of all compensation, totalling US$13.4 million. Three states were represented by 43.9% (n=51) of contributors. Of the 116 contributors, 65 (56%) received financial compensation from industry, totalling US$14.6 million.

## Conclusion
Female contributors accounted for less than one-third of all DSM-5-TR contributors and had fewer financial ties to industry compared to their male counterparts. There was a disproportionate representation of contributors in three states, while 23 states were not represented. To promote gender parity and equitable geographic representation in the DSM, sixth edition, the adequate inclusion of male and female contributors, as well as those from various regions of the USA, should be prioritised to increase diversity. Finally, financial conflicts of interest should be minimised and more thoroughly managed in future DSM editions.

## WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC
Women have been historically underrepresented in clinical psychiatry, with female psychiatrists accounting for less than half of all psychiatrists in the USA in 2023.
Notable differences exist among the geographic regions of the USA, such as lifestyle factors, social and environmental determinants of health, and healthcare accessibility that influence the mental health outcomes of communities.

## WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
Women accounted for less than one-third (30.2%, n=35) of all Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), fifth edition, text revision contributors, and three USA states were over-represented by 43.9% (n=51) of contributors.
Men accounted for 70.8% of the contributors with financial ties to industry and received 91.8% of all compensation, totalling US$13.4 million.

## HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY
When selecting contributors for future DSM editions, adequate proportions of male and female contributors, as well as those from various regions of the USA, should be prioritised. Financial conflicts of interest should be managed more thoroughly to minimise industry influences in future editions.

## Background
 Female representation in academia has increased in recent years, yet gender disparities among high-ranking positions remain prevalent. The Association of American Medical Colleges reports that nearly half of all medical education faculty are women; however, women account for less than 30% of full professors and only one-fourth of department chairs. In academic psychiatry departments, female representation in senior positions exceeds the national average, though the percentage of women holding high-ranking positions declines with increasing seniority. Indeed, women have been historically under-represented in clinical psychiatry, with female psychiatrists accounting for only 41.8% of all psychiatrists in 2023 in the USA. 
The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), published by the American Psychiatric Association, establishes a standard for symptom criteria and classifies psychiatric disorders. Often referred to as the ‘bible’ of psychiatry, the DSM is used by health professionals and researchers in the USA and abroad, and it is largely influential in the approval of new psychiatric medications. Previous research on the DSM fifth edition (DSM-5), revealed a disproportionate under-representation of female authors. 
Earlier editions of the DSM have been criticised for insufficient transparency of financial ties to industry. Although financial conflicts of interest are not evidentiary of any wrongdoing, there is a wealth of literature demonstrating that they can create implicit bias and promote pro-industry habits of thought. As such, the National Institutes of Medicine (USA) (now known as the National Academy of Medicine) recommends that guideline development groups should be free of commercial ties. We have previously reported on financial conflicts of interest among the DSM-5-text revision (DSM-5-TR) and found that nearly 60% of authors received payments from industry, totalling US$14 245 551.54. 
It is widely acknowledged that notable differences exist among various geographic regions in the USA, such as lifestyle factors, social and environmental determinants of health and healthcare accessibility. Previous research indicates that the geographic location of upbringing has profound impacts on personality traits, emotional states and community values, and it also acts as a predictor for mental health issues linked with social and environmental influences. Earlier studies demonstrate geographical variations of clinical psychiatric practices in the USA, with reduced use of amphetamines in western states and significant differences in the use of psychotropic medications among individual states. Although the DSM-5-TR is represented by internationally based contributors, geographical representation within the USA has not been reported. In this study, we evaluated the gender and geographical location (USA) of the DSM-5-TR task force, panel and cross-cutting review group members with respect to financial conflicts of interest.

## Methods

## Procedures
In our previous study, the inclusion criteria consisted of DSM-5-TR task force and panel members who were based in the USA and held physician (MD or DO) credentials. For the present study, the inclusion criteria were expanded to include contributors of the four DSM-5-TR cross-cutting review groups (ie, culture, sex and gender, suicide and forensics) and the ethnoracial equity and inclusion review group. The cross-cutting review groups evaluated material pertaining to their specific expertise within each chapter, and the ethnoracial equity and inclusion review group examined all texts to ensure the usage of nondiscriminatory and non-stigmatising language. For simplicity, the ethnoracial equity and inclusion review group was combined with the four cross-cutting review groups in this analysis. The addition of these groups was implemented to encompass a more extensive group of contributors, thereby providing a more inclusive depiction of representation in the DSM-5-TR. Although the American Psychiatric Association does not provide detailed information regarding the duties of task force and panel members, the task force has historically held decision-making authority. 
To determine the gender and geographical location (USA state of primary medical practice) of the DSM-5-TR task force, panel and cross-cutting review group contributors, eah contributor’s name was manually entered in the Centres for Medicare and Medicaid Services’ National Provider Identifier (NPI) registry. The NPI registry is a self-reported database in which individuals input and update their own information.
The Physician Payment Sunshine Act was passed in 2013, mandating all pharmaceutical and device manufacturers to report payments made to physicians and teaching hospitals. Subsequently, the Centres for Medicare and Medicaid Services developed Open Payments, a publicly accessible database that quantifies and categorises payments given by industry to individual physicians and teaching hospitals. The name of each cross-cutting review group contributor was manually entered into Open Payments to determine the type and extent of compensation received during the years 2016–2019. This period was selected to include the year in which development of the DSM-5-TR began and the three preceding years, a period consistent with previous studies and the American Psychiatric Association’s disclosure policy for the DSM-5. Middle initials, NPI numbers and location (USA state) were cross-referenced with the NPI registry to confirm the correct individual was identified in both systems.
Open Payments categorises physicians’ compensation into 10 types of payment: research payments, associated research funding, food and beverage, travel and lodging, consulting fees, other compensation, honoraria, education, gifts, and ownership and investment. For the present study, the research payments category was combined to include both associated research funding and research payments.
Individuals without entry in Open Payments were coded as receiving no compensation, and individuals without entry in the NPI registry were excluded from this analysis. Since Open Payments and the NPI registry are dynamic databases, 14 May 2024 and 30 April 2024, were selected as cut-off dates for no further consideration of data, respectively. The Geisinger Institutional Review Board deemed this not human subjects’ research, and the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology checklist for cross-sectional analyses was used in the creation of this report.

## Data analysis
Descriptive analysis and formulation of heat maps were completed with Microsoft Excel. Other figures were constructed with GraphPad Prism V.10.4.2. A p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results
After duplicate names were removed, 225 physicians were identified as contributors to the DSM-5-TR task force, panels and cross-cutting review groups. There were 116 contributors (5 task force, 83 panel and 28 cross-cutting review group members) who met the inclusion criteria of being a USA-based physician with an active NPI and industry payments tracked in the Open Payments database ( ). Over two-thirds of the contributors were men (69.8%, n=81), and the rest were women (30.2%, n=35), with no contributors of unspecified or another gender identity (X) or undisclosed (U).
 Contributors’ primary medical practices were unevenly distributed across the USA. Just three states: New York (n=20, 17.2%), California (n=17, 14.7%) and Massachusetts (n=14, 12.1%), accounted for 43.9% of contributors (n=51), and 23 states had zero contributors ( ). Sixteen states were represented by at least one female contributor.
 At least one female contributor was included in nearly three-fourths (73.1%) of the task force, panels and cross-cutting review groups ( ). Female contributors who met the inclusion criteria accounted for at least half of group membership in 7 of 26 groups (26.9%) (ie, sex and gender review (12/12), anxiety disorders (1/1), sexual disorders (2/3), obsessive-compulsive disorders (3/5), ethnoracial equity and inclusion (5/10), disruptive, impulse-control and conduct disorders (1/2) and dissociative disorders (2/4)).
 Sixty-five contributors (56%) had financial ties to industry, totalling US$14.6 million. The 46 male contributors (70.8%) received a total of US$13.2 million, which represents 91.8% of all payments made to DSM-5-TR contributors. On average, male contributors received a total of US$287 000, while female contributors received US$72 000. Thus, men received payments from commercial entities nearly four times greater than those received by women.
The most common of the 10 types of payments as listed in Open Payments were food and beverage (received by 93.5% of men, total US$82 792.45; 51.4% of women, total US$13 156.16), travel and lodging (received by 67.4% of men, total US$637 073.99; 28.6% of women, total US$62 708.78) and consulting (received by 65.2% of men, total US$1 035 593.38; 28.6% of women, total US$178 242.45). The greatest difference in the proportion of male and female contributors receiving remuneration was in the honourarium category (6 men:1 woman), followed by education (4.3 men:1 woman) and research payments (3.4 men:1 woman) ( ).
 

## Discussion
In this study, we found that nearly 70% of contributors to the DSM-5-TR were men. Although 30% of female contribution is a step in the right direction, there is still work to be done considering approximately 40% of psychiatrists were women in 2023. The decrease in female representation among high-ranking positions in medical academia has been termed the ‘leaky pipeline’. Consistent with this analogy, only one of the five (20%) task force contributors was female. It has been postulated that a lack of mentorship and networking opportunities for women may contribute to the gender disparities within senior roles in medical academia. Therefore, it is necessary to promote equal representation of male and female contributors within the task force of future DSM editions.
Moreover, previous research has shown that female patients seek psychiatric care and report receiving treatment at higher rates than male patients. It was reported that 63.2% of women reported discussing mental health concerns with their primary care provider compared with 54.2% of men. A report by the USA Centres for Disease Control concluded that 28.6% of women received mental health treatment in 2021 compared with 17.8% of men. The increased proportions of female psychiatry patients necessitate equitable representation among DSM contributors. Accurately representing the patients that the DSM serves to diagnose enhances communication and trust between patients and their mental health specialists. 
There were seven panels in which none of the contributors were women (ie, gender dysphoria, neurodevelopmental disorders, somatic symptom disorders, feeding and eating disorders, elimination disorders, substance-related and addictive disorders, and paraphilic disorders). The lack of female representation among these specific panels is notable, considering that somatic symptom disorders and feeding and eating disorders affect female patients at a higher prevalence than male patients and would therefore benefit from the input of female contributors. This finding is congruent with an earlier report that found that among the authorship of two textbooks widely used in training physicians, Katzung’s Basic and Clinical Pharmacology and Harrison’s Principles of Internal Medicine, where men outnumbered women by about fourfold. 
This investigation also found that female contributors were less likely to have financial conflicts of interest, and when they did, they were of smaller magnitude. The reduction in conflicts of interest among women is certainly positive and is also observed within other medical specialties. Pronounced gender disparities in industry relationships were previously observed for radiation oncologists, where men accounted for 72 and women accounted for none of the royalty recipients. Similarly, the top 10 most highly remunerated contributors to UpToDate received US$56.1 million, and all 10 were men. 
There were 15 male contributors and six female contributors who received ‘compensation for services other than consulting, including serving as faculty or as a speaker at a venue other than a continuing education programme’. Male contributors received 96% of all funding in this category, totalling US$1.7 million ( ). The ‘other compensation’ category encompasses what is known as a key opinion leader in the pharmaceutical industry—‘physicians who influence their peers’ medical practice, including but not limited to prescribing behaviour’. The role of a key opinion leader is rooted in marketing and is widely acknowledged as a conflict of interest. Individuals who have participated in pharmaceutical companies’ speakers bureaus and thus have effectively worked as a key opinion leader should be prohibited from DSM panel membership. Additionally, we recommend that the American Psychiatric Association reinstates its disclosure policy for future editions of the DSM.
Furthermore, it is essential to ensure the perspectives expressed in a diagnostic manual are representative of the population intended for diagnosis. While the DSM-5-TR includes international representation, it is also important to consider the geographic representation of contributors within the USA, given the diverse environmental, societal and psychological factors that impact mental health across the country. Three states, New York, California and Massachusetts, were over-represented and accounted for 43.9% of the DSM-5-TR authors that met inclusion criteria despite only accounting for one-fifth (20%) of the USA population in 2019. It is also noteworthy that the DSM-5-TR had no contributors from some of the more racially and ethnically diverse states (eg, Hawaii and Florida) or from rural states (eg, Alaska and Oklahoma). 

## Strengths and limitations
A primary strength of this study is that it considers the gender and geographical information of DSM-5-TR authors in relation to conflicts of interest, which has not previously been reported. Our investigation serves to shed light on the disproportionate representation of DSM-5-TR authors. We hope this work will increase transparency and promote equity in authorship of future DSM editions.
Similar to our earlier report, the present study used information provided in the Open Payments database and NPI registry, which does not include payment and demographic information for physicians outside the USA. Of the 225 contributors, 116 met the inclusion criteria, and thus it is possible that the 109 excluded contributors were free of industry ties. Furthermore, the NPI registry is a self-reporting database in which physicians are responsible for updating their own data. Therefore, it is possible that a subset of information provided in the NPI registry is outdated or absent.
Although the DSM-5-TR has international representation among its contributors, only USA states were considered for geographical analysis in this report due to the aforementioned limitations of Open Payments and the NPI registry. It is of note that a subset of 20 contributors had multiple states of residency, primary and secondary medical practices located in more than one state and/or held medical licensure in multiple states. Geographical location was determined by contributors’ state of primary medical practice, and it is thus possible that contributors could represent more than one state each. Future work might also consider examining whether there are racial and ethnic disparities in the composition of contributors, or patient representatives, to the DSM-6.

## Conclusion
To ensure the DSM is representative of the population it serves, a diverse group of contributors constituting adequate proportions of male and female contributors should be selected for future editions of the DSM. When appointing contributors, when male and female reviewers have equivalent experiences, opportunities should be provided to ensure the disparities observed here are not replicated. In instances where representative proportions of men and women are not feasible, contributors should be chosen to reflect the patient population affected by a set of disorders, rather than the population of psychiatrists. The implementation of gender parity among contributors may procure more robust perspectives on current diagnoses. Additionally, advocating for the representation of contributors from all regions of the USA will facilitate a more diverse and informed perspective on diagnostic criteria, leading to improved patient care.
Finally, financial conflicts of interest should be minimised in future DSM editions. We uphold the recommendations made in our previous study: individuals who have served on pharmaceutical companies’ speakers’ bureaus should be prohibited from serving as contributors to the DSM, and there should be a rebuttable presumption of prohibiting conflicts of interest among such contributors. When no independent individuals with the necessary expertise are available, individuals with industry ties could consult panels butwould not have decision-making authority on revisions or inclusions of new disorders.
