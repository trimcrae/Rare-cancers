## Abstract

## Objective
This review aims to examine the effectiveness of interventions in increasing faecal occult blood test (FOBT) uptake rates among older adults with an average-risk of colorectal cancer (CRC) and to identify essential components of such interventions based on current evidence.

## Methods
Five databases were included in a systematic literature search for studies reporting randomized controlled trials (RCT) and interventions aimed at increasing FOBT uptake rates among average-risk individuals aged 50–75. Review Manager 5.4.1 was used for conducting meta-analyses and subgroup analyses.

## Results
A meta-analysis of the 20 included studies demonstrated that health promotion interventions led to significant increases in FOBT uptake rates (odds ratio [OR] ​= ​1.55, 95% confidence interval (CI) ​= ​1.30–1.85; I2 ​= ​95%). Provision of information, mailing of FOBT outreach, and reminders were identified as core components of promotion interventions to increase FOBT uptake rates. Among the different significant reminder strategies, a digital message (via text) plus telephone calls (automated and navigator-initiated) had a larger effect size than a telephone call alone. In addition, there was no significant evidence that financial incentives were associated with FOBT uptake. Most studies included a diverse mixture of components, but only a few studies utilized theoretical framework-based interventions.

## Conclusions
Future studies with rigorous methodologies are warranted to examine the effectiveness and understand the mechanisms of theoretical framework-based multi-component educational programmes aimed at increasing FOBT uptake rates.

## Introduction
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a considerable global public health concern, as it is the third-most prevalent cancer and the second primary cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. It is estimated that in 2022, there were approximately 1.93 million new cases of CRC and 904,019 CRC-associated deaths. Most cases of CRC are associated with adenocarcinoma, which develops very slowly (over at least 10 years) from precancerous polyps to abnormal growths in the colon and rectum. , Polyps and early stages of CRC are asymptomatic but can be detected by screening. Moreover, preneoplastic lesions, which will eventually progress to cancer, can be removed using endoscopic methods. , Multiple studies have demonstrated that CRC screening allows prompt treatment, alleviates complications, reduces incidence and mortality rates, and improves prognoses. , , Therefore, CRC screening programmes have been deemed highly cost-efficient and beneficial for public health. 
Population-based screening programmes for the prevention of CRC have been implemented in many countries, such as the United States of America (USA), the United Kingdom (UK), Canada, Australia, Hong Kong, Japan, South Korea, Singapore, and Taiwan. In screening programmes in Asian and Western countries, the faecal occult blood test (FOBT) is typically the primary screening modality. , , Other screening modalities are sigmoidoscopy and colonoscopy, which involve the insertion of a flexible fiber-optic endoscopic tube into the anus to directly visualize the large bowel. However, these are more expensive than the FOBT and thus are typically utilized in opportunistic screening settings. 
The FOBT is a non-invasive, low-cost, and simple test for the detection of invisible blood in the stool. Its subtypes are the guaiac-based FOBT (gFOBT) and the immunochemical-based FOBT (iFOBT) or faecal immunochemical test (FIT). The gFOBT requires three stool specimens for every screening round and may generate false positives unless the patient has made certain dietary modifications. In contrast, the iFOBT/FIT is more convenient, as it requires only one stool specimen and the patient does not need to make any dietary modifications. Furthermore, compared with the gFOBT, the iFOBT/FIT has better sensitivity and specificity for CRC. Patients with positive FOBT results subsequently undergo colonoscopy for further examination and treatment. The United States Preventive Service Task Force recommends that those with an average-risk of CRC should be screened from the age of 50 until the age of 75. In addition, it is recommended that such people should undergo gFOBT or an iFOBT/FIT screening yearly, sigmoidoscopy every 5 years, and colonoscopy every 10 years. 
Evidence suggests that people may encounter many barriers to CRC screening, and that these contribute to low screening utilization rates. , , Several reports have indicated that people are likely to decline screening if they perceive themselves as healthy and as having no bowel symptoms; , , , have a low perceived susceptibility to CRC and consider that they have no family history of CRC; , , or have a lack of knowledge about CRC and CRC screening. , , In addition, people’s CRC screening intention may be hindered if they regard cancer as an untreatable and invariably fatal disease. , Furthermore, some people considered collecting and saving their own stool to be a disgusting procedure that made them feel embarrassed. 
Several previous reviews have examined the effects of interventions aimed at increasing CRC screening participation based on approaches such as FOBT outreach, education, motivational interviewing, navigation, reminders, and digital technology. However, these reviews have yielded inconclusive findings. Moreover, these reviews have been limited to specific geographic areas, , , , , or have assessed the uptake rates of any type of CRC screening tests. , , , , Furthermore, the study designs in previous reviews have not been limited to randomized controlled trials (RCTs), , , and hence the synthesized evidence may not be of good quality. Overall, few systematic reviews and meta-analyses have focused on health-promotion interventions aimed at enhancing FOBT uptake rates for CRC screening among older adults with an average-risk.
Accordingly, the objectives of this systematic review were (1) to determine the effectiveness of health-promotion interventions in increasing FOBT uptake rates for CRC screening among older adults with an average-risk, and (2) to identify and summarize the key elements of effective CRC screening promotion programmes.

## Methods
This systematic review was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. 

## Eligibility criteria
The details of the inclusion and exclusion criteria of the included studies according to the types of studies, participants, interventions, comparison and outcome, are given below:

## Types of studies
Only full-text studies reporting RCTs and published in English were included.

## Participants
Studies were included if the participants were aged 50–75, had an average-risk of CRC (i.e., no history of CRC, polyps, or inflammatory bowel disease and no family history of CRC), and were eligible for population-based screening but were due to undergo CRC screening or whose screening was not up to date. Studies were excluded if the participants had signs and symptoms of CRC.

## Interventions
Studies were included if they examined intervention(s) that could enhance the completion of FOBT. Studies were excluded if they examined intervention(s) promoting a non-FOBT screening modality or only CRC prevention.

## Comparison
Studies were included if the control group received usual care or no active intervention.

## Outcome
Studies were included if their primary outcome was the FOBT uptake rate. Studies were excluded if their outcome was the intention to undergo CRC screening or the uptake of a non-FOBT screening modality.

## Search strategies
A systematic literature search of relevant studies was conducted in five English databases, namely Ovid Medline (R), EMBASE, American Psychological Association PsycINFO, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library, from inception to February 8, 2024. A preliminary search was conducted on Ovid Medline (R) using the following keywords and Medical Subject Heading terms as the subject headings, titles, and abstracts of records: “colorectal cancer,” “colorectal neoplasm,” “screening,” “health promotion,” “health education,” “health intervention,” “faecal immunochemical test,” “faecal occult blood test,” “uptake,” “participation,” “utilization,” “adherence,” and “completion”. The search outcomes were broadened by using the Boolean operators “AND” and “OR” and the truncation symbol “∗” ( ). In addition, the reference lists of included studies were manually searched to reduce the risk of missing eligible studies. The search strategies are fully described in .

## Study selection
All of the identified studies were uploaded to Covidence (http://www.covidence.org). Screening was performed to identify duplicate and ineligible studies, which were then removed. The titles and abstracts of the remaining studies were independently screened by two reviewers (WK and DC). Subsequently, the full texts of potentially eligible studies were examined with reference to the inclusion criteria. Any discrepancies between the two reviewers were resolved by discussion or consultation with a third reviewer until a consensus was obtained.

## Data extraction
Two reviewers (WK and DC) independently extracted data from the included studies with the aid of a self-developed data extraction sheet. These data comprised article information (authors, publication year, and country), study information (study design, setting, theoretical framework, sample size, participants' characteristics, intervention details, and control group), outcome measures (method and time for data collection), and results. Any discrepancies between the two reviewers were resolved by discussion or by consultation with a third reviewer until a consensus was obtained. The authors of the studies were contacted by email to obtain supplementary information, if necessary.

## Risk of bias assessment
Two reviewers (WK and DC) independently conducted a quality appraisal of the included studies using version 2 of the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials. This tool evaluates the risk of bias in RCTs in five domains, namely the randomization process, deviations from the intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. The ratings in each domain are categorized as “low risk of bias,” “some concerns of bias,” or “high risk of bias” based on stipulated evaluation criteria. A study is appraised as having a low risk of bias if all domains are rated as having a low risk of bias; as having some concerns of bias if at least one domain is rated as having some concern of bias but no domain is rated as having a high risk of bias; and as having a high risk of bias if at least one domain is rated as having a high risk of bias or multiple domains are rated as having some concerns of bias.

## Certainty of evidence
The certainty of the evidence for each outcome was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) profiler Guideline Development Tool. The assessment criteria in this tool are risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. The quality of evidence for each outcome is classified as very low, low, moderate, or high.

## Data synthesis
The findings of each study were narratively synthesized to summarize the study’s characteristics, the participants' information, the theoretical framework that had guided the study, and the features of the intervention that had been used (e.g., type, contents, delivery mode, frequency, and interventionists).
A meta-analysis was conducted using Review Manager 5.4.1 to obtain data on the outcome (FOBT uptake rate). Odds ratios (ORs) were calculated by comparing the odds of FOBT uptake by the intervention group with those of the control group to assess the intervention effects. To determine the effect of promotion interventions with a given intervention component, only studies in which an intervention with the given component had been delivered to only intervention groups were included in the corresponding meta-analysis. The unit of analysis was individual participants. Given the variation in the included studies' interventions, the studies' ORs were pooled by using a random effects model in the meta-analysis. Thereafter, a 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated to reflect the uncertainty of the pooled effect estimate. Cochrane’s Q test and I2 statistics were used to evaluate heterogeneity across the studies. A P-value of equal to or less than 0.10 in Cochran’s Q test or an I2 of greater than 75% was considered to indicate high heterogeneity. 
Additionally, subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate the effects of intervention subgroups on FOBT uptake rates. Furthermore, the risk of publication bias was explored by constructing a funnel plot and visually assessing whether it was asymmetric, given that a minimum of approximately 10 studies are required for the meta-analysis. 

## Results

## Search results
The search of the five databases yielded 4496 records. After the removal of duplicates (n ​= ​3123), 1373 records were screened for eligibility based on titles and abstracts. Following title and abstract screening, 162 records proceeded to full-text review, which resulted in the exclusion of 143 records that were conference abstracts (n ​= ​25) or study protocols (n ​= ​12), reported non-RCTs (n ​= ​7), did not meet population inclusion criteria (n ​= ​59), used the wrong comparator (n ​= ​8), used an intervention promoting other CRC-screening modalities (n ​= ​17), or had a primary outcome measurement that was not the FOBT uptake rate (n ​= ​15). An additional study was identified by manual searching of reference lists of records. The remaining 20 records were studies reporting RCTs and were included in this systematic review. The PRISMA 2020 flowchart of the record retrieval and selection process is depicted in .
 

## Study characteristics
The 20 included studies reported RCTs, were published between 2014 and 2024, and were conducted in the USA (n ​= ​12), , , , , , , , , , , , Europe (n ​= ​5), , , , , the Middle East (n ​= ​2), , and Australia (n ​= ​1). All of the studies were carried out in community settings, namely health clinics, general practices, pharmacy offices, and community health service centres. The characteristics of the studies are summarized in .
 Only six studies employed theoretical frameworks to help develop the contents of interventions comprising educational programmes to promote CRC screening. These frameworks were the preventive health model (PHM) (n ​= ​3), , , protection motivation theory (PMT) (n ​= ​1), health belief model (HBM) (n ​= ​1), and precaution adoption process model (PAPM) (n ​= ​1). 

## Characteristics of the participants
The studies recruited a total of 140,949 participants (81,614 in intervention groups and 59,335 in control groups). The sample sizes ranged from 76 to 48,091. All participants were recruited from community settings, were aged 50–75, had an average-risk of CRC, and were either due to undergo CRC screening or whose screening was not up to date.
The participants in 11 of the studies had low literacy, were living in rural areas, and were members of vulnerable or low-income groups, such as African-American, American Indian/Alaskan Native, and veteran populations. , , , , , , , , , , 

## Characteristics of the health promotion interventions
The health promotion interventions were categorized based on their inclusion of four components, namely provision of information, mailing FOBT outreach, reminders, and financial incentives. summarizes the component(s) of the health promotion interventions in the included studies. Most of the studies used multi-component interventions and diverse combinations of components, but some interventions (i.e., usual care) were implemented in both the intervention and control groups. Therefore, some studies examined the effectiveness of promotion interventions including the provision of an information component, , , , , , , , , , , , a mailed FOBT outreach component, , , , a reminder component, , , , , , , , , and a financial incentives component. , Two studies used single-component interventions. , In both of those studies, the intervention comprised the provision of information via face-to-face education with supplementary self-reading materials, namely a photonovela booklet and a DVD or pamphlets. Additionally, the included studies varied in terms of the mode of delivery, the interventionist, and the content of the information provided. They commonly utilized face-to-face and/or remote one-on-one coaching, while only one study conducted interventions in small-group face-to-face settings. 

## Provision of information
The provision of information is defined as the delivery of information to participants using various means to enhance their awareness and improve their knowledge of CRC screening and specimen collection, or to general practitioners (GPs) to enhance their awareness of CRC screening-nonadherent patients and provide tips for patient follow-up. All the studies supplied FOBT kits and instruction sheets for stool collection to participants in both the intervention and control groups. Some studies also provided supplementary self-reading materials (i.e., booklets, pamphlets, or brochures), , , , , educational DVDs, , a video demonstrating kit completion, a motivational narrative video sharing a positive story, or a video/audio brochure on CRC and screening to the participants. The materials for self-reading were photonovelas comprising storylines, photographs, and graphics illustrating the procedures of the FOBT and its benefits and significance for bowel health. One study conducted face-to-face training sessions with the aid of a PowerPoint presentation and educational clips. One study sent an invitation letter with a GP’s name and signature to invite participants visit to the GP’s office to obtain a kit. Some studies sent an invitation letter containing a behavioral economic message or a postcard primer to participants to inform them that they were due for CRC screening and to provide information about CRC screening. One study provided a list of nonadherent patients and tips for patient follow-up to the GPs. In addition to the usual materials and information, participants received small media interventions, such as large-font health messaging and detailed instructions and photos to guide stool collection. 

## Mailing FOBT outreach
Regarding FOBT outreach, participants who were eligible and due to undergo CRC screening or whose screening was not up to date would receive FOBT kits directly by mail. The participants received an invitation letter informing them about CRC screening and a parcel containing an FIT or gFOBT sample container, an instruction sheet on stool specimen collection, and a stamped envelope for returning the specimens by mail. Seven studies delivered FOBT outreach as usual care to participants in both the intervention and control groups. , , , , , , Four studies delivered FOBT outreach only to the intervention groups. , , , The remaining nine studies did not include an FOBT outreach component. Thus, their participants were required to visit general practices, clinics, or pharmacies to obtain FOBT kits and to return specimens via mail or in person to pharmacy offices, laboratories, clinics, or cancer centres. , , , , , , , , 

## Reminders
Reminders are defined as any follow-up interventions intended to encourage participants to return the FIT kit and complete CRC screening after they have received invitations and/or FOBT kits via various methods. Eight of the studies used reminders as usual care and delivered them to both the intervention and control groups. , , , , , , , Nine studies delivered reminders only to the intervention groups. , , , , , , , , Most studies sent reminders to participants who had not returned stool specimens within a specific timeframe such as 2 days to 6 months. Only one study automatically played promotion videos via a weblink delivered via short message service (SMS) 3 months after the intervention started. Participants received one to five reminders via different means, such as a letter only, , , a telephone call only, , a digital message only, , or a digital message plus telephone call. , The digital messages were delivered using either text or SMS. The telephone calls were either generic , , or provided tailored counselling. For the tailored counselling telephone calls, counselling protocols were developed to provide responses based on individuals' feedback with the aid of motivational interviewing. Trained CRC screening navigators , or a community health representative contacted participants who had not completed the FIT by telephone. The telephone calls were “live”, , , , automated, , or delivered via a computer-assisted telephone interview system. Three studies did not include a reminder component. , , 

## Financial incentives
A financial incentive defined as any form of monetary benefit given to a participant to encourage their screening behaviour. Two studies included a financial incentive component, meaning that the participants were given an unconditional gift card, a conditional gift card, and/or a conditional lottery ticket if they had returned their FOBT kit. , The gift cards were worth USD5, USD10, or USD100 (allocated by lottery).
Mode of delivery for provision of information. Five studies involved solely face-to-face interactions with participants during the interventions, , , , , 10 studies included mailing self-reading materials to the participants, , , , , , , , , , two studies involved both face-to-face information delivery and mailing of self-reading materials, , two studies provided both telephone contact and mailing of self-reading materials, , and one study provided information via a digital message (e.g., SMS). 
Interventionists. The interventionists in 16 studies were study or project coordinators (n ​= ​2), , trained administrative staff (n ​= ​1), nurses and psychologists with motivational interviewing skills (n ​= ​1), clinic providers serving as CRC screening navigators (n ​= ​2), , community health representatives (n ​= ​1), research coordinators or assistants (n ​= ​3), , , general practitioners (n ​= ​2), , and clinic providers or interns (n ​= ​4). , , , The interventionists in the remaining four studies were not described.
Content of the information provided. All the participants in the intervention groups received information on the procedures for stool specimen collection and return. Nine studies also provided information regarding the CRC screening programmes that highlighted their importance and benefits. Furthermore, six studies also provided illustrations of the anatomy of the large bowel and information on the causes and clinical manifestations of CRC and associated risk factors and preventive measures. Four studies discussed barriers to CRC screening and supplied strategies to overcome these barriers. Three studies provided contacts to allow participants to seek answers to questions. ## Characteristics of the outcomes
All studies measured the FOBT completion rates within 2–12 months. Seventeen studies used the FIT, and three studies used the gFOBT. , , Following stool specimen collection, the kits were returned to various collection points, including central laboratories, clinics, and health centres, by mail or in person. Nineteen studies verified the return of specimens by reviewing the participants' electronic health records, retrieving data from health centres or laboratories, or checking the receipts of returned kits. Only one study accepted self-reports on kit uptake. 

## Risk of bias
The results of the methodological quality assessment of the studies are presented in . Twelve studies were identified as having a low risk of bias, , , , , , , , , , , , six studies were identified as having some concerns of bias, , , , , , and two studies were identified as having a high risk of bias. , Regarding the randomization process, 16 studies were identified as having a low risk of bias; three studies were identified as having some concerns of bias due to the baseline differences between groups; , , and one study was identified as having a high risk of bias due to inadequate information on the process and allocation concealment. Regarding deviations from the intended interventions, 15 studies were identified as having a low risk of bias, and four studies were identified as having some concerns of bias. These concerns were due to these studies having used unclear analytical methods although this probably had not substantially impacted their analyses of outcomes , , or having had some non-adherence to the intervention protocol due to a high turnover of clinical staff. Moreover, one study was identified as having a high risk of bias as it had used per-protocol analysis, which can increase the risk of bias in estimating the effect of an intervention in an RCT. Furthermore, regarding missing outcome data, 18 studies were identified as having a low risk of bias, and two studies were identified as having some concerns of bias. , These concerns were due to the proportion of missing participants exceeding 5% (largely due to loss to follow up), which might have introduced bias into their results. Regarding outcome measurement, 19 studies were identified as having a low risk of bias, and one study was identified as having some concerns of bias. This concern was due to this study having accepted participants' self-report of return of stool specimens, meaning that the outcome was likely to have been influenced by the non-blinding of the outcome assessor. Finally, regarding selection of the reported result, all studies were identified as having a low risk of bias, as all the findings had aligned with predetermined analytical plans.
 

## Effects of promotion interventions on FOBT uptake rates
All studies reported RCTs with comparison groups. The studies were subjected to meta-analysis ( a) using a random effects model. The pooled results of promotion interventions showed that the intervention groups had significantly higher FOBT uptake rates than the control groups (OR ​= ​1.55, 95% CI ​= ​1.30–1.85; I2 ​= ​95%). The meta-analysis was performed despite the substantial heterogeneity of studies, as it enabled effect estimates from multiple studies to be combined to afford an estimated synthesized effect size.
 As more than 10 studies were included, a funnel plot was constructed ( b). It showed no evidence of asymmetry. In addition, the Harbord test for asymmetry of funnel plots was performed and generated a P-value of 0.355, indicating no evidence of publication bias.
Use of the GRADE profiler Guideline Development Tool revealed that the quality of evidence was low. This finding was due to very serious limitations resulting from inconsistency (high heterogeneity), which downgraded the certainty of evidence by two levels. However, there were no serious limitations on other parameters of the quality of evidence, such as risk of bias, imprecision, indirectness, and publication bias ( ).
(1)Effects of promotion interventions including provision of information
All studies used various means to provide information to the participants to improve their knowledge about CRC screening and specimen collection, as mentioned. Six studies employed theoretical frameworks to guide the development of CRC screening promotion programmes and teaching materials. One of these studies applied the HBM and reported a marked difference between the intervention group and the control group concerning FOBT uptake rates (90% vs 15%; P ​< ​0.05) at 3 months after eight education sessions including PowerPoint and educational clips and pamphlets. The remaining five studies adopted other frameworks, namely the PHM, , , PMT, and PAPM. None of these studies found significant differences in FOBT uptake rates between intervention and control groups.
One study that included a brief informative telephone call along with the invitation letter and brochure reported a significant difference in FOBT return rates between the intervention and control groups (P ​= ​0.017). A study that included tailored invitation letters demonstrated a significant increase in FOBT uptake rates (P ​= ​0.002). In the study by McIntosh et al., the delivery via SMS of a narrative video sharing a positive story and a video demonstrating stool specimen collection to participants also led to a significant increase in screening uptake in the intervention groups compared with the control group (P ​< ​0.03). However, studies that included a postcard primer, invitation letters including the GP’s name in capital letters and a small media intervention reported no significant difference between the intervention and control arms.
(2)Effects of promotion interventions including an FOBT outreach component
The meta-analysis of four studies , , , that used an FOBT outreach component showed that there were significantly greater FOBT uptake rates in the intervention group than in the control group with high effect size, 3.57 (OR ​= ​3.57, 95% CI ​= ​1.93–6.57; I2 ​= ​90%) ( a).
(3)Effects of promotion interventions including reminders component
 The meta-analysis of nine studies , , , , , , , , that used a reminder component indicated that these had increased FOBT uptake rates (OR ​= ​2.04, 95% CI ​= ​1.54–2.71; I2 ​= ​96%) ( b). The results of subgroup analysis indicated the effects of various reminder strategies on FOBT uptake rates, and these results are presented below.
Use of a letter alone (n ​= ​3) did not significantly increase FOBT uptake rates in the intervention group compared with the control group (OR ​= ​1.17, 95% CI ​= ​0.98–1.40; I2 ​= ​40%). Use of a telephone call alone (n ​= ​2) significantly increased FOBT uptake rates in the intervention group compared with the control group (OR ​= ​2.48, 95% CI ​= ​1.45–4.26; I2 ​= ​84%). Use of a digital message alone (n ​= ​2) did not significantly increase FOBT uptake rates in the intervention group compared with the control group (OR ​= ​1.58, 95% CI ​= ​0.85–2.92; I2 ​= ​99%). However, use of a digital message plus a telephone call (n ​= ​2) significantly increased FOBT uptake rates in the intervention group compared with the control group (OR ​= ​5.12, 95% CI ​= ​2.24–11.69; I2 ​= ​85%).
(4)Effects of promotion interventions including financial incentives component
The meta-analysis of the two studies , that gave financial incentives to participants showed that this did not affect FOBT uptake rates in the intervention group compared with the control group (OR ​= ​0.99, 95% CI ​= ​0.85–1.16; I2 ​= ​20%) ( c).

## Discussion

## Summary of findings
This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the ability of CRC screening promotion interventions to enhance FOBT uptake rates among average-risk older adults. According to our findings, promotion interventions resulted in significantly higher FOBT uptake rates in the intervention groups than in the control groups. In particular, promotion interventions involving reminder and/or FOBT outreach components appeared to have even larger effect sizes (OR ​= ​2.04–3.57 vs 1.55); this echoes the findings of a previous systematic review, namely that multi-component promotion interventions might be more effective than single-component promotion interventions. Four main components of the interventions were identified: provision of information, mailing of FOBT outreach, reminders and financial incentives. Overall, provision of information, mailing FOBT outreach, and reminders were identified as key components of interventions for increasing FOBT uptake rates. However, the effective combination of these key components could not be determined. Further studies are warranted to identify which components are most effective in educational programme-based interventions aimed at increasing FOBT uptake rates.
The evidence regarding the provision of information via education session, booklet, pamphlet, brochure, postcard, educational DVD, or video was synthesized. Most of the self-reading materials were tailor-made using appropriate language and levels of health literacy to facilitate participants' comprehension. This might have been due to the age of participants. Thus, developing suitable teaching materials for the target age group should be considered in future studies. In particular, appropriate materials could enhance people’s understanding of cancer screening and modify their attitudes to facilitate behavioral change. One study employed small-group face-to-face educational sessions, which substantially increased FOBT uptake rates. This might have been due to the ability of face-to-face interventions to effectively convey messages and to engage participants in expressing their health concerns in an open manner. However, the study accepted self-reports on FOBT uptake. Thus, the intervention effects should be interpreted with caution, and a more reliable method for verifying FOBT uptake should be used in future studies.
A distinctive feature of this review was that the included studies delivered reminders via different modalities, namely a letter, telephone call, digital message, or digital message plus telephone call. The subgroup analysis showed that FOBT uptake rates were significantly improved among participants who received reminders via telephone call (n ​= ​2), and digital message plus telephone call (n ​= ​2). The effect size for the digital message plus telephone call reminder was highest, followed by those for a telephone call reminder. In two studies, participants received not only text message and automated phone calls but also “live” telephone call reminders from navigators, who provided additional information according to the participants' needs. , Navigators can serve as resource staff to provide further support, guidance, and assistance to individuals who are due for screening, thereby ultimately eliminating these individuals' barriers to screening. In addition, trained interventionists can serve as navigators.
The meta-analysis synthesized the results on FOBT outreach, revealing that mail-based FOBT outreach had a significant positive impact on FOBT uptake. This finding is consistent with previously obtained evidence showing that directly mailing test kits to participants encouraged test completion. This result may be attributable to this approach’s ability to save participants' time by eliminating their need to visit practices or pharmacies to obtain test kits; it also may have served to remind participants who were due to undergo CRC screening or whose screening was not up to date. , , However, FOBT outreach may not be feasible in some countries or regions, such as Europe, the Middle East, and Asia, due to policy constraints and the probable wastage of unused test kits. Thus, interventions may need to be strengthened to ensure that FOBT kits are readily accessible to participants.
Furthermore, the meta-analysis indicated that financial incentives of USD5, USD10 and USD100 may not be adequate to increase individuals' motivation to complete screening tests. Issaka et al. reported similar findings. This suggests that factors other than money may have a greater influence on individuals' screening behavior.
Certain methodological concerns could influence the interpretation of results. Most studies reported reasons for attrition and analyzed their findings on an intention-to-treat basis to estimate the actual effects of the interventions on participants. However, two studies had high attrition rates due to invalid addresses and telephone contacts for participants. , Hence, clear and current contact details are essential during interventions and follow-ups. Moreover, unclear information about allocation concealment and randomization increased the risk of selection bias in one study. Regarding analytical methods, one study adopted a per-protocol analysis rather than an intention-to-treat analysis to estimate the impact of interventions on participants who had completed FOBTs and returned kits. Furthermore, one study did not maintain intervention fidelity due to a shortage of staff. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted with caution.
Adopting a behavioral change theory in formulating teaching materials can provide a framework to guide the development and evaluation of interventions. In this review, only six studies utilized theoretical framework-based interventions. Moreover, only one of these studies, which applied HBM, reported a substantial significant impact on FOBT uptake rates. Compared with other theoretical frameworks, such as the PHM, PMT, and PAPM, the HBM places more emphasis on understanding individuals' perceptions of the threat of a disease, their susceptibility to a disease, and the severity of a disease and the benefits of, barriers to, and cues to action for behavioral change. In addition, the HBM is rather simple and widely used in investigations of cancer prevention behavior. , , In a systematic review, the HBM explained that people with high perceived susceptibility to CRC and high perceptions of the benefits of CRC screening are more likely than other people to perform a preventive health behavior, as they perceived themselves as vulnerable to adverse health outcomes. In contrast, perceived barriers are inversely associated with the desire to perform screening. As only one study employed the HBM and adopted self-reporting of screening uptake, its findings should be interpreted carefully. Further studies are warranted to examine the effectiveness of theoretical framework-based interventions in encouraging preventive screening behavior.

## Strengths and limitations
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to focus on promotion interventions aimed at increasing FOBT uptake among average-risk older adults. A rigorous approach was used to identify 20 studies that reported RCTs. The funnel plot was symmetrical, demonstrating that there was a low probability of publication bias.
However, limitations should be acknowledged. First, our search strategies were limited to articles in English; hence, studies published in other languages were excluded. Second, there was substantial heterogeneity among the included studies due to differences in their intervention designs, modes of delivery, interventionists, and times of data collection. Consequently, it was difficult to compare the effects of interventions between studies, which meant that the evidence obtained was low quality, as indicated by the evaluation conducted by the GRADE profiler Guideline Development Tool. Third, the majority of the included studies had obtained informed consent from the participants before baseline data collection and randomization. Therefore, our findings regarding primary health promotion were mainly derived from studies that included only consented participants. Thus, our findings may not be generalizable to population-based screening programmes in which people are supplied with FOBT kits regardless of their prior interest.

## Implications for practice and research
The review findings indicate that promotion interventions including provision of information, mailing FOBT outreach, and reminders increase FOBT uptake rates. In addition, the findings show that a promotional programme for older adults with an average-risk of CRC can be conducted in community settings, such as health clinics, general practices, and community health centres. Moreover, the findings show that information on CRC and its screening can be delivered with the aid of self-reading materials or face-to-face teaching. Concerning educational contents, these may include instructions on the collection and return of stool specimens; introductions to a screening programme; information on the anatomy of the large bowel, CRC, and the benefits of CRC screening; strategies for overcoming screening barriers; and contacts for enquiry. The information should be comprehensible by the target age group. Furthermore, reminders regarding screening are essential, and a digital message via text message, automated phone calls and telephone call by a navigator appears to be the most effective strategy, as it had the largest effect size. Further studies may consider investigating the feasibility and effectiveness of these intervention components for increasing FOBT uptake rates.
Few studies included in this systematic review applied theoretical frameworks to guide the development of interventions. Thus, intervention mechanisms remain unclear. More rigorous methodologically designed studies are warranted to examine the effectiveness and understand the mechanisms of theoretical framework-based multi-component educational programmes for increasing FOBT uptake rates.

## Conclusions
This systematic review and meta-analysis provided evidence that promotion interventions that include provision of information, FOBT outreach, and reminders increase FOBT screening uptake among average-risk older adults. A digital text message and automated phone calls in addition to a telephone call by navigators to non-respondents were identified as effective reminder strategies to increase FOBT screening uptake rates. However, few theoretical framework-based promotion interventions have been explored. Thus, more rigorous studies are needed to examine the effectiveness and mechanisms of multi-component interventions in increasing FOBT uptake rates.
