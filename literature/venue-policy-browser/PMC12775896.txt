## ABSTRACT

## Introduction
Dengue is a mosquito‐borne disease prevalent in Latin America and the Caribbean. Transmission is determined by ecological, biological and social (eco‐bio‐social) factors. Understanding the knowledge, attitudes, practices and perceptions of dengue's eco‐bio‐social factors among at‐risk populations is essential for dengue prevention. We used the Ecohealth approach to investigate the socioeconomic and demographic factors associated with dengue knowledge, attitudes, practices and perceptions among residents of São Paulo.

## Methods
We conducted a concurrent nested mixed‐methods study between May and November 2024 using an online knowledge, attitudes, practices and perceptions survey, focus group discussions and Photovoice activity. Quantitative data were analysed using descriptive statistics and multivariable logistic regressions. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Qualitative data were analysed thematically. Integration from both method strands used a weaving, narrative approach.

## Findings
Overall, 388 participants completed the knowledge, attitudes, practices and perceptions survey. Having some or a lot of vegetation around the home (aOR 2.63, 95% CI: 1.05–6.60; aOR 3.90, 95% CI: 1.57–9.69, respectively) and never having dengue (self‐reported; aOR 1.80, 95% CI: 1.10–2.94) were associated with high knowledge scores. Higher education was associated with positive vaccine attitudes (aOR 2.04, 95% CI: 1.14–3.64). Women (aOR 1.57, 95% CI: 1.01–2.45) and individuals with three and more than three household members (aOR 2.04, 95% CI: 1.07–3.87; aOR 3.10, 95% CI: 1.62–5.94, respectively) had higher odds of using mosquito repellents at home. Those who earned more than R$6000 monthly had higher odds of having a high perception of self‐efficacy to prevent dengue compared to those who earned less than R$2000 monthly (aOR 2.03, 95% CI: 1.08–3.82). Focus group discussions and Photovoice participants perceived climate change as a risk factor of dengue transmission. The high cost of mosquito repellents was a perceived challenge to prevention.

## Conclusion
Several individual‐ and household‐level factors were associated with dengue knowledge, attitudes, practices and perceptions in São Paulo. There is a need to increase public awareness about dengue and its vaccine. Future research should assess the effectiveness of community‐tailored interventions to reduce dengue risk in this urban, climate‐vulnerable setting.

## Risk Perceptions of Dengue
Based on a scale of 1 to 10, respondents' perceived susceptibility to contracting dengue demonstrated a mean score (SD) of 6.7 out of 10 (2.1). On average, respondents rated the severity of contracting dengue as 8.0 out of 10 (1.8). Respondents ranked the difficulty of avoiding contracting dengue as 5.7 out of 10 (2.4) on average. ## Correlation and Bivariate Analyses Results
The Pearson's correlation test found no correlation between knowledge‐attitude (r = −0.015, p = 0.771), knowledge‐practice (r = 0.055, p = 0.276), attitude‐practice (r = 0.013, p = 0.802), perception‐knowledge (r = −0.018, p = 0.727), attitude‐perception (r = 0.035, p = 0.492) and perception‐practice (r = 0.019, p = 0.713). The correlation and multicollinearity analyses revealed that sex and gender variables had a VIF greater than 10 and were highly correlated (r = 0.955, p < 0.001). The sex variable was removed from subsequent analyses to eliminate redundancy, and the gender variable was retained since it is a theoretically relevant socioeconomic determinant. 3.1.5

## Determinants of High Dengue‐Related Knowledge Scores
Respondents who reported having some or a lot of vegetation around the home had 2.6‐ and 3.9‐times higher odds of having a high knowledge score, respectively, compared to those who reported having no vegetation (aOR 2.63, 95% CI: 1.05–6.60, p = 0.084; aOR 3.90, 95% CI: 1.57–9.69, p = 0.014). After accounting for other variables, those without a previous history of dengue infection had 80% higher odds of having a high knowledge score compared to those previously infected with dengue (aOR 1.80, 95% CI: 1.10–2.94, p = 0.048). Having a high perceived difficulty to preventing dengue increased the odds of having a high knowledge score by 61% (aOR 1.61, 95% CI: 1.03–2.51, p = 0.080); however, having a high perceived susceptibility to dengue infection was not significantly associated with a high knowledge score. Race, educational attainment and monthly income level, among other variables, were not significantly associated with a high dengue‐related knowledge score.

3.1.6

## Determinants of Positive Attitudes Towards Vaccinating Against Dengue
Age was significantly associated with having positive attitudes towards dengue vaccination. Respondents between the ages of 26 and 35 had 2.7 times higher odds of having positive vaccine attitudes compared to those in the 18–25 age group (aOR 2.70, 95% CI: 1.26–5.79, p = 0.032). Respondents who were older than 55 had 1.9 times higher odds of believing that DENV vaccination will protect them from infection compared to the youngest age group, although this finding was not statistically significant (aOR 1.88, 95% CI: 0.83–3.18, p = 0.202). Those who had attained university level education or higher had 2 times higher odds of having positive dengue vaccine attitudes compared to those with secondary level education or lower (aOR 2.04, 95% CI: 1.14–3.64, p = 0.044). Respondents who were self‐employed had 59% lower odds of having positive dengue vaccine attitudes compared to full‐time employees (aOR 0.41, 95% CI: 0.21–0.81, p = 0.032). None of the other variables in the model were significantly associated with positive dengue vaccine attitudes.

3.1.7

## Determinants of Using a Mosquito Repellent at Home
Women had 57% higher odds of using a mosquito repellent at home compared to men and other gender groups (aOR 1.57, 95% CI: 1.01–2.45, p = 0.096). Respondents who were older than 55 had 2.2 times higher odds of using a mosquito repellent compared to the youngest age group (aOR 2.24, 95% CI: 1.01–4.99, p = 0.099). Respondents living with 3 or more household members had 2‐ and 3.1‐times higher odds of using a mosquito repellent at home, respectively, compared to those living alone (aOR 2.04, 95% CI: 1.07–3.87, p = 0.069; aOR 3.10, 95% CI: 1.62–5.94, p = 0.004). Individuals with a family member who had never been vaccinated for dengue had 1.9 times higher odds of using a mosquito repellent compared to those with a vaccinated family member (aOR 1.87, 95% CI: 1.12–3.14, p = 0.046). Having a high perceived susceptibility to dengue infection increased the odds of using a mosquito repellent at home by 81% (aOR 1.81, 95% CI: 1.19–2.77, p = 0.021). Other variables were not significantly associated with self‐reported mosquito repellent use at home.

3.1.8

## Determinants of High Perceived Self‐Efficacy
Adjusting for other factors, increasing monthly income level was significantly associated with having a high perceived self‐efficacy regarding dengue prevention. Those who earned R$2000–R$6000 monthly and those who earned more than R$6000 monthly had 1.6 times and 2 times higher odds of having a high perception of self‐efficacy, respectively, compared to those who earned less than R$2000 monthly (aOR 1.64, 95% CI: 0.96–2.79, p = 0.127; aOR 2.03, 95% CI: 1.08–3.82, p = 0.066). Additionally, those who perceived that dengue was a serious disease had 51% higher odds of having a high perceived self‐efficacy (aOR 1.51; 95% CI: 1.03–2.23, p = 0.080), whereas those who perceived high levels of difficulty with preventing dengue had 41% lower odds of having a high perceived self‐efficacy (aOR 0.59; 95% CI: 0.41–0.87, p = 0.023). Other variables were not significantly associated with having a high perceived self‐efficacy.

3.3

## Perceptions of Climate Change and Dengue in São Paulo
3.3.1

## São Paulo's Artificial Environment and Climate Change
Community members in FGDs noted that rapid urban growth in São Paulo has led to overcrowding and the construction of uninhabited buildings, resulting in vacant lots and abandoned homes where garbage accumulates and maintenance is lacking.
Residents perceived significant climate changes in São Paulo, noting drier winters with fluctuating temperatures, hotter summers with fewer rainstorms and worsening air quality from nearby forest fires. Seasons are not as defined as they used to be, and São Paulo is no longer the ‘Terra da Garoa’ (Land of Drizzle; FGD2, Participant [P] 1). Some residents also highlighted that the inner city has a distinct microclimate marked by ‘greater urbanization, less water permeability, [and] fewer trees’ (FGD1, P6).
Photovoice participants linked São Paulo's changing built environment to its shifting climate, citing increased cars on roads and deforestation for new construction as key contributors. Figure  shows Photovoice participants’ perceived climate changes in São Paulo.
 
3.3.2

## Dengue in São Paulo
Dengue is a serious problem in São Paulo, according to all FGD participants. Community members recalled several past dengue epidemics. The most recent dengue epidemic at the beginning of 2024 was fresh on their minds. Some recalled the dengue epidemic in 2021, and others mentioned the epidemic of 2015.
Despite the severity of the problem, residents noted persistent misconceptions about dengue. Misconceptions that residents heard most frequently in their community included the following: dengue is transmitted by person‐to‐person contact via respiration; any mosquito can transmit dengue; and the mosquito culpable for dengue transmission can only breed in clean water.

3.4

## Perceptions of the Risk Factors of Dengue
3.4.1

## Socioeconomic and Political Risk Factors of Dengue
Residents of São Paulo described that infrastructural arrangements and access to public services were socioeconomic determinants of dengue risk, especially in peri‐urban regions farther from the city's centre and neglected by public policies, such as Marsilac. Frequent water shutoffs in outer and higher regions force residents to store water, creating breeding grounds for mosquitoes. Residents also made links between poor sanitation, inadequate healthcare access and lack of reliable dengue information with heightened socioeconomic vulnerability.

3.4.2

## Climate and Physical Environment Risk Factors of Dengue
Residents linked climate changes and patterns of dengue outbreaks. Increasing temperatures were a major concern for those who understood that higher ambient temperatures ‘decrease the time it takes for the mosquito to complete its lifecycle’ (FGD1, P3). Rainfall was also a concern, since ‘torrential rains cause flooding, which is another place for mosquitoes to breed, meaning more standing water’ (FGD2, P2).
Location of residence was a major physical environmental risk factor for dengue, according to residents. Those living near parks, construction sites, and abandoned buildings expressed feelings of helplessness since ‘it's usually in these places that people throw things away […] and all of this serves as a reservoir [for mosquitoes]’ (FGD4, P1). Residents living in the peripheral areas, such as the Heliópolis and Paraisópolis favelas, cited poor sewage connection, lack of sanitation, and garbage buildup as risks, worsened by public policy neglect. Concerns also existed in affluent neighbourhoods, such as Pinheiros, where unkempt pools (Figure  ) and gardens (Figure  ) are ideal mosquito breeding sites. Living in tree‐lined neighbourhoods near a river, such as the Pinheiros river and near cemeteries, wherein water accumulates in lampposts and flowerpots, were also areas of concern for residents. Figure  illustrates Photovoice participants’ perceived potential mosquito breeding sites in São Paulo.
 

3.5

## Perceptions of Mosquito Abundance and Behaviour
3.5.1

## Mosquito Abundance and Behaviour
Residents indicated a noticeable increase in mosquito densities compared to previous years. Those that lived near a river or vegetated areas perceived a greater abundance of mosquitoes, whereas other residents who lived in apartment buildings noted that ‘there are very few mosquitoes in buildings’ (FGD3, P1). The duration of the mosquitoes has been prolonged over 1 year, according to residents.
Community members perceived that the Aedes mosquito has adapted to the urban environment. They noted that mosquitoes are a nuisance and perhaps have adapted to biting through long clothing. Importantly, mosquito resistance is a challenge that several community members observed.

3.6

## Perceptions of Dengue Preventative Practices
3.6.1

## Preventative Practices
Residents shared methods that they use, or practices that they do, to control the abundance of mosquitoes including: using topical mosquito repellents, insecticide aerosols (Figure  ), incense sticks, citronella candles, electric swatters, mosquito traps (Figure  ), and/or plug‐in insect repellents; covering containers that accumulate water and eliminating standing water; cleaning garbage; fumigating; using screen covers on doors and windows (Figure  ); covering up the body with long clothes; and educating themselves about dengue. Figure  describes Photovoice participants' perceptions regarding their dengue prevention practices.
 Vaccination as a prevention measure was also considered. All participants expressed willingness to vaccinate against dengue; however, older members expressed frustrations since a dengue vaccine is not yet accessible for them.

3.6.2

## Information Seeking and Accurate Dengue Prevention Resources
Disseminating accurate information and educating oneself about dengue were methods described by residents to prevent dengue. Retrieving timely notifications on dengue outbreaks was important to residents. Trusted sources included: official communications from the Ministry of Health or the State Government, and reputable newspapers and television news broadcast agencies. Residents described that they also asked a health professional, researcher or expert to receive dengue information.
Community members expressed concerns about the spread of dengue‐related misinformation through mass‐messaging platforms regarding the credibility of vector control agents and health professionals, and misinformation about vaccines.

3.6.3

## Challenges and Impediments of Preventative Practices
When asked about the challenges that they face when preventing dengue, residents described that prevention is a collective effort and that ‘the difficulty lies in depending on other people for help’ (FGD3, P1). Additionally, the consistency, texture, smell and taste of topical repellents were a concern. Residents also described having allergies to insecticides as a deterrent for use. Frequently quoted was the economic challenge of high prices of insecticides. Equally cited was the lack of access to insecticides at pharmacies and stores during epidemic times.
Residents perceived that mosquitoes evolving resistance to insecticides pose a serious challenge when controlling the dengue vector. Other residents expressed their frustrations about being blamed by local authorities for facilitating community transmission of dengue, which demotivated them in their uptake of prevention practices.

3.6.4

## Government Action for Dengue Prevention in São Paulo
Community members were concerned about the absence and growing distrust of vector control agents. While they witnessed authorities using fumigation trucks in favelas, where transportation infrastructure is inadequate, ‘the fumigation trucks can't climb up there’ (FGD3, P2), leading them to suspect more mosquitoes breeding sites in more impoverished areas. Some voiced that control efforts often happen during outbreaks, encouraging an alarmist and improvised response.
When asked about the advice they would give to authorities to prevent community dengue transmission, residents mentioned the following: carrying out frequent community awareness campaigns to eliminate standing water; reintroducing vector control agents; having free repellent distribution schemes, mainly in favelas; and providing frequent training to health professionals in the clinical setting.
Residents noted that São Paulo's social and economic diversity calls for prevention efforts tailored to each community's specific needs, ‘[taking] into account the particularity of each little piece […] because each place will have its own particularity, and you can't treat the city as a whole.’ (FGD3, P1).

## Discussion
4.1

## Data Integration
We conducted a concurrent nested mixed‐methods study in the metropolitan city of São Paulo, supplementing quantitative data with qualitative data (QUAN+qual). A weaving, narrative approach was used to integrate the data from QUAN and qual methods strands. In doing so, we discuss a comprehensive understanding of dengue KAPP among this at‐risk population. Findings from FGDs and Photovoice were used to build on findings from the KAPP survey to present a cohesive story that describes what was observed about dengue KAPP and negotiates with the previous literature to explain why and how these observations may exist within this urban community.

4.2

## Dengue‐Related Knowledge
Survey respondents generally demonstrated high knowledge of the eco‐bio‐social determinants of dengue transmission, with nearly a tenth of respondents answering all knowledge items correctly. This may be explained by the presence of dengue media coverage nationally during epidemic periods, which may increase public knowledge locally [ ]. This may also be explained by the fact that the convenience sample was generally highly educated, and nearly all of the respondents had heard about dengue.
Additionally, previous research shows that prior dengue infection experiences can influence dengue knowledge [ ]. In this study, respondents without prior self‐reported dengue infection had higher knowledge scores than those previously infected with dengue. Those without a history of dengue may have a reduced sense of complacency compared to previously infected individuals, who might feel immune and disengage from information‐seeking behaviours due to negative experiences with dengue. Relatedly, having high perceived barriers to preventing dengue was associated with a high knowledge score since increased awareness may lead to recognition of obstacles rather than immediate confidence in action [ ].
Moreover, the finding that more vegetation around the home was significantly associated with high knowledge scores was intriguing. In regions where residents actively manage their surroundings to reduce mosquito breeding sites, such as removing standing water, there is likely a greater knowledge about dengue. An eco‐bio‐social study in LAC supports this notion, demonstrating that informed communities are more likely to reduce mosquito habitats [ ]. Alternatively, vegetation may reflect wealth disparities in São Paulo, where the wealthiest boroughs are characterised by a large amount of vegetation, while the downtown and peripheral areas are almost devoid of vegetation [ ]. Therefore, indicators of wealth may mediate the relationship between vegetation and knowledge.
Further, KAPP survey results showed that nearly two‐thirds of respondents knew that Aedes mosquitoes can become resistant to insecticides. Perspectives from FGDs were accordant with this KAPP finding since several participants mentioned that they perceived that the evolving resistance to insecticides poses a serious challenge to mosquito control. This is corroborated by existing scientific evidence that demonstrated that 
A. aegypti
 resistance to temephos and deltamethrin, the main larvicide and adulticide for this vector, respectively, is widespread in Brazil [ ].

4.3

## Dengue Attitudes
4.3.1

## General Dengue Attitudes
Survey results demonstrated that, generally, respondents shared the attitude that climate change is impacting the patterns of dengue epidemics in the community. FGDs and Photovoice participants noted that São Paulo's inner‐city climate and landscape had changed significantly and expressed concern that rising temperatures and heavier rainfall could support vector ecology and sustain dengue transmission. These perceptions are supported by climatological trend studies conducted in the region [ , ], and research linking dengue incidence rate to the changing urban climate system in the city [ ]. Participants also raised concerns about local deforestation linked to urban sprawl, UHI effects and environmental changes that favour vector proliferation, consistent with existing literature [ , ].

4.3.2

## Dengue Vaccine Attitudes
Until recently, Brazil was the first and only LAC country to incorporate the dengue vaccine into the national immunisation programme at no cost [ ], and 2024, the year of this research study, was the first year of its implementation. However, generally, some Brazilians are concerned about, but not opposed to, novel vaccines that are introduced in the program, such as the COVID‐19 vaccine, due to beliefs of a lack of established efficacy and safety [ ]. A minority of others, driven by distrust in vaccines and health authorities and the spread of misinformation online [ ], contribute to anti‐vaccine expression, which is on the rise in the country [ ]. Positive attitudes towards vaccine acceptance in our study were moderate, with two‐thirds of survey respondents agreeing that vaccinating against dengue would provide protection.
Based on regression analyses, a higher level of educational attainment was significantly associated with dengue vaccine acceptance attitudes, a finding consistent with another study on general vaccines in Brazil [ ]. Less‐educated individuals may lack awareness of Brazil's Sistema Único de Saúde (SUS) campaigns, increasing their susceptibility to misinformation, while higher levels of education can support awareness of public health messaging, supplementing a belief system that reinforces comprehension of how vaccines work and compliance with vaccine uptake [ ].
Another factor associated with vaccine attitudes was age, although not significantly, where the oldest group had lower odds of having vaccine‐hesitant attitudes compared to the youngest group. Accordantly, the intention to get vaccinated for dengue was most expressed by older groups in FGDs. Older individuals who recall experiences with vaccine‐preventable diseases in the past may internalise vaccination as a protective and important cultural obligation [ ]. This notion aligns with qualitative findings on trust in Brazil's immunisation legacy [ , ]. Importantly, education may mediate the link between age and vaccine attitudes, since younger individuals are less likely to have higher‐level educational experience.
Nearly a third of online comments about dengue vaccines in Brazil in 2024 contained some form of misinformation [ ]. Qualitative study findings from this study supported that anti‐vaccine expressions and misinformation can spread through mass‐messaging platforms. The observation that self‐employed respondents had lower odds of having positive vaccine attitudes may reflect the reception of false information disseminated on social media. While there is no conclusive evidence in the literature, Brazilian lay press reports have highlighted the spread of misleading content about vaccines among young people and groups that may overlap with self‐employed individuals who often rely on digital platforms for work.
Finally, although previous qualitative studies in Brazil demonstrated a link between perceived susceptibility to dengue and willingness to vaccinate against dengue [ ], our study showed no statistically significant association between these variables in regression models. It is likely that this relationship is dependent on nuanced factors such as perceptions of trust, access, and vaccine safety not evaluated in this study.

4.4

## Dengue Prevention Practices
Older age was significantly associated with using a mosquito repellent at home, likely due to greater perceived vulnerability and prior experiences with vector‐borne diseases. This may be supported by our finding that high perceived susceptibility for dengue increased the odds of repellent use by over 80%. One interesting finding was that women had significantly higher odds than men and other gender groups to practice mosquito repellent use at home. This may be due to women's more frequent medical visits [ , ], greater compliance with health guidelines [ ], and previous exposure to public health campaigns in Brazil which have targeted reproductive‐age women due to the risk of contracting Zika virus from Aedes mosquitos [ ]. Likewise, in many LAC cultures, women are primary caregivers, responsible for family health and household cleanliness, due to assumed social gender norms and roles [ , ].
Further, respondents living with more household members had higher odds of using repellents. Living with more people increases the likelihood of exposure to mosquito bites and the collective risk of dengue [ , ]. Larger households with children, the elderly or other vulnerable members who are at greater risk of severe dengue diseases and hospitalisation may prioritise preventive measures such as using repellents to safeguard these individuals. This may also explain our finding that having family members who were not vaccinated for dengue was associated with mosquito repellent use at home, assuming the individual lives with family members.

4.5

## Dengue Risk Perceptions
4.5.1

## General Dengue Risk Perceptions
Based on survey results, perceived susceptibility to contracting dengue among this urban population was moderate. Individuals that had higher perceived susceptibility to dengue infection had higher odds of using mosquito repellents at home. This finding suggests that those who believe they are at higher risk of contracting dengue are more proactive in protecting themselves. In FGDs, all participants expressed that dengue was a serious problem in their community and explained that their perceived risk of contracting dengue depended on several environmental, social and political factors, including their place of residence and mosquito abundance. Those who lived in apartments in the inner city described seeing fewer mosquitoes compared to those who lived in houses near rivers. Studies corroborate these observations, indicating that environmental factors associated with differential settings influence mosquito abundance [ ] and, consequently, perceived risk of dengue infection.

4.5.2

## Dengue Perceptions of Self‐Efficacy
Survey respondents generally perceived success towards carrying out necessary dengue prevention measures as moderate to high. In FGDs, participants generally described several barriers to dengue prevention. For instance, monthly income level was significantly associated with having a high perceived self‐efficacy towards preventing dengue, indicating that the more financial flexibility an individual has, the more success they perceive when controlling mosquito abundance. As demonstrated by other studies from LAC [ , ], the high cost of repellents is a main challenge for 
A. aegypti
 control. Concordantly, our qualitative study findings highlighted that repellents have become increasingly expensive, and individuals in higher income brackets can afford to prioritise purchasing them. In regions where repellents are unaffordable, individuals may rely on traditional alternatives, such as natural repellents or citronella plants, which could make the role of income less clear in some regression analyses. Other factors such as repellent availability and access and cultural practices could be considered to further illuminate the relationship between income, the practice of using a mosquito repellent, and the perceived success of preventing dengue.

4.6

## Recommendations for Dengue Prevention and Control in São Paulo
Our quantitative study findings demonstrated that community members of São Paulo preferred to receive dengue‐related information through digital media. Qualitative study findings showed that community members trust official communications from the Ministry of Health or the State Government, and reputable newspapers and television news broadcast agencies. Increasing public knowledge about dengue using multi‐media methods of communication through government efforts should prioritise targeting individuals who have previously experienced dengue infection and live in less vegetated neighbourhoods. Digital media campaigns that emphasise the awareness of dengue vaccines should target younger audiences, leveraging education in public health strategies to improve understanding and address concerns about vaccinations in school and work settings. Meanwhile, efforts must attempt to eliminate the spread of misinformation about vaccines and perturb the rise of anti‐vaccine rhetoric via mass‐messaging platforms. Engaging young online influencers or trusted figures to promote vaccine confidence may support this. Dengue prevention and control interventions should prioritise targeting men, people who live alone, younger individuals, and those in lower income brackets to assist them in navigating diverse, affordable, and more sustainable vector control options. Finally, gender inequity is a determinant of poor dengue prevention outcomes in LAC [ , ]. Interventions should leverage women‐led leadership as this group leads by example and plays an integral cultural role in educating family members and making collective health decisions in the domestic setting.

4.7

## Study Limitations and Strengths
Our study is not without some limitations. First, the non‐probability sampling strategy introduced selection bias, which limited internal validity. Recruitment mainly took place in the affluent West Zone, overlooking dengue risk perceptions in underserved areas. Participants affiliated with FMUSP had higher education and health literacy levels, inflating knowledge scores. Recruitment outside the FMUSP Clinical Hospital possibly attracted individuals with prior healthcare exposure and those more interested in dengue. Therefore, selection bias limited generalizability (i.e., external validity), as this sample does not fully represent São Paulo's socioeconomic and demographic diversity and heterogenous population. Second, self‐reported KAPP survey data may have introduced recall and social desirability bias, which could impact the accuracy of responses related to dengue prevention. For example, participants may have overreported the use of mosquito repellents at home to align with social expectations. Third, the cross‐sectional design of the study limited the ability to establish causality between socioeconomic and demographic factors and dengue‐related KAPP outcomes. Lastly, our study may have confounding bias, particularly from socioeconomic status, prior dengue exposure, geographic factors and access to information. While the multivariate regression controlled for some confounders, residual and unmeasured confounding remains possible, for example, between the vegetation‐wealth‐knowledge relationship.
Despite these limitations, our study maintains several strengths. Employing a mixed‐methods approach, this research study provided a comprehensive understanding of dengue KAPP by complementing quantitative findings with rich qualitative data. The Photovoice activity documented valuable insights into real‐world dengue challenges through visual methods. We successfully engaged community members in this research, demonstrating that lived experiences and local perspectives were incorporated. Presenting these photos to local authorities can raise awareness for the need for community‐oriented prevention strategies, such as neighbourhood clean‐up and sanitation campaigns, and free repellent distribution schemes. Finally, this study is the first of its kind, to the authors' knowledge, to investigate KAPP in relation to the eco‐bio‐social determinants of dengue transmission in LAC, offering novel insights for public health interventions.

5

## Conclusions
This study significantly contributes to the limited body of evidence on the determinants of dengue KAPP in urban settings. This work also broadens preliminary literature on dengue vaccine attitudes in LAC. We found that individuals in São Paulo with higher levels of vegetation around their homes and who never had dengue had higher odds of having high knowledge scores. Higher educational attainment was associated with positive dengue vaccine attitudes. Women and individuals with more household members had higher odds of using mosquito repellents at home, and having a higher monthly income was associated with high perceived self‐efficacy regarding dengue prevention. Perceptions from FGDs and Photovoice activities demonstrated that climate change, socioeconomic vulnerability, and the spread of misinformation were risk factors of dengue transmission, among other factors, and the high cost of mosquito repellents was a major challenge to dengue prevention. Ultimately, greater public awareness about dengue, the dengue vaccine and the risk factors associated with community dengue transmission is needed.
We recommend that future research on dengue further explore the individual behavioural and psychosocial factors determining uptake of dengue prevention practices and vaccine acceptance attitudes; use an urban ecosystems approach to health (Ecohealth) to investigate populations' spatial vulnerability to dengue in big cities; and assess the effectiveness of targeted, community‐tailored and co‐designed interventions to reduce dengue risk among this receptive community in this urban, climate‐vulnerable setting.
