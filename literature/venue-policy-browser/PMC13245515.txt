## Objectives
Community-based digital weight management programs have shown potential to address the public health concern of obesity, but often face implementation barriers. This study aimed to identify and evaluate current implementation barriers, facilitators, and further develop strategies.

## Methods
A prospective qualitative study was conducted via workshops of stakeholders from community-based weight loss management services across Singapore, alongside a separate series of interviews for adopters. A hybrid deductive-inductive approach to thematic analysis was then adopted, informed by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF), and the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change framework (ERIC). Interview transcripts were qualitatively coded based on relevant CFIR and TDF domains using a coding template. Subsequently, identified barriers and domains were mapped to ERIC strategies.

## Results
A total of 48 and 145 barriers were identified based on adopter and implementer transcripts, spanning across 26 and 35 subdomains of the TDF and CFIR, respectively. Several barriers were consistently identified by both adopters and implementers, with the main consistency being a mismatch of expectations. These include differing expectations of public awareness on obesity as a disease, expectations on the flexibility of implementations, and expectations on support for personal motivation. Furthermore, 43 adopter and 57 implementer facilitators were identified, spanning across 25 and 28 distinct subdomains of the TDF and CFIR framework, with the provision of incentives, societal and doctor endorsements as common facilitators. A total of 53 distinct ERIC implementation strategies subthemes were also identified.

## Conclusion
The study identified underlying themes linking barriers and facilitators across implementers and adopters of community-based weight management services. These were mapped to ERIC strategies and contextualized using the Action, Actor, Context, Target, Time (AACTT) framework to specify actors and actions. Many of these strategies could be operationalized by policymakers and nurses, highlighting their central role in facilitating program adoption and delivery.

## What is known?

•Obesity is a rising concern in Singapore. While multidisciplinary weight management programs have been documented to be effective, they are often resource-intensive, have limited scalability, and lack long-term sustainability.
•There are existing strategies and facilitators that can improve engagement and uptake, but their effectiveness may vary across disciplines and contexts.

## What is new?

•This study applies a novel co-design approach to analyze the implementation of community-based weight management programs.
•Using implementation science frameworks, it systematically maps barriers to actionable strategies. It notably highlights the presence of a “dual expectation gap,” reflecting reciprocal misalignment between adopters and implementers.
•It also highlights the strategic role of nurses as adaptable and capable implementers in community-based weight management.

## Introduction
Through decades, obesity remains one of the most persistent public healthcare concerns that increases the population’s risk of chronic diseases, including cardiometabolic diseases and certain cancers [ ]. Weight management programs are traditionally managed by multidisciplinary teams comprising endocrinologists, bariatric surgeons, dietitians, physiotherapists, psychologists, and nurses at specialist medical centers or primary care clinics. However, this model of care for obesity faces several challenges. Firstly, most individuals with obesity prefer to manage their weight loss independently and tend to seek medical care only when it becomes unmanageable [ ]. Secondly, multidisciplinary team-based weight management programs are resource-intensive [ ]. Thirdly, current evidence suggests that there is a high rate of weight regain after program completion, where the majority regain half of the lost weight within 2 years and 80 % within 5 years [ ].
Community-based programs have therefore grown in popularity due to the potential to be more scalable, cost-effective, and sustainable at a population-level [ ]. This includes the use of digital technologies such as mobile apps, telehealth, and automated messaging to support various forms of health coaching to achieve sustainable lifestyle modification outside traditional clinic settings [ ]. However, while studies have shown the effectiveness of such community-based weight management programs, implementation remains challenging due to poor adoption, unsustained use, difficulties in workflow integration, digital inequities, lack of intersectoral coordination, and resource constraints [ , ]. One strategy to enhance program implementation is to engage stakeholders from the outset of project inception and co-design the program with implementation considerations and strategies embedded from the start.
Co-design has emerged as a critical approach in the development of health interventions, particularly in complex, real-world settings where successful implementation depends on alignment with adopter needs, contextual realities, and system constraints. Broadly defined, co-design involves the active and meaningful engagement of key stakeholders, including end adopters, clinicians, policymakers, and other relevant actors, throughout the intervention development process. This collaborative approach shifts the paradigm from researcher-driven design to a more participatory model, where stakeholders contribute to problem identification, intervention refinement, and implementation planning. In doing so, co-design enhances the relevance, acceptability, and feasibility of interventions, thereby increasing the likelihood of sustained adoption, engagement, and retention. Importantly, co-design also enables early identification of potential implementation barriers and facilitators, allowing these to be proactively addressed within the intervention design. As such, co-design is increasingly recognized not only as a method for intervention development, but also as a foundational strategy for bridging the gap between efficacy and real-world implementation. In the context of weight management in the community, co-design approaches have been reported by Langford [ ].
We propose that nurses are essential for the implementation of community-based programs, given their healthcare knowledge, skills, and competencies in providing foundational health checkups, patient education, health coaching, phlebotomy, and patient management [ ]. Additionally, supporting such programs allow nurses to practice their skills in a non-traditional setting, offering an alternative professional environment while still utilizing their clinical expertise. We hence designed a novel, locally developed, weight management program called the Lifestyle Intervention for Gentle Healthy Transformation and Eating Regulation (LIGHTER) program. The LIGHTER program is a nurse-led program that combines one session of nurse- or health coach-delivered obesity counseling and a 6-month personalized health coaching app called the eating Trigger Response Inhibition Program (eTRIP). At baseline, individuals with excess adiposity will undergo physical assessments and blood tests administered by a nurse. eTRIP is a mobile application designed to enhance self-regulation through just-in-time nudging and prevent overeating. To improve the chances of implementation success, we conducted a co-design workshop with 18 key stakeholders to 1) identify and evaluate current barriers, facilitators, and strategies in the implementation of the community-based weight management program, and 2) develop corresponding implementation strategies for the LIGHTER program.

## Methods
A qualitative study was conducted to identify the implementation barriers, facilitators, and strategies perceived by implementers (e.g., healthcare professionals and policy makers) and adopters (individuals with excess body weight). As obesity is still a relatively stigmatized condition in the Asian context, interviews with adopters were conducted individually face-to-face, while those of the implementers were conducted in workshops of four to eight members each. The workshops were also useful in stimulating discussions on similarities, discrepancies, and consensus in obesity management practice across Singapore. After the first aim was achieved, participants were engaged in a session to co-design implementation strategies for the LIGHTER program. All sessions were held at the National University of Singapore between 28 February and 10 March 2025. The study was approved by the National University of Singapore (NUS) Institutional Review Board (IRB) (number: NUS-IRB-2024-898) [ ].
2.1

## Participants
Implementers were purposively sampled nationwide based on their expertise and institution of practice, whenever applicable. Adopters were purposively sampled from an obesity management clinic and a community-based weight management program based on their experience and ability to provide thick and rich information.

2.2

## Procedure
Each workshop began with brief self-introductions on the implementers’ professional roles and responsibilities, organization of employment, followed by structured discussions on implementation barriers, facilitators, and potential strategies. Three workshops were conducted in total, all facilitated by H. S. J. Chew (a registered nurse and university academic with ten years of experience in qualitative research methodologies) and R. Vashishtha (postdoctoral level health services and implementation researcher), with each one lasting approximately 2 h. contains the semi-structured topic guide used during workshops. During individual interviews, adopters first shared about their personal weight management journeys, then elaborated on barriers and facilitators to effective weight management. All individual interviews lasted between 42 and 75 min and were conducted face-to-face by H. S. J. Chew. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to study participation. Discussions were audio-recorded, verbatim, and anonymized for subsequent analysis. Light refreshments were provided after workshops, and all participants received an SGD $60 token of appreciation for their time and transportation.

2.3

## Data analysis
We adopted a hybrid deductive–inductive approach to thematic analysis following the method described by Fereday and Muir-Cochrane (2006) [ ], informed by implementation frameworks: Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) was used to identify implementer barriers, Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) was used to identify adopter barriers, Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) was used to identify implementation strategies and the Action, Actor, Context, Target, Time (AACTT) framework was used to operationalise the implementation strategies identified. The deductive phase of the analysis aligns with our first aim of identifying current implementation barriers, facilitators, and strategies. The subsequent inductive phase supports our second aim of developing specific strategies for community weight management program implementation.
The analytic process consisted of six steps. 1) development of a coding template on Microsoft Excel (deductive phase): an initial codebook was constructed from CFIR and TDF constructs; 2) application of the coding template: two researchers independently immersed in the verbatim transcripts to identify meaning units that correspond with the coding template for implementation barriers, facilitators and strategies; 3) identification of emergent themes (inductive phase): based on the meaning units identified, barriers and facilitators between implementer and adopters were compared using a 2 × 2 matrix where themes emerged; 4) iterative refinement of the codebook: resultant codes and themes were discussed collectively with H. S. J. Chew, merged or split as needed, and definitions were refined to ensure clarity; 5) establishing reliability and consensus: coding discrepancies were resolved through multiple iterative rounds of consensus discussions, with reflexive memos documenting analytic decisions. The refined codebook was then applied across all transcripts again, and 6) linked to implementation strategies: based on the common themes on barriers identified, corresponding ERIC strategies were identified and operationalized using the AACTT framework to implement the LIGHTER program locally. Coding was iteratively refined after multiple rounds of discussion with N. Sevdalis (senior behavioral and implementation scientist).

2.4

## Implementation frameworks
CFIR is a framework to guide the systematic assessment of potential barriers and facilitators, mainly by collecting data from agents who have influence over implementation outcomes [ ]. Hence, we have adopted updated CFIR as the guiding framework to code barriers and facilitators that may have been mentioned in the implementer’s workshops [ ]. Any barrier or facilitator was coded to one of the five core domains: innovation, outer setting, inner setting, individuals and implementation process involved in the implementation. This framework would therefore provide comprehensive and categorized structural flaws and strengths in a layered manner, allowing implementers to understand why interventions may have worked.
TDF is a framework to map barriers/facilitators of behavior change through 14 domains covering 84 theoretical constructs. Unlike CFIR, the TDF captures barriers and facilitators to behavioral changes at an individual level, reflected in its smaller-scale and individual-oriented domains. These include knowledge, skills, social/professional role and identity, beliefs about capabilities, optimism, beliefs about consequences, reinforcement, intentions, goals, memory, attention, and decision processes, environmental context and resources, social influences, emotion, and behavioral regulation. This framework was used to code barriers and facilitators mentioned by adopters of community weight management programs in their interviews, allowing us to better map what hinders the adoption of such programs from the viewpoint of adopters.
ERIC is one of the most widely used implementation science tools to help identify, describe, and select across 73 implementation strategies grouped into the broad categories [ ]. It has been used to map barriers to solutions for research [ ]. ERIC enables researchers to determine feasible actions that may facilitate adoption, improve implementation fidelity, and support the sustainability of interventions. ERIC was utilized to code strategies mentioned during both the implementers workshop and the adopters interview transcripts. These strategies were eventually also mapped to the inductively derived overarching latent themes to identify possible approaches that address the identified determinants.
AACTT framework was used to specify implementation-related behaviors based on the details specified in the 5 domains of Action, Actor, Context, Target, and Time [ ]. The AACTT framework would be used in tandem with ERIC to substantiate strategies as solutions, by mapping what needs to be done to support implementation, by whom, when, and so on.

2.5

## Trustworthiness
Several strategies were employed to ensure analytic rigor. Multiple coders enhanced reliability, with consensus processes to resolve discrepancies. Reflexive memos and audit trails were maintained throughout the analysis. Member checking was conducted with selected participants to validate the interpretation of findings. These steps enhance credibility, confirmability, and transferability of the results.

3.2.1

## Expectations of care ownership, prioritization, and time allocation
This theme was developed based on the integration of the adopter barrier: Social/Professional Role and Identity, and implementers barriers: 1) Individual - Innovation Recipients: Motivation; 2) Individuals Domain - Innovation Recipients: Needs; and 3) Individual Domain - Innovation Recipients - Capability. While adopters mentioned the expectation of healthcare professionals to deliver person-centric care and provide new information that could improve their health, they often felt a disconnection in the adopter-implementers relationship consultations were ineffective, either too brief or long-winded. While this was acknowledged and explained by the implementers to be attributed to time constraints and the need to prioritize immediate complaints at the expense of preventive care, implementers also expected adopters to take the initiative to perform self-care, resulting in occasional nagging seen as long-winded.

3.2.2

## Expectations on public awareness of obesity as a disease
This theme was developed based on the integration of the adopter barrier, Social Influences - Social Norms, and the implementer barrier, Outer Domain - Local Attitudes. Both groups agreed that obesity is currently still being recognized as a lifestyle problem rather than a disease that requires treatment, resulting in the continued public indulgence in unhealthy habits.

3.2.3

## Expectation on implementation target and flexibility
This theme was developed based on the integration of the adopter barriers: 1) Environmental Context and Resources - Environmental Stressors, and 2) Environmental Context and Resources - Resources/Material Resources, and the implementers’ barrier of 1) Outer Domain - Local Conditions, and 2) Inner Setting Domain - Available Resources: Space. Both adopters and implementers emphasized the need for flexibility in how obesity management clinics were organized. Adopters described difficulty attending weekday sessions due to work and family commitments, noting that evenings or weekends were often the only feasible times. Healthcare implementers echoed this concern, observing that participation was largely limited to those with flexible working hours, while others struggled to attend.

Adopters and implementers highlighted the challenges of sustaining long-term engagement in weight management programs. ## Provision of incentive systems
This theme was developed based on the integration of the adopter facilitator, Reinforcement – Incentives, and the implementer facilitator, Inner Setting Domain - Incentive Systems. Adopters emphasized the appeal of tangible rewards such as cash or vouchers, while implementers described structured incentive systems such as health credits that also allowed for performance tracking. These findings suggest that material reinforcements can enhance engagement and sustain participation.

3.3.2

## Endorsement by society through vicarious experiences
This theme was developed based on the integration of the adopter facilitator, Social Influences – Modeling, and the implementer facilitator, Implementation Process Domain – Engaging: Innovation Recipients. Adopters were influenced by stories from friends or family members who had attempted similar programs, reflecting the role of social modeling in shaping willingness to participate. Implementers also highlighted the power of peer testimony in showcasing real-life success, which they viewed as a credible and persuasive strategy to encourage sign-up.

3.3.3

## Endorsement by doctors as figures of authority
This theme was developed based on the integration of the adopter facilitator: Beliefs about Consequence - Outcome Expectancies, and the implementer facilitator: Outer Setting Domain - Local Attitudes. Adopters described trust in doctors’ recommendations as more convincing than community-led advice, while implementers acknowledged that their professional credibility could be leveraged to legitimize the program and encourage uptake.

## Discussion
Based on the 2 × 2 matrix, we identified four common themes on barriers, namely expectations of care ownership and prioritization, public awareness of obesity as a disease, implementation targets and flexibility, and support for personal motivation and affordability. At the same time, common themes on facilitators were consistently recognized, including the provision of incentive systems, endorsement by society through vicarious experiences, and endorsement by doctors as figures of authority. Twenty-two examples illustrating how each implementation strategy could be operationalized.
4.1

## Dual expectation gap
The findings confirm prior research showing misalignments between expectations of people with excess body weight for person-centered counseling and implementers’ prioritization of acute biomedical concerns within time-limited consultations [ , ]. A distinctive feature in this study was the dual expectation gap: service adopters described counseling sessions as either too brief or “long-winded but unhelpful”, while implementers expected adopters to take greater initiative in their own care. This reciprocal attribution is less often described in international literature, which tends to foreground implementers barriers [ ]. A possible explanation is Singapore’s high-throughput care model, where physicians balance multiple chronic conditions in short visits, reinforced by key performance indicators that healthcare services must meet, thus privileging efficiency over preventive counseling [ ]. Addressing this requires role revision (e.g., delegation to health coaches, nurses, or allied health staff), which may enable the formation of new clinical teams to manage follow-up weight management sessions and support program sustainability. Additional system supports, such as electronic health records (EHR)-based prompts or educational materials developed by an information communication team, can also relieve the workload of implementers while managing adopter expectations. Engaging consumers directly during consultations or in community settings, through goal setting, allows implementers to set expectations. In turn, adopters would also better understand the capabilities and roles of the implementers, rather than expecting more within unchanged workflows or performance metrics [ ].

4.2

## Public awareness of obesity as a disease
Consistent with global evidence, both service adopters and implementers agreed that obesity remains widely viewed as a lifestyle issue rather than a chronic disease requiring treatment [ ]. The difference lies in our implementers’ emphasis on local attitudes: even when clinicians medicalize obesity, societal norms continue to normalize overeating and trivialize medical care. In Singapore, food-centric culture and strong emphasis on personal responsibility may blunt the impact of clinical framing [ , ]. This suggests that awareness efforts must extend beyond clinical labeling, combining consensus messaging with community champions and vicarious patient stories to shift social norms [ ]. Social media campaigns, workshops, and community endorsements provide valuable platforms for these actors to engage with the public and potentially motivate patients who are less inclined toward weight management. Social media, particularly, is exceptionally suitable for this role due to its broad outreach and widespread use.

4.3

## Implementation targets and flexibility
Flexibility in timing and mode of delivery was a key expectation, consistent with international findings that evening/weekend sessions and telehealth improve participation in lifestyle programs [ , ]. However, our data highlighted space scarcity and venue coordination as particularly salient barriers — less frequently reported in systems with dedicated lifestyle clinics. This may reflect the structural realities of Singapore’s polyclinics and community health posts, which often operate at capacity [ ]. Thus, flexibility should encompass not only timing but also infrastructure, with hybrid and virtual models positioned as core solutions rather than contingency measures [ ]. Accordingly, the implementation team should prioritize promoting program adaptability, particularly by targeting patients during post-consultation periods. During the early stages of implementation, the team should also conduct local needs assessments to better understand consumer needs and inform adaptations, alongside any necessary changes to infrastructure or equipment.

4.4

## Support for personal motivation and affordability
The difficulty of sustaining motivation in obesogenic environments is well documented [ ]. Our findings similarly indicate that environmental cues undermine intention, while adopters and implementers alike emphasized the need for motivational scaffolding. A notable difference was adopters’ skepticism regarding program legitimacy and value for money — a concern less reported in settings where preventive programs are publicly financed [ ]. In Singapore’s mixed public–private market, where commercial weight-loss programs coexist with variable insurance coverage, skepticism may be heightened [ ]. Addressing this requires pairing behavioral supports with trust signals (institutional branding, transparent outcome reporting) and financial strategies (subsidies, health credits) to reduce out-of-pocket burden [ ].
However, this theme is closely tied to individual-level constraints (e.g., motivation, knowledge, behavioral regulation, and emotional factors), which are less directly modifiable through implementation strategies alone and may reflect more deeply embedded influences. As such, while barriers in this theme are critical, they are comparatively less amenable to direct intervention within the health system than implementer- or system-level factors. Therefore, implementation efforts may be most effective when prioritizing modifiable implementer and system-level barriers, with the expectation that improvements in program design, accessibility, and credibility will, in turn, support positive changes in individual motivation and engagement [ ].

4.5

## Facilitators: incentives, peer modeling, and physician endorsement
This results reaffirm prior studies showing that incentives, social modeling, and clinician recommendations can enhance uptake [ , ]. For example, adopters emphasized tangible rewards (e.g., vouchers), while implementers described structured systems (e.g., health credits with tracking). Physician endorsement emerged as especially influential, perhaps reflecting high cultural trust in medical authority in Singapore and recent national experiences with voucher-based health schemes [ , ]. The key insight is that these facilitators act through distinct mechanisms — reinforcement (incentives), normative influence (peer modeling), and credibility (physician endorsement) — which together form a synergistic “nudge + norm + trust” package [ ].

4.6

## Implications for policy and practice
Prior research in this field typically catalogs barriers and facilitators, but few studies to date have specified how these might map onto concrete implementation strategies - i.e., there still is a major gap between knowing what the real or perceived challenges are and how best to address them in practice. By integrating ERIC and AACTT frameworks, this study operationalizes who acts, what actions are taken, in what context, for which targets, and at what time. For example, aligning care ownership may involve EMR prompts delivered by informatics teams during consultations, while reframing obesity as a disease may require campaigns led by local champions post-consultation. The implication is that isolated strategies are unlikely to succeed; rather, bundled approaches integrating role redesign, flexible delivery, financing, and multi-level endorsement are more appropriate to Singapore’s context [ ].
These findings highlight the need for system-level interventions. Delegating obesity counseling to allied healthcare implementers and embedding EHR prompts may realign adopter-implementer expectations. Public awareness campaigns must combine clinical framing with community-level messaging to shift norms. Hybrid and virtual delivery models should be institutionalized to overcome space and scheduling constraints. Finally, financial sustainability can be strengthened by integrating weight management into existing financing mechanisms (e.g., MediSave, health promotion credits) and reporting outcomes transparently to bolster trust. By addressing these implementers- and system-level factors, there is the potential for downstream effects on individual-level barriers, supporting adopters’ motivation and engagement to adopt healthier behaviors.

4.7

## Implications for nurses
Nurses are uniquely positioned in the healthcare infrastructure to facilitate the implementation of such community programs. Their expertise in clinical education, experience in holistic care and care coordination, and training in patient education and behavioral counseling equip them with skills to support such programs. Their operational experience in interacting with patients also further bridges the relationship between healthcare implementers and community settings.
The range of skills nurses possess highlights both their versatility and competence in supporting any community-based initiative. As such, they are well-positioned to take on multiple roles within the strategies outlined in , either as actors responsible for implementing new strategies or as key targets of these strategies who support and facilitate program delivery. For example, their clinical knowledge enables them to support community outreach efforts that improve public awareness of obesity as a disease. They could also function as health coaches, promoting care ownership and providing post-consultation guidance through their training in behavioral counseling [ ]. In addition, their experience in care coordination and their role as frontline professionals position them well to support program implementation and ensure the sustainment of program goals. Beyond conventional responsibilities, such programs may also create opportunities for nurses to engage in emerging roles, including program implementation and evaluation as research nurses or digital health positions such as telehealth nursing. Evidence cites activities such as the monitoring of health indicators, providing guidance and education, and coordinating case management as factors that contributed to the success of such programs [ , , ]. Because of their versatility and accessibility within healthcare infrastructure, nurses play a critical role in the successful implementation of community-based weight management programs.

4.8

## Implementation strategies for community-based weight management services in Singapore
Building on our findings, these implementation strategies can be designed based on the socio-ecological model to structure strategies across individual, organizational, and policy levels [ ]. In summary, strategies to be undertaken at each one of these levels include:
Individual level: Tailor motivational messaging to readiness; provide vouchers or health credits to reinforce engagement; train lay health coaches with lived experience to act as peer role models. These strategies address barriers around motivation, social influence, and self-efficacy.
Organizational level: Integrate obesity prompts into EHRs; redistribute roles to nurses and dietitians for initiating digital referrals; identify clinical champions; and schedule hybrid delivery formats to increase accessibility. These strategies target inner setting barriers, time constraints, and role ambiguity.
Policy and system level: Establish sustainable financing models, subsidize digital weight management programs, invest in digital equity, and support healthier food environments. These strategies respond to adopters’ concerns about cost and access, while addressing upstream structural barriers.

4.9

## Strengths and limitations
This study has several strengths. First, through the co-design approach, it integrates perspectives from adopters, implementers, and administrators, capturing the interplay between adopters expectations and system-level realities. The use of hybrid deductive–inductive analysis and complementary frameworks (TDF, CFIR, ERIC) enabled a comprehensive mapping from barriers and facilitators to actionable strategies, while the application of the AACTT framework operationalized these strategies into clear, context-specific actions. The inclusion of participants from all three major healthcare clusters of Singapore enhances the transferability of findings across the country’s health system.
Several limitations should also be acknowledged. Firstly, the small number of adopters interviews limits the diversity of perspectives, particularly from groups underrepresented in obesity programs, such as men and younger adults. Although this paper primarily focuses on changing organizational and policy and system level structural infrastructure, it may further benefit from the inclusion of more perspectives and constraints on weight loss from adopters and their reluctance to participate in weight management programs. Subsequently, more encompassing strategies could have been developed. The group workshop’s face-to-face format could have introduced social desirability bias, potentially suppressing more critical views.
While mapping strategies to barriers provided structured strategies, the feasibility and effectiveness of these strategies remain to be tested in practice. These strategies, while informed by many frameworks, would benefit from empirical validation. Finally, as the study was conducted within Singapore, findings may not fully generalize to other healthcare systems with different financing structures, cultural norms, or resource constraints.

4.10

## Future research directions
An initial pilot study would help with the validation of the strategies and assess their feasibility of implementation. Comparative studies are needed to test how bundled strategies perform across subgroups, who were underrepresented in our study. Further investigation into the mobilization of physicians and opinion leaders is warranted, given their central role in program legitimacy, but must also address workload concerns. Finally, cost-effectiveness analyses of incentive systems and financing models will be essential to guide policy decisions in Singapore's mixed healthcare system.

5

## Conclusion
This study identified multilevel barriers and facilitators to community weight management program implementation in Singapore and mapped them to actionable strategies. Misaligned care expectations limited public recognition of obesity as a disease; logistical constraints and concerns around motivation and affordability remain critical barriers. Yet, the consistent facilitators of incentives, peer modeling, and physician endorsement provide promising levers for engagement. By linking these findings to ERIC strategies within the AACTT framework, this study offers a structured strategy for implementation. Future work should focus on testing integrated bundles of strategies that are contextually tailored, financially sustainable, and culturally resonant.
