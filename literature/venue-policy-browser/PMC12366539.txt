## Abstract
This study provides a content and frame analysis of the news media advocacy of prominent environmental non‐governmental organizations (ENGOs) in Canada. We find that these organizations have an important voice in shaping how climate change is framed in news media, but that ecological modernization frames and narratives, which avoid issues of power, conflict, and social‐transformative change, are dominant. Core elements of this discourse are contested, however, as some ENGOs oppose the fossil sector, critique the shortcomings of proffered (technological) climate solutions, and call for muscular interventions aimed at energy transition. We also find that environmental justice frames – particularly those focused on Indigenous rights – are gaining traction, revealing a promising pathway of influence for ENGOs focused on climate justice.

## INTRODUCTION
News media is a key site of political contention in advanced capitalism. Social movements make strategic use of media to shape public perceptions and to debate solutions to social and ecological problems. This study provides a content and frame analysis of the news media advocacy of eight prominent environmental non‐governmental organizations (ENGOs) in Canada, covering 2018−2023. We examine the extent of ENGO claims‐making in mainstream newspapers and how different organizations frame environmental and climate change issues therein, by communicating key aspects of the issue, who is responsible, and what should be done (Benford & Snow, ).
Although most studies consider media and ENGO framing of climate change separately, examining the frames of movement protagonists in media coverage is illuminating. As ENGOs act as critical sources in news media, at stake is the extent to which alternative perspectives enter this important component of the public sphere. While we comprehensively track ENGO framing, we are particularly interested in how this advocacy addresses the climate crisis in the present context, including the national narrative to simultaneously support climate action and fossil economy expansion.
We find that environmental organizations have an important voice in shaping the framing of the climate crisis in news media, but that ecological modernization (EM) frames, which repress issues of power and inequality, remain dominant. At the same time, and compared with findings of previous studies, environmental justice frames – particularly those focused on Indigenous rights – are gaining traction, revealing a promising avenue of influence for ENGOs focused on climate justice.
To situate our findings, we begin by outlining broad contending discourses of EM and climate justice. Next, we address scholarly research considering how frames supporting these discourses are advanced by movement protagonists in Canada.

2.

## FRAMING AND CLIMATE CHANGE: FROM EM TO CLIMATE JUSTICE
Framing – the strategic efforts by organizations to create common understandings of a problem in order to mobilize collective action – is fundamental to the work of social movements (McAdam et al., ). Frames guide cognition in several ways. They provide a simplified description of a problem and attribute responsibility (diagnostic framing), by providing justification for action (motivational framing), and by advocating ameliorative actions and alternatives (prognostic frames) (Benford & Snow, ). Frames are often contested within and between social movement organizations and not just between organizations and governments (Goodwin & Jasper, ).
Although climate change is a key focus of today's environmental movement, interpretations of the issue vary considerably, leading to the deployment of different frames and framing strategies. A leading policy framework for addressing the climate crisis is EM, which stresses a “technology‐based and innovation‐oriented strategy focusing on the efficient use of resources and providing co‐benefits for both ecology and economy” (Mol & Janicke, , p. 18). At the core of EM theory is that existing social, economic, and political institutions can effectively deal with environmental issues, and there is no need for radical structural changes in capitalist society (Mol & Spaargaren, ). Such discourse has been characterized as ‘post‐political’ (Kenis & Lievens, ; Swyngedouw, ; Wetts, ), in so far at it eschews conflict‐based diagnoses of social problems that see them as rooted in social structures or the actions of powerful interests, in favor of technocratic management, consensus formation and problem‐focused governance.
EM is hegemonic within official policy‐making circles (Bäckstrand & Lövbrand, ; Haas et al., ) and core to the messaging of some mainstream and professionalized segments of the environmental movement, which highlight the economic benefits of climate action and promote market strategies to internalize environmental costs (Caniglia et al., ). Such framing reflects an insider messaging strategy that promotes collaborative engagement with both governments and the private sector (Caniglia et al., ). This framing is contested, however, and other perspectives are part of the debate.
A more conflict‐laden climate justice (CJ) discourse, which approaches climate change through the lens of inequality and power, is prominent within the environmental movement, especially among grassroots activists (Bäckstrand & Lövbrand, ; Newell et al., ; Schlosberg & Collins, ). CJ framing highlights the uneven impacts of climate change and climate action, and the ways decision‐making processes for addressing climate change can reinforce power relations (Schlosberg & Collins, ). By seeking solutions that address the root causes of climate change and mitigate multiple forms of injustice, CJ frames offer a potential pathway to re‐politicize the climate crisis in ways that connect the environmental movement with other movement sectors and allow for “socio‐ecologically more egalitarian transformations” (Swyngedouw, , p. 214).
While EM and climate justice are broad contending discourses, movement framing is a strategic and dynamic process (Benford & Snow, ), and must also be understood in relation to political opportunity structures – dimensions of the political environment that affect the development and success of movements – including the degree of openness of the political system to reform, splits among elites, and the availability of influential allies (Tarrow, ). Below we outline shifting political opportunities facing Canada's environmental movement, and consider how these may shape framing processes and strategy.

3.

## BACKGROUND
3.1.

## Canada's environmental movement and shifting political opportunities
In seeking to mobilize an effective response to the climate crisis, Canada's environmental movement has faced a challenging structure of political opportunities, including a federal political context that until recently could hardly be described in terms of post‐political consensus on the seriousness of climate change.
The Conservative federal government under Stephen Harper expanded oil and gas production and export, centering on Alberta oil sands, alongside fracked shale gas and offshore oil, a central plank of its economic development strategy (MacNeil, ). This vision was accompanied by policies to rapidly expand the oil sands, including federal regulatory rollbacks of environmental protection (Gibson, ). During international climate summits the government was found to behave “in a recalcitrant and even obstructionist manner” (Cited in Callison & Tindall, , p. 1) and in 2011, Canada withdrew from the Kyoto climate accord.
Policy changes were accompanied by significant federal and provincial (especially in Alberta) funding for pro‐oil sands public relations campaigns, which sought to counter criticism of the industry (Davidson & Gismondi, ). These campaigns were in turn accompanied by a punitive auditing of environmental groups voicing concerns over oil and gas development (Lakanen, ).
Canada's environmental movement has nonetheless successfully influenced individual values and behaviors, as well as environmental policies, including addressing acid rain, ozone, deforestation, and toxic emissions (Robinson et al., ; Tindall & Robinson, ). Meanwhile, First Nation‐led and ENGO supported challenges have contributed to the delay or abandonment of major oil and gas projects, such as Northern Gateway (Neubauer & Gunster, ; Thomas‐Muller, ). Yet, even Canada's most visible environmental leader, David Suzuki, suggested that the environmental movement has been largely ineffective in mobilizing climate action (Suzuki, ).
Led by Justin Trudeau, the Liberal Party, which was first elected in 2015 and again in 2019 and 2021, campaigned on climate action and Indigenous reconciliation, including enhanced environmental review processes, and Canada sent a full delegation in signing on to the Paris Agreement. Subsequent federal climate policy expresses a commitment to an ecomodernist green growth program centering market‐based climate measures, especially federal carbon pricing, and supports for technological innovation, especially efficiency enhancement and electric vehicles. Yet, political commitments to climate action are contradictorily coupled with continued support for the fossil economy, including via direct investments in pipeline projects, such as the TransMountain Expansion (TMX) (See Tienhaara & Walker, ).
The provinces have adopted individual climate action plans over the last decade, and these diverge significantly. BC, Quebec, Nova Scotia, and PEI began implementing policy frameworks that mirror those of the federal government, while Quebec legislated a province‐wide ban on fossil fuel extraction in 2022 (Brooks et al., ). However, governments in regions central to carbon extractive development, particular Alberta, Saskatchewan, and Newfoundland and Labrador have often actively obstructed transition, pushing for further fossil fuel development, while moving slowly on climate action plans (Carter, ). Alberta's United Conservative government recently implemented a 7‐month pause on green energy projects, further limiting pathways to climate action (Weber, ). Thus, an acceptance of the reality of climate change and commitment to act remains fragile and Canada is home to elite coalitions opposed to virtually all forms of climate action (Graham, ).

3.2.

## Literature on environmental movement frames and news media
Empirical research analyzing the shifting frames of environmental movement organizations in Canada, especially in news media, is limited. Haluza‐DeLay and Fernhout ( ) examine the deployment of environmental justice discourses via the website communication of numerous ENGOs, and conclude that, “the discourses of social inclusion and justice are a marginal component in English‐speaking Canadian environmental organizations’ communication” (p. 740).
Other work finds that justice lenses feature in movement opposition to fossil fuel expansion. Ramos ( ), for example, considers how ENGOs use justice narratives to counter the frames utilized by governments to justify carbon resource extraction. Likewise, Neubauer and Gunster ( ) consider how environmental organizations, in alliance with Indigenous groups, have articulated a regional ecological populist storyline, in which opposition to pipeline projects, emphasizes local ecological (more so than climate) impacts and the “inequitable distribution of risk and benefit” (p. 9). Given Canada's settler‐colonial foundation and frequent clashes between extractive resource projects and Indigenous sovereignty, such climate justice messaging often incorporates Indigenous rights and resurgence (See Coats, ; Thomas‐Muller, ).
Environmental movements in Canada likewise appear to be responding to the failure to address the urgency of the climate crisis by forging new discursive frames, such as those of green jobs and just transition (Allan & Robinson, ; Robinson, ), and through appeals to a social‐transformative versions of the Green New Deal, which marry decarbonization with investments in universal programs, such as public transportation, eco‐efficient public housing, and in the caring economy (Klein, ; MacArthur et al., ).
However, other scholarship suggest that movement advocacy may become more moderate and affirmative following federal climate commitments. Brooks et al. ( ) suggest that environmental organizations in Canada face “governmental capture of the [energy] transition discourse” (24), which may lead ENGOs to organize their action narrowly around governments’ net zero plans. Carroll et al. ( ) similarly see the embrace of an ecomodernist paradigm in Canada as possessing “significant co‐optative potential, [that] could attract ENGOs toward the mainstream, in search of funding and legitimacy” (19). Moreover, given the continued opposition to climate action within a segment of Canada's elite policy network (Graham, ), environmental organizations may be encouraged to defend existing policy initiatives and commitments, while transformative views are kept to the margins. This reading is partly confirmed by Spiegel ( ), whose recent examination of climate change website communications of several prominent ENGOS finds that EM discourses predominate.
Alongside these studies, there is a sizeable recent literature on climate change communication in Canadian news media, especially in national print news (Callison & Tindall, ; Howe et al., ; Stoddart, Haluza‐DeLay, et al., ; Stoddart et al., ; Stoddart & Tindall, ), and mainstream regional media (Deacon et al., ; Dusyk et al., ; Gunster & Saurette, ; Olive, ). Callison and Tindall ( ) report that Canada has comparatively high national news coverage of climate change, while Stoddart, Haluza‐DeLay, et al. ( ) find that Canada's national news media is notable for its focus on policy solutions and policy debate in climate coverage.
Research on social movement interactions with the media mostly examines quantity of coverage (Corrigall‐Brown, ; Hackett & Adams, ; Howe et al., ), while only a few studies track ENGO frames within news stories (Ramos, ; Stoddart et al., ; Stoddart et al., ; Stoddart, Smith, et al., ) and little work has examined recent media advocacy on climate change. However, examining how ENGOs shape news media coverage of climate change and the nature of their framing work is illuminating. Such coverage is a primary means through which the environmental movement transmits its ideas to the larger public and to influential elites.
Our study asks two questions: (1) How successful are environmental movement organizations in having news media circulate their arguments and frames? and (2) How do their frames respond to the climate crisis in the present context, including the national narrative to simultaneously support climate action and fossil economy expansion? Addressing these questions contributes to understanding the changing nature of the environmental movement in Canada, including new discursive frames that reach the broad public and confront the need for rapid and deep climate action.

## DATA AND METHODS
We selected eight environmental organizations based in Canada. Three of them – Canadian Parks and Wilderness Society, Wilderness Committee and World Wildlife Fund Canada – are conservation focused, while four others – David Suzuki Foundation, Environmental Defence Canada, Greenpeace Canada and Sierra Club Canada – target multiple environmental issues, including climate change. We also included the Pembina Institute, which concentrates on climate and energy policy research.
The organizations are by no means representative of all ENGOs or of the environmental movement in general; however, they are among the most established and well‐resourced in Canada and as professionalized organizations with sizable staff, they lead in media standing. Geographically, most are headquartered in Ontario or BC, but each organization has offices in multiple provinces.
In terms of media, we selected seven mainstream news outlets. This includes two national dailies – the Globe and Mail and the National Post – as well as CBC News. We also included provincial media in BC (the Vancouver Sun and the Province), Ontario (Toronto Star) and Quebec (Montreal Gazette) that enjoy high circulation. Four belong to the right‐leaning Postmedia chain, which serves as a concerning example of the consolidation of media ownership in Canada.
While other venues, including social media, are important to climate communication, news media continue to play a critical role in this process and are the most widely used sources of information on climate change across numerous countries (Newman et al., ). Other scholarship demonstrates that much of the climate change‐related content shared on social media stems from news media (Pearce et al., ). Thus, news media are often the primary source of information on climate change for the average citizen and are therefore the primary mode of delivery of issue frames to the public.
Next, using both LexisNexis and Factiva databases, we searched for mentions of each organization in the above media outlets from January, 2018 to February, 2023. This yielded an initial sample of 4161 individual stories. The sample was trimmed as nonrelevant stories (including references to international ENGO branches, generic mentions of organizations without a frame present, or posts that contained ad hominem criticism of organizations) were deleted. The final sample includes 2196 stories.
We begin by quantitatively tracking ENGO access and representation in news media. Other data points were coded to track the nature of ENGO input, including organization‐authored op‐eds, when either reports, studies, petitions, or letters were named and cited, as well as when lawsuits were discussed.
Downloaded articles were subsequently uploaded to Atlas.ti to facilitate a qualitative frame and content analysis. An initial list of frames was developed based on existing literature and our field‐relevant knowledge, while others were added inductively during the coding process. Frame coding was conducted by the lead author and multiple codes were often assigned to a single story, or single quoted segment of text.
Our analysis includes a sizeable number of frames (n = 45), providing a fine‐grained examination. This includes a distinction made across most frames between advocacy focused on climate change versus other environmental issues, and a close tracking of political scale. These distinctions follow from previous scholarship finding that localized environmental harms predominate in movement opposition to carbon extractive development (Neubauer & Gunster, ) and research finding that policy debate in national news media is focused on international and national scales (Stoddart, Haluza‐DeLay, et al., ). We also track multiple dimensions of climate justice advocacy, outlined above.
To allow for a broader analysis, the various frames were later categorized by frame type – diagnostic, prognostic, and motivational framing. Next, to consider how contending discourses of EM and climate justice are at play, prognostic frames were subsequently placed in two main categories: (a) technology, markets, and conservation, or (b) climate justice and transformative change. The former includes measures that accord closely with ecomodernist policy frameworks, such as technological and market‐based solutions, and others that in themselves do not readily challenge power relations, such as conservation, waste reduction, recreation and greenspace advocacy, or individual level solutions. Those categorized as transformative change include climate and environmental justice lenses and other actions considered as challenging incumbent industry power, including fossil wind down, environmental regulations, public transportation and mode switching, renewable energy and energy transition advocacy, as well as refutations of proposed solutions (i.e., false solutions).

## FINDINGS
5.1.

## News media access and representation
Table  displays the distribution of the 2196 stories. Considering 2018–2023, Environmental Defense (ED) is the most successful in having its research, initiatives, and frames (re)circulated and discussed in the above media outlets. It is closely followed by Greenpeace, as well as the David Suzuki Foundation (DSF) and the Pembina Institute. Sierra Club and the three conservation focused organizations are less prominent sources, but each is still regularly cited and quoted.
 By news outlet, the Globe and Mail followed by the Toronto Star cite ENGOs most widely, while The Province and the Montreal Gazette contained the least number of relevant mentions. Some ENGO‐media relations follow provincial lines; ED's work is often discussed in the Star, while Wilderness is mostly cited in BC media – The Vancouver Sun and The Province.
In terms of the nature of input, most ENGO citations involve interview‐based quotations from organization representatives, alongside the recirculation of positions found via organization websites and press releases. In all, 8 percent of stories discuss organization reports or studies (including polls), while 3 percent discuss petitions or letters sent to the government and another 65 stories discuss legal action taken by ENGOs. Further, 6 percent of stories were op‐eds penned by organizations (whether independently or in collaboration with other groups), which provide greater space for ENGO advocacy.

5.2.

## ENGO framing across the sample
Table  displays the top 30 frames by count, which are categorized as diagnostic and prognostic frames.
 In terms of critiques of industry, climate effects outstrip other environmental harms, with the former cited 392 times and the latter 367 times. The same holds for governance, as the need for enhanced environmental policy was cited 312 times, compared to 469 calls for improved climate policy. The climate crisis is the central focus of ENGO media work.
In terms of diagnostic frames, fossil capital was by far the most critiqued (criticized 385 times in total) and compared to previous research, we find that emphasis is placed on climate impacts. Other extractive industries and projects, which have often generated environmental conflict, especially mining and forestry, were critiqued 149 times, primarily focusing on environmental harms, such as habitat destruction and biodiversity loss. Various other industries and projects were critiqued 200 times, mostly for environmental impacts, while the finance sector was critiqued 25 times, exclusively for its contributions to climate change via fossil fuel holdings, thus extending the critique of fossil capital to financial enablement. In framing criticizing governmental environmental or climate (in)action or policy, the provincial scale predominates with 468 critiques of provincial governance, compared to 313 critical appraisals of federal level governance. This reflects the presence of provincial news outlets in our sample.
In terms of prognostic or solutions‐focused frames, conservation advocacy – promoting the protection of wildlife, natural habitats, and preservation of ecological integrity – leads. Also prominent was advocacy of various forms of waste reduction, such as plastics and food waste. The next most prominent are market‐based climate solutions (especially carbon pricing advocacy), and technological solutions (particularly electric vehicles and efficiency enhancement measures), alongside advocacy of renewables and energy transition.
Table  homes in on environmental and climate justice frames, including a short description of each frame. As displayed there, environmental justice (EJ) and climate justice (CJ) frames were deployed 180 times. Of the 11 distinct frames, four address unequal environmental or climate impacts and seven focus on the enhancement of justice in resolving environmental problems. In terms of harms, negative impacts to Indigenous communities from extractive resource development was the most deployed, alongside unequal climate impacts, with several stories highlighting unequal harms facing youth. In terms of solutions, just transition lenses were prominent, alongside arguments that environmental remedies must include and are an opportunity for reconciliation and decolonization. Especially common was advocacy of Indigenous leadership in conservation and to a lesser degree in assertions of nature‐based climate solutions – arguments that ecological preservation is a component of climate solution.
 We also tracked instances of motivational framing. Among these, the frame of ‘climate emergency’ (which expresses urgency and a narrowing time frame for meaningful action) was cited 41 times, alongside “climate costs,” which was cited 48 times and points to both future and especially current impacts of climate change, including by connecting recent extreme weather events, such as fires and floods, to global warming.
Equally revealing as the most prominent frames is the absence of certain lenses. By sector, there are remarkably few critiques of the agri‐food industry, while in terms of scale, there are just 25 critiques of or calls to enhance international climate governance and only three critical appraisals of municipal scale climate policy. Alongside a limited focus on international governance, global justice concerns are virtually absent in the sample, as were equity lenses, and democratization lenses.
Below we investigate further by providing examples of how leading frames are deployed in media, while considering complementarity and divergence among the ENGOs, including in the use of more contentious frames.

5.3.

## Leading frames and ENGO complementarity and divergence
Considering prognostic frames, conservation advocacy is the most deployed by the three conservation organizations, as well as David Suzuki Foundation (DSF) and Sierra, while waste reduction advocacy leads for Greenpeace (GP) and Environmental Defense (ED). Across the groups, such advocacy was often accompanied by critiques of governance, and sometimes by environmental justice frames, particularly Indigenous leadership in conservation. For example, a CBC News article discussed a Canadian Parks and Wilderness Society (CPWS) report finding that several provinces were “dragging their feet” on reaching conservation goals and quoted CPWS’ Alison Woodley stating that “We have enormous leadership on the ground from Indigenous peoples who are identifying areas for protection across the country… But we have this blockage in terms of provinces and territories which are not stepping up in most cases to …support Indigenous‐led conservation.”
All the ENGOs critiqued negative impacts of the fossil fuel industry and those focused on climate change often highlighted the incompatibility of fossil expansion and climate commitments. For example, in a CBC News article Sierra Club's Heather Elliott asserted that the federal approval of Bay du Nord – an oil megaproject offshore of Newfoundland – was “impossible to justify” as “Scientists have warned that new production of oil is incompatible with climate targets and … not part of a just transition,” while ED's Julia Levin called the approval a “slap in the face” and “another leap towards an unlivable future,” in the Vancouver Sun. Meanwhile, in a letter to the editors published in The Globe and Mail, Greenpeace's Keith Stewart described the federal purchace of the TMX pipeline as a ‘Faustian bargain,’ wherein the project would only succeed economically if the Paris agreement failed.
In terms of climate solutions, many ENGOs including ED, DSF, Greenpeace, and Pembina expressed support for market‐based climate measures, especially support for federal carbon pricing, alongside technological solutions. These were sometimes approached as a component of a bundle of needed climate actions, such as in a Globe and Mail article wherein DSF's Ian Bruce asserted that “the carbon tax is an essential part of a successful climate change plan, but not the only thing we need to do.”
The Pembina Institute is the leading advocate of technological climate solutions, focusing on efficiency enhancement and sometimes carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) technology. As program director Nina Lothian asserted in CBC News article, “Carbon capture is one of the critical tools in our toolbox, and it is necessary for Canada and the world to achieve our climate targets.” The organization also led in employing a ‘fossil sector climate challenge’ frame, which expresses support for fossil industry emissions reduction ambitions, but critiques the inadequacy of current efforts. For example, an article in The Globe and Mail discussed a study by Pembina asserting that “energy companies that undertake deep emissions reductions now are likely to be more competitive in a low‐carbon future – including Canada's oil sands producers,” but found that “oil sands companies have yet to make the necessary investment decisions.” While such framing criticizes industry inaction, it avoids positioning the industry as a foe or opponent of climate action, pointing instead to the merits of all‐around cooperation.
These positions reflect Pembina's broader framing of climate action as a race between states and corporations for advantageous positioning vis‐à‐vis the changing landscape of low carbon technology. This understanding, here captured as a ‘clean growth and market environmentalism’ frame is exemplified in a Globe and Mail article, wherein Pembina's Isabelle Turcotte asserts that “The basic rules of capitalism will eventually force still‐reluctant companies to commit to a low or no‐carbon future” and that “There is a race on now to attract that capital, and obviously Canada needs to be positioning itself to get a piece of that pie.”
As expected, WWF is conservation‐focused and sometimes promotes nature‐based climate solutions, which it likewise asserted in market‐centric terms. For example, in a Globe and Mail article, WWF president Megan Leslie stated that “there's an opportunity for businesses to take the next step to reducing greenhouse gas emissions by supporting nature‐based solutions, which are about finding the most efficient way to help nature do its job when it comes to the climate crisis and biodiversity loss… [This can enable] Canada to take a global leadership role in the clean economy by leveraging our abundant natural spaces and pristine habitats.”
The positions of Pembina, particularly its advocacy of CCUS and efficiency enhancement in the oil and gas sector, are contested by other ENGOs, especially ED and Greenpeace, which lead in ‘false climate solutions’ frames. These were often bound up with critiques of the fossil fuel sector and Canada's failure to address climate change, such as in a National Post article, wherin ED's Dale Marshall responded to Trudeau's pledge to put a cap on emissions from the oil and gas sector by asserting that, “Focusing on emissions from oil and gas production but not production itself will allow oil and gas companies to keep putting forward false solutions, such as carbon capture and storage, fossil‐based hydrogen, and far‐off net zero plans, all while pumping out more and more atmosphere‐destroying fossil fuels.”
As suggested above, many ENGOs also advocate climate actions that go well beyond the current status quo. This includes supply‐side climate policies – policies to directly halt fossil expansions, or to wind down and phase out fossil fuels – which were common among ED, GP and DSF. An op‐ed by ED's Keith Brooks in The Globe and Mail asserted that “to avoid the most devastating consequences of climate change…leaves no choice but to wind down Canada's oil and gas industry. And unless we start planning for this now, workers and communities will be left out of the transition to a low‐carbon future.” Renewable energy advocacy and calls for energy transition were also common across the organizations especially by DSF, GP, ED and Pembina. These sometimes combined with justice lenses, such as in an op‐ed by Greenpeace's Christy Ferguson in the Toronto Star, which asserted that “The good news is that addressing the climate emergency will put a lot of people to work…It can also mean more equitable sharing of resources and wealth, better working conditions, a job guarantee for every affected worker and authentic reconciliation with, and leadership from, Indigenous peoples.”

5.4.

## EM and climate justice frames
To address whether ENGO framing in media conforms to discourses of EM, or if and how it goes beyond this narrative through more conflict‐laden framing and via appeals to climate justice, Table  combines prognostic frames that accord closely with ecomodernist policy frameworks and compares these to more transformative justice frames. Diagnostic frames were also organized into two categories: (1) state (in)action and poor governance; and (2) industry impacts and critique.
 In terms of prognostic framing, measures corresponding to EM predominate. As suggested above, this is especially prominent within Pembina's advocacy as well as that of the World Wildlife Fund. Nevertheless, advocacy of more transformative changes, including critiques of false solutions, are still prominent across the sample, and especially in the media advocacy of ED, Greenpeace and DSF.
Considering diagnostic framing, state (in)action and poor policy is cited in equal measure to industry impacts and opposition. With the exception of the World Wildlife Fund, ENGOs in the sample regularly demarcate specific actors as responsible for climate change or for forestalling climate action, moving beyond the tendency in post‐political discourse to target only abstract and impersonal opponents (e.g., carbon dioxide emissions) (Wetts, ).
Interestingly, we find a remarkable consistency in framing across the news outlets. Technology, market, and conservation focused solutions are slightly amplified in CBC News (accounting for 33 percent of frames), while frames associated with the environmental justice and transformative change category are more prominent in the Gazette (26 percent of frames). Yet, compared to the overall tabulations, we do not find considerable variance in framing across the outlets.

6.

## CONCLUSION AND DISCUSSION
News media is an important venue of social movement advocacy, especially for reaching broad audiences, and can be considered part of the structure of political opportunities that enable social movements to succeed. High rates of climate change coverage and a national news environment that focuses on climate policy (Callison & Tindall, ; Stoddart et al., ) is a valuable opening for organizations struggling to shape opinion, consciousness, and policy on climate change. We find that ENGO have seized upon this opportunity, as their climate‐related frames, claims and initiatives regularly circulate across mainstream news media. ENGOs thereby have an important voice in shaping how the climate crisis is framed in news media.
In addition to quantitatively tracking movement representation, we provide a qualitative frame analysis. In terms of motivational framing, we find that ENGOs have moved beyond assertions of the scientific consensus on climate change and instead frame climate change as an emergency, stressing both the current impacts and the relevant time frame for action. Public polling research suggests that such framing is effective; a 2019 nation‐wide poll found that only 7 percent of Canadian believe climate change is a myth and while over 60 percent believed climate change to be an emergency, or that it will become so in the next few years (Abacus Data, ).
In terms of prognostic or climate solutions‐focused framing, advocacy of market‐based policy measures and technological innovations is pronounced. This is especially prominent in the media work of Pembina and the World Wildlife Fund but features across all organizations. Thus, much ENGO media advocacy mirrors the tendency of governments and policymakers to focus on EM. Such an approach, as Haas, Syrovatka, and Jürgen assert ( ), sees climate action as occurring “through incumbent structures according to tightly disciplined technical knowledges and innovations” (p. 248), while avoiding “questioning of social power relations” and actions that have “a socially transformative thrust” (p. 247). Due to the prevalence of such framing, climate discourse has been characterized as ‘post‐political’ (Kenis & Lievens, ; Swyngedouw, ; Wetts, ).
ENGO framing goes beyond this narrative. Assertions of the incompatibility of fossil expansion and climate commitments, together with critiques of technological measures like CCUS, which are common among Environmental Defense, Greenpeace and the David Suzuki Foundation, pose a challenge to a consensual post‐politics that aims to “eliminate fundamental conflict” in favor of “participatory negotiation of different interests” (Swyngedouw, , p. 228). As Ramos suggests ( ), ENGOs also use social justice narratives – especially highlighting harms to Indigenous communities posed by extractive projects – to counter the economy‐centric frames utilized by governments to justify resource extraction, a strategy that we find has garnered media attention.
Critiques of the fossil sector are accompanied by assertions of need for both increased public investments in energy transition and muscular regulatory measures to phase out fossil fuels. Such supply‐side climate policies likewise pose a clear challenge to incumbent industry power and are essential as governments and companies must decommission ongoing production to reach current decarbonization targets (Trout et al., ).
We also find that ENGO media advocacy moves beyond regulatory interventions to advance solutions‐focused climate and environmental justice lenses, which appear to be a growing part of ENGO discourse. This includes appeals to just transition and green jobs. Reflecting the settler colonial context, ENGOs also often assert that the resolution of environmental problems must advance Indigenous rights and decolonization and advocate Indigenous leadership in environmental efforts. While there are historic and ongoing tensions between Indigenous and “mainstream” environmentalisms (Bacon, ; Thomas‐Muller, ), ENGO campaigns no longer proceed without regard to the territorial claims of First Nations.
How justice‐based claims of ENGOs shape government and industry action is challenging to assess. Some research suggests that governments and industry leaders are co‐opting the implementation of environmental justice policies (Harrison, ). Indeed, the federal government's current climate framework, A Cleaner, Greener Future names a commitment to several dimensions of environmental and climate justice, including Indigenous‐led conservation, the protection of marginalized communities from environmental hazards, and just and equitable energy transition, yet it deviates from core principles of EJ and CJ in its broader reliance on market‐based and voluntary mechanisms of change and its failure to meaningfully redistribute power over decision making (see also Indigenous Climate Action, ).
While it is vital to avoid a process of co‐optation, the inclusion of justice concepts in federal policy may present a valuable opening for ENGOs focused on climate justice. Indeed, our research suggests a growing connection between climate justice and Indigenous rights that is beginning to resonate in mainstream news media. Increasingly, advocacy of Indigenous leadership in conservation efforts is extended to field of climate action through the promotion of nature‐based or nature‐derived climate solutions. Such solutions, if they are designed and implemented in ways that respect Indigenous rights and authority, can be a source of Indigenous empowerment (Indigenous Climate Action, ). Likewise, renewable energy advocacy sometimes combines with calls for Indigenous leadership in energy transition. This suggests that an important tactic for ENGOs to shape climate policy lies in building cross‐sectoral coalitions with Indigenous activists.
In pressing for justice‐oriented transitions, it is worth noting key absences in the discourse. Overall, we find few frames in media that point to the need for broader processes of democratization, via avenues such as participatory planning and public ownership in energy. While such an approach to climate justice is currently outside of mainstream news media discourse, it is needed to address issues of corporate power and corporate control over decarbonization pathways (Newell et al., ). We also found little international focus, and little focus on global justice. Recognition of the privileges of environmentalists in the Global North and their obligations to support the Global South (through means such as technology transfer, repayment of climate debt, and other forms of global redistribution) is virtually absent in their media stories. Social justice advocacy rarely crosses borders.
Our study has some limitations, offering space for further research. Key among them is that we did not track the accuracy of ENGO citations or examine how claims are mediated by journalists. In this vein, our finding that there is high degree of consistency in ENGO framing across the media outlets, despite different media geographies (western vs. central Canada, national vs. provincial), is noteworthy. This may partly reflect media consolidation – four of the outlets belong to Postmedia, and news stories are often repeated across the chain – but it points more generally to media gatekeeping and the way news reporting remains tethered to existing narratives on energy and resource development. To consider such gatekeeping, triangulation is needed via an analysis of website and social media communications. Interviews with ENGO protagonists could also consider the tactics they employ in relation to media. Therefore, an additional activist‐centered layer of analysis would enrich and support our text‐centered and parameterized approach.
Our study was limited to English speaking organizations and mainstream English language media, while comparison with other media, including alternative media and social media would be illuminating and may simultaneously offer additional insights into both ENGO media strategy and media gatekeeping (Wood & Goldstein, ). Finally, whether and how the mediated visibility found here translates into policy impacts is another question beyond the scope of our study.
While climate action plans have been adopted federally and provincially, Canada has missed all major federal climate targets set to date and has subsequently gained notoriety as a laggard in taking effective climate action (Burck et al., ). There is ample space to go beyond the status quo and build a new environmentalism that is effective in articulating an ecologically sustainable and egalitarian path forward. Commitments to climate action by industry and government, alongside significant climate policy debate within mainstream news media, offer valuable opportunities for environment movements struggling to shape public consciousness and policy. The emergence of climate justice frames by Canadian environmental organizations and their resonance in the news media reveals a schism in the post‐political order and offers a potential opening for new narratives that can be mobilized for rapid and deep climate action.
