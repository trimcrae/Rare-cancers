## Background
Little is known about the oral health profile of forcibly displaced persons (internally displaced persons (IDPs), and refugees), and migrants in Nigeria, despite being one of the largest communities of people with humanitarian service needs in the world. This study assessed the associations between dental caries prevention knowledge, self-care dental caries preventive practices, and dental caries prevention knowledge among IDPs, refugees, and migrants in Nigeria.

## Methods
This was a secondary analysis of the data of a cross-sectional study conducted in five states in Nigeria—Borno, Taraba, Yobe, Cross River, and Benue— with large populations of refugees, IDPs, and migrants in December 2024. The data of 1554 women (IDPs: 690; migrants: 281; and refugees: 583) who had children in their care were extracted for this study. The data extracted were the dependent variables (knowledge of dental caries prevention, daily use of fluoridated toothpaste, daily tooth brushing frequency, and daily frequency of consumption of refined carbohydrates between meals), the confounding variables (educational level, current marital status, and age at last birthday), and the independent variables (IDPs, refugees, and migrants’ status). Multivariable logistic regression models were used to determine the associations between the dependent and independent variables after adjusting for the confounders.

## Results
Both IDPs (AOR: 3.68, p < 0.001) and migrants (AOR: 3.89, p < 0.001) had significantly higher odds of good dental caries prevention knowledge compared to refugees. On the other hand, IDPs (AOR = 0.22, p < 0.001) and migrants (AOR: 0.56, p < 0.001) were less likely to brush twice daily than refugees. of daily use of fluoride-containing toothpaste every time or almost every time than refugees, but the difference was not statistically significant. On the converse, both IDPs and migrants had lower odds of consumption of refined carbohydrates in-between-meals daily, but the difference was not statistically significant.

## Conclusion
Although IDPs and migrants demonstrated better knowledge of dental caries prevention compared to refugees, they seemed less likely than refugees to brush twice daily. This highlights a disconnect between knowledge and practices in displacement settings. Studies are needed to explore contextual drivers of dental caries preventive knowledge and practices among IDPs, migrants, and refugees.

## Introduction
Forcibly displaced persons (Internally displaced persons (IDPs), and refugees) and migrants face significant oral health inequities compared to local populations [ , ]. They have a higher burden of untreated dental caries [ ] and periodontal diseases [ ] than the local population. These disparities are rooted in systemic inequalities, compounded by the trauma of displacement and limited access to healthcare services [ ]. While global research has increasingly drawn attention to the oral health burdens among these populations often facing humanitarian crisis, little is known about their oral health burden in Africa despite the continent hosting about 26% of the world’s refugees [ ], and the largest number of forcibly displaced persons globally [ ]. The proportion of women, girls, and children among this population is high, with women and girls constituting 51% of all IDPs and 49% of IDPs being children [ ].
Nigeria has the largest population of IDPs in Africa [ ]. In 2014, Nigeria recorded the highest number of IDPs in Africa with 3.3 million people, accounting for approximately one-third of the continent’s IDPs and 10% of the global total [ ]. The Boko-haram crisis in the northeast primarily drives this, farmers-herders clashes in the middle belt, the bandits in the northwest, as well as communal land disputes, all of which are further intensified by environmental disasters such as flooding, soil erosion, and drought [ , ]. As of May 2025, Nigeria was hosting 138,154 refugees and asylum seekers, and 3,575,114 IDPs [ ]. IDPs are largely identified in States in Northern Nigeria (Sokoto, Zamfara, Katsina, Kano, Borno, Yobe, Adamawa, Taraba, Benue), and refugees are largely identified in Kano, Borno, Yobe, Adamawa, Taraba, Benue, Cross-River, Akwa-Ibom, and Lagos [ ]. The number of migrants is also large: in 2022, the country was home to about 1,308,568 million international migrants, which represents 0.63% of its population [ ].
The few studies on the oral health care needs of IDPs, refugees, and migrants in Nigeria indicate that the unmet treatment needs are huge. A study conducted among IDPs in Adamawa State indicated that the proportion of those with dental caries is as high as 26.7%, with 24.5% of them experiencing pain, and the majority had poor oral hygiene [ ]. The unmet treatment need among the population ranged from 76.7% to 93% [ ], with a low dental treatment index of 30.6%, a zero restorative index, and only 16.2% seeking treatment in the hospital for dental pain [ ]. PUFA/pufa score was 0.06/0.02 in one of the IDP camps, with a poorer education level associated with the risk for periodontal disease [ ]. Information about the health status of refugees and migrants in Nigeria is less accessible despite the growing number of intra-African migrations [ ]. Where information is accessible, the health status of IDPs tends to be worse due to the particularly vulnerable situation of IDPs relative to other populations, including reduced access to health services [ ].
With such a large population of IDPs, refugees, and migrants in Nigeria, with a fragmented health system depended on out-of-pocket financing [ ], poor access to oral health care [ ], negligible presence of trained oral health care workers in conflict-affected areas [ ], negligible funding of oral health [ ], and a poorly prepared primary health care system to handle crisis [ ], it is important to promote oral health and oral disease prevention among the population. Oral health preventive practice such as twice daily toothbrushing [ ], use of fluoridated toothpaste [ ], low sugar consumption in-between-meals [ ], and the use of dental services for preventive care [ ] are driven by oral health knowledge: when the oral health knowledge is good, oral health preventive practices is better [ ].
The aim of this study, therefore, was to assess the oral health knowledge and dental caries preventive practices of forcibly displaced persons (female IDPs, female refugees), and female migrants in Nigeria who have heightened vulnerability from gender-based marginalization to severely limited access to healthcare [ ], including oral health services. Their specific oral health needs, shaped by factors such as pregnancy [ ] and caregiving roles, are often overlooked in humanitarian settings. As primary caregivers, improving their oral health knowledge can lead to better outcomes for entire families [ ]. Yet, gender-disaggregated data on oral health within these groups is scarce. Data on the oral health needs of women can highlight their specific oral health needs that can help in the planning of targeted, equitable interventions.
Specifically, the study assessed the associations between the dental caries prevention oral health knowledge and self-care dental caries preventive practices (toothbrushing frequency, use of fluoridated toothpaste, and consumption of refined carbohydrates) among women with children who identified as IDPs, refugees, and migrants in Nigeria.

## Methods

## Study design and participants
This was a secondary analysis of data collected through a larger cross-sectional study conducted across five Nigerian states—Borno, Taraba, Yobe, Cross River, and Benue—selected due to high concentrations of refugees, IDPs, and migrants. The primary study assessed HIV response needs in these populations. The data of women with children who were participants in the primary study were extracted.

## Study locations
Participant recruitment sites were identified through state-level stakeholder meetings, taking into account population distribution, accessibility, and security. Each state had designated camps and settlements where data were collected: (i) Borno: Jere, Maiduguri, and Konduga LGAs (multiple IDP, refugee, and migrant camps). (ii) Taraba: Bali, Sarduna, and Adro-Kola LGAs (Bali Camp, Gembu Settlement, Kuffia Jalingo). (iii) Yobe: Geidam, Bada, and Damaturu LGAs (various IDP, migrant, and refugee locations). (iv) Cross River: Ogoja LGA (Adagom 1, Adagom 3, and Ukende refugee settlements). (v) Benue: Kwande and Guma LGAs (Ikyogen Settlement, Mega, Daudu, and Abagena IDP Camps).

## Study populations
The primary study enrolled women who were refugees, IDPs, and migrants with children 0-5 years old in their care. Participants provided informed consent. Individuals unable to participate due to severe health issues were excluded.

## Sample size
The Yamane formula for finite populations [ ] was applied to calculate the sample size for the primary study, ensuring a statistically valid representation of the population. Using a total population (N) of 1,036,959 and a 5% margin of error (e), the minimum required sample size was determined to be 1,928. To improve representativeness across states with varying camp densities and demographic profiles, this base sample was proportionally weighted according to each state’s share of the total population, resulting in a final weighted sample size of 3,856. In addition, proportional stratification by age group, covering infants, children, adolescents, and adults, was implemented to reflect their estimated distribution within each state’s IDP population. This sub-study extracted the data of 1554 adult women (aged 18 years and above) who had children below the age of 6 years in their care (IDPs: 690; migrants: 281; and refugees: 583).

## Sampling and recruitment
A mixed non-probabilistic sampling approach was employed. Specifically, a combination of venue-based sampling at the designated camps and settlements and snowball sampling were used for recruiting study participants. Community leaders and peer networks facilitated participant recruitment by educating their community members about the study. Respondents then received coupons to invite their peers, implementing a snowball sampling technique to expand reach into hidden populations. Respondents received coupons to invite peers, expanding reach into hidden populations.
State-assigned monitors supported data collectors to ensure diversity and logistical coordination. Informed consent was obtained (read aloud and interviewer-signed), and surveys were completed by in-person interviews, uploaded using encrypted tablets, to maintain confidentiality.
Each study team included a site leader, three research assistants, three community entry leaders, and a state monitor selected for prior project experience. Following ethical approval, site leaders and research assistants received protocol, ethics, and communication training in November 2023 and 2024. State teams also held sensitization meetings with community entry leaders and monitors, followed by one-day site-level training to plan data collection.
A field worker was dedicated to recruiting a specific population (IDP, refugees, or migrants). The field worker worked with community entry leaders to engage community members. Efforts were made to ensure diversity of study participants by typology (children, adolescents, men, and women – single, married, mothers, widows), and vulnerability (HIV positive, sexual and gender minority individuals). Participants’ recruitment continued for the six days in the respective states in December 2024.

## Questionnaire development and field testing
The structured survey tool was developed using a validated questionnaire used for the Nigeria Demographic Health Survey [ ] and the community HIV service delivery survey used to collect Community Led Monitoring Data [ ]. It covered health and HIV service access, essential supplies, barriers to care, and human rights violations. The tool also included an oral health survey questionnaire adopted from prior community-level oral health survey tools used in Nigeria [ , ]. Field testing of the survey tool and stakeholder consultations (Jan 29–Feb 2, 2024) ensured the cultural relevance of the questionnaire.

## Language and translation
The survey was administered in English and translated into Yoruba, Hausa, and Ibibio. For other dialects, real-time translation was facilitated by local leaders. All translations were performed by native speakers, back-translated for consistency, and refined through pilot testing.

## Data collection
Data were collected through encrypted tablet/phone-assisted self-interviews or in-person interviews using an online platform. Consent was read aloud and documented before participation. All responses were securely transmitted and stored. The research team that collected the data was trained on data collection methodology. Community sensitization meetings were held ahead of the data collection through identified community leaders. Site-level training was also conducted for the community leaders who served as community entry leads, to ensure readiness for fieldwork. The questionnaire was administered in the language the participants were comfortable with. All field workers were familiar with the local language where the study was conducted.

## Study variables

## Independent variable

## Displacement status
IDPs, refugees, and migrants were recruited for the study.
An IDP is someone who has been compelled to leave their home due to conflict, violence, or disaster but remains within their country’s borders [ ]. A refugee is an individual who has been forced to leave their country to escape persecution, conflict, or violence and has been officially granted asylum in Nigeria [ ]. A migrant is someone who relocates—either within or across borders—primarily in search of employment or improved living conditions [ ]. Participants self-identified as any of these statuses and were confirmed as a member of the community by the community lead.

## Dependent variables

## Knowledge of dental caries prevention
Respondents rated eight statements on dental caries diagnosis and prevention using a five-point Likert scale: “Strongly agree”, “Agree”, “Disagree”, “Strongly Disagree”, and “Do not know”. The statements covered fluoride use, sugar intake, sealants, brushing habits, and dental visits. Each statement was scored from 5 (Strongly agree) to 1 (Do not know or missing), yielding a total possible score range of 8–40 [ , ]. Responses of “Strongly Agree” and “Agree” were considered correct. The mean score per respondent was calculated as their knowledge score. Scores were dichotomized using the median cut-off of 21: scores below 21 indicated poor knowledge, while scores of 21 or above indicated good knowledge [ ].

## Daily use of fluoridated toothpaste
Participants reported how often they used fluoride toothpaste when brushing their teeth daily, choosing from: “Always,” “Quite often,” “Seldom,” “Not at all,” or “no response”. To help determine the fluoride content of toothpastes used, participants were asked to name the toothpaste. The field worker was able to determine if the toothpaste contained fluoride by verifying the product label or confirming the brand’s fluoride content through standard reference lists available during data collection.
For analysis, responses were recoded into a binary variable: ‘yes’ (Always/Quite often) and ‘no’ (Seldom/Not at all). “Always” and “Quite often” were the acceptable responses as regular fluoride use had been identified as associated with dental caries prevention for populations in Nigeria [ ].

## Daily tooth brushing frequency
Participants indicated how frequently they brushed their teeth daily, with options ranging from “Irregularly or never” to “More than twice a day,” including a no-response option. Responses were dichotomized as ‘yes’ (Twice a day or more) and ‘no’ (Once a day or less). Toothbrushing twice a day or more was the acceptable cut-off mark for dental caries prevention for populations in Nigeria [ , ].

## Daily frequency of consumption of refined carbohydrates in-between meals
Participants were asked how often they consumed refined carbohydrates between meals, with options from “Rarely or never” to “Three times a day or more,” plus a no-response option. Responses were recoded as: ‘yes’ (Less than 3 times per day) and ‘no’ (3 or more times per day). Consumption of refined carbohydrates in-between-meals less than three times per day was the acceptable cut-off mark for dental caries prevention for populations in Nigeria [ ].

## Confounders
Information was also collected on the women’s educational level. The level of education was classified as follows: no formal education, Quranic and primary school education, secondary school education, and tertiary education. Information was also collected on the current marital status and age at last birthday. Participants reported their marital status by selecting one of the following categories: Single, Married, Cohabiting, Separated, Divorced, or Widowed.

## Statistical analysis
Descriptive statistics included frequencies and percentages for categorical variables (displacement status, marital status, education, and oral health practices) and means with standard deviations for age. Multivariate logistic regression models were used to estimate the adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for indicators of good dental caries prevention knowledge, good toothbrushing practice (≥ 2 times/day), regular use of fluoridated toothpaste, and consumption of refined carbohydrates in-between meals less than three times a day. The independent variable was the displacement status (IDPs, refugees, migrants). The confounders were age, marital status, and education level.
No multicollinearity (VIF < 2.0) was detected, and the study model had a good fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow p > 0.05). In addition, there was < 5% missing data for all variables, and this was handled using complete-case analysis. Binary variables were defined using validated cut-offs. Findings were disaggregated by displacement status to expose disparities. Analyses were conducted in Stata 18.0. AORs with 95% CIs not crossing 1.0 and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Sensitivity analyses excluding incomplete responses confirmed result robustness.

## Ethical considerations
Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institute of Public Health (IPHOAU/12/2408) and the respective State Ministries of Health in Benue (MOH/STA/204/260), Borno (190/2023), Cross River (CRS/MH/HREC/023/Vol 1/280 and CRS/MH/HREC/2024/Vol 2/022), Taraba (TRSHREC/2023/APP/034 and TRSHREC/2024/APP/034), and Yobe (MOH/GEN/747/Vol 1). The study adhered strictly to ethical guidelines, including obtaining informed consent, maintaining confidentiality, and ensuring participant anonymity.
Efforts were made to ensure inclusive gender representation throughout the study. Gender-sensitive needs assessments were conducted, and HIV response activities were tailored to address specific gender-related needs. Data collectors received training on gender sensitivity and inclusive practices.
Participant safety and data security were prioritized, drawing on insights from previous surveys and stakeholder consultations to inform study design. The study was conducted anonymously without collecting personal identifiers. Instead, unique codes were assigned to maintain confidentiality and mitigate any potential risks of harm or exploitation.
Data collection was conducted via a secure, SSL-encrypted platform, without the need for personal identifiers, email addresses, or phone numbers. All data transmissions were encrypted using Transport Layer Security protocols in compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation and Privacy Shield standards. No geolocation or IP address data was collected. Comprehensive safeguards were implemented to protect participants from discrimination, stigmatization, and breaches of confidentiality or anonymity.

## Results
Table  shows that IDPs formed the largest bulk of the 1554 participants (n = 690), followed by refugees (n = 583) and migrants (n = 281). The age of study participants ranged from 18 to 94 years, while the mean age across participants was 32.5 years (Standard Deviation = 10.8). In addition, 855 (55.0%) of respondents demonstrated good knowledge of dental caries prevention, 581 (62.6%) brushed at least twice daily, fluoridated toothpaste use was reported by 863 (55.5%) participants, and 1340 (86.2%) reported “never or sometimes” consuming sugar products in between meals.
Those with good knowledge of dental caries prevention were slightly older (mean = 33.4 years) than those with poor knowledge (mean = 31.5 years). IDPs showed relatively better knowledge of dental caries prevention, with 484 (70.1%) of the 690 participants having good knowledge. They, however, had the lowest proportion of respondents using fluoridated toothpaste “every time or almost every time” (n = 356, 51.6%).
Refugees had the least proportion of the population with good knowledge about dental caries prevention, with 181 (31.0%) categorized as having good knowledge, but had the highest proportion of respondents who reported using fluoridated toothpaste frequently (n = 345; 59.2%) and the highest proportion of respondents who consumed refined carbohydrates every time or almost every time in-between-meals daily (n = 87; 14.9%).

 

Table  shows that both IDPs (AOR: 3.68, p < 0.001) and migrants (AOR: 3.89, p < 0.001) had significantly higher odds of good dental caries prevention knowledge compared to refugees. On the other hand, IDPs (AOR = 0.22, p < 0.001) and migrants (AOR: 0.56, p < 0.001) were less likely to brush twice daily than refugees. Both IDPs and migrants had higher odds of daily use of fluoride-containing toothpaste every time or almost every time than refugees, but the difference was not statistically significant. On the converse, both IDPs and migrants had lower odds of consumption of refined carbohydrates in-between-meals daily, but the difference was not statistically significant.

 

## Discussion
The current study identified significant disparities in oral health knowledge and dental caries preventive practices among women IDPs, refugees, and migrants in Nigeria who had children. Notably, women IDPs with children exhibited better knowledge of dental caries prevention compared to women refugees and migrants with children, with refugees having the lowest level of oral health knowledge. However, this higher level of knowledge among women IDPs with children did not consistently translate into preventive practices, as they were significantly less likely to brush their teeth twice daily than women refugees with children.
One of the strengths of the study is that it addresses a critical gap in data from one of Africa’s most affected regions. Its emphasis on women, who face intersecting vulnerabilities due to gender and caregiving roles, enables the development of gender-responsive interventions with potential family-level benefits. The study also recruited a large, geographically diverse sample of participants, enhancing generalizability. It employs robust statistical methods, adjusting for key confounders, and uses validated tools adapted to ensure methodological credibility. Data collection was culturally tailored through multilingual administration and community-based recruitment strategies, improving accessibility and trust. Furthermore, the comprehensive assessment of oral health knowledge and oral health practices provides a nuanced view of the determinants of oral health in displacement settings.
Despite the strengths, the study had limitations. The cross-sectional study design precludes causal inference, making it unclear whether displacement preceded the observed knowledge differences. The use of non-probabilistic sampling methods may have resulted in selection bias, potentially overrepresenting more accessible groups and undercounting hidden populations such as undocumented migrants. Self-reported data are vulnerable to social desirability bias, likely contributing to overestimation of preventive practices. In addition, the exclusive focus on women engaged in caregiving roles limits the generalizability of findings to men and non-caregiving women, though this focus aligns with intersectionality frameworks emphasizing marginalized groups. The secondary nature of the data also restricted access to key clinical oral health indicators, narrowing the scope of behavioural insights. These biases may likely skew results toward an overoptimistic portrayal of the use of dental caries preventive strategies. Furthermore, we also note that while tooth brushing frequency was assessed, data on the duration of brushing and the type of toothbrush used (e.g., manual or electric) were not collected. These factors are important determinants of brushing efficacy and should be included in future studies to provide a more comprehensive assessment of oral hygiene practices. Despite these limitations, the study highlights some important findings.
The current study revealed a compelling paradox: while IDPs in Nigeria had significantly higher odds of possessing good knowledge about dental caries prevention compared to refugees, they were far less likely to practice effective toothbrushing. This disconnect between knowledge and behaviour highlights the possible complex realities of forced displacement, where structural, psychosocial, and gendered barriers can undermine even well-informed health practices. Forced displacement often triggers mental health issues such as depression and post-traumatic stress disorder [ ], which can disrupt decision-making [ – ], motivation for daily self-care routines [ ], and reduce adherence to health advice [ ].
The disconnect between knowledge and practice among IDPs may reflect low perceived susceptibility to dental caries (due to competing survival needs), high perceived barriers (e.g., lack of toothpaste, clean water, or time), and insufficient cues to action (e.g., absence of community health reminders) as explained by the Health Belief Model (HBM) [ ]. Concurrently, Andersen’s Behavioural Model of Health Service Use [ ] highlights how structural deprivations—lack of enabling resources (fluoridated toothpaste, safe water) and contextual stressors (trauma, poverty)—undermine the translation of knowledge into behaviour.
We posit that IDPs lack critical enabling factors needed to translate knowledge into action. While awareness of oral health principles exists, access to fluoridated toothpaste, clean water, and toothbrushes is severely constrained in IDP camps [ ]. Material deprivation is exacerbated by extreme poverty in conflict-affected northern Nigeria, where survival needs (food, shelter) dominate daily priorities. This forces reliance on cheap, refined carbohydrates [ ], further elevating caries risk. In addition, IDPs face unique mental health burdens distinct from refugees and migrants. Unlike refugees, IDPs remain in proximity to active conflict, experiencing recurrent trauma, chronic insecurity, and learned helplessness [ , – ].
In the context of the current study, for IDPs, immediate survival needs (food, shelter, safety) may dominate daily priorities, pushing long-term health concerns like dental caries lower down the agenda. The limited access to enabling resources, the involuntary nature of their displacement erodes agency and long-term health motivation [ ], and the disruption of social networks and community structures (family, school programs, or community health workers that provide regular health reminders) further limits concerns about oral health. Furthermore, mental health sequelae such as depression, PTSD, impair decision-making [ ], and may reduce adherence to self-care routines [ ], directly undermining oral hygiene practices. Together, these models reveal a dual burden: even when knowledge exists (addressed by HBM’s perceived benefits), the absence of enabling resources (Andersen’s enabling factors) and psychosocial trauma (affecting HBM’s cues to action) creates insurmountable barriers to preventive practices.
We strengthened the argument by linking our findings to Andersen’s model, which highlights that the ability to perform a health behavior is not just about knowledge but depends on enabling factors. We argue that refugees, often in more urban or established settings, may have better access to these factors (diaspora networks, non-governmental organisations, urban markets) than IDPs, who are frequently confined to overcrowded camps with fragmented aid and limited infrastructure. This structural disadvantage directly impedes the translation of knowledge into practice.
For women IDPs, caregiving responsibilities intensify these challenges. Intersectional vulnerabilities [ ] —gender, displacement trauma, and poverty—create “time poverty” and limit autonomy [ ]. High rates of gender-based violence [ ] and associated stigma [ ] compound psychological distress, further marginalizing oral health. Refugees, conversely, may access urban health services or diaspora networks [ ], partially mitigating these barriers. This aligns with the Socioecological Model [ ], which considers the interplay of individual, community, and systemic influences, and the rights-based frameworks that demand equitable access to care [ ]. Individual trauma (microsystem), disrupted social networks (mesosystem), and inadequate policy protections (macrosystem) converge to impede IDPs’ health behaviours. Thus, knowledge alone cannot overcome the displacement’s structural and emotional toll. Our findings underscore that oral health initiatives in humanitarian settings must address the entire knowledge-practice-enabling environment continuum.
Addressing the gap requires culturally grounded, multi-sectoral interventions that tackle the real-world barriers to practicing good oral hygiene in humanitarian settings. Future programmes should address HBM constructs (e.g., campaigns to amplify perceived susceptibility and self-efficacy) while ensuring Andersen’s enabling resources (e.g., distributing oral hygiene kits) are accessible. There is also a need to advocate for dedicated funding and policy reforms that recognize the rights of IDPs to health [ ]. Future research should quantify mental health-oral health linkages and test trauma-informed interventions.
Of interest, however, is ‘why’ and ‘how’ the mental health impact of forced displacement seems to impact on IDPs than migrants and refugees in the current study. Unlike refugees, however, IDPs lack international legal protection, and many live near active conflict zones, experiencing repeated trauma and ongoing insecurity that fuel chronic stress, post-traumatic stress disorder, and anxiety [ , ]. This mental burden is compounded by resource deprivation [ ]. IDP camps are also frequently overcrowded, with limited access to hygiene supplies [ ]. In such environments, the focus shifts to immediate survival, pushing preventive practice to the margins. Social and cultural disruption further exacerbates the crisis as IDPs are disconnected from ancestral communities and kinship networks, lose vital sources of emotional and practical support, and are often stigmatized by host populations [ ]. These gaps leave mental health conditions untreated, feeding a cycle where trauma undermines the very motivation or capacity to engage in healthy behaviours. The experience of forced displacement also robs IDPs of agency. While economic migrants often choose to move, IDPs are uprooted suddenly, with no say in their trajectory. IDPs are, thus, more vulnerable to psychological distress than refugees and migrants.
These patterns align with the Socioecological Model [ ], which highlights how individual trauma, fractured community networks, and systemic policy failures converge. When viewed through an intersectional lens [ ], the compounded vulnerabilities of gender, poverty, and displacement intensify mental health risks. Bridging this gap not only requires integrated mental and oral health services but also community-led support systems and gender-sensitive interventions. Without such holistic responses, knowledge alone cannot overcome the structural and emotional toll of displacement.
The findings also raise critical implications for health systems and policy reform in Nigeria to address the oral health care needs of IDPs, refugees, and migrants. Given the uneven distribution of oral health resources, particularly in displacement settings, targeted policy interventions are needed. These include training and deploying community-based oral health workers, integrating oral hygiene kits into routine humanitarian aid, and establishing mobile dental clinics in camps and remote settlements. Furthermore, oral health must be embedded into Nigeria’s broader Universal Health Coverage agenda, ensuring no displaced population is left behind. This aligns with the World Health Organization’s commitment to rights-based health equity and Sustainable Development Goal 3 on good health and well-being. In addition, culturally adapted oral health literacy campaigns that address trauma, stigma, and local dietary practices, incorporated into adult literacy and maternal education programs in camps, could strengthen intervention uptake. These recommendations build on the earlier ones made by the FDI World Dental Federation and the International Association for Dental, Oral and Craniofacial Research [ ] by ensuring the context appropriateness of the recommendations.
In conclusion, this study highlights that despite the social vulnerabilities of IDPs, refugees, and migrants, female IDPs have better knowledge of dental caries prevention compared to female refugees and female migrants, yet have poor preventive oral health practices. The findings suggest that knowledge is necessary but not sufficient to drive good oral health practices of female IDPs compared to refugees and migrants. Studies are needed to explore these findings to understand the contextual drivers of the differential dental caries preventive knowledge and practices of IDPs, refugees, and migrants in Nigeria. Future studies should explicitly examine regional variations and the duration of displacement as moderators of knowledge-practice gaps.
