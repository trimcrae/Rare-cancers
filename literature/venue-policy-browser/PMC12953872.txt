## Abstract
Despite proven oral pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) efficacy, adherence barriers among men who have sex with men (MSM) persist. Economic viability of digital adherence tools in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) requires validation. We assessed the 6-month within-trial cost-effectiveness of a digital intervention for real-time enhancement of oral PrEP adherence (DIRECT-PrEP) in China, from health system, multipayer, and societal perspectives. Costs were derived from hospital records, and effectiveness was evaluated using quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) and PrEP-adherence days (PADs). Among 442 participants (247 intervention, 195 standard care), incremental costs per QALY gained for the smart pillbox intervention were $19,575.93 (health system), $19,656.66 (multipayer), and $19,844.42 (societal)—all below the cost-effectiveness threshold of three times per capita gross domestic product ($31,200). Costs per PAD gained were $30.46–$30.86, achieving >95% probability of cost-effectiveness at willingness-to-pay thresholds of $47.50–$48.50 per PAD. Notably, the intervention demonstrated greater cost-effectiveness per QALY among participants stratified as more socially vulnerable ($16,259.77–$16,490.41) compared with those classified as less socially vulnerable ($23,514.83–$23,834.38). These results establish the smart-pillbox digital intervention as a cost-effective strategy for oral PrEP adherence within primary healthcare settings in LMICs, particularly when prioritizing more socially vulnerable populations, though long-term scalability requires further validation.
Subject terms: Public health, Epidemiology, Disease prevention, HIV infections

A recent randomised controlled trial demonstrated effectiveness of a smart pillbox for oral HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis adherence in men who have sex with men in China. Here, the authors show that the intervention is cost effective, with greater benefits for more socially vulnerable groups.

## Introduction
Oral pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is a cornerstone of HIV prevention for high-risk populations, including men who have sex with men (MSM) in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) – . Despite its efficacy, suboptimal adherence remains a critical barrier, diminishing protection at low adherence levels and increasing risks of HIV transmission and drug resistance . MSM, as a key sexual minority group, experience heightened vulnerability due to Social Determinants of Health (SDH) spanning (1) conditions of daily life (e.g., behavioral patterns, social support) and (2) structural determinants (e.g., economic status, education) . Consequently, investigating cost-effective interventions for more socially vulnerable MSM is crucial to advance health equity. The urgency of this issue has been further amplified by the COVID-19 pandemic and persistent health inequities have underscored the need for scalable digital solutions in LMICs, where structural constraints—limited healthcare infrastructure, funding shortages, and power supply instability—hinder conventional interventions .
Achieving equity in HIV prevention requires an understanding of not only which interventions are effective, but also the mechanisms underlying their differential performance across SDH strata. SDH factors—such as socioeconomic status, education, and housing stability—directly influence PrEP persistence . Furthermore, behavioral risk patterns among MSM (e.g., multiple sexual partners, engaging in condomless sex, and substance use) are shaped by these broader structural factors and collectively influence HIV risk and PrEP cost-effectiveness. Across diverse settings, suboptimal PrEP adherence among MSM remains a persistent challenge , driven by stigma, socioeconomic marginalization, and discrimination . These barriers are not unique to China but reflect structural determinants common to MSM globally. Moreover, high-risk MSM subgroups may demonstrate greater willingness to adopt PrEP , yet structural barriers—such as limited psychosocial support or economic resources—often undermine adherence , leading to variations in cost-effectiveness across different SDH strata.
UNAIDS estimates that adopting innovative technologies, efficiencies, and approaches could reduce annual HIV response costs by approximately US$7 billion . Although digital adherence technologies [e.g., short message service (SMS) reminders, mobile apps] show promise, evidence of their effectiveness is heterogeneous. For instance, an interactive text-messaging intervention improved PrEP adherence among young MSM in Chicago , yet SMS-based interventions failed among MSM in China and other vulnerable groups – . Moreover, whereas interactive messaging reduced sexual risk behaviors at incremental costs of $19.70–$131.60 per risk-reduction unit , , these approaches face scalability barriers: high start-up costs ($1021 per participant) , declining engagement without reinforcement, and low suitability for populations with limited digital literacy . Crucially, none adequately address the operational challenges of LMICs as effectively as electronic medication monitors (e.g., smart pillbox). Our randomized controlled trial (RCT) showed telemonitoring using electronic medication monitors increased oral PrEP adherence to 83.1% (21.0% net improvement over controls) over 6 months among Chinese MSM . This efficacy stems from its user-centered design: real-time dose confirmation, minimal user input, and offline functionality align with people-centered strategies recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) .
Technologically, electronic medication monitors circumvent LMIC infrastructural barriers via low operational costs, accurate passive data collection, and seamless system integration. Unlike SMS or app-based platforms requiring continuous connectivity, these devices use durable, battery-powered hardware with predominantly one-time procurement costs . Their automated monitoring eliminates reliance on self-reporting, thereby mitigating reporting inaccuracies common in LMICs . Most importantly, their capacity to function independently of grid electricity or internet connectivity is critical in regions such as Sub-Saharan Africa, where mobile network coverage gaps are about 50% .
Nevertheless, cost-effectiveness analyses specific to electronic medication monitors remain scarce. Existing economic evaluations of HIV-related mHealth focus disproportionately on SMS ($132–$314/participant) or video-based interventions ($1021/participant) , , overlooking electronic medication monitors’ potential for higher marginal returns at scale. For example, while two-way texting achieved cost savings for antiretroviral therapy retention in Malawi , similar efficiencies were plausible for electronic medication monitors given their reusable infrastructure.
China’s digital infrastructure has seen comparatively greater investment in recent years. However, contextual differences across LMICs may limit the generalizability of our findings to regions such as Sub-Saharan Africa. Evidence shows that LMIC health facilities often lack reliable internet, electricity, and digital infrastructure essential for implementing adherence technologies. For example, a survey of over 5000 facilities in eight LMICs reported very low digital infrastructure scores . Similar assessments in South and Southeast Asia highlight persistent gaps in electricity, affordable connectivity, and policy support . These disparities highlight the need for a systematic, human-centered, and contextually grounded approach to implementing digital HIV interventions in LMICs.
This study addresses two critical research gaps in digital health interventions for LMICs. First, it provides the economic evaluation of electronic medication monitors for oral PrEP adherence using LMIC-specific cost metrics, including cost per adherence-unit gained and incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs). Second, it adopts an equity-focused lens to assess the affordability of electronic medication monitors among more socially vulnerable and less socially vulnerable MSM by examining device durability and maintenance within LMIC resource constraints, thereby aligning with priorities for sustainable, device-led innovations.
Ultimately, by demonstrating the superiority of electronic medication monitors in cost per adherence-unit metrics and scalability within LMIC health systems, this study aims to inform global oral PrEP rollout strategies—advancing health equity through frugal, context-adapted medical devices.

## Results

## Participant characteristics
Figure  presented the overall study design. Among the 516 participants who completed the baseline assessment, 247 were randomized to the smart pillbox intervention group and 195 to the control group (Supplementary Fig.  ). Baseline health utilities were comparable between groups, and no significant between-group differences in direct healthcare utilization costs were observed at baseline (Supplementary Table  ).
 

## Cost components
Table  summarized the 6-month cost and health outcome profiles. From the health system, multipayer, and societal perspectives, the mean (standard deviation [SD]) cumulative total costs in the intervention group were $1826.77 (434.88), $1846.00 (434.71), and $1950.76 (434.82), respectively. The corresponding values for the control group were $1373.68 (585.34), $1390.19 (585.21), and $1488.52 (585.23). Healthcare utilization costs represented the predominant cost component for both groups (intervention: 78.37%; control: 78.69%; Supplementary Fig.  ), while intervention-related costs accounted for 8.08% of total intervention expenditures. Unadjusted intergroup cost differences were detailed in Table  .
 

## Direct costs: testing and intervention
Costs related to testing averaged $140.27 per participant. The mean intervention cost was $157.66 per participant (Table  ), of which $28.99 (18.39%) was allocated to the smart pillbox. Personnel expenditures constituted the majority of intervention costs at $121.32 per participant (76.95%). Costs associated with questionnaire administration and follow-up support were $2.88 per participant, representing 1.83% of total intervention expenditures (Supplementary Fig.  ).

## Direct costs: health care utilization
The mean total healthcare utilization cost was significantly higher in the intervention group ($1528.85 [434.89]) compared with the control group ($1171.37 [585.35]; P < 0.001, Table  ). Healthcare utilization represented the predominant non-intervention cost category across both groups (Supplementary Fig.  ).

## Indirect costs
Venue and cohort maintenance costs were comparable between groups. However, the intervention group incurred slightly higher expenditures for food and transportation ($19.23 [1.37]) than the control group ($16.52 [1.38]). Time costs and lost productivity were also higher in the intervention group ($20.27 [1.63] vs. $13.83 [1.45]; Table  ).

## Health effect
Table  showed significantly greater improvements in both QALYs and PrEP-adherence days (PADs) in the intervention group compared with the control group during the 6-month study period. The unadjusted mean difference was 32.72 (95% CI: 11.94–40.91) for PADs and 0.02 (95% CI: 0.01–0.02) for QALYs (intervention: 0.96 [SD 0.04] vs. control: 0.94 [SD 0.05]).

## Cost-effectiveness
Table  presented the ICERs calculated from 6-month cost and effectiveness differentials using the SUR model. From the health system, multipayer, and societal perspectives, the smart pillbox intervention yielded ICERs per QALY gained of $19,575.93, $19,656.66, and $19,844.42, respectively. Cost-effectiveness acceptability curves demonstrated a 0.01–98.80% probability of intervention cost-effectiveness across willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds ranging from $10,400 to $31,200. For PADs, the intervention produced ICERs of $30.46, $30.58, and $30.86 per PAD gained from these respective perspectives. A WTP threshold between $47.50 and $48.50 per PAD was needed to achieve >95% probability of cost-effectiveness. Notably, at a WTP threshold equivalent to three times China’s per-capita GDP, the intervention demonstrated cost-effectiveness across all examined perspectives and outcomes compared with standard care. Results from the seemingly unrelated regression (SUR) model that was adjusted for baseline characteristics were presented in Table  and Supplementary Fig.  – .
 The smart pillbox intervention was more cost-effective in the more socially vulnerable group, with ICERs ranging from $16,259.77 to $16,490.41 and a cost-effectiveness probability of 2.71–98.99%. In contrast, the less socially vulnerable group exhibited higher ICERs ($23,514.83–$23,834.38) and lower probability (0.08–82.15%). For PADs, the intervention showed ICERs of $26.73–$27.09 in the more socially vulnerable group but higher ICERs ($30.08–$30.47) in the less socially vulnerable group, both evaluated from the three perspectives (Table  ; Supplementary Fig.  – ).
 

## Sensitivity analyses
Figure  displayed the distribution of bootstrap-replicated net cost and QALY estimates on the cost-effectiveness plane. From the health system perspective, the probability of cost-effectiveness ranged from 0.02% (1/5000 replications) at a WTP threshold of 1 × GDP per capita ($10,400) to 98.80% (4940/5000) at 3× GDP per capita ($31,200). Similarly, under the multipayer perspective, the probability ranged from 0.02% (1/5000) to 98.75% (4938/5000), while the societal perspective showed a range of 0.01% (1/5000) to 98.63% (4932/5000) across the same WTP thresholds. These findings were further supported by the CEAC illustrated in Fig.  .
 

## Robustness checks
Our results exhibited consistent robustness across all sensitivity analyses. First, reanalysis adjusted for baseline characteristics maintained significant improvements (PADs: 29.91 [95% CI: 15.68–44.14]); QALYs: 0.03 [95% CI: 0.02–0.03]), demonstrating consistent robustness (Supplementary Table  ). Second, ordinary least squares (OLS) analysis (baseline-adjusted) yielded comparable ICERs (QALYs: $18,897.47–$19,263.99; PADs: $30.71–$31.30; Supplementary Table  , Figs.  , ). Third, generalized linear models (GLM) estimation of incremental costs revealed a 0.00–97.08% cost-effectiveness probability for QALYs at WTP thresholds of 1–3× GDP, with QALY ICERs of $21,121.95–$21,235.81 and PAD ICERs of $34.14–$34.32 across perspectives (Supplementary Table  ; Supplementary Figs.  , ). Fourth, treating overhead expenses as variable costs yielded an unadjusted societal ICER of $20,741.40, below three times GDP per capita (Supplementary Table  ). Fifth, using an alternative pooled HIV/AIDS utility value produced an unadjusted societal ICER of $19,793.40, also below this threshold (Supplementary Table  ). Sixth, when using an alternative WTP threshold (Supplementary Tables  and ), the cost-effectiveness probability from the societal perspective among the more socially vulnerable group ranged from 9.30% to 98.57%. Finally, reanalysis using Truvada-specific costs (baseline-adjusted) showed that the probability of cost-effectiveness ranged from 5.64% to 99.94% (Supplementary Table  ). Critically, all analyses converged on a near-100% cost-effectiveness probability at the 3 × GDP WTP threshold.

## Discussion
This is an economic evaluation conducted alongside a randomized controlled trial assessing the smart pillbox intervention for improving oral PrEP adherence among MSM in LMICs. Conducted as the largest trial in this domain to date, our multicenter evaluation demonstrated that a 6-month, medication-tailored smart pillbox intervention yielded favorable cost-effectiveness outcomes. The intervention consistently showed ICERs below three times China’s per capita GDP across health system, multipayer, and societal perspectives. Notably, the smart pillbox was more cost-effective and provided greater health promotion value in the more socially vulnerable group than in the less socially vulnerable group. Because the less socially vulnerable group derived no significant benefit, no widening of health disparities occurred across SDH strata. Instead, this digital intervention served as an equitable resource to mitigate inequalities. These findings support the implementation of the smart pillbox as a cost-effective adherence strategy for oral PrEP among MSM, while underscoring the need for contextual adaptation in diverse global settings.
We demonstrate that the smart pillbox is a cost-effective and equity-promoting intervention in low-resource settings. Our analysis demonstrates superior cost-effectiveness in the more socially vulnerable group, with ICERs 31% lower than in less socially vulnerable group, revealing its potential as an equity-promoting technology. In regions like Sub-Saharan Africa, about 50% of rural populations lack stable internet access . Traditional SMS-based PrEP reminders often fail low-literacy groups due to text comprehension barriers and incur recurrent costs from mobile network dependencies. The MPYA trial in Kenya, for instance, showed reduced efficacy among women with lower education . Conversely, smart pillbox bypasses these constraints through offline monitoring (e.g., door-open sensors) and SDH-responsive design (e.g., visual/audio alerts for low-literacy users). This reduces marginal costs by shifting expenditure from recurrent connectivity fees to one-time hardware procurement. Policymakers should pair subsidies stratified by level of social vulnerability with task-shifted maintenance programs training community health workers (CHWs) in basic repairs, as seen in rural South Africa’s community-led models that enhanced device use . Future research should quantify long-term equity gains from hybrid designs (e.g., pillbox-SMS integrations) and assess the feasibility of cross-border manufacturing to mitigate LMIC (e.g., in Zimbabwe and other settings across Sub-Saharan Africa , ) cost burdens. By recalibrating PrEP delivery from blanket coverage to precision equity, this technology helps reduce—not reinforce—structural health disparities.
Reducing and adjusting the pricing of oral PrEP to improve accessibility for vulnerable populations, combined with digital adherence interventions such as the smart pillbox, is critical for advancing health equity. Our economic evaluation specifically assessed Truvada, which costs $286.96 per box (30 tablets) in China. However, domestically manufactured generic emtricitabine–tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF/FTC) formulations are now available, including Taihe (Anhui Baker Biopharmaceutical, $41/box), Teweida (Qilu Pharmaceutical, $37/box), and Xinbitai (Shanghai Desano, $130/box) . In contrast, emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide (Descovy, Gilead Sciences) and its domestically produced generic equivalents remain prohibitively expensive . This price differential indicates that reducing the cost of domestic generic PrEP formulations, when integrated with digital adherence tools like the smart pillbox, could substantially improve the cost-effectiveness of oral PrEP delivery among MSM in China, thereby promoting health equity.
Despite the promise of long-acting HIV medications , , their high costs and limited cost-effectiveness , necessitate the continued role of oral PrEP, enhanced by digital interventions and tailored approaches for diverse populations. Meanwhile, future HIV prevention strategies must address diverse mechanisms, treatment phases, and clinical contexts. Although long-acting PrEP holds promise, it alone cannot eradicate HIV. This reality underscores the imperative for innovative, participant-centered approaches tailored to heterogeneous population needs. Ensuring access to diversified prevention options—including long-acting formulations—is critical, particularly for individuals experiencing SDH barriers and suboptimal oral PrEP adherence. Given their user-friendly, customizable, and cost-effective profile, reminder-supported daily and event-driven oral PrEP regimens remain vital alternatives within comprehensive HIV prevention programs . Advancing health equity demands accessible, cost-effective, and adaptable prevention options prioritizing marginalized groups. This reduces disparities and promotes universal health through inclusive strategies.
A critical consideration in interpreting our results concerns the choice of WTP thresholds. Since no specific threshold has been formally established in China, we followed the WHO-CHOICE framework and prior publications – , adopting a cost-effectiveness threshold of 1–3 times China’s 2020 per capita GDP. While this benchmark has been widely used, WTP thresholds are inherently arbitrary. They may fail to capture societal values or budget constraints in LMICs, especially from an equity perspective. To address this limitation, we conducted sensitivity analyses using alternative thresholds. Specifically, we applied a Value of Statistical Life (VSL) conversion , which yielded a point estimate of 1.45 times GDP per capita (range: 1.16–2.90). Under this threshold, the probability range of cost-effectiveness narrowed, which enhanced the practicality of the results, but the intervention was no longer considered cost-effective at the point estimate. This finding suggests that conclusions drawn from the conventional 3× GDP rule must be interpreted with caution, because such thresholds have limited external validity and often fail to reflect the societal preferences or budget realities of LMIC settings.
Personnel expenditures constituted the dominant share of intervention costs and raised concerns regarding scalability. Much of this burden reflected trial-specific activities (e.g., additional monitoring, intensive data collection, and study-related counseling), which are not fully representative of routine service delivery. Evidence from LMIC studies suggests that task-shifting to community or paraprofessional workers can reduce personnel costs substantially, while maintaining acceptable quality of care . For digital adherence interventions such as smart pillboxes or digital pillbox programmes (as observed in tuberculosis adherence studies), once infrastructure is in place and activities move from trial-specific to more routine settings, labor cost offsets and economies of scale tend to emerge, lowering the per-participant personnel cost . Taken together, while personnel expenditures were a major driver of our trial costs, they are likely to diminish during programmatic roll-outs, which would enhance the feasibility of implementing this intervention at scale in LMIC health systems. However, in settings with extreme resource constraints or fragmented health infrastructure, further adaptation and contextualization of the staffing model would be essential to ensure sustainability.
Our findings on the cost-effectiveness of smart pillboxes add to the growing evidence on digital PrEP adherence tools by highlighting scalability and sustainability in LMICs. Although methodological differences limit direct comparison, digital interventions show wide economic variability. For instance, a two-way texting program in Malawi reported an ICER of $24,705 per additional client retained , while our analysis estimated cost per QALY gained—underscoring the contextual diversity of evaluation approaches. Compared with SMS systems reliant on continuous network access and costly at small scale , and app-based platforms with high upfront costs and limited reach in low-resource settings , , smart pillboxes represent a mid-complexity alternative. Operating offline and requiring minimal user input, they may provide a more sustainable and equitable solution, especially where infrastructure gaps persist. This hypothesis warrants validation through future comparative effectiveness studies. Notably, smart pillboxes embed an equity logic: automated data capture mitigates self-report bias and their offline functionality bridges the digital divide that often excludes more socially vulnerable populations , supporting the value of passive monitoring tools in marginalized groups.
In our study, cost-effectiveness differed across SDH strata, underscoring the need to consider the mechanisms driving these disparities. As a key sexual minority group, MSM experience heightened vulnerability due to SDH factors that encompass both behavioral patterns and structural determinants. These factors jointly shape PrEP uptake, persistence, and the feasibility of digital health tools. Evidence shows that high-risk behavioral profiles are strongly associated with elevated HIV acquisition among MSM , while socioeconomic constraints such as unstable housing and limited income significantly undermine PrEP adherence in LMIC settings . Psychosocial factors, particularly stigma and discrimination, further exacerbate adherence barriers and contribute to intersectional inequities . Illustratively, a door-to-door HIV testing initiative in Uganda , although costing only $8.29 per participant, failed to reach daily-wage workers who feared losing income during clinic waits, highlighting how unaddressed socioeconomic barriers erode nominal efficiency gains. Against this backdrop, smart pillboxes enhance adherence by automating data capture during routine use, aligning with findings that user-friendly digital devices can improve uptake among vulnerable groups in resource-limited settings . Collectively, these findings suggest that stratified cost-effectiveness reflects broader contextual burdens rather than intervention efficacy alone. In our trial, the greater cost-effectiveness among socially vulnerable groups likely resulted from the smart pillbox reducing barriers such as forgetfulness in unstable living environments and privacy concerns linked to stigma, which are often insufficiently addressed in conventional care.
This study presents the economic evaluation of a smart pillbox in LMICs. Methodological innovations include real-world SDH integration (simultaneous modeling of sexual behavioral, economic, educational, and marital determinants—beyond common dichotomized approaches) and holistic cost accounting (including participant-incurred expenses such as travel and lost wages, typically excluded in high-income country trials). The smart pillbox intervention enables the collection of objective passive monitoring data, reducing bias associated with self-reported measures. Additionally, it can operate offline, providing a distinct advantage for implementation in low-resource settings with limited internet or smartphone access. Smart pillboxes enhance efficiency and precision—critical amid unprecedented funding cuts to HIV services. They facilitate early responses, optimize resource allocation, and empower communities to address daily challenges through actionable, equitable solutions.
However, six limitations warrant caution. First, the 6-month follow-up likely underestimates long-term costs and outcomes. Although our evaluation demonstrates robust short-term cost-effectiveness, it does not capture maintenance-phase impacts. Many digital intervention studies rely on static models that may overestimate cost-effectiveness by ignoring long-term adherence and system adaptations. In contrast, dynamic models suggest sustained value, but further research is needed to incorporate full cost components, subgroup heterogeneity, and the entire technology lifecycle into long-term assessments . Second, our health economics evaluation of oral PrEP relied on 2020 Truvada pricing, which may not reflect subsequent price reductions. Moreover, the introduction of domestic generic oral PrEP formulations has substantially lowered costs, potentially enhancing the cost-effectiveness of digital PrEP interventions among populations vulnerable to SDH. Third, although our sensitivity analyses examined multiple statistical methods and cost perspectives, they primarily varied patient-level costs and therefore produced only marginal changes in ICER estimates. This approach did not adequately account for system-level uncertainties—such as the balance of fixed and variable program costs, potential scale effects in digital interventions, and the health system’s ability to maintain long-term adherence gains. These structural drivers may have a much larger impact on cost-effectiveness and thus limit the generalizability and policy relevance of our findings. Future studies should incorporate scenario analyses and system-level modeling to identify conditions under which more substantive economic differences may emerge. Fourth, system-side costs were reported as point estimates (e.g., per-participant software development costs) without clarifying fixed versus variable cost proportions, limiting scalability assessment in larger populations. Fifth, while our societal perspective cost analysis incorporated comprehensive indirect costs, we acknowledge the inherent limitations in fully quantifying intangible expenditures such as informal caregiving time, household productivity losses, and potential accommodation expenses. Last, our China-focused sampling strategy may limit generalizability to other LMIC contexts, particularly regions with more fragmented health systems, such as Sub-Saharan Africa. Differences in digital infrastructure, health system capacity, and resource availability mean that both costs and effectiveness may not translate directly. Contextual adaptation of cost parameters, informed by local epidemiological and economic profiles, will therefore be critical to ensure that both efficacy and cost-effectiveness are maintained when scaling up implementation in diverse settings. Although these limitations warrant future validation through multinational trials, the analysis provides a foundational blueprint for equity-focused device evaluation in resource-limited settings.
The smart pillbox reconfigures oral PrEP adherence in LMICs by transforming structural constraints (offline needs, objectively and passively collected monitoring data, and SDH disparities) into catalysts for equity-centric innovation. Our findings confirm that optimizing medical devices for low-resource settings requires not only durability but also intentional design that prioritizes vulnerable populations. Policymakers should integrate such devices into universal health coverage essential packages using vulnerability-indexed pricing, while researchers should prioritize hybrid hardware–software models to balance robustness with scalability. As LMICs address the syndemic of infectious diseases and health inequities, this intervention demonstrates a fundamental principle: equitable health technology must first reach marginalized populations facing system-level barriers, rather than initially targeting easier-to-reach groups.

## Methods
The prospective interventional China Real-world Oral Intake of PrEP (CROPrEP) study evaluated the HIV prevention efficacy of TDF/FTC among MSM in four cities: Beijing, Shenzhen, Chongqing, and Shenyang . Participants were stratified by baseline preference into daily or event-driven PrEP subgroups, or a non-PrEP group. Daily PrEP participants received one TDF/FTC tablet per day. The event-driven PrEP regimen involved administering two tablets 2–24 h before sexual intercourse, followed by single tablets 24 and 48 h after the initial dose. If additional sexual activity occurred within 48 h of the initial dose, participants transitioned to daily dosing until 48 h after the final sexual act. Subsequent sexual encounters triggered reinitiation of the event-driven regimen. Cross-over between daily and event-driven groups was permitted during follow-up.
The pragmatic RCT of the digital intervention for real-time enhancement of oral PrEP adherence (DIRECT-PrEP) study ran concurrently in the same cities. It was approved by the First Hospital of China Medical University Ethics Committee (No. 2019-253-3). Participants were stratified by PrEP regimen and randomized (1:1) to intervention or control groups using opaque sealed envelopes containing computer-generated random numbers. The trial protocol was previously published . We adhered to Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting (CHEERS) 2022 (Supplementary Note  ).

## Participants
CHWs and volunteers shared details of DIRECT-PrEP via WeChat to CROPrEP participants. After enrollment, MSM were randomized to intervention or control groups. Eligibility criteria included being biologically male, aged 18–65 years, engaging in anal sex with men, having at least one high-risk behavior in the past six months (condomless anal intercourse with males, ≥2 male partners, or any sexually transmitted infection diagnosis), confirmed HIV-1 negative status, participation in CROPrEP, ability to understand study protocols, and provision of written informed consent (Supplementary Protocol). Recruitment occurred between September 20, 2019, and November 27, 2020.
Five risk factors associated with higher social vulnerability were defined: ≤ high school education, monthly income < $580, relationship instability, having casual partners, and engaging in condomless anal intercourse with casual partners. Participants meeting two or more factors comprised the more socially vulnerable group, while others were classified as the less socially vulnerable group (Supplementary Note  ).

## Intervention
This intervention leveraged an integrated technology-support model linking digital monitoring with clinical care to improve oral PrEP adherence. Key components included a multidisciplinary team, tailored participant engagement, structured follow-up, real-time communication, and supplementary resources to ensure sustained adherence and data integrity. Supplementary Fig.  illustrates the electronic medication monitoring device and its operation.
(1) Multidisciplinary team collaboration: The intervention involved a coordinated team of researchers, experienced physicians, and technical support personnel. Researchers facilitated proactive monitoring and participant contact, while physicians provided personalized adherence counseling and managed adverse events during follow-up visits. Technical experts ensured smart pillbox functionality. All roles were defined through standardized training before study initiation.
(2) Customized participant engagement protocols: Each participant received individualized support, including tailored instructional materials and demonstration videos for daily and event-driven PrEP use. Training content was refined iteratively based on feedback from pilot testing with researchers and users, emphasizing participant-centered engagement to promote self-management and adherence motivation.
(3) Structured monitoring and follow-up schedule: Participants underwent scheduled follow-ups at 3 and 6 months ( ± 7 days), which included biological testing (e.g., HIV screening, HIV RNA pooling PCR, syphilis serology, HSV-2 antibody testing, routine blood and urine tests, liver and renal function tests, blood glucose and lipid profiles, and bone mineral density assessment) and online questionnaires assessing sociodemographic and behavioral factors. Continuous remote monitoring using the smart pillbox logged adherence data via cloud-based systems accessible through a WeChat Mini Program.
(4) Real-time alert and intervention mechanism: A multi-tiered reminder system was triggered by preset dosing schedules, incorporating multimodal alerts (sound, vibration, light, and WeChat push notifications) for missed doses. Missed-dose reminders escalated to repeated mobile notifications every 30 min (up to twice), followed by researcher alerts. Time-sensitive interventions were employed: doses missed before 22:00 were addressed immediately, while those after were handled by 10:00 the next day. Repeated pillbox openings without intake also triggered system alerts for researcher follow-up.
(5) Supplementary technical and operational resources: The intervention was supported by enhanced training materials, real-time technical assistance, and the provision of NB-IoT-enabled smart pillboxes with 600 mAh rechargeable batteries and automatic cloud logging. Post-study, devices were returned per a predefined protocol to ensure sustainability and data security. Supplementary resources such as customized instructional videos and a dedicated management platform facilitated seamless implementation.

## Health effects
To quantify intervention effectiveness, two primary metrics were used: (1) health utility (QALYs) and (2) health effects (PADs) during follow-up.
QALYs provided a longitudinal measure of treatment effectiveness by integrating health-related quality-of-life (HRQoL) states with time-in-state duration. Health utilities were derived from individual PrEP adherence levels. PrEP use as prescribed reduces sexual HIV acquisition risk by approximately 99% among MSM ; thus, perfect adherence was modeled as complete HIV protection. This framework allowed estimation of HIV incidence through adherence metrics. QALYs were calculated as a function of health utilities and estimated HIV incidence. To calculate PADs, PrEP medication adherence was assigned a value of 1 for perfect adherence and 0 for non-adherence. PrEP adherence was treated as a continuous variable, with imperfect adherence representing partial protection. PADs were calculated as (PrEP adherence) × (follow-up days) (Supplementary Note  for methodology).

## Cost Components
Costs were categorized as testing, healthcare utilization, intervention, and indirect costs.

## Direct costs: testing
Biological testing occurred at baseline, 3-month, and 6-month follow-ups. Total testing expenditure equaled the per-participant fixed cost multiplied by the eligible cohort size.

## Direct costs: healthcare utilization
At the time of procurement, Truvada (TDF/FTC 300/200 mg; Gilead Sciences) cost $286.96 per 30-tablet bottle. Pill counts at baseline and follow-up visits determined total medication expenditure. Per-participant Truvada costs were calculated by distributing total expenditures across all randomized participants.

## Direct costs: intervention
Smart pillbox costs included unit procurement, system development and maintenance (including software design), and charging expenses. Supplementary costs covered training materials, technical support, and NB-IoT-enabled smart pillboxes with rechargeable batteries and cloud auto-logging functionality. These expenditures also supported sustainability and data security through customized instructional videos and a dedicated management platform.
Labor costs included CHWs training and backend management (e.g., manual reminders for missed doses). Salaries were provided for two researchers and nurses conducting pill counts and adherence interventions. Additional costs covered travel, accommodation, and meals for training-related trips. One researcher responsible for pill counting and one nurse performing blood collection received part-time salaries for 15 months. Per-participant labor costs were calculated by dividing total expenditures across randomized participants. Two researchers implementing adherence interventions were compensated for 15 months, with costs allocated only to intervention group participants.
Questionnaire collection costs were based on the annual subscription fee ($579.57) for the Jinshuju data platform (https://jinshuju.net/home). Follow-up support expenses included communication costs covered by a 15-month phone plan at $28.84 per month. Per-participant questionnaire costs were calculated by dividing total expenditures across all randomized participants.

## Indirect costs
venue, cohort maintenance, clinical visits, time, and lost productivity. Venue costs represented total expenditures across all four study sites. Cohort maintenance costs (a fixed fee) covered all participants at baseline and follow-up visits. Participant expenditures for food and transportation during clinical visits were extracted from survey data. Lost productivity was estimated by multiplying the number of clinical visits by the average daily wage, assuming a productivity loss of 2.5 h per visit for the intervention group and 2 h for the control group.
All costs were reported in 2020 U.S. dollars, using an exchange rate of 1 USD = 6.9 CNY. Given the 6-month time horizon, no discounting was applied to costs or outcomes. Cost categories and analytical perspectives are presented in Supplementary Table  .

## Statistical analysis
We assessed efficacy using intent-to-treat analysis and trial-wide data from DIRECT-PrEP’s economic evaluation. The within-trial cost-effectiveness analysis compared the smart pillbox intervention with the control group across three perspectives: health system, multipayer, and societal. The healthcare perspective incorporated only direct medical expenditures. The multipayer perspective included health sector expenditures, intervention costs, and indirect costs associated with food and transportation. The societal perspective encompassed all additional indirect costs, including participants’ opportunity costs related to healthcare engagement and productivity losses due to illness. As recommended for public health economic evaluations, we prioritized the societal perspective because it provides an inclusive assessment of all costs affecting both beneficiaries and payers, thereby offering a comprehensive evaluation of societal impact (Supplementary Note  , Supplementary Table  ) .
We quantified cost-effectiveness using the ICER, calculated by dividing the difference in mean costs between the digital intervention and control groups by the corresponding difference in health outcomes (measured in both QALYs and PADs). The ICER represented the marginal cost required to achieve one additional unit of health benefit when implementing the smart pillbox intervention versus standard care.
Because a specific WTP threshold has not been established in China , we adopted the cost-effectiveness threshold framework based on WHO-CHOICE and previous publications – , corresponding to 1–3 times China’s 2020 per capita gross domestic product (GDP). This yielded an evaluative range of $10,400–$31,200 per QALY gained, based on China’s economic parameters . WTP thresholds were also estimated using a VSL conversion , yielding a point estimate of 1.45 times GDP per capita, with a lower bound of about 1.16 times and an upper bound of about 2.90 times GDP per capita. Regarding PADs, in the lack of predefined WTP benchmarks, we derived the theoretical threshold required to attain a 95% probability of cost-effectiveness through probabilistic sensitivity analysis.
We applied the two-sided SUR model to specify cost and effectiveness regression equations, producing incremental cost and utility estimates. To address sampling uncertainty, we used a nonparametric bootstrap resampling approach with 5000 iterations. This method generated an empirical joint distribution of incremental costs and effects by repeatedly sampling with replacement from the original dataset , .
These bootstrap replications were used to construct 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The resulting uncertainty estimates were visualized on cost-effectiveness planes, providing a comprehensive depiction of the variability in our cost-effectiveness estimates. There were no missing data in this trial; all essential medical records were fully available and analytically adequate.
We subsequently developed cost-effectiveness acceptability curves (CEACs) to estimate the probability that the intervention was cost-effective across a range of WTP thresholds for QALYs, analyzed from multiple economic perspectives. These CEACs provided policymakers with robust probabilistic evidence of cost-effectiveness, while accounting for key uncertainties inherent in economic evaluations .
To evaluate the robustness of our primary findings, we conducted comprehensive sensitivity analyses. First, we accounted for residual confounding by controlling for baseline outcome measures (age, income, casual partner status, and PrEP regimen). Second, we reassessed cost-effectiveness using two-sided GLM as well as OLS regression models. Third, we reanalyzed cost-effectiveness after including healthcare expenditures attributable to Truvada. Fourth, we conducted a sensitivity analysis that disaggregated system costs into fixed and variable components, treating overhead expenses as variable. Finally, we performed a sensitivity analysis based on an alternative pooled utility value for HIV/AIDS.
All analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.1. Two-sided independent samples t-tests and two-sided Mann–Whitney U tests were applied for normally and non-normally distributed continuous variables, respectively. Estimates were reported with 95% confidence intervals, using two-tailed tests with α = 0.05 (P < 0.05). The trial was registered on the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR1900025604).
