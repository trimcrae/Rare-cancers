## Abstract

## Introduction
Achieving universal health coverage requires the use of health research evidence in decision-making; however, this remains understudied in lower and middle-income countries (LMICs) such as Tanzania. Despite several health sector reforms and the availability of locally generated research, evidence indicates that the use of such research in health planning and decision-making remains limited in Tanzania, creating a gap between research production and its practical application. This study examined the extent of research evidence used in health planning and the factors influencing its use among health planning teams.

## Methods and analysis
A sequential exploratory mixed-methods design was employed, starting with qualitative data from focus group discussions (N=6) and KIIs (N=34) with health planners from selected regions in Tanzania, recruited based on their direct involvement in health planning, using semistructured guides informed by the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation-Behaviour (COM-B) model. This was followed by a quantitative survey of 422 participants meeting the same inclusion criteria, administered with a structured questionnaire derived from the model. The COM-B framework guided both tool development and analysis. Qualitative data were thematically analysed using a framework approach, yielding three themes, while quantitative data were analysed using descriptive statistics and binary logistic regression.

## Results
Qualitative findings revealed barriers such as limited knowledge and skills, inadequate access to knowledge translation tools, poor dissemination processes, financial and technical constraints, and lack of training. Opportunities included supportive guidelines, research coordinators, collaborations, dedicated budgets and improved internet access. Motivators such as job promotions, professional development, allowances and targeted training were also identified. Quantitative results showed moderate evidence use (66.2%), slightly higher than in other LMICs. Barriers included suboptimal dissemination (74.5%; OR=2.035, p=0.0008), inadequate resources (70.0%; OR=0.965, p=0.8759) and lack of training (63.7%; OR=1.361, p=0.1806). Integrated findings highlighted convergence on dissemination, resource and training challenges, with divergence in statistical significance between methods.

## Conclusions
Barriers related to dissemination, resources and training hinder evidence use. Interventions such as digital repositories, guideline development and capacity building, alongside institutionalised frameworks, resource allocation and accountability mechanisms, are essential to strengthen evidence-based health planning in Tanzania.

## STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY
The study provides a holistic picture of the use of health research evidence in health planning, focusing on determinants among planning teams in Tanzania.
The Capability, Opportunity, Motivation-Behaviour model enhanced theoretical rigour, guiding data collection and analysis of factors influencing evidence use.
The exploratory mixed-methods design integrated qualitative and quantitative data, providing a comprehensive understanding of barriers, opportunities and motivators for research evidence.
The study did not provide trends on using health research evidence in Tanzania.

## Introduction
 Research evidence is essential for policy-makers and planners to understand problems, develop interventions and learn from others’ successes and failures. It provides updated information, identifies opportunities for policy development, informs policy direction and evaluates impacts, contributing significantly to health policy processes. However, the use of health research evidence in policy-making is limited in many lower and middle-income countries (LMICs), impeding progress towards universal health coverage (UHC). 
Examples of successful research utilisation in LMICs include Bangladesh, Vietnam and Nepal, where urban health challenges have been addressed through high-quality evidence, and the Democratic Republic of Congo and Ghana, where research has informed humanitarian responses and primary healthcare programmes. These experiences are relevant to Tanzania, which faces similar challenges of resource constraints and health system gaps, though its decentralised planning structure and policy reforms provide unique opportunities for integrating research evidence into decision-making.
Organisational and systemic determinants, infrastructure, evidence availability, collaboration between researchers and policy-makers, and knowledge translation tools influence the use of health research in planning. Additional factors include leadership commitment, organisational culture, capacity for evidence appraisal and external influences such as political priorities and resource constraints. Theories such as the Diffusion of Innovations Theory and the Knowledge-to-Action Framework provide insights into how these factors facilitate or hinder evidence use. For this study, ‘health research evidence’ is defined to include published peer-reviewed research, routine health system data and policy-relevant outputs such as briefs, guidelines and reports, which collectively inform planning and decision-making. This transition highlights how lessons from comparable contexts can inform the Tanzanian experience.
The COM-B model (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation-Behaviour) was selected over other behaviour change frameworks because it explicitly integrates both individual-level and contextual determinants of behaviour. This makes it particularly suitable for understanding the complex interplay of factors influencing research evidence utilisation. However, a limitation of COM-B is its generality, requiring complementary models or context-specific adaptation to provide more detailed mechanisms of change. Despite this, COM-B has been successfully applied in health systems research globally, making it an appropriate choice for examining evidence use in Tanzania. Its use in this study allows for a structured exploration of barriers and facilitators, and it guides the identification of practical strategies for enhancing evidence uptake. In Tanzania, however, there is limited evidence of these determinants posing challenges to achieving UHC under the Sustainable Development Goals. 
To enhance the use of research evidence in policy-making and planning, Tanzania has implemented initiatives like the Tanzania Essential Health Intervention Project (TEHIP), the Health Research Users Trust Fund and the Regional East Africa Health Policy Initiative (REACH Policy Initiative) to enhance evidence utilisation, but these efforts have been hindered by funding limitations. These initiatives have played a critical role in promoting structured use of research in decision-making by providing guidance, capacity-building and platforms for integrating evidence into planning processes, thereby shaping the research utilisation landscape in Tanzania. Additionally, existing studies on evidence use in health planning are few, mostly qualitative, geographically restricted and unable to provide a comprehensive overview. 
Despite these efforts, a research gap remains. Existing studies on evidence use in health planning in Tanzania are few, geographically restricted, predominantly qualitative and have not provided a comprehensive overview of the determinants influencing research utilisation. This lack of robust and wide-ranging analysis has limited actionable insights for strengthening planning processes.
This study aims to analyse the determinants for using health research evidence among health planning teams at the regional and council levels in Tanzania. While previous studies have mostly focused on measuring the extent of evidence use, there is limited understanding of the factors influencing its utilisation in the Tanzanian context. This study, therefore, addresses both aspects: assessing the current level of research evidence use and exploring the determinants that facilitate or hinder its uptake. Its specific objectives include: (1) Analysing the current usage of health research evidence among planning team members at the regional and council levels, (2) Analysing the capacity of health planning team members to utilise health research evidence, (3) Identifying opportunities for enhancing the use of health research evidence in health planning and (4) Exploring the motivations for using health research evidence among health planning members at both regional and council levels.

## Methods and analysis

## Study setting
This study was conducted in the United Republic of Tanzania, an LMIC located in East Africa, with a population of about 62 million people. The country has a budget for the Ministry of Health (MoH) 2023/2024 estimated to be US$443.6 million, with a US$1.2 million (0.27%) budget allocated for evidence production. The health research evidence users in Tanzania involve three important Ministries, namely the MoH, the President’s Office, Regional Administration and Local Government (PO-RALG) and Regional/Councils/health facility Health Management Teams (RHMT/CHMT/HMT). The health evidence producers in Tanzania are the National Institute for Medical Research (NIMR), the Tanzania Commission for Science and Technology, Public and Private Universities, Health-related institutions or authorities, local and international nongovernmental organisations (NGOs) and civil society organisations.
The study site for this research was in 18 councils and 9 regions out of 26 Regions of Tanzania Mainland from 9 geographical zones (Northern zone, Eastern zone, Central zone, Lake Zone, Southern zone, South-west Highlands zone, Southern Highlands zone, Western zone and Dar es Salaam). The reason for selecting nine zones is to seek the country’s geographical representation. Together, these regions have a total population of 21 119 700, which represents 35.7% of the Tanzanian population. These regions are heterogeneous in population size, distribution of health facilities, distribution of human resources for health and institutions carrying out health research activities. A similar approach has been done in major Tanzanian health studies. This approach provided a comprehensive understanding of the status quo for the use of health research evidence in health planning and a heterogeneous study population for random sampling, hence acting as a representative snapshot of all regions in Tanzania. The study was conducted in 63 randomly selected health facilities from the Regional Referral Hospitals (RRHs) to the Dispensary levels. In order to ensure inclusion of urban and rural health facilities, stratification was conducted, followed by randomisation. The information on the study settings and landscape for health planning in Tanzania has been detailed in our published protocol. 

## Study design
This study used a sequential exploratory mixed-method design for data collection and analysis. The sequential explanatory component refers to the collection and analysis of data into two phases, starting with qualitative data and then transitioning to quantitative data. The qualitative phase aims to explore the research topic in depth, informing the subsequent quantitative phase. To implement this design, insights from the qualitative analysis, particularly the emerging themes on capacity, motivation and systemic challenges, directly informed the development of the quantitative survey instrument. This ensured that the survey questions were contextually relevant and captured dimensions highlighted during the qualitative phase. Unlike explanatory or concurrent designs, the exploratory approach starts with qualitative exploration to build foundational knowledge, while explanatory designs test hypotheses generated from quantitative data, and concurrent designs collect both types of data simultaneously, potentially limiting the iterative refinement of quantitative tools. By prioritising the qualitative strand, this study ensured a comprehensive and contextually relevant investigation of the research problem. The findings from both phases were integrated to create a more comprehensive understanding of the use of evidence in health planning. To report our findings, we followed the Good Reporting of A Mixed Methods Study framework. We also used the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology checklist for the quantitative part (see ) and the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist for the qualitative part (see ).

## Patient and public involvement
Members of the public, specifically those serving in health planning teams through the Health Facility Committees, were actively involved in the study. Their engagement included supporting data collection processes and playing a pivotal role in disseminating baseline information. This dissemination was instrumental in fostering community awareness and participation both during the study and in subsequent health planning interventions. Direct patient involvement was not applicable in this study, as the focus was on health planning processes rather than individual patient experiences.

## Qualitative phase of the study

## Study population
The study targeted health planning team members, including representatives from regional and local government authorities at management and facility levels, as well as public and private sectors. Participants were selected based on their roles in health planning to ensure broad representation and richness of information. In this study, the majority of the participants were recruited from the district level because most of the planning activities take place at this level. We included some key informants from the national level, including the MoH, the PO-RALG and development partners, who were also included. Targeted health planning team members, including National-level participants like the Director of Policy and Planning (MoH) and PO-RALG coordinator, regional participants such as Regional Medical Officers (RMOs) and Regional Planning Officers (RPLOs), and council-level District Medical Officers (DMOs), District Planning Officers (DPLOs) and private sector representatives. Health facility participants included in-charges from referral hospitals, district hospitals (DHs), health centres (HCs) and dispensaries.

## Sampling
We used maximum variation purposive sampling; participants were selected for their expertise in health planning, and recruitment continued until information saturation was reached. 

## Data collection tools and procedures

## Data collection tools
In this study, we used focus group discussions (FGDs) and in-depth interviews (IDIs) to gather information from the participants. The two methods were selected to capture both group dynamics in FGDs and in-depth individual insights through IDIs, providing a comprehensive understanding of the topic. Both FGDs and IDI guides were piloted before the actual data collection. We used semistructured guides for FGDs and IDIs, which collected information on the utilisation of health research evidence during health planning and the factors influencing use. The tools had a set of 10 open-ended questions for both FGDs and IDIs to ensure consistency in data collection. The questions for both FGDs and IDIs were adopted and modified from previous studies. 

## Focus group discussions
Data were collected from six face-to-face FGDs. The number of participants per FGD ranged from 7 to 12, and FGD lasted around 60–90 min, with an average of 75 min. Three research assistants, with training in public health and experience in qualitative data collection, were recruited to collect each FGD. The FGDs were conducted in Swahili, the lingua franca in Tanzania. The venue for the FGDs was meeting rooms of RHMTs and CHMTs at the Regional and Council level, respectively.

## In-depth interviews
A total of 34 face-to-face IDIs with key informants were conducted to explore individual experiences using research evidence in health planning. An interview guide with ten open-ended questions was used, and written consent was obtained. Two research assistants at health facility offices conducted each IDI in Swahili, lasting 45–60 min. Data collection for the qualitative phase continued until thematic saturation was achieved, at which point no new themes or insights emerged from additional interviews.

## Data collection procedures
The first author and three trained research assistants collected data for both FGDs and IDIs from October to November 2023. Data were gathered electronically using Android devices equipped with a voice recording application. Audio files were systematically labelled and saved, followed by verbatim transcription and translation.

## Data analysis
The transcribed data were analysed by two independent researchers (the first and last author). The analysis employed the framework method to identify meaningful information and organise it into themes or categories and was facilitated by the NVivo software developed by QRS International. This method was selected because our study was guided by a theory, the COM-B model. The approach is suitable due to its flexibility, transparent step-by-step interconnected stages that facilitate the analytical process, and the option to employ a mixed (inductive-deductive) coding approach. The selected approach to data analysis was aligned with our study design, given the predominantly descriptive approach. Analysis followed the six stages proposed by the framework approach: familiarising with the data, coding, developing an analytical framework, applying an analytical framework, charting the data into the framework matrix, and interpreting the data. Coding followed a case and theme‐based approach, whereby conceptual themes were developed, a case‐theme matrix was created, and data from each case (FGDs or IDIs) were transferred into the conceptual cells to ensure that the individual case stories contributed towards defining a single comprehensive whole. Coding of the transcribed data started by applying a deductive approach, which relied on codes based on pre-existing theoretical constructs. As we proceeded through the analysis, codes that emerged directly from the transcribed text were added. As the analysis continued, we reflected and modified our initial codes. Codes were grouped into categories before generating the main themes evolving from the data. To ensure coding reliability, intercoder agreement was established by having two researchers code the same transcripts independently, followed by iterative discussions to reconcile differences and reach consensus. In addition, the emerging themes were reviewed and discussed among coauthors until agreement was achieved. The emerging themes were discussed by the coauthors, who also agreed on the final quotes to substantiate the results; trustworthiness, credibility, transferability, dependability and conformability were ensured.

## Trustworthiness of qualitative tools
The content validity of qualitative tools was assessed by qualitative research experts and member checking. Furthermore, qualitative tools were pilot-tested before data collection to ensure the clarity of data collection tools.

## Data collection tools
Quantitative data were gathered using a Swahili-translated survey adapted from previous studies. The survey, administered via Open Data Kit, collected information on the use of health research evidence and its determinants among planning team members at national, regional, council and facility levels.

## Data collection procedures
Data were collected in person by trained research assistants with health system experience. They underwent three training days on data collection methods, tools and ethics. Pilot testing was conducted at Dodoma RHMT, Chamwino CHMT, Chamwino Council Hospital, Chamwino Health Centre and Buigiri Dispensary to ensure the validity, reliability and effectiveness of the tools.

## Variables and their measurements

## Dependent variable
The dependent variable of this study, that is, the use of health research evidence, was obtained from a set of four questions that were measured. The questions were a mixture of binary and multiple-choice questions. Those questions were divided into four areas. The mean score was calculated from those sets of questions; the score had a value of 0 or 1. Those respondents who scored 1 were regarded as having used the health research evidence, while those who scored 0 were regarded as not having used the health research evidence. For multiple-choice questions, the respondents were provided with a set of predefined options, allowing them to select the one that best represented their response, which would capture varying degrees or categories of the use of health research evidence.

## Independent variables
The study’s independent variables included demographic factors (sex, age, education), professional attributes (position, years of schooling) and contextual elements (facility type, stakeholder group, district, region). It examined determinants influencing health research evidence use in planning, framed by the COM-B Model’s capability, opportunity and motivation constructs.
Capability determinants were measured using 12 Likert scale questions (1=strongly agree to 5=strongly disagree) and one multiple-choice question. Mean and SD were calculated to assess central tendency and variability, while χ2 tests determined whether response distributions were significantly different from chance. For opportunity determinants, 17 Likert scale questions (1–5) were used, with numerical values assigned to responses (lower values indicating stronger agreement). Mean or median values quantified the overall trend, and multiple regression analysis examined relationships between independent and dependent variables. Motivation determinants were assessed using eight Likert scale questions with a similar scoring system. Again, mean and median values were calculated to identify trends, and multiple regressions were applied to explore the relationships between variables. This approach provided a structured analysis of the determinants influencing the use of health research evidence.

## Data analysis
Responses were reported for all survey items across all response categories, and the data were summarised using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and means and SDs for non-categorical variables. Given that the outcome variable had two categories (0=did not use, 1=used), a binary logistic regression model was used to assess factors associated with the use of health research evidence during planning. Potential confounding variables (such as demographic characteristics, professional experience and institutional level) were adjusted for in the regression analysis to reduce bias in estimating associations. The model results are regression parameter estimates and OR. The data analysis was conducted using STATA V.18, and a threshold for statistical significance was set at α=0.05. Incomplete survey responses with substantial missing information on key study variables were excluded from the analysis. However, the overall proportion of missing data was minimal (<5%), and therefore, no data imputation methods were applied. This approach ensured that the final analysis was based on complete cases without compromising the validity of the results. The dataset generated and analysed is available in the Figshare repository, with the dataset reference number figshare.26 354 737.V.1 

## Validity and reliability of the quantitative tools
To ensure validity and reliability, the validity of quantitative data collection tools was reviewed by independent subject matter experts from the University of Dodoma. The reliability assessment of the questionnaire was done using the internal consistency test, with the alpha reliability coefficient being the statistic. The range of the alpha coefficient, also known as Cronbach’s alpha, was 0.71 acceptable for this study. Moreover, exploratory factor analysis was conducted to establish the construct validity of the questionnaire, and a pilot study was conducted before field data collection to ensure clarity of the data collection tool.

## Integration of mixed-method analysis
Qualitative and quantitative data integration happened at the interpretation levels. Qualitative data analysis happened before the collection of quantitative data. On completing the qualitative data analysis, the qualitative findings provided rich insights for using health research evidence and determinants among planning team members. This iterative process ensured that quantitative data collection tools accurately capture the complexity of the subject matter. Integration happens through a narrative approach, whereby the results of each step are reported in stages as the data are analysed separately to more fully address the research questions. Interpretation-level integration occurred, connecting the qualitative data from phase 1 of the study with the quantitative data from phase 2 of the survey using the Cresswell model for integrating exploratory mixed methods. 

## Results

## Qualitative results
From the analysis of both 6 FGDs and 34 IDIs, a total of 3 themes emerged: (1) current use of health research evidence, (2) barriers to effective use and (3) determinants influencing evidence use. The sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants, including 49 (70%) males, 21 (30%) females, 30% worked as RHMT, 47% CHMT and 23% as facility in charges, their roles, years of experience, and professional backgrounds, were diverse, providing a comprehensive perspective on the health planning process. These findings are mainly presented descriptively, outlining the health planning process and the views of the planning team members. Verbal quotes are used to illustrate the findings.

## Background information of the participants
In this study, a total of 34 IDIs were conducted with key informants at RMOs, DMOs and facility in-charges from the Regional Hospitals, DHs, HCs and dispensary level (who are leaders of health planning teams). A total of six FGDs were conducted with planning team members at the RHMTs and CHMTs from two regions and four councils. provides the details on the background information of the qualitative study tools and participants.
 

## Current use of health research evidence
Findings reveal the low utilisation of health research evidence at both regional and council levels due to different factors, such as a shortage of resources, both human and non-human, and inadequate or local knowledge of research. The use of research evidence in health planning was reported to be happening at all levels of health planning, though minimally. At least the level of the RRHs, they reported frequently to be using evidence than the facilities at the Council levels; otherwise, most planners reported to have used data from the health information system. Members of the FGD stated:
Normally, we do use research findings, though not to a large extent. For example, we use malaria research findings from a malaria survey, and we get the prevalence of malaria from that survey. So, on some indicators, we use research evidence, and on others, we use baseline data from the HIMS. (FDG 01)

## Barriers to effective use of research evidence
Commonly reported barriers included low accessibility to research findings, the perception that research is more for academic purposes, inadequate training on interpreting and integrating evidence, low accessibility of evidence, the perception that research is more academic in purpose, lack of dissemination, contextual inappropriateness and lack of guidelines for health research evidence utilisation. Participants also noted a gap in collaboration between research institutions and health planners, which hindered the timely use of evidence as elaborated below.

## Low accessibility to research findings
The low or minimal utilisation of research evidence was attributed to challenges related to the accessibility of research findings. Participants reported research findings being more available to professionals and the community involved, and the data being kept in places where the majority cannot access, hence lower chances for other people to use the data or be known to others. They reported a lack of dissemination of research findings (the most recurrent hindrance factor) to their utilisation, as verbalised by a member of the CHMT:
There is a need for frequent dissemination of research findings through accessible platforms like media to ensure they are not overlooked by both professionals and the community, suggesting that repeating statistics in simple language aids in retention and understanding. For example, UNICEF’s 2011 research on child abuse, where consistent repetition of the statistic “1 child in 3 has been reported to succumb to some form of abuse, led to increased awareness and utilization of the findings (FGD 05).

## The perception that research is more for academic purposes
There was a perception among the health planning team that research findings were for academic purposes and not for any other uses; hence, they were not used for planning purposes. This was stated by a participant from the FGD:
The speaker finds academic forums inaccessible for community-level practitioners due to the complex nature of research presentations, primarily targeting academics, hospital directors, and major NGOs. They perceive a lack of consideration from organizers, as invitations to attend are scarce and the content appears geared toward higher education levels, excluding health center planners. Suggesting a need for simplified abstracts and involvement of council leaders, the speaker highlights the forums’ emphasis on academic audiences, which does not cater to practical application at lower levels of healthcare planning (FDG 04).
Similarly, another participant emphasized: “Most of the time, when we hear about research, it is being discussed in universities or at big conferences. For us at the council level, it feels like research is for academics, not for helping us in daily planning” (IDI 22).

## Traditional way of planning
Planners were most used to the traditional way of planning practice using information such as facility data (mentioned by most of the participants) and did not think of using health research findings as reliable evidence during health planning, especially at DHs, HCs and dispensaries. Those who reported using research evidence did use it very rarely. Some planners were not even aware that health planning was supposed to be backed up by research findings or even guidelines for planning. IDI participant reported:
We have never used health research; we often use facility routine data, and we use common disease, hygiene, and rehabilitation data, but we do not use health research evidence (IDI 15)

## Lack of research dissemination
The absence of an effective feedback system was identified as a contributing factor to the limited utilisation of research evidence during planning, despite the existence of numerous conducted research studies. This phenomenon was attributed to a tradition among many researchers, as reported by a dispensary in charge:
It is the tradition of the responses to the research results, the research feedback does not return to the relevant place where research has been done, the environmental diversity where the research has been conducted, and the place where the research is supposed to be used (IDI 28)

## Lack of guidelines for health research evidence utilisation
Health planners expressed concerns about the lack of clear guidelines regarding the utilisation of research evidence in health planning, leading to confusion and difficulties in understanding and following appropriate directions. This confusion stemmed from contradictory directives from higher authorities and the absence of direct support for the use of research evidence in some health ministerial guidelines, as stated and reiterated by the facility in charge of a dispensary and hospital:
Some people from higher levels come with a lot of directives which hinder the process of the use of health research; there is no clear guideline, especially in the health sector, everyone comes with their or directives (IDI 11)

## Determinants for the use of evidence
The main determinants identified were Capability determinants, Opportunity and Motivational determinants, both derived from the COM-B Model as predetermined subthemes.

## Lack of necessary knowledge and training in research
The absence of knowledge and training on research was identified as the most recurrent significant bottleneck hindering the utilisation of research evidence in the health sector, despite the presence of an enabling environment. The skills required to interpret available research and integrate research evidence into health planning were inadequate or lacking. Furthermore, there was an acknowledgement of the lack of emphasis on research within the health planning programme. This was reported by both a DMO and reiterated by a member of the CHMT:
Another thing to add is about knowledge of us CHMT members regarding research use issues. We do have some knowledge but it is not enough for utilization of research during health planning (FDG12).

## Inadequate human and material resources for research use
Health planners identified several challenges hindering the utilisation of research findings, primarily stemming from inadequate human and material resources. These challenges include understaffing for HCWs to participate in research use initiatives, as well as a lack of equipment like computers, especially at the lower-level facilities, resulting in the minimal use of health research evidence. A medical officer in charge further emphasised these challenges by stating:
Lack of working equipment like computers makes difficult in the use of interpretation of already available research because is written in English language but if the research has been placed in our Swahili language it would be easy to use and people to understand, also unavailability of current studies to be published on time and lastly, many studies are not lowered to peripheral areas they remain at high levels. (IDI 14)

## Lack of information and/or data management systems
Health planners acknowledged the importance of utilising health information data management systems. However, they highlighted challenges with underdeveloped health data systems, such as the District Health Information System (DHIS), which is intended to support health planning, as explained by a medical officer in charge of an HC:
The health data systems are not yet well-established, such as DHIS2, which is the system we use to get data that can help us access health research evidence in the process of health plans, but DHIS2 often lacks accurate data (IDI 08)
Not knowing websites or journals where relevant research findings have been published was another challenge mentioned. This posed a challenge when some members of the health planning team wished to visit such sites to get results of studies conducted in their settings, as put forward by a CHMT member:
I think that we should know the websites where these studies are uploaded to so that, even with individual efforts, we can be able to search, visit, and get the results. I know that publications are done in different journals, but now we don’t know whether the research done at our council or regional levels was published in which journals, so I can search for them. So, not knowing where the research journals are published is a challenge. If we knew that the research so and so is published in particular journals, it would be easier for us to look at the interesting results and get the information in them; so, we fail to follow up on our own due to lack of knowledge on the websites (FDG 16)

## Difficulties in accessing research evidence at lower health facilities
Health planners highlighted significant challenges regarding the accessibility of health evidence and other opportunities, particularly at lower levels of health facilities. Several reasons were cited, especially disadvantaging those in peripheral areas who are infrequently reached, except when supported by development partners, as emphasised by the medical officer in charge of HCs:
Not having easy access to health research information and different opportunities compared to those who are in high-level hospitals such as Muhimbili, eg, opportunities to study and use research evidence, and the attachment to NIMR. (IDI 09)
Difficulties in accessing research information were noted to be particularly challenging for new employees lacking knowledge and skills in health planning and research. The need for periodic refresher courses was emphasised as a crucial measure to address this challenge, as highlighted by an IDI participant at an HC:
Difficulty in accessing hard copies of research and handling them as well, also if the soft copy were available, it would be better, furthermore, there are many newly employed staff who also lack education in health plans and research, so fresher’s courses are required from time to time. (IDI 11)

## Opportunities for the use of health research evidence
The use of health research evidence has numerous opportunities to improve healthcare policies, practices and outcomes. By integrating research findings into planning, stakeholders can enhance the effectiveness and efficiency of healthcare interventions. The following are opportunities that facilitate the use of evidence:

## Enabling the utilisation of information generated from the healthcare system
Members of the health planning teams verbalised the existence of various opportunities for the utilisation of health research evidence at the regional and council levels. They emphasised that the use of health research evidence helps in generating important information to be used during health planning, together with the existing opportunities such as the presence of research coordinators, academic institutions and regulatory authorities, and the presence of infrastructure. The diversity in the utilisation of these existing opportunities in some areas, while in others, the situation differs:
The first opportunity is to have a lot of information generated from the healthcare system from the Regional and council level, the issue comes from the capacity and resources. Another opportunity is the availability of partners in our regions; we have research coordinators and clinical coordinators, who need skills and resources to use evidence at all levels. All research conducted in our local areas should involve the team and be used during planning, RHMT or CHMT during the research process (IDI 13).

## Availability of research experts, equipment at the facility
The presence of staff with research use expertise, equipment and facilities at the facility was mentioned as an opportunity for the utilisation of research evidence in health planning at both regional and council levels. This would also be supported by existing leadership structures within the health planning teams, such as the presence of a research coordinator, as stated by a participant from an RRH.
First, we have the research coordinator at the regional level; this is the one who should help to coordinate all research evidence so that we can use it during planning. We have full support from our leaders, the Regional Administrative Secretary (RAS) is a medical doctor, and as Regional Commissioner (RC), is a very understanding and smart person. (IDI 13).
The presence of health providers and other health stakeholders (such as implementing partners) with an interest in generating research evidence was identified as another opportunity by health planners. These individuals may play a crucial role in the utilisation of research evidence in health planning, as stated by a respondent from a DH.
The opportunities are there, for example, we have health practitioners who are interested in generating of the research evidence in our municipality, which can be used by our council, and I am one of them, also, there are studies that have already been done, and the environment allows us to get the research evidence through the internet (IDI 30)
The presence of equipment facilities needed for supporting the utilisation of research evidence in health planning was also reported as an opportunity:
The opportunities we have that can help my team to use computers, projectors, and the internet, one can look for health research evidence on the internet. A good environment exists for researching the internet is not disturbed. We also have a nice and modern conference room we can sit and discuss the available research for use on health plans (IDI 30)

## Availability of the planned budget for research
The availability of funds planned for research was identified as another opportunity by health planners. They mentioned that in the process of using health research evidence, budgets are allocated to facilitate the process, thereby providing opportunities for staff training, as noted by a medical officer in charge of the RRH:
The opportunity we have money in our facility, we also have medical professionals who have been based in research evidence utilization. At the moment, we are doing urine culture, which helps us do research and generate evidence. There are some of us already conducting research, and we have allocated money for it, so when staff want to use research, there is money that is allocated (IDI 33)

## The presence of academic institutions, that is, universities
The existence of universities with students who are required to do health research was considered another opportunity for health planning teams to use health research evidence in health planning. A member of CHMT stated:
Adding to what my fellow has said, another opportunity is the presence of health universities or colleges. We have students who come for field-work attachments that we can use in doing this research and generate evidence that can be used during planning. (FDG 08)

## Motivations for the use of health research evidence
Motivations for health planners to use health research evidence in health planning were reported to include provisions of incentives, the presence of support systems and the involvement of research institutions.

## Provision of incentives to health planners (automatic motivation)
Participants suggested providing incentives to the health planning team to encourage the use of health research evidence in health planning. Some of the mentioned incentives included extra duty allowances, support from guardians and opportunities for team outings to prepare health plans. A medical officer in charge of an HC reported:
There are extra duties allowances that we get during the preparation of health plans. We receive support from the facility guardian, cascade accountant, and the district secretary during who preparation of health plans encourage us to use health research evidence. (IDI 04)

## The presence of a support system, including manpower and institutions (reflective motivation)
The availability of research support systems at work was mentioned as an encouragement for the majority of health planners to use health research evidence, as it increases the chances for research training, collaborations and networking. Factors such as the availability of guidelines, teamwork, support from supervisors and support from institutions like the NIMR were cited as motivating factors for the health planning team to use research, as echoed by a member of CHMT:
Among the things which motivate us are firstly the presence of planning guidelines because they help us to know what we plan and to what degree. Also, the presence of NIMR, which generates health research evidence. Also, the presence of planning allowances from preplanning-to-planning-to planning to post planning; so, it is one of the things that motivates. Also, involvement of the planning teams on the plans from the lower levels, so you get the research evidence and get to know where there is a problem; so, when you start planning, it gets easy because you are fully informed. (FDG 10)

## Availability of training or seminars (automatic motivation)
Training and seminars were reported to play a role in motivating health planners to use health research evidence during health planning. They considered it important in exposing health planners to research knowledge and skills, as reported by the Regional Planning Officer:
Short-term seminars and short courses for staff help to gain exposure and learning, there should be a financial resource for supporting the use of research evidence, eg, an Incentive (IDI 27).

## Determinants for health research evidence use

The analysis includes variables such as age, region, type of health facility, gender, professional background, stakeholder group, highest level of education, position held in health planning and years of participation. ## Significant variables and Interpretation

## Region
The analysis compares various regions to the reference category, Kilimanjaro. The ORs show how likely individuals from other regions are to use health research evidence in health planning compared with those from Kilimanjaro. For instance, if a region has an OR greater than 1, individuals from that region are more likely to use research evidence than those from Kilimanjaro. If the OR is less than 1, they are less likely. The p values indicate whether these differences are statistically significant.

## Level of education
Compared with individuals with a diploma (reference category), those with higher education levels were more likely to use research evidence:
Undergraduate degree (OR=2.396, 95% CI 1.094 to 5.249, p=0.0290): Those with an undergraduate degree are over twice as likely to use research evidence compared with diploma holders.
Master’s degree (OR=2.278, 95% CI 0.921 to 5.631, p=0.0747): Similar to undergraduates, master’s degree holders are also more likely to use research evidence in health planning, although this was not significant.
These OR values reflect the increased likelihood of individuals with higher education levels using research evidence compared with the reference group, with p values indicating the strength of this relationship.

## Gender
Interestingly, gender was not a significant predictor of research evidence use (p=0.97). This suggests that both male and female health planners face similar barriers and opportunities in accessing and applying research evidence, indicating that determinants of research use are more likely related to structural and institutional factors (eg, facility type, professional training, availability of resources) rather than individual gender differences.

## Years of participation
6–10 years (OR=1.092, 95% CI 0.677 to 1.761, p=0.7184): Individuals with 6–10 years of participation in health planning show a higher likelihood of using research evidence compared with those with fewer years, though this was not statistically significant.
More than 10 years (OR=1.399, 95% CI 0.648 to 3.019, p=0.3924): Those with over 10 years of participation have a 40% higher likelihood of using research evidence compared with those with fewer years of experience, and this difference was also not statistically significant.
The adjusted ORs reflect the likelihood of each category of the variable influencing the use of research evidence, controlling for other variables in the model. Significance is determined by both the size of the OR and the p value, with confidence intervals providing a range within which the true effect likely falls.

## Integrated results
Integrated results reveal both convergence and divergence between qualitative and quantitative findings. Convergence was evident in the identification of dissemination challenges, resource constraints and training deficiencies as significant barriers. Both data sources highlighted the importance of supportive infrastructure and motivational factors in fostering evidence utilisation. However, there was divergence regarding the statistical significance of some barriers, such as resource inadequacy and training gaps, which qualitative insights suggest are more impactful than the quantitative findings imply. This divergence demonstrates how the qualitative narratives revealed the lived realities of health planners, whereas the quantitative data, though statistically robust, understated some of these contextual influences.
The differences between qualitative and quantitative findings can be attributed to the unique strengths and limitations of each method. Qualitative methods captured rich, detailed accounts of contextual barriers and opportunities, while quantitative surveys provided measurable patterns and statistical relationships. This methodological complementarity provides a more holistic picture of research evidence use, showing not only what is happening but also why it occurs.

## Discussion
This is the first comprehensive study that provides important insights into the utilisation of health research evidence and the determinants influencing its use in Tanzania.
Despite these encouraging demographic and professional characteristics, the study revealed persistent barriers to using health research evidence in health planning. Participants reported minimal engagement with such evidence due to challenges like over-reliance on routine facility data, limited awareness of research value and insufficient training in evidence use. These findings are consistent with global literature suggesting that traditional planning practices (use of evidence from routine data because of its availability) often overshadow evidence-based approaches. Furthermore, the lack of clear guidelines for using health research evidence underscores the need for a structured framework to guide planners. 
The determinants of evidence use were explored using the COM-B model, which identified capability, opportunity and motivation as key factors. The capability dimension of the COM-B model emerged as a key determinant, and limited knowledge and skills to interpret and apply research evidence emerged as a significant challenge. Planners also faced barriers such as inadequate resources, restricted access to research articles and weaknesses in health information systems, particularly the DHIS. These systemic shortcomings hindered the incorporation of evidence into decision-making processes, particularly in lower-tier health facilities. This aligns with previous systematic reviews that highlight the critical role of capacity building in enhancing health professionals' competencies to use research evidence. The report of inadequate training on research utilisation is significant, suggesting that systematic educational interventions are necessary to bridge this knowledge gap. 
Inadequate human and material resources further exacerbate the challenges faced by health planners. Understaffing and a lack of necessary equipment hinder the effective use of health research evidence, which is consistent with findings from recent studies that indicate limitations in human resources significantly constrain evidence use in low-resource settings. The comments regarding the lack of access to computing resources are particularly concerning, as they may restrict planners’ ability to engage with current research literature, thereby perpetuating a cycle of ignorance regarding available evidence. 
When compared with prior studies in Tanzania, such as the TEHIP, our findings reaffirm that while efforts have been made to integrate research evidence into health planning, utilisation remains inconsistent and often limited to higher-level facilities. Similar patterns have been observed in other LMICs, including Kenya and Malawi, where structural constraints, weak dissemination strategies and capacity limitations have hindered research uptake. This suggests that the barriers identified in our study are not unique to Tanzania, but reflect broader systemic challenges across LMICs.
To address these barriers, a multifaceted approach is essential. Capacity-building programmes tailored to fill knowledge gaps are critical. These programmes should focus on enhancing planners’ skills in research interpretation and application. Strengthening health information systems and improving access to digital tools and resources are equally crucial. By addressing these gaps, planners can be better equipped to integrate research evidence into their plans effectively.
The findings of this study underscore the importance of systemic reforms to enhance research utilisation. Developing clear guidelines, fostering collaboration between researchers and planners, and instituting regular dissemination mechanisms can facilitate the use of evidence in health planning. Encouraging local-level involvement and co-creation of solutions further ensures that research evidence is contextually relevant and actionable. Such initiatives would improve planning outcomes and build trust in evidence-based approaches among stakeholders.
Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge potential biases in self-reported data. Participants may have overestimated their use of research evidence due to social desirability bias, particularly given their professional roles. While the COM-B model provided a robust framework to understand determinants of research use, some contextual and political factors (such as funding priorities, institutional culture or political commitment) may not have been fully captured by this model. Future studies may consider integrating frameworks like the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research or the Theoretical Domains Framework to provide a more comprehensive understanding of determinants influencing research utilisation

## Methodological considerations
The study employed an exploratory mixed methods design, combining qualitative and quantitative approaches to analyse the determinants of health research evidence use among planning teams in Tanzania. The qualitative component involved FGDs and IDIs to gather detailed perspectives on health planning processes. The quantitative aspect likely involved the collection of numerical data to identify patterns or correlations related to the determinants of evidence use. This methodological approach allowed for a comprehensive understanding of the barriers, facilitators and contextual factors influencing the use of health research evidence in the planning process.

## Policy and practice implications
The findings from this study offer important guidance for creating targeted strategies to strengthen evidence-based decision-making within the framework of a learning health system. They highlight distinct roles for various stakeholders such as Producers of Research Evidence: Focus on generating high-quality, relevant research and ensuring its accessibility through user-friendly digital repositories and dissemination platforms, Central Ministries: Prioritise capacity building for evidence utilisation, formulate clear guidelines for integrating research evidence into policy processes, and allocate resources to support evidence-informed decision-making. Such interventions should be aligned with existing Tanzanian health policies, such as the Health Sector Strategic Plan V and the National Digital Health Strategy, which already prioritise digital innovations and capacity building for health systems strengthening. Local governments: Facilitate the implementation of research findings at the community level, promote collaboration between planning teams and researchers, and address context-specific barriers to evidence use. Researchers: Enhance communication with policy-makers, tailor research outputs to decision-makers’ needs, and actively engage in partnerships to bridge the research-to-policy gap. These efforts could significantly amplify the impact of health research on policy formulation and implementation. Beyond technical solutions, political and institutional factors play a decisive role. For example, limited funding for knowledge translation activities and competing policy priorities can undermine efforts to embed evidence into planning processes. Sustained government commitment, earmarked funding and stronger accountability mechanisms are therefore critical to ensure that interventions such as digital repositories and training programmes are effectively implemented in practice.

## Methodological limitations
This study acknowledges its cross-sectional design, which limits causal inferences. Another limitation is the potential for response bias, as participants may have overreported their engagement with research evidence due to social desirability. In addition, the findings are context-specific, focusing on regional and council-level planners, and may not be generalisable to national policy-makers or other sectors of the health system. The study also did not examine trends over time regarding the use of health research evidence in Tanzania, and therefore, does not provide longitudinal insights into how evidence use has evolved or the long-term impact of knowledge translation initiatives.
Future research should, therefore, include longitudinal designs to track changes in research utilisation over time, as well as comparative studies across different levels of the health system. Such studies would provide stronger evidence on trends, determinants and effective strategies to strengthen the integration of research into planning and policy processes.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, while routine data often serves as a readily available source of evidence for health planning in Tanzania, this study highlights the need to address barriers that limit the effective use of health research evidence. The most critical barriers identified include limited access to user-friendly research outputs, inadequate capacity among health planners to appraise and apply research, and weak institutional support mechanisms for evidence use. Strengthening readiness for knowledge translation through capacity building, resource allocation and enhanced infrastructure can help bridge the gap between evidence and practice. These findings have important policy implications for Tanzania, as they call for stronger integration of research translation into existing national health policies and strategies, investment in digital repositories to improve accessibility of research outputs, and sustained funding for training and institutional capacity building. Aligning these actions with Tanzania’s ongoing health sector reforms can enhance evidence-informed planning and decision-making. Future initiatives should emphasise fostering an enabling environment that values and integrates a broad range of evidence, ensuring that health planning processes are both inclusive and evidence-driven. This approach can lead to more effective health policies and improved health outcomes for Tanzanian communities.
