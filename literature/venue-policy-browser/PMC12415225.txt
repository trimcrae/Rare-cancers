The Blavatnik Family Foundation aims to foster scientific relationships, promote collaboration, and enhance cooperation between the United States and Israel. This partnership of the US National Academy of Sciences and Israel Academy of Sciences and Humanities, supported by the Blavatnik Family Foundation as part of its annual forum series, brought together leading scientists and engineers with the goal of highlighting research in both nations and to build collaboration between the scientific communities that are actively addressing this global challenge ( ).
Key topics included investment in water infrastructure, innovations in desalination and brine management, water reuse technologies, environmental and societal implications, and managed aquifer recharge. The symposium underscored the necessity of interdisciplinary collaboration to address global water scarcity through technological and policy innovations. The major themes and insights are highlighted below, such that they can be of value to the broader academic and professional communities.

## Global Water Scarcity Challenges and the Need for Innovation
Water scarcity is a pressing global challenge exacerbated by climate change, population growth, and unsustainable water management practices. Desalination and water reuse have emerged as critical strategies to enhance water security. Both the United States and Israel face persistent water challenges and have made significant progress in implementing advanced technologies to address these issues over the past several decades.
Israel has developed a unique, holistic approach to water management, balancing both natural resources and cutting-edge technologies to address the country’s water scarcity challenges. Central to this approach is the integration of water planning schemes including water reuse, desalination, rain enhancement, rainwater harvesting, and conservation. Israel has significantly advanced water production, with desalination now supplying 75 to 80% of its drinking water and wastewater reuse covering about 50% of irrigation needs, ensuring reliability amid rapid population growth ( , ). Still, challenges remain due to increased anthropogenic activity and the impact of climate change on the region.
In contrast to Israel’s centralized water regime, the US approach to water management is complex and highly variable by region, reflecting both the country’s size and the diversity of its water resources and needs ( ). Thus, the US research community is not only interested in desalination and wastewater reuse but also increasingly interested in distributed water treatment and reuse, which may require tapping unconventional water sources (e.g., brackish groundwater and wastewater). This is an important proactive strategy to enhance resilience in water security due to uncertainty in water supply from droughts and disruption by extreme weather events, exacerbated by climate change, and to reduce the cost of water supply and energy intensity. Thus, overcoming water scarcity remains a shared challenge, shaped by financial limitations and the ongoing demand for innovation.

## State of the Science
As part of the Blavatnik workshop, experts presented the current state of seawater desalination technologies, including case studies on agricultural and municipal water reuse, as well as novel membrane-based systems and membrane materials that can enhance the selectivity toward targeted dissolved solutes. Although reverse osmosis (RO) membrane treatment is a well-established technology, there was a shared sentiment that further innovation is needed to enhance desalination efficiency, expand opportunities for resource recovery, reduce operational costs, and minimize environmental impact.
The initial session on desalination technologies featured talks by Roy Bernstein, Razi Epsztein, and Shihong Lin, all of whom emphasized the important role of advanced membrane materials and process design in enhancing resource recovery and maximizing water treatment efficiency. One presentation described a system that combines an anaerobic membrane bioreactor with granular ferric hydroxide columns. The system was shown to recover high levels of phosphate before RO membrane treatment. The session also explored innovative membrane designs, such as nanofiltration (NF) polyelectrolyte complex (PEC) membranes, with different polymer brush architectures that resist fouling and scaling while maintaining stable performance. Novel membrane designs with ion-specific selectivity were presented and their potential to reduce costs and enhance water and ion recovery was highlighted. A recurring theme was the need to further improve the energy efficiency of seawater reverse osmosis (SWRO). For example, optimizing pretreatment steps, especially through more cost-effective or robust approaches, can substantially reduce specific energy consumption. The longstanding assumption that two-stage RO is somewhat ineffective for SWRO was challenged. A thoughtful case was made that when paired with ultra-permeable membranes, this configuration may regain feasibility. The session concluded with a discussion on the economics of brine disposal—emphasizing that a shift of design target toward higher water recovery, and novel hybrid strategies, such as integrating SWRO with low-salt-rejection RO using leaky membranes, could enable high recovery with moderate energy penalties.
One of the most challenging environmental and cost-related aspects of membrane processes identified by the forum is brine management. Several experts presented the challenges and opportunities of brine disposal, focusing on emerging brine management methods with the potential for resource recovery. Pei Xu introduced the use of selective electrodialysis in water reuse, desalination, and resource recovery applications, which applies monovalent permselective ion-exchange membranes for targeted ion separation and is a valuable option for nonpotable water reuse and concentrate management. Work to date has shown selective recovery of lithium, a critical mineral, from the brine. Pilot studies using tertiary effluent of municipal wastewater, brackish water, and RO concentrate suggest that selective electrodialysis may significantly reduce operating costs to meet water quality requirements for uses in irrigation and cooling. Further research is needed to improve selectivity, low-resistance behavior, and production costs. The key message from this session was that while brine may hold promise as a source of valuable recovered elements, additional work is necessary for these approaches to be scalable and cost-effective at resource extraction.
Technological innovation in the domain of water quality is often reactive, driven by evolving standards. The session led by Adi Radian and Ines Zucker explored challenges in the development of new water treatment technologies and their implementation in response to these standards. An example of this is the surge of research on PFAS due to new stringent limits adopted by the US Environmental Protection Agency (April 2024). The most mature and commonly implemented technologies to remove PFAS from water are based on separation methods, including activated carbon, ion-exchange resins, or RO membrane processes. Thus, leading research is primarily focusing on degradation technologies (e.g., advanced oxidation and reduction processes) that, unlike separation-based approaches, go beyond transferring them from one phase to another to eliminate future liability. To achieve this, novel and sustainable material design and optimized processes are critically needed. The use of nature-based materials, such as clay minerals, as adsorbents and catalysts in water treatment technologies can offer a sustainable alternative to more toxic and costly conventional materials. However, several challenges remain in scaling up nanotechnology-based water treatment solutions, including issues related to cost, long-term maintenance, and potential environmental and health risks. Moreover, the critical need for improving water quality monitoring and assessment was identified as essential for ensuring the safety and effectiveness of reuse technologies. Specifically, precision monitoring for pathogens, antibiotics, resistance genes, disinfection byproducts, and emerging contaminants to meet stringent regulatory requirements are key for designing and implementing successful novel treatment regimes.
Beyond material advancements, process design was identified as a pitfall for translating fundamental research into large-scale, practical applications. Specifically, the need for pairing novel materials with stable conventional platforms was identified. Notably, Tzahi Cath presented a three-year study utilizing a new mobile direct potable reuse (DPR) system that combines several treatment technologies. The study showed how advanced control systems and data analytics can improve process efficiency and increase public confidence in DPR as a viable water source. Work with the mobile DPR system highlighted challenges associated with combinations of pretreatment strategies such as ozonation, biologically active filters, and adsorption columns. Thus, optimizing water treatment processes and tailoring the remediation chain to fit local demand can contribute substantially towards maximizing efficiency, reducing energy consumption, and ensuring high water quality. The DPR system also served as a testbed for collaboration and innovation, allowing researchers to generate high-quality data for process optimization and digital twin development.
The issue of collaboration for innovation was another key theme of the symposium. Speakers emphasized the importance of partnerships between sectors (universities, water agencies, and industries) in addressing water reuse challenges, barriers to implementation, and for improving the economic and practical feasibility of technologies. Water agency leader, Megan Plumlee highlighted how water agencies can serve as research testbeds for innovative ideas in real-world settings. The challenges faced by the Orange County Water District led to their large membrane-based potable reuse plant becoming a platform and venue where emerging technologies can be evaluated.

## State of Technology Implementation and Impacts
While the forum’s discussions centered on new technologies and cutting-edge research, concerns resonated throughout regarding feasibility and implementation. As desalination and reuse strategies expand globally, questions regarding the ecological, social, and political challenges need to be addressed. Israel and the US have valuable experiences to share in this regard, having gained extensive knowledge over years of practical implementation and its consequences. The acronym PESTLE (Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, and Environmental) was introduced as a framework to illustrate the various factors influencing the diffusion of technology from the lab to practical use. Other adaptation challenges raised in the forum included upscaling of technologies and material design, test-site availability, adapting operational settings across scales, and alignment utility plants’ needs with the ongoing research.
One major adaptation and transfer barrier of technologies from lab to field is social legitimization, especially for potable water. Speaker David Sedlak highlighted the efforts of the Orange County Water District’s potable water reuse program that gained legitimacy through pragmatic, moral, and cognitive legitimizations, where projects were framed as serving the public interest by creating a safe, sustainable, and resilient water supply, rather than a means of sewage disposal or low-cost water. This integration approach is a primary reason why California has largely favored potable water reuse over building more seawater desalination plants.
Higher energy consumption and the cost of seawater RO desalination compared to conventional water treatment presents another key challenge to widespread implementation. Additionally, large-scale infrastructure investment is needed to repair aging water systems and prepare for future climate-driven uncertainties. To address these challenges, investing in digitized, adaptable, distributed, and inclusive water infrastructure was proposed. Illustrating a concept of the water innovation cost space, speaker Meagan Mauter shared the Santa Barbara desalination plant as an example of cost optimization through strategic adjustments of operating hours. It was noted that technologies with high capital expenditures and low operating costs favor steady production, whereas those with low capital expenditures encourage a more dynamic response to changing conditions (i.e., droughts). Thus, exclusive focus on large, centralized treatment plants might overlook opportunities for smaller, decentralized systems.
Amy Childress introduced another strategy for optimizing yield while minimizing costs by integrating desalination and potable reuse treatment processes. The benefits of blending water sources with different salinities were presented, highlighting that treating seawater alongside wastewater leverages their nearly identical treatment processes, leading to cost savings and a reduced environmental footprint.
The final aspect that was discussed by the forum was the environmental footprint of desalination and water reuse technologies. Several ecosystems were identified as being directly at risk by the speakers Edo Bar Zeev and Osnat Gilor. One major concern is the coastal marine environment where large-scale seawater desalination facilities discharge hypersaline brine, posing significant challenges to aquatic ecosystems, including coral reefs, seagrass meadows, and benthic organisms. Brine plumes can extend several kilometers, alter nutrient fluxes, and affect the physiology of marine species. Chemical additives such as iron-based coagulants and polyphosphonate-based anti-scalants contribute to nutrient levels that may lead to eutrophication. Additionally, marine organisms are at risk during the intake process, as they can be harmed when water is drawn into the plant.
Another area of concern is the discharge of effluent into water bodies and agricultural areas. Current wastewater treatment technologies do not fully eliminate all organic pollutants, leaving trace contaminants and biological materials (such as antibiotic-resistant genes) that threaten both ecosystems and human health. These residual substances can enter food and water supplies, exposing humans to trace-pollutants, contributing to dangerous pathogens, and posing long-term environmental risks.

## Recommendations and Next Steps
Ensuring water security in the face of climate uncertainty will require a fundamental shift from traditional approaches. First, there is an urgent need to rethink water infrastructure and accelerate the development and deployment of advanced desalination and water reuse technologies, alongside sustainable strategies for brine management. Second, building more adaptive and resilient systems will depend on integrating circular economic principles, increasing system versatility, leveraging digitization, and adopting adaptive management practices. These strategies can also enhance the performance and reliability of mature technologies. Third, sustained collaboration across academia, industry, and policymakers is essential to identify research priorities, establish relevant testing and demonstration sites, and design policy frameworks that support public acceptance and responsible implementation. Together, these efforts can drive innovation and enable scalable solutions for providing safe, socially acceptable, and sustainable water to communities worldwide.

The Blavatnik US–Israel Scientific Forums are co-organized by the National Academy of Sciences (NAS) and the Israel Academy of Sciences and Humanities (IASH). A generous grant by the Blavatnik Family Foundation supports an annual scientific forum, as well as a visit by a senior researcher from one country to the other to promote collaboration and stimulate academic cooperation and understanding between academic institutions. The NAS was established in 1863 by an Act of Congress, signed by President Lincoln, as a private, nongovernmental institution to advise the nation on issues related to science and technology. Members are elected by their peers for outstanding contributions to research. Chartered by law in 1961, the IASH acts as a national focal point for Israeli scholarship in both the natural sciences and the humanities. The Academy membership consists of 152 of Israel’s most distinguished scientists and scholars, who monitor and promote Israeli intellectual excellence, advise the government on scientific planning, fund and publish research of lasting merit, and maintain active contact with the broader international scientific and scholarly community.
