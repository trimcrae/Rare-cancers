## Background
Digital health technologies have the potential to improve health outcomes in underserved settings. However, in low and middle-income countries with weak public health systems, unequal access to digital tools can worsen existing healthcare disparities. The DIG-EQUITY study explored the facilitators and barriers to equitable use of mobile and telehealth solutions for diabetes care in India, incorporating perspectives from people with diabetes, their family members (caregivers), healthcare providers, policymakers, and community organisations.

## Methods
A qualitative design was employed across urban (Chennai) and rural (Chunampet) settings in Tamil Nadu, following the 32-item COREQ checklist. A total of 54 participants (including type 1 diabetes (T1DM), type 2 diabetes (T2DM), and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM)) were included in four focus group discussions (FGDs) and 12 key informant interviews (KIIs). Participants were purposively sampled to ensure diversity in demographics and healthcare exposure. The analysis was guided by the Social Ecological Model (SEM), which informed the structuring and interpretation of themes across domains.

## Results
The different domains of the SEM influenced the utilisation of digital health. Individual factors such as age, digital literacy, and diabetes type shaped preferences. Interpersonal support from caregivers enabled access, particularly for older adults and children. Organisational and community influences included urban–rural infrastructure gaps, socioeconomic constraints, and shared device ownership. Policy-level concerns regarding data privacy, security, and app reliability affected trust and continued engagement.

## Conclusion
Socioeconomic status, sex, and geographic location influenced access and adoption of digital solutions. Bridging the divide through targeted digital literacy initiatives and inclusive strategies is essential to ensure equitable and effective use of digital health solutions for diabetes care in India.

The trial was registered with Central Trials Registry of India (CTRI/2022/04/041941).

## Background
Noncommunicable diseases are a growing threat to global society, accounting for 43 million deaths annually [ ]. Of the 589 million adults living with diabetes worldwide, 81% are from Low- and Middle-Income Countries [ ]. India reports having 101 million individuals with diabetes, placing enormous pressure on the healthcare system [ ]. Moreover, just 7.7% of people with diabetes in India have their glycosylated haemoglobin, blood pressure, and cholesterol under control [ ], which further increases the risk of diabetes complications [ ].
Digital health has a huge potential to enhance healthcare delivery in low-and middle-income countries. Particularly, it can strengthen diabetes prevention, early detection, and long-term management by expanding access to care and improving self-management [ , ]. The broad domain of digital health encompasses various categories, including mobile health (mHealth), health information technology (IT), wearable technologies, telehealth and telemedicine, as well as personalised medicine [ ]. The World Health Organisation introduced the Global Strategy on Digital Health 2020–2025 to improve health experiences for people of all ages [ ]. Over the past decade, many low-and middle-income countries have embraced digital health solutions, with more than 125 countries announcing national digital health strategies [ ]. Governments, donors, and corporations are investing in ambitious digital health programs to enhance healthcare access and quality [ , , ].
In India, the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission was launched by the Government of India in September 2021. It also undertook the development of a National Digital Health Ecosystem to advance digital health and innovation within the country [ ]. This initiative leverages Digital Public Goods (e.g. open-source software, data, AI models, standards, and content that are made available for public use) to enhance the availability, accessibility, affordability, and acceptability of healthcare through various building blocks [ ]. The rapid adoption of smartphones and the internet, along with favourable government policies, has driven the expansion of the mhealth market. In 2022, India’s digital healthcare market generated a revenue of INR 440.49 billion and is expected to reach INR 1.16 trillion by 2028, growing at a Compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 15.76% from 2023 to 2028 [ ].
While several studies in India have examined mHealth, telemedicine, and digital health adoption in diabetes care [ – ], much of the existing literature has focused on specific technologies [ , ], single user groups, or service utilisation patterns [ ] with limited attention to how multi-level factors interact to shape equitable digital health utilisation across diverse settings. Despite these advancements, a “digital divide” often persists among individuals and communities, leading to unequal access to and benefits from digital health interventions [ ]. If digital health initiatives are widely adopted as part of the continuum of care, without addressing issues of digital equity, there is a significant risk of worsening existing disparities in healthcare access and outcomes, particularly in low-and middle-income countries with weaker public health systems [ ]. Effective implementation of digital health interventions requires addressing barriers and leveraging facilitators at the health facility level, with active involvement from all stakeholders.
This study was thus undertaken to explore the facilitators and barriers to equitable utilisation of mHealth and telehealth among individuals with diabetes [type 1 diabetes (T1DM), type 2 diabetes (T2DM), and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM)] in India, through focus group discussions with patients and family members in diverse healthcare settings. We also conducted interviews with representatives from community organisations, policymakers, and health care providers (HCPs) working in the field of digital health solutions for diabetes care to gain a comprehensive understanding of the factors that may influence equitable access, adoption, and utilisation of these solutions in India.

## Methods

## Study design and settings
We employed a descriptive qualitative study approach, guided by the Social Ecological Model (SEM) [ ]. To ensure adherence to qualitative reporting standards, we followed the 32-item Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies (COREQ) checklist (Additional File 1). The study was approved by the Madras Diabetes Research Foundation Institutional Ethics Committee and registered with the Central Trials Registry of India (CTRI/2022/04/041941).

## Participant and screening
Participant recruitment was guided by purposive sampling [ ] but ensured heterogeneity by including key stakeholder groups, such as people with diabetes and caregivers across geographic settings (urban and rural), sex, age groups, and diabetes types (T1DM, T2DM, and GDM), healthcare providers, policymakers, and community organisations. As per Malterud et al. [ ] and Palinkas LA et al. [ ], sample size adequacy in a qualitative study can be determined based on representation across predefined participant categories, inclusion of diverse socioeconomic and healthcare access contexts, and feasibility. Using this context, the sampling strategy adopted helped us identify individuals with lived experiences of using or those aware of digital health technologies for diabetes care. This ensured the depth and richness of the data collected. Iterative data collection and concurrent analysis were undertaken across major analytical domains informed by the SEM. Participants for the focus group discussions (FGDs) were recruited from the outpatient department of a tertiary care diabetes centre in Chennai and a rural diabetes unit in Tamil Nadu. Eligibility criteria included being 18–65 years old, having diabetes or being a caregiver of a person with diabetes, using a smartphone, and being willing to participate. All individuals who met these criteria were approached, and those who provided informed consent were included in the study.
The patient FGDs (n = 2) included individuals with T1DM, T2DM, and GDM conducted separately in urban and rural areas, while the caregiver FGDs (n = 2) included family members of people with diabetes (Fig.  ). The key informant interviews (KIIs) were held with representatives from community organisations (n = 2), policy makers (n = 2), and HCPs (n = 8) (Fig.  ). The community organisation representatives who attended the KIIs were from Diabetes & You, Kolkata, and Program for Appropriate Technology in Health, Delhi. The policymakers involved in the study were from the Department of Public Health in Tamil Nadu and the e-Sanjeevani program from the central government. In urban areas, one physician and one diabetes educator from the government sector, as well as one physician and one diabetes educator from the private sector, were selected for KIIs of HCPs. In rural areas, one physician and one nurse from the government sector and one physician and one dietician from the private sector were selected.

 

Although recruitment was not explicitly terminated based on formal saturation criteria, iterative data collection and simultaneous analysis were undertaken. During the later stages of data collection, no substantially new themes emerged, suggesting that thematic saturation was approached across major analytical domains informed by the SEM. However, credibility of the findings were strengthened through a combination of purposeful sampling, high information power, methodological triangulation (FGDs and KIIs), inclusion of multiple stakeholder groups, use of a standardised reporting guideline (COREQ), and team-based coding and analysis. These strategies enhanced the robustness and trustworthiness of the findings.

## Data collection
We used the elements of digital health equity illustrated by Lyles et al. [ ], mapped to the SEM of health [ ]. SEM is useful in understanding the various forces that shape an individual’s capacity and ability to engage in healthful behaviours. Separate guides were developed for each stakeholder group to help capture a wide range of perspectives. Questions were mapped to SEM domains - individual, interpersonal/family, community, and health system/policy levels and explored individual experiences and skills, family and social support, community infrastructure and access, and health system and policy influences (Fig. ). The guides have been attached as a supplementary file (Additional File 2). Trained qualitative researchers (HR (primary interviewer) and SN (notetaker)) conducted the interviews in a closed venue to ensure better sound quality and the convenience of the participants. The interview began with a round of introductions, an explanation of consent, and open-ended questions using an interview guide. Informed consent was obtained from participants before the discussions, and the discussions were conducted in a language familiar to all participants. Each KII and FGD lasted approximately 40–60 min. Interviews were recorded with a voice recorder, and field notes were taken by an observer. FGD participants were given a small monetary incentive after the discussions as a token of appreciation for their participation and time. The recordings were transcribed by a neutral transcriber not part of the study, and the transcripts were coded and analysed for themes by SK, SN, and HR.

 

## Data analysis
The FGDs and KIIs were first transcribed verbatim to ensure an accurate representation of participant responses. The transcriber was well-versed in both languages and sent the transcription notes in English. These transcripts were then carefully compiled into a structuredExcel spreadsheet to facilitate systematic organisation and analysis. The reviewers used notes as well as the tapes during analysis.
Thematic analysis was conducted within a constructivist paradigm, which acknowledges that meanings and realities are co-constructed through interaction between researchers and participants. The analytical process followed Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach [ ], beginning with data familiarisation, then generating initial codes, identifying themes, reviewing, defining, and ultimately constructing a coherent narrative.
The analysis commenced with a thorough familiarisation phase, during which researchers engaged deeply with the data by repeatedly listening to the audio recordings and closely reading through the transcripts. This step helped build an immersive understanding of the context and content of the discussions. Descriptive codes were then developed to capture key concepts, recurring ideas, and patterns across the data. These codes were systematically applied across all transcripts to ensure consistency and comparability.
To further enhance the comprehensiveness of the analysis, the framework analysis approach was adopted, allowing data to be organized within a matrix structure and enabling comparisons across cases and themes using the detailed framework given in Fig.  . This combined methodological and theoretical approach, using inductive and deductive coding strategies, facilitated the identification of nuanced and multi-level insights.
HR developed the initial coding frame and iteratively refined it through discussions with RMA, SN, and SK to ensure coherence and analytical rigor. The final phase of the analysis involved interrelating themes and constructing a rich narrative that reflected the depth and diversity of participants’ experiences. All data analysis was conducted using QSR NVivo 10 software to support systematic coding and data retrieval.

## Results
A total of 54 individuals participated in the study (Fig.  ), comprising 42 participants from four FGDs and 12 participants through KIIs. All consented participants completed the study.
Table  summarizes the socio-demographic profile of participants across four FGDs, divided by urban and rural settings and participant type (patients and caregivers). The overall mean age of the participants was 45 ±10.7 years, and around 60% of them were males. All of them were smartphone users except 1. Duration of diabetes was longest among rural patients (13 years), while caregivers reported 7–8 years of caregiving experience.

 

Table  categorizes information from all stakeholders into SEM informed themes and sub-themes, highlighting urban–rural differences in digital health utilization. The perspectives related to challenges and opportunities in digital health for diabetes patients across urban and rural India, as well as men and women, were found to be partially congruent, but also unique. The findings are presented below according to the five levels of the Social Ecological Model: individual, interpersonal, organisational, community, and policy.

 

## Individual-level influences
This level reflects participants’ personal experiences, perceptions, skills, and preferences related to digital health use.
Urban participants described greater access to digital tools such as teleconsultations, mobile health apps, and wearable devices, which offered a convenient, flexible healthcare experience. In contrast, rural participants primarily depended on in-person consultations and used social media platforms for general information. Both urban and rural patients and caregivers were familiar with self-monitoring devices for blood glucose monitoring.
Acceptance and comfort with digital platforms varied by diabetes type. Women with GDM favoured various apps for information and monitoring. Meanwhile, T1DM patients expressed a preference for direct consultations due to the need for physical assessments. However, during the COVID-19 pandemic, caregivers and patients alike found digital options supportive for remote management.
Many participants perceived teleconsultation as an advantage for follow-ups and sharing lab results via messaging platforms and email. Patients appreciated fitness and diet-tracking apps for helping them remain engaged in self-care. Participants also preferred maintaining digital health records instead of paper reports due to ease of access, storage, and long-term reference.
Despite growing acceptance, several individual-level barriers were reported. Limited digital literacy, especially among older adults, emerged as a significant challenge. Some participants found digital health impersonal or incomplete. Urban T1DM patients raised concerns about the accuracy and reliability of app-based readings. Many patients mentioned difficulty integrating app recommendations into daily life due to irregular work schedules. 

“I used to record my diet and all using the app. If we could not reach the hospital, reports were sent through WhatsApp and email, and the doctor followed up the next day.” — Urban T2DM patient
“We go to the doctor and act as per the advice given. We follow his walking and simple exercises in our routine. We are not satisfied with these apps.” — Rural T2DM patient.
“Most elders do not know anything about using the phone. They do not even know what are apps. They only use button phones. All they can do is to make a call and cut the call. For them, opening their phone and opening the app, and getting the information is very much complicated. They feel uncomfortable.” - Urban T2DM caregiver.

## Interpersonal-level influences
This level captures the role of family members, caregivers, and close social networks in shaping digital health use.
Caregivers played an important role in facilitating access to digital health services, particularly for older adults and children with diabetes. Urban caregivers described using teleconsultation to manage elderly family members and reduce unnecessary hospital visits. During the COVID-19 pandemic, caregivers relied on online services for consultations and educational sessions.
Rural caregivers reported learning about diet and exercise from online videos and supporting patients in following these recommendations. Shared use of smartphones within families also influenced access and utiliation, particularly in rural settings.

“We used it for my mother, who is 76 years old. Only when it is absolutely necessary, I will bring her to the hospital.” — Urban T2DM caregiver.
“My father watches videos on YouTube about diets and walking. He takes that information seriously and follows it.” — Rural T2DM caregiver.
“During corona we couldn’t take our children to the centres, so online services were very helpful. We used WhatsApp video calls to connect with doctors and attend online classes on diet.” — Urban T1DM caregiver.

## Organizational-level influences
This level reflects how healthcare institutions and providers influence access to and use of digital health technologies.
Urban private-sector healthcare providers reported using electronic health records, telehealth, and wearable devices. Rural private-sector providers mainly used digital tools for consultations and screening. Government healthcare providers in both urban and rural sectors used digital platforms to communicate with patients through telehealth, video calls, and messages.
Health professionals highlighted that digital tools improved patient reach, reduced travel burden, and facilitated real-time monitoring using glucometers and wearables. However, some providers noted challenges related to connectivity, communication quality, and patient comprehension during virtual consultations.
Healthcare providers also emphasized the importance of secure systems and regulated app certification before recommending digital tools.

“Tele consultation acceptance is high only when using WhatsApp rather than using any app or video.” – Rural HCP government sector.
“If the connectivity is bad, we may not be able to hear them, we will not be able to understand what they are saying.”- Urban HCP government sector.
“In a call, we may tend to forget things, or we may not get the flow of information properly while speaking to the doctor.” — Urban T1DM patient.

## Community-level influences
This level reflects broader social, geographic, and infrastructural contexts influencing digital health utilisation.
Urban participants reported better digital infrastructure, internet connectivity, and exposure to teleconsultation platforms. In contrast, rural participants faced greater challenges related to connectivity, language barriers, and awareness of digital health services.
Socio-economic disparities influenced access and confidence in using digital technologies. Participants noted that many rural households had only one smartphone, and lower-income and older adults often struggled with digital literacy and trust in technology.
Gender-based barriers were more pronounced in rural areas, where women had limited access to smartphones and were reluctant to use video consultations.

“The issue of connectivity is no more a problem now in urban areas.”— Urban T2DM patient.
“There will be only one smartphone in a family in rural areas.” – Community organisation.
“Women at times in certain sections of the society, don’t even own a phone, and prefer to not to talk to a doctor through a camera. They are camera cautious and they don’t want their picture to be sent to the other end.“- Policy maker.

## Policy-level influences
This level reflects regulatory, governance, and system-level factors affecting digital health use.
Participants consistently raised concerns about data safety, privacy, and governance of digital health platforms. Urban healthcare providers and community organizations emphasized confidentiality and secure data storage. Caregivers and patients expressed uncertainty about how their data were used.
There was a call for stronger national health data protection policies. Policymakers highlighted that platforms such as e-Sanjeevani complied with existing guidelines, with no reported breaches.
Overall, privacy and regulatory frameworks were viewed as essential for building trust in digital health systems.

“We enter all our health data, but we don’t know where it goes or who accesses it.” — Urban T2DM patient and Caregiver.
“We conform to electronic health records guidelines of the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare. And also we comply with the norms mentioned in telemedicine practice guidelines. So there are no issues pertaining to the mishandling or mishap on using data in the wrong way.” -Policy maker.

## Cross-level strategies to improve digital health uptake
This theme reflects strategies that operate across multiple SEM levels to strengthen digital health adoption.
Participants across settings emphasized the importance of user-friendly interfaces, intuitive app design, and regional language support. Hands-on demonstrations, short videos, and visual educational content were suggested to enhance comprehension and engagement.
Healthcare providers recommended integrating local-language chat options, short exercise videos, and patient education materials. Participants agreed that culturally relevant content and practical guidance were essential for sustained use.“It should be very easy to access. Then, only people can learn more things and become aware. But with these apps, if you are stuck somewhere in between, you would normally be confused where to go, and we tend to close the app.”
“Video is the best way to reach the heart of the people. Our span of attention is only 2 min, we cannot see anything more than 2 min, we would only change the channel.”

## Discussion
The ‘DIG-EQUITY’ study advances existing research by integrating the multi-stakeholder perspective to demonstrate how the SEM domains of individual, social, institutional, infrastructural, and policy factors collectively shape equitable digital health utilisation for diabetes care in India. A summary of the findings is as follows; At the individual level, factors such as age, digital literacy, socioeconomic status, and type of diabetes were associated with the preference for digital or face-to-face approaches. At the interpersonal level, caregivers were identified as enablers of access, but the sharing of devices, especially among rural women, was a hindrance for independent use of digital health approaches. At the organisational/community level, the rural-urban disparity was a factor, and at the policy level, ongoing issues of data privacy, security, and app accuracy affected the confidence and trust of all participants in digital health approaches.
India offers a wide range of digital health applications for diabetes prevention and management, including education, monitoring, insulin titration, and lifestyle management tools [ ]. Evidence from mHealth-based intervention studies in India demonstrates improvements in cardiometabolic risk factors and lifestyle behaviours when digital tools are designed and implemented effectively [ – ]. A six-month mHealth coaching trial demonstrated significant reductions in body mass index, waist circumference, blood pressure, and fasting glucose, using simplified video lessons, short text messages, and infographics to overcome navigation barriers among urban Indian adults [ ]. Another mHealth randomised control trial demonstrated greater reductions in HbA1c, lipids, BMI, and blood pressure among urban and rural users compared to controls, supported by a regional language interface and culturally tailored content [ ]. Many participants viewed digital tools as complementary to traditional care [ , ]. Our findings further indicate that older adults, individuals with limited education, and those with irregular work schedules experience greater difficulty navigating digital platforms. Type 1 diabetes patients, in particular, expressed a preference for in-person consultations due to the value placed on physical assessment and interpersonal rapport. Similar preferences have been reported in previous studies [ ].
Interpersonal relationships, particularly caregiver support, played a crucial role in shaping digital health utilization. Caregivers often facilitated teleconsultations, supported monitoring practices, and helped interpret online information, especially for elderly patients and children with diabetes. However, in rural settings, shared device ownership and gendered access to technology limited the effectiveness of such support. Women were often dependent on family members for phone access, reflecting broader structural inequities in digital ownership and control [ – ]. National data indicate that nearly 51.6% of rural women in India do not own a mobile phone [ ], and even when they use one, it is often not personally owned, limiting privacy, autonomy, and independent healthcare decision-making [ ]. Patriarchal norms in rural central India give women phone ownership but limit their actual use. Women who own phones often face restrictions on usage due to family surveillance, mobility limits, and norms prioritising male control [ ]. Addressing these inequities requires promoting women’s independent access to devices, strengthening digital literacy, and implementing gender-sensitive digital health strategies [ ].
At the organisational and community levels, disparities in infrastructure, service delivery, and socio-economic conditions influenced digital health adoption. Urban healthcare providers were more likely to use electronic health records, teleconsultation platforms, and monitoring devices, while rural providers relied primarily on basic digital tools. Major challenges were similar to those reported by other studies; namely, limited internet connectivity, low bandwidth, lack of electricity, unfamiliarity with digital tools, and low health literacy in rural communities [ , – ]. Financial constraints related to smartphone ownership, data charges, and wearable devices further restricted access among low-income populations [ ]. These structural barriers were compounded by language limitations and limited awareness of digital services. Urban areas were better positioned to benefit from digital health innovations due to better connectivity and service availability. These findings are consistent with earlier studies demonstrating stronger digital health effects in urban settings [ – ].
At the policy level, participants expressed significant concerns regarding data privacy, security, and governance. Skepticism toward large-scale digitalisation reflected limited trust in existing regulatory frameworks [ ]. The absence of a robust data protection framework has previously been identified as a major barrier to digital health adoption [ ]. Initiatives under the Digital India campaign have significantly driven India’s push towards a digitally empowered healthcare system by promoting digital infrastructure, e-governance, and technology-enabled public services [ ]. Supporting policies such as the National Digital Health Blueprint and National Health Policy 2017 promote telemedicine, electronic health records, and digital health innovation [ ]. Platforms such as e-Sanjeevani have expanded access to remote consultationsthrough government-supported telemedicine services. Recent developments, including the Digital Personal Data Protection Act (2023) and the DPDP Rules (2025), aim to strengthen data governance, regulate personal data processing, and safeguard citizens’ rights [ ]. However, our findings suggest that effective implementation and transparent communication remain essential for building public trust.

## Implications for practice and policyf
Our findings indicate that efforts to promote equitable digital health in diabetes care must move beyond improving access alone and address barriers across multiple SEM levels. At the individual level, targeted digital literacy initiatives are required to enable meaningful use of digital tools. At the interpersonal level, caregiver engagement strategies should be strengthened to support sustained utilisation. Organisational and community interventions should focus on improving infrastructure, language accessibility, and affordability to translate access into effective engagement. At the policy level, stronger enforcement of data protection regulations is needed to ensure trust and equitable benefit. Integrated and context-sensitive approaches are essential for addressing urban-rural, gender, and socio-economic disparities in digital health access, utilization and outcomes.”

## Strengths and limitations
The key strength of the study is that it adopts a digital equity lens to examine not only access and use, but also the factors influencing digital health utilization for diabetes care in urban and rural India. A limitation could be that the eligibility criteria required participants to be smartphone users, which may have excluded individuals who are most digitally marginalised. As a result, the study may underestimate the depth of the digital divide.

## Conclusion
Digital health has considerable potential to improve diabetes management in India; however, substantial challenges remain. Limited digital skills, shortages of trained professionals, and linguistic diversity continue to hinder widespread adoption [ ]. Targeted eHealth initiatives such as Tele-MANAS, Nikshay 2.0, and e-Sanjeevani demonstrate India’s progress in leveraging digital tools to enhance healthcare accessibility [ ]. Bridging technological, socio-economic, and cultural divides while strengthening policy implementation will be essential for achieving equitable and inclusive digital healthcare.
