## Abstract
Nutrition experts point to the importance of a multipronged approach to address high stunting rates in rural areas. This can include nutrition‐specific and ‐sensitive interventions, such as the provision of micronutrient powder, eggs, and chlorine, and nutrition training to improve feeding practices. In 2018, an agricultural nongovernmental organisation initiated a multipronged approach as part of a randomised trial. However, adherence to a programme with so many components can be challenging for participants. The aim of this study is to understand which factors are associated with high adherence in complex multifaceted nutrition‐sensitive agricultural programmes. We used a mixed method approach in which we used bivariate and multivariable analyses to estimate the relationship between child and caregiver demographic factors with product adherence. We analyzed data from six focus groups and 120 feedback sessions on barriers to product adherence. We found that the age and sex of the child did not influence product adherence, but caregivers were more likely to adhere to all products if they were not the child's biological mother (most often grandmothers) (0.28 higher adherence score; p < 0.001) and if caregivers were older (0.34 higher adherence score, p < 0.001). A higher monthly training attendance, combining product distribution and interactive training, predicted stronger product adherence. Participants noted that adherence was supported by the early demonstration of positive results, regular reminders, interactive trainings, and the encouragement of family members. These findings underscore the importance of combining product distribution with training and include potentially targeting grandmothers and other caregivers who may demonstrate higher product adherence.
Keywords: behaviour, child nutrition, knowledge, micronutrients, nutritional interventions, training

## Key messages

Combining product distribution and training can increase both training attendance and product adherence in multipronged programmes combating malnutrition.
While most nutrition programmes target women of childbearing age, this research highlights the importance of including grandmothers and other caregivers who may be more likely to adhere to products and practices and extended family members whose support is crucial in overcoming barriers to product adoption.
Overall adherence to products and practices can be enhanced by regular reminders and narrative‐ and dialogical‐based trainings, which provide ample opportunity for discussion and reflection.

## INTRODUCTION
While gains have been made in recent decades, child stunting continues to affect an estimated 21% (144 million) of children under five (Kinyoki et al.,  ; UNICEF/WHO/World Bank,  ; Vaivada et al.,  ) and leads to a host of deleterious long‐term outcomes such as poor cognitive development, poor academic performance and lower lifetime wages and productivity relative to nonstunted children (Adair et al.,  ). On average, children in rural areas have even worse rates of malnutrition and stunting than those in urban areas, despite living in closer proximity to agriculture, largely due to population‐level differences in wealth and education (Van de Poel et al.,  ).
The high rates of stunting in rural areas and the fact that agriculture is the main source of income and food for the majority of rural households means that there is a strong opportunity to reduce stunting by changing agricultural practices. However, in general, agricultural programmes alone have had limited success in improving nutritional outcomes, highlighting the importance of nutrition‐sensitive strategies such as the promotion of nutrient‐rich foods, water sanitation and hygiene (WASH) programmes and behaviour change campaigns alongside agricultural programmes (Fanzo,  ; Ruel et al.,  ). In 2018, One Acre Fund, an agricultural nongovernmental organisation (NGO), which serves rural farm families in Eastern and Southern Africa, embarked on just such a campaign in which rural families with children aged 6–35 months were randomly selected to receive either an agricultural intervention only (agricultural inputs on credit with training good agricultural practices) or the agricultural programme plus nutrition‐specific interventions (micronutrient powders (MNP), poultry to increase egg consumption and seeds to grow red onions and indigenous greens intended to increase vegetable consumption) and nutrition‐sensitive interventions (soap, chlorine, oral rehydration solution (ORS) and zinc for treatment for acute diarrhoea) accompanied by monthly nutrition and WASH trainings. The results of this randomised trial on linear growth (change in height‐for‐age z‐score, HAZ) are presented in detail elsewhere (Wegmüller et al.,  ). Overall, the study found a modest improvement in linear growth for the intervention group (change in HAZ over 2 years of 0.26 vs. 0.16 in the control group, p = 0.011) but found a slightly stronger effect on the improvement of dietary diversity and lower morbidity, suggesting that adherence was a core driver of positive outcomes. Maintaining consistent use of multiple novel products is often the most challenging aspect of long‐term nutrition studies. Understanding the drivers of adherence across multiple simultaneously introduced products and interventions can unlock the potential of nutrition‐sensitive agriculture programmes.
Despite the fact that adherence to nutrition protocols is crucial for programme success, there are relatively few peer‐reviewed studies examining adherence to nutrition products and strategies for multipronged programmes such as this (Brown & Clasen,  ; de Barros & Cardoso,  ; Roschnik et al.,  ). Of all of the products offered in this trial, the largest body of research on adherence is related to MNPs, possibly due to MNP's relatively strong efficacy and suitability to low‐income contexts. Multiple studies, which examined the factors that increase MNP adherence, found that the caregiver's perception that the product was delivering a positive change increased adherence (D'agostino et al.,  ; de Barros & Cardoso,  ; Mirkovic et al.,  ; Roschnik et al.,  ; Tumilowicz et al.,  ; Tumilowicz et al.,  ). Studies have observed that the child's taste and acceptance of MNP increased adherence (Brewer et al.,  ; D'agostino et al.,  ; Mirkovic et al.,  ; Samuel et al.,  ; Tumilowicz et al.,  ; Tumilowicz et al.,  ). Broader family and community support were also found to be important, with trust in field staff, community support and family support, all increasing the likelihood of adherence (Brewer et al.,  ; Samuel et al.,  ; Tumilowicz et al.,  ; D'agostino et al.,  ; Pelto et al.,  ). Multiple channel messaging including sending reminders and social marketing were also found to improve product adherence (Tumilowicz et al.,  ; D'agostino et al.,  ; Mirkovic et al.,  ). There is relatively less evidence on the factors that influence adherence to the other nutrition and WASH products offered in this trial, such as chlorine solution for water treatment, chickens to increase egg consumption and vegetable seeds to increase growth and consumption of vitamin‐rich vegetables. While adherence is particularly important in household water treatment, it has not been measured in the majority of household water treatment field trials (Brown & Clasen,  ); however, some studies find that chlorine use is associated with a high‐risk perception of contracting diseases, family social support, knowledge of use and age, with older participants exhibiting higher use (Lilje & Mosler,  ). The promotion of egg consumption has emerged as a relatively new strategy to combat malnutrition (Baum et al.,  ; Iannotti et al.,  ; Iannotti et al.,  ; McKune et al.,  ; Omer et al.,  ), so there are few studies examining the precise correlates of high uptake or adherence. However, some note that social marketing campaigns hold promise in increasing egg consumption overall (Lutter et al.,  ).

## METHODS
2.1.

## Study design and intervention
This cluster‐randomised controlled trial was implemented in the subcounties of Lugari and Likuyani of Kakamega County, in Kenya's western region from March 2018 to April 2020 with the goal of assessing the effectiveness of a bundle of agricultural intervention (agricultural inputs on credit with training on good agricultural practices) and nutrition‐specific (MNP, chickens to increase egg consumption, and seeds to grow red onions and indigenous greens intended to increase vegetable consumption) and ‐sensitive (soap, chlorine, ORS and zinc for treatment for acute diarrhoea) interventions on child growth compared with a control group receiving the agricultural intervention only. Clusters mainly consisted of part of a village, one village, or up to two villages to have an average cluster size of approximately 20 eligible participants. Children aged 6–35 months at enrolment from farming households participating in the One Acre Fund programme whose legal guardian provided written informed consent were included in the study, provided the family intended to stay within the study area for the following 24 months and the child had no symptoms of severe disease as verified by the study doctor (e.g., severe wasting).
All participating households (both control and intervention arms) were provided with agricultural training (every 2 weeks on average). Caregivers of participating children in the intervention group additionally received micronutrient powders (MNP; MixMe [15 micronutrient formulation (21)], DSM) and oral rehydration solution (ORS) plus zinc (10 mg/tablet) for treatment of acute diarrhoea. Every month, 10 MNP packages were delivered and the caregivers were reminded to give one sachet every 3 days; ORS/zinc was resupplied if caregivers had used up the previous allocation. All households in the intervention group were provided with soap (600 g every month), chlorine solution (500 ml every 6 months), poultry (8 chickens at the start of the intervention, with a resupply between December 2018 and June 2019 to compensate for losses), and seeds to grow red onions and indigenous greens to increase vegetable consumption (March 2018 and March 2019). Caregivers were given training on caring for chickens at the time of chicken delivery and regularly encouraged to feed one egg per day to the participating child through monthly nutrition trainings. The nutrition intervention bundle was provided free of charge.
Intervention product distributions were accompanied by monthly nutrition and WASH trainings, along with product‐specific demonstrations and instructions for use. These trainings were adapted by the nutrition trainers depending on their participants' knowledge but were based on materials developed to engage caregivers and foster discussions, such as pictorial brochures, storybooks, and toys. Modules included a product demonstration, growth monitoring, introduction of complementary foods, psychomotor and mental development, responsive feeding, nutrition and care for sick children, germ transmission, food/dietary diversity (including home gardening and small animal breeding), safe food handling, personal hygiene, child's hygiene and handling of faeces, water treatment and storage, and micronutrient powders.

2.2.

## Data collection
We used a mixed method approach to understand the drivers of adherence to MNP, eggs, and use of chlorine individually and also the full package of nutrition products and interventions of MNP, chlorine, eggs, vitamin‐rich vegetable seeds, and soap. We measured adherence as the use of items distributed to the households of intervention children during quarterly adherence and morbidity monitoring visits. Chlorine bottles were weighed at each visit to determine the amount of chlorine solution used in the prior quarter. Caregivers were given two distinct storage containers and encouraged to store unused MNP sachets in one and the other to store the used/empty sachets for collection and disposal by the study team. Therefore, there was near total compliance on sachet storage making it easy for the enumerators to count the used MNP sachets at each quarterly monitoring visit. The enumerators verified the number of used sachets against the number of sachets available in the unused sachet container, which helped them get an accurate number in the event that the caregiver forgot to store the used sachets. The adherence indicator was the total number of MNP sachets consumed by the study child during the follow‐up period. During the quarterly monitoring visits, the enumerators also asked the caregivers to recall and report the number of eggs consumed by the child in the past 7 days preceding the visit day. The adherence indicator was the number of eggs consumed by the study child during the 7 days preceding the quarterly monitoring visit day. Households received a 600 g bar of soap with four equally marked slices (quarters) per month. During the quarterly monitoring visits, the enumerators collected data (by recall or observation if only a portion of given soap has been used) on the number of quarters of the provided soap used in the month as the adherence indicator of soap used. Because the ultimate objective of distributing seeds of greens and onions was consumption of the resulting crop by household members, the adherence indicator was the number of years these crops were consumed in the study household. (We did not include ORS and zinc in the overall adherence index because we only asked respondents about diarrhoea episodes during a 2‐week recall period every 3 months and therefore detected only a small portion of episodes. In addition, ORS and zinc use were already quite high, so there is less reason to study factors that increase adherence.) The quantitative analysis was based on household surveys (n = 984) in which enumerators collected both self‐reported (e.g., the number of eggs consumed by the study child in the past 7 days) and verified data (e.g., enumerators counted empty sachets of MNP, counted chickens, and used a pocket scale to weigh bottles of chlorine to assess usage) once every quarter. At the end of the study, we also collected qualitative data through focus groups with participants to understand caregivers' experience with the interventions, as well as their perceptions, uptake, and adherence to the intervention products.
2.2.1.

## Quantitative data collection and analysis
Dedicated data collection teams composed of experienced interviewers were trained on the study‐specific procedures before the baseline and before subsequent assessment rounds. Within a week of obtaining written informed consent, a baseline assessment was conducted, in which demographic information was collected: child's age and sex, household food security, household wealth indicators, and caregiver's age and education. After the programme roll‐out, quarterly adherence and morbidity monitoring was conducted for the 2 years of the study. During these visits, enumerators counted empty MNP sachets, used a pocket scale to weigh chlorine bottles to determine use (the weight difference between a distribution and the quarterly monitoring determined the quantity of chlorine used over the 3‐month period), counted chickens, and obtained feedback about all products distributed. Questionnaire data were collected using tablets with pre‐installed CommCare data collection software (Dimagi).
Data analysis on adherence was conducted using SPSS (Version 27). We used principal component analysis to create a combined adherence index, considering individual measures of adherence for each of the six products for each child. Bivariate statistical tests (t‐tests and one‐way ANOVA tests) were conducted to estimate the association between child and caregiver demographic variables and adherence to each product as well as the child and caregiver demographic variables with the adherence index for all products together. We also generated a dichotomous variable for training attendance with a separation at the median and similarly tested the association of this dichotomous training attendance variable with product adherence. Finally, to more precisely estimate the impact of training, we ran OLS regressions of product adherence on training attendance, age of the child, mother's education, household size, wealth, and marital status and clustered our standard errors at the cluster level.

2.2.2.

## Qualitative data collection
Midway through the study, the nutrition trainers, who administered the monthly training, were trained to conduct feedback sessions targeting caregivers or the representatives (secondary caregivers) during specific monthly training sessions to document the main barriers that made it difficult for caregivers to give their child one sachet of MNP every 3 days. Using the nutrition trainers to conduct the barrier‐focused feedback sessions allowed us to cost‐effectively reach a large number of study participants during the scheduled monthly training sessions. Altogether, the nutrition trainers and qualitative data collection team conducted 120 such feedback sessions. While nutrition trainers were not independent observers, we feel that bias was likely minimized as the focus of these barrier‐focused feedback sessions was barriers to adoption, rather than any programme or trainer feedback. At the end of the study, dedicated qualitative data collection teams, composed of experienced interviewers and facilitators, were trained to conduct focus group discussions (FGDs) with caregivers, to understand their perceptions, attitudes, beliefs, and opinion on the interventions. They conducted six FGDs with six and eight participants each.
The FGD groups were randomly selected from the study clusters, and six to eight participants, who consented, were randomly selected to take part in the discussions. The FGD sessions were audio‐recorded and later transcribed, whereas the barrier‐focused feedback sessions were paper‐recorded. The thematic analysis method was used in the analysis of qualitative data to identify determinants: barriers and enablers to intervention uptake and adherence as well as the general caregivers' experiences with the interventions. Three cycles of barrier‐focused feedback sessions were conducted within training groups and were facilitated by the nutrition trainers, who shared the paper‐recorded session notes. The sessions helped to identify behavioural determinants of the uptake of MNP. Cycle 1 targeted all intervention clusters, whereas Cycles 2 and 3 targeted 49 clusters randomly selected from the intervention arm and 8 clusters with the lowest MNP adherence, respectively.

## RESULTS
3.1.

## Adherence rates
Overall, adherence to the nutrition and WASH products was fairly high, but there was some decrease in adherence in the second year of the study. Caregivers used an average of 112.6 sachets of MNP in the first year, as was visually verified by enumerators, which was quite close to the target of 120 sachets per year (see Table  ). However, this declined to 84.5 sachets per year in the second year. This may be due to the slight decline in monthly training participation, which also served as a distribution point for MNP sachets, among caregivers themselves. Chlorine use actually increased over the study from 267.9 ml per quarter in the first year to 322.7 ml in the second year and exceeded the overall use targets in both years. Children consumed 3.3 eggs on average per week in the first year; however, this dipped to 2.2 eggs per week in the second year of the program, and both years fell short of the goal of one egg per day. This was likely due to the higher‐than‐anticipated chicken mortality rate and possibly reflects a natural ceiling of what is considered culturally appropriate in terms of egg consumption among young children.
 
3.2.

## Predictors of adherence
We first consider whether the characteristics of the child may have influenced adherence to any of the individual products or a composite score of adherence to all products (see Table  ). Neither the age nor the sex of the child influenced the adherence to any of the products. While child characteristics did not appear to influence adherence, caregiver characteristics proved more influential. Interestingly, caregivers who were not the child's biological mother had higher overall adherence to all products (p < 0.001) and also higher adherence to chlorine (p < 0.001) (see Table  ). Ninety‐three percent (93%) of nonmaternal caregivers were grandmothers to the child, so what we are observing is a “grandmother effect” whereby grandmothers exhibited higher overall adherence. This likely also influenced the relationship we see in the age of the caregiver with older caregivers also exhibiting greater adherence to all products (p < 0.001) combined and chlorine (p = 0.019). Caregiver marital status was correlated with higher adherence, though this was only weakly significant (p = 0.061). Caregiver educational status did not predict adherence to all products combined but did influence adherence to MNP and to eggs; however, the absolute differences were quite small. Those caregivers who worked on their farm as their primary occupation had higher adherence to all products combined (p = 0.020). Both having had a prior pregnancy and having more previous live births were positively correlated with higher adherence to all products combined.
 Turning to household characteristics, we find that households with more members overall tended to have higher adherence to all products combined, but this relationship was weakly significant (p = 0.052). The number of children under five in the house did not predict adherence to any products. Interestingly, household wealth did not predict adherence to any products except for eggs, with households in wealthier quintiles feeding more eggs per week (p = 0.029).
Participants in this study attended monthly training sessions which, in addition to being a vehicle for information dissemination, provided a platform to encourage continued product use. These training sessions also included the distribution of soap and the monthly supply of MNP sachets. If participants were unable to attend a training, they were encouraged to send a representative from their household. Overall, training attendance was quite high, with nearly all months achieving over 95% attendance, but the percent of participants who were the primary caregiver decreased over time. By the 22nd month, just under 75% of caregivers were attending training, but another 25% sent a representative in their stead.
Because participants were allowed to send a representative to training sessions, we look at the relationship between training attendance by the caregiver and attendance overall with adherence to the study products. We see in the bivariate relationships that overall training attendance predicted adherence to all products combined (p = 0.001) and also with adherence to MNPs (p = 0.032) (Table  ). When looking solely at primary caregiver attendance, we also see that training attendance predicted adherence to all products combined (p < 0.001) and with eggs (p = 0.005). Because caregiver and household characteristics might be variables influencing the relationship between training attendance and adherence, we also ran ordinarily least squares regressions controlling for age and sex of the child, whether the child was the biological child of the caregiver, education, marital status, food insecurity (according to the Household Food Insecurity Access Scale), household size, and wealth quintile, as these variables emerged as variables influencing the relationship in the bivariate analyses (See Table  ). Even when controlling for these variables, we find overall training attendance was a highly statistically significant positive predictor of chlorine use (p = 0.005) and egg consumption (p = 0.005). However, greater overall training attendance was negatively correlated with MNP use (p = 0.029). This may be due to the fact that nutrition trainers sought out participants who had not attended training to deliver their MNPs, so training attendance was not as highly related to MNP delivery.
 
3.3.

## Qualitative results
3.3.1.

## Factors influencing training attendance
As noted above, monthly training sessions were well attended by study participants' caregivers. Caregivers reported that their attendance was motivated by a number of factors including a noticeable improvement in children's health, the educative and interactive nature of training, the fact that the training sessions provided a good platform to freely and comfortably discuss and share ideas, and intervention products received during the sessions.
The majority of the caregivers mentioned that their training attendance was motivated equally by the fact that it led to improved care practices and a positive impact on the health of their children and also that they would receive intervention products during the training sessions. They said that combining training and product delivery sessions saved time and ensured they went home with products that were important for their children's growth.
Caregivers also highlighted that their meeting attendance was motivated by the trainer's interesting, easy‐to‐understand, and participatory approaches. This included stories with pictorials that reflected their real‐life scenarios, demonstrations and practical sessions like creating a kitchen garden, interactive activities that encouraged all to participate, and role‐playing that impacted practical skills (see Table  ).
 The main reason given by the majority of caregivers who failed to attend training sessions was that their spouses were not supportive as they perceive these meetings as wasted time. A few caregivers also reported that they were busy, did not receive mobilization texts or calls due to poor network coverage or broken phones, and had challenges coming due to the long distance from their homes to the meeting venue.

3.3.2.

## Factors influencing intervention product use
3.3.2.1.

## Factors influencing the consumption of MNP
One strong motivation for good MNP use was the positive change some participants saw in their children after feeding them food sprinkled with MNP. The majority of the caregivers reported that consistent MNP use appeared to result in better growth and improved health status of their children, while others reported that MNP improved appetite, increased activity levels and that children fell sick less. These early and obvious changes helped to encourage some continued consumption. Both the nutrition trainers and caregivers also said that the study calendars were useful in reminding caregivers when to give MNP to the child.
The feedback on barriers to MNP consumption conducted by the nutrition trainers allows for a deeper analysis of the dynamics around MNP consumption. The majority of caregivers reported that limited knowledge or failure by family members left to care for the children while the caregiver had travelled, was away for work, or was sick, which was the biggest barrier to optimal MNP use. This was followed by a bigger proportion of caregivers who reported being busy or forgetting to give MNP to children and a small proportion who reported travelling with the child away from home while leaving the MNP pack behind. A few caregivers also reported that they initially had challenges following the administration routine until the customized study calendar was distributed; stopped when the child was unwell; and that occasionally some children did not like the taste of MNP, mainly due to incorrect preparation and mixing preparation, but the demonstrations helped manage this.

3.3.2.2.

## Factors influencing the consumption of eggs
All caregivers reported that the chicken they received ensured that they had eggs to feed to their children, without relying on purchasing, which could be challenging. They all agreed that they were motivated to keep the chickens rather than sell them because they were a source of good quality food (eggs and meat), long‐term investment when production increases, and a good source of farm manure. The majority of caregivers also reported that eggs appeared to help improve their children's health.
However, the majority of caregivers reported that the high mortality of chicken resulted in minimal egg production, hence they could not give their children an egg every day as recommended. Slightly fewer caregivers reported that they lacked the money to purchase eggs to supplement what they got from their chicken, and also, they were unsure that the eggs in the shops would be of good quality to be given to the children.

3.3.2.3.

## Factors influencing chlorine use
The majority of caregivers (>80%) said that chlorine was the most preferred technique for purifying water because it was used for a long and known to be effective and was easier to use when compared with the common method, boiling, which takes much longer, requires firewood, which is not always available, and that smoke alters the taste of water. Slightly fewer caregivers also reported that chlorine, if used correctly, does not affect the taste of water as compared with boiling and they were accustomed to chlorine use because it is readily available in Western Kenya and relatively inexpensive—some would obtain it for free from the government at water points. The majority of caregivers said that they would buy chlorine to treat their water when the study concludes, an indication that caregivers like and understand the importance of chlorine in water treatment. A few caregivers reported that some household members, mostly their partners, disliked the taste of water treated with chlorine and thus stopped using it.

4.

## DISCUSSION
The high rates of stunting in rural agricultural areas of the global South have prompted great interest in providing nutrition‐sensitive agriculture, which generally includes a broad range of products and services. However, expansive programmes, such as these, can suffer from poor adherence because they are encouraging multiple behaviour changes simultaneously. The results from this study are instructive for how to sustain adherence in the ambitious multipronged programmes necessary for combating malnutrition.
Both the quantitative and qualitative evidence support the notion that combining product distribution and training can increase both training attendance as well as product adherence. The free products serve as an incentive to attend training and the interactive trainings provide peer pressure/acceptance in encouraging adherence. Participants in this study were able to discuss challenges and encourage others in the use of various products. We did find a decline in caregiver attendance of training over time, as caregivers were more likely to send a representative in their stead, which indicates the importance of potentially adding in additional incentives, encouragements or combining with household visits.
The quantitative analysis strongly supports a “grandmother effect” whereby adherence across multiple products was better for caregivers who were not the biological mothers, most often the child's grandmother. While most nutrition interventions target women of reproductive age, this ignores the large percent of children who are being reared by aunts and grandmothers who may be even more, rather than less, likely to adopt novel practices. Somewhat related, our qualitative research demonstrated that a primary barrier to attending trainings was the lack of support among key members of the family such as spouses and mothers‐in‐law (when not primary caregivers to the child). Altogether, this highlights the importance of targeting not only the primary caregiver but a broad extended family network in multipronged nutrition programming. Older caregivers and grandmothers who appear to be more engaged in the intervention can be used as champions/mentors to help accelerate acceptance among mothers. The importance of gaining buy‐in from extended family members, spouses, and mothers‐in‐law, in particular, is a well‐known best practice in nutrition promotion interventions (Kavle & Landry,  ).
Our qualitative work highlighted the importance of reminders, which were not seen as a nuisance but instead a welcome prompt. The importance of reminders to nudge such behaviour change in nutrition interventions agrees with other's work in this area (D'agostino et al.,  ; Mirkovic et al.,  ; Tumilowicz et al.,  ). Finally, feedback from participants highlights the importance of narrative and dialogical work in promoting behaviour change, with enthusiastic support for the storybooks, visuals, relatable narratives and opportunity for discussions with similarly situated peers, a principle for which there is much theoretical and empirical support (Hinyard & Kreuter,  ; Slater et al.,  ).
Multipronged programmes such as this one have a high potential for combating malnutrition, but they also have a greater challenge in promoting strong adherence across multiple products and services. The evidence presented here suggests the importance of interactive, dialogic group training, which combines behaviour change with product distribution, active inclusion of older primary caregivers who can act as champions and mentors, concerted outreach to family members, and regular prompts and reminders.
