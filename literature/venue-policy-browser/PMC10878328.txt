The arctic rabies variant, which is present throughout the circumpolar North, is maintained by arctic foxes and occasionally red foxes, where the ranges of these two fox species overlap [ ]. Public health measures in the North, like in many other regions, focus on the vaccination of dogs and post-exposure prophylaxis of potentially exposed humans. Wildlife vaccination is not practiced at scale in the North. Apart from logistical difficulties such as limited physical and administrative infrastructure to support surveillance and vaccination efforts in the sparsely populated North, the various aspects of rabies management fall under multiple administrative jurisdictions, as rabies poses a threat to human health but is largely managed through prevention efforts in animals that maintain the virus. In a systems theory framework, the overall administrative system to manage rabies prevention consists of subsystems (various agencies charged with improving animal or human public health services at different (national, regional, and local) administrative scales) that interact dynamically to manage rabies. They interact in the context of an environment characterised by social (political and economic) and natural (geography, animal population dynamics, and disease spread) processes. Understanding how these agencies interact within the overall rabies management system is vital to assessing rabies management and the larger question of how the management of diseases at the interface of human and animal health can be optimised in the North.
The One Health concept acknowledges the linkages between human, animal, and environmental health. Rabies offers a model to examine the implementation of this principle [ , ]. One Health is more holistic than traditional and reductionist Western medicine that focuses largely on the physiological disease of individual humans. The One Health paradigm recognises the interrelationships among people, animals, and the environment and the impact of their interaction on health outcomes. Interactions among various agencies and organisations responsible for the three parts (or even just two parts) of this paradigm have been identified as a major hurdle to implementing One Health approaches [ ]. Analysing rabies management as a system is therefore not only important to improving the management of a specific disease; lessons learned can be applied to other threats to well-being that fall within the One Health triad or parts of that triad and can build capacity to tackle complex problems [ ].
In many siloed approaches to public health, human and animal health are separated by disciplinary and bureaucratic boundaries. My main research question is: How do various regions in the Arctic manage rabies, given that this disease affects both animals and humans? To address this overarching research question, I developed the following sub-questions: What governmental scale (national, regional, local) has the primary regulatory authority to manage rabies? What governmental sector (human or animal health) has the primary regulatory authority to manage rabies? How do agencies (subsystems) interact between different scales of government? How do agencies (subsystems) interact between different sectors of government? What are the challenges to rabies management in the northern environment? How does rabies management compare across the study regions?
I analyse rabies management as an example of a simplified One Health approach through a systems theory lens. More specifically, I examine challenges in a limited application of the paradigm that focuses on the interaction of animal and human health agencies but also considers environmental impediments to rabies management. While One Health has many definitions [ ], I use the definition from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) that the One Health concept is “is a collaborative, multisectoral, and transdisciplinary approach – working at the local, regional, national, and global levels – with the goal of achieving optimal health outcomes recognizing the interconnection between people, animals, plants, and their shared environment” [ ]. This definition requires cooperation among horizontally organised subsystems (disciplines or agencies) and differentiated organisational scales (from local to national to global administrative systems). I use a comparative approach in examining three cases of rabies management in the circumpolar North, a region with significant health disparities and for which the Arctic Council, an international policy body, has identified One Health as an effective approach to improving human health [ ].

## Methods

## Ethics statement
I submitted the study to the UAF Office of Research Integrity, which determined it to be exempt from further Institutional Review Board (IRB) review.

## Case selection
To provide meaningful comparisons among cases, I developed the following criteria to select cases for my thesis research: 1. The cases must lie in the North. 2. Regions must be in a country with a Western democratic style of government, as examining fundamentally different approaches to government would introduce too many variables to make meaningful comparisons. 3. Regions must have reported recent cases of rabies in animals to explore how these cases are managed. 4. The cases must be in different countries to assess differences at the national scale.
Western-style democratic governments in the North are found in Fenno-Scandinavia and North America. Within North America, only two countries lie in the North (defined as above 60 degrees northern latitude): the United States and Canada. Within the United States, Alaska is the only state in the North, while Canada has several political units in the North. The Northwest Territories (NWT) is the closest territory to Alaska that has reported animals infected with rabies to federal authorities (Fehlner-Gardiner personal communication, 2018). In North America, I therefore focused on Alaska and the NWT.
In Scandinavia, only Norway has sporadic rabies outbreaks within its territory, on the Svalbard archipelago [ ]. Therefore, I compared the two North American regions (Alaska and NWT) to Svalbard.

## Semi-structured interviews
I conducted 14 semi-structured interviews with 15 key informants. I interviewed two key informants for one national agency in Norway at the same time for scheduling reasons. I analysed this interview separately for the two respondents. I identified public officials involved in rabies management as key informants using purposive sampling and snowball sampling. As a starting point, I used my professional network of rabies managers and researchers. I selected respondents who work on rabies management in human and animal health agencies at the regional and national levels. At the end of the interview, I asked for additional informants to interview. The interview protocol is provided in Supplemental Materials.

## Data analysis
I recorded and transcribed interviews verbatim using the online transcription service Temi, followed by manual editing of the entire transcript while listening to interview recordings. For most questions seeking straightforward information, I tabulated answers for each interviewee and added responses for each case. Using word frequency analysis and interview coding, I analysed the more open-ended questions about challenges in rabies management for themes.

## Word frequency analysis
I determined the frequency of words used in responses to the question on challenges of rabies management (Question 9) by interviewees by creating a word frequency list using online resources (design215.com). I adopted my analysis from Ryan and Weisner (G [ ], who use word frequency analysis to describe how parents describe their children: I reduced word lists by removing words not related explicitly to rabies management. Next, I combined words with similar meanings. I retained those terms used more than once during an interview and merged the word lists within each case. To compare relevant word usage between cases and highlight differences between cases, I kept the 30 most frequently used words and removed words present on all three lists. I visualised word frequencies with word clouds using online resources (worditout.com).

## Interview coding
Using transcripts for question 9 of the interview (“What are the greatest challenges to managing rabies in your region?”), I identified line-by-line content themes and assembled an initial codebook. I developed the codes from the content themes of the interviewee responses in an inductive manner rather than based on pre-existing theory [ , ]. Initially, I identified 14 codes.
Using these 14 codes, I analysed all entire interviews. Coding of all complete interviews yielded 15 total codes for the final codebook (see supplemental material for final codebook). I collected verbatim quotations during the coding process, increasing documentation of my methodology [ ].

## Results
Of the 15 key informants, 6 work in Norway, 4 in Canada, and 5 in the United States. Eight interviewees are affiliated with governmental agencies at the regional scale, and seven work at national governmental agencies. These agencies include the Norwegian Veterinary Institute, Norwegian Public Health Institute, Governor’s Office Svalbard, and Norwegian Food Safety Authority; the Government of the Northwest Territories, Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA), and Health Canada; the Alaska Department of Environmental Conservation (DEC), Alaska Department of Health and Human Services (DHHS), Alaska Department of Fish and Game (ADFG), United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) Wildlife Services, and United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Nine participants have veterinary training, three have medical training, and three have primarily biology training. Three veterinarians also have biology training, and six have public health or epidemiology training. Three respondents list other additional training. More information on respondents is provided in the supplemental material.
All respondents clearly expressed the primary mission of their agency as either animal or human-health-focused. However, respondents from animal health agencies in all three cases also identified the prevention of human rabies cases as a major, if not the major, goal of their rabies management activities. In contrast, respondents working for human health agencies did not consider improving animal health as part of their mission. All physicians interviewed for this study worked for human health agencies, while four veterinarians worked for human health agencies.
When asked what scale of government has primary rule-making authority in rabies management, six respondents listed multiple scales of government, suggesting shared authority. shows which scale of government (national, regional, or local) has rule-making authority in rabies management.
 
A typical comment from a territorial official exemplifies some of the complexities of rabies management in the Northwest Territories (NWT): “ … we work with the federal government, we have our own regulations in terms of reporting, reporting incidents, provide the guidelines, standards manual, and then local governments would regulate around those like dog control” (respondent 10). The comment by a U.S. federal official, “In the United States, the authority primarily lies with the state or local governments” (respondent 11), clearly exemplifies the importance of state and local level regulation in rabies management in the United States. The comment of one respondent points to the importance of the national government and the particular situation of Svalbard within Norway due to the Svalbard Treaty of 1920 [ ]: “It’s national regulation, of course…but we also have to take care about the … border, because Svalbard is not a member of the European economic community” (respondent 5).
When asked which agency (animal or human health) has principal rule-making authority over rabies management, 10 respondents listed both agencies as responsible ( ).
 
One respondent from Canada said: “I feel like we’re going to have to be both, that is, making it difficult” (respondent 9). Another interviewee identified animal health agencies as mainly responsible for rabies management, specifically at the federal level: ” … at the federal level, anything to do with the legislation concerning reportability and that’s all within CFIA [Canadian Food Inspection Agency] under Health Animals Act” (respondent 8). All respondents from the United States identified both animal and human health agencies as responsible depending on the species involved in a given policy question: “It will depend on the species we’re working with” (respondent 12), said one respondent. The comment from a respondent affiliated with a Norwegian human health agency, “If there was a disagreement, we would take it with our health department, and the veterinary side will take it with the Ministry of Agriculture” (respondent 2), exemplifies the shared responsibility. In contrast, the comment, “Well, I guess, it’s the food and agriculture department” (respondent 5), illustrates the opinion that the animal health agency is mainly responsible for rabies management but also expresses some uncertainty.
When asked about working relationships with agencies at different government scales, most respondents pointed out both positive and negative aspects of these working relationships. Overall, the impressions of working relationships across governmental scales of rabies management are more positive in Alaska and Svalbard, while they appear to be predominantly negative in the NWT ( ).
 
The comment from a Canadian respondent, ” … it used to be a lot more collaborative … .over the past years [the federal agencies] have cut back a lot of their support to the province and territories” (respondent 10), points to the recently reduced CFIA involvement in regional rabies management efforts [ ]. The comment, “There could be a little bit better coordination there with the local level, but, um, you know, we’re, we’re trying” (respondent 15) exemplifies a challenge identified by a U.S. respondent, while the comment, “I think we have some of the most continuous and frequent interactions with the state public health vets for probably any disease … ” (respondent 11), emphasises that especially in rabies management in the United States the cooperation works well across scales. The comment, “So every time we have a case at the lower level, we cannot seek help from upper level” (respondent 5), illustrates perceived resistance to collaboration from the higher level of authority expressed by a Norwegian respondent. In all cases, some respondents who mention both positive and negative aspects of the working relationship talk about differences in relationships between different agencies. Some relationships between agencies are good, while relationships with others are more challenging. Personal relationships are frequently identified as reasons for the difference in the relationship between agencies as well as the sudden reduction in support from CFIA to the territorial government mentioned above, in the case of the NWT.
Additional differences emerged among the three cases regarding the perceived relationship between animal and human health agencies. Respondents from the United States and Norway characterised the relationships as positive, while respondents from NWT expressed more mixed opinions ( ).
 
Comments such as “I’m not sure how productive that’s going” (respondent 8) and ” … it works like shockingly better than most other situations. I think they work really well together” (respondent 7) exemplify the contrasting sentiments in the NWT. The comment from a U.S. respondent, “rabies has always been a One Health project before it was … cool, … we are able to get together and collaborate and talk about things” (respondent 12), clearly reflects such positivity. The comment from a Norwegian respondent, “we have exercises regularly … . what you do in peacetime works when it’s a war hour” (respondent 2), emphasises the positive aspects and suggests that frequent interactions enhance cooperation, while comments such as “We have to be honest. Uh, there are some problems … when it comes to the zoonoses I find there is some friction … ”. (respondent 4) expresses some concerns about the working relationship between human and animal health agencies.
I use the frequency of word usage to discern respondents’ emphasis on various challenges. ( ).
 
While respondents from Alaska and the NWT use the word “dog” frequently, respondents from Norway did not use this term. On the other hand, the words “hunt”, “reindeer”, “outbreak”, “endemic” and “introduced” are used frequently only by Norwegian officials. However, “endemic” is used exclusively in a negative context (not endemic). These words frequently used by Norwegian respondents point to rabies in reindeer as a unique challenge (“reindeer” and “hunt”) as well as the sporadic nature of rabies on Svalbard after the introduction by rabies-infected foxes crossing the sea ice to reach Svalbard (“outbreak”, “introduced” and not “endemic”). Respondents from the NWT use the word “sample” more frequently than those associated with Alaska and Svalbard. Samples from the NWT must be transported to Alberta for testing in a federal diagnostic laboratory supported by the Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA). Furthermore, respondents from the NWT use the word “lack” more frequently than those from Norway or the United States in referring to the perceived lack of resources. The frequent usage of the word “bylaw” by NWT respondents points to the importance and challenges of local regulation, suggesting difficulties in the efficient interactions between subsystems. Similarly, Norwegian respondents frequently used the word “Sysselmann”, referring to the unique office of the governor of Svalbard, indicating that the unusual administrative structure is related to challenges in rabies management. The word “vaccine” is used most frequently in Alaska when discussing challenges to achieving high vaccination rates in dogs in remote communities.
Respondents from all three cases used the words “rabies”, “people”, “risk”, “animals”, “challenge”, “remote”, and “management” (data not shown). The words “people” and “animals” relate to the limited One Health paradigm used in this research, as rabies spans human and animal health. The word “risk” describes uncertainty in risk assessment and difficulty assigning risk to any given situation. The word “remote” relates to characteristics of the northern environment common to all three regions, showing how the environment influences the rabies management system.
A coding approach showed that cooperation between different agencies and scales of government is a significant challenge in all cases. It is followed by remote small communities and scale and environmental conditions, which represent environmental conditions influencing the rabies management system ( ).
 
While respondents from the NWT most frequently mention issues related to the theme of cooperation, followed by remote small communities and a perceived low priority of rabies management for decision-makers, Alaska respondents most frequently say imports of rabid animals, followed by cooperation, wildlife reservoirs, and limited resources. Finally, respondents from Norway most frequently cite issues related to cooperation, followed with equal frequency by the Remote and small communities, the Scale and Environmental conditions, and the sporadic outbreaks on the archipelago. When analysed by the number of respondents who touch on a given theme, a similar trend emerges, although some differences arise ( ).
 
The high incidence of low priority reflects repeated mentions by one NWT respondent rather than multiple respondents. Detailed results and quotes of the coding approach are provided in supplemental materials.

## Discussion
To address the question of how various regions in the Arctic manage rabies, I conducted and analysed semi-structured interviews with rabies managers using a case study approach. I examined where the perceived regulatory authority is located in the rabies management system, how subsystems (agencies) interact, and what the challenges in rabies management are in the three cases (Alaska, NWT, and Svalbard).
Apparent differences emerge between, and more surprisingly even within, the individual cases regarding the perceived governmental scale of rule-making authority in rabies management. While all respondents from the United States assigned rule-making authority to the state level with some additional regulation at the local level, respondents from the NWT and especially Svalbard had more mixed understandings of the scale of government with the primary rule-making authority and responsibility. This uncertainty among rabies managers in the NWT can be partially explained by a transfer of some responsibility within the last decade [ ]. In Svalbard the special administrative structure of the archipelago is likely responsible for this uncertainty.
The greater or equal focus on animal health agencies in all three cases as the primary rule-making authority, together with the goal to prevent human rabies cases, even in animal health agencies, emphasises the importance of the One Health paradigm. The importance of veterinary agencies aligns with the higher number of veterinarians among the respondents. In contrast, the role of human health agencies is mainly limited to direct responses to potentially exposed humans. These findings also suggest that the concept of One Health, which links the importance of human and animal health, is not as well established in human public health agencies, given that animal health is not a priority for these agencies if it does not directly improve human health outcomes.
Frequent interactions and training are necessary to maintain well-functioning inter-agency relationships. Apart from the personal relationships, several respondents noted the unique nature of rabies as a disease that requires a collaborative One Health approach, which aligns with the literature [ , , ].
Assessing the individual challenges shows clear distinctions among the three cases of this study. Dogs, limited resources, and low priority of rabies management are significant challenges identified only by Canadian and American rabies experts, likely because of the different socio-economic situations in Svalbard compared to rural Alaska and the NWT. Svalbard is an archipelago with no Indigenous communities and is populated by individuals of relatively high socio-economic status. People move to Svalbard to work and often return when employment ends [ , ]. The communities in the far North of Canada and the U.S. are often characterised by limited socio-economic opportunities, limited cash economies, and limited infrastructure [ ]. While most inhabitants of Svalbard live in only two communities with significant infrastructure, the more decentralised small communities in Alaska and NWT pose challenges in delivering social services, including veterinary care [ ].
The perceived low priority given to rabies management by policymakers poses a challenge. With many pressing public health and safety challenges in remote areas where rabies occurs, such as high rates of domestic violence, accidents, and alcohol abuse, potential cases of rabies in animals are not always a high priority for on-the-ground public safety personnel. In addition, the success of rabies management in preventing human rabies cases limits the visibility of this zoonotic disease to public health officials and policymakers.
In addition to these challenges, two Canadian respondents also mentioned cultural differences as a challenge in successful rabies management. Relationships with dogs, animal welfare, concepts surrounding pet ownership, and value systems were cited as specific challenges. While similar cultural challenges do not exist in Svalbard, they also affect rabies management in Alaska, where many dogs are kept outside by mushers, and even pets and guard dogs are often housed outdoors. Why U.S. respondents did not identify this challenge in my interviews is unclear.
Respondents from Svalbard also identified hunting as a risk factor and challenge in rabies management. This is likely owed to the frequent involvement of reindeer in rabies outbreaks on the archipelago [ ]. It is unclear why the rabies virus frequently infects reindeer on Svalbard.
Another unique challenge to Svalbard is the sporadic nature of rabies outbreaks on the archipelago. While rabies is enzootic in most of the circumpolar North, only sporadic outbreaks are reported on Svalbard. Likely, arctic foxes incubating the virus travel over the sea ice from endemic regions to Svalbard [ , ]. While the epizootic nature of rabies ecology on Svalbard reduces the overall presence of the virus to intermittent outbreaks, it creates uncertainty about the current disease status and necessary control measures. This difference clearly shows how environmental conditions that drive disease ecology can influence the rabies management system in the far North. These last two challenges specific to rabies management in Svalbard highlight how the ecological environment can affect the rabies management system.
Finally, respondents from Norway frequently discussed Svalbard’s unique administrative structure resulting from the Svalbard Treaty [ ]. This treaty led to Svalbard’s unique organisational structure and policies [ ]. The Norwegian government’s rule-making authority in many economic matters is limited on Svalbard. In addition, the archipelago is, in contrast to the rest of Norway, not part of the European Economic Area. This creates a de facto customs border between Svalbard and mainland Norway. This administrative structure can complicate the shipping of diagnostic samples, delay the diagnosis of rabies cases on Svalbard, and likely contributes to the uncertainty about which scale of government has rule-making authority.
Other obstacles, such as cooperation, remote small communities, scale and environmental conditions, and wildlife reservoirs, pertain to all three regions. In a system theory framework, cooperation can be understood as a critical interaction between subsystems and feedback loops [ , ]. Interestingly, respondents identified cooperation as a challenge even when they considered cooperation between agencies to be good. This discrepancy between characterising working relationships as good and identifying cooperation as a challenge could indicate the need to maintain good working relationships and the effort necessary to support these positive working relationships. Similarly, in Switzerland, most respondents perceived cooperation between veterinary and human health agencies as positive. However, different professional cultures and administrative structures that do not support collaborations were the two most often identified barriers to implementing One Health in Switzerland [ ].
Another challenge to rabies management identified by respondents from all three cases not specific to the North relates to the wildlife reservoir. While globally rabies is maintained and transmitted to humans, most often by dogs, in developed countries, wildlife rabies became the primary source of human cases after eliminating canine rabies through widespread dog vaccinations [ , ]). It is therefore not surprising that respondents from all three cases identified wildlife reservoirs as a challenge linked to the environment in which the rabies management system functions, as arctic foxes maintain the rabies virus throughout the circumpolar North [ , ].
Challenges common among the three cases and more prevalent within the circumpolar North are remote small communities and scale and environmental conditions representing unique social and natural aspects of the northern environment that influence the rabies management system. The North is characterised by harsh weather, remotely located small communities, and often limited infrastructure and economic opportunities. Despite the social differences between Svalbard and Alaska and NWT discussed above, the remote locations of the settlements, which are accessible by airplane or boats only, coupled with the large expanses of the landscape, pose challenges to rabies management in all three cases. Outbreak responses and surveillance are logistically challenging due to long travel distances and the harsh environment of the far North.
The findings that the two North American (and federal) cases were similar to each other in some challenges (limited resources and dogs) but different in others (perceived quality of interactions) is surprising as I postulated that Norway as a unified country would be the divergent case, especially in the interaction between agencies.
The finding that the unique environment of the far North creates common challenges between the three cases suggests that policymakers working in the circumpolar North can learn from each other and that solutions from elsewhere might be challenging to implement in the far North without considering its unique environmental conditions. Fora such as the Arctic Council working groups can provide a venue for these collaborations of public health officials who face similar harsh environments in the circumpolar North.
Public health managers working across health sectors and governmental scales can utilise these findings and focus on inter-agency cooperation in the context of the social and physical environment to improve the functioning of disease management, including but not limited to rabies. The specific environmental context and personal relationships should be at the centre of programmes when developing disease management systems under a One Health paradigm.

## Positionality and limitations of this study
As a researcher who has worked on rabies ecology, pathogenesis, and host (fox) biology, I bring preconceptions to this study. My professional network has been the starting point for identifying key informants for my interviews. While this perspective as a rabies researcher allows me to put comments into context, it also colours my interpretation of respondents’ remarks.
A possible limitation pertains to the validity and reliability of the qualitative analysis [ ]. My reliance on a single coder (myself) for data analysis introduces inherent risks to the findings’ validity and reliability. The absence of inter-coder agreement checks raises possible concerns about the internal validity of the findings. Furthermore, a single coder responsible for all data interpretation brings into question external validity. It becomes challenging to generalise the findings beyond the specific context or population under examination, as the results may reflect interpretations unique to the coder rather than broader, objective truth if such truth even exists.
Additionally, all interviews were conducted in English. However, English is not the native language of several respondents, especially those from Norway. This limitation could have influenced responses and especially influenced word frequency analysis.
