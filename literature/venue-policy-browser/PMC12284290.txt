## Abstract
In this Backstory, the collaborative team behind a recent One Earth study discusses how their work examines how climate policies across the U.S.—from federal action to state-level initiatives—can improve air quality and public health. Using an interdisciplinary modeling approach, they identified where and why these health benefits occur, offering insights that help guide more effective, equitable, and locally relevant climate solutions.

## Main text

## Proximity

## Who were the players in this project, and how did you bring everyone together?
Our team’s journey began as a collaborative effort between the Center for Global Sustainability at the University of Maryland and the group led by Dr. Wei Peng from Princeton University. This collaboration first produced a policy-focused report launched at COP28 in December 2023 that quantified the potential health co-benefits of an “All In” climate strategy with enhanced climate actions from federal and non-federal actors across the United States (see more in Peng et al. 2023 ). This analysis provided a high-level, policy-relevant assessment: the combined impact of federal climate policies, like the Inflation Reduction Act, and widespread subnational climate initiatives, such as renewable portfolio standards and electric vehicle sales mandates, could deliver on U.S. climate targets while preventing thousands of premature deaths through improvements in air quality. The first publication of our joint work opened up opportunities to further examine underlying scientific questions that are policy relevant, for instance, how demographic shifts, air quality control policies, or future healthcare conditions might interact to shape these outcomes—or how those effects might vary across space and over time.

## How did this collaborative work lead to your publication at Cell Press?
These questions served as the foundation for our One Earth paper. We expanded our initial analysis to explore the complex and interacting drivers of future health impacts under different decarbonization policies and socioeconomic scenarios. Rather than focusing solely on the combined outcomes, we asked: what explains where and why health benefits occur? And how can coordinated efforts across different policy domains and actors enhance those benefits? The academic paper offered a rigorous modeling design to unpack these dynamics systematically, through decomposition analyses, sector-specific results, and a range of sensitivity analyses on policy and socioeconomic drivers. Shifting the aim of our research from a broad policy message to a deeper scientific inquiry allowed us to inform both climate-health scholarship and policy decision-making with greater rigor and insight.

## Research methods

## Did this project require tailoring your research methods to adjust to working interdisciplinarity?
Bringing this project to life required close collaboration across researchers trained in energy systems modeling, atmospheric sciences, public health, and environmental policy. Drawing from their expertise in modeling, Alicia Zhao and Gokul Iyer led the development of future energy and emissions scenarios using an integrated assessment model that covers the energy, land, water, climate and socioeconomic systems, with detailed representation of state-level climate policies. Xinyuan Huang and Wei Peng worked on the pollution and health impact assessments involving emissions downscaling, air quality modeling, and exposure and mortality assessments. Other co-authors—including Yang Ou, Shannon Kennedy, Haewon McJeon, Ryna Cui, and Nate Hultman—provided essential insights into the scenario design and policy framing, and contributed to advancing the modeling tools. One of the most difficult—and also most rewarding—parts of the project was making it more relevant to subnational decision-makers; for instance, we needed to understand what the key determinants for health co-benefits in each state are and how to attribute the health benefits to federal versus subnational policy actions.
Improving the modeling methods in a way that maintained consistency across spatial scales was technically challenging. Our team spent considerable time discussing how to disaggregate the national results into meaningful state-level findings, and we developed a method that allowed us to assess in-state health co-benefits from in-state policy actions. The findings were compelling: we showed that climate action from a single state can produce measurable public health improvements within that state, but the magnitude and policy drivers of those benefits varied. For example, a coal retirement policy would be particularly effective in Texas—one of the country’s largest coal-consuming states—while policies that mandate or incentivize electric vehicles (EVs) would bring greater benefits in California, which has a cleaner electricity mix to minimize the upstream emissions from EVs. These insights added granularity to the national story and made the results more actionable for state-level policymakers.
Methodologically, building the analysis required connecting tools and data from different fields in a way that was both conceptually clear and technically sound. Each step of the process, from projecting energy activities and greenhouse gas emissions, to modeling air pollutants and assessing health impacts, came with its own assumptions and spatial and temporal resolutions. In addition to the technical challenges associated with linking models, we also faced challenges in constructing a tractable analytical framework that preserved the conceptual integrity of each component while addressing the overarching research question. For example, while energy models focus on national and state trends, pollution and health risks are evaluated at much finer spatial scales. This discrepancy required us to translate broad system-level shifts into localized outcomes through careful coordination of methods across scales and sectors. Emerging from this process was not only a new technical model but a broader framework for understanding climate and health as interconnected systems.
 

## Publication

## When publishing this or any interdisciplinary paper, how do you decide which community/venue to target? What are the challenges during publication of this or any such interdisciplinary research?
The interdisciplinary nature of the work also shaped our approach to communicating the findings. We chose One Earth because of its focus on actionable science that bridges academic domains, which aligned well with our work. We were also deliberate in how we framed the study to ensure that it would be both scientifically credible and relevant to real-world decision-making.
To establish credibility, we explained how our analysis could contribute to the knowledge gaps on system dynamics and uncertainties, and grounded each component of the analysis in established methods. Assumptions at each stage were clearly stated to ensure transparency and reproducibility. Furthermore, we highlighted the policy relevance of our study; by building the scenarios around plausible policy pathways already under discussion or implementation at federal and subnational levels, rather than focusing on stylized or idealized outcomes, we made our findings more applicable for policymakers.
Sensitivity analyses added another layer of scientific credibility and policy relevance, showing how outcomes might vary depending on local policy choices or technological transitions. In this sense, the interdisciplinary process influenced not only how the research was conducted, but also how the narrative was constructed. Learning to write for both scientific and policy audiences required us to ask relevant questions, identify the stories behind our results, and convey them in a clear and effective manner. This was a skill we developed iteratively—through internal team discussions, feedback from collaborators across fields, and engagement with policymakers. It meant constantly asking ourselves not just what we found but why and to whom it matters to shape a narrative that could connect across different audiences.

## Final thoughts

## What did you learn about interdisciplinary research from the project and what tips would you give to anyone considering undertaking such work?
Reflecting on this project, we developed a deeper appreciation for both the complexity and the value of interdisciplinary research. We learned that while climate actions can yield health co-benefits through reduction in air pollutants, the distribution of these benefits can look very different across regions depending on local energy systems, population vulnerability, and policy design. It was only through combining tools across fields that allowed us to capture these dynamics and offer more targeted guidance for equitable policymaking. Bridging across fields is urgently needed to tackle complex societal problems; it can give rise to new, synergistic questions, spur innovative methods, and uncover policy impacts and interactions that may be overlooked in more narrowly focused work. One of the biggest lessons from this collaboration was the importance of building a shared understanding early in the process. This includes not only aligning technical methods, but also investing in the relationships and communication needed to foster mutual trust and clarity. For researchers undertaking similar interdisciplinary efforts, beginning with a question that genuinely requires multiple perspectives, and allowing room for the collaboration to evolve over time, can be especially valuable. In our case, bringing together diverse forms of expertise was not simply a technical necessity, but a defining feature of what made the research impactful.
