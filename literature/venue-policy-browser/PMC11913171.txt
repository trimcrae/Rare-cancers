## Objective:
This report highlights state and local practices for optimizing the pediatric COVID-19 vaccination program for children ages 6 months through 11 years.

## Methods:
State and local practices designed to optimize pediatric COVID-19 vaccine uptake were identified from a range of sources, including immunization program, CDC, and partner staff; and media stories or program descriptions identified via online searches.

## Results:
A range of practices were identified across different categories: provider-focused practices, school-based practices, jurisdiction or health department-based activities, community-focused practices involving partners, use of vaccination incentives, and Medicaid-related practices.

## Conclusions:
Immunization programs and stakeholders implemented a variety of practices to meet the challenge of the pediatric COVID-19 vaccination program. The key findings may serve to inform not only the current pediatric COVID-19 vaccination program, but also future outbreak response work and routine immunization activities.

## Introduction
In the United States (US), there are approximately 73.5 million children under the age of 18 years. Everyone ages 6 months and older is recommended for COVID-19 vaccination.[ ] In December 2020, the first COVID-19 vaccine became available under a US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Emergency Use Authorization (EUA) and was subsequently recommended by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) for persons 16 years and older.[ , ] Additional authorizations and recommendations for younger age groups down to 6 months followed in 2021 and 2022 ( ).[ – ].
The US immunization infrastructure is built upon public–private partnerships, involving federal agencies, state and local health departments, tribal nations and organizations, medical providers, vaccine manufacturers, pharmacies, and other stakeholders;[ ] and these entities are long experienced in conducting activities to improve vaccination coverage, including but not limited to school-located vaccination, pharmacy vaccination, and mobile vaccination.[ – ] The existing network of CDC-funded immunization programs, representing 64 jurisdictions (50 US states, Washington DC, 5 cities, and 8 territories and affiliated nations), along with a wide range of their partners, leveraged their expertise and implemented a range of approaches in an effort to efficiently and effectively vaccinate large cohorts of children by sharing pediatric COVID-19 vaccine information, increasing vaccine confidence and demand, and ensuring access to vaccination. This manuscript will highlight several state and local immunization practices used during the COVID-19 response for optimizing the pediatric vaccination program.

2.

## Materials and methods
Practices designed to optimize pediatric COVID-19 vaccine uptake were identified via three CDC activities or CDC-funded activities undertaken throughout the COVID-19 response, as follows.
2.1.

## Strategies in action report
Beginning in November 2021, when COVID-19 vaccination for children ages 5–11 years was initially recommended, and through May 2022, CDC staff collected information on pediatric vaccination program implementation in various ways: directly from the CDC-funded jurisdictions or via reports from CDC project officers charged with overseeing and providing technical assistance to the jurisdictions, from listening sessions with CDC partners, and through monitoring media stories and reports. CDC conducted outreach to each jurisdiction or organization that implemented an identified vaccination strategy to obtain its review of the practice or program (CDC unpublished data, 2021–2022). 

2.2.

## Listening sessions
In December 2021, CDC began a project in collaboration with the Association of Immunization Managers (AIM) to understand the landscape of the national pediatric COVID-19 vaccination program for children ages 5 years and older and to prepare for an anticipated COVID-19 vaccination program for children ages 6 months through 4 years. AIM facilitated a series of four 1-hour, virtual listening sessions with immunization program managers and key staff during January and February 2022. Topics included challenges, successes, and lessons learned; ensuring an adequate pediatric provider network; building vaccine confidence; and preparing for vaccination of children younger than age 5 years. The sessions were recorded and transcribed, and de-identified findings were summarized in a series of reports and shared with immunization program staff in a virtual webinar format. A final meeting was held in March 2022 where AIM and CDC staff shared key findings and addressed key needs that had been communicated during the sessions (CDC unpublished data, 2022). 

2.3.

## Promising practices
In October 2022, CDC and AIM continued to collaborate to help build capacity among U.S. immunization programs for pediatric COVID-19 vaccination efforts. As part of this project, AIM collected information from several sources: CDC project officers overseeing and providing technical assistance to the jurisdictions, AIM staff and leadership, a structured literature review (including published and unpublished literature), and existing internal AIM activities and projects (including AIM’s Annual Survey of immunization program managers, COVID-19 roundtable discussions at AIM’s annual conference, and AIM’s general immunization program practices database). Practices were also collected during two regional Vaccine Access Cooperative meetings held from March through June 2023; these meetings were a collaboration between AIM, Academy Health, and National Academy of State Health Policy, and attendees included key staff from state immunization programs and from partners such as state Medicaid offices, the American Academy of Pediatrics, the American Academy of Family Physicians, immunization coalitions, and pharmacist organizations (CDC unpublished data, 2023). ,[ ].
Data collected from the above sources were reviewed by the authors of this report to identify practices used by jurisdictions and partners to increase uptake of pediatric COVID-19 vaccines. Outreach to immunization program staff and online searches using the Google search engine were conducted when additional details were needed. For practices identified from the AIM-facilitated listening sessions, outreach was also conducted to immunization program staff for approval to include a description of the practice in this manuscript. Practices were considered for inclusion if they included pediatric populations. The final list of practices was selected by study author consensus and prioritized based on demonstrated innovation (defined as a novel practice or a preexisting practice repurposed for COVID-19) and actual or perceived impact/success (e.g., number of doses administered, indications that the practice was well-received). Practices were then grouped by general themes.

3.

## Results
Several themes of notable practices were identified: provider-focused practices, school-based practices, jurisdiction or health department-based activities, community-focused practices involving partners, use of vaccination incentives, and Medicaid-related practices ( ).
3.1.

## Provider-focused practices
Beginning in September 2021, the Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment (CDPHE), with the assistance of Colorado Health Institute (CHI), offered an incentive program for provider/practice enrollment in the COVID-19 vaccination program and administration of COVID-19 vaccines. Primary care providers (PCPs) received grant funding dependent on practice size to support the distribution of COVID-19 vaccines: sites with 1–3 PCPs were eligible to receive up to $60,000; sites with 3–8 PCPs were eligible to receive up to $90,000; sites with ≥ 9 PCPs were eligible to receive up to $120,000. These enrolled sites received an initial $25,000 (if already enrolled in the COVID-19 vaccination program) or $30,000 (if not enrolled at time of program application) when they joined the incentive program plus an additional $50 payment for each vaccine dose administered up to the maximum amount determined by eligibility criteria. ,[ ] Immunization program staff offered the following insight on the program:
And then we finally got to a point where we financially incentivized primary care providers to actually enroll in the COVID program… There [was] a pretty significant uptake with this. I mean, a lot of providers just, they needed a little extra push. And if that ended up being actual financial incentives that got them over the line, it helped… 
The California Department of Public Health (CDPH) offered financial support for Vaccines for Children (VFC) providers. KidsVaxGrant 3.0, funded by CDPH and administered by Physicians for a Healthy California (PHC), offered a $10,000 grant to VFC providers who enrolled in the California COVID-19 vaccination program (myCAvax) through February 2023 or until all funding was disbursed. Providers were required to place one complete COVID-19 vaccine order within 30 days of their grant award. The VFC providers approved for KidsVaxGrant 3.0 were able to request a supplemental $5,000 system support grant to enhance or upgrade their Electronic Health Record (EHR) system to meet California’s AB 1797 reporting requirements for immunization and race/ethnicity information. ,[ , ] Twenty-nine organizations were funded, and these funds helped finance staffing/training, EHR-related support, administrative overhead, advertisement, community outreach, and extended hours of operation.[ ].
The Illinois Department of Public Health (IDPH) awarded a grant to the Illinois Chapter of the American Academy of Pediatrics (ICAAP) to work directly with a diverse group of health care providers, including hospitals, urgent care centers, and primary care and specialty providers, in order to support the integration of COVID-19 vaccination into routine clinical workflows and to increase access to pediatric COVID-19 vaccination services. ICAAP worked in partnership with the Illinois Academy of Family Physicians and ECHO-Chicago to implement this project, called Illinois Vaccinates Against COVID-19 (I-VAC). I-VAC delivered guidance and technical support to health care providers and assisted clinical practices as they planned and integrated COVID-19 vaccination into their clinical routines and workflow processes. This support included: continuing medical education (CME) sessions, implementation assistance with physician advisors offering guidance on vaccine administration, learning collaboratives, resources (including toolkits, posters, and FAQ documents), and a podcast addressing barriers to vaccination uptake. I-VAC reached hundreds of physicians and helped to identify and address system-level, organizational, and individual barriers to COVID-19 vaccination program implementation in Illinois. 
In several jurisdictions, pharmacies were a common location for COVID-19 vaccination for children rather than their medical home. This option also allowed for whole families to get vaccinated in a single setting, often in a convenient and familiar location with evening and weekend options. 
In Philadelphia, immunization program staff reported the following:
Well, when we look at where our…5- to 11-year-olds are being vaccinated…It’s in large part in retail pharmacy. So, it’s not at pediatric offices. We’re really talking about Rite Aids and CVSs of the world right now. We do have one very large pediatric hospital, Children’s Hospital in Philadelphia, that has done the other large bulk of that 5- to 11-year-old. But it has been the pharmacies that have done the most in the 5- to 11-year-olds. So yes, we have providers going [in] to schools…but that’s not where we’re seeing large numbers of people be vaccinated. We’re really seeing them go into the pharmacies to be vaccinated. And I think some of what’s driving that is because the pharmacies can just vaccinate the whole family. So, you bring in the five-year-old, you bring in the 12-year-old, you bring in the 17-year-old, you bring in the mom who needs her booster shot or the dad who needs their booster shot. It just makes it easy for them to all go to one place to be vaccinated. 
Immunization program staff in Colorado also reported that children ages 5 to 11 years were vaccinated more than expected at pharmacies:
…we did see a fair amount of the five to 11-year-old group going to pharmacies, which we didn’t really expect. We expected more of that [age] group to be seen in their medical homes or…the bus or the mobile units in Colorado that we’ve been using. We’ve been reaching a lot of these populations via that method, but we did see a fair amount of that age group go to the pharmacies… 

3.2.

## School-based practices
Chicago Public Schools (CPS) hosted a “Vaccination Awareness Day” on Friday, November 12, 2021, giving the district’s 330,000 students a day off to receive COVID-19 vaccination and city employees two hours of paid leave to get their children vaccinated. Vaccines were available to children at more than 20 sites designated for the day, including regional CPS Youth and Family Vaccination Clinics, CPS mobile clinics, school-based health centers, local hospitals and health systems, community health clinics, doctors’ offices, pharmacies, and additional community-based sites. More than 5,800 children ages 5–11 years received COVID-19 vaccination on this day. ,[ , ].
In Pennsylvania, the Abington School District (ASD) used COVID-19 federal funds to share COVID-19 vaccine information with community members, hold vaccination clinics, and provide transportation to and from clinics. To help administer vaccines at their school-located vaccination clinics, ASD partnered with a local pharmacy and Jefferson College of Nursing. ASD also opened its school-located vaccination clinics to the community and offered free transportation, food, movies, and costumed characters. The Director of ASD Student Services stated, “Our goal is to provide the facts about the vaccine, answer the questions, remove the barriers, and provide the opportunity.” As of February 1, 2022, about 40 % of elementary schoolers in the district between ages 5 through 11 years had been vaccinated. 
In Texas, the Austin Independent School District (ISD) held school-located vaccination clinics strategically located in communities highly impacted by COVID-19 and vaccinated children ages 5 years and older. The clinics were posted on ISD’s website and promoted on social media. Child life specialists, who are trained to help children and families cope with stress, were on site to help calm anxious children. ,[ ] Topical anesthetic spray was used to mitigate injection site pain for children. During one of these clinics, more than 250 children received COVID-19 vaccination. 
In Maine, the Department of Health and Human Services (DHHS) partnered with the Department of Education (DOE) to host a contest for children ages 5–17 years to create 30-second videos to encourage other children to get COVID-19 vaccination.[ ] Three winning videos were promoted on social media, with nearly 3k views on YouTube,[ , ] and schools attended by winning teams received cash prizes of up to $50,000. , Maine DHHS’s federally-funded Coronavirus Relief Fund supported the prizes, which could be used by schools for funding items/activities including healthy treats, equipment, and field trips.[ ] Members of the winning team offered this insight: “By spreading awareness and empowering fellow young people with facts about vaccination, helping younger Mainers to not be afraid, and using skills we learned in video production, we hoped to contribute to positive public health messaging.”[ ].

3.3.

## Health department-based practices
The Tennessee Department of Health (TDH) utilized a third-party company to redistribute COVID-19 vaccine doses, which were only available at that time in a large minimum order size and multi-dose vials. This company ordered vaccine doses directly from CDC, and providers placed orders through the jurisdiction’s immunization information system to get smaller quantities of doses from the organization. The TDH also created a transport team to move vaccine doses between COVID-19 providers in order to prevent wastage and expiration of doses as well as to move smaller quantities of vaccine doses around the state. 
The Chicago Department of Public Health funded and implemented an on-demand, in-home vaccination program for Chicago residents that began in 2021 to help homebound seniors and those with disabilities receive COVID-19 vaccination.[ ] The program expanded to include all residents ages 6 months and older eligible for receipt of a COVID-19 vaccine, with vaccination for up to 10 people at a time in a household, including family and friends.[ ] The program was free to participants and did not require government-issued identification or insurance.[ ].
In collaboration with community-based organizations (CBOs), the Vermont Department of Health hosted mobile clinics across the state in areas with greatest need using highly visible yellow vans. Mobile clinics were available to CBOs upon request for schools, employers, local health departments, skilled nursing or long-term care, local events, and other sites. Mobile clinics were held, for example, at churches, town halls, schools, senior centers, community centers, and libraries. Also, in partnership with state and local governments, the health department sent outreach workers to build awareness in the community about upcoming events hosted by the mobile clinics. 

3.4.

## Community-focused practices involving partners
In Alaska, the Alaska Black Caucus, a non-partisan community-based advocacy organization, sponsored community-based health and social activities that helped address concerns in the Black community around COVID-19 vaccination and empower individuals to make informed decisions about vaccination. The organization received a $1.15 million COVID-19 Community Funding Program grant from the Municipality of Anchorage and used this funding to help improve vaccine confidence and access among Black Alaskans, including holding a monthly webinar series on topics such as COVID-19 vaccination for kids and youth. [ , ] Immunization program staff from Alaska provided this insight:
They usually have different events where they do different things in the community, whether it ranges from healthcare or just even social aspects. Once we partnered with them, we [were] able to get many people vaccinated who did not have access to vaccination, as well as get some people [a] booster [dose]… by us partnering with the Black Caucus, we [were] also able to stop some misinformation and address some [people’s] concerns. 
In Vermont, Confident Care for Kids (CCFK) was a statewide initiative to increase sensory supports for children with disabilities and special health care needs when receiving COVID-19 vaccination. Twenty-eight participating community-based pediatric and family physician practices received a sensory toolkit aimed to reduce children’s anxiety when getting vaccinated. These toolkits included bubble timers, stress balls, wand spinners, and a vibrating bee with blue ice-pack wings that helps to block sharp pain and provides distraction. Parents and caregivers were provided a Vax Visit Prep Kit that included a free, downloadable social story and suggestions to plan for their child’s vaccination visit. ,[ ]
The CDPHE held vaccination clinics for children aged 5–11 years at child-friendly locations, including local zoos, museums, sporting events, recreation centers, and libraries across the state. The first event was held at the Denver Zoo, and children receiving their COVID-19 vaccination were given free access to the zoo for that day or a voucher for a future date. 
Austin ISD partnered with Vax Together Austin and Walgreens to host COVID-19 vaccination clinics for children 5 years and older, and these clinics included therapy dogs to comfort more than 1,000 children during vaccination. ,[ ] Similar activities were conducted in other jurisdictions, including using service animals at COVID-19 vaccination clinics held by the Pennsylvania Department of Health. Pennsylvania immunization program staff reported the following:
At some of our mass vaccination events [in Pennsylvania], we had the opportunity to work with a group called KPETS and their volunteer service animals. They are great at attracting kids to the vaccine booth and put them at ease. One time that stands out to me involved an eight-year-old and she was just crying so much. She just absolutely refused to get a shot…we said, we have the dog here. And the dog actually could go into the booth with you. She was playing with the dog and because they don’t jump up and are super well-behaved, she was able to play with the dog throughout the whole vaccine administration. And when they were done, they said, “By the way, you’re done with your vaccine shot.” And she didn’t even know that it had happened. So, it was a great way to distract her and take her mind off of it. 

3.5.

## Use of vaccination incentives
Gift cards were used as an incentive for pediatric vaccination in several jurisdictions. In San Antonio, TX, parents and guardians who vaccinated their children aged 5–11 years at local public health clinics received a $100 gift card to a local grocery store. The Minnesota Department of Health provided a $200 Visa gift card to families whose 5–11-year-old child received both doses of a COVID-19 vaccine between January 1 and February 28, 2022; this pediatric program was modeled after the state’s successful program to vaccinate older children aged 12–17 years. The Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands’ Commonwealth Healthcare Corporation provided gas gift cards to parents of children who received COVID-19 vaccination. 
In Vermont, the governor and the Agency of Education implemented their “School Vaccine Incentive Program” that awarded funds to schools achieving an 85 percent student vaccination rate for COVID-19 vaccination. Schools were awarded $15 per vaccinated student with a minimum award of $2000 and a maximum award of $10,000. Schools achieving at least a 90 percent student vaccination rate could apply for an additional 50 percent of the initial award, up to a maximum award of $15,000. ,[ ]
In New York, parents and guardians of children aged 5–11 years who received their first vaccine dose by December 19, 2021, could enter the state’s “Vaccinate, Educate, Graduate” incentive program for a chance for their child to win a full scholarship (tuition, room, board) to any two- or four-year college or university within the State University of New York (SUNY) or City University of New York (CUNY) systems. Fifty scholarship winners were selected through this program. 

3.6.

## Medicaid-related practices
From June 22, 2021, through the end of the federal Public Health Emergency (PHE) for COVID-19, North Carolina’s Medicaid program implemented a billing code that allowed for provider compensation for spending up to 15 minutes counseling patients on COVID-19 vaccination. This includes time that providers spend counseling parents or guardians of children enrolled in Medicaid on the benefit of receiving the COVID-19 vaccination, even if the parent or guardian is not enrolled in North Carolina Medicaid. The counseling services could be provided in person, via telehealth, or over the phone. ,[ ].
In California, the Medicaid program (Medi-Cal) linked its data with the state’s immunization registry to track its beneficiaries’ COVID-19 vaccination status and shared information with managed care organizations (MCOs). This linkage allowed identification of Medi-Cal populations, including children, who were not yet vaccinated and implementation of targeted outreach efforts. The California Department of Health Care Services supported partners (including local health departments and MCOs) in these targeted outreach efforts by providing monthly updates of coverage rates for Medi-Cal beneficiaries, including at the county-level and MCO-level.[ ].

4.

## Discussion
Immunization programs across the United States had the unprecedented challenge of vaccinating large cohorts of children against COVID-19 while mitigating barriers related to vaccine access, misinformation, confidence, and motivation. Programs tried innovative approaches to meet the challenge of pediatric COVID-19 vaccination and effectively tapped into partner resources, utilizing an all-hands-on-deck approach. Some of these practices have been previously used for routine, non-COVID-19 immunizations but adapted in the present environment (e.g., school-located vaccination clinics [ ] in areas with high COVID-19 morbidity) or implemented on an unprecedented scale (e.g., pharmacy-located vaccination [ ]); others were more novel (e.g., federal funding used to support incentives for vaccination uptake). These strategies were often multi-layered and included, but were not limited to, provider-focused practices and financial incentives, school-based practices, jurisdiction- or health department-based activities, community-focused practices involving partners, use of vaccination incentives, and Medicaid-related practices.
Primary care providers are typically the main provider of pediatric vaccination. During the COVID-19 pandemic, medical providers were faced with competing demands, such as testing and treatment for COVID-19, on top of routine activities. The COVID-19 vaccination requirements around reporting, vaccine storage and handling, and minimum vaccine order size posed additional challenges and could deter providers from enrolling or actively participating in the pediatric vaccination program.[ ] Education, financial support, and technical assistance to encourage provider enrollment in the pediatric COVID-19 vaccination program and to build infrastructure helped to ease those demands and ultimately provide access in a setting familiar to children and families. In some jurisdictions, these supportive activities improved provider enrollment; however, in other jurisdictions, provider incentives did not have substantial impact, and a better understanding of how best to support and engage providers in the pediatric COVID-19 vaccination program is needed.
Pharmacies were an integral part of vaccination efforts and a convenient point of access for entire families, with 37 % of vaccinated children ages 5–11 years receiving their vaccines at a pharmacy.[ ] A total of 21 retail pharmacy partners, including more than 41,000 locations nationwide, participated in the Federal Retail Pharmacy Program. [ ] This program, along with the Public Readiness and Emergency Preparedness (PREP) Act, which allowed pharmacy staff to administer vaccines to children as young as age 3 years, expanded access to pediatric COVID-19 vaccine.[ ] A potential downside of vaccinating children at pharmacies is less routine engagement with the medical home for routine screening and routine immunizations; however, especially in areas where opportunities for COVID-19 vaccination are limited, pharmacies can fill a crucial gap. Future pharmacy-based vaccination efforts might be limited after the sunset of the PREP Act, [ ] when pharmacists’ ability to vaccinate children reverts back to state law. Where state-based laws currently do not allow for pharmacists to vaccinate certain populations or provide specific vaccines, there is an opportunity for states to introduce their own provisions for allowing pharmacists to continue or begin vaccination for children as young as 3 years of age, especially children without a medical home or who have difficulty accessing their medical home.
Schools can serve as an important alternative venue for vaccination, including during a pandemic response. School-based health professionals are often viewed as trusted messengers and can help build vaccine confidence by sharing vaccine information with students, families, and communities. Also, schools are located throughout communities, and offering vaccination services at schools can enhance equitable and convenient access to vaccine. Administration of vaccine through school-located vaccination clinics and events were widely used during the 2009–10 H1N1 influenza pandemic, resulting in approximately one-third of vaccinated school-aged children being vaccinated in a school setting.[ ] School clinics have also been used for seasonal influenza vaccination and occasionally for other routinely-recommended childhood vaccines prior the COVID-19 pandemic. Some jurisdictions successfully offered students and sometimes others in the community COVID-19 vaccination at school, but overall participation was not high, resulting in 8.1 % of vaccinated children ages 5–11 years receiving vaccination at a school.[ ].
Health departments responded to the need for COVID-19 vaccination with great flexibility, often filling gaps in accessibility and adapting to local community needs. Mobile clinics provided access to vaccination across communities. Chicago’s in-home vaccination program also expanded access while providing a convenient way for families to get vaccinated in a single setting. Many providers have reported that the large order size required for COVID-19 vaccination was a barrier to of-fering vaccine, ,[ ] and Tennessee’s use of a third-party company for redistribution of smaller quantities was an innovative solution to address this barrier as well as helped prevent wastage and expiration of doses.
Partnerships were a key component of successful pediatric vaccination activities. Existing partnerships were leveraged, and new collaborations with traditional and non-traditional vaccination partners were formed. These partnerships, especially with trusted messengers (e.g., community-based organizations), helped communicate COVID-19 information to communities, build vaccine confidence, motivation, and provide more equitable access.
Incentives, ranging from event admission to gift cards to scholarships, were used in an unprecedented manner to encourage children and families to get children vaccinated. Use of incentives may not be a long-term, sustainable method of motivation, especially if funding was tied to limited federal funds. However, incentives can be a powerful tool and can reduce burden for transportation, help compensate for time off from work, or provide motivation. One study found that incentives for vaccination are generally most effective when they are guaranteed, delivered immediately, and hold value for the recipients.[ ] However, this study also found that, while incentives can help support vaccination uptake, they can also potentially exploit financial insecurity, lead to mistrust of vaccination, and might not have long-term impact on future vaccination uptake.[ ] Another recent study demonstrated that the use of incentives for COVID-19 vaccination has limited, if any, negative unintended consequences.[ ].
Billing code and data-sharing practices related to Medicaid helped facilitate beneficial changes to improve vaccine confidence/reduce vaccine hesitancy and enhance equitable access. According to the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS), “Stand-alone vaccine counseling has been shown to help address vaccine hesitancy by helping beneficiaries and their families learn about vaccines from trusted health care providers.”[ ] While the North Carolina COVID-19 vaccine counseling billing codes ended with the PHE, in mid-2022, CMS released billing codes for “stand-alone” counseling without requiring COVID-19 vaccine administration for Medicaid and Children’s Health Insurance Program (CHIP) beneficiaries nationwide.[ ] In California, COVID-19 vaccination coverage for Medicaid beneficiaries lags substantially behind the general population, with coverage of at least 1 dose for children aged 5–11 years at 25.9 % for Medi-Cal beneficiaries versus 37.4 % for recipients state-wide.[ ] California’s linkage of Medi-Cal data with its immunization registry may help address this disparity in coverage as it enables outreach to be more focused on unvaccinated individuals and communities.
Despite great efforts on the part of the public health community and health care partners broadly, including the many notable approaches to encourage vaccination and ensure broad and equitable access described in this report, approximately 70 percent of children ages 6 months through 11 years have not received a first dose of COVID-19 vaccine. [ , ] Vaccination coverage varies across states and by age groups. [ , ] Issues of vaccine confidence to include concerns about vaccine safety, and low motivation continue to be barriers to vaccination. [ , ] For children 6 months through 5 years of age, a recent study demonstrated that the following significantly influenced the decisions of parents regarding COVID-19 vaccination: concerns about immediate and long-term side effects; a perception that FDA approval for the COVID-19 vaccines was rushed; distrust in government, science, or pharmaceutical companies; a lack of community and family support for pediatric vaccination; disinformation in the media; and lower socioeconomic status.[ ].
There are some limitations to this report. First, each data source largely relied on passive data collection. Second, the report is not a comprehensive assessment of activities conducted in each state and local jurisdiction. As a result of these two limitations, some innovative practices, including those addressing specific populations at high risk of having low COVID-19 vaccination coverage may have been missed. Finally, program evaluation findings and impact assessments of these practices are limited, most likely due to resource constraints and the need to deprioritize evaluation in light of the public health emergency. Retrospective evaluations of practices would be valuable, as feasible, to assess whether practices are feasible and/or sustainable and to determine whether broader application is warranted. Sustainability may be especially challenging for practices that were supported by emergency or one-time funding.

5.

## Conclusion
There continue to be opportunities and challenges to vaccinating all eligible children ages 6 months through 11 years against COVID-19, and uptake of pediatric COVID-19 vaccination currently remains low. To date, efforts by state and local partners in implementing a large-scale vaccination program have resulted in many notable strategies. These practices demonstrate that state and local pediatric vaccination approaches vary and that one size may not fit all. The key findings from this work and further studies can serve to inform not only the current pediatric COVID-19 vaccination program but future outbreak response work as well as routine immunization activities.
