## Abstract

## Background
Although adolescent aggression has received increasing attention, existing interventions often lack specificity and fail to meet the needs of digital-native youth. This study developed a filmmaking intervention, grounded in the Social Information Processing model, art therapy, and narrative therapy, to reduce proactive and reactive aggression.

## Methods
An explanatory sequential mixed-methods design was used. Sub-study 1 was a randomized controlled trial with 82 adolescents assigned to an intervention group (n = 40) or waiting-list control (n = 42). The filmmaking intervention comprised ten sessions over two months, with assessments of aggression, emotion regulation, and empathy at baseline, post-test, and 3-month follow-up. Sub-study 2 qualitatively explored underlying mechanisms through analysis of 4 micro-films and interviews with 12 students and 4 facilitators using Grounded Theory.

## Results
Quantitative analyses showed significant time×group interactions in overall aggression (F = 3.28, p = .047, ηp2 = 0.05), reactive aggression (F = 4.69, p = .013, ηp2 = 0.07), and emotion regulation (F = 7.54, p = .001, ηp2 = 0.11). Post hoc tests indicated reductions in overall aggression (MD = − 5.18, p<.001) and reactive aggression (MD = − 3.82, p<.001), and an increase in emotion regulation (MD = 3.97, p<.001) from pre- to post-test. Compared with the control group, the intervention group showed lower overall aggression (MD = − 4.57, p<.001) and reactive aggression (MD = − 3.30, p = .001) and higher emotion regulation (MD = 5.01, p<.001) after the intervention. Qualitative analyses identified 10 categories, suggesting that the intervention reduced aggression by enhancing cognitive competence, emotional intelligence, interpersonal competence, engagement, and psychological capital. Filmmaking was characterized as a narrative therapy approach integrating humanistic and art therapy elements, with recommendations for implementation identified.

## Conclusions
The filmmaking intervention is moderately effective in reducing adolescent aggression, suggesting its potential as an innovative approach for fostering multiple aspects of adolescent development.
Trial registration: This study was retrospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (TRN: ChiCTR2500113469; Registration date: 28/11/2025; https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html?proj=289296).

## Background
Adolescent aggression has emerged as a persistent and increasingly serious public concern worldwide. According to a cross-national survey involving 72 countries, approximately 23% of school-age students reported experiencing bullying at least a few times per month [ ]. Moreover, a recent systematic review indicated that the prevalence of cyberbullying perpetration among children and adolescents increased between 2020 and 2023, particularly in Asian contexts [ ]. Adolescents, typically defined as those aged 10–24 years [ ], are in a critical period of rapid physical, cognitive, and socio-emotional development. Aggressive behaviors in adolescents can inflict significant harm on both individuals and society [ ]. Rather than subsiding with the onset of puberty, aggression often persists and continues to shape adolescents’ developmental trajectories [ ]. Early manifestations of aggression are known to predict sustained aggressive tendencies, poorer psychosocial adjustment, and even criminal involvement in adulthood [ ]. Hence, timely and effective interventions are crucial to prevent and mitigate aggressive behaviours during this formative stage.

## Reactive and proactive aggression in adolescents
Much of the existing research on aggression has tended to conceptualise aggression as a relatively unitary construct, namely treating individuals who engage in aggressive behaviour as belonging to a single, undifferentiated category. Such a one-factor perspective on aggression may overlook the fact that similar aggressive behaviours can be driven by fundamentally different motivations and goals [ ]. To address this limitation, Dodge and Coie introduced a two-factor model that distinguishes between reactive and proactive aggression according to their functions and underlying goals [ ]. Subsequent studies have consistently demonstrated that the two-factor model provides a superior fit compared with the one-factor framework [ – ].
Reactive and proactive aggression differ markedly in their antecedents, mechanisms, and outcomes [ ]. Reactively aggressive behaviour typically arises as an impulsive defensive response to perceived threat, provocation, or harm [ ]. In contrast, proactive aggression is instrumental and goal-oriented, serving purposes such as obtaining resources, achieving dominance, or asserting social status [ ]. At the process level, reactive aggression is affectively charged and unplanned, whereas proactive aggression involves deliberate planning concerning when, where, and how to act [ ]. In terms of consequences, reactive aggression is more strongly associated with internalising difficulties (e.g., depression, anxiety, and attention deficits) and interpersonal conflicts [ , ], whereas proactive aggression is linked to externalising problems, including conduct disorder, oppositional defiant disorder, and juvenile delinquency [ ].
The Social Information Processing (SIP) model delineates six sequential stages in children’s social adjustment: encoding, interpretation, goal setting, response generation, decision-making, and enactment [ ]. Reactive aggressors are prone to attentional and attributional biases during the initial two stages: they selectively attend to hostile cues and interpret ambiguous information as threatening [ , ]. In contrast, proactive aggressors exhibit cognitive distortions at later stages of the process. They deliberately set instrumental goals, such as gaining dominance, reputation, or material rewards, and plan aggressive acts based on previous successful experiences or perceived efficacy [ ]. These individuals often display inflated self-confidence and underestimate the potential consequences of their behaviour. Accordingly, effective interventions should address both the perceptual–attributional biases characteristic of reactive aggression and the distorted, goal-oriented cognitive evaluations underlying proactive aggression.

## Traditional intervention for adolescents with aggression
A substantial body of treatment studies and meta-analyses has shown that interventions aimed at helping young people regulate anger and other emotions produce small to moderate reductions in aggressive behaviour [ , ]. Moreover, numerous studies employing cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT) for adolescent aggression have consistently demonstrated its effectiveness [ ]. In another study, adolescents who received CBT exhibited a greater reduction in aggression than those who underwent emotional training [ ]. Similarly, Lee and DiGiuseppe found that interventions focusing directly on behavioural modification achieved superior outcomes relative to those primarily targeting cognitive or emotional adjustment [ ].
Recent innovative interventions have demonstrated efficacy in reducing aggression. For instance, a forgiveness-based programme for juvenile delinquents was found to enhance participants’ empathy and capacity for forgiveness [ ]. The effects of six-month omega-3 supplementation were examined, revealing that girls who received supplementation exhibited significant reductions in antisocial and aggressive behaviour compared with controls [ ]. Additional evidence indicates that structured activities, including Chinese martial arts combined with moral philosophy training [ ], storytelling programmes [ ], and mindfulness-based interventions [ ], can further mitigate aggressive tendencies in adolescents.
A key limitation of prior intervention studies for adolescents with aggression is that many of them generally targeted overall aggression rather than explicitly differentiating between reactive and proactive aggression. While these interventions may still be beneficial for both subtypes, they provide limited direct evidence regarding effects on reactive and proactive aggression. Importantly, there is evidence that intervention content specifically tailored to reactive and proactive aggression may achieve greater efficacy [ , ]. Therefore, interventions should be designed to incorporate theoretical insights on reactive and proactive aggression into their content, enabling more targeted and effective outcomes for each subtype.
A further limitation of previous interventions is their limited use of technology-based approaches. Studies have shown that such traditional programmes may be less suitable for youths growing up in the digital era [ ], as they may not fully capture attention and sustain engagement. Evidence showed that integrating modern technologies into psychological interventions may enhance engagement and adherence, thereby improving both the effectiveness and accessibility of treatment [ ]. That is, technology-based and interactive approaches may align with the preferences of contemporary adolescents [ ]. These considerations highlight the need for interventions that incorporate contemporary digital technologies, including smartphones, mobiel apps, and short videos.

## Justification for the use of filmmaking as an intervention for aggressive adolescents
Among contemporary technology-assisted interventions, filmmaking has attracted increasing scholarly and practical attention [ ]. Unlike traditional film therapy approaches that rely on watching and discussing films for therapeutic purposes [ ], filmmaking involves guiding participants in producing films or videos based on their personal experiences to promote psychological well-being [ ]. Evidence suggests that, compared with traditional methods, filmmaking may more effectively engage youths who have grown up in the digital era [ ].
Research indicates that filmmaking and digital media can enhance youths’ mental health [ ] and reduce depression, anxiety, anger, and stress [ , ]. Filmmaking tasks have also been shown to improve cooperation and communication skills [ ] and to boost self-confidence upon completion of complex projects [ ]. In addition, participants often develop greater empathy for others and a stronger sense of community consideration [ , ]. Prior research demonstrates that emotional factors (e.g., depression, anxiety, anger), social factors (e.g., social skill deficits), and self-identity issues (e.g., low self-esteem) are strongly associated with reactive aggression [ , ], whereas emotional and cognitive factors (e.g., poor empathy, positive attitudes toward aggression) are closely linked to proactive aggression [ , ]. These findings suggest that filmmaking may represent a potentially effective intervention for reducing both reactive and proactive aggression.
This prediction is further supported by theoretical evidence. Empirical studies have identified filmmaking as an innovative arts-based methodology [ , ]. Filmmaking integrates the advantages of both timeless arts (e.g., drawing, sculpture) and time-based arts (e.g., drama, dance) to convey the meaning of participants’ work, functioning as a reflective medium for introspection by both creators and viewers [ , ]. Both the process of producing films or videos (artistic creation) and the resulting products (artistic outputs) can exert therapeutic effects [ ]. Other studies have highlighted the strong alignment of filmmaking with narrative therapy principles [ ]. During pre-production and production, filmmaking facilitates externalisation of problematic stories and the reconstruction of dominant narratives, thereby supporting participants’ healing [ ]. At the final stage, when digital products are exhibited or shared with audiences such as family members, participants’ self-esteem is enhanced through outsider witness practices [ ]. Collectively, art therapy and narrative therapy provide theoretical support for the therapeutic potential of filmmaking. Nevertheless, theoretical frameworks underpinning filmmaking as a therapeutic intervention remain underdeveloped and lack consensus, warranting further conceptual elaboration.

## The present study
Based on the research gaps and practical needs outlined above, this study aims to develop a filmmaking intervention to reduce adolescent aggression from the perspective of the two-factor model and to evaluate its efficacy. As a pioneering study on the application of filmmaking with adolescents, it also intends to derive implementation insights from participants and group facilitators following the intervention. In summary, the study has two primary objectives: (1) to test the efficacy of filmmaking in addressing reactive and proactive aggression in adolescents, and (2) to examine the underlying mechanisms through which filmmaking influences these forms of aggression.
To enhance the validity of the first objective, the study additionally assesses emotion regulation skills and empathy, as reactive aggression is closely associated with deficits in emotion regulation, whereas proactive aggression is linked to impaired empathy [ ]. Accordingly, two hypotheses were formulated for the first research objective, which will be tested using quantitative analyses:

H1: Adolescents’ overall aggression, reactive aggression, and proactive aggression decrease after attending the filmmaking intervention.
H2: Adolescents’ emotion regulation skills and empathy improve following the filmmaking intervention.

To elaborate the second study aim, we proposed two research questions that are well-suited to qualitative analysis:

Q1: What emotional, cognitive and social aspects does filmmaking contribute to adolescent aggression reduction?
Q2: How does the filmmaking intervention reduce reactive and proactive aggression in adolescents?

## Methods

## Design
An explanatory sequential mixed-methods design was employed, comprising two sub-studies. Sub-study 1, the quantitative component, utilized a randomized controlled trial (RCT) with a treatment group and a waiting-list control group to evaluate the efficacy of the filmmaking intervention in reducing reactive and proactive aggression in adolescents. The waiting-list group design was adopted for ethical reasons to ensure that all students would have the opportunity to participate in the filmmaking intervention. Sub-study 2, the qualitative component, combined film-product analysis with semi-structured interviews of participants and group facilitators to investigate the mechanisms through which the intervention influences reactive and proactive aggression and to explore its theoretical foundations and potential recommendations for future implementation. The clinical trial was retrospectively registered, but the study underwent rigorous ethical review and approval prior to data collection. The College Human Subjects Ethics Sub-Committee of the City University of Hong Kong approved this study (Approval No. 2021-22-CIR3-5 and 2022-23-CIR6-6 for the quantitative and qualitative components, respectively).

## Filmmaking intervention
The design of the filmmaking intervention was informed by prior empirical studies on filmmaking as a therapeutic tool [ , ] and grounded in the two-factor model of aggression, targeting both reactive and proactive aggression throughout the study. Drawing on theoretical foundations, the intervention was guided by the Social Information Processing (SIP) model of aggression, as well as principles from art therapy and narrative therapy. The program comprised ten 90-minute sessions conducted twice weekly. Participants worked in groups to produce a micro-film related to the theme of aggression, not exceeding 30 min in duration. The intervention was structured into five phases corresponding to the stages of filmmaking [ , ]. Intervention content was shown in Table  . Four professionals with three to six years of experience in youth psychology and education served as group facilitators. All had formal training in psychological counselling, educational psychology, or related disciplines and completed a three-hour preparatory training session. Group facilitators were responsible for organising and guiding the overall intervention process. They supported the filmmaking process by providing procedural and logistical assistance (e.g., clarifying task requirements and managing time), while maintaining a neutral stance on the content or creative decisions of the participants’ films. Additionally, a technical instructor provided guidance and assistance with editing tasks throughout the intervention.
 

## Participants and procedure
The study was conducted in a special school located in South China, which serves as the Juvenile Delinquency Prevention Education Base of the Juvenile Correctional Centre in the province. The school enrols youth exhibiting aggressive or violent behaviour, truancy, school-rule violations, vandalism, substance abuse, and related problematic conduct. Students are typically referred after dropping out of ordinary schools, either by their parents or by the Public Security Bureau. Attendance is mandatory for a designated period ranging from one month to over a year, with no option for voluntary withdrawal.
Sub-study 1 was conducted from December 2022 to April 2023. Questionnaire assessments were administered at three time points: a pre-test in early December, a post-test in late January, and a follow-up assessment in late April. At the beginning of the study, the activity was introduced as a Filmmaking Art Workshop to avoid potential labelling effects. All the students in the school, totaling 103 individuals, were invited, and 82 students and their parents provided informed consent to participate. Given the generally high levels of aggression among students in this special school, no further screening was required. Forty participants were randomly assigned to the treatment group and 42 to the waiting-list group. The treatment group was further divided into four subgroups of ten members each, with each subgroup led by a group facilitator. Participants collaboratively produced a microfilm (≦ 30 min) over ten sessions. Apart from the filmmaking intervention, both groups continued receiving the school’s regular education programme. Some students withdrew due to COVID-19 infection or early graduation. Ultimately, 62 students (Mean age = 14.42 ± 1.76; 33.91% females), consisting of 33 in the treatment group and 29 in the control group, completed all three waves of data collection (see Fig.  ).
 Sub-study 2, conducted from May to August 2023, comprised microfilm analysis and semi-structured interviews with both participants and group facilitators. Four microfilms were produced by the treatment subgroups in Sub-study 1. Three students were randomly selected from each subgroup for interviews (12 in total) to ensure representation and minimise researcher selection bias. Additionally, four facilitators who had led the filmmaking sessions were interviewed. In total, four films and 16 interview recordings were collected.
Although a follow-up intervention identical to that of the treatment group had been planned for the waiting-list control group, it was replaced with a psychoeducation course due to policy influence during the COVID-19 period. A full debriefing was provided to all participants after study completion, during which the study purpose was explained, and mental support was offered. Students were explicitly informed to seek support from the school psychological counsellor if they experienced any psychological distress or concerns.

## Measures and interview guides
Given the characteristics of the participating school, students are frequently required to complete psychological questionnaires as part of routine assessments. Overly lengthy measures are particularly likely to elicit resistance, burden, or careless responding [ ]. Accordingly, except for the assessment of reactive and proactive aggression, which necessitated a specialised questionnaire to capture these distinct constructs, emotion regulation and empathy were assessed using subscales from well-established general measures with strong reliability and validity, thereby achieving a balance between brevity and measurement precision.

## The reactive-proactive aggression questionnaire (RPQ)
 The RPQ is a 23-item self-report measure comprising two subscales that assess reactive and proactive aggression. Each item is rated on a 3-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater levels of self-reported aggression. The traditional Chinese version was translated and validated by Fung et al. [ ], while a simplified Chinese version was subsequently revised and adopted in the present study. In this study, all participating students completed this scale and Cronbach’s α coefficients were 0.91 for the total RPQ, 0.90 for the proactive subscale, and 0.82 for the reactive subscale.

## Social Competence Scale (SC)
 The SC was developed by the Fast Track Project and was employed in this study to assess participants’ emotion regulation [ ]. The Emotional Regulation Skills subscale (SC-E), consisting of six items, was translated and used. The subscale primarily assesses the skill aspects of emotion regulation, focusing on participants’ ability to manage negative emotional arousal, inhibit impulsive reactions, and maintain goal-directed and socially appropriate behavior in challenging or interpersonal situations, such as remaining calm when emotionally aroused, coping with failure, and controlling temper during disagreements. All participating students completed this scale. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating better emotion regulation ability. The SC-E demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.88).

## Antisocial process screening device (APSD)
 The APSD was developed by Frick and Hare [ ]. The Callous–Unemotional traits subscale (APSD-Cu), comprising six items, was used to assess empathy deficits. The questionnaire, completed by all participating students, uses a 3-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating lower levels of empathy. The Cronbach’s α was 0.73 in this study.

## Interview guide for students
 It was developed by the authors in this study and comprised six questions. The first three explored perceived changes in aggressive behaviour, emotional regulation, communication, and cooperation. Two subsequent scenario-based questions, adapted from Crick and Dodge [ ], were designed to elicit deeper insights into hostile attribution, consequence evaluation, problem-solving flexibility, etc. The final question examined participants’ reflections and overall perceptions of the filmmaking intervention.

## Interview guide for group facilitators
 It was adapted from Cohen’s framework for video therapy experts [ ], and further refined by authors in this study. The guide included nine questions addressing facilitators’ views on how filmmaking influences reactive and proactive aggression among adolescents, its underlying theoretical mechanisms, and practical considerations for future implementation.ethical.

## Data analysis
In sub-study 1, the two hypotheses presented in “The Present Study” would be examined. Chi-square tests and one-way analyses of variance (ANOVA) were conducted to examine baseline differences across variables. Also, we used the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test, Shapiro-Wilk (S-W) test and Q-Q Plots to test whether the data follow a normal distribution. In addition, the homogeneity of variances for the between-subjects factor was assessed using Levene’s test at each measurement occasion. Hypotheses were then tested using the interaction term (Time×Group) in a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA, which compared changes among groups across three time points. Sphericity was assessed using Mauchly’s test. When the sphericity assumption was violated, Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied. If significant interactions were observed, Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons were conducted to further explore these effects. All quantitative analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0.
In sub-study 2, the two research questions presented in “The Present Study” would be explored. A senior psychological counsellor first reviewed the four micro-films and provided written feedback from the perspectives of social and socio-psychological processes, following the questioning approach of classic grounded theory [ ]. The first author and the counsellor then discussed and reached consensus on these written evaluations. Subsequently, 12 student and four facilitator interviews were transcribed verbatim. Grounded theory, which is an open and flexible qualitative research methodology that enables researchers to gain an in-depth understanding of the underlying causes and patterns of specific phenomena or problems [ ], was applied to code and analyse all textual data using MAXQDA software. Two authors independently conducted the three-level coding steps of Grounded Theory [ ]: (1) open coding, generating initial concepts and preliminary subcategories from the raw data; (2) axial coding, integrating subcategories into broader main categories based on their attributes and relationships; (3) selective coding, examining the relationships among main categories to identify core categories representing the central phenomenon of the study, which led to the development of a theoretical model to address the research questions. Any discrepancies were resolved through consultation with a senior researcher until full consensus was achieved.

## Results

## Quantitative results in sub-study 1

## Demographics and baseline performance
 Descriptive statistics and data of the two groups in the baseline were tested. No significant group differences were observed on age, sex, only child character, baseline RPQ, SC-E, and APSD-Cu, suggesting that randomization of groups was successful (see Table  ).

That is, adolescents’ emotional regulation skill enhanced after completing the filmmaking intervention.
 For APSD-Cu scores, no significant time×group interaction, F(2, 120) = 0.47, p =.63, ηp2 = 0.01, or main effects of time, F(2, 120) = 1.53, p =.221, ηp2 = 0.03, or group, F(1, 60) = 3.47, p =.067, ηp2 = 0.06, were found (see Fig.  ). Namely, adolescents’ empathy did not change significantly after completing the filmmaking intervention.
In summary, the two hypotheses of this study were partially supported. Participants exhibited reductions in overall and reactive aggression, alongside improvements in emotion regulation skills, following the filmmaking intervention. Although proactive aggression showed a decreasing trend and empathy demonstrated a slight increase, neither reached statistical significance. Moreover, scores for overall and reactive aggression slightly increased at the three-month follow-up, suggesting a limited maintenance effect of the intervention.

## Qualitative results in sub-study 2
Before using the grounded theory method, we tried to conclude some direct responses related to the intervention effectiveness. All students in the treatment group reported reductions in reactive aggression and demonstrated improved emotion regulation. Unlike the results of sub-study 1, which did not reveal statistically significant changes in participants’ proactive aggression or empathy, the interviews here indicated that all participants also exhibited notable decreases in proactive aggression. Moreover, they showed greater consideration for others and employed more diverse problem-solving strategies, rather than relying solely on aggressive behaviour as a solution.

## Results of open coding
 The sub-study 2 obtained a total of 1206 paragraphs from 20 original materials. A total of 72 initial concepts were identified, which consisted of 791 conceptual label points. The examples for giving conceptual labels were shown in Table  . On this basis, these initial concepts were summarized into 28 preliminary subcategories through continuous comparison and exploration of relationships between concepts, such as interest arousal, improved problem-solving skills, and enhanced emotional regulation abilities.
 

## Results of axial coding
 Each preliminary subcategory, as well as its corresponding concepts and original texts, was read repeatedly to gradually refine these 28 subcategories into broader categories that are more psychologically meaningful, more conceptual, and more in line with the purposes of this study. Finally, 10 main categories were identified: engagement, cognitive competence, emotional intelligence, interpersonal competence, psychological capital, humanism, narrative therapy, art therapy, key considerations, and appropriate themes (see Table  ).

## Results of selective coding
We repeatedly cross-analysed and explored the 10 main categories combined with this study’s idea, frequently referring back to the subcategories and original materials to construct, break, rebuild, and finally generate three core categories to represent the study’s phenomenon:

Treatment effects and contributing pathways of filmmaking intervention: enhancing cognitive competence, emotional intelligence, interpersonal competence, psychological capital, and engagement.
Theoretical basis of filmmaking: humanism, narrative therapy, and art therapy.
Implementation recommendations for filmmaking: key considerations and film themes.

Based on the three core categories, two grounded theories were generated in this study. First, filmmaking appears to reduce both reactive and proactive aggression in adolescents by enhancing engagement, cognitive competence, emotional intelligence, interpersonal skills, and psychological capital. Second, filmmaking functions as a narrative therapy approach rooted in humanism, integrating methods from art therapy. These two theories collectively address the study’s research questions.
In addition to the theoretical insights, key considerations and thematic recommendations were identified to guide the implementation of future filmmaking interventions. Filmmaking shows potential in addressing problematic behaviors, self-concept issues, and familial or interpersonal concerns among children and adolescents, warranting further investigation. Effective implementation, however, requires adequate personnel and technical support, careful grouping of participants by gender and age, and sufficient, consistent time allocation. ## Discussion

## Effects of a filmmaking intervention on adolescent aggression
Findings from the quantitative data provide the first direct empirical evidence for the efficacy of filmmaking interventions in reducing overall and reactive aggression. These results align with earlier evidence that filmmaking can mitigate problematic behaviours in youth [ ]. The moderate effect size observed in this study (ηp2 = 0.05 for the effect in reducing overall aggression and ηp2 = 0.07 for the effect in reducing reactive aggression) are comparable to, or slightly higher than, the small-to-moderate effects reported in a meta-analysis of 60 intervention studies targeting youth aggression, anger, and related outcomes [ ]. Furthermore, adolescents’ emotion regulation skills improved following the filmmaking intervention, providing additional support for its effectiveness in reducing reactive aggression, given the well-established association between emotion regulation and reactive aggression [ ]. Complementing these quantitative findings, qualitative data indicated that enhanced emotion regulation is a key mechanism through which filmmaking reduces aggression. These findings are consistent with prior research identifying poor emotion regulation as a vital factor in adolescents’ engagement in reactive aggression [ ].
No statistically significant changes were observed in adolescents’ proactive aggression or empathy following the filmmaking intervention. One possible explanation is that participants were not pre-screened for proactive or reactive aggression in order to retain as many subjects as possible. Previous studies indicate that proactive aggressors typically constitute the smallest proportion of all aggressor subtypes [ ]. In the Chinese school context, only 2.6% of students are classified as proactive aggressors [ ]. Moreover, RPQ scores generally show that students’ proactive aggression is lower than their reactive aggression, consistent across both parent [ , ] and teacher reports [ ]. This pattern was also observed in the quantitative data of the current study, suggesting that proactive aggression is less frequent or less overt than reactive aggression in adolescents. However, qualitative data supported the reduction of proactive aggression. It further indicated that the filmmaking process enhanced adolescents’ empathy, as reflected in greater perspective-taking, supportive and appreciative behaviors, and emotional resonance with others. Several factors may help explain the discrepancy between the quantitative and qualitative findings. First, proactive aggression was less frequent, which might reduce statistical power to detect changes in standardised measures over a relatively short intervention period. Second, quantitative instruments might be less sensitive to subtle emotional or cognitive shifts that occur during the filmmaking process, whereas qualitative interviews and self-reflections can capture nuanced changes in perspective-taking, empathy, and social cognition. Considering both quantitative and qualitative evidence, we contend that filmmaking holds promise for promoting empathy and reducing proactive aggression, even though the effects were not as pronounced as anticipated.
The qualitative component of this study applied Grounded Theory to develop explanatory models of how filmmaking influences adolescents and reduces their reactive and proactive aggression. Findings indicated that filmmaking decreases aggression by enhancing cognitive competence (ability to better understand oneself and others, assess consequences, adaptive attribution strategy, problem-solving skills), emotional intelligence (empathy, emotional regulation abilities), interpersonal competence (relational competence, interpersonal skills), engagement (interest, involvement, focus), and psychological capital (self-esteem, sense of worth, psychological resilience). This insight aligns with prior research while extending current understanding. First, reactive aggressors often face difficulties in relationships, adjustment, problem-solving, and emotional regulation [ ]. Improving cognitive competence, emotional intelligence, and interpersonal skills through filmmaking thus helps reduce reactive aggression. Second, collaborative aspects of filmmaking promote perspective-taking [ ], enhance empathy [ ], and improve participants’ evaluation of the consequences of negative behaviors [ , ], contributing to reductions in proactive aggression. Third, these findings suggest that filmmaking may influence broader behavioral tendencies, as factors such as cognitive competence, emotional intelligence, interpersonal skills, and psychological capital are closely linked to conduct problems, interpersonal conflicts, and psychological trauma [ ], warranting further investigation.
The maintenance effects were observed only for emotion regulation skills, whereas reductions in aggression were not maintained over time, suggesting that the filmmaking intervention may primarily exert short-term benefits, rather than durable changes in aggressive behaviour. Several interrelated factors may help explain this pattern. First, the intervention period was relatively limited, lasting approximately one and a half months due to disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic. Behavioural change, particularly in aggressive tendencies, often requires longer and more consistent exposure to consolidate new habits [ ]. A systematic review of filmmaking as a therapeutic intervention for children and adolescents suggested that programmes with a higher number of intervention sessions (e.g., around 20 sessions; [ ]) tend to demonstrate more favourable outcomes [ ]. Second, the timing of outcome measurement and changes in contextual conditions may have attenuated the effects of the filmmaking intervention. Although the intervention was delivered in a structured school environment, the follow-up assessment took place after many participants had left this context, where peer dynamics, supervision, and daily routines differ. Given that adolescents’ emotions, cognitions, and behaviors are highly dynamic and sensitive to environmental influences [ ], the removal of this supportive context may have reduced the persistence of observed improvements. Overall, the differential maintenance of outcomes indicates a possible mechanism-specific effect. Although emotion regulation skills were retained, aggression may require more intensive cognitive and behavioural components to achieve lasting change.
For adolescents in the waiting-list control group, aggression, emotion regulation, and empathy showed relatively stable trajectories over time, without the marked changes observed in those who received the filmmaking intervention. Interestingly, although the effects of the intervention were not fully maintained at three months, participants in the treatment group still exhibited lower aggression than the waiting-list group at follow-up, albeit not statistically significant. Together, these patterns provide modest additional support for the moderate effectiveness of the filmmaking intervention. These findings suggest that even when short-term gains diminish, the filmmaking intervention may leave slight benefits, highlighting the importance of strategies to sustain long-term effects. Future filmmaking interventions could explore extending the intervention duration, incorporating booster sessions, or integrating the program across multiple contexts to enhance and consolidate its effectiveness.

## Theoretical foundations of filmmaking
Using a Grounded Theory approach, this study concluded that filmmaking is a narrative therapy approach rooted in humanism and integrating art therapy methods. This research is the first to highlight the role of humanism in filmmaking. Humanistic theory emphasizes individual self-realization, inner potential, and subjective experience [ , ], asserting that each person is unique and capable of realizing their fullest potential [ ]. During filmmaking, facilitators emphasized participant-centered principles and unconditional positive regard, closely aligning with humanistic concepts [ ]. In the field of psychotherapy, relatively systematic approaches have been proposed and practiced under the humanistic branch, such as Person-Centered Therapy, Gestalt Therapy, and Existential Therapy. However, our study did not engage with such in-depth concepts or models. Therefore, this study assumes that filmmaking incorporates humanistic principles from a relatively broad, macro-level perspective.
Simultaneously, filmmaking can be conceptualized as a method with art therapy characteristics. It shares fundamental components with arts-based methodologies, including the use of images, symbols, and active imagination [ ]. Group facilitators frequently highlighted symbolic or metaphorical elements during the filmmaking process, such as using tangible objects to represent thoughts or emotions and employing cinematography or framing to convey abstract ideas. Art therapy posits that the art-making process itself is inherently cathartic [ ], a notion reflected in this study, as adolescents’ scriptwriting and acting facilitated deeper emotional experiences and release. Furthermore, within art therapy, the artistic product itself—here, the film—possesses inherent therapeutic value [ ], a finding supported by this study, where video creation benefited both participants and external viewers. Art therapy thus enables individuals to resolve emotional conflicts, enhance self-development, and achieve psychological and emotional balance through artistic expression [ , ]. From this methodological perspective, filmmaking can be understood through the lens of art therapy theory.
Most importantly, the study highlighted that the filmmaking process aligns closely with narrative therapy, encompassing externalization, deconstruction, reconstruction, and consolidation. Group facilitators particularly emphasized externalization, noting that participants transformed their inner stories into external scripts or videos. In creating a script, participants appeared to separate personal problems from themselves and present them for group discussion. By externalizing and separating problems from the self, these issues are no longer conflated with the individual’s core identity, enabling participants to view and engage with the problems, thereby enhancing their sense of control and agency [ ]. The study observed that initial storytelling within the group resembled children narrating the main problematic stories of their lives. Filmmaking provided not only an opportunity to narrate these stories but also a space to explore turning points, re-author them, and construct more positive life narratives. These deconstruction and reconstruction processes constitute the core of narrative therapy [ , ]. In the final stage, the viewing of films by relatives and friends served as an external witness, significantly augmenting participants’ psychological resources and consolidating the newly constructed positive narratives [ ]. Thus, narrative therapy principles guided the filmmaking process throughout all stages, from pre-production, through production and post-production, to the final exhibition, underpinning its theoretical and practical framework.
Although this study draws on three psychotherapy theories, their roles differ. Humanistic theory primarily guides the facilitator–participant relationship, centering the participant, recognising their competence, thus serving as a broad theoretical premise. Art therapy, while valuing a supportive and collaborative alliance, often depends on the therapist’s professional guidance to help clients interpret and reflect on the meanings of their artwork [ ]. Narrative therapy, in contrast, emphasizes de-authorizing the therapist and affirming the client as the primary expert, a view aligned with humanistic principles [ ]. Narrative therapy offers a systematic integration with filmmaking across all stages from development to post-production. Overall, these considerations support framing narrative therapy as the core theoretical foundation, humanistic theory as the guiding premise, and art therapy as a key methodological approach for filmmaking interventions.

## Implementation recommendations for filmmaking in adolescents
Findings identified two key considerations for future filmmaking interventions. First, group facilitators should strengthen their professional competence and efficiently organise activities, supported by adequate technology, equipment, props, and venues to ensure smooth implementation and allow participants to fully realise their creative ideas. Second, participant groups should be formed with narrow age ranges and balanced gender ratios, fostering resonance and connection that enhance therapeutic efficacy and satisfaction [ ]. Additionally, both participants and facilitators indicated that longer, more stable intervention durations would further improve outcomes.
Three primary film themes emerged from participants’ repeated references, indicating areas particularly suited for intervention: (1) problematic behaviors (e.g. internet addiction, general aggressive behavior) and illegal behaviors (e.g. theft, robbery, gambling, drug abuse); (2) self-concept issues (e.g. identity crisis, setbacks and growth) and emotional and cognitive issues (e.g. seeking self-value, self-centeredness); and (3) family and interpersonal issues (e.g. parent-child issues and peer conflicts). Adolescence is a critical, sensitive developmental stage marked by increased self-exploration and reflection [ ]. Biological and psychological changes can give rise to risky behaviors [ ], while shifting social relationships from family to peers and broader society create new relational challenges [ ]. These themes therefore closely reflect the developmental concerns of children and adolescents.

## Limitations and future directions
This study has several limitations. First, the clinical trial was retrospectively registered. Second, the sample was small and drawn from a single site. Third, Using a waiting-list control group instead of an active control may limit the ability to account for nonspecific factors, such as attention, expectancy, or engagement, which could result in an overestimation of the intervention’s effects. Fourth, emotion regulation and empathy were quantitatively assessed using brief subscales rather than comprehensive, domain-specific instruments, which may have limited the breadth of the assessment, potentially overlooking more fine-grained or specific components of these core variables. Fifth, the Grounded Theory approach may overlook infrequently mentioned yet important insights. For example, two participants noted that filmmaking was too indirect for those lacking interest or reflective engagement, but this view is scarcely represented in the final theoretical framework. Sixth, the study did not assess the maintenance effects of the intervention in the qualitative study. Seventh, although group facilitators were carefully selected, trained, and supervised, no comprehensive fidelity check for the intervention was conducted.
Future research can address the above limitations and consider three directions. First, separating proactive and reactive aggressors into distinct groups, where feasible, could enhance intervention precision and allow nuanced comparisons of effects on different aggression types. Second, refining the filmmaking protocol to target diverse aggression forms (e.g., physical, verbal, relational, and cyber) may broaden its applicability. Third, filmmaking interventions could be extended to other developmental issues, such as self-concept, interpersonal relationships, and family issues.

## Conclusion
Taking all findings into account, this study integrates results from two sub-studies and concludes that, although the effects were somewhat lower than anticipated, the filmmaking intervention is moderately effective in reducing both reactive and proactive aggression in adolescents. The intervention mitigates aggression by enhancing adolescents’ cognitive competence, emotional intelligence, interpersonal competence, psychological capital and engagement. Conceptually, filmmaking can be understood as a narrative therapy approach grounded in humanistic principles and incorporating art therapy techniques. Furthermore, the study offers practical implementation recommendations that may inform and improve future filmmaking-based interventions.
