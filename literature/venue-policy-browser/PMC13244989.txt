Using data from the 2020 wave of the China Longitudinal Aging Social Survey (CLASS), a mediation analysis using linear regression and the Karlson–Holm–Breen (KHB) mediation testing method is conducted to investigate both the direct and indirect pathways linking residential environment to depressive symptoms among community-dwelling adults aged 60 and above. Results indicated that superior housing quality (β = -0.093, p < 0.001) and abundant community facilities (β = -0.074, p < 0.01) were significantly associated with lower levels of depressive symptoms. Social participation partially mediated this association, accounting for 24.66% of the total effect of housing quality on depressive symptoms, and 27.18% of the total effect of community facilities on depressive symptoms. The findings provide robust evidence that investments in age-friendly housing and community infrastructure are conducive to enhancing mental health of older adults in China.

## Introduction
According to the latest statistics, the nationwide population of older adults aged 65 and above accounted for 15.6% of the total population in China [ ]. As population aging intensifies, the mental health of older adults has attracted increasing academic and policy attention. Depression, as one of the most prevalent mental health disorders among older populations, is significantly associated with reduced quality of life and elevated mortality risk, making it a core priority for healthy aging policy [ , ].
The residential environment, encompassing housing quality and community built conditions, has been consistently recognized as a significant determinant of older adults’ mental health [ – , , , , ]. The linkage between residential environment and mental health is theoretically rooted in two important frameworks in environmental gerontology, including the Ecological Theory of Aging [ ] and the Activity Theory [ ]. The Ecological Theory of Aging posits residential environment as a source of “environmental press,” where supportive environments reduce stressors and facilitate social engagement, which in turn are associated with improved mental health. However, the specific psychosocial mechanisms linking residential environment to depressive symptoms remain less elucidated [ ]. Building on the ecological model, the study further integrates the Activity Theory, which suggests social engagement as a key pathway for maintaining better mental health and achieving successful aging. We conceptualize the residential environment not merely as a set of physical resources, but as a form of social infrastructure that either facilitates or impedes opportunities for social interaction for older adults. The integration of these theories enables us to propose a specific associational pathway: supportive residential environments are associated with greater social participation, which in turn correlates with lower depressive symptoms.
Existing literature has shown a robust association between housing quality and health outcomes [ ]. Won and Lee [ ] discovered that positive perceptions of residential environments conducive to daily needs significantly contribute to social capital and health outcomes, whereas perceived access to recreational amenities did not show a significant correlation in the context of South Korea. Li and Zhou [ ] found that both external and indoor housing quality were significantly associated with older adults’ depressive symptoms. Favorable housing quality can provide a safe and comfortable living space for older adults, contributing to improvements in their quality of life and mental health. For instance, factors such as stable housing structures, good ventilation, and adequate lighting were positively associated with the psychological health of older adults [ , , , ].
Furthermore, community facilities, as another dimension of residential environment, can offer a wealth of social opportunities and activity venues, promoting health and quality of life among older adults [ , ]. Based on a longitudinal analysis, Zhou et al. [ ] found that community facilities played a crucial role in reducing the risk of depression. Specifically, access to family-based older-care centers and outdoor exercise facilities was significantly associated with a lower risk of depressive symptoms. Li [ ] further corroborated findings that middle-aged and older adults residing in neighborhoods with superior physical and social environments exhibited a protective influence against depressive symptoms. Based on latent growth curve models, Liu et al. [ ] found that an age-friendly neighborhood environment significantly buffers the negative impacts of functional decline on mental health of older adults. Facilities such as parks, fitness centers, libraries, and other community resources can fulfill the leisure and entertainment needs of older adults, fostering social interactions and physical activities known to mitigate depressive symptoms [ , , ]. In contrast, a deficiency of community facilities may precipitate social isolation and sedentary behavior, thereby constraining the activity range of older adults and exacerbating feelings of loneliness and the risk of depression [ ]. Overall, the mainstream literature suggests significant associations between community resources and mental health outcomes of older populations.
Prior research has investigated the mechanisms underlying the relationship between residential environment and health outcomes [ ]. For instance, Gibson et al. [ ] identified three principal pathways through which housing and neighborhood conditions are associated with health outcomes: internal housing conditions, area characteristics, and housing tenure. Gan et al. [ ] found that sociobehavioral attributes and loneliness played a mediating pathway in the relationship between residential environment and mental health outcomes. Residing in areas equipped with more community facilities enables older adults to engage more readily in social activities and community organizations [ , ]. A longitudinal study by Wang et al. [ ] discovered that depressive symptoms of older adults decreased in tandem with increased neighborhood social participation, greater engagement in physical activities, and higher frequencies of contact with their neighbors. Notably, while existing literature has explored the mediating pathway of social participation in mental health [ ], fewer studies have explicitly contextualized this role within the framework of residential environment and mental health. Richard et al. [ ]’s Voisinuage study found that accessible neighborhood resources promoted social participation among older adults, which in turn correlated with improved self-reported mental health. The study therefore hypothesizes that social participation may be a potential mediator in the relationship between residential environment and depressive symptoms.
Despite extensive research conducted in previous studies on the relationship between residential environment and depressive symptoms, there remains a compelling need to investigate the underlying mechanisms that connect the residential environment with depressive symptoms. The objective of this study is to investigate the relationship between the residential environment—including housing quality and community facilities—and depressive symptoms, and to examine the influence mechanism among older adults. ## Methods

## Data and sample
The present study utilized data from the 2020 wave of the CLASS dataset, a comprehensive nationwide social survey project managed by the National Survey Research Center (NSRC) at Renmin University of China. CLASS serves as a pivotal platform for understanding the multifaceted aspects of aging in China, encompassing demographic, familial, societal, and economic dimensions. The survey involved 462 communities and 11,398 individuals, collecting extensive data on Chinese older adults. The inclusion criteria for this study focused on older adults aged 60 and above. After excluding cases with missing data on key study variables, the final sample comprised 9,883 older adults, with an average age of 71.40 years (SD = 6.53). Descriptive statistics of the research sample are summarized in Table  . 

## Measurements

## Dependent variable
The dependent variable, depressive symptoms, was measured using the 9-item shortened version of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D), which was revised by Santor and Coyne [ ]. This instrument has been validated and widely employed in prior research examining depressive symptoms in older populations, including in Chinese cultural contexts [ , , , ], demonstrating sound construct validity. Participants rated the frequency of experiencing each symptom (e.g., depressed mood, loneliness, poor appetite, restless sleep) over the past week on a 3-point scale ranging from 0 (rarely or none of the time) to 2 (often). The three positively phrased items were reverse-scored. The total scores were summed ranging from 0 to 18, with higher scores indicating greater severity of depressive symptoms. Notably, the scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency within this sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.680). An item-omission analysis indicated that the alpha values remained stable (ranging from 0.62 to 0.69) after sequential exclusion of each item, indicating that no single item significantly influenced the scale’s reliability.

## Independent variable
Housing quality was assessed as a composite index based on respondents’ self-reported housing conditions across ten objective indicators. The questionnaire items included the following aspects: availability of facilities such as (a) an indoor bathroom, (b) an indoor toilet, (c) age-friendly sit-down toilet equipment, (d) wireless Internet service, (e) handrail installations, and (f) an emergency calling system; (g) presence of potential hazards such as tripping thresholds or uneven floors; (h) access to utilities including gas/natural gas/biogas and (i) running water; and (j) adequacy of lighting. Each item was dichotomously scored (yes = 1, no = 0), following reverse-coding of the negatively phrased items. We employed Kuder-Richardson-20 to assess the internal consistency of the scale, yielding a reliability coefficient of 0.734, suggesting acceptable consistency among the items. A composite housing quality score was subsequently constructed by equally weighting each relevant indicator, with higher values indicating superior overall housing quality.
The availability of community facilities was assessed based on the presence of five commonly utilized amenities within the respondent’s community, which include senior activity rooms, fitness centers, chess (card) rooms, libraries, and outdoor playgrounds. To measure the availability of community facilities, we employed an equally weighted summation of these five items. The resulting total score ranged from 0 to 5, where higher values indicated greater accessibility to community facilities. This approach is consistent with prior studies in gerontological research [ ]. The scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.703).

## Mediating variable
Social participation was assessed by measuring the frequency of engagement in four offline social activities: attendance at senior colleges or educational courses, participation in mahjong (chess or card) games, engagement in singing or musical instrument playing, and taking part in square dancing. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (almost every day). The scores obtained from these assessments were summed, with a higher composite score indicating a greater level of social participation.

## Covariates
The study included a range of demographic and socioeconomic variables as confounding variables. These included age (continuous), gender (male = 1, female = 0), hukou (urban = 1, rural = 0), educational attainment (1 = illiteracy to 4 = high school or above), and living arrangement (living with children and spouse = 1, with spouse only = 0). Health-related covariates included activities of daily living (ADL) limitation ( at least one limitation = 1, no limitation = 0), chronic conditions (presence of at least one condition, including hypertension, heart problems, diabetes, and pneumonia, coded as 1; none = 0), self-rated health (good or above = 1, fair or below = 0), and smoking status (smoker = 1, non-smoker = 0).

## Statistical analyses
Descriptive statistics were conducted to summarize participant characteristics, with means and standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. To examine the association between residential environment (housing quality and community facilities) and depressive symptoms, as well as the mediating pathway of social participation, we conducted a series of multivariable linear regression analyses following the three-step approach proposed by Baron and Kenny [ ]. All models were adjusted for the full set of covariates described above. To test the indirect pathway, we applied the KHB decomposition method, which allows for the decomposition of total associations into direct and indirect components in models with binary or continuous variables and facilitates coefficient comparisons across nested nonlinear models [ ]. Heterogeneity analyses were then conducted to explore associations between specific components of housing quality and community facilities and depressive symptoms. All analyses were performed using Stata Software 17.0 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA).

## Results

## Descriptive statistics
The descriptive statistics of the participants are presented in Table  . Notably, male participants constituted 50.32% of the sample, and over one-third reported living with children. Approximately 85% of the participants rated their health as good, nearly half held non-agricultural hukou, and about half reported engaging in social activities. The mean depressive symptoms score was 6.78 (SD = 3.27) in the study.

## Regression results
As shown in Table  , after adjusting for covariates, both better housing quality and greater availability of community facilities were inversely associated with depressive symptoms among older adults (β = −0.093, p < 0.001; β = −0.074, p < 0.001, respectively). From a clinical perspective, a one-standard-deviation (SD) increase in housing quality was associated with an approximately 9.3% reduction in depressive symptoms relative to the mean, reflecting a modest yet meaningful difference in depressive symptoms. Similarly, a one-SD increase in community facilities corresponded to a 7.4% decrease in depressive symptoms. These results suggest that improvements in residential conditions may have clinically appreciable associations with older adults’ depressive symptom severity. 
As illustrated in Model 2 and Model 3 (Table  ), social participation was found to be a clinically relevant factor in the association between residential environment and depressive symptoms. Specifically, Model 3 indicates that both housing quality and community facilities retained a significant negative association with depressive symptoms, a relationship partially accounted for by social participation, as the direct effects of residential environment factors on depressive symptoms remain statistically significant even after including the social participation into the model. In other words, an improved residential environment is associated with greater social participation among older adults, which is in turn associated with lower depressive symptoms, suggesting an indirect and potentially intervention-targetable associational pathway. The KHB test indicated that the indirect pathway via social participation for housing quality and community facilities was found to be 24.66% and 27.18%, respectively (Table  ). These proportions suggest social participation as a clinically meaningful mechanism linking residential environments to depressive symptoms. In Model 4 (neighborhood fixed effects), housing quality (β = −0.063, p < 0.01) and community facilities (β = −0.075, p < 0.01) remained significantly negative, showing only minimal coefficient reduction while retaining statistical significance. The indirect pathway via social participation also persisted. These findings confirm that the associations are robust to unobserved neighborhood heterogeneity. 

## Heterogeneity analysis
In the heterogeneity analysis, we disaggregated the ten indicators of housing quality into five conceptually distinct components, namely sanitary facilities (including bathroom, indoor toilet, and sit-down toilet), basic supply infrastructure (including water and gas supply), safety and assistive facilities (including handrails and emergency calling system), comfortable and safe environment (including balanced floor and adequate lighting), and Internet access. We then investigated the domain-specific associations with depressive symptoms to identify which housing attributes were significantly associated with mental health. The results revealed that, after controlling for confounding variables, the availability of better sanitary facilities (β = −0.118, p < 0.001), a more comfortable and safe indoor environment (β = −0.331, p < 0.001), and Internet access (β = −0.645, p < 0.001) were significantly associated with lower depressive symptoms. These results imply that improving environmental comfort and digital inclusion may have clinical implications for reducing older adults’ depressive symptoms. Interestingly, a significant positive association between the safety and assistive facilities and depressive symptoms was found in this demographic (Table  ). Clinically, this counterintuitive finding may reflect reverse causality, whereby older individuals with pre-existing depressive symptoms or greater functional impairment are more likely to have assistive devices installed. 
Furthermore, we explored the association between five distinct community facilities and depressive symptoms. After adjustment for all confounding variables, the presence of senior activity rooms (β = −0.809, p < 0.001), and chess and mahjong rooms (β = −0.147, p < 0.05) was significantly and inversely associated with depressive symptoms (Table  ). The results highlight that both senior activity and chess and mahjong rooms may hold clinical potential as community resources for promoting mental health. In the unadjusted model, access to fitness facilities and outdoor activity areas was significantly associated with fewer depressive symptoms. However, these associations were no longer statistically significant after adjusting for potential confounding variables. 

## Discussion
The findings of this study significantly enhance our understanding of how residential environments are associated with the mental health of older adults in China, utilizing the 2020 wave of the CLASS dataset. The results underscore the critical role that residential factors, such as housing quality and community facilities, play in shaping depressive symptoms among older adults. By examining both direct associations and the mediating pathway of social participation, the present study contributes to the current understanding of the underlying mechanisms. The results indicate that housing quality was significantly associated with the mental health of older adults, with superior housing conditions, characterized by adequate maintenance, bigger living spaces, and modern amenities, being significantly associated with reduced depressive symptoms. This finding aligns with previous research that emphasizes the physical and psychological advantages of residing in well-maintained dwellings [ , , – , , , , ]. Quality housing is linked to physical comfort and a sense of security, both of which are important for maintaining mental health. Furthermore, the heterogeneity analysis presented in the study offers valuable insights into the association between specific housing features and depressive symptoms. Notably, the availability of better sanitary facilities, a comfortable and safe indoor environment, Internet access emerged as essential factors that contributed positively to the mental health of older adults. These findings echo Krieger and Higgins’ [ ] emphasis on accessible and functional housing designs as foundational for supporting mental well-being in aging populations. Sanitary facilities designed for those with mobility impairments, for instance, are associated with reduced accident risks and the associated psychological risks. At the same time, Internet access fosters social connection with family, friends, and community resources, thereby addressing social isolation—a key determinant of depressive symptoms among older adults. The presence of these housing features is associated with diminished fall risk, enhanced mobility, and stronger social connectedness, each of which correlates with reduced levels of depressive symptoms. Community facilities, as an important component of the residential environment, were significantly associated with the mental health of older adults. Access to amenities such as healthcare services, recreational spaces, and fitness centers supported greater levels of social engagement and reduced feelings of loneliness, which correlated with fewer depressive symptoms [ , , , ]. These findings align with the ecological model of aging, which highlights the importance of environmental resources in promoting healthy aging [ , , , , , – ]. Furthermore, heterogeneity analysis suggested that the availability of senior activity rooms, and chess and mahjong rooms was inversely associated with depressive symptoms, which indicates that social engagement and leisure activities played a crucial role in maintaining the mental health of older adults. These findings align with previous research highlighting the importance of social connections in promoting the mental health of older adults [ , , , ]. While the initial crude model indicated a significant association between the presence of fitness facilities and outdoor activity areas and a reduction in depressive symptoms, this relationship did not retain statistical significance after adjusting for confounding variables. This finding suggests that other factors, such as personal preferences, physical capabilities, or social capital, may play a more dominant role in determining the impact of these facilities on mental health. Conversely, the positive association between the availability of a library and depressive symptoms is counterintuitive and requires careful interpretation. It is possible that the libraries in the study communities may not have been adequately designed to cater to the needs and preferences of older adults, leading to feelings of isolation or irrelevance. Overall, this heterogeneity indicates that not all community facilities uniformly benefit older adults’ mental health, underscoring the necessity for targeted community infrastructure investments that prioritize socially interactive spaces to ameliorate geriatric mental health more effectively.
Furthermore, the study found that the association between residential environment and depressive symptoms was partially explained by the association with social participation. This finding is consistent with previous studies suggesting that neighborhood environment is associated with greater willingness for social engagement, which is associated with improved mental health outcomes [ , , , , , ]. Social participation is associated with opportunities for social interaction, support, and a sense of belonging, which are correlated with reduced levels of depressive symptoms [ , , , , ]. A systematic review further corroborated the significant positive association of social participation and older adults’ well-being [ ]. However, the interpretation of our findings should be contextualized within the early pandemic period. During this period, online social participation emerged as an important alternative for maintaining social connections. Our measure of social participation, which excluded Internet-based activities, may therefore not fully capture the social engagement strategies. Research found that technology-based social interactions could improved the quality and quantity of social connection and reduced social isolation [ ]. Overall, the findings suggest that a favorable residential environment not only provides comfortable living spaces but also fosters abundant social opportunities, thereby reducing the risk of depressive symptoms.
Residential environment can be associated with older individuals’ mental health outcomes through complex psychosocial and behavioral mechanisms [ ]. Specifically, the observed association between housing quality and mental health may be statistically accounted for by its concurrent associations with factors such as physical functioning, sleep quality, and chronic stress levels. Community facilities were associated with better mental health through facilitating physical activity, fostering social participation, and obtaining neighborhood social capital. Through interactions and communication with others, older adults can obtain social support and share life experiences, thereby diminishing depressive symptoms [ , ]. The potential moderators in the relationship include individuals’ demographic (e.g., age, gender, marital status), economic (e.g., socioeconomic status), and social factors (e.g., social support), which may either strengthen or attenuate these associations [ ]. These findings warrants future studies to explore these intricate relationships and formulate targeted interventions aiming to improve the health and well-being of older adults.

## Policy implications and limitations
The present study holds some practical implications. Policymakers and health professionals should prioritize the further development of age-friendly residential environment that not only provide adequate and comfortable housing but also offer rich social and recreational neighborhood resourses for older adults. First, housing quality improvement initiatives should be prioritized. Targeted age-friendly housing renovation programs can be implemented for older adults, focusing on basic amenities and accessibility, as the study highlighted that superior housing quality is significantly associated with lower depressive symptoms both directly and indirectly via its association with social participation. Second, community infrastructure development merits investment. This involves renovating and investing in community centers, playgrounds, parks, green spaces, and transportation networks. These improvements are likely to enhance daily life convenience and foster social interactions, which are associated with improved mental health. Third, strategies to enhance older adults’ social participation are important. Policy interventions should support community-based activities tailored to older adults’ various needs, as the findings confirm that social engagement is an important associational pathway, connecting improvements in the residential environment to enhanced mental health outcomes. These comprehensive strategies address the links between residential environments and depressive symptoms, offering actionable pathways to support older adults’ mental health.
Despite these contributions, this study was not devoid of several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design cannot fully address reverse causality or reciprocal relationships. Future longitudinal and quasi-experimental studies are therefore encouraged to rigorously elucidate these associations and illuminate the potential reciprocal interplay between residential environment, social participation, and depressive symptoms. Second, reliance on self-reported measurements may introduce response biases, as some older adults may either overestimate or underestimate their symptoms and participation behaviors. Finally, while the present study’s focus on offline social participation delineates the boundaries of the current investigation, it is worth noting that the pandemic may have amplified older individuals’ dependence on their immediate residential surroundings. This finding emphasizes the importance of future research to incorporate both online and offline dimensions of social participation while exploring the association between residential environment and mental health outcomes in post-pandemic settings.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, this study highlighted a significant association between the housing quality and community facilities and depressive symptoms in older adults. This association was partially explained by social participation: more favorable living conditions fostered greater social engagement, which in turn was associated with lower levels of depressive symptoms. Policymakers and community planners are encouraged to incorporate these insights into the development of age-friendly residential environments aimed at promoting the mental health of older adults.
