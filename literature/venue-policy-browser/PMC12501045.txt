## ABSTRACT

## Background
To reverse the epidemic of unhealthy eating and related chronic disease in the United States, intervening early in life is essential.

## Objective
Identify features of community‐ and school‐based programmes, policies and environments related to child intake of fruits and vegetables (FV) and sugar‐sweetened beverages (SSB).

## Methods
Dietary intake of children 4–15 years old (n = 5138) from elementary and middle schools in 130 US communities was collected by survey (of parent and/or child) in 2013–2015. Features of community programs and policies (CPPs) and school environments were collected by staff surveys, direct observation, key informant interviews, and/or document review. Community characteristics included socioeconomic status and predominant race/ethnicity. Classification and regression trees identified CPP and school environment features related to child dietary intake.

## Results
Community sociodemographic characteristics were the first‐selected variables related to child intakes. Children in communities with lower socioeconomic status and larger proportions of African Americans tended to have lower FV and higher SSB intakes. Associations of dietary intake with race/ethnicity varied with SES. CPPs that addressed certain social determinants of health were related to higher FV intake among community race/ethnicity–SES sub‐groups. Few other CPPs and no school environment characteristics were related to child FV or SSB intake.

## Conclusion
Findings suggest the importance of addressing social determinants of health such as education, poverty and transportation to reduce disparities in dietary intake, and the need to tailor interventions to community characteristics. More research is needed to explore the interaction of race/ethnicity and SES on dietary intake and intervention effectiveness.

## Results
All 9689 CPPs had complete data for the CART models that examined CPP characteristics in relation to community‐level intake of FV (cup/day) and SSBs (tsp/day of added sugars) by children. Of the 5138 children in the HCS dataset, 5035 had complete data that examined school characteristics in relation to individual‐level intake of FV, and 5077 had complete data for the model that examined individual‐level SSB intake. The mean age of the study sample of children was 9.3, range 4–15. The mean percentage of boys in each community was 48.2, range 20.0–83.3. Over half of the study schools were high poverty (≥ 75% of the student body eligible for free or reduced‐price meals).

## 
CPP Regression Tree for Child FV Intake at the Community Level
The regression tree examining the relationship between CPP and community sociodemographic characteristics and community‐level FV intake had 10 splits resulting in 11 terminal groupings of CPPs (Figure  ). The average of the standardised residuals (or average Z‐score) for FV intake in the lowest/middle SES communities was −0.2 (66% of CPPs) compared to 0.39 for the highest SES communities (34% of CPPs).
Six CPP characteristics were associated with FV intake in various subpopulations that included from 2% to 56% of the CPPs. In the lowest/middle SES communities, if the CPP addressed education as a social determinant of health (SDOH), FV intake was higher (−0.029; 10% of CPPs) than if the CPP did not (−0.23; 56% of CPPs). In the lowest/middle SES communities where the CPP did not address education, but did address poverty, the FV intake was higher (−0.0086; 4% of the CPPs) than for those CPPs that addressed neither education nor poverty (−0.24; 53% of CPPs). Examples of SDOH‐Education include ‘agriculture and food education’ and ‘gardening instructions/education’. Examples of SDOH‐Poverty include expansion or implementation of the Summer Meal Program and expansion of SNAP benefits or redemption sites. In the lowest/middle SES communities where the CPP addressed neither education nor poverty, if the CPP goals addressed only nutrition but not physical activity, FV intake was higher (−0.22; 47% of CPPs) than for CPPs with goals that included both nutrition and physical activity (−0.41; 6% of CPPs). Among lowest/middle SES communities where the CPP did not address education nor poverty, and goals addressed both nutrition and physical activity, if the CPP behavioural objective included decreasing consumption of sugary drinks, FV intake was higher (−0.1; 2% of CPPs) than if this was not a behavioural objective (−0.57; 4% of CPPs).
In the lowest SES, predominantly Hispanic communities where the CPP did not address education or poverty and the goals addressed nutrition but not physical activity, if the CPP reach was higher (reach score ≥ 0.33), FV intake was higher (0.32; 2% of CPPs) than if the reach was lower (reach score < 0.33) (−0.18; 8% of CPPs).
In the highest SES communities, FV intake was higher (0.54; 28% of CPPs) in communities that were predominantly White than if they were predominantly Hispanic or African American (−0.44; 5% of CPPs). In the highest SES, predominantly White communities, FV intake was higher (1.1; 1% of CPPs) if transportation was a social determinant of health addressed by the CPP than if it was not (0.52; 27%). Examples of SDOH‐Transportation include: enhancements to the built environment which improve walkability or bikeability; trail creation, expansion or networking; complete streets or land use policies/plans; and other efforts related to bike lanes.

## School Regression Tree for Child FV Intake at the Individual Level
The regression tree examining the relationship between school environment and community sociodemographic characteristics and individual‐level FV intake had two splits resulting in three terminal groupings of children (Figure  ). Children in the highest SES communities had higher FV intake (0.084; 35% of children) than those in the lowest/middle‐income communities (−0.046; 65% of children). Among the highest SES communities, children in predominately White communities had higher FV intake (0.13; 30% of children) than those in predominately Hispanic or African American communities (−0.19; 5% of children). None of the included school environment characteristics were associated with FV intake.

## 
CPP Regression Tree for Child SSB Intake at the Community Level
The regression tree examining the relationship between CCP and community sociodemographic characteristics and community‐level sugar intake from sweetened beverages had 8 splits resulting in 9 terminal groupings of CPPs (Figure  ). Children in the highest SES communities had the lowest standardised residuals for SSB intake (−0.63; 34% of CPPs). In middle SES communities, children in predominantly Hispanic communities had slightly lower SSB intake (−0.17; 8% of CPPs) than those in predominantly White communities (−0.16; 19% of CPPs) and those in predominantly African American communities had the highest SSB intake (0.66, 4% of CPP). In the lowest SES communities, children in predominantly White, Hispanic and African American communities had SSB intakes of 0.11 (7% of CPPs), 0.31 (14% of CPPs) and 1.2 (15% of CPPs), respectively.
Two CPP characteristics were associated with SSB intake but only in small sub‐populations that included 1%–11% of the CPPs. In the lowest SES predominantly African American communities, if the CPP involved working with youth organisations, children had lower SSB intake (0.83; 3% of CPPs) than if the CPP did not (1.3; 11% of CPPs). In the lowest SES, predominantly African American communities where the CPP did not target youth organisations, if the CPP affected community organisations, children had lower SSB intake (0.77; 1% of CPPs) than if the CPP did not (1.4; 10% of CPPs).

## School Regression Tree for Child SSB Intake at the Individual Level
The regression tree examining the relationship between school environment and community sociodemographic characteristics and individual‐level sugar intake from sweetened beverages had 5 splits resulting in 6 terminal groupings of children (Figure  ). In this model, only measures of community and school SES were associated with SSB intake. Children in the highest SES communities had the lowest SSB intake (−0.27; 35% of children) followed by children in the middle SES communities (0.0086; 33% of children). In the lowest SES communities, if the proportion of students approved for free school meals was less than half, the children had a lower SSB intake (0.082; 8% of children) than in communities where half or more qualified (0.36; 25% of children). In the lowest SES communities where half or more students qualified for free school meals, SSB intake was 0.6 (8% of children), 0.26 (13% of children) and 0.15 (4% of children) in predominantly African American, Hispanic and White communities, respectively.

## Discussion
This study aimed to identify combinations of characteristics of nutrition‐related CPPs and school environments related to children's intake of fruits and vegetables (FV) and sugar from sweetened beverages (SSB). Several CPP characteristics, primarily those categorised as addressing SDOH, but no school environment characteristics, were related to dietary intake for certain sub‐groups.
Community SES and race/ethnicity were included in the models in order to determine if and how the associations between dietary intake and CPP or school characteristics might differ by community profile. Community SES and race/ethnicity emerged as the most consistent and first‐selected variables related to child dietary intake. Consistent with other studies, higher SES and predominately white communities tended to have higher FV intake and lower SSB intake. These findings align with existing literature on the influence of place and structural barriers on dietary intake [ ]. People living in lower‐income and communities of colour often experience lower access to healthy foods, lower food quality and greater exposure to unhealthy foods and unhealthy food marketing than higher income and predominantly White communities [ ]. Although many studies have found similar results, most of these studies were based on surveys only of individuals without capturing comprehensive information on community efforts to improve nutrition. This study extends these findings by establishing that similar relationships exist between dietary intake and community‐level SES and race/ethnicity. These relationships persisted even in these models that included a very large number of independent variables, suggesting that community efforts to the date of the study had not been able to fully overcome the stronger SDOH.
The study also revealed interactions between SES and race/ethnicity in dietary intake. Differences in FV intake by race/ethnicity were only observed in our study among higher‐income communities. Consistent with our findings, Dubowitz et al. found that community SES had twice the effect size for FV intake among White individuals than among African American or Mexican American individuals, suggesting that people of colour may encounter more barriers than White individuals to improving FV intake in affluent communities [ ]. For SSB intake, race and ethnicity differences emerged primarily among low‐ and middle‐income communities. Campaigns to reduce SSB consumption may have been more effective in higher‐income communities, where residents face fewer financial constraints, experience less exposure to SSB marketing, have more agency to adopt healthier behaviours, and have greater trust in tap water [ , ]. Contrary to our findings, at least one study found that racial/ethnic disparities in SSB intake persisted across SES groups [ ]. Further research is needed to better understand how SES and race/ethnicity interact to shape dietary behaviours.
Among CPPs, addressing SDOH (SDOH‐education, ‐poverty and ‐transportation) was the first‐selected CPP characteristic related to FV intake. CPPs that addressed education and poverty were related to FV intake only among the lowest/middle income communities. The SDOH‐poverty CPPs primarily expanded access to supplemental food programs and may thereby provide needed economic support for FV consumption in lower SES settings [ ]. Although results vary by program, other studies have also found associations between supplemental food program participation and fruit and vegetable intake [ , ]. Nutrition education that includes an agriculture or gardening component, a common SDOH‐education strategy in this study, has also been shown to increase fruit and vegetable intake [ ]. The experiential component may increase nutrition education effectiveness [ ], and may be most important in low‐income communities with less access to a variety of fresh quality produce and gardening opportunities [ ]. Interestingly, CPPs focused on transportation emerged as important predictors in higher‐SES communities. Improved transportation infrastructure may help reduce time constraints on parents, allowing for more opportunities to purchase and prepare healthy foods. Alternatively, built environment efforts to increase physical activity may be more common in communities that support healthy eating.
Lower‐level variables associated with FV intake included CPPs with goals that focused exclusively on nutrition (rather than combining nutrition with physical activity), CPPs aimed at reducing SSB consumption, and CPPs that reached larger proportions of the community. Greater population reach would logically contribute to a greater effect if intensity is not correspondingly reduced [ ]. It is less obvious why the first two would affect FV intake. It is possible that combining nutrition and physical activity goals reduces the intensity of nutrition‐specific efforts, thereby limiting their impact. Programs targeting SSB reduction may also indirectly promote healthier dietary behaviours, as health‐related behaviours often cluster within individuals [ ].
In contrast to FV intake, no CPP characteristics were among the first few selected predictors of SSB intake. Working with youth and community organisations, however, was associated with lower SSB consumption among children in low‐SES, predominantly African American communities. This finding suggests that community partnerships may be particularly important for addressing SSB consumption in these settings. The absence of broader associations between CPPs and SSB intake could reflect limitations in program implementation or reach. Many communities may not have employed the most effective strategies for reducing SSB intake, such as price increases, targeted restrictions on purchases, labelling regulations, or efforts to counter SSB marketing [ ]. Built environment factors, such as proximity to fast‐food outlets and convenience stores, have also been linked to higher SSB intake and warrant further investigation [ ]. Similarly, no characteristics of the school nutrition environment were associated with FV or SSB intake. One explanation may be that the school environments within the sample lacked sufficient variation to produce differences in dietary behaviours. More effective interventions may have occurred since the data for this study were collected. Alternatively, community‐level factors such as food access and marketing—variables not measured in this study—may exert stronger influences on children's dietary intake than school‐level practices and environments.
This study builds upon previously published analyses of HCS data that examined relationships between dietary intake and certain aspects of CPPs [ , ]. These previous studies did not include any school environment variables and used CPP indices based on a limited number of the available CPP characteristics. The CART analyses used in the present study made it possible to examine the full complement of relevant CPP and school data, and to examine these relationships in specific community demographic sub‐groups. The present study results therefore complement the previous findings that more healthy dietary behaviours were associated with higher numbers and intensity of CPPs, and CPPs that addressed more strategies and more behaviours [ ]. Some of these relationships were modified by region and urbanicity and, to a lesser extent, by community‐level race/ethnicity and income [ ].
Limitations of this study include the cross‐sectional design, which precludes making causal inferences. While CPPs were assessed retrospectively over a ten‐year period, they were compared to current child diets rather than changes in intakes over time. The sample, although diverse, was not nationally representative, limiting generalizability. Despite adjusting for age, the results may not apply equally to all age groups in the rather wide age range of the study sample. Oversampling of predominantly African American and Hispanic communities increased our ability to examine associations in those communities, but limited our ability to draw conclusions for communities of other racial/ethnic compositions. Additionally, CPPs were self‐reported by community informants, which may have introduced recall error and social desirability bias. Although a validated protocol was used, dietary intake was also self‐reported and only select dietary components were examined, which may not fully capture the complexity of children's diets. This study only examined those nutrition‐related CPPs and school environments that occurred in the sampled communities. There may be effective interventions that were not tried or not present in large enough numbers to be detected in this analysis. Furthermore, since these data were collected 10–12 years ago, it would not have examined more recent and potentially more effective interventions. Community food access was not measured as a part of this study, but may have influenced observed differences in dietary intake.

## Conclusion
Overall, the study found that children's dietary intake was most strongly associated with community SES, racial/ethnic composition and CPPs addressing SDOH. These findings underscore the importance of addressing broader social and economic inequities to improve dietary intake and reduce disparities. This does not negate the value of targeted nutrition or physical activity interventions. In fact, some types of nutrition education were associated with improved FV intake. But the findings suggest that additional structural changes addressing SDOH may be necessary for long‐term, population‐level improvements in dietary behaviours. Study findings align with equity‐oriented obesity prevention frameworks, such as Kumanyika's comprehensive approach, which emphasises increasing access to healthy options, reducing barriers, improving social and economic resources and building community capacity [ ]. Specifically, these findings suggest that interventions that address education (e.g., agriculture, gardening and food education); poverty (e.g., expanded access to supplemental food programs); and transportation (e.g., connectivity, walkability, bikeability and complete streets) are promising, at least in communities with certain race/ethnicity and SES profiles. The finding that the prediction of dietary intake by CPPs varied by community sociodemographic characteristics reinforces the idea that interventions must be tailored to the specific needs of different communities. Prior research similarly highlights that a one‐size‐fits‐all approach is unlikely to succeed in promoting healthy eating and active living [ , ]. The observed interaction of community SES with race/ethnicity on dietary intake also supports the need to consider community characteristics in intervention approaches.
