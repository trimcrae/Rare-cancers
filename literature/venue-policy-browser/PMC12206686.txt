## Abstract

## Background
Nurses' critical input and firsthand disaster management experience are vital to healthcare quality.

## Aim
This study aims to comprehend nurses' experiences during and after the earthquakes, their coping mechanisms for posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and the consequent effects on their profession, given the nurses' crucial role in disaster response.

## Methods
A descriptive qualitative study using semistructured interviews was conducted between April and May 2024. Fifteen nurses were selected using criterion and snowball sampling methods from those working as earthquake survivor nurses in the affected areas. The COREQ guidelines for reporting qualitative research were followed.

## Findings
Five main themes were: emotional reactions/physical difficulties, career and motivation, PTSD and well‐being, suggestions, and work performance. The results highlighted the significant psychological and professional challenges that nurses encounter, including the effects of severe PTSD symptoms, coping strategies, and the influence on their capacity to deliver care.

## Conclusions
Nurses dealing with earthquakes face unique challenges that test their resilience on all levels. Learning from their experiences and coping mechanisms can help healthcare systems support these frontline workers better. Investing in disaster preparedness and mental health resources for nurses is crucial for their well‐being and the overall strength of the healthcare system during crises.

## Implications for nursing and health policy
Effectively managing workloads and rest periods can reduce physical fatigue and prevent burnout. Shift rotations and adequate staffing levels are necessary. Access to psychological mental health support is vital for nurses to recover from the psychological effects of disasters. Regular disaster preparedness training is imperative for physical safety and psychological resilience. Robust support systems, including safe accommodation and peer support groups, are crucial for nurses in disaster response.

Keywords: Coping mechanisms, disaster preparedness, disaster response, earthquake, healthcare professionals, nurses, posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), qualitative research, Turkey

## INTRODUCTION
Natural and artificial disasters have historically caused significant harm to human populations, resulting in fatalities, injuries, and societal disruptions (Li et al., ). In the past decade, natural disasters like earthquakes, tsunamis, and floods have affected over 2.6 billion people, straining medical resources and causing substantial casualties (Rezaei et al., ). On February 6, 2023, Turkey experienced a major disaster when two powerful earthquakes near Kahramanmaraş, measuring 7.7 and 7.6 in magnitude, struck. The aftermath was devastating, with 50 783 fatalities and 115 353 injuries across 11 provinces. Approximately 14 million people were impacted, and 37 984 buildings collapsed, marking Turkey's most significant earthquake in the 21st century (AFAD, ).
Nurses in emergency settings face significant challenges, including conflicts, disasters, and pandemics, requiring diverse competencies to manage complex patient needs. These include handling multiple traumas and stabilizing critically injured patients (Said & Chiang, ). During mass trauma events, nurses need not only clinical skills but also coping strategies for responders and survivors. Nurses must be prepared to care for individuals needing hospitalization after traumatic incidents. Both physical and psychological readiness are essential for supporting victims and themselves (Said et al., ). However, natural disasters, such as earthquakes, pose significant challenges for healthcare practitioners, especially nurses, who frequently serve as emergency primary responders. The psychological impact on nurses during and after an earthquake can substantially influence their mental health and capacity to fulfill their professional responsibilities (Groves et al., ). Psychological preparation, despite general disaster preparation, should focus on self‐efficacy, self‐esteem, and optimism while addressing PTSD and anxiety. Yet, the complex situation of being both a victim and a caregiver creates considerable stress for nurses, who are required to manage their trauma while simultaneously providing care to others.
The psychological effects of navigating this dual role are significant and intricate, highlighting the importance of this subject in disaster nursing. Disaster psychology examines the mental health of individuals and communities affected by disasters, focusing on frontline healthcare professionals like nurses. It addresses the acute stress, trauma, and burnout they face in crises. Recent research highlights the importance of supporting nurses' psychological health to enhance resilience (Mert & Koksal, ) and job satisfaction, ultimately improving care standards (Labrague, ; Shechter et al., ). Thus, understanding the need for psychological support in disaster preparedness and response is essential for the well‐being and effectiveness of nursing professionals.
Nurses are vital in disaster response as they are at the forefront of healthcare delivery. Their ability to offer immediate medical aid and emotional support to those impacted is essential in disaster management and recovery efforts (Akbari et al., ). However, nurses are frequently ill‐prepared for such situations despite their importance in crisis management (Al Harthi et al., ). When nurses become survivors of such disasters, their experiences are multifaceted and deeply complex. They must navigate their trauma while fulfilling their professional responsibilities. This dual role can lead to significant emotional and psychological stress, compounded by the chaotic and resource‐constrained environments that typically follow natural disasters. Understanding how nurses cope with these challenges is crucial for improving support systems and ensuring they can effectively perform their duties during such crises (Mavrouli et al., ).
This study investigates the firsthand experiences of nurses who endured earthquakes in Turkey on February 6, 2023. The primary research questions are: (1) What were the nurses' experiences during and after the earthquakes? (2) How did these nurses handle PTSD following the earthquakes? (3) How did their experiences and the earthquakes affect their nursing practice? Through qualitative research, we aim to gain insights into their emotional reactions, professional obstacles, coping mechanisms, and personal strengths. We can identify common themes and patterns highlighting these healthcare professionals' unique challenges and strengths by digging into the stories of nurses working in earthquake‐affected regions. The thematic and content analysis examined the data and found recurring themes and patterns.
Previous research has primarily focused on the broader impact of earthquakes on healthcare systems and patient outcomes (Aydin et al., ; Disel et al., ; Gökalp et al., ; Okuyama et al., ). However, there is a lack of studies examining nurses' experiences as survivors. This gap in the literature emphasizes the necessity for a more nuanced understanding of their dual roles. By addressing this gap, this study aims to develop targeted interventions that support the well‐being of nurses and enhance their ability to provide care in disaster‐stricken areas.
Moreover, this research is timely and relevant, considering the increasing frequency and intensity of natural disasters caused by climate change. As the global community confronts these challenges, we must equip our healthcare workforce with the necessary tools and support to manage and recover from such events. Enhancing our understanding of the experiences of nurse survivors can inform policies and practices that promote resilience and preparedness within the healthcare sector.
The stress and coping theory (Lazarus & Folkman, ), which examines how people cope with stress and adjust to difficult circumstances, is the theoretical foundation for this investigation. Research indicates that after a tragedy, nurses and other healthcare professionals are particularly vulnerable to developing PTSD. Organizational, interpersonal, and intrapersonal elements are factors that affect PTSD in nurses (Schuster & Dwyer, ). There is a substantial correlation between nurses' symptoms of PTSD and their exposure to stressful events (Shamia et al., ). The literature also revealed posttraumatic growth as a theme, which is a beneficial psychological shift brought about by overcoming challenging life situations (Mustikasari et al., ). Disaster response nurses must be psychologically resilient and have stress management training since they deal with unique obstacles. Health personnel involved in disaster response should receive the necessary training regarding psychological resilience, stress management, and emotional expression (Tahernejad et al., ).

## METHODS

## Design
Nurse researchers are responsible for establishing a transparent methodology to ensure the credibility of their research results. Content and thematic analysis are commonly used methods in nursing research. However, some critics question the reliability of these approaches. Researchers can comprehensively understand the subject matter by utilizing content and thematic analysis. Each method has unique benefits that complement each other, ultimately strengthening the validity and reliability of the research. Additionally, integrating these methods allows for more thorough exploration, with thematic analysis uncovering intricate details and content analysis revealing overarching patterns (Vaismoradi et al., ).
While combining thematic and content analysis has advantages, there are also drawbacks. This approach requires much effort and qualitative themes to align with quantitative trends. Content analysis helps quantify data but may remove personalization, potentially diluting nuanced meanings and interpretations. Researchers must address these challenges directly to maintain their research findings' reliability and validity (Elo & Kyngäs, ; Nowell et al., ).
The study focused on following guidelines for presenting qualitative research, as outlined by and Tong et al. ( ), to improve transparency, consistency, and reliability. The study diligently applied these considerations, demonstrating high sensitivity and meticulousness with the COREQ.

## Participants and data collection
Three male social scientists with Ph.D. degrees in organizational psychology conducted the research. They are not directly affiliated with the health discipline. One of the researchers' wives is an emergency nurse, and the other's wife is a lecturer at the nursing faculty. However, they have no direct relationship with the participants in the study. The researchers used criterion and snowball sampling methods to reach the earthquake victims' nurses who were still working in the earthquake‐stricken region. One of the researchers is also an earthquake survivor working in this area. Using personal contacts, one of the researchers reached seven participants. Through the acquaintances of these seven participants, the authors reached 13 more participants.
Invitation letters detailing the study's objectives were sent to them through the chiefs of the clinics. These nurses were unaware of the researchers' academic background. The selection of these 20 nurses was based on the criterion of having been affected by the earthquake and having been working in an area affected by the earthquake at the time of the first earthquake, which occurred on Monday, February 6, 2023, at 04:17 AM, and the second at 01:24 PM. This method facilitated a comprehensive grasp of the difficulties faced and provided a substantial data pool for the research. Through snowball sampling, the researchers reached 20 nurses working in Hatay (the most affected), Kahramanmaraş, and Osmaniye.
Only 15 nurses agreed to take part. Five nurses said they did not want to remember the moment of the earthquake and what happened afterward and did not participate in the research. Guest et al. ( ) stated that the sample size required for thematic analysis is 6–12 participants. These numbers are generally sufficient, especially in terms of thematic saturation. Hence, the researchers considered that 15 nurses were enough for the qualitative study.
The research involved 12 females and three males aged 24–48. Twelve were married, and 13 had children. Most (9) had bachelor's degrees, with three having a master's and two having associate degrees. Experience levels ranged from 2 to 30 years. Seven of the participants worked in Osmaniye, five in Kahramanmaraş, and three in Hatay when an earthquake occurred, and they are still working in these cities. Six worked in emergency, four in surgical care, three in anesthesia, and two in neonatal care units. Table  displays detailed information about the participants.
 

## Setting and rigor
A descriptive qualitative study was conducted using semistructured interviews. The interviews were carried out between April and May 2024, carefully considering the interview setting, timing, and location to ensure the highest level of rigor and reliability. Participants were free to choose the most convenient and comfortable time and place for their interviews, which included options such as hospitals, social spaces within hospitals, and cafes. The primary objective was to establish a comfortable atmosphere that would encourage participants to freely express themselves, thereby enhancing the credibility and trustworthiness of the data collected. By snowballing the sample, one of the researchers reached the participants, arranged the interviews, and conducted all interviews in three different cities. The interviews were conducted in person, with some audio recordings taken, using an interview guide that consisted of open‐ended questions. Field notes were also taken during the interviews and typed in Word by one of the authors. Typically, the interviews took place after nearly one year the earthquake occurred. Each session lasted between 60 and 90 minutes. Only the participants and the researcher were present during the interviews, ensuring a confidential and focused environment. Participants were asked to participate in a follow‐up interview 2–4 weeks after the initial one, allowing them to reconsider and adjust their responses based on new insights. They reacted positively during the follow‐up. Table  shows the interview questions, coded answers, and narrative explanations.
 

## Data analysis
During data analysis, three researchers used ATLAS.ti 9 for content analysis. They independently coded research questions with 75% consistency. The remaining 25% of discrepancies were resolved collaboratively. A frequency analysis determined answer composition and frequency. Researchers compared coding with 90% agreement, resolving the remaining 10% through discussion. Discrepancies were identified and resolved through consensus‐building discussions. Regular meetings were held to review and refine codes. A total of 65 codes were used in the analysis (Table  ).
To ensure reliability and replicability, the authors used several strategies. First, they employed thematic and content analysis together. Second, they kept records (raw data and field notes) of the research process. The authors independently coded research questions, and the agreed codes were discussed with some participants.

## Ethical considerations
The study was conducted under the principles of the Helsinki Declaration. The approval and institutional permission were obtained through the Ethics Committee Decision dated April 29, 2024, and decree no 2024/2/3, from the Osmaniye Korkut Ata University Social and Human Sciences Research Ethics Committee.
After explaining the study, an informed consent form was obtained from the participants who agreed to participate. In the informed consent form, it was emphasized that participation in the study was completely voluntary. It was stated that the participants had the right not to participate in the study or to leave the study at any time during participation. Information was given about the study's aim and methods and the possible benefits of this research for the voluntary participants.

## RESULTS
Five themes were created based on participants' feedback: Emotional reactions/physical difficulties, career and motivation, PTSD and well‐being, suggestions, and work performance.

## Emotional reactions/physical difficulties
In response to the initial query regarding the experiences of participants during the earthquake, respondents indicated that they primarily felt fear (19), heard a loud noise (15), experienced a concussion (12), felt panic (11), and felt desperation.
In response to the second question, participants predominantly reported difficulties related to the earthquake, including housing issues (33), fear of another quake (28), concern for the safety of their relatives (27), demanding work schedules and frequent night shifts (26), and insomnia (23).
In response to the seventh question, while all participants said they felt burnout (15), most thought of tiredness (14), reluctance (13), unmotivating (13), fear (8), and sleepiness (8). They felt that managers viewed nurses as emotionless robots (6). The following statement of Participant 2 highlighted the emotional situations and physical difficulties during the earthquake.

…my daughters were terrified; I tried to calm them down. I have petrified myself. …we prayed and waited for the earthquake to stop. When the earthquake started to slow down, I ran to the kitchen to get my bag, but I slipped and fell to the ground. My leg was very sore. My daughters picked me up, took my bag, and we went out quickly. I had a tough time getting out, and my leg was aching. It was raining very badly when we went outside. My little daughter tried to run in a panic, but in the meantime, her foot slipped, and she fell to the ground and hurt her foot. I quickly lifted my daughter with my aching leg and got into our car in the parking lot of the building. As soon as we got in, we were caught in the car in the second earthquake that came after the first earthquake 10 minutes later. …we thought the car would tip over. We experienced the second shock before I could get over the first shock. I didn't know what to do with the fear of the earthquake on the one hand and my leg and the screaming of my daughter, whose foot was hurt on the other hand. … the fear of the earthquake on the one hand and the pain of my leg and my daughter on the other hand, we could not come to ourselves for 1 hour out of panic.

## Career and motivation
In response to the question about whether their career goals and motivation changed after the earthquake, the participants stated that their work motivation decreased due to excessive workload and shifts (29) and that they were not motivated (13) and career goals (11) for the future, and that they experienced a general reluctance (11) and despondency (10). Participant 3 reported:

My goals were to continue my master's degree and become an academic, but I cannot find that energy in myself now. …My motivation changed a lot. …Seeing so many people dying and having to take care of so many injured people, we already postponed ourselves. Trying to give hope to my daughters, who were scared and timid, made me so tired that I couldn't even think about my career and future. Even my daughter, who was preparing for university, changed her perspective on life and couldn't think about anything because she lost her friends in the earthquake.

## PTSD and well‐being
All participants reported that they experienced PTSD (15), and they did not demand professional support (15) from their hospital or managers. Only five participants took professional support for their insomnia from private clinics. The participants indicated that the primary symptoms of PTSD were fear of losing loved ones (27), insomnia (23), feelings of sadness, vulnerability, uncertainty, and anxiety (15), and exhaustion (12). Most participants (12) have experienced these symptoms for one year. The strategy employed by the participants to mitigate the effects of PTSD involved identifying secure locations (12), spending time with family (7), engaging in prayer (6), and attempting to detach from the traumatic experience (6).
When participants compared their well‐being before and after the earthquake, 11 participants stated that it was worse than before, and two said it was the same level. Two participants thought that overcoming the consequences of the earthquake empowered them. Participant 14 stated about PTSD and well‐being, respectively:

There was panic, anxiety, and constant unhappiness. Palpitations, sweating, trembling, shortness of breath, I experienced all of these. We seem to be slowly recovering. My husband and I have sleep problems. We sleep in turns at night. And we sleep with the children. We never talk about the earthquake. I am worse [compared to before the earthquake], but I have been trying to stay at the same level for the last six months. If I get worse, I will become unable to do this profession. During the earthquake, the family and the patients we cared for in the hospital exhausted me a lot—[Hearing about] lost children. As I saw the destroyed families, I got cold from the profession. I became unable to empathize.

## Suggestions
The participants identified three key areas requiring attention. Firstly, the provision of safe accommodation (19). Secondly, there is a need for more coworkers to reduce workloads (15), and thirdly, compassionate leaving is essential (14). To mitigate the adverse impact of the earthquake, participants must receive support from managers (13), colleagues (13), and psychological professionals (13). Nurses need to feel valued (9), family/friends support (9), financial support (8), and the right to a compassionate transfer (7). They also stated that hospitals should first meet the needs of nurses (7) as survivors of the earthquake (e.g., hygiene, day off to care for their children and elderly) and secondly organize support programs (7) to boost nurses' morale.
Ten participants proposed a policy and practice recommendation to address the need for safe accommodation to reduce the negative impact of the earthquake on nurses. Providing psychological support (8), assigning more nurses (7), and morale support programs (4) were other policy and practice recommendations. Participant 7 stated:

The ministry and institutions should accept that nurses are earthquake victims. They behave as if nurses have not experienced the earthquake. They do not give any value and respect. We have experienced the same during the pandemic and have no value.

## Work performance
Most participants think their work performance (12) and motivation (11) have decreased in the last year. They feel exhausted (4) and tired (3) when working. Only three interviewed thought they were performing the same as before the earthquake. Participant 14 stated:

We've gotten worse. It has become unbearable, especially for those who dislike [nurses] profession. This profession, which we already do with a thousand sacrifices, has tired us during and after the earthquake. I can say that compassion fatigue has started. We lost many of our relatives overnight. I cannot continue with the same sense of compassion. We have seen so many shortcomings of the institutions and the state that we are both angry and sad.
Another finding is that gender and having children significantly affected nursing PTSD, perception of work performance, career goals, and motivations. Male and single (without children) nurses reported lower PTSD, higher work performance, and maintaining career goals than female and with children nurses.

## DISCUSSION
The results showed that nurses exposed to natural disasters such as major earthquakes experience these natural disasters' adverse psychological and physical effects for a long time.
During the earthquake, especially the fear of losing children and their helplessness are the factors that profoundly affect the nurses. The nurses caught in the quake while on duty cared for their patients without thinking about their own lives and the lives of their loved ones. They did their duty without knowing whether their relatives were alive and without receiving any news from them. This result aligns with previous research indicating that the acute stress experienced during disasters can lead to long‐term psychological trauma, including PTSD (Naushad et al., ). Lebel and Charette ( ) found that the simultaneous obligation of attending to patients and being concerned for their families intensifies this stress, leading to increased feelings of helplessness and anxiety. Farokhzadian et al. ( ) stated that the considerable emotional strains of tending to disaster victims, combined with their safety apprehensions, may result in significant mental health obstacles. The fear of losing loved ones, especially children, and the sense of helplessness during the earthquake were the most profound psychological stressors for the nurses.
After the earthquake, one of the most significant physical challenges faced by the participants was the lack of secure accommodation. Most participants were forced to reside in public spaces, including parks and cars, or one‐story houses in remote areas. While they could not find a safe place to stay, having to work was highly exhausting for the nurses. All these worries caused sleep problems, exhaustion, tiredness, and reluctance in the nurses. Similar to this study results, many healthcare facilities failed to provide adequate provisions, leaving nurses to fend for themselves during long shifts and demanding work hours during the Kermanshah earthquake (Abdi et al., ). Working in disaster‐affected areas often involves individuals enduring physical exertion, fatigue, and potential exposure to hazardous situations (Farokhzadian et al., ). After the earthquake, the lack of secure accommodation emerged as a significant physical challenge. This lack of stable housing not only affected their physical well‐being but also compounded their psychological stress.
Nurses did not receive professional support to eliminate the earthquake's harmful effects. They generally feel a sense of worthlessness. They try to reduce the adverse effects, especially with the support of relatives/family, but this situation partially relieves them and does not provide a definite solution. Salik et al. ( ) indicated that nurses utilized various coping mechanisms in response to the disaster in Turkey, including discussions with family and friends, deliberate efforts to minimize excessive rumination, and finding comfort in religious activities. Hugelius and Adolfsson ( ) noted that psychological support was crucial in delivering nursing services during disasters. This result underscores the critical need for comprehensive support systems for nursing professionals in the aftermath of natural disasters.
Nurses were forced to fulfill their duties with excessive work and no support for their problems. The severe trauma they experienced and the excessive workload adversely affected their professional motivation and career goals. Wijoyo et al. ( ) reported that the excessive amount of work and the emotional strain of handling trauma and distress resulted in burnout and compassion fatigue. These factors diminished the capacity of nurses to perform efficiently and uphold their health and wellness. Xue et al. ( ) stated that physical fatigue from working long hours without adequate rest or support affected the overall health of nurses and the quality of care they provided. The intense need for care after the earthquake increased the workload of nurses. The increased workload and the psychological and physical difficulties caused by the earthquake resulted in a “professional earthquake.”
All participants thought they had done their duties sacrificially, but in return, they generally did not receive the support they needed from their institutions (Ministry of Health and Hospitals). Studies reported that nurses who worked sacrificially during and after the earthquake (Çetinkaya Özdemir et al., ) experienced managerial and individual difficulties (Deniz Doğan et al., ; Taskiran & Baykal, ), resulting in exhaustion and fatigue (Grochtdreis et al., ). Addressing psychological and physical needs through institutional support can help mitigate the adverse effects on professional responsibilities and personal well‐being.

## Implications for nursing practice and policy
The authors explained nursing practice and policy entitled “Education, Practice, Management, and Policy.”

## Education
Integrate disaster preparedness and response training into nursing curricula and continuing education programs. These programs should cover psychological first aid, stress management, and coping strategies for personal and professional situations. Nursing schools and healthcare institutions should provide training on mental health awareness and resilience‐building techniques. They should educate nurses on recognizing signs of PTSD and other stress‐related conditions and on seeking help when needed.
Regular disaster preparedness training is crucial for both physical safety and psychological resilience. This training should include stress management techniques and emergency response protocols.

## Practice
Health institutions and nursing leadership should regularly schedule support group meetings facilitated by trained mental health professionals. By establishing support systems, they can create peer support groups and mentorship programs where nurses can share experiences, provide mutual support, and implement debriefing sessions after traumatic events.
Healthcare institutions and nursing managers should implement rotating schedules for sufficient recovery time between shifts. They can enforce policies that ensure nurses have adequate rest periods and time off after working in disaster situations and avoid mandatory overtime and excessive shift hours.

## Management
The Healthcare Administration and the Ministry of Health should provide comprehensive support, including psychological counseling, secure accommodation, and necessities for nurses working in disaster zones. They can establish dedicated crisis intervention teams that offer immediate and ongoing support to affected healthcare workers.
Hospital management should ensure clear and effective communication channels during and after disasters and provide regular updates to staff about the status of their families and the overall situation. They can use mobile apps to keep nurses informed and connected with their families during emergencies.

## Policy
The Ministry of Health and policymakers should develop and implement comprehensive disaster response policies that include provisions for healthcare workers' psychological and physical well‐being. Policies should mandate immediate mental health support and secure accommodation for healthcare workers in disaster‐affected areas.
Government and funding agencies should ensure adequate funding for disaster preparedness and response initiatives, including training programs, support services, and infrastructure improvements. They can allocate a budget for building resilient healthcare facilities and emergency response units.
In addressing the challenges faced by countries with limited resources (e.g., Turkey) in disaster situations, it is essential to prioritize and allocate resources effectively to enhance the outcomes for nurses and healthcare workers. Key strategies include developing cost‐effective training programs, such as low‐cost disaster preparedness modules and peer‐led initiatives, to strengthen collaboration and reduce costs.
Engaging community support through partnerships with nongovernmental organizations can provide crucial emotional and logistical assistance. Optimizing existing infrastructure by repurposing community facilities can facilitate immediate relief without incurring new construction costs.
Implementing basic psychological support programs, like mental health first aid training, can help alleviate distress among healthcare workers. Strengthening policies with clear disaster response protocols and low‐cost incentives can boost morale and efficiency.
International collaboration is vital for securing financial and technical support while learning from best practices in other low‐resource countries can improve local disaster response strategies. Promoting resilience through culturally appropriate practices and empowering local communities in disaster preparedness can further support healthcare workers.
In conclusion, efficient resource allocation should prioritize essential services and invest in low‐cost technology solutions to enhance communication and coordination during disasters, maximizing the impact of limited resources while addressing healthcare workers' needs.

## Limitations
Despite exciting findings, it is important to acknowledge limitations. The authors considered that the varying degrees of impact of the earthquake on the participants is a limitation. Self‐disclosed information can lead to biased results. The use of a retrospective approach could impact the precision of memory recall. Sampling methods may introduce bias in selection and constrain the ability to generalize findings. The study concentrated on earthquakes in Turkey in 2023, which may limit its relevance to other geographical areas. The distinctive sociopolitical and healthcare characteristics of Turkey could uniquely influence nurses' experiences.

## Future studies
The findings of this research are constrained by the particular data obtained from the participating nurses. The characteristics of the institutions where these nurses are employed, the specific regions they operated in during the earthquake, and their previous disaster‐related experiences may have shaped the study's outcomes. For example, the prior disaster experiences of the nurses could have influenced the results, as those with such backgrounds may possess enhanced preparedness and knowledge in crises, thereby restricting the applicability of the findings to a broader context.
Recruiting a more extensive and varied cohort of participants is advisable to mitigate these limitations in subsequent research. Incorporating nurses from diverse geographical locations, different healthcare facilities, and a range of disaster experience levels could enhance the generalizability of the results. Furthermore, a detailed evaluation of the circumstances under which nurses operated during the disaster would lead to more thorough and dependable findings. This strategy would enable the study's conclusions to be relevant to a larger population of nurses and provide more robust recommendations for disaster management practices within the healthcare sector.

## CONCLUSIONS
Nurses who endure earthquakes encounter distinctive obstacles that assess their emotional, psychological, and professional fortitude. By comprehending their encounters and the strategies they utilize to cope, healthcare systems can enhance their assistance to these frontline caregivers. It is imperative to allocate resources toward disaster preparedness and mental health support for nurses, as this is vital for their welfare and the overall resilience of the healthcare system in times of calamities.
