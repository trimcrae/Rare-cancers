Decompressive craniectomy (DC) is usually done in medically refractory intracranial hypertension and shows beneficial results, but not without significant complications (73.73%).[ ] Early complications of DC include bleeding, contusion, and hematoma, even requiring redo surgery (13%), whereas late complications include hydrocephalus, subdural hygroma, and sunken bone flap syndrome.[ ] Post-DC imaging can be done to look for complications if clinical signs and symptoms warrant it.[ ]
Non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT) scan is the most preferred imaging modality in patients with TBI. It allows us to visualize both brain parenchyma and bony structures. It is robust, reliable, and easy for clinicians to interpret. Due to short scanning times and widespread availability, it has become the ideal imaging modality in emergency neurosurgical conditions. Most neurosurgical emergencies, such as acute hemorrhage, ischemia, brain herniation, and hydrocephalus, can be detected well in computed tomography (CT) scans. Contrast CT may be needed in certain conditions.[ , , , ]
Ultrasound is used in neurocritical care setups for central venous cannulation, as point-of-care imaging for lungs, heart, and other organs.[ ] In neurosurgical patients, it is used to measure the optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD), cerebral blood flow velocities using transcranial Doppler or transcranial color-coded duplex.[ , ]
Transcranial ultrasound (TCUS) is an alternative bedside imaging modality that has recently gained popularity.[ , , , ] Its use in neonates and infants is well established as a screening tool for hemorrhage, hypoxic-ischemic events, cerebral malformations, and hydrocephalus. Skull bones are very thin and fragile, providing adequate windows for examination in these patients. The intact, thick skull hampers reproducibility in adults; however, DC may offer the ideal window into the brain for obtaining high-resolution images using TCUS.[ , , , , ] In acute and chronic settings, TCUS may help to identify conditions such as midline shift, hydrocephalus, hematomas, contusions, and parenchymal bleed. It also helps to assess cerebral hemodynamics with the help of color Doppler and Duplex.[ , , , - ] TCUS in adults needs proper validation for its use in managing neurosurgical patients. Given its operator-dependent nature, it is essential to compare TCUS with established gold standard imaging modalities, such as NCCT, to assess the reliability of its findings and its applicability in routine clinical practice.[ , , , ]
Even though portable CT scans provide the option of bedside imaging, they are not widely available.[ ] In contrast, TCUS, being simple, portable, and non-invasive, can be performed at the bedside without shifting the patients to a CT suite.[ , ] It carries the potential to be a credible and faster diagnostic method to identify neurocritical issues and their management. As bedside ultrasound carries no harm, it may be incorporated into the daily routine of neurocritical care in triage and resuscitation as a point-of-care imaging modality.
At present, evidence on the use of TCUS in neurocritical care is limited. Hence, we planned this study to compare the midline shift and ventricle size using TCUS with NCCT in patients who underwent DC for evacuation of acute post-traumatic subdural hematoma (SDH).

## MATERIALS AND METHODS
After obtaining approval from the Institute Ethics Committee (IEC) (JIP/IEC/2022/228) and registration with CTRI (CTRI/2022/12/048274), this study was conducted at a tertiary care center between October 2022 and June 2024. Informed written consent was taken from the relatives or guardians of patients. We included all adult patients who underwent fronto-temporo-parietal DC for evacuation of acute post-traumatic SDH. A CT scan was done within 12–24 h post-surgery. A TCUS was done before shifting the patient for a CT scan.

## Sample size
Patients who underwent DC following acute SDH between October 2022 and June 2024 were included. Twenty cases were initially evaluated to refine the technique and streamline the methodology, after which recruitment was started. Sixty patients were included in our trial during the study period.

## Procedure
A TCUS scan (GE Healthcare Venue Go™) was performed with a standard abdominal convex (curvilinear) of 3–8 MHz frequency. The procedure was done under strict aseptic precautions using skin disinfection and the application of sterile ultrasonic gel. Care was taken to place the probe gently over the skin to avoid pressure effects and distortion artifacts. Image gain, depth, and amplitude were adjusted individually. A safety distance of 1 cm from the surgical wound was kept to prevent contamination, without causing much disturbance to the sterile dressing. Scans were done in a head-up position without disturbing the routine intensive care units (ICU) management of the cases. NCCT scans were taken after performing TCUS. CT scans were acquired (no contrast enhancement) using a CT scanner for image acquisition at 5 mm slice thickness from the occipital condyles to the vertex using the brain window.
The procedure was standardized for all patients as follows:
In the axial plane, the probe was positioned along the orbitomeatal line (to resemble the corresponding CT-scan axial images)
The probe was moved from the orbitomeatal line toward the vertex to visualize the brainstem, basal cisterns, third ventricle, foramen of Munro, and lateral ventricles. Complications such as contusion and hematoma were identified and noted during the procedure
Then, examination of the brain parenchyma in the coronal plane was done with the probe positioned across the orbitomeatal line perpendicular to the initial placement of the probe as in step 1, and the examination was performed to identify the contusions and hematomas.

## Midline shift
The axial section of the brain was scanned for identification of the body of the lateral ventricle, and when the septum pellucidum was visible, the image was frozen. The anterior and posterior falx were then identified and joined by drawing a line. The shift of the intraventricular line (septum pellucidum) from the joining line of the anterior and posterior falx was noted [ ].
The qualitative parameters, such as bleeding, contusion, parenchymal changes, and basal cistern patency, were noted. Bleeding and edema (parenchymal changes) were noted as hyperechoic findings. Basal cistern patency was noted along the perimesencephalic plane as a butterfly wing-shaped structure. The clear visibility of the structure indicated that the basal cisterns were not compressed and ensured their patency.
After the procedure was completed, patients were shifted for NCCT brain. Results of the above parameters were noted by a radiologist who was blinded to the results of the TCUS. Midline shift, ventricle size, and the incidence of contusion and bleeding were noted in both ultrasound and NCCT at a single time. The decision regarding patient management was taken based on the clinical findings and NCCT findings.
All TCUS examinations were performed by an experienced neuroanesthesiologist/neurointensivist. Any procedure-related complications, such as examination site hematoma and infections, were noted and treated accordingly. Data collection was done using a standard pro forma.

## Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were carried out using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences Version 19.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). A frequency and percentage scale was used to express the distribution of categorical characteristics, and continuous variables were expressed as mean±standard deviation. The comparison of the primary outcome variable was done using a paired Student’s t-test. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used for the correlation of ultrasonography (USG) and CT scan for quantitative variables. The level of agreement was plotted using a Bland-Altman graph. The categorical variables were compared between the groups using the Chi-square test. Cohen’s kappa coefficient was used for comparison of qualitative secondary outcome variables. All statistical analyses were carried out at a significance level of a error of 5%.

## RESULTS
Patients with acute SDH who underwent DC for hematoma evacuation were included in the study. Sixty-eight patients were screened for the study. Among them, eight patients were excluded from the study. Finally, 60 patients who underwent DC for acute SDH were enrolled and included in the final analysis [ ]. Out of 60 enrolled patients, 84% were male, and 16% were female. DC was performed on the left side in 31 (51.7%) of patients and right side in 29 (48.3%) [ ]. The primary outcome variables, such as midline shift and ventricle sizes, were noted and tabulated [ ]. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated for the variables in comparison with TCUS and NCCT. The mean values of midline shift were 4.7 ± 3.7 mm and 4.3 ± 3.6 mm in the TCUS and NCCT groups (r = 0.984, P < 0.001). This signifies that there was a strong correlation between TCUS and NCCT. The mean size of the lateral ventricles was 13.3 ± 1.3 mm and 13.1 ± 1.5 mm in the TCUS and NCCT groups, respectively (r = 0.667, P < 0.001). The mean size of the third ventricle was 3.8 ± 0.3 mm, and 3.7 ± 0.3 mm in the TCUS and NCCT group (r = 0.914, P < 0.001) [ ]. The limits of agreement for midline shift and ventricle sizes with TCUS and NCCT were represented using the Bland-Altman plot. The mean values of midline shift and ventricle sizes were taken in the X-axis, and the difference between them was taken along the Y-axis. Separate plots were marked for midline shift, lateral ventricle, and third ventricle. It depicts that all the values are within± 1.96 standard deviation [ - ]. With respect to midline shift, the average mean difference was 0.4 (P < 0.001, 95% confidence interval [CI] of 0.24–0.58) [ ]. For the ventricle sizes, the lateral ventricle had an average mean difference of 0.21 (P < 0.001, 95% CI of −0.08–0.51) [ ]. The third ventricle had an average mean value of 0.03 (P < 0.001, 95% CI of −0.005–0.06) [ ]. The tight cluster around the center indicates a good level of agreement between the two imaging modalities.
 The secondary parameters, such as hematoma, contusion, parenchymal changes, and basal cistern patency, were noted [ ]. Sensitivity and specificity values for TCUS were calculated considering NCCT as the gold standard. Among 60 patients, bleeding was noted postoperatively in three patients. Two of them had residual SDH while one had an intracerebral hemorrhage. Sensitivity and specificity in detecting bleeding-related complications were similar in both imaging techniques. Contusion was observed in 15 patients (25%) using TCUS, and 14 (23.4%) patients had the same diagnosed with NCCT. A sensitivity of 92.86% and a specificity of 95.65% were noted for detecting contusion in TCUS. Parenchymal changes were noted among 18 (30%) patients during TCUS, whereas 8 (13.3%) had it detected in NCCT. TCUS was found to have 100% sensitivity and 80.77% specificity concerning parenchymal changes. Basal cisterns were patent in most patients. It was not visualized among 7 (11.7%) patients in TCUS and 12 (5%) in NCCT. A sensitivity of 90.57% and a specificity of 100% were noted for basal cistern patency in TCUS. Cohen’s kappa correlation coefficient was calculated as a measure of agreement between NCCT and TCUS. Hematoma and contusion had values of 1 and 0.8, respectively, showing strong agreement. Parenchymal changes and basal cistern patency had values of 0.5, indicating moderate agreement [ ]. No procedure-related complications were observed in the study population.
 

## DISCUSSION
This observational study was conducted to compare NCCT and TCUS in detecting postoperative complications among acute SDH patients who underwent DC. In our study, we enrolled 60 post-DC patients, and parameters were compared using both imaging modalities. The ventricular size measured among both modalities had a good correlation.
The levels of agreement were within acceptable limits in Bland-Altman’s analysis. The secondary parameters, such as contusion, hematoma, and basal cistern patency, were found to be comparable between NCCT and TCUS.
The utility of CT scans is broad in neurocritical care setups.[ , ] Wide availability and easy interpretation of both bony and parenchymal structures in the brain make it a gold standard imaging modality.[ ] Post-DC imaging rules out most of the complications, such as cerebral edema, residual hematomas, and hydrocephalus.[ ] However, the patient needs to be shifted to the radiological suites for CT scans, and the transfer of critically ill patients is very challenging.[ , ] Radiation hazard is yet another implication of CT scans5.
USG has become an important tool in neurocritical care units for the assessment and management of various neurological and systemic pathologies and complications.[ , ] It is utilized for both systemic and neurological indications. Neurological indications include assessment of ONSD, vasospasm, stroke, and brain death. Due to advantages such as easy availability, repeatability, and lack of radiation hazards, it has become an indispensable tool in neurocritical care units.
TCUS is the echography of the brain for the assessment of neurological structures.[ , ] It is a dynamic, real-time, and non-invasive imaging modality that identifies anatomical and pathological structures. The anatomical structures, such as ventricles, peri-mesencephalic cisterns, and the circle of Willis, could be identified.[ , ] Pathological findings such as hydrocephalus, contusion, and bleeding could also be picked up. Using the Doppler, the flow velocities and pulsatility index of the cerebral blood vessels could be quantified.[ , ] Assessment of midline shift and ventricle size plays a pivotal role in managing critical patients in neuro-ICU.[ , ] However, thick bone hampers the view of brain structures. Since our study was conducted among craniectomized patients, better acoustic windows were obtained for the proper identification of the structures.[ , ] In addition, the false positives and negatives are less likely when the imaging is done through the DC site rather than intact bone. Furthermore, the images were obtained by an individual who had received adequate training before the study began, thereby minimizing the potential for errors.

## Ventricle size
The mean ventricle diameter in our study had a strong correlation between TCUS and NCCT (Lateral ventricle [r = 0.6, P < 0.001], third ventricle [r = 0.9, P = 0.001]) [ ]. This is also depicted by Bland-Altman’s analysis within the acceptable range. These results are consistent with a study conducted by Bendella et al., which compares cranial CT (CCT) versus sonographic duplex technique (SDT).[ ] A high correlation was found between CCT and SDT in measuring all four ventricular diameters (right lateral r = 0.978, P < 0.001; left lateral r = 0.975, P < 0.001; third r = 0.987, P < 0.001, and fourth ventricle r = 0.954, P < 0.001).[ ] Another study by De Bonis et al. also revealed statistically significant results comparing CT and TCS.[ ] A high correlation coefficient (ρ) between CT and TCS was found for frontal horn (ρ 0.9929), median cella (ρ 0.9516), and third ventricle (ρ 0.8989) dimensions. Similarly, Chouhan et al. conducted a study among 40 patients who presented to the casualty with post-DC, comparing TCS and CT brain.[ ] They also found a good correlation between TCS and CT of the head in measuring ventricular dimensions. The above-mentioned studies were conducted among patients with varied diagnoses. However, our study was conducted with a homogeneous population of SDH patients who underwent DC.

## Midline shift
Midline shift is a clinically important parameter in determining short-term and long-term outcomes. The mean values of midline shift in TCUS and NCCT in our study were 4.7 ± 3.7 and 4.3 ± 3.6 [ ], showing a strong correlation (r = 0.984, P < 0.001). Kapoor et al. conducted a study among 17 patients and found that the midline shift was 0.52 ± 0.90 cm using TCS and 0.58 ± 0.39 cm using CT scan (r = 0.002, P < 0.05).[ ] Similar results were demonstrated in Bendella et al. and Tang et al. in terms of deviation of midline shift using TCUS and NCCT.[ , ]

## Assessment of other parameters
The postoperative complications following DC may include bleeding, contusion, and hydrocephalus which can significantly affect the neurological outcome. Therefore, timely postoperative imaging is essential for early detection and optimal management of these complications. In our institute, it is a routine practice of taking NCCT, 12 h post-DC, to assess the complications irrespective of GCS status. In a study done by De Bonis et al., they found that TCS was similar to CT in detecting complications.[ ]
In a study conducted by Masaeli et al. on pediatric head trauma using ultrasound for the detection of brain hemorrhage and skull fractures, it was reported that, in older children, the level of agreement was low for detection of brain hemorrhage (kappa 0.3) as there were small frontal bleeds which were diagnosed in CT, and not detected in USG.[ ] In our study, two patients had residual SDH, and one patient had an intracerebral hemorrhage (kappa 1) after DC, which was picked up in both NCCT and TCUS. It indicates that TCUS had sensitivity and specificity similar to NCCT, although with a low incidence.
In our study, basal cisterns were predominantly patent. It was found to be absent in 7 (11.7%) cases in TCUS, whereas in 12 (20%) cases in NCCT, it was obliterated. The obliteration of the basal cisterns could be attributed to raise ICPs due to the TBI. NCCT (13.3%) had a lower incidence of parenchymal changes when compared to TCUS (30%) because cerebral edema was also taken as a parenchymal change. It was noted as moderately hyperechoic when compared with the surrounding structures. Another reason attributed to this was the technical limitation due to the brightness gain beyond the ventricles. Similar results were obtained in the study done by De Bonis et al.[ ] However, the routine management of cerebral edema was done by providing a head-up position and hyperosmolar therapy. No TCUS-related complications were reported in terms of wound infection and brain contusion during the study.

## Strength of the study
NCCT only provides a snapshot of the events, whereas TCUS is a real-time monitoring modality that helps in serial assessments. It has the potential to decrease the frequency of CT scans or the interval between CT scans. For the purpose of this study, CT, if done first, will affect the treatment parameters and can affect USG findings. Hence, in our study, TCUS was performed before NCCT. It was done in a homogeneous population of post-SDH craniectomy patients. Another significant advantage is that TCUS is not associated with radiation, which is of concern with NCCT. The use of TCUS is also cost-effective when compared to NCCT.

## Limitations of the study
The use of TCUS needs expertise. Although the procedure is reproducible, interobserver and intraobserver variations are possible during scanning. The utility of TCUS in other conditions warrants further prospective trials in future.

## CONCLUSION
In this prospective observational study conducted on patients who underwent DC for acute post-traumatic SDH, we found that there was a good level of agreement in the estimation of midline shift and size of the lateral and third ventricles between TCUS and non-contrast CT. Being simple and repeatable, TCUS can be used as a complementary, noninvasive tool in neurocritical care units. Validation of TCUS in other pathologies of the brain needs prospective trials in the future.
