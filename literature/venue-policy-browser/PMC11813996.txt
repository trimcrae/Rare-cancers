Local jurisdictions often lack the data and tools necessary to detect and translate such moments into actionable and effective responses. Informed by a risk environment framework, this case study adopted a mixed-methods design spanning two rapid assessment studies with PWUD in Lowell (n = 90) and Lawrence (n = 40), Massachusetts, during an HIV outbreak (2017, Study 1) and following the outbreak (2019, Study 2). Survey responses (n = 130) were summarized by computing descriptive statistics, ethnographic field notes were reviewed, and qualitative interviews (n = 34) were thematically analyzed to contextualize quantitative findings. Study 1 participants reported constrained syringe access, high injecting frequency, syringe reuse, and unsafe syringe disposal practices, and uncovered entrenched social and structural factors exacerbating existing substance use and HIV risks: housing instability, limited harm reduction supplies and services (e.g., MOUD access), and tensions between law enforcement and PWUD. Following the outbreak and substantial local investments in harm reduction and treatment infrastructures, Study 2 participants indicated lower injection risk reporting fewer syringe reuse episodes, improved syringe disposal practices, high frequency of naloxone administration at recent overdoses, frequent testing for HIV and HCV, and greater engagement with syringe services programs. A rapid assessment approach to fentanyl’s emergence and a concomitant HIV outbreak provided time-sensitive, critical insights and identified needed and local response strategies. However, unless social and structural risk factors are also addressed, community vulnerability to future HIV outbreaks remains.

## Background
Fatal and non-fatal overdoses continue to rise in the United States, primarily due to the burgeoning presence of synthetic opioids such as illicitly manufactured fentanyl (IMF) in the drug supply [ , ]. Between 2022 and 2023, 92% of the approximately 84,567 opioid-involved overdose deaths (OOD) documented nationwide can be largely attributed to IMF [ ]. While recognition of IMF and the potential harms associated with its presence was not publicly addressed in Massachusetts until November 2015 [ ], IMF was noted as an adulterant of heroin in the Massachusetts drug markets as early as 2013 [ – ]. An ethnographic study conducted in 2016 based in the Massachusetts cities of Lawrence, Lowell, and Worcester—situated in or near the Merrimack Valley—recorded participants describing a notable change in the drug supply toward an increased presence of IMF within heroin [ ]. Concurrent with drug market shifts toward IMF, the Merrimack Valley experienced the highest rates of OOD in the state between 2013 and 2016 [ ]. The counties of Middlesex and Essex, which include portions of the Merrimack Valley, reported a greater than 40% spike in OOD between 2013 and 2014 [ ]. While a general upward trend in OOD between 2013 and 2016 can be observed among nearly all counties in Massachusetts, Middlesex County’s OOD count remained consistently above other counties over this time period [ ]. In terms of OOD rates, Essex County was among the most burdened Massachusetts’ counties, reporting between 23.1 and 32.9 OOD per 100,000 people [ ].
Coincident with the infiltration of IMF into drug markets and the significant increase in rates of OOD over the past decade, Massachusetts experienced a surge of HIV infections commencing in 2015. This surge was largely concentrated in a few cities in the Merrimack Valley and driven by injection drug use [ ]. Extant research has linked heightened risk of infectious disease transmission (e.g., HIV, HCV) to opioid-involved overdose [ – ]. Between 2015 and 2017, two clusters of HIV cases emerged in the cities of Lowell and Lawrence. One study conducted in response to this outbreak identified 80% of its sample of 129 HIV seropositive individuals as originating from the Middlesex and Essex counties [ ]. Subsequently, Massachusetts declared a statewide HIV outbreak, based on broader geographic detection of case clusters [ , ]. By June 2020, a total of 180 HIV infections had been linked to this original clustering of outbreaks [ ]. One cluster of new cases that emerged in the Boston area among people who inject drugs (PWID) in 2019 has since been linked to the outbreaks in Lawrence and Lowell and totaled at least 76 people living with HIV as of December 2020 [ , ]. In a report released by the Massachusetts Department of Public Health in early 2022, an estimated 164 cases diagnosed since November 2018 can be linked to the Boston cluster, stemming from the outbreak originating in the cities of Lawrence and Lowell [ ], and incident cases continue to rise in the Boston area—exacerbated by the recent disruptions to encampments [ ]. Other states and municipalities such as Scott County, Indiana [ , ], rural West Virginia [ – ], and northern rural Minnesota [ ] have also experienced HIV outbreaks among PWID that coincided with other risk factors like increased IMF into the drug supply, high OOD incidence, and housing instability.
Congruent with a rise in HIV infections during the same time period, HCV was the third most common infectious disease reported to the Massachusetts Department of Public Health (n = 7,013) [ ]. While most reported cases of HCV are chronic infections, the health department estimates that over 250,000 residents of Massachusetts are currently living with HCV [ ]. Between 2002 and 2018, Massachusetts had experienced a significant increase in the number of HCV cases reported, especially among PWID and younger individuals under the age of 30 [ ]. In the study noted above conducted by Alpren and colleagues, 90% of participants who were living with HIV were also living with HCV [ ], thus demonstrating high HCV positivity rates and comorbidities in infectious diseases among PWID in Massachusetts. Research conducted in other locations corroborates this comorbidity. In Kanawha County, West Virginia, 86% of the 85 persons who were linked to the HIV outbreak were also living with HCV [ ].
The myriad and intersecting harms (e.g., HIV, poverty, homelessness, overdose risk) associated with substance use among people who use drugs (PWUD) can be explored through a ‘risk environment’ lens, which posits that the dynamic between individuals and their social, structural and physical environments together shape disease and health distributions [ , ]. Research exploring practical applications of risk environment frameworks has suggested that public health approaches emphasizing individual behavior change, while not also targeting the social and structural conditions in which the individual is immersed, are less effective at predicting harm mitigation, especially among PWUD [ – ]. Thus, drug-related harms (e.g., HIV infection) experienced by—and subsequent public health/harm reductive responses developed for—PWUD should be examined using an intersectional risk environment approach that considers the social and structural landscapes of at-risk communities of PWUD, in tandem with individual behavioral risk [ ].
Communities can address public health crises such as HIV outbreaks by engaging in community-based studies like rapid assessments to inform tailored responses [ – ], especially in circumstances when substance use harms (e.g., increased overdose risk) and related health crises (e.g., HIV outbreaks) coincide. Due to the illicit nature of drug use and the hidden, stigmatized populations of PWUD, there is often a lack of readily accessible administrative or clinical data and established tools necessary to detect and translate such moments into actionable and effective public health responses; community-engaged, primary data collection involving multiple methods is warranted. Carrying out rapid assessments allows researchers to swiftly gain in-depth knowledge and insider perspectives of rapidly emerging health crises and the influences of social and structural (i.e., economic, political) factors, some of which may be addressed through policy change [ – ]. Acknowledging the importance of rapid assessments and attempting to address the aforementioned gaps and challenges associated with quickly evolving health crises, we—informed by a risk environment framework—contribute to the literature by presenting a case study that highlights (1) the execution of two rapid assessment studies, referred to here as ‘Rapid Assessments of Consumer Knowledge’ (RACK), conducted amidst and following the identification of a clustering of HIV cases in two Massachusetts communities, (2) the evolution of public health responses and activities that followed from the perspective of PWUD in these communities, and (3) how our findings can inform future data collection and public health responses.

## Methods
The current case study is comprised of two mixed-methods rapid assessment studies (Fig.  ) [ – ]. The first assessment was completed in August 2017 in Lowell, Massachusetts, while the second study took place between August and November 2019 in Lawrence and Lowell, Massachusetts. Four co-authors (TCG, WP, PC, TS) took part in both studies. The focus of Study 1 was opioid overdose, while Study 2 focused on overdose, harm reduction and HIV service needs.

 

## Study Settings

## Massachusetts
As part of a new initiative to expand public health surveillance data, the Massachusetts Department of Public Health supported the research team in development and application of rapid assessments in communities with rising overdose rates to understand factors driving these trends and possible intervention points from the perspective of PWUD. During this undertaking in 2016, a rise in cases of HIV was detected. In response to this spike in HIV cases in Lawrence and Lowell among PWID, the Department of Health requested support from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Following this request, the CDC and the Department of Public Health partnered to further investigate the HIV outbreak in Middlesex and Essex counties [ ].

## Lowell and Lawrence
Lowell and Lawrence are Massachusetts cities located in the northeastern part of the state, approximately 30 miles from Boston and 5 miles from the New Hampshire border. Situated along the Merrimack River that powered enormous textile mills, Lowell and Lawrence represented some of the most important cities of the industrial revolution. Today, Lowell is characterized by socioeconomic disadvantage with 17% of its residents living in poverty [ ]. 50% of Lowell’s population is comprised of residents identifying as a race other than White [ ]. Approximately 18% of its residents identify as Hispanic or LatinX, which together represents substantially greater racial and ethnic diversity than the racial-ethnic composition of the state [ ]. The neighboring city of Lawrence is characterized by a large ethnic presence of Hispanic/LatinX individuals, which now comprises approximately 81% of a population of 80,000 [ , ]. Lawrence is similarly diverse in its racial makeup, with over 50% of its residents identifying as a race other than White [ ]. Akin to Lowell, Lawrence is also socioeconomically disadvantaged: 21% of Lawrence residents live below the poverty line and the median household income is substantially below that of the state [ ].
Lowell and Lawrence have become central cocaine and opioid distribution hubs for New England Dominican Drug Trafficking Organizations [ , , ]. The prominent drug markets have long attracted “day trippers,” or PWUD who travel from other areas within or outside of the state to purchase illicit drugs daily while simultaneously maintaining employment, schooling demands, and/or raising a family [ ]. Among the many adverse consequences that have accompanied increases in substance use and the introduction of IMF into the drug supply in the immediate and surrounding areas of Lowell and Lawrence are increases in drug-related overdoses. In 2019, both Lowell (51.4 OOD per 100,000) and Lawrence (77.5 OOD per 100,000) had elevated rates of OOD compared to the state average (28.8 OOD per 100,000) [ ].

## Rapid Assessment Studies

## Study 1: Lowell, MA (2017)
The RACK method was carried out to investigate fentanyl use and the perceived effects of opioid-related policies by PWUD in Lowell. The RACK approach entails an environmental scan, preparatory ethnographic observations, targeted recruitment, and the administration of surveys and interviews [ – ]. Data collection was interviewer-administered, with a subsample of surveyed participants purposively sampled based on survey participants’ willingness to further discuss topics covered in the survey and invited to participate in a subsequent interview. Interviews were conducted in English and Spanish and were audio-recorded with the participant’s permission and later transcribed for further analysis.
For Study 1, recruitment sites included community outreach programs, homeless shelters, coffee shops, barber shops, churches, a soup kitchen, and other service providers and public locations. Peer-to-peer recruitment occurred if a potential study participant learns of the study through “word of mouth,” or from their peers who may have been approached by RACK team members [ – ]. Study flyers were disseminated in conspicuous and highly frequented locations. A total of 50 participants completed the survey while a subsample of 15 were interviewed.
Inclusion criteria for Study 1 included the ability to read and write English, 18 years or older, and past 30-day illicit substance use (other than marijuana). All participants were screened and assessed for eligibility, invited to participate, and provided informed consent. Verbal informed consent was required before study participation. All study components were approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Boston University Medical Center and the Massachusetts Department of Public Health. Upon completion of the survey and/or interview, participants were compensated for their time in the form of $20 gift cards for each study component.

## Study 2: Lowell and Lawrence, MA (2019)
The second study used a cross-sectional design to complete 80 surveys and 19 in-depth interviews with PWUD in the cities of Lawrence and Lowell between August and November 2019. The same survey eligibility criteria from Study 1 in 2017 applied to this study, however, we stratified by use of syringe services programs (SSPs) to avoid bias of venue-based recruitment and learn about the needs of those less connected to these services. We also conducted semi-structured interviews with individuals 35 years old and younger who were purposively recruited due to potential differences in service uptake by age identified in Study 1 and trends noted from HIV and HCV case findings [ ]. Unlike Study 1, participating in a survey was not a requirement to be eligible for a qualitative interview.
To broaden and diversify participant characteristics and risk profiles, the team employed a combination of recruitment techniques in Lowell and Lawrence, including venue-based recruitment from community organizations and agencies, social media postings on Facebook and Craigslist, and street-based recruitment from information provided by key informants and stakeholders. Community partner sites included a mobile SSP, homeless shelters, coffee shops, barber shops, churches, a soup kitchen, and other service providers [ – ]. Street-based participants were recruited from within public parks and commons, train stations, and areas near the Merrimack River and nearby waterways.

## Survey Measures for Studies 1 and 2
Survey domains included questions on socio-demographic characteristics, substance use, injection behaviors, overdose history and response, syringe service utilization, and infectious disease testing and treatment. The survey tool was interviewer-administered and recorded by paper in Study 1 and electronically in Study 2.

## Socio-Demographic Characteristics
Participants reported identifying as either male or female. The age of participants was categorized ordinally: 18–30, 31–45 and 46 + years. Race was grouped into five categories, including (1) Black, African, Haitian, or Cape Verdean, (2) Native American, (3) Asian or Asian American / Pacific Islander, (4) White, and (5) Multi-Racial or Another Race. Ethnicity was measured as Hispanic or non-Hispanic. Housing status was measured as either unstably housed or housed.

## Substance Use, Injecting Behaviors and Syringe Access
Participants were asked which substance(s) they had used in the past 30 days, with response options of cocaine, crack cocaine, heroin, fentanyl, prescription pain medication, methadone, prescribed buprenorphine, and non-prescribed buprenorphine. Injecting drugs in the past 30 days was measured in a binary fashion (yes/no). Participants who reported any injecting in the past 30 days were asked to report an ordinal frequency of injection (daily or more often, about weekly, or about monthly), a count of injections per syringe before disposal (median and interquartile range [IQR] reported), whether or not drug injection equipment was shared in the past 30 days (yes/no), their syringe source(s) (categorical), and perceptions surrounding ease of syringe accessibility (ordinal).

## SSP Utilization
PWID from Study 2 reported on various measures of syringe access with a focus on use of SSPs. Participants who injected reported on syringe accessibility and their perceptions and awareness of accessibility of others around them (ordinal measures), main source of syringe disposal (categorical), frequency of SSP use, and median number of syringes obtained and disposed during their most recent SSP encounter.

## Overdose Experience and Response
Participants reported their lifetime personal and witnessed experiences with overdose, the number of overdoses they experienced and witnessed in their lifetime (median [IQR]), and whether naloxone was administered (yes/no) and 911 was called (yes/no) at their last witnessed overdose. Accessibility to naloxone was initially measured on a Likert scale ranging from extremely easy to extremely difficult, but were recoded into two groups—(1) perceived as difficult or uncertain (i.e., did not know where to access naloxone) and (2) perceived as easy or extremely easy to access naloxone.

## Substance Use Treatment and Medications for Opioid Use Disorder (MOUD)
Participants reported on whether they had experienced any formal types of substance use treatment (e.g., detox, inpatient treatment) in their lifetime (yes/no) and if they were currently prescribed buprenorphine or methadone (MOUDs) that are partial and full opioid agonists, respectively (yes/no).

## Sexual Risk Behaviors-Study 2 only
Questions on current sexual risk behaviors were posed, asking participants about sex work in the past 30 days (i.e., whether they engaged in vaginal or anal sex in exchange for cash or something else in the past thirty days and how frequently they did so), sexual activity in the past 30 days (yes/no), the frequency of condom use (ordinal) and safer sex supply access in the community (categorical).

## Infectious Disease Testing and Treatment-Study 2 only
Questions inquired about where participants’ most recent HIV test occurred, awareness, and knowledge of infectious disease treatment for HIV and HCV (true/false/unsure). Willingness to take preventative HIV medication daily (i.e., pre-exposure prophylaxis, PrEP) was measured on an ordinal scale from extremely willing to extremely unwilling. Participants were asked to disclose awareness of or having ever taken PrEP (yes/no). Participants who indicated living with HIV and/or HCV were asked whether they could access HIV and HCV treatment and necessary care (yes/no).

## Analyses for Studies 1 and 2
Univariate and bivariate descriptive statistics were computed using SPSS (IBM, Chicago, Version 26). Qualitative data were collected with the intention of complementing, contextualizing, and unpacking quantitative findings during analysis and to elicit notable themes through the development of new insights and areas of further inquiry [ – ]. Thus, ideas and themes derived from transcripts and ethnographic field notes were triangulated with survey data thereafter [ ]. In both studies, qualitative data were collected via semi-structured interviews (n = 34) and ethnographic observations, which were recorded throughout the study periods. Qualitative interviews were audio-recorded, sent to a professional transcription service, coded, and analyzed as described in a prior study [ ].
Qualitative data analysis was conducted using NVivo (Lumivero, Denver, Version 12). The following thematic codes were used to identify relevant transcripts for further thematic analysis: injecting drugs and syringe use/utilization, sexual risk, bloodborne illness, safety measures when using, substance use consequences, naloxone access, and infectious disease screening and treatment. The first and second author engaged in memoing, open coding, and focused secondary coding of the broader parent codes indicated above to identify subthemes that were relevant to this study and in contextualizing the quantitative analysis including injection behaviors, SSPs, transmissible infectious diseases, and access to harm reduction services [ ]. Our coding approach was iterative whereby contextualizing subthemes were explored by both coders and refined for consensus through the coding process. Coders met to reconcile discrepancies, discuss subthemes, and further conceptualize the data through intercoder consensus [ ].

## Results
Data presented in Tables  and describe the socio-demographics and risk profiles of Study 1 and 2 participants from Lowell and Lawrence, while data presented in Tables  and focus exclusively on Study 2 participants, homing in on SSP utilization and infectious disease prevention, testing, and treatment. Qualitative findings are presented throughout to contextualize quantitative results.

 

 

 

 

## Study 1: August 2017

## Socio-demographic Characteristics, Housing, and Substance Use
The majority of the Study 1 sample was male (64.0%), between 31 and 45 years old (46.0%), White (82.0%) and non-Hispanic (84.0%) (Table  ). Housing instability in the Lowell area was a prominent concern for many participants. About half of the participants reported being unstably housed (44.0%). The city hosts several shelters to support the needs of the unhoused [ , ]; however, unique to this community were policies that shifted the sites from “wet” (i.e., guests may be using substances) to “dry” (i.e., guests may not be using substances) shelters based on the ambient weather temperature. As described by study participants and documented in observational field notes, during colder times, the shelters adopted more inclusive policies; when warmer outside, the shelters were more restrictive. This seasonal shifting contributed to instability and an expected surge in homelessness in the city that created conditions of concentrated drug use and inconsistency in available supports:…If you’re homeless […] there’s no help for you and then when you do need help like a roof over your head or a place to take a shower, they want you to, like, be sober. It’s like, if I was sober, I would have an apartment and nothing would be wrong. It’s kind of—it’s a Catch-22. (Participant 015, Male).

Nearly all participants reported general opioid use (94.0%), but not everyone knew about or suspected fentanyl in their heroin at the time of Study 1 in 2017. Fentanyl’s presence was recent and overwhelming as described by one participant:It’s [fentanyl in the drug supply] been getting worse as time goes on. When it first started out, it was once in a while and now there is more fentanyl than there is heroin. There is really no more heroin around, honestly. (Participant 022, Male).

The qualities of fentanyl (rapid onset, short acting, high potency) were alluring if not nostalgic as described by the same participant: “It’s stronger. Even though it doesn’t last longer, it gets you higher, and in the end that’s what they’re chasing is that first high all over again.” But participants also found that “it doesn’t have any legs” (i.e., short acting) and this led to the need for more frequent—and costly—use. Still, the presence of fentanyl in the heroin drug supply was shifting preferences in people continuing to use drugs, as the same participant articulates:Lately, more people are coming forward with it, telling you that, you know, I have fentanyl….Because actually people are now searching for it…Like people would rather get fentanyl than heroin. There is actually a lot of people are switching over, like they will go out and ask, ‘Can I get fentanyl instead of dope?’ (Participant 022, Male).

Fentanyl’s presence was also shaping new drug use practices, as one participant conveys:It [fentanyl] makes you want to do more dumb stuff. It makes you feel invincible…Injecting more heavily, bigger shots. (Participant 016, Male).

## Injecting Behaviors, Syringe Access, and Sexual Risk
Among Study 1 participants who reported injecting in the past 30 days (68.0%), the majority indicated daily use (71.9%) and syringe reuse was high (median = 3, [IQR: 2, 5]). Despite the high frequency of injecting among study participants, accessibility to sterile syringes among participants within the community was poor. During this period, participants in Lowell relied on pharmacies where syringes could be purchased, an official mobile SSP from a nearby city that frequented the area one to two days per week, or an informal, volunteer mobile SSP that visited the community twice per week for two hours in total. Lacking a brick-and-mortar SSP, there was a heavy reliance on individual actors to disseminate syringes:They need a needle exchange. I don’t know. Literally it’s [volunteer mobile SSP] a guy that just does it out of the kindness of his heart that does it. I’m pretty sure he funds it himself, too. A lot of times it’s people just doing things, you know, to help out. They know what needs to be done. (Participant 015, Male).

Nearly half the sample reported using local pharmacies for syringe access (41.2%). Some participants shared that pharmacies were easily accessible throughout the city and often at all hours of the day, while others indicated challenges, including stigma, identification, and cost. For example, one participant described a preference toward the mobile SSP relative to pharmacies for both ease of access and financial reasons:I prefer to go to the needle exchange. Number one, it’s free. They’ll give you as many kits as you need. They have kits that contain a tie, a cooker, cottons, alcohol pads…Boxes of 100 needles….Basically instead of going a spending $4 on a pack of ten needles at the pharmacy and having to try to find somebody with an ID, I can just go to the needle exchange. (Participant 026, Male).

While pharmacies sell sterile syringes, they do not provide a means of disposing of used syringes. Disposal of syringes was available only at the senior center and the city’s transfer station at this time. Consequently, participants described frustrations of both lacking sterile materials and no secure spaces to dispose of them, such as in the scenario described below by another participant:Like they need an exchange. Like if they want to make the city cleaner, they need to figure out a way to get an exchange going…Like fast. I can show you a few spots where, like, literally, like, you would look and there are just hundreds of used syringes. You couldn’t take a step without stepping on them…Yeah, there are places around Lowell that are just like what the fuck?…Like a stationary spot where people know that they can go and get them [syringes] from would be best. Mobile, people just never figure out where the hell it was. It would be like a hit or miss type of thing. (Participant 016, Male).

Multiple participants described finding themselves in a position where they had to reuse a syringe or improperly dispose of it due to the lack of public disposal locations and accessibility of sterile syringes in times of need:Like literally you walk down the street and there are syringes all over the streets. It’s disgusting. Like I don’t do that. I’ll throw them out and I’ll break the tips off and I’ll throw them inside and then throw them out so nobody can get poked by them, but people throw uncapped syringes right on the road. (Participant 015, Male).
They should have public posts… Yeah because you always find them [used syringes] in the park or on the street or in school zones and it’s just not fair to the kids, but they’re [PWUD] not thinking about them at the time. So we just throw them in the sewer drain. (Participant 042, Male).

While the focus of Study 1 was opioid overdose, the prominence of sexual risk behaviors, especially sex work in the context of fentanyl use, emerged as a qualitative theme with direct link from drug use to heightened sexual risk. Stimulant use in this community was cocaine-dominant. The sex work cycle entailed heavy and prolonged cocaine use followed by fentanyl to crash. One participant pointed out this link:There are girls that walk up and down these streets all day long from five in the morning to five at night and then five at night to five in the morning, they’re chasing it [fentanyl]. …And you see some of these girls will be awake three or four days and then they just crash, and then they’re back out there running again. (Participant 026, Male).

Similar to the situation with syringe access in this community, ethnographic observations cataloged that condom access was also very limited: pharmacies sold condoms if one could afford to purchase them, condoms were available for free in the sexual health clinic at the community health center but only during the daytime hours when it was open, and condoms could be obtained for free during the limited hours of operation when the mobile SSP was nearby.

## HIV & Other Bloodborne Infections
New cases of HIV diagnoses were reported to the Department of Health from neighboring Lawrence in August 2016 and the following year more incident cases were identified in Lowell (see Fig.  ). Still, an outbreak had not been declared, and the risk factors had not been identified at the time. Unknown to the research team, the highest number of injection-related cases had been diagnosed in the two months leading into Study 1 data collection [ ]. Indeed, HIV transmission and other bloodborne infectious diseases emerged as a clear theme while discussing injecting and syringe access with participants. One participant expressed their incredulity about the number of new cases of HIV in the community, stating that they:…heard there are eight new cases of HIV in Lowell. Why are there eight new cases when there are needles that are legal? You can legally buy them. (Participant 023, Female).

Rather than disbelief, other participants conveyed not only fear about contracting infectious diseases, but also shared the adaptive and protective behaviors they used to reduce transmission risk, like secondary distribution of sterile syringes, despite limited access to these materials:They [the mobile SSP] just started coming on Thursdays that I learned of, so if I don’t get enough on Tuesday, I get enough—because I sometimes just give people needles, you know what I mean, to try to keep down on the HIV, hep C. […] I have hep C, so obviously I’m not worried about that, but HIV, yes. Absolutely. (Participant 042, Male).

During Study 1, two participants disclosed that they had recently been diagnosed with HIV and a third indicated that their partner had also been recently diagnosed. Several service providers and other stakeholders expressed their concerns about possible other cases and newly diagnosed HIV cases in Lowell, a threat responded to by the mobile SSPs with increased hours of (official and volunteer) operation in the community. Only one participant knew of other sources for harm reduction materials to mitigate the community’s burgeoning safer injection needs:It’s [HIV] here. It’s in Lowell. […] If you don’t have money for needles, you can go and they’ll give you kits. You can go to [the health center] right now and get a kit that has a cooker, cottons, alcohol swabs, band-aids, just everything so you don’t get HIV. So, you don’t have to share needles […] So I mean, I’m glad that there is shit like that because I’m sure there would be more of a Hep C and HIV outbreak than there already is. (Participant 049, Female).

## Overdose Experience and Response
Experiencing or witnessing an overdose was a common experience among Study 1 participants. Three-quarters of participants (72.7%) reported that 911 was called during their most recent witnessed overdose experience. The Massachusetts Good Samaritan Law protects overdose victims (and those who call 911) from charges, prosecution, and conviction for possession or use of controlled substances [ ]. Although most participants reported engaging in 911 help seeking behavior during an overdose, interview data suggested that participants harbored negative sentiments and mistrust toward emergency personnel, specifically law enforcement, based on poor police interactions. One participant spoke about the social tensions between PWUD and the police:…My friend that overdosed, I heard about [the Good Samaritan Law] then, but I thought it was a crock of shit, so I didn’t really believe it. And then she kind of like verified it in jail…Big load of crap if you ask me. The police and the law, but mainly the cops don’t care. They truly don’t care. They think we just all should overdose and die and not get saved, to be honest. I’ve heard some say it. Yeah, when I got arrested the last time. His words were, ‘Too bad you didn’t overdose, so there’d be one less junkie on the street.’ (Participant 001, Female).

Similarly, 72.7% reported that naloxone was administered by someone in the most recent witnessed overdose. Despite this, over a third of participants reported either difficulty obtaining naloxone in the community or that they did not know where to get it (36.7%). Participants expressed the desire for increased access to and education about naloxone in the community:There needs to be a lot more places that can give out Narcan. Something that can bring addicts in, some way, like a meeting that addicts can go to and learn about Narcan, learn about saving people’s lives. (Participant 026, Male).

Participants who reported easy naloxone access usually sourced it, once again, from the mobile SSPs, as the only established programs at this time providing naloxone locally were a treatment center and the sexually transmitted infections clinic at the health center, and PWUD were reticent to go there to obtain just naloxone. One participant spoke highly of the contribution of the mobile SSPs to their community:It was awesome. They were all like, ‘Need any extra Narcan?’ They ask you how many you want. I have one on hand at all times and the rest I have at the house. I have maybe about six of them. (Participant 045, Male).

## Substance Use Treatment and MOUD
Most participants reported having experienced formal drug treatment at some point in their lives (86.0%). Formal modes of drug treatment (e.g., detox) and involuntary civil commitment (known as Sect. 35 in Massachusetts) were reported as recent and common responses to substance use. Current MOUD use was rarer (buprenorphine, 10.0%; methadone, 14.0%). As part of a post-overdose outreach model common in Massachusetts municipalities and federally funded through a special grant to Lowell Police Department, participants reported that police department staff engaged in outreach to local homeless encampments to facilitate linkage to drug detox programs nearby but rarely to MOUD [ ]. Although, within these interactions, police-led outreach teams also leveraged the application of Sect. 35, a Massachusetts state law that permits petitioning by police or others to forcibly, involuntarily commit someone to mandated treatment when they are at imminent risk of harm to themselves or others, with concerning impacts on overdose risk:Because they’re [law enforcement] so quick to Section [Sect. 35, involuntary civil commitment] people and shove them through a door and lock them up. Once they get out, these people just want to come out and use again, but heavier. Next thing you know it, you’ve got another fucking body. (Participant 016, Male).
Yeah. Because that’s what’s going on. People are going and getting sectioned and they’re getting forced into treatment and then they come out and use again and they’re ODing. So because I’ve had, since March, without fentanyl or heroin, I think my chances of overdosing are extremely high. (Participant 047, Female).

In addition to the city of Lowell’s preference toward detox-focused treatment models, according to participants, the methadone clinic moved from the downtown area—a more central location—to the outskirts of the city on the Lowell-Chelmsford line as part of a municipal re-zoning action, which posed further barriers to MOUD access for PWUD in the area. One participant described the challenge of accessing methadone in Lowell:There’s no methadone clinic in Lowell anymore. It’s in Chelmsford [the neighboring city], so you literally have to walk three or four miles to the methadone clinic to even get there, so it’s kind of stupid. They made it so far that it’s impossible to get there anymore… You can fill out a PT-1 [free transportation service through MassHealth, Massachusetts’ Medicaid program] form or whatever it is, like a transportation form, but a lot of people don’t qualify for it, so they’ll just have to walk there. So rain, snow, the whole nine yards… It’s not easy. (Participant 015, Male).

## Study 2: August-November 2019

## Socio-demographic Characteristics, Housing, and Substance Use
The majority of Study 2 participants were male (62.5%), between 31 and 45 years old (51.3%), White (72.5%) and non-Hispanic (77.5%) (Table  ). Most participants from Lowell and Lawrence reported opioid use (81.3%). In Study 2, the presence of fentanyl in the drug supply was more readily known among participants, as is evidenced by qualitative accounts of exposure to, and experiences with, fentanyl. Some participants discussed the strength of fentanyl relative to heroin, its potential consequences, including the short duration of the high and overdose, and the changing and unpredictable drug supply in Massachusetts:Heroin lasts a little bit longer, and fentanyl you get that rush, but you don’t get, like for me I get the rush, but I don’t stay high. I need to have benzos to stay high, and then it goes away and you need more. Like, I want to do a shot every 10 minutes rather than heroin, I can a do a heroin and be good for fucking five hours. Sorry. Fentanyl you want to keep shooting shot, doing a shot, doing a shot. So, I just think it’s, I mean you get a good rush, but it doesn’t last. (Participant HQ016, Lowell, Female).
’Cause fentanyl is really bad here, especially Massachusetts. ‘Cause back in Vegas when [my ex] was doing fentanyl, it was cut a certain way so that you wouldn’t overdose so easily. But like if he were to get a half here, the same amount as he was getting in Vegas, he would overdose instantly because the stuff here is super strong, super-duper strong, and I’ve witnessed it a couple times. (Participant HQ012, Lowell, Female).

Every three out of four Lowell participants from Study 2 reported housing instability, nearly double the rate of housing instability reported in Study 1 (Table  ). Lawrence participants reflected similarly high rates with roughly two out of every three participants reporting housing insecurity. Participants discussed the many challenges associated with being unstably housed (e.g., having to relocate when encampments are cleared) and its detrimental impact on efforts to reduce the harms associated with substance use (e.g., disease exposure, MOUD access issues):Well they [PT-1, free transportation service through MassHealth, Massachusetts’ Medicaid program] would, but it was just… so you have a time and an address that you have to be picked up at. So, I mean when you’re living outside, like, we used to get picked up right at the [church], and we had a tent right across the street. Well the city kicked us out… If we could have stayed there and our PT-1 pick us up, we never missed a day [at methadone clinic]. It started with like we were missing days, and we had missed two days, and they decrease your dose when you miss two days. So you’re sick, and then you don’t want to use, but yeah, it’s just, and I mean it definitely…we would have been doing awesome right now if it weren’t for that [the encampment clearing], because…I mean it definitely was an immediate factor of why we stopped going [to the methadone clinic]. And like the cops weren’t called there [the encampment] all the time. Like it was a group of decent people that you know, it just… I didn’t understand. They said it was like state land or something, I don’t know why they broke it up, but I mean it definitely caused a rift in a lot of the people who stayed there, that I know, that I was friends with, are worse off. They’re in a worse situation then they were when they were living there [the encampment]. (Participant HQ003, Lawrence, Female).

## Injecting Behaviors, Syringe Access, and SSP Utilization
Among Study 2 participants who reported injecting in the past 30 days (71.3%), the majority indicated daily use (82.1%) (Table  ). Participants reported a median of two uses of the same syringe before disposing (median = 2 [IQR: 1, 3]), a notable decrease in syringe reuse relative to Study 1 participants. A quarter of participants (26.1%) who reported injecting indicated that they shared drug supplies. Participants who reported sharing drug materials described that they only shared with a limited number of individuals, often a significant other or close friend:I don’t share needles. Yeah, cookers. All that. I don’t do any… If it’s not me or her, then I don’t… I guess I share with her, but if I don’t know ‘em and I don’t know what it is, I don’t do it. (Participant HQ013, Lawrence, Male).

Others disclosed that sharing supplies was reserved only for special circumstances but some, especially in Lowell, named these as occurring in times of desperation (e.g., lack of sterile syringes, withdrawal):I don’t share anything with anyone. Until my addiction got really bad…six months ago it like really, it just took off… And like if I didn’t have another needle like I’m borrowing yours. But like it was from like [significant other] or people I know… Really, it’s like out of like desperation and like lack of supplies at the time. Like sometimes, you know, we wouldn’t plan ahead. (Participant HQ016, Lowell, Female).

Lawrence received state approval to open a SSP in 2016, which was enacted in January 2017, while Lowell opened a privately funded SSP in 2018 (Fig.  ) following the sharing of Study 1 results with community stakeholders and the successful submission and funding of a grant led by co-authors (TS, WP). Interview participants shared the impact of the recently instituted SSPs in Lowell and Lawrence on their sterile syringe access. For example, one participant from Lawrence shared:I mean when I remember when I first started using, using the same needle for months. Not months, but like a month of like jabbing yourself, and it was awful. I mean having a needle exchange is… and the one here, I mean if you want help, they’ll help. Like they will help you. But you have to like really… they’re not going to like push you. (Participant HQ003, Lawrence, Female).

The overwhelming majority of participants who reported injecting (94.8%) indicated easy access to sterile syringes and other harm reduction supplies (Table  ). Among PWID, SSP utilization was high with nearly three out of four participants indicating the local SSP in Lawrence as their main syringe source (Table  ). The majority of PWID in both cities felt they were able to access SSPs when in need (86.0%), which is evidenced by a median of 7 [IQR: 3,15] SSP engagements in the past 30 days and a median number of 20.5 sterile syringes obtained while most recently engaging with the SSP (Table  ). While Lowell’s SSP was a new entity and privately funded for the first year of its operation through the Lowell Peer Education and Risk Reduction Services (PEERRS) Grant – a process that later required approval from the Lowell Board of Health approval (i.e., a declaration of a public health emergency) and Department of Public Health funding – frequent use of the services was evident. Demonstrating the essential role of SSPs among communities of PWUD, interview participants articulated their experiences accessing sterile syringes from the local SSPs in Lowell and Lawrence, in addition to other harm reduction supplies and services:I exchange every day. I’ll probably use, like, 30 or 40, well, between me and my boyfriend…This SSP is, like, the best place to go. Like, I come here, there are, like, counselors there that, like, they…I just have a good relationship with everybody here. They all understand…I love them all, yeah. Yeah, it’s amazing here, yeah…There’s more. Like, there’s not, like, counseling here, but they have, like, if you’re looking for, like, say you feel sick one day, like, they’ll have the little health clinic thing outside that’s like a mobile health clinic. Yeah. If you need help with your MassHealth (Massachusetts’ Medicaid program) or insurance, like, somebody will help you with that. If you want to be tested, Narcan, needles, like, everything is here. (Participant HQ014, Lawrence, Female).
It [the SSP] was interesting, I just got all of these supplies […] It’s like, they’re giving me cookers, cottons, tourniquets, alcohol swabs and I’m like, it’s weird because the state’s funding it, I’m assuming? […] They’re giving us all these supplies to use drugs […] But then it’s like, ‘Well, if you’re going to use drugs, do it safely because we’re concerned about spreading disease.’ You know. (Participant HQ009, Lowell, Male).

Not only was there noticeably greater availability of harm reduction supplies among Study 2 participants in the short two-year period following Study 1, but also most participants reported more syringe disposal engagement through SSPs as compared to Study 1 participants (Table  ). Almost no Study 2 participants reported accessing syringes through pharmacies, a noteworthy change from Study 1. Participants in Lowell indicated a median 1:1 syringe exchange ratio, which was described by one participant in Lowell:They give you a sharps container and they basically tell you to bring back the dirty needles. Once you bring back a sharps container filled with dirty needles, you will get new needles. (Participant HQ002, Lowell, Female).

In Lawrence, syringes were obtained from the SSP in larger quantities than they were returned (median difference of 10), an approach that met the community need, if not establishing policies to keep HIV at bay.

## Overdose Experience and Response
Similar to Study 1 participants, experiencing or witnessing an overdose was common for Study 2 participants, with a median of 4 witnessed overdoses reported in the past year (Table  ). When compared to Study 1, a similar rate of Study 2 participants reported that 911 was not called during their most recently witnessed overdose experience (around 30.0%), which may be a reflection of increased availability of naloxone in the communities. Perceptions of increased naloxone accessibility and increased administration of naloxone among participants (Table  ) may have resulted in effective overdose responses by laypersons, which is consistent with strong preferences among PWUD to manage overdoses amongst themselves [ ]. These actions, in turn, may have resulted in perceptions of reduced need for 911 (i.e., fewer calls made) and the EMS and/or police intervention that such a call entails [ ].
Participants’ reduced reliance on 911 may also be related to the continued tensions between communities of PWUD and law enforcement, especially among individuals living in encampments in Lowell and Lawrence. When reflecting on their relationship with law enforcement while being homeless, one participant stated: “Around here I think it’s just a ticking clock at this point, living in the park in a tent…Any minute now they might come by… Detectives have stopped by, I’ names, and seein’ faces, and threatening, and warning, and stuff like that…” (Participant HQ013, Lawrence, Male), while another participant stated, “I mean, when you’re out on the streets you really don’t get much sleep either. ‘Cause you’re constantly trying to worry about who is coming, who is around. Are the police going to catch me here?’ (Participant HQ018, Lowell, Female). Participants also expressed negative sentiments toward police based on prior experiences. One participant shared that they, “…avoid them at all costs. I don’t even go within five feet of them. I completely avoid the police. They treated me like shit.” (Participant HQ002, Lowell, Female). Another participant described multiple past traumatic experiences with a police officer in Lowell, which impacted her future perceptions of law enforcement and subsequent help-seeking behaviors:I was raped by a Lowell police officer […] this was years ago […] I’m the [person] that was coming from [location], and he […] he had took me there and forced me to give him head and threw two dollars and a pack of cigarettes at me the first time, and then the second time he forced me to have sex with him in [location]. So, this was a while ago…I was [a minor]. So, I don’t trust the police. I don’t have any respect for them. I mean they’re not all rapists, but I’d just rather not deal with them. (Participant HQ016, Lowell, Female).

Although some Study 2 participants stated not calling 911 in an overdose, naloxone administration during participants’ last witnessed overdose increased between Studies 1 and 2, with almost 90% of Study 2 participants reporting that naloxone was administered by someone at their most recent witnessed overdose experience (Table  ). Additionally, the percentage of participants either reporting “don’t know” or perceiving naloxone accessibility as difficult reduced four-fold between study periods. Demonstrating the increasing presence and accessibility of naloxone in Lowell among PWUD, one participant enumerated at least four locations where naloxone and other harm reduction supplies could be secured in Lowell:It’s such a system around here right now that everybody knows everybody that nobody is like really lost as to where to find it [naloxone]. It’s usually they go up to the [church where the SSP is located], which is…people call it the ‘Brazilian church’ around here… [and] Like next to the [gas station] on [street]. You can get them there like nothing. You get them at the hospital. They bring them around the shelter. They have like those… I don’t even know what they’re called. But like those like syringe clean, like safe kits. Yeah. Like those and Narcan, there’s no shortage of around here. Like there’s plenty of them. Everybody who is like a… Like a shelter or co-op [post-overdose follow-up program aimed at connecting people to services and reducing the recurrence of overdose]…they all have it. (Participant HQ010, Lowell, Male).

## Substance Use Treatment and MOUD
Current MOUD among participants was similar to that in Study 1 and low (buprenorphine, 15.0%; methadone, 25.0%) (Table  ). Continued low rates of current MOUD reported by participants in Study 2 can, in part, be explained by similar barriers to treatment experienced by Study 1 participants, including issues of access and transportation, as described by one study participant:Well, I need to get on the methadone clinic, and I need a way to get there every day ‘cause I am not walking my […] ass to [the methadone clinic] every fucking day just to get dosed, like, I’m not doing it. Transportation’s a barrier. I think that for addicts, there, like, you should automatically, once you sign up for the clinic, you should automatically get a PT1 [free transportation service]. I think they need to make it less complicated to get a PT1. (Participant HQ002, Lowell, Female).

## Sexual Risk, Infectious Disease Transmission, Testing, & Treatment
Most Study 2 participants in the communities of Lowell and Lawrence reported awareness of the recent cluster of HIV cases. Despite high awareness of the threat of HIV in the community and fear surrounding contracting HIV, most Study 2 participants (61.5%) reported being sexually active within the past 30 days (Table  ), while a few reported engaging in sex for cash or other goods within the past 30 days (7.5%) (Table  ). Additionally, 40.3% of participants in both Lowell and Lawrence still reported engaging in unprotected sex (see Table  ), of which some participants shared qualitatively that these encounters were mostly with sexual and romantic partners and, in a few cases, with strangers. One Lawrence participant shared that they rarely use protection but are selective in who they engage in sex with:I mean I have sex with people that I kind of know, you know? I don’t have sex with just random people. I don’t really use condoms. (Participant HQ011, Lawrence, Male).

Another participant expressed their concern with sex workers engaging in unprotected sex in their community without disclosing their HIV status:I know a majority of the people I know have both [HIV, HCV] or one or the other. So, yeah, I would say it is a big problem, and I think another problem is there’s the girls that work the streets out here have it, and they’re not telling the guys. So, they’re spreading it. Like, I know one girl in particular that is doing it, and she’s doing dates without condoms and not even telling these guys, so she’s putting people at risk you know? (Participant HQ016, Lowell, Female).

Other participants engaging in unprotected sex described use of protective measures during sexual encounters, such as serosorting (selecting sexual partners of the same HIV status), exchanging existing documentation of HIV status, and/or testing:… like, I was talking to this one girl, right? And she didn’t want to have sex with me because she thought I have HIV. So what she did was, she made an appointment for both of us and we went to check. So we got our results back and she was like, all right, you are clean. (Participant HQ001, Lawrence, Male).

All but one Study 2 participant reported partaking in infectious disease testing on multiple occasions, namely for HIV and HCV but sometimes including a full panel (i.e., testing for a wide range of viral and bacterial infections contracted through sexual activity such as human papilloma virus, chlamydia, gonorrhea and more). Most participants had been tested in a medical setting, including community health centers, hospitals, or doctor’s offices (Table  ). Most participants reported favorable testing experiences, such as the following:I think at the [Lawrence] clinic they do a really good job. They’re just like really friendly and they’re very open, it’s like a very like, you know, non-judgmental atmosphere. (Participant HQ013, Lawrence, Male).

While most participants in both cities reported that treatment for HIV and HCV among PWUD was generally “good and accessible” (Table  ), some reported difficulty with treatment linkage for HCV specifically due to issues of stability and continued substance use, shedding light on the intersectional risks PWUD face, as well as limited knowledge about treatment regimens. Two participants articulated these challenges:It’s positive again [HCV], so I need to get treatment for it…You can go to a virologist, and then they approve it, and then they… I don’t know what… […] Yeah, it’s pretty simple. I guess you can take, like, six pills a day or three pills a day, and it just kills it. [Interviewer: So how are you gonna get treatment for it? ]…I don’t know. I think I need to figure out how to get a place to live and a steady income before I even worry about that… (Participant HQ013, Lawrence, Male).
The doctor can see me and, you know, start me on treatment and things like that. But I haven’t followed up…But you know, I’ve been slacking on the follow up…Because, you know, things come up, drugs come up, and yeah. (Participant HQ014, Lawrence, Female).

Participants indicated a high level of awareness about the resources available to them in the community to treat and care for infectious disease. The majority of participants expressed a willingness to take daily preventative HIV medication (73.4%) and were knowledgeable of Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP) (72.6%). Relative to participants’ perceptions and knowledge of PrEP, uptake was lower (20.8%). One participant shared the difficulty in maintaining PrEP in the longer term:So, when I was on the streets still working, I was taking PrEP [from] a hospital in Boston gave it to me. After I had my surgery for my hand. I had an abscess and cellulitis there. So, I was in the hospital… yeah, I was in the hospital for two months, and, you know, they said, ‘Listen, you know, we’re not going to fool ourselves and say that you’re going to leave here and stay clean. Will you at least try this medication’ And they told me all about PrEP and what it does and how it’s supposed to help you stay safe if you’re in dangerous situations, you know, or if you choose to not use condoms. And, you know, so, that’s what I did. I took my PrEP everyday and… [Interviewer: Are you still taking it? ] No. I think it was actually just like I ran out of refills and I just didn’t go and get another refill. It wasn’t that like I just decided to stop taking it. I just ran out and never went back. But it was actually… I’ll say that it saved me…(Participant HQ008, Lowell, Female).

Of the seven participants who reported being diagnosed with HIV, six reported taking antiretroviral medications at the time of their survey or in-depth interview. Among those who self-reported living with HCV, some described “spontaneous clearance” (when the virus is no longer detectable; occurs more commonly among women) [ ], while only three participants were currently taking prescribed medications to treat their infection. Treatment or not, some participants reported taking additional precautions while living with HCV, per one participant’s description:Like I’d let anyone know who like wants to use anything of mine. I’m like, ‘I have Hep C, like I don’t know my genotype.’ Like I was like, ‘I’d prefer that you don’t borrow my stuff,’ but some people, you know, they’re really sick, they’re dope sick, they don’t care. (Participant HQ014, Lawrence, Female).

## Discussion
This case study documented the findings from two rapid assessments conducted in the midst of and subsequent to HIV outbreaks in Lowell and Lawrence, Massachusetts. The outbreak was linked to 166 cases of HIV from 2015 to 2019 and a subsequent outbreak in Boston traced to cases originating in Lawrence and Lowell [ , ]. The Boston outbreak persists to this day, and the fentanyl-driven overdose crisis has claimed nearly 12,000 lives statewide during the same period [ ]. We describe the life-saving, central role of volunteer harm reduction efforts early on in the outbreak, and the evolution of public health responses and activities that followed the initial HIV outbreak, weaving into this accounting of the perspectives of PWUD and PWID in these communities. The data were especially important in elucidating real-world experiences with IMF and the potential overdose and infectious disease risks within communities where exposures to synthetic opioids was increasing. At the time of Study 1, state, regional, and national leadership were focused on the role of prescribers and limiting opioid prescribing; few data pointed to IMF as driving these new and concerning health trends and fewer initiatives were designed to address it [ ]. Drawing on ethnographic observations and survey and qualitative interview data, our findings reflected high frequencies of injecting and syringe reuse, limited perceived access to syringe services and naloxone, and difficulty safely disposing of used syringes and other materials. Data collected during 2019 from PWID in two California cities identified IMF as a correlate to the high frequency of injection as compared to using other opioids like heroin, thus demonstrating the transmission risk of infectious disease [ ]. Our findings and others conducted thereafter [ – ] shaped how we respond to overdose and HIV in the fentanyl era. 
The centrality of injection-driven risk factors for overdose identified in Study 1 and for HIV transmission confirmed by other investigations conducted with the CDC pointed to necessary steps to expand harm reduction services, especially syringe access. Unlike the case of Scott County, Indiana, where an HIV outbreak investigation led to the establishment of an emergency mobile SSP that was subsequently closed [ ], the actions taken by the Massachusetts state and local communities embraced a longer-term investment. These unifying efforts to expand the services of a state-funded SSP in a health center in Lawrence—the Greater Lawrence Family Health Center—followed shortly after by the establishment of a brick-and-mortar SSP housed within a broader food and meals program, initiated through a grassroots collaboration among a community-based agency, the Life Connections Center, the Lowell Department of Health, and local academic institutions—the Tufts University School of Medicine and the University of Massachusetts Lowell [ ]. The public health response of instituting SSPs in communities with the intention of reducing drug-related harms is supported by strong empirical evidence encompassing their effectiveness, safety, and cost-savings in preventing HIV infection among PWID [ , – ]. SSPs aim to minimize the many harms associated with drug use, including reducing the spread of infectious diseases, formation of skin and soft tissue infections, and OOD, while providing a secure location, constant source for syringe disposal, and connection to social and health services [ , , , ]. In Lowell, that took the form of addressing food insecurity as well as harm reduction service needs and grew, in subsequent years, to bring in partnerships and co-location of services with the Lowell Community Health Center for health screenings and linkage to care, HIV testing and counseling by the Massachusetts Alliance for Portuguese Speakers, and the launching of a low-barrier harm reduction housing program onsite for unhoused PWUD during the COVID-19 pandemic [ ].
The rapid assessment method also uncovered areas where public health leadership could act to address substance use risks, harm reduction supply needs, new service connections, and substance use and infectious disease treatment gaps across two communities. Study 1 findings in 2017 suggested a high-risk environment of intersecting harms, including increased injecting episodes, unprotected sex, frequent involvement in sex work, infectious disease transmission, and overdose, driven heavily by the proliferation of IMF in these local drug markets [ ]. The analysis also pointed to social and structural issues, including housing instability; over-reliance on volunteer-run services; barriers to accessing harm reduction materials like sterile syringes, syringe disposal, and naloxone; and social tensions between law enforcement and PWUD that exacerbated the existing risks associated with IMF [ – ]. While Study 2 findings in 2019 cataloged the continued impact of the confluence of social and structural factors like housing instability [ ], social tensions between police and PWUD [ , ] and challenges accessing MOUD [ ] for PWUD in these communities, the data suggest that community and academic collaborations, and public health investments in local capacity brought about improvements in the uptake of harm reduction supplies and services, and suggest a better quality of life for PWUD living there. The HIV caseload and overdose deaths would likely have been much higher without the initial volunteer harm reduction group efforts, which paved the way for substantial harm reduction resource mobilization between Studies 1 and 2, including the independent initiation of the SSP at the Life Connection Center. In addition, the Department of Public Health partnered with local community organizations to expand services to address social instability and to increase knowledge about safe injection practices among PWID [ ], ultimately contributing to the body of literature on how behavioral and environmental factors intersect to exacerbate HIV risk among PWUD and support national and international calls for broad-scale investments in local harm reduction programming [ , ].
Findings from Study 2 chronicle positive harm reductive changes in the community of Lowell and nearby Lawrence following the initial HIV outbreak and public health response, including greater ease of access to harm reduction supplies like naloxone relative to Study 1 participants. While increases in naloxone education and distribution in communities of PWUD have long been associated with reductions in OOD [ ], naloxone distribution has also been shown to be cost-effective and to provide greater health benefits among PWID; naloxone distribution is maximized when combined with PrEP and connections to OUD treatment [ , ]. These data suggest that increases in naloxone access between Study 1 and 2 may translate to reductions in risk associated with OOD, as well as HIV and HCV transmission and warrant future study. 
MOUD utilization remained low between Studies 1 and 2, suggesting that structural risks in the community may be contributing to continued poor access. Our findings highlighted the challenges PWUD in these communities face when accessing MOUD, namely issues related to clinic location and transportation, which is not an uncommon challenge [ ]. Participants in Lowell described how the city’s decision to move a large methadone clinic from a central location to the outskirts of town impeded many PWUD from accessing sustained MOUD treatment. Prior research has demonstrated that MOUD reduces drug-related harm by decreasing opioid use and injecting [ ], suggesting that future investments to improve MOUD access and uptake among PWUD in Lowell and Lawrence could serve as an additional HIV prevention strategy. Expansion of COVID-19 era permissions on the use of take-home methadone, per new guidance from the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA), and allowing for telehealth for MOUD induction and care may be useful for many PWUD, but for individuals like our participants who are largely unhoused or lack access to a phone, such expansions may be less impactful. Pharmacy- and mobile-van based MOUD models may be more responsive to their needs [ – ].
Housing instability persisted as an environmental risk factor among Study 1 and 2 participants. Participants conveyed how being unhoused presented challenges that exacerbated and impeded efforts to reduce many pre-existent drug-related risks. Research has identified myriad risks and health harms with involuntary displacements of PWUD from homeless encampments, including increased risk of HIV transmission, disruption to healthcare, treatment, and medications; dismantling of protective social networks and loss of contact with outreach workers; incarceration; negative impacts on trust of law enforcement and other formal support systems; increased risk of overdose and hospitalizations; and greater mortality [ – ]. Housing stability reduces risky substance use behaviors, like increased injection and has been shown to be associated with reduced risk of transmission of infectious disease [ , ]. Our findings suggest that to maximize harm reduction infrastructure and mobilization efforts, environmental factors like housing instability and poor relations between the police and unhoused individuals must also be addressed.
Finally, we found high willingness to use HIV preventative medication like PrEP but very few participants reported ever accessing PrEP, irrespective of current HIV status. In a systematic review of the PrEP care cascade in PWID, Mistler et al. identified multi-level barriers to PrEP uptake and adherence among PWID and recommended that providers and sites like SSPs that offer testing services also prioritize linkages to care [ ]. With recent investments in local harm reduction capacity in Lawrence and Lowell, there is an opportunity to broaden this prevention pathway.
There are several important limitations to consider while interpreting the current study’s findings. Although two rapid assessments were included in this case study, the results cannot be interpreted as would the results of an experimental or quasi-experimental pre-post study design. The introduction and amplification of community-based efforts, unique collaborations, and state-led harm reduction efforts contributed to improvements in the communities of Lowell and Lawrence amid and following the HIV outbreak; plausibility but not direct causality can be inferred about these services. Study 2 included additional survey items that Study 1 did not capture. For example, Study 1 did not assess changes in sexual risk behaviors and infectious disease testing and treatment; thus these outcomes cannot be compared over time and improvements after the outbreak response cannot be deciphered. Further, while case study designs are an important means of documenting and investigating a specific event in great detail, they are also externally limiting in that study findings may not be generalizable to other locations in Massachusetts or beyond that are also experiencing HIV outbreaks and intersectional risk [ ]. Finally, because the two studies employed different sampling plans, we took care not to make direct statistical comparisons between them.

## Conclusion
Using a mixed-methods rapid assessment approach, the present case study chronicles both (1) the emergence of, and the contributing risk factors and environment, that led to a cluster of HIV outbreaks in the cities of Lowell and Lawrence, as well as (2) the impacts of efforts at both the local and state level to bolster harm reduction services, supplies, and access to PWUD in the affected areas. Findings suggest harm reductive improvements within the communities of Lowell and Lawrence following the establishment of formal brick-and-mortar SSPs as a touch-point for service connection and safe supplies. These changes are positive steps toward reducing the risk of OOD and mitigating future infectious disease outbreaks, but may be undermined without also investing in efforts to bolster drug treatment, PrEP uptake, and health service connections; address housing stability; and better align public safety, law enforcement, and public health goals.
