## Abstract
Breast cancer is one of the most common cancers affecting women worldwide. It includes a group of malignant neoplasms with a variety of biological, clinical, and histopathologic characteristics. There are more than 35 different histologic forms of breast lesions that can be classified and diagnosed histologically according to cell morphology, growth, and architecture patterns. Recently, deep learning, in the field of artificial intelligence, has drawn a lot of attention for the computerized representation of medical images. Searchable digital atlases can provide pathologists with patch-matching tools, allowing them to search among evidently diagnosed and treated archival cases, a technology that may be regarded as computational second opinion. In this study, we indexed and analyzed the World Health Organization breast taxonomy (Classification of Tumors fifth ed.) spanning 35 tumor types. We visualized all tumor types using deep features extracted from a state-of-the-art deep-learning model, pretrained on millions of diagnostic histopathology images from the Cancer Genome Atlas repository. Furthermore, we tested the concept of a digital “atlas” as a reference for search and matching with rare test cases. The patch similarity search within the World Health Organization breast taxonomy data reached >88% accuracy when validating through “majority vote” and >91% accuracy when validating using top n tumor types. These results show for the first time that complex relationships among common and rare breast lesions can be investigated using an indexed digital archive.
Keywords: digital pathology, histopathology, breast cancer, WHO, deep learning, search

## Introduction
Computerized calculations for a machine to emulate or even surpass human capabilities for performing a specific task are commonly referred to as “artificial intelligence” (AI). A physicians’ ability to identify patterns in data, such as images, and interpret those patterns in the context of other patient information as a whole is frequently a key component of diagnostic specialties such as radiology and diagnostic histopathology. Studies have shown that AI is capable of performing at least as well as human experts in a number of medical image–processing tasks. , , , Numerous fields of science, including those in health, are being rapidly transformed by AI and machine learning. High-performance computers have recently emerged to expedite the training and deployment of AI models. Recent results from the application of AI to patient data in the field of medicine, and more particularly in diagnostic disciplines, are encouraging. , In digital diagnostic histopathology, pathologists perform meticulous visual examination of whole-slide images (WSIs), a tedious task that is being increasingly supported by AI. Wider adoption of WSI analysis by the pathology community would facilitate improving the diagnostic process. , Pathologists are increasingly using digital pathology, which logically paves the way for computational pathology. The gold standard for many diseases is the visual inspection of tissue morphology on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained glass slides. Hence, we have focused on H&E images in this study.
In high-income countries, breast cancer is the most prevalent cause of morbidity and also the second leading cause of death from cancer. , Annually, there are 2.3 million new cases of breast cancer worldwide, accounting for 1 in 8 cancer diagnoses. Although mammography is a widely used radiology screening tool, it is still difficult to interpret mammograms. Even the decisions of expert physicians may exhibit variability, as accuracy in the detection of cancer varies greatly among professionals. An accurate and timely diagnosis is critical for reliable prognosis and treatment planning, which can significantly increase patient survival rate. , Early stage diagnosis 5-year survival is on average 91%, whereas late-stage diagnosis 5-year survival is on average 26%. Chemotherapy raises the 15-year survival rate for patients with breast cancer who are under 50 years of age by 10%; for older patients, the increase is 3%. , The majority of patients with breast cancer have invasive breast (ductal) carcinoma of no special type (50%-75% of patients), followed by invasive lobular carcinoma (5%-15% of patients), with the remaining cases having mixed ductal/lobular carcinomas and other uncommon or rare histologies. Breast tumor is a collective term for a number of malignancies with differing biological and pathologic traits, clinical presentations, therapeutic strategies, clinical behaviors, and prognoses. According to patterns of cell morphology, tumor growth, and architecture, there are more than 35 distinct histologic types of breast tumors. The most prevalent forms of breast lesions are invasive ductal breast carcinomas of no special type and invasive lobular carcinomas, although there are also a number of rare tumor types. The biology and behavior of uncommon tumors can differ, necessitating potential targeted therapeutic modalities. Several uncommon forms, such as “encapsulated papillary carcinoma” and “solid papillary carcinoma,” have distinctive symptoms and clinical manifestations. Some uncommon tumors, such as “metaplastic carcinoma” and “neuroendocrine tumors” are less well understood and may have more unpredictable outcomes. To customize treatment strategies and enhance outcomes, it is crucial for afflicted patients who health care professionals are aware of the numerous forms of breast tumors, including the rare ones. As insights from research are rapidly incorporated into clinical practice, the classification of breast tumors continues to evolve. Core biopsy, along with ancillary studies, such as immunohistochemistry and sometimes molecular testing, assists with rendering an accurate diagnosis and is widely used. In this study, the digital data of World Health Organization (WHO) from its breast tumor classification (fifth ed., 2019) with 35 different tumor types (containing both common and rare lesions) are processed, analyzed, and visualized using state-of-the-art deep-learning models. For the first time, this study introduced the t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) method as a visualization approach to assist cluster analysis for the entire WHO breast taxonomy data using deep features. This article is organized as follows: data acquisition, preparation, and deep model processing are described in the “Methods” section; results and analysis are presented in the “Results” section; and the “Discussion and Conclusion” section summarizes the findings and provides the concluding remarks.

## Materials and Methods

## Data Set Preparation and Acquisition
The data acquisition and preparation methods are described first, including annotations to focus on breast lesions when extracting features. Subsequently, we explain image representation via deep models, particularly KimiaNet, that has been developed primarily for histopathology images using the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) repository.

## Preparation
WSIs are gigapixel digital image files that exhibit extremely large dimensions (generally containing millions of pixels). At the highest resolution and for large tissue sections scanned on slides, WSIs can exceed billions of pixels, which may accordingly exceed the hardware limitations for necessary computations. , , For many computational pathology applications, working with a WSI remains a challenge mainly because of its gigapixel size. , Due to these factors, proper patch selection is the main emphasis of most computational techniques. , , An optimal patching algorithm serves as the “Divide” in a “Divide & Conquer” paradigm to solve a complex problem. Hence, patching should select a rather small number of patches (for efficiency) but should not miss any relevant patches (to ensure correct diagnosis).
In our experiments, patches of size 512 × 512 pixels were densely extracted (with 20%-80% patch overlap) from the WSIs within the malignant regions delineated by the pathologist ( ). The extent of overlap between patches is determined by the area of the tissue that has been delineated. If the delineated area is small, there is more overlap between patches to increase the number of patches. Conversely, if the area is larger, the overlap between patches is smaller, hence generating a smaller number of patches. The delineated regions may contain healthy/benign patches, which would then be falsely indexed as malignant patches and affect reliable information retrieval. To avoid additional visual inspections, “low cellularity” can be used as a filter to identify patches that are more likely to be benign or the connective tissue. Some cancers, such as carcinomas, may typically be characterized by excessive cell growth, which is mostly manifested in “hypercellular” patches. , We calculated the cellularity of each patch using H&E color deconvolution from red, green, and blue to H&E channels. , The cellularity ratio for each patch was then determined by creating a binary mask from the hematoxylin channel using an empirically determined constant threshold ( ). The number of white pixels in the produced mask over the total number of patch pixels approximates the “cellularity” in this context. shows a few samples of tumors with different cellularity levels, and patches with cellularity >8% were selected as abnormal patches and other patches were discarded as healthy/benign. Finally, every patch employed in the study was characterized as tumorous, being part of a delineated region outlined by a pathologist.
 

## Acquisition and Annotation
The Department of Anatomical Pathology, Singapore General Hospital, provided anonymized archival digital slides from the time period 2011-2017. Using the Philips Ultra Fast Scanner (Philips), the selected slides were converted into WSIs using the Philips Intellisite Pathology System (Philips). WSIs were subsequently downloaded from the Philips Intellisite Pathology System in the iSyntax format. In this initial study, 38 WSIs from different tumors were used, containing 35 lesion types, including some rare cases ( and ). From a total of 38 WSIs, 35 distinct WSIs (constituting 21,847 high cellular patches filtered from 32,530 total patches) represented our digital atlas using data from the WHO classification of breast tumors. A minimum of 216 and a maximum of 1002 patches were extracted from a total of 35 WSIs ( ). From the limited available data, the remaining 3 WSIs (2406 high cellular patches with the following primary diagnoses: 11, 12, and 32 from ) represented the challenging test cases for search and matching against our digital atlas.
 The Automated Slide Analysis Platform (developed by the Computational Pathology Group of the Diagnostic Image Analysis Group at Radboud University Medical Center) was used for annotations. All lesions were finely delineated with the polygon tool using high magnifications by a pathologist (R.G.) involved in the study. The specific magnification factors varied among different lesions and fields of view according to the degree of irregularity along the lesion borders. For noncircumscribed lesions (eg, radial scars), the annotation boundaries were arbitrarily selected to include only relevant epithelial and/or stromal tissue components (some annotated examples are shown in ). All annotated contours were exported to XML format to generate masks for patch selection/extraction ( ).

## Deep Models for Image Representation
Deep neural networks (DNNs) have supplanted other machine learning techniques for the recognition of visual objects. , DNNs have the intrinsic ability to learn complicated characteristics directly from image data, which is a significant advantage over conventional (handcrafted) computer vision algorithms. , The use of trained deep models enables us to define intelligent software to assist pathologists in diagnostic and research purposes. Whereas DNNs may be employed to make a decision, “representation” of diagnostic histopathology images through “deep features” is a pivotal aspect of DNNs. Various factors, such as color, cellular density, gland size, tissue textures, magnification levels, and other factors may be captured in deep image representation. Diagnostic histopathology images are represented by feature vectors, often called “embeddings,” a very compact representation that can be used for a number of tasks, such as identification, segmentation, categorization, clustering, as well as search and matching. Unstructured data can be represented by such embeddings in a d-dimensional feature space, enabling visual inspections in novel ways.
In our investigation, we used a pretrained model, namely KimiaNet, which is based on DenseNet-121 architecture. This model has been specifically trained on a vast data set of over 240,000 histopathologic patches sourced from the TCGA. It has the capability to differentiate among 30 distinct tumor classifications, encompassing normal tissue, benign neoplasms, and a range of malignant tumor types. We used this pretrained network as a feature extractor; no training was performed. This architecture is known for its ability to extract rich feature representations from images with densely connected layers. DeneseNet weights, the starting point for training KimiaNet, were trained using 1.2 million natural images to separate 1000 classes. Riasatian et al adapted the DenseNet-121 architecture by fine-tuning it for histopathologic images, emphasizing the importance of capturing their distinct features and morphologic attributes. This adaptation ensures the superior generalization of histologic attributes compared with the natural image representations.
The densely extracted patches from the annotated WHO-based WSIs were fed into the pretrained KimiaNet to generate embeddings from the last pooling layer ( ). The feature vector with a length of 1024 elements acquired from KimiaNet represents the tissue morphology of the patch and hence can be stored as the “index” of that tissue fragment in a digital atlas. Furthermore, principal component analysis (PCA) was used to reduce the dimensionality and pick the top 50 principal components (PCs) to remove noise and redundancy. Finally, these representations (the full-length vector and the top 50 PCs) can be used for cluster analysis and t-SNE visualization. , 
 

## Results
In this investigation, 35 types of breast tumors were analyzed and visualized using indexing images through a specific type of deep model called KimiaNet. Moreover, we introduced the notion of a digital breast tumor atlas that encompasses reference images of all breast lesions. Such an atlas can be employed for visualization as well as for matching new (undiagnosed) patients with standard cases in the WHO classification of breast tumors. We performed the experiments on a Dell Desktop with 1× Intel(R) Core(TM) i9-7900X Central Processing Unit (10 cores, 3.30 GHz), 1× Nvidia GeForce Giga Texel Shader eXtreme 1080 (Video Random Access Memory 8 GB), and 64 GB Random Access Memory (Dell Technologies and NVIDIA).

## Search and Matching
Verifying and validating histologic similarity is a challenging task. The ideal setting for a comprehensive validation would require matching many patients from different hospitals and visual inspection by multiple pathologists over a period of time. To quantify the performance of the search task in this preliminary research, we treated search and matching “like a classifier” to simplify things. One of the main advantages of employing classification methods is that they are simple to validate; every image can be classified as either belonging to a class or not belonging to it, which is a binary concept that can be quantified by counting the misclassified instances. However, the concept of resemblance in image search is fundamentally a gradual concept (ie, in many situations, the question of similarity may not be answered with a straightforward yes/no) and primarily a matter of degree (“very similar,” “quite dissimilar,” etc). In addition, based on existing literature, a distance measure is typically used to determine how similar (or dissimilar) 2 images are, a measurement that uses metrics, such as Euclidean distance that quantifies the dissimilarity between 2 given feature vectors representing the 2 images. , In image retrieval, Euclidean distance is favored over metrics, such as cosine similarity, because it accounts for both the magnitude and direction of feature vectors. In addition, it is a more intuitive approach. However, it is established that for retrieval purposes, cosine similarity provides similar performance as Euclidean distance. The classification-based tests we use may be overly conservative to evaluate the search results and blind to anatomical commonalities across many tumor types.
In this study, we used the Euclidean distance to enable k-nearest neighbor histologic matching to find the top-matched (most similar) embeddings when searching through the atlas to find the best matches for the evaluation samples. Although k-nearest neighbor is evaluated like a classifier, it provides a set of similar cases to explain its decision, an attribute that other classifiers do not offer. We can then retrieve the top-matched images from the atlas that meet the search criteria (nearest neighbors based on the Euclidean distance). For all the distance metrics calculations in search and matching, full-length feature vectors of the extracted patches are used. Computer vision literature focuses on top n accuracy (if any one of the n search results is correct, the search is considered to be successful). However, we use “majority n accuracy,” which is a much more reliable validation scheme for medical imaging; only if the majority among the n search results were correct, the search was considered correct ( ; in our case, n = 1, 3, 5, and 7).
 shows the detailed precision, recall, F1 score, accuracy, and the range of 95% CI of top 1 search results for histologic matching when treated as a classifier. Furthermore, shows the overall accuracy across diverse retrieval configurations using both the majority vote among n retrievals (MV-n) and top n retrievals for all the 3 test WSIs. The accuracy of MV-n persistently trails that of top n, as MV-n adheres to stringent criteria to establish consensus (ie, majority) among the n retrievals, enhancing retrieval reliability. When the results are the same for different n values, it is an indication that the majority of retrievals are correct for large n values. Furthermore, looking at the confusion matrix is another method for evaluating the effectiveness of the search ( ). The relative frequency of each subtype among the top n and majority n search results for a specific subtype can be used to determine the values needed to create the confusion matrices.

## Feature Analysis
Understanding spatial and temporal patterns can be aided using cluster analysis. Data patterns and anatomical groupings are detected using unsupervised cluster analysis when there is a higher degree of similarity between the points in one cluster than between the points in another cluster. Identification of meaningful and comprehensible groups in the data, which can help reveal underlying patterns and structures, is the major objective of cluster analysis. , Furthermore, clustering is crucial for offering a more explainable and understandable representation of complex data sets. , Additionally, cluster analysis can identify connections and linkages between data points that may not be obvious in the raw data.
In this study, for the first time, we performed Euclidean distance–based inter- and intraclass analysis of deep feature vectors for the 35 breast lesions listed in the WHO classification of breast tumors using 35 WSIs (see for the tumor types). We used k-means clustering to determine the centroid (ie, the typical instance for each tumor type) for each WSI. The Euclidean distance of each point (ie, each patch represented by its feature vector) can be calculated from the centroid for each tumor type. Subsequently, we can analyze the SD of the distance within each tumor type as shown in A. Furthermore, for intercluster analysis, centroids from each tumor type are used to calculate the single linkage among tumor types , , ( B). Cluster linkage aims to build a hierarchical representation of the data, with dissimilar data points being divided into different clusters at higher levels of the hierarchy and comparable data points being grouped together at lower levels. The hierarchical clustering single-linkage algorithm starts with each data point as a separate cluster and then iteratively merges the closest pairs of clusters into larger clusters until all points belong to a single cluster. In this study, we used this method to identify meaningful groupings and the nearest similar subtype ( B).
 Additionally, we also tried to retain the top 50 PCs of our 1024-length embeddings using PCA, from which we calculated the silhouette coefficient, Davies-Bouldin score, and Calinski-Harabasz score using both types of feature vectors (1024-length and top 50 PCs) of all patches and their corresponding labels ( ). These 3 commonly used measures of cluster quality can be used to evaluate the features of the atlas ( ). The silhouette coefficient ranges from −1 to 1, with higher values indicating better separation between clusters. With the full 1024-length vector, the silhouette coefficient was 0.14, but with the top 50 PCs of the vector, the silhouette coefficient improved slightly (which was 0.17; ). Similarly, a Davies-Bouldin score for the 2 types of vectors was also calculated. A Davies-Bouldin score of 2.17 was calculated for full-length vectors and the reduced vector scored 2.00 ( ). Ideally, a Davies-Bouldin score closer to 0 would indicate better clustering. The scores, we observed, indicate that choosing the top 50 principal embeddings marginally improves the clustering. In the end, the Calinski-Harabasz index (also known as the variance ratio criterion) was also calculated. For full-length vectors, a score of 666.79, and for top 50, a score of 822.22, for 35 clusters was achieved ( ). These numbers suggest that the clustering is relatively good, given the complexity of the data. However, clustering improved a little by removing the redundant components of the embeddings and just retaining the top 50 ones. Moreover, similar to other clustering analyses, we investigated the variance represented by the top 50 PCs, as shown in . The visual representation shows that the first 10 PCs encapsulate >50% of the aggregate variance, indicating a progressive attenuation in variance as it approaches the 50th component. Collectively, 50 components, selected from the original 1024 features, account for a substantive 85% of the cumulative variance ratio.
 

## Breast Pan-Subtype Visualization
For the visualization of high-dimensional data through scatter plots, the neighborhood- preserving multidimensional scaling method (short t-SNE) is frequently used.
In this study, we used t-SNE to visualize the patches of 35 subtypes ( A-C). t-SNE was used to generate the 2-dimensional projections for the feature vectors to visually compare the difference that we observed in feature analysis. This is an actual visualization of the breast cancer atlas although every group is one prototypical patient represented through its densely sampled patches. In A, we can see the embeddings of all 32,530 patches from the 35 primary diagnoses. Many benign or healthy patches that are mislabeled as malignant patches are seen here because there is not a distinct boundary separating them. Often, cancer patches, particularly in the case of carcinomas, exhibit hypercellularity. For this reason, we filtered 21,847 high cellular patches out to generate a more distinct visualization of subtypes in B, in which all the subtypes are more distinctly separated, a better reference for search and matching.
 To visually match the high-dimensional embeddings, we used the 2406 patches from 3 test cases (WSIs) to plot their embeddings in 2-dimensional space and overlaid on our atlas ( C). The bounding boxes in C point to the embeddings of test cases and their corresponding matching primary diagnoses.

## Discussion and Conclusion
The concept of building and using an atlas is not a new idea as it dates back to ancient times. However, the modern concept of a “digital atlas” as a well-curated collection of cases to be indexed using modern AI for search and matching is novel. In this study, we have used well-curated WHO breast images to create the first iteration of a digital atlas as a reference for search and matching. This preliminary study aimed at analyzing tumor types through their vector representations from state-of-the-art deep networks using cluster analysis and visualization techniques. The mere availability of such visualizations and mapping a new case/tumor into the atlas is a new venue for breast cancer researchers and could one day perhaps replace human expert consultation to help diagnose challenging breast cancer cases.
In our experiments, we successfully demonstrated that the concept of an atlas for matching breast tumors may provide value especially when we examine the entirety of known tumor types. Our results show >88% overall accuracy for all the 2406 test patches matching against 21,847 patches from the atlas ( ). Other than accuracy, we also analyzed the matching results on the basis of the majority vote and top n accuracy ( ). In the literature, the medical community prefers to use the majority vote approach considering the sensitivity of the domain. Additionally, rare cases are less well understood and may have more unpredictable outcomes. For this reason, for the very first time, we have introduced the top k most likely matching primary diagnoses among top n search results to narrow down the broad diagnosis observations on the basis of the matching confidence (percentage of matching instances), which can be seen in .
Analyzing the WHO breast classification of breast tumors book data with diverse lesion types ( ) provides an opportunity to examine the complexity of the diseases for breast as a primary site and identify meaningful groupings and linkages among different primary diagnoses. Because of this, we applied Euclidean-based inter- and intracluster analysis on feature vectors ( ). From A, we could see a high intracluster variation in “Carcinoma with apocrine differentiation” (subtype number 6 according to ) owing to the ink artifact in the WSI, which experts often use to identify and mark the resection margins during the gross examination of a tissue specimen. Carcinoma with apocrine differentiation is a rare breast cancer type and accounts for less than 1% of all breast cancer cases. For the intercluster linkage ( ), the centroid from each case (prototypical patch for a subtype) is used to provide an insight into the nearest possible subtypes, which is very important when observing rare diseases. From B, we could observe that “Invasive lobular carcinoma” and “Tubular carcinoma” (subtype numbers 14 and 34 according to ) have the shortest link with each other because they are both malignant, meaning that they have the potential to invade within the breast tissue and may metastasize to other parts of the body, though tubular carcinoma is an invasive breast cancer type with excellent prognosis. Invasive lobular carcinoma is a type of breast cancer that tends to infiltrate more widely than clinicoradiologically suspected as compared with invasive ductal carcinoma. It accounts for approximately 10% to 15% of all breast cancers, whereas tubular carcinoma is characterized by the formation of small, tube-like structures and accounts for approximately 1% to 5% of all breast cancers. On the other hand, “Intraductal papilloma” (subtype number 12 according to ) is distinct with the greatest distance from all other cases because it is a type of benign breast tumor that arises from the epithelial cells lining the ducts of the breast. It is a common breast abnormality accounting for approximately 10% of all breast biopsies. Intraductal papilloma is characterized by the growth of small, finger-like projections (papillae) within breast ducts. These papillae can be lined with cells that are similar in appearance to those found in the ducts of the breast and may contain a central core of fibrous tissue, blood vessels, and/or small ducts. Intraductal papilloma can occur at any age but is most commonly seen in premenopausal women. In addition, it is crucial to distinguish intraductal papillomas from intraductal carcinoma, a type of breast cancer that can have a similar appearance on mammography and biopsy. Although intraductal papillomas are benign, intraductal carcinoma is a malignant lesion that requires prompt treatment.
Finally, we assessed the patch embeddings statistically and visually using Euclidean distance–based cluster analysis and t-SNE dimensionality reduction technique. The full-length feature for inter- and intracluster analysis can be seen in , whereas, metrics show the quality of 35 clusters. We also used PCA to choose the top 50 PCs, which were then compared with the full-length vectors to assess the quality of these class-based clusters. The quality of the top 50 PCs was slightly better than the full-length vector clusters as shown in . Full-length vectors and the top 50 PCs were used for visual mapping, which can be seen in A-C. Visually, we have not observed any significant difference between full-length embeddings and the top 50 PCs, as we have in cluster quality metrics. Nonetheless, we had observed and differentiated the total number of patches ( A) extracted from the high cellular patches ( B), which were then used for the atlas. In B, we can clearly see the well-separated clusters for 35 classes in the WHO breast atlas when compared with A, which can be exploited for search and matching. C finally shows the visualization of the matching results using 3 test cases, as we can see the embeddings of the test images are overlapping or are in close proximity to their correct subtype patches in the atlas.
In conclusion of this preliminary work, we examined the idea of a digital atlas indexed by deep embeddings for search and matching in the diagnostic histopathology for breast lesions. We also analyzed and visualized the embeddings (generated by KimiaNet, a deep model specialized in histopathology via training on TCGA data) with 35 different primary diagnoses, including rare tumor types. Additionally, we analyzed the 50 PCs of the embeddings and compared them with the full-length feature vectors. Furthermore, we introduced the notion of “top-3@top-n,” a histogram of search results that the top 3 diagnoses suggestion concept when retrieving top n matching results to narrow down the broad diagnosis spectrum.
For future work, the atlas concept should be expanded to WSI or regional matching. On the other hand, it could also be used and tested in clinical practice as the “virtual second opinion,” which may improve the diagnostic accuracy, increase the efficiency, and reduce the variability among pathologists. Furthermore, should novel tumor subtypes emerge, the atlas can be easily updated by adding “new patients.” This ensures that the indexed repository can be updated continuously. As the concept of image search is inherently unsupervised, adding, and removing new patients or changing ontology would not require any retraining.
