## Editorial message
Every advancement in the field of laboratory animal science and technology, from exploring basic mechanisms to validating the development of new drugs, and from establishing disease models to responding to public health emergencies, strengthens the foundation for human health and well‐being, serving as an important cornerstone for promoting global scientific innovation. For this reason, Animal Models and Experimental Medicine (AMEM) has always been committed to its mission of building an international academic exchange platform and promoting mutual learning and communication in the global field of laboratory animal science and technology since its establishment. We are well aware that breaking down regional information barriers and gathering global wisdom is the key way to advancing the standardized development of the discipline and accelerating the translation of research outcomes. With this vision in mind, we are issuing a special report on the field of laboratory animal science and technology in Asia, which is compiled under the coordination of Prof. Mangala Gunatilake with contributions from 12 member organizations of the Asian Federation of Laboratory Animal Science Associations (AFLAS). It not only provides a systematic overview of the current state of laboratory animal science and technology in Asia but also represents AMEM‘s significant practice in fulfilling its core mission. The issuing of the special report is not an ending but a beginning. AMEM will continue to publish more special reports on the development and research progress in the field of laboratory animal science and technology from across the world in the future. We hope that the practical experiences and cutting‐edge research outcomes from different countries and regions can be fully presented and cross‐regional collaboration can be accurately matched through this series of measurement. We hereby sincerely call for the active participation from all countries and regions to share the industry updates, advanced studies, animal welfare standards as well as innovative experience and technological breakthroughs. AMEM will always provide opportunities for the international display of research outcomes and deep cooperation. Let us work together to inject continuous momentum for the breakthroughs in global life science research with laboratory animal science as the connecting belt and academic exchange as the bridge.

## Asian Federation of Laboratory Animal Science Associations (AFLAS)
The Asian Federation of Laboratory Animal Science Associations (AFLAS) is an alliance organization of laboratory animal science associations and societies based in countries and regions across Asia. It was founded on 29th of November 2003 by Professor Shigeru Sugano and Professor Kazuyoshi Maejima from Japan, Professor Qin Chuan from China, and Professor Sang‐Seop Han from South Korea as initiators, with a current membership of 12 member organizations. Year of establishment of member associations/societies are given in Table  .
 AFLAS, as an academic organization of laboratory animal science professionals in the Asian region, aims to promote through international co‐operation Asian Congresses on Laboratory Animal Science for the purpose of reviewing scientific, technical and educational problems in Laboratory Animal Science, to develop other relevant activities in the interest of Laboratory Animal Science, and to contribute to animal welfare.
The AFLAS Congress, held biennially, is a scientific event that has successfully been held 10 times in different member countries and regions across Asia. It brings together top scientists, researchers, industry experts, and academic representatives from the global laboratory animal science field, aiming to promote academic exchange and cooperation in the global laboratory animal science field (https://aflas2023.com/about/aflas.php and http://www.aflas2025.cn/#/en?id=47605346). ## CHINESE ASSOCIATION FOR LABORATORY ANIMAL SCIENCE (CALAS)

## Highlights of progress

## Research findings
China's laboratory animal technology has witnessed a leap‐frog development from scale growth to quality, with abundant project outcomes, including leading breakthroughs in gene‐edited primate models, technological outputs of humanized mouse models, a leading position in animal models for vaccine development, and tremendous progress in xenotransplantation.

During the COVID‐19 pandemic, China was the first to confirm that the novel coronavirus is a pathogenic agent with Koch's postulates, identified and verified that ACE2 is the main receptor of the virus as well as its role in the immune process. Mouse and rhesus macaque models were established, which elucidated the pathogenic mechanisms of the novel coronavirus and facilitated drug and vaccine evaluation.
A comparative study of primate genomes was conducted in 2023, using interdisciplinary technologies to investigate the origins of primate species, discovering a new case of hybrid speciation. The study reveals the evolutionary genetic basis of complex traits in primates such as brain, social systems, immunity, and behavior.
In 2023, a 3‐D embryo culture system using Cynomolgus monkey as a model has been developed, achieving long‐term development of primate embryos in vitro and setting an international record for the longest period of time.
An aging cell lineage tracing technique was built and combined with the system spatial transcriptome in 2024, which accurately located and tracked dynamic changes of aging cells and microenvironment in vivo and built an assessment system for the aging clock within primates.

## Laboratory animal welfare construction and development
Refining the standard system and ensuring the healthy development of animal welfare. The layout of national standards centered on laboratory animal welfare has been put forward, with 22 frameworks and over 200 standard planning. Currently, there are 92 effective national standards for laboratory animals in China and 167 association standards released by CALAS, with a standard system that has basically covered the entire life cycle of laboratory animals. Our standard system in laboratory animal science has been increasingly refined and international thanks to the efforts of CALAS and the National Laboratory Animal Standardization Technology Committee.

## Laboratory animal institutions assessment and accreditation
CALAS has established a domestic evaluation system for laboratory animal institutions, carrying out assessments of welfare and ethics. It helps comprehensively and objectively evaluate the compliance, welfare and ethics, and quality management systems of institutions engaged in the breeding and production of laboratory animals and in animal experiments.

## Educational training
China has built a multi‐level and systematic training system for professional talents in laboratory animal science through continuous exploration and practice, laying a solid foundation for the high‐quality development of life science research and bio‐medicine industry.

## School record education system
Nationally standardized textbooks are used by Chinese universities for the teaching of laboratory animals among postgraduates and undergraduates, laying a solid foundation for the standardized development of the discipline. In terms of high‐level talent cultivation, more than 30 universities, including Peking Union Medical College, Zhejiang University, and Sun Yat‐sen University, offer Master's and Doctoral programs in laboratory animal‐related fields, focusing on nurturing innovative research talent. At the same time, several institutions have established undergraduate programs, promoting the education of the fundamental discipline.

## Large‐scale non‐degree education
A well‐structured and large‐scale non‐degree vocational education system has been established nationwide in the field of biomedical research, including pre‐job training, professional skills training, and vocational competency certification training.

All personnel working with laboratory animals must undergo pre‐employment training to master the standardized procedures for laboratory animal experiments, in order to meet job requirements and ensure proper experiments and animal welfare.
CALAS has implemented the “Laboratory Animal Personnel Competency Assessment” system and established a complete professional capability certification system, which targets not only technicians, but also laboratory animal veterinarians who will receive specialized training and recognition. To date, more than 1200 individuals have obtained the competency certificate for professional personnel, over 70 have received the competency certificate for laboratory animal veterinarians, and more than 500 have participated in Advanced Training on Laboratory Animal Managers.
Non‐degree training in China has greatly enhanced the professional competitiveness of practitioners and contributed to the development of the laboratory animal industry.

## Academic journals
China has three national‐level academic journals—Animal Models and Experimental Medicine (AMEM), Acta Laboratorium Animalis Scientia Sinica, and Chinese Journal of Comparative Medicine. Among them, AMEM has been indexed by several major databases including ESCI, MEDLINE, PMC, CSCD, DOAJ, and CNKI, fully demonstrating its authoritative status and wide influence in the international academic community.

## Challenges and opportunities
Despite the remarkable progress that has been made, we still face challenges like insufficient basic research on laboratory animals, lack of original theories, techniques, and resources.
Opportunities come with challenges; in the future, we expect the following:

To promote the development and revolution of laboratory animal analysis techniques to achieve real‐time, dynamic panoramic observation and deepen the understanding of life processes and disease mechanisms;
To integrate bid data with cross disciplines to drive the R&D of human‐disease animal models in a more accurate, humane, and high‐volume way;
To promote the transformation of disease animal models into more diversified, complex and dynamic “humanized models,” enhance the analysis in‐depth of gene functions and disease mechanism, and provide a more predictable and irreplaceable “clinical pre‐trail field” for the evaluation of drug development and clinical treatment.

## Regional collaborations
CALAS has twice served as the AFLAS presidency and actively contributed to AFLAS's growth as an effort to promote the collaborative development of laboratory animal science in Asia. For instance, it advocated the introduction of new member organizations, revision of the statutes and by‐laws, and the establishment of AFLAS awards. At the same time, it has organized the 10th AFLAS conference, inviting experts and scholars from world‐leading institutions to deliver academic presentations and exchanges, soliciting academic papers and posters, and holding welfare training sessions and the young scientist camp during the Congress.
Meanwhile, CALAS has long attached great importance to in‐depth academic interaction and friendly cooperation with member countries and regions across Asia. It regularly organizes Chinese experts, scholars, and technical personnel to participate in academic conferences and visit relevant institutions, continuously expanding exchange networks and sharing advanced experiences. Starting this year, CALAS has innovated forms of cooperation by participating in in‐depth dialogue and collaborative discussions on cutting‐edge topics through regular roundtable meetings and other mechanisms, further strengthening the cohesiveness and innovative vitality of the Asian laboratory animal science community.

## CHINESE TAIPEI SOCIETY OF LABORATORY ANIMAL SCIENCES (CSLAS)

## Highlights of progress
Over the 2023–2025 period, CSLAS focused heavily on professional development and educational activities, evidenced by courses like “Advanced IACUC,” “Animal Handling and Welfare,” and practical workshops on small animal techniques and surgical skills. Novelties introduced include the establishment of a dedicated online professional course and a scenario‐based IACUC writing workshop, complemented by projects focused on optimizing digital learning mechanisms for laboratory animal science. Addressing ethics and the 3Rs principle was central, highlighted by the “Project on Enhancing the Quality and Management of Animal Alternative Experiments” and the “Revision of Laboratory Animal Care Guidelines and Use Guidelines for Taiwan” reflecting concurrent changes in regulatory frameworks and oversight, including external institutional audits. Research and technology progressed through training in pathological analysis and regional facility integration initiatives, the latter step enhancing reproducibility and translatability by standardizing environments. Finally, the commitment to international collaboration, welfare, and high standards—including the AAALAC International Conference—strongly underpins efforts to enhance the One Health concept through responsible animal science and the formal reporting of research findings at two annual academic symposia.

## Challenges and opportunities
The effective advancement of laboratory animal science faces inherent challenges and opportunities, with funding stability being a primary concern. CSLAS contends with the difficulty of inconsistent funding, creating a degree of operational uncertainty when driving core initiatives. To overcome this, the organization actively seeks sponsorship from the industry and leverages its extensive educational activities (such as the “Advanced IACUC” and surgical workshops) to generate income. Crucially, when financial capacity allows, CSLAS strategically utilizes these resources to sponsor young laboratory animal professionals and students, enabling their participation in various training programs and international conferences. This investment in the next generation helps ensure the future availability of skilled personnel, a critical resource for the field. Furthermore, CSLAS addresses public concerns over animal research by emphasizing ethical compliance and international collaboration. For the issue about setting of Asia‐specific guidelines adherence when reporting data similar to ARRIVE guidelines, CSLAS tends to follow the international guidelines, so CSLAS does not think it is necessary.

## Collaborations
A key strategic initiative for CSLAS during this period is the strong collaborative effort with the Taiwan College of Laboratory Animal Medicine (TCLAM) to establish a comprehensive internet‐based training curriculum. The central purpose of this partnership is three‐fold: to enhance research capabilities within the field of laboratory animal science by providing accessible, up‐to‐date professional knowledge; to actively promote the highest standards of animal welfare through specialized educational modules; and to facilitate the vital continuing education requirements for certified Laboratory Animal Veterinarians. By leveraging digital platforms, this collaboration ensures that professionals across Taiwan, China can access essential training regardless of their location, thereby driving consistent quality in research, care, and ethical practice, and supporting the overarching goal of optimizing digital learning mechanisms as outlined in CSLAS's annual projects.

## HONG KONG LABORATORY ANIMAL SCIENCES SOCIETY (HKLASS)
The Hong Kong Laboratory Animal Sciences Society (HKLASS) became a member of AFLAS at the AFLAS‐CSLAS Congress in 2025. Although the HKLASS was established in 2020 and the first committee formed in July 2024, Hong Kong's longstanding tradition of excellence in laboratory animal care and use dates back over four decades, beginning with the Faculty of Medicine at The University of Hong Kong, China receiving AAALAC accreditation in 2005. Building on this enduring foundation, HKLASS has currently 198 members from 8 institutions, promoting the advancement of laboratory animal science, welfare, ethics, and veterinary care in Hong Kong and across Asia. The society supports education, training, collaboration among professionals, and facilitates communication and meetings with similar societies regionally and globally. HKLASS has organized symposia in 2024 and 2025, both of which reinforced the 3Rs framework. The 2024 topics included reducing animal use in IVF through predictive germline transmission, refinement via AAALAC welfare standards, and sharing best practices across Hong Kong universities. The 2025 symposium expanded to cover replacement strategies, environmental enrichment, the vital role of animal technicians in ensuring reliable results, and topics such as experimental design, safe handling of carcinogens and toxic agents, microbiome research, emergency response, and GLP‐compliant practices. At the AFLAS Congress, HKLASS emphasized a Culture of Care that prioritizes animal and personnel welfare, institution openness, and research integrity, quality and reproducibility.

## INDONESIAN ASSOCIATION FOR LABORATORY ANIMAL SCIENCE (IALAS)
The field of laboratory animal science, which emphasizes the care, use, and welfare of animals in research mainly for human, animal and environmental health purposes, is an essential component of translational science. In the Indonesian context, this field is shaped by a unique archipelagic geography, diverse ecosystems and a growing national research capacity. Here, we summarize the current status, regulatory framework, key actors, challenges, and opportunities for laboratory animal science in Indonesia.
Indonesia comprises over 17 000 islands within 38 provinces, with a population of more than 280 million. Research infrastructure for laboratory animal science is primarily concentrated on Java Island, where major universities, government research agencies, and pharmaceutical/vaccine industries are located. The Indonesian Association for Laboratory Animal Science (IALAS) was founded in 2012 in Bogor, and that same year joined the regional federation Asian Federation of Laboratory Animal Science Associations (AFLAS). As of 2025, IALAS has 376 members drawn from scientists, veterinarians, technicians, IACUC members, lab animal breeders, students, and administrators across universities, CROs, and government agencies. IALAS works closely with the Indonesian Laboratory Animal Veterinarians Association (ILAVA), which is an organization established under the Indonesian Veterinary Medical Association. Active collaborations within IALAS and ILAVA have strengthened training, standardization, and advocacy efforts in the country.
IALAS' mission includes (1) enabling communication among professionals in laboratory animal care and use; (2) promoting laboratory animal science and welfare nationally; (3) supporting research and scientific use of laboratory animals; and (4) encouraging national standards/policies on animal welfare. The programs comprise regular trainings, workshops, seminars/conferences, institution‐specific in‐house training, and dissemination of information and resources to members on topics such as Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees (IACUC), ARRIVE Guideline, Norecopa's PREPARE, 3Rs (replacement, reduction, refinement) implementation, humane endpoints, animal handling and standard experimental procedures, animal models, facility management, and AAALAC accreditation. Under its website (www.ialas.id) IALAS offers a members‐only webpage as a comprehensive information resource, which is also available via an android‐based mobile phone application called P3HLInk.
Most common laboratory animal species used in Indonesia include rats and mice, primarily conventional strains and stocks, but the importation and use of transgenic and spontaneous mutated mouse strains are still rare. Among the other laboratory animals used are nonhuman primates (i.e., macaques), agricultural animals (e.g., cattle, swine, poultry, sheep, horses), and fish (notably zebrafish and medaka). Research topics mainly span tropical infectious diseases, metabolic and cardiovascular diseases, cancer, herbal studies, regenerative medicine, functional foods, biologics (e.g., vaccines, biosimilars), medical devices development, and 3R innovations.

## Highlights of progress
Indonesia has a legal framework for animal welfare. The principal legislation is Law No. 18 of 2009 on Husbandry and Animal Health that was revised into Law No. 41 of 2014, which includes provisions on animal welfare (physical and mental condition) for animals in husbandry and other activities. This regulation applies to vertebrates and some invertebrates that can feel pain. The implementing regulation, Government Regulation No. 95 of 2012 on Veterinary Public Health and Animal Welfare (PP 95/2012) sets out detailed obligations, including the Five Freedoms of Animal Welfare, with specific mention of the use of laboratory animals in comparative medicine. While mainly written for animal husbandry and veterinary public health, these welfare provisions provide an important legal underpinning for animals used in research. Importantly, current development in the animal welfare legislation was the inclusion of several articles on animal cruelty in the newly enacted Indonesian Criminal Code No. 1, Year 2023. The most srecent significant milestone is the issuance of the Minister of Agriculture's Regulation No. 32, Year 2025 in December 2025 regarding the implementation of animal welfare, which includes five articles (i.e., Articles 64–68) that outline animal welfare practice in comparative medicine. This marks an important progress in the regulation on laboratory animal welfare in the country as it is the first national regulation that stipulates the implementation of animal welfare in various aspects of laboratory animal care and use, including its accordance to the 3R principles, and the oversight by the institutional animal care and use committee.
Although not mandated by the law, in recent years more institutions in Indonesia voluntarily established their IACUCs. It remains a challenge, however, to effectively implement animal welfare in a standard manner, as reported in a recent 2025 cross‐sectional study which found that researcher knowledge of IACUC, ethical clearance and animal welfare in Indonesia remains variable. This, however, is an improvement compared to the finding in the 2020 study that noted some institutions lacked formal IACUCs, with researchers being reluctant to engage with IACUCs, and facilities often lacked welfare‐compliant infrastructure. This underscores the importance of the capacity‐building efforts undertaken by IALAS (e.g., trainings, workshops, seminars) but also points to deeper needs: standardized curricula, certification of personnel, infrastructure upgrades, and a structured system for enforcement of regulations.
Other recent advances in the country include implementation of a certification program for laboratory animal veterinarians. While a formal specialization track such as a residency training with a board certification system that can formally produce diplomates of laboratory animal medicine remains under development, the national certification exists to provide a more formal competence recognition for laboratory animal veterinarians.
Moreover, some institutions have been more active in conducting importation of laboratory mice and rats of various strains and stocks, supported by the fact that few institutions obtained national license as quarantine facility for laboratory animals. Notably, there is also a growing awareness of bio risk management with an establishment of ISO 35001 certification system in the country, and more interest shown by various institutions regarding AAALAC accreditation. These developments indicate a maturing landscape of laboratory animal science in Indonesia.

## Challenges and opportunities
Despite progress, the field faces several notable challenges:

Wide variation in the quality of program and facility, ranging from traditional laboratories to those with AAALAC‐level accreditation.
Many institutions lack barrier or containment systems suitable for transgenic or mutated models, limiting the opportunity to use more advanced model systems.
Absence of national certification or licensing for assurance of competence for animal care personnel.
Variation in IACUC practices (membership, authority, oversight) between institutions.
Lack of local specific pathogen‐free (SPF) rodent breeders; dependency on imports often subject to funding constraints.
Limited enforcement of welfare regulation in the research context and gaps in infrastructure, training, and standardization.
As challenges come with opportunities, the key opportunities for Indonesia include:

Development of domestic SPF breeding programs for rodents and zebrafish.
Introduction and expansion of nonconventional models (transgenic or mutated mice) as more advanced models.
Regional and international collaboration in 3Rs training.
Advancement of research with a focus on large animal and nonhuman primate models.
Utilizing Indonesian government–matching funds to support joint research such as with CALAS and JALAS and potentially other AFLAS member associations/societies.
Leveraging increased regulatory alignment and welfare awareness to raise Indonesia's capacity in biomedical research to harmonize with global standards.
The Indonesian experience reflects both the promise and the complexity of developing a robust laboratory animal science ecosystem in a country with diverse research needs, varied institutional capacities and evolving regulatory frameworks. The existence of national welfare law and implementing regulations is a positive foundation; however, the translation of these legal frameworks into research‐specific animal use governance remains uneven. The major challenge of restricted access to specialized animal models (SPF rodents, transgenic strains) and the infrastructural gaps in containment or barrier facilities may limit Indonesia's current ability to engage fully in advanced translational research. Addressing these gaps offers both a national benefit (reduced dependency on imports, cost control) and a strategic advantage (regional hub for laboratory animal science).
Laboratory animal science in Indonesia is at a developmental juncture: supported by positive legal foundations and an active community (via IALAS and ILAVA), but constrained by infrastructure, standardization, enforcement, and training gaps. The opportunities are substantial: aligning with global 3Rs standards, expanding model availability, strengthening IACUC and welfare oversight, and participating in regional collaborations.

## Highlights of progress

## Educational activities conducted
JALAS conducted several educational activities during 2025. ## Novelties introduced
Genome editing technology: JALAS members reported the advantages of new Cas protein, Cas3, for genome editing of laboratory animals and its practical commercial uses such as diagnostics of infectious diseases. , New analytical tools related to genome editing have enabled precise genetic modifications and identification of unintended genetic modifications. , Genome editing has been applied to a wide range of laboratory animals other than mice (rats, hamsters, opossum, marmosets, dogs, etc.). , , Knockin using AAV vectors is being developed for pigs.
Other new original technologies in Japan include low‐invasive imaging using functional MRI (fMRI) developed for awake marmosets to achieve functional brain mapping and evaluation of brain disease models. Unique disease animal models were established using artificial chromosome engineering technologies and have been used for drug discovery. , 

## How the research and technologies progressed—what steps taken to enhance reproducibility and translatability
Improving laboratory animal genetic reporting: LAG‐R guidelines. A new guideline that complements the ARRIVE guidelines by promoting accurate documentation of laboratory animals' genetic information in scientific publications to enhance reproducibility and appropriate model selection.

## Reporting of research findings
During the 72nd annual meeting of JALAS (May 2025), a total of 68 oral presentations and 118 poster presentations were given as general presentations. In the 2024 issue of Experimental Animals, 43 original research papers were published. JALAS members have published numerous findings in other scientific journals and academic societies.

## Animal use and selecting alternatives—adherence to 3Rs principle
Recent advances in JALAS reflect a strong commitment to the principles of the 3Rs. Alternative and complementary models such as virtual reality (VR) systems, organoid cultures, and organ‐on‐a‐chip technologies are being developed to reduce dependence on animal testing. In parallel, funding programs have been established to support research and studies promoting the implementation of the 3Rs and to recruit new projects in this field. The 72nd annual meeting of JALAS 2025 featured LAS seminars that further encouraged discussion and education on these topics. Additionally, innovations in animal welfare are progressing through refinement approaches, including improved anesthesia and analgesia protocols, enhanced environmental enrichment, and the adoption of individualized housing technologies when appropriate, all aimed at minimizing pain and stress while improving animal well‐being.

## Enhancing one‐health concept
JALAS has not undertaken any initiatives specifically promoting the One Health concept in the past 2–3 years.

## Challenges and opportunities

## Funding issues
In Japan, government competitive research grants have seen a slight increase, but the government budget allocated to national universities and research institutes is gradually decreasing. With personnel costs rising, it is becoming impossible to conduct sufficient research without securing competitive funding.

## Availability of resources
Japan's National BioResource Project (NBRP) provides important mammalian genome research resources accessible to researchers in Asia and worldwide. The paper highlights NBRP's systematic collection, preservation, quality control, and distribution of not only mice and rats but also other animal species, DNA‐related materials, human and animal cells, and microbes.

## Public concerns over animal research and how these were addressed or planning to address
Creation and distribution of educational posters advocating the importance of animal testing.

## High impact journal for publications and their charges—How they overcome these or planning to overcome
There is no specific way to address the issue. Experimental Animal, the official journal of JALAS, charges only ¥120 000 (JPY) for publication fees, and all papers are open access. While aiming for high‐impact journals, it is also important to develop journals that serve as our home ground.

## Do we need a set of Asia specific guidelines adherence when reporting data similar to ARRIVE guidelines
If there are Asia‐specific issues in the fields of animal experimentation and laboratory animal science and technology, they should first be identified, and efforts should be made toward global harmonization.

## PHILIPPINES ASSOCIATION FOR LABORATORY ANIMAL SCIENCE (PALAS)

## Introduction
The Philippines has made notable progress in promoting the humane use of animals in research and teaching. This progress is underpinned in Philippine laws, institutional initiatives, and professional efforts that emphasize ethical and responsible animal use. Anchored in the Republic Act (RA) 8485 or the Animal Welfare Act of 1998, as amended by RA 10631 (2013), the country's framework for laboratory animal welfare continues to evolve. , These laws mandate the ethical treatment of animals used in research and teaching, supported by the Department of Agriculture (DA) Administrative Order (AO) No. 40, series of 1999, which provides guidelines for the care and use of animals in scientific research and education. 

Over the past decade, the Philippine Association for Laboratory Animal Science (PALAS), universities, and research institutions have actively advanced laboratory animal science (LAS) education and the 3Rs (replacement, reduction, and refinement) principles, while confronting structural and logistical challenges inherent to a developing scientific community. This discussion highlights recent progress, identifies continuing challenges and opportunities, and outlines the country's collaborative engagements with regional and international LAS organizations.

## Highlights of progress

## Educational activities promoting humane use
Educational efforts have been central to the Philippines' progress in laboratory animal science. Various academic institutions and professional organizations, particularly PALAS, have led in fostering awareness and technical competency among scientists, educators, and students. These initiatives embody the principles of humane science and align with national and international ethical standards.

PALAS Annual Conferences
The Philippine Association for Laboratory Animal Science (PALAS) has consistently organized annual conferences that address emerging topics in laboratory animal science and welfare. These conferences serve as the main platform for sharing best practices, recent scientific developments, and policy updates related to animal care and use in research. Over the years, PALAS conferences have featured discussions on the refinement of animal use protocols, the importance of IACUC oversight, biosafety and biosecurity integration, and the promotion of alternatives to animal experimentation. Such conferences have significantly enhanced awareness of the 3Rs principles and strengthened institutional compliance with ethical standards. Recent PALAS conferences (2024–2025) highlighted the One Health Approach emphasizing the interconnectedness of humans, animals and the environment. In parallel, the PALAS‐PCLAM Joint Scientific Conference on Laboratory Animal Research (JSCLAR) has also been conducted since 2023, highlighting the importance of ethical research practices, regulatory updates and humane responsible use of laboratory animals.

PALAS–UP College of Veterinary Medicine Basic Laboratory Animal Training (April 13–14, 2023)
The collaboration between PALAS and the University of the Philippines (UP) College of Veterinary Medicine in April 2023 provided basic laboratory animal training to researchers, veterinarians, and students. This training covered animal handling, ethics, and welfare, focusing on laboratory rodents and small animals. The activity underscored the need for standardized animal care protocols and promoted competency‐based education in laboratory animal procedures. The inclusion of veterinarians and young researchers ensures the sustainability of humane science by cultivating a new generation of ethically aware professionals.

UP Manila Regular IACUC Trainings
At UP Manila, the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) regularly conducts training to enhance ethical review processes, protocol evaluation, and post‐approval monitoring. These activities help ensure that all animal research conducted under the university adheres to the Animal Welfare Act and international ethical standards such as those set by the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Sustained training also builds the capacity of IACUC members and researchers to manage ethical issues, improve animal welfare outcomes, and maintain compliance with local regulations.

UP Manila Department of Biology Workshop: “The Use of Invertebrate Model in Biomedical Research—C. elegans”
The UP Manila Department of Biology's workshop on Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans) represents a shift toward alternative and nonvertebrate models in biomedical research. This initiative is aligned with the replacement principle of the 3Rs, encouraging researchers to adopt simpler organisms that can provide valuable biological insights while minimizing ethical concerns associated with vertebrate use. The workshop also demonstrates growing institutional interest in diversifying research models and reducing reliance on mammals.

UP National Science Research Institute (NSRI) Regular Laboratory Animal Workshops.
The UP NSRI continues to provide hands‐on training and workshops on laboratory animal care, focusing on handling, restraint, anesthesia, and euthanasia techniques consistent with humane standards. These workshops emphasize the ethical responsibilities of researchers, promoting refinement techniques to minimize pain and distress. They also function as continuing professional development for animal care staff and researchers, fostering a culture of empathy and accountability in animal experimentation.

Central Mindanao University College of Veterinary Medicine Workshop on Animal Care, Use, and Handling.
In Mindanao, the Central Mindanao University College of Veterinary Medicine has conducted workshops to expand awareness and competence in laboratory animal handling and welfare. Such decentralized training efforts are crucial to ensuring that laboratory animal science education is not confined to Metro Manila or Luzon but is accessible across the country's major islands. These initiatives reflect progress toward equitable access to training and capacity‐building in humane animal use. Collectively, these educational efforts demonstrate the Philippines' commitment to promoting ethical and responsible animal research. They serve as vital channels for translating policy into practice and for reinforcing the humane use of animals across the academic and scientific community.

## Regulatory framework developments
The Philippines' regulatory framework for animal welfare in research is primarily guided by DA AO No. 40 (1999), which operationalizes RA 8485 and its amendment RA 10631. However, after more than two decades, the need to update this administrative order has become increasingly recognized. Emerging issues such as new animal models, advanced biotechnology, and education‐based animal use necessitate revisions that reflect current scientific and ethical realities. A key aspect under review is the limitation of animal use in elementary and high school education, where nonessential dissection and experimentation using live animals persist in some contexts. The intent of such reforms is to safeguard animal welfare while promoting the use of simulation‐based and digital alternatives, aligning with the 3Rs and global educational practices. Throughout these developments, PALAS has been consistently regarded as a subject matter expert (SME) by the DA and other agencies. The association provides technical input on proposed revisions and helps ensure that policy changes remain evidence‐based, ethical, and harmonized with international standards. This institutional recognition affirms PALAS' central role in shaping laboratory animal science governance in the country.

## Advancing the 3Rs principle
While the Philippines has made notable efforts to promote the 3Rs principles (replacement, reduction, and refinement), the implementation landscape remains uneven. Mice and rats continue to dominate as the primary laboratory animal models, with the use of alternative models, such as zebrafish (Danio rerio), fruit flies (Drosophila melanogaster), and nematodes (C. elegans), lagging. This limited adoption of alternatives may be attributed to resource constraints and insufficient training opportunities on these emerging model systems. Additionally, the scarcity of local suppliers and technical expertise for maintaining alternative models hampers their integration into research workflows. Nonetheless, ongoing workshops on invertebrate models signal positive momentum toward broader acceptance of alternatives. Universities and institutions are increasingly aware that promoting the 3Rs not only fulfills ethical obligations but also enhances research quality, reproducibility, and international competitiveness. Hence, continued capacity‐building and policy alignment are essential to drive the 3Rs agenda forward.

## Challenges and opportunities

## Resource scarcity
Despite progressive frameworks, resource scarcity is still a significant impediment to the growth of LAS in the Philippines. The limited source of laboratory animal species, specialized housing facilities, and trained personnel restricts the expansion of animal‐based research. Many institutions rely on imported animals and supplies, which increases the cost and complicates logistics, including procurement. Furthermore, the country lacks a dedicated national laboratory animal breeding and supply center, which could serve as a sustainable source for ethically bred, pathogen‐free animals. The establishment of such a center could significantly reduce dependence on foreign imports and promote consistency in research quality. Addressing these resource gaps requires strategic investment, public‐private partnerships, and stronger coordination among universities, regulatory agencies, and research networks.

## Limited institutionalization of IACUCs

Another pressing challenge is the limited institutionalization of Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees (IACUCs). Although mandated by RA 8485 and DA AO 40, many research institutions, particularly smaller universities and private laboratories, have yet to establish fully functional IACUCs. The lack of administrative support, awareness, and trained personnel often hinders compliance. Consequently, ethical oversight and post‐approval monitoring are inconsistent across the research landscape. Recognizing this, PALAS has intensified its IACUC training programs, assisting institutions in forming and capacitating their committees. The widespread institutionalization of IACUCs presents an opportunity to standardize ethical review processes nationwide and to cultivate a self‐regulating scientific culture committed to humane research.

## Collaborations
Collaboration has been pivotal in advancing laboratory animal science in the Philippines. Given resource limitations, regional and international partnerships have enabled knowledge exchange, training, and alignment with global standards.

## International collaborations
The Philippines, through PALAS and key universities, maintains collaborative linkages with major organizations such as the International Council for Laboratory Animal Science (ICLAS) and the Asian Federation of Laboratory Animal Science Associations (AFLAS). These partnerships facilitate participation in international conferences, access to updated LAS resources, and alignment with global ethical standards. Through these networks, Filipino researchers and veterinarians gain exposure to advanced practices in laboratory animal care and welfare, contributing to regional integration and capacity building.

## National collaborations and outreach
Domestically, PALAS continues to partner with universities, colleges, and research institutions to conduct training, consultations, and audits on animal care and use. These collaborations extend beyond veterinary and biomedical disciplines to include pharmacy, pharmacology, and biotechnology programs that utilize animal models. Such activities not only enhance technical skills but also foster a sense of shared responsibility among institutions to uphold humane and ethical research standards. Continued partnership across sectors—including government, academia, and industry—remains essential to sustaining progress in laboratory animal welfare.

## ANALYSIS OF PROGRESS OF AFLAS MEMBER ASSOCIATIONS/SOCIETIES DURING THE 2‐YEAR PERIOD
Review of information across AFLAS member associations/societies reveals a regional advance in laboratory animal science. However, there is a notable heterogeneity in capabilities, infrastructure facilities, and regulatory functions. China, Japan, Republic of Korea, and Singapore, having high‐income economies, demonstrate sophisticated technological development—including genome editing, primate models, and advanced imaging—while low‐ and middle‐income countries face persistent challenges related to funding, facility limitations, and inconsistent IACUC implementation.
Despite these variations, a consistent regional commitment to capacity building, ethical oversight, and adherence to the 3Rs principle is evident. Training workshops, standardization initiatives, and curriculum development constitute major strengths across associations/societies. Regional collaborations, particularly through AFLAS programs, have facilitated knowledge transfer and supported countries with resource constraints. The overall landscape reflects both rapid scientific advancement and ongoing structural disparities that require coordinated strategies for harmonization.

## REFLECTIONS AND THOUGHTS
The collective progress in the region underscores the centrality of education as the main mechanism for raising standards in animal welfare, research reproducibility, and ethical compliance in the laboratory animal science field. While technological advances are concentrated in a few countries, the region demonstrates shared recognition of the importance of alternatives to animal use and emerging non‐mammalian models.
At the same time, variations in regulatory frameworks, oversight capacity, and facility standards hinder consistent implementation of best practices. Countries such as Sri Lanka, Indonesia, and the Philippines highlight the impact of economic instability and limited infrastructure, yet also demonstrate resilience through targeted initiatives and international partnerships. These disparities reinforce the need for structured regional mechanisms that bridge gaps and promote equitable access to training and resources.

## RECOMMENDATIONS
Based on the information provided by AFLAS member associations/societies, in order to minimize variability, there is a need for the following.

## Strengthening regional training infrastructure
Establish AFLAS‐endorsed centers of excellence to provide standardized, competency‐based training in animal handling, IACUC operations, 3Rs implementation, and emerging technologies.

## Developing Asia‐specific reporting and ethical guidelines
Complement global frameworks (ARRIVE, PREPARE) with context‐specific guidelines addressing regional species diversity, resource variability, and local regulatory constraints.

## Enhancing access to specialized models and resources
Facilitate shared access to SPF colonies, transgenic models, primate resources, and advanced research platforms through structured inter‐institutional agreements.

## Expanding digital learning and certification pathways
Create an AFLAS‐wide e‐learning platform offering modular training, certification, Standard Operating Procedures repositories, and continuing professional development opportunities.

## Strengthening IACUC capacity and harmonization
Implement standardized training, regional accreditation, and peer‐review mechanisms to reduce inter‐institutional variability in ethical oversight.

## Promote collaborative research across member associations/societies
Encourage multicenter studies focused on regionally relevant diseases, welfare refinements, and validation of alternative models.
