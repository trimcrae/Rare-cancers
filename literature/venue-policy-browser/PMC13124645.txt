## Abstract

## INTRODUCTION
Awareness of Alzheimer's disease (AD) prevention remains limited in community settings, contributing to low adoption of evidence‐based recommendations. This study aimed to design a context‐tailored communication strategy for older adults and health‐care providers.

## METHODS
A three‐phase, multi‐method study using a design‐thinking framework was conducted in senior centers, including qualitative research, contextual observations, and participatory codesign.

## RESULTS
A total of 101 older adults and 54 health‐care providers contributed to the development phase. The resulting strategy, CULTIVAMENTE, uses positive aging imagery and nudge‐based cues to deliver evidence‐based AD prevention messages through posters, brochures, and websites. After implementation, older adults in intervention centers reported increased knowledge of prevention strategies (32.7%–61.4%) and more frequent discussions with health‐care providers (9.9%–21.8%). Health‐care providers reported high acceptability and use of campaign materials.

## DISCUSSION
CULTIVAMENTE is a low‐intensity and context‐tailored approach that was feasible to implement and supported improvements in AD prevention awareness.

Keywords: community‐based participatory research, consumer health information, dementia, health communication, preventive health services

## Highlights

This study is the first to describe the co‐design of an AD prevention communication strategy for older adults and healthcare providers in Latin America.
Using qualitative insights, contextual observations, and participatory design, we developed CULTIVAMENTE, a multi‐channel AD prevention awareness campaign delivering context‐tailored, evidence‐based messages.
The CULTIVAMENTE campaign was feasible to implement, highly acceptable to users, and increased dementia prevention awareness and provider–older adult conversations.

## INTRODUCTION
Scientific literature has shown that up to 45% of Alzheimer's disease (AD) and other dementia cases could be prevented if a series of potentially modifiable risk factors were addressed. Evidence from randomized trials has also demonstrated that interventions targeting multiple risk factors can benefit cognitive health and reduce risk. , Nevertheless, major challenges remain for the adoption and implementation of evidence‐based dementia risk‐reduction measures in real‐world settings. Clear and context‐sensitive communication about dementia prevention is essential, as limited knowledge and misconceptions may hinder the adoption of healthy brain lifestyles and preventive programs. , 

It is estimated that nearly 80% of people still believe dementia is a normal part of aging, and only 60% recognize that dementia is linked to lifestyle. Approximately 65% of health‐care providers hold similar misconceptions, which may reinforce stereotyped and fatalistic views about the possibility of preventing dementia. In the United States, fewer than 10% of older adults report having discussed ways to prevent AD or dementia with a health‐care provider, and non–evidence‐based strategies are often the most reported approaches for promoting cognitive health. This misconception gap is even greater in low‐ and middle‐income countries, which face both the highest burden of dementia and the highest prevalence of modifiable risk factors. , Designing and implementing effective solutions to challenge these misconceptions and reinforce evidence‐based recommendations is therefore critical.
Health communication campaigns aimed at increasing dementia prevention awareness represent a potential solution. These strategies use targeted messages and multiple communication channels to combat misconceptions and reinforce accurate information about dementia and its prevention. Although such efforts have been tested in various settings, not all campaigns demonstrated strong effects on knowledge or behaviors, , most have focused on the general population rather than health‐care providers, and only a few studies have addressed Latinx or Latin American populations. , , Moreover, many existing strategies rely on general, predefined messages that are not adapted to local linguistic preferences, social representations of dementia, or care settings, which may limit their effectiveness among older adults in diverse contexts.
To address these gaps and develop a communication strategy that could meaningfully influence dementia prevention beliefs and practices, we designed a dementia prevention awareness campaign to be implemented in senior centers in Latin America. Senior centers serve as community hubs for older adults, where activities related to dementia risk reduction (social, physical, and cognitively stimulating programs) are often part of the routine schedule. In the United States alone, > 11,000 senior centers serve one million older adults per day, and similar programs have been implemented in several countries as part of national aging policies. These settings represent an ideal venue for disseminating evidence‐based information about dementia prevention.
Within Latin America, Chile offers a particularly relevant context for this work. Although it has the highest life expectancy in the region, the country also faces a high prevalence of modifiable dementia risk factors (45%–56%), largely related to lifestyle and chronic conditions. , Since 2013, Chile has developed a nationwide senior center program targeting low‐income older adults living with frailty. More than 160 centers now operate across the country, staffed by health‐care providers who guide activities and support access to community services. This system creates a well‐established platform in which a context‐tailored prevention strategy can be developed and implemented.
Therefore, the aim of this study was to design, through a participatory and theory‐informed process, a context‐tailored health communication strategy to promote AD prevention among older adults and health‐care providers in senior centers, and to assess its feasibility and acceptability.

2.

## METHODS
2.1.

## Study design
This study used a three‐phase, multi‐methods design based on a design‐thinking framework to develop an AD prevention communication strategy tailored for community contexts. Design thinking, which integrates iterative prototyping, participants’ engagement, and real‐world contextual assessment, was selected because it is particularly well suited for developing context‐tailored, user‐centered tools. In this study, context tailoring refers to adapting message content and delivery channels to the linguistic preferences, social representations of dementia, and environmental dynamics identified within senior centers. This approach aligns with implementation science frameworks emphasizing the importance of contextual fit to enhance feasibility, adoption, and sustainability of interventions in real‐world settings. , 

The study was approved by the National Service of Older Adults in Chile (SENAMA), the Universidad Central de Chile Institutional Review Board (IRB), and the Yale University IRB, and was performed in accordance with the ethical standards as laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

2.2.

## Setting and participants
This study was conducted in publicly funded senior centers in Santiago, Chile, community‐based programs designed to support socially vulnerable older adults. These centers provide free services to low‐income, community‐dwelling older adults who often experience physical or cognitive frailty. Senior centers are administered by local municipalities and staffed by interdisciplinary teams of health‐care and habilitation professionals, including occupational therapists, social workers, psychologists, and physical therapists. Although they do not deliver direct medical care, staff routinely monitor participants’ functional and psychosocial needs and coordinate with municipal health and social services when required. As part of usual programming, older adults attend the centers twice per week for ≈ 2.5 to 3 hours per session. Each visit includes structured group‐based physical, cognitive, and social activities (typically 10–15 participants per group) and provision of a light meal (breakfast or snack). Centers also facilitate referrals to municipal social programs and public primary care services as needed.
Participants included in the study were older adults attending the senior centers and the health‐care providers who staffed them. We included community‐dwelling adults aged ≥ 60 years, without dementia or cognitive symptoms interfering with daily independence (determined by the health‐care team following Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders Fifth Edition criteria for major neurocognitive disorder); with at least 1 month of attendance; and with no terminal illness, severe psychiatric disorder, or significant sensory or speech impairment. Health‐care providers were eligible if they were staff members working at the centers during the study period.

2.3.

## Intervention development
The strategy was developed by a multidisciplinary team consisting of one gerontologist with expertise in dementia prevention, a graphic designer, and a publicist. Prototype materials were iteratively reviewed and refined with input from a geriatrician, a neurologist, two expert gerontologists, senior center staff, governmental stakeholders, and three community‐dwelling older adults. These contributors provided feedback throughout all stages to ensure scientific accuracy, cultural relevance, visual clarity, and feasibility within senior centers. ## RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: We reviewed the literature on dementia prevention awareness strategies using databases such as PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar. Most campaigns relied on web‐based materials, targeted the general population, or were implemented in high‐income countries, with limited evidence from Latin America or from settings involving interactions between older adults and healthcare providers. Few studies used participatory or design‐thinking approaches to develop contextually grounded prevention messages.

Interpretation: Our findings show that a participatory, theory‐informed communication strategy implemented in senior centers can enhance AD prevention awareness and stimulate conversations between older adults and healthcare providers. This study contributes new evidence from a Latin American context and highlights the scalability of low‐intensity, context‐tailored messages.

Future directions: Future research should test similar campaigns in diverse community and healthcare settings, evaluate long‐term effects on prevention behaviors and risk‐factor management, and examine strategies to optimize content delivery across digital and non‐digital platforms.

2.3.1.

## Phase 1: needs and contextual assessment
The aim of Phase 1 was to characterize existing knowledge, attitudes, contextual barriers, and opportunities for promoting AD prevention in senior centers to inform the development of a meaningful and contextually relevant communication strategy. Information was gathered from three complementary sources: (1) focus groups with older adults and health‐care providers, (2) direct observations of senior centers, and (3) a literature review of dementia prevention campaigns.
We invited 10 senior centers across Chile to participate in our focus groups. In each center, two focus groups were conducted: one with older adults attending the center (7–19 participants) and one with center staff and health‐care providers (5–7 participants). Sessions lasted ≈ 60 minutes, were moderated by two trained interviewers, and followed a semi‐structured guide. Both groups were asked the same core topics to allow comparison: cognition and aging, dementia and AD, risk and protective factors, attitudes toward risk reduction, current and feasible behaviors for dementia prevention, and preferred ways to learn about dementia prevention.
These discussions provided insights into knowledge gaps, misconceptions, cultural framing of aging, communication preferences, and contextual facilitators or barriers to intervention.
A trained observer conducted semi‐structured observations in participating centers to understand environmental and organizational factors influencing the feasibility of a communication strategy. Field notes were taken focusing on physical environment (wall spaces, lighting, signage, activity rooms), center dynamics and daily schedules, typical flow and engagement of older adults, interactions between older adults and staff, and existing communication materials or health‐promotion posters.
These observations helped identify where materials could be placed, what formats were most visible, and how information naturally circulates within centers.
We previously conducted and described a scoping review of existing dementia prevention awareness strategies without restrictions on language or publication date. Inclusion criteria were studies targeting the general population, using any research design, and assessing the effects of a dementia prevention communication or awareness strategy. Searches were conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Google Scholar, LILACS, and SciELO. After removing duplicates using EndNote, two independent reviewers conducted blinded screening of titles, abstracts, and full texts.

2.3.2.

## Phase 2: design of the health communication strategy
The development of the communication strategy was guided by three theoretical frameworks that helped determine the type of messages, imagery, and educational cues used in the prototypes. First, social cognitive theory highlights the role of environmental cues and observational learning in shaping health behaviors. Following this framework, we prioritized messages and visuals that could be easily noticed during routine activities in senior centers and that showed older adults engaging in behaviors related to dementia prevention. Second, stereotype embodiment theory explains how negative age‐related stereotypes, both internalized and reinforced through the social and physical environment, can influence cognitive and health outcomes. This guided our use of positive, realistic images of aging and the avoidance of an alarmistic messages linking dementia as an unfavorable outcome of aging. Finally, we incorporated nudge‐based strategies, which use simple, low‐effort prompts to increase awareness and support behavior change. These principles helped shape the wording, layout, and placement of educational materials. Together, these frameworks informed the behavioral mechanisms through which the intervention sought to influence attitudes and actions, while the contextual assessment conducted in Phase 1 ensured that message content and delivery were aligned with the linguistic preferences, social representations, and environmental dynamics of senior centers.
Information gathered from Phase 1 and the theoretical principles described above guided the development of the initial concepts for the communication strategy. The design team first defined the campaign's aim, key messages, and potential communication channels. This was done through a series of brainstorming sessions in which the team discussed how dementia prevention could be presented in a clear, encouraging, and contextually relevant way for older adults and health‐care providers in senior centers. Using these ideas, the team generated multiple low‐ and high‐fidelity prototypes, including early sketches, draft messages, color palettes, icons, and preliminary layouts. Each prototype was reviewed by members of the intervention development team, who provided feedback on clarity, tone, readability, and visual appeal. When needed, prototypes were adjusted to better fit the senior‐center context (e.g., wall space, lighting, typical movement of older adults through the center) and to align with literacy needs.
The design process was iterative, and a new version of each prototype was created after incorporating feedback. This cycle repeated several times, allowing the team to refine the messages, imagery, and layout before producing the final set of materials that were later evaluated in Phase 3.

2.3.3.

## Phase 3: implementation and feasibility assessment
Phase 3 aimed to evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of the communication strategy after its installation in senior centers. Feasibility was defined as the practicality of implementing the intervention within senior centers, including acceptability among users, integration into routine activities, and engagement with campaign materials. The strategy was implemented in three senior centers that participated in a previously described pragmatic cluster randomized trial. 

At 6 months, a random subsample of older adults attending the centers and fulfilling inclusion criteria were surveyed to assess their exposure to the strategy and selected preliminary effectiveness outcomes. These included the percentage of older adults reporting that they know ways to prevent AD or dementia, reporting that they had discussed ways to prevent AD or dementia with a health‐care provider, and indicating that they had received a referral to manage a dementia risk factor.
Health‐care providers staffing the senior centers were invited to complete a brief structured questionnaire evaluating their experience with the communication strategy. Feasibility‐related outcomes assessed after 6 months included acceptability and recommendation of the materials, frequency of use, perceived utility, and perceived efficacy.
Finally, to assess how the communication materials were integrated into the senior‐center environment, we documented the number of campaign materials available in each center and the observed interactions between people and the materials.

2.4.

## Data analysis
For Phase 1, qualitative data from the focus groups and semi‐structured observations were analyzed using open coding and inductive analysis following a grounded theory approach. Transcripts and field notes were coded, and themes were identified separately and in conjunction for each study population (older adults, health‐care providers, and senior centers). For the literature review, a rapid narrative synthesis was performed to identify campaign characteristics associated with effectiveness, including delivery channels and target audiences.
For Phase 3, older adults’ feasibility outcomes were summarized using descriptive statistics and analyzed using pre–post generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) with a logit link, accounting for clustering by senior center. Responses from health‐care providers and observational measures of intervention use were summarized descriptively.
Qualitative data analysis was conducted using ATLAS.ti. Quantitative data management was performed with Python (version 3.9.6), and statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.1). All analyses followed a significance level of p < 0.05.

## RESULTS
3.1.

## Phase 1: needs and contextual assessment
3.1.1.

## Focus groups
Table  describes the characteristics of the study participants by study phase. A total of 115 older adults from nine senior centers participated in focus groups. The mean age was 74.6 years, most were women (n = 91), and 8.7% (n = 10) self‐identified as Indigenous (Mapuche or Aymara). Nearly half had incomplete formal education (49.6%), and 43.0% had attended the center for > 1 year. In addition, 54 health‐care providers participated (Table  ). Their mean age was 30 years, most were women (n = 39), and they were mainly occupational therapists (25.5%), physical therapists (25.5%), and psychologists (19.6%). Thirty‐seven percent had > 5 years of experience working with older adults.
 Several key themes emerged as relevant for informing the design of the health communication strategy, and these are summarized in Figure  .
 Health‐care providers were able to correctly identify dementia symptoms and signs. In contrast, older adults described “Alzheimer's disease (AD)” as the most socially recognizable term related to cognitive health, whereas “dementia” was often confused with mental health and neuropsychiatric symptoms that can affect people of any age. Across groups, the term “prevention” was easier to understand and more commonly used than “risk reduction,” which was perceived as technical or unclear.
Health‐care providers mentioned a limited set of dementia risk factors, mostly genetics, education, and cognitive activities, and rarely identified lifestyle or chronic disease‐related risks. Older adults mentioned a broader range of potential risk factors, including social isolation, nutrition, and engagement with nature (e.g., gardening). Across both groups, cognitive activities (e.g., puzzles, reading, cognitive stimulation therapy) were viewed as the primary action for preventing dementia.
Older adults frequently referenced well‐known public figures (e.g., “If people like Ronald Reagan had Alzheimer's, with all their resources, why wouldn't we?”) to argue that dementia is not preventable. Participants had difficulty naming examples of older adults who represent successful cognitive aging, reflecting a lack of positive role models in this area.
Both groups identified age‐related stereotypes in health‐care settings as barriers to adopting cognitively healthy behaviors. Health‐care providers reported that negative assumptions about older adults remain common in institutions and may limit referrals or lead to premature normalization of cognitive concerns. Although older adults did not explicitly use the term “agism,” they described situations in which their memory complaints or other health concerns were dismissed as “normal for their age” or where they felt less prioritized because they were older. In contrast, when consultations were perceived as attentive and respectful, older adults reported feeling more motivated to follow cognitive‐health recommendations. Participants also noted that providers rarely discuss ways to prevent AD or other dementias.
Older adults primarily learned about brain health through vicarious experiences, including stories from peers, family members, and neighbors. However, they noted that respectful interactions with health‐care providers increased trust in their recommendations. Health‐care providers relied more on formal trainings and web‐based resources but reported limited opportunities to learn about evidence‐based dementia prevention strategies. Both groups expressed a clear interest in gaining access to reliable prevention information.

3.1.2.

## Observation findings
Observational data highlighted several contextual characteristics relevant for designing a communication strategy. Conversations between healthcare professionals and older adults were identified as key moments, with both groups regularly exchanging personal information and practical advice. Several areas within the centers, such as the waiting room, dining areas, and activity rooms, were consistently occupied and visited by both groups. These “hot zones” represented natural locations for communication materials, as they generated repeated exposure simulating an echo chamber.
Centers often displayed a large number of posters and printed materials, many of which were overlooked or treated as part of the background environment. This indicated the need for visually appealing materials with simple, high‐impact messages, preferably placed in less saturated areas where they could stand out. The health‐care providers’ office also emerged as a strategic location, as providers planned activities and met privately with older adults in this space, offering opportunities to reinforce prevention messages.

3.1.3.

## Literature review
Of 3901 articles retrieved, 18 studies met inclusion criteria and were analyzed (Table in supporting information). The most commonly used communication strategies were webpages (52.9%), social media (47.1%), talks or lectures (41.2%), massive open online courses (29.4%), and printed materials (29.4%). Campaigns varied considerably in the type of messages delivered, including brain‐health promotion, risk‐factor education, and dementia‐prevention messages. Many used similar visual aesthetics, often relying on purple‐based color palettes associated with dementia awareness.
Overall, 84% of the campaigns reported increases in dementia‐prevention knowledge, 53% reported improvements in attitudes, and three out of four reported increases in self‐reported preventive behaviors. Campaigns that (1) used three or fewer delivery strategies, (2) tailored messages to a specific population or setting, and (3) were implemented with targeted groups rather than population‐wide audiences were more likely to report positive outcomes.

3.2.

## Phase 2: design of the health communication strategy
Insights from Phase 1 were used to generate the core content, framing, and delivery channels of the communication strategy. Based on older adults’ and health‐care providers’ preferences, the contextual characteristics of senior centers, and the evidence from the literature review, the team concluded that a nudge‐based health communication strategy framed as a campaign would be the most suitable format for promoting AD prevention in this setting.
Because older adults and health‐care providers have different informational needs and ways of accessing health information, the strategy was designed using three complementary communication channels: (1) posters displayed in high‐traffic areas (“hot zones”) of the senior centers; (2) information brochures for older adults; and (3) web‐based materials, including a webpage for older adults and a separate webpage for health‐care providers.
Three low‐fidelity and two high‐fidelity prototypes were iteratively reviewed and refined with the full intervention development team, resulting in the final health communication strategy.
3.2.1.

## CULTIVAMENTE campaign
From this design process, the CULTIVAMENTE campaign (translation: “cultivate the mind”) was developed. The name reflects the metaphorical idea of nurturing the mind and cognitive health in the same way one cares for plants; an image that was familiar and meaningful to the participating communities. The campaign's visual design and summary of main components are presented in Figure  .
 The campaign aimed to empower older adults and health‐care providers with accessible and encouraging information about AD prevention, and to help make this a routine topic of conversation during provider–older adult encounters. Messages were designed to be simple, action oriented, and culturally relevant, addressing the gaps in knowledge and communication identified in Phase 1. For example, because cognitive stimulation activities were widely accepted and socially normalized as the primary prevention strategy, we deliberately chose not to include cognitive stimulation as a focus of the campaign. Instead, we highlighted preventive measures that were less socially incorporated but supported by evidence, such as hypertension control, nutrition, diabetes management, hearing loss management, and lifestyle behaviors including avoiding smoking, reducing alcohol intake, maintaining healthy sleep, engaging in social activities, sustaining a positive mindset, and participating in physical activity. This approach aimed to broaden community understanding of dementia prevention beyond cognitive exercises alone.
All materials presented prevention using positively framed language and imagery to counter negative age‐related stereotypes identified in Phase 1. Moreover, following recommendations for effective prevention messaging, each statement was accompanied by an impactful percentage derived from hazard ratios, illustrating how much of AD risk could be prevented or reduced when a specific action is performed. These percentages were included to give concrete meaning to the potential benefits of preventive behaviors and to increase message salience.
The strategy incorporated positively framed images of older adults who resembled senior‐center attenders and who were shown engaging in everyday activities associated with cognitive health (e.g., social activities, physical activity, hobbies). The text was brief and accessible, using familiar terms such as “Alzheimer's prevention” rather than more technical language. Colors, icons, and layout choices were adapted to stand out within the visually saturated environments observed in Phase 1 and to align with previous dementia prevention awareness campaigns.

3.2.2.

## Description of final materials

Posters were installed in the identified “hot zones”: strategic locations where older adults and health‐care providers routinely spent time, including activity rooms, lunchrooms, hallways, and providers’ offices. Figure  shows examples of the areas in senior centers where campaign posters were placed.
Brochures offered concise evidence‐based information about AD prevention, highlighting recommended behaviors such as physical activity and a healthy diet.
The general public webpage (www.cultivamente.cl) presented evidence‐based information on activities that may help prevent or delay dementia.
The health‐care provider webpage offered a learning section with brief training videos and curated, evidence‐based guidance on international dementia‐prevention recommendations.

 All the messages and information provided within the campaign material were based on scientific evidence from the World Health Organization's “Risk Reduction of Cognitive Decline and Dementia,” the Lancet Commission 2024 consensus, and the Alzheimer's Association report “Reducing Dementia Risk: A Summary of the Science and Public Health Impact.” 

The campaign materials produced through Phase 2 were then implemented and evaluated in Phase 3.

3.3.

## Phase 3: implementation and feasibility results
3.3.1.

## Older adults
The communication strategy was implemented in three senior centers, and 101 older adults from these centers were followed over the 6‐month period (Table  ). Older adults in the intervention centers showed increases in all three feasibility outcomes. The percentage of participants reporting that they knew ways to prevent AD or dementia increased from 32.7% at baseline to 61.4% at follow‐up (P = 0.002). Reports that a health‐care provider had discussed AD or dementia prevention increased from 9.9% to 21.8% (P < 0.001). In addition, the percentage of older adults who received at least one referral for managing a dementia risk factor increased from 53.5% to 73.3% (P = 0.018).

3.3.2.

## Health‐care providers
A total of 18 health‐care providers working in the implementation centers were included in the 6‐month follow‐up (Table  ). All of them reported that they would recommend the communication strategy to other providers (82.4% strongly agree; 17.6% agree). They identified the posters (55.6% very useful; 44.4% useful) and the health‐care providers’ website (52.9% very useful; 47.1% useful) as the most useful components. Most providers indicated that the strategy improved their knowledge about dementia prevention (strongly agree: 50%, agree: 44%), their attitudes toward dementia prevention (strongly agree: 61%, agree: 33%), and their preventive actions (strongly agree: 56%, agree: 33%). Furthermore, all providers agreed that the campaign supported improvements in older adults’ knowledge (strongly agree: 44.4%, agree: 55.5%), attitudes (strongly agree: 50%, agree: 50%), and behaviors (strongly agree: 50%, agree: 50%).

3.3.3.

## Use of the communication materials
After 6 months, all posters remained in place, and all brochures had been taken by center participants. Across the intervention centers (270 older adults attending the three senior centers and 18 health‐care providers), 23.5% interacted with the patient website (19.6% of older adults). Among health‐care providers (n = 18), 70.6% accessed the provider website, 58.8% viewed at least one training video, and 17.6% viewed two or more videos.

4.

## DISCUSSION
In this study, we designed a context‐tailored, theory‐informed health communication strategy to promote AD prevention among older adults and health‐care providers in Chilean senior centers. Using qualitative findings, observations, and a literature review, we identified key informational needs, environmental opportunities, and barriers, including the social images and language used to describe dementia prevention, the presence of negative age‐related stereotypes, and restricted opportunities for providers to discuss prevention. Guided by these insights and relevant behavioral theories, we developed the CULTIVAMENTE campaign, a multichannel strategy combining posters, brochures, and web‐based materials. When implemented in senior centers, the strategy was feasible, well accepted, and associated with increases in prevention knowledge, provider–older adult conversations about AD prevention, and referrals for dementia risk‐factor management. Together, these findings show that simple, low‐intensity, theory‐driven communication tools embedded in existing community programs can support dementia prevention efforts in real‐world settings.
Previously, we demonstrated that older adults at risk of dementia who were exposed to the CULTIVAMENTE campaign showed significantly greater improvements in cognitively healthy behaviors and cognitive performance compared to participants in control centers. Beyond these clinical benefits, the present study highlights that the campaign was highly acceptable and led to meaningful implementation outcomes for both groups, demonstrating its internal validity. These findings suggest that testing the incorporation of low‐intensity, nudge‐based messages into existing community programs may be a valuable tool for strengthening dementia prevention efforts, particularly in settings where resources for formal health promotion are limited. 

We found that designing a communication strategy that considers the perspectives of different actors, contextual needs, and the local social perceptions, combined with an iterative process of feedback and refinement, can lead to simple but powerful health communication messages. This participatory approach helped create materials that aligned with the lived experiences of older adults and the daily routines of health‐care providers. By grounding the strategy in participatory design principles, we were able to identify communication elements that may not emerge through top‐down approaches, reinforcing the value of co‐creation for developing dementia‐related interventions that are contextually grounded and meaningful for users. , 

Dementia is a culturally sensitive and often stigmatized topic, shaped by the language, beliefs, and symbolism within each sociocultural setting. , Research has shown that misconceptions about dementia risk reduction are especially common in low‐ and middle‐income countries, where the burden of dementia is highest and access to preventive information is often limited. Incorporating context‐tailored analysis and understanding local conceptions of cognitive aging is therefore essential for designing communication strategies that are not only scientifically accurate but also socially resonant. In our study, terms such as “AD” and “prevention” were more recognizable and acceptable to older adults than terms like “dementia” or “risk reduction.” This does not imply that all campaigns should adopt the same terminology, but it underscores the importance of adapting prevention messages to the linguistic and symbolic framework of each target audience. Such contextually attuned communication strategies are especially important in regions facing inequities in dementia prevention resources, where tailored messages may help reduce information gaps and support more equitable access to prevention knowledge. 

Another important finding was the emphasis that both older adults and health‐care providers placed on cognitive stimulation as the primary or sometimes the only strategy for dementia prevention. This reflects global trends, in which cognitive activities such as puzzles, reading, or group exercises are often seen as the most familiar preventive actions. , While these activities are beneficial, an exclusive focus on cognitive stimulation may overshadow other evidence‐based strategies, such as hypertension control, diabetes and hearing‐loss management, diet, physical activity, sleep, social engagement, and reducing alcohol or tobacco use. The CULTIVAMENTE campaign sought to broaden this perspective by highlighting preventive behaviors that were less socially incorporated but strongly supported by international guidelines. This approach aligns with findings from large multidomain trials showing that interventions combining several risk‐reduction strategies yield the greatest benefits. Awareness initiatives should therefore avoid reinforcing narrow views of prevention and instead showcase the diverse range of evidence‐based modifiable factors that contribute to cognitive health.
Although older adults and health‐care providers may have different informational needs, our results show that they share important concerns regarding barriers and opportunities for dementia prevention. Previous initiatives often targeted isolated levels, either the general population or health‐care providers; here we demonstrated the value of designing communication strategies that incorporate the perspectives of both. Our findings also highlight the central role of health‐care providers in shaping older adults’ engagement with prevention, emphasizing that communication strategies must address not only individual beliefs but also the structural barriers that limit providers’ ability to discuss dementia prevention in routine encounters. Because dementia is stigmatized at individual, structural, and institutional levels, communication strategies that support positive interactions across these levels may be especially impactful. This highlights the importance of creating communication environments that not only aim to influence individual knowledge or behaviors but also reshape the social atmosphere in which conversations about dementia occur. This relational dimension may be particularly relevant for dementia prevention in contexts in which older adults and health‐care providers interact frequently. 

In line with previous research demonstrating that physical health‐care environments influence trust, communication, and health outcomes, our study found that placing positively framed prevention messages in shared physical spaces was associated with more conversations and referrals related to dementia prevention. These findings suggest that modifying everyday environments through strategically placed, low‐intensity cues may serve as a practical and scalable approach to facilitate preventive discussions in other health‐care or community settings. Because the campaign relies on low‐cost materials and minimal training, it may be highly scalable and adaptable to other community or primary care settings. Future work should examine how placement in the environment, message design, and social context interact to influence prevention behaviors across other populations and settings.
In addition, our findings highlight the value of using multiple communication platforms. Although web‐based materials are increasingly common in dementia prevention campaigns, their reach may be limited in community settings. In our study, only 19% of older adults accessed the website, which aligns with previous research showing that < 14% of older adults visit university or foundation websites for health information, whereas most (87%) consider information from health‐care providers to be the most trustworthy. As many emerging dementia risk‐reduction initiatives rely exclusively on digital content, there is a risk that digitally literate older adults (who are often those with fewer social disadvantages) will benefit the most, potentially widening disparities for those with limited digital access or literacy. Strategically placed analog communication channels, such as posters and printed materials, therefore remain essential for maximizing message reach and ensuring that populations at greater dementia risk have access to evidence‐based information, and should be tested in larger implementation and effectiveness trials.
Finally, to our knowledge, this is one of the first and largest studies to develop and evaluate an AD prevention awareness campaign in Latin American populations and Spanish‐speaking audiences. Our findings demonstrate that tailored, evidence‐based messages delivered within participatory community settings can be a low‐cost and sustainable strategy to increase dementia prevention awareness. Given the high burden of modifiable dementia risk factors in Latin American countries and among Latinx populations in the United States, investing in linguistically meaningful communication strategies may be an important step toward increasing awareness, motivating preventive behaviors, and reducing dementia‐related disparities.
This study has some limitations. First, the communication strategy was designed and implemented in Chilean senior centers, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other community settings or regions. Second, most outcomes were based on self‐report, which may be affected by recall or social desirability biases. Third, although both older adults and health‐care providers contributed to the design process, including older adults from different places and sociocultural contexts may have led to additional refinements. Fourth, although we assessed website access, digital metrics may not fully capture the depth or quality of user engagement with online materials. Finally, as a pilot investigation, this study was primarily designed to establish feasibility and internal validity and was not powered to draw definitive conclusions about long‐term effectiveness or broad external applicability. Future research should explore mechanisms of action across diverse health‐care and community settings, and evaluate broader implementation in different settings and the long‐term impact of such interventions on dementia risk reduction.
In conclusion, designing theory‐informed health communication strategies using participatory approaches to promote AD prevention among older adults and health‐care providers can be an effective way to support dementia risk reduction in community settings. The CULTIVAMENTE campaign was feasible, well accepted, and associated with meaningful improvements in prevention awareness and provider–older adult communication. These findings suggest that simple, low‐intensity, and contextually tailored communication tools could complement existing dementia prevention efforts. Looking ahead, multi‐level campaigns that target settings in which older adults and health‐care providers regularly interact may offer a particularly promising direction for dementia prevention awareness research, and broader implementation of such strategies could be tested to strength public health initiatives aimed at reducing dementia risk in diverse populations.
