## Abstract

## Objective
Low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) continue to bear the major burden of morbidity and mortality associated with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) and life-threatening bleeding post-trauma. While bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and bleeding control trainings in developed countries have assessed their impact on bystanders’ knowledge and ability to act during an emergency, limited studies have been conducted in low-resource settings, specifically in the context of bystanders’ self-efficacy and confidence after training. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the impact of a novel bystander training program by assessing the change in knowledge, confidence, and self-efficacy of laypeople in performing CPR and bleeding control before and after training.

## Methods
A prospective study was implemented at a healthcare facility in a South Asian LMIC to determine the impact of a national initiative aimed at providing CPR and bleeding control skills to its citizens. Adult (≥ 18 years), non-medical employees of the institution were included. Data on participants’ demographics, knowledge, confidence, and self-efficacy in performing CPR and bleeding control were collected before (Time 1), immediately post-training (Time 2), and six-months post-training (Time 3). These responses were compared to ascertain the change in identified parameters using percentages and paired t-tests, with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

## Results
Of 200 respondents, 89.0% (n = 178) had no prior training in CPR or bleeding control. A significant increase in knowledge and self-efficacy was noted for both the skills from time 1 to 2 and from time 1 to 3 (all p-values < 0.001), without any association noted with their educational status. However, a decrease was noted in both these parameters from time 2 to 3, indicating the need for timely refresher trainings. A considerable increase was seen in both short- and long-term confidence of participants in performing CPR and bleeding control, with a notable decrease in fears/concerns about injuring themselves/victims or getting sued.

## Conclusion
The empowerment of bystanders as first responders can play a pivotal role in saving lives. Conducting biannual refresher trainings, encouraging bystander protection at a policy level, and expanding the program regardless of the participants’ education can make such training effective and sustainable in low-resource settings.

## Introduction
Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is a major cause of morbidity and mortality all over the world. Analysis of data from previously established registries in high-income countries (HICs) revealed increased patient survival and better neurological functionality with the provision of bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) [ ]. Such evidence led the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) to develop a chain of survival for CPR, which includes the crucial role of its awareness and education among laypeople who are often the first responders for OHCA [ ]. This adaptable model also applies to low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), which are responsible for approximately 80% of the global OHCA burden [ ]. However, such initiatives are still limited in low-resource settings, leading to persistently high OHCA-associated mortality [ ].
Another major cause of increasing mortality globally is injury secondary to road traffic crashes (RTCs), with 92% of these occurring in LMICs [ ]. A significant barrier to survival for such patients is the lack of timely hemorrhage control by bystanders, which can prevent mortality [ , ]. Data from the World Health Organization (WHO) predicts an increase of 40% in injury-related deaths globally by 2030, with a significant proportion affiliated to LMICs [ ]. Training bystanders with first aid skills, predominantly hemorrhage control post-trauma, can prevent 1.8-5% of associated deaths [ , ]. Strengthening the capability of bystanders to initiate immediate first aid can activate the chain of survival earlier, ultimately decreasing preventable mortality in low-resource settings [ ].
Pakistan is a South Asian LMIC, with a population of more than 225 million people [ ]. Rudimentary pre-hospital services within the country contribute to its high mortality secondary to OHCA and life-threatening bleeding post-trauma [ , ]. There is an extreme lack of bystander CPR, as reported by a multi-center study conducted in Pakistan, where only 2.3% of 310 OHCA patients received it [ ]. Another study from the region reported similar findings where none of the 187 traumatic OHCA patients received bystander CPR, even when over 50% of these incidents were witnessed [ ]. Bystander first aid to control life-threatening bleeding is also limited within the country, leading to injury being within the top ten contributors of morbidity, disability, and mortality [ ].
Owing to this burden, there is a dire need to implement national-level interventions in Pakistan that can empower bystanders and enhance their confidence in providing CPR or hemorrhage control. To bridge this gap, a national life-saving program in Pakistan, called the Pakistan Life Savers Programme (PLSP) was initiated in 2020 [ ]. This program, recognized by ILCOR as a modified solution for bystander CPR, aims to empower 10 million citizens in CPR and bleeding control through training programs [ , ]. While continuous modifications have been made to the program to ensure the provision of high quality skills to participants, limited assessments have been conducted to determine the program’s impact. Since it is an ongoing initiative with a long-term goal, it is vital to identify strengths, areas of improvement, and participants’ changes in behavior after receiving such training. Being a novel national training program within its initial years, these areas have not been explored prospectively and need investigation to better assess the association of such training with bystanders’ learning. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the change in knowledge, confidence, and self-efficacy of community members in performing CPR and bleeding control before, immediately after, and six-months post-training.

## Methods

## Study setting
The PLSP is a national initiative aiming to impart basic life support capabilities of CPR and bleeding control to 10 million citizens over a period of 10 years [ ]. This program was initiated at one of the country’s largest Academic Medical Center (AMC), the Aga Khan University Hospital (AKUH), by the Centre of Excellence for Trauma and Emergencies (CETE), a department dedicated to alleviating the burden of trauma in Pakistan [ ].
A previous study describes the methodology of the program’s implementation within the country [ ]. The trainings are conducted in collaboration with public and private partnerships in multiple settings nationwide, including hospitals, schools, and corporate organizations. However, to ascertain an adequate response for determining PLSP’s impact, this study was conducted at the AKUH, where trainings were conducted to teach basic life-saving skills of CPR and bleeding control to non-medical personnel of the institution. This study was carried out in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants gave informed consent before joining the study. This study received ethical approval from the Ethical Review Committee at the AKUH (Reference No. 2022-7767-22213).

## Study design and duration
This prospective study occurred over eleven months, from July 2022 to June 2023. A total of 13 training sessions were conducted within three months, i.e. from July 2022 to September 2022, during which participants were enrolled in the study. The included participants were then followed up to six months post-training to collect data. To ensure maximum follow-up, participants who did not respond at the 6-month post-training interval were followed for a maximum of 3 more months (a total of 9 months post-training). Each training session spanned over two hours encompassing both didactic and hands-on teaching for CPR and bleeding control skills. A detailed curriculum for this training has been described in a previous study [ ].
For this study, the PLSP sessions were conducted to include multiple departments of the AMC, including finance, nutrition and food services division, and safety and security. Furthermore, regardless of their occupational level, all employees of these departments participated in the training. Data collection for each participant occurred before the training session, immediately after the training, and six-months post-training. These time points are referred to as time 1, time 2, and time 3, respectively, within this paper. The pre-training form contained a consent section at the beginning with all relevant details, including the potential risks, benefits, and timeline of the study for data collection.

## Sampling strategy and participant selection
A sample size of 200 was calculated for the study to achieve 80% power with at least 10% increase in mean outcome variables and 30% or less change in the coefficient of variation at two-sided 5% level of significance [ , ]. Predicting an estimated 50% loss to follow-up [ ], the recruitment of approximately 400 participants was targeted for initial data collection.
Non-healthcare workers ≥ 18 years of age who attended the PLSP training sessions were eligible to take part in the study. Only participants who consented to take part in the study were included.

## Data collection tools
The study questionnaires contained a demographic section that collected information on the participants’ age, sex, marital status, affiliated department, occupational role, and the highest level of education received (Supplementary File ). Data collection primarily focused on three primary areas of learning CPR and bleeding control, i.e. theoretical knowledge, confidence, and self-efficacy in performing the learned skills. An extensive literature review guided the development of individual questionnaires. Each participant responded to all three sections of the questionnaire before the training (time 1), immediately post-training (time 2), and six-months post-training (time 3). To ensure the comprehension of the questions, the surveys were translated into Urdu, the country’s national language.
The details of the developed questionnaire for each outcome are as follows:

## Knowledge assessment
This section comprised 15 theory-based multiple-choice questions (MCQs) on CPR and bleeding control (Supplemental File ). Since the curriculum of PLSP training has been derived from the American Heart Association (AHA) and Stop The Bleed (STB) guidelines for CPR and bleeding control respectively [ , ], this section assessed participants on the taught content. The questions were adapted from previously published studies that utilized similar surveys to assess CPR and bleeding control knowledge among non-medical participants [ , – ].

## Confidence assessment
This section evaluated the participants’ concerns, fears, and confidence in performing CPR and bleeding control (Supplemental File ). The questions for this section were adapted from a previously validated study conducted in Malaysia that solely focused on CPR [ ]. The six-month post-training survey for this section also included questions associated with participants’ long-term confidence, their self-efficacy of performing these skills as a bystander using telephone assistance [ ], and experience of real-life performance of either CPR or bleeding control after the training (as applicable). While PLSP training did not test for bystander telephone assistance, this question was incorporated for the six-month assessment to inquire whether such an intervention could be helpful for participants’ recall of CPR and bleeding control steps.

## Self-efficacy assessment
This section was constructed by the authors (AAHM, HA) using Bandura’s guide for self-efficacy scales [ ]. Study participants self-reflected their efficacy in performing each step of CPR and bleeding control (Supplemental File ). The response scale measured self-efficacy from 0 to 100, where 0 referred to no self-efficacy, 50 depicted moderate self-efficacy, and 100 reflected high certainty in performing the specified skill.
Since follow-up was required for participants who provided consent, responses were stored in a password-protected file on a password-protected computer with access to only the immediate data collection research team. For analysis, all responses were anonymized by assigning each participant a de-identified number.

## Data collection procedure
Pre- and immediately post-training data was collected in-person via self-administered paper-based surveys at the training venue by four research members (AAHM, SH, UC, NA). The data was then entered on REDCap forms for ease of analysis. For six-month post-training data collection, participants who consented were contacted through their institutional email addresses or in-person visits by the research members to respective departments. Anticipating loss to follow-up, each participant was emailed at least thrice during the six-month post-training data collection, while the in-person visits were conducted at least twice within the respective departments to ensure adequate data and sufficient power of the study. The in-person visits were deemed to be more beneficial for follow-up data collection. Therefore, the respective managers of the departments were requested for their support and proved to be critical in collecting six-month post-training data since they ensured presence of training participants during the visit times or provided filled questionnaires within a week for those absent during the in-person visit. Lastly, participants with an office phone number on record were called during business hours as a reminder to fill in the form after the specified number of emails/in-person visits as applicable. Data was then entered electronically on REDCap forms accordingly.

## Statistical analysis
Data was analyzed using the Stata Statistical Software: Release 14. College Station, TX: StataCorp LP. Frequencies and percentages were calculated for categorical variables and means were derived for continuous variables. Paired t-tests were used to compare pre- and post-training mean percentages of knowledge and self-efficacy of participants who responded at all three data collection points. The comparisons included changes from time 1 to time 2, time 1 to time 3, and time 2 to time 3. To determine the association of participants’ education level with their learning and retention of knowledge and self-efficacy, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted. Since the level of education was defined into three categories, i.e., up to secondary level, intermediate and bachelor, and above bachelor, ANOVA was applicable. For all statistical analyses, a p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results
A total of 409 participants from 13 trainings provided their consent to take part in the study. After the removal of missing responses from pre- and immediately post-training surveys, 356 records were included for initial analysis. At six-months of follow-up, 200 participants responded to the survey (response rate: 56.2%), resulting in achieving the required sample size. Hence, the final analysis was conducted using the pre- (time 1), immediately post- (time 2), and six-months post-training follow-up (time 3) responses of the 200 participants.
Table  provides demographic information for the final cohort (n = 200). The majority of the respondents were males (n = 188, 94.0%) and were married (n = 153, 76.5%), with 11.0% (n = 22) participants having either no or only primary level education. Only 9.0% (n = 18) of respondents had prior CPR training, while 2.0% (n = 4) had prior bleeding control training. After receiving training, 4.0% (n = 8) and 5.5% (n = 11) of respondents reported performing CPR and bleeding control on a real-life victim, respectively.
 As shown in Fig.  , paired t-tests indicated a significant increase in knowledge for both CPR and bleeding control from time 1 to 2 (CPR: 44.1% to 65.8%; bleeding control: 54.6% to 70.4%) and from time 1 to 3 (CPR: 44.1% to 63.2%; bleeding control: 54.6% to 71.6%), with all p-values < 0.001. A decline was noted in CPR knowledge from time 2 to 3, with a mild increase in bleeding control knowledge. However, these differences were not significant (CPR: p-value = 0.1159; bleeding control: p-value = 0.5171). Figure  further shows the mean CPR and bleeding control self-efficacy at the three intervals. Paired t-tests for self-efficacy in both skills revealed a significant increase from both time 1 to 2 and time 1 to 3 (p-value < 0.001), with a significant decrease from time 2 to 3 (p-value < 0.001).
 The stratification of the mean change in knowledge and self-efficacy scores based on participants’ level of education did not reveal significant associations. While an increasing trend was noted in CPR knowledge from time 1 to 2 with increasing level of education, it was not significant (p-value = 0.061). Additionally, with increasing educational status, a significant decline in retention was noted for CPR knowledge at the six-month follow-up (p-value = 0.027). No trends were noted for the mean change in bleeding control knowledge. Similarly, while a significant difference was noted in the mean percentage change for CPR and bleeding control self-efficacy (CPR: p-value = 0.033; bleeding control: p-value = 0.031), it did not show an association with the level of education.
The study further revealed significant changes in all 15 individual items assessing knowledge for both time 1 to 2 and time 2 to 3 (p-value < 0.001). Figure  highlights the top 3 knowledge components that had the highest change from incorrect at the pre-training survey to correct in the immediately post-training survey. These included the minimum duration of pressure application to the bleeding site (66.0%), the required rate of chest compression (61.0%), and the assessment of absence of respiration (43.5%). Figure  further indicates the components with the highest retention (correct to correct) from time 2 to 3, specifically the identification of life-threatening bleeding (81.5%), appropriate method of covering of a deep wound (79.0%), and assessment of the victim’s consciousness (78.0%).
 A decrease was seen in participants’ fears and concerns about performing CPR and bleeding control from time 1 to 2, as shown in Table  A. However, an increase was only noted for high fear of acquiring infection through CPR (12.5% to 15%) and bleeding control (18.5% to 21.5%). In the long-term, fears/concerns of participants decreased from their pre-training responses in all domains, except for high fear of getting sued after performing bystander CPR (27.0% to 27.5%) and contracting an infection by performing bleeding control (18.5% to 23.0%). Responses showed a consistently high fear/concern of getting sued for performing both skills at all three survey intervals as compared to other concerns.
 With decreasing fears, a concurrent increase was seen in the participants’ confidence, as shown in Table  B. The increased confidence in performing both CPR and bleeding control skills persisted even at the six-month post-training assessment (CPR: 34.5% to 70.0% to 49.0%; bleeding control: 46.5% to 67.5% to 57.5%). A decrease was also noted in participants’ hesitance in performing CPR and bleeding control, both at time 2 and time 3 as compared to time 1. Approximately 45.5% and 57.5% believed it was easy to remember the steps of CPR and bleeding control in the long term, respectively, while more than half of the participants favoured the role of telephonic assistance in remembering the learned steps of CPR (56.0%) and bleeding control (63.5%).
A substantial increase was seen in the participants’ self-efficacy in performing CPR and bleeding control from time 1 to 2, as shown in Table  . Specifically, participants showed a notable increase in their ability to place hands correctly while performing chest compressions from pre- to immediately post-training (mean increase in the score: 64.6), while ensuring adequate depth of chest compressions had the highest increase from pre- to six-month post-training (54.1). Furthermore, as depicted in Table  , respondents showed a consistently higher self-efficacy in recalling ambulance numbers and calling them timely during emergencies at all three data collection points. While six-month post-training self-efficacy scores were higher than the baseline, all components indicated a decline in self-efficacy from time 2 to 3, with the highest decrease of 18.8 noted in identifying when to stop CPR.
 

## Discussion
The results of our study demonstrated adequate knowledge retention, confidence, and self-efficacy among non-medical personnel 6 months after CPR and bleeding control training. While a decrease was observed in these three domains from immediately post-training (time 2) to six-month follow-up (time 3), an overall positive change persisted from baseline pre-training scores to post-training assessments. Within both the domains of knowledge and self-efficacy, higher retention was noted for bleeding control components than for CPR. Furthermore, participants’ education level did not have an association with their learning and retention of knowledge. Respondents indicated a consistently greater fear of getting sued or facing legal issues in the event of performing any of these skills, followed by a fear of injuring the victim and contracting an infection from the patient. Self-efficacy in performing all steps of CPR and bleeding control increased from time 1 to 2, however; after six months, an overall decline was noted in self-efficacy scores from those measured at time 2.
A significantly positive change was seen among participants in our cohort for knowledge of CPR and bleeding control skills from time 1 to 3. Similar findings were concluded in a previous study done within the same city to assess knowledge retention of OHCA and CPR initiation among potential first responders (e.g., teachers and policemen) [ ]. Similar to our findings, this study demonstrated a similar trend in the mean percentage score of CPR knowledge (pre-score: 48.5 ± 13.5; immediately post-training score: 70.7 ± 12.2; six-months post-training: 63.5 ± 10.8) [ ]. Our results also showed a decrease in CPR and bleeding control knowledge after six months of training, however, it was not significant. This indicates that while decline in retention can happen within six months of training, it could be significant in the long-term, underlining the need for refresher training. A study from Korea indicated bystanders with a CPR training interval of less than 2 years to have significantly greater knowledge retention than those with a training interval of more than two years (adjusted OR: 3.03, 95% CI: 1.83, 4.99) [ ]. This decline in knowledge is also translated within the performance of the skills, where less than 50% of participants pass their recertification test one year after the training [ ]. Therefore, refresher courses are vital between six to twenty-four months of the initial training, with a course within twelve months preferable to achieve optimum outcomes.
Our study showed similar trends for bleeding control knowledge, where a significant increase was seen from both time 1 to 2 and time 1 to 3. A study from the United States reported similar findings where knowledge scores among non-medical potential first responders increased from 3.21 ± 1.14 pre-training to 4.63 ± 1.32 immediately post-training [ ].The Stop the Bleed (STB) campaign has conducted a few long-term studies to assess the retention of skills among bystanders trained for bleeding control [ ]. While our results focused on theoretical knowledge, these studies support our findings by indicating a decline in the adequate performance of bleeding control after three to nine months of training [ , ]. Therefore, it is vital to conduct refresher courses for both CPR and bleeding control among bystanders to equip them with adequate knowledge [ , ], which can translate into improved practical skills. Furthermore, as concluded by a previous study [ ], our results did not show a consistent trend in the learning and retention of the taught knowledge with increasing education of participants. It has been shown that people with lower education levels are less likely to be provided CPR skills and have fewer opportunities to partake in bystander training sessions [ , ]. Since acquiring knowledge about CPR and bleeding control is not associated with prior educational status, it is imperative to provide greater opportunities to neglected communities as a means to empower them with CPR and bleeding control skills.
It was interesting to note that a higher percentage of our participants reported increased fear of facing legal issues and contracting an infection at six months post-training as compared to baseline for performing CPR and bleeding control, respectively. Litigation fears have been continuously reported in the literature as a potential barrier for bystanders to performing CPR [ , ]. While these fears have been shown to significantly decline immediately after training [ ], our results portray an increase in these fears in the long term without any refresher courses. This is specifically relevant in the context of Pakistan, where public awareness remains limited on formal laws about bystander protection. This may discourage them from intervening in life-threatening situations despite receiving proper training. Additionally, more than 50% participants had positive feedback for the incorporation of telephonic assistance in remembering the steps of CPR and bleeding control, which could be leveraged for skills retention. This signifies the need to bring about national-level policy changes, such as the introduction of the Good Samaritan Law that can advocate for bystander protection and the promotion of telephone CPR (T-CPR) by emergency dispatchers that can enhance bystanders’ confidence [ , ]. Such policies need to be strengthened, and public awareness campaigns should be conducted on a national scale to ensure that every individual is aware of the legalities associated with bystander CPR and protection provided to the ones who perform bystander CPR or bleeding control. It is also equally important to inform the public about these policies through continuous awareness campaigns and the trainees through initial and refresher training sessions, along with keeping them aware of any changes within the policies to sustain low levels of fear and high confidence [ ]. Another potential reason for the increasing fear after the training could also be associated with the corruption in law-enforcing agencies and inconsistent enforcement of policies [ ], which can also be clarified in the training.
For bleeding control, litigation issues, contracting infection, and injuring the victim were the most highly rated fears amongst our participants before training. Arkoubi et al. and AlSabah et al. have highlighted similar barriers; however, none have reported the long-term impact of bystander training on these fears [ , ]. While these fears diminish immediately after training, they do recur to their original level, as shown by our study. This can potentially be due to the decreased knowledge overtime and strategies taught during the training of how to protect oneself from infection in cases of life-threatening bleeding, or prevent further injuries to the victim. Since a decrease can be seen in these fears immediately after the initial training, refresher courses and policy reinforcement can help sustain knowledge and awareness about legal policies among participants (both new policies and any amendments within existing ones), minimizing their fears and improving their willingness to perform CPR and bleeding control when needed.
Our results signify increased confidence and self-efficacy immediately after CPR and bleeding control training among participants. This is similar to previous studies published in Malaysia and Sweden which showed a significant increase in these domains after CPR training [ , ]. A study from India reported an increase from 37.5% to 95.5% in the confidence among bystanders to control haemorrhage [ ]. However, this rise in confidence and self-efficacy declined after 6 months, as also shown by our results. The 2020 International Consensus on CPR supports these findings through an array of systematic and scoping reviews, indicating that confidence in performing CPR declines 3–12 months after training [ , ]. Components with the greatest decline in self-efficacy at six-month follow-up in our study were identifying when to stop CPR and staying calm during emergencies. Potential reasons for this observation could be the limitation of actively performing these skills within a training or simulation and lack of instances to practically apply these skills during the six-month interval. It is vital to consider the elements with greatest decline within the initial and refresher trainings and focus more on the components which cannot be actively assessed in these trainings through reiterations. An important finding of our study is the increase in participants’ confidence in performing CPR and bleeding control concurrently with a decrease in associated fears. This finding, coupled with those from the literature [ , , ], can be leveraged to hypothesize that refresher courses for bystanders at least every 6 months or at most within a year of the initial training can provide them with the confidence to perform life-saving skills, potentially leading to a decrease in the burden of OHCA and life-threatening bleeding after trauma. This interval can also ensure the prevention of excessive burden of resources (trainers, funding, etc.) in the context of low-resource settings. Alternatively, blended learning models can be utilized for refresher bystander trainings as a cost-effective approach [ ]. However, such an intervention requires access to mobiles phones and internet connectivity, which can only be suitable for limited regions and populations of Pakistan, owing to the differences in internet connectivity, literacy, and availability of resources within the country [ , ].
Our study presents novel findings for long-term knowledge, confidence, and self-efficacy in CPR and bleeding control, the combination of which has not been assessed previously within the same population from an LMIC perspective. However, there are some limitations as well. While knowledge, fears, confidence, and self-efficacy were effectively evaluated, the practical skills of CPR and bleeding control in the long term were not determined. This was majorly due to the limited time and resources at the follow-up of participants, which could have provided a holistic view of the training. The study was conducted at a single center, with the majority of the participants being males and staff-level employees. These factors might limit generalizability. However, our participants belonged to diverse educational and socioeconomic levels that can allow extrapolation of the findings to similar low-resource settings. Having a longer follow-up time with multiple checkpoints in future studies can provide evidence for the exact refresher training interval. Assessing the skills during these checkpoints can further strengthen the refresher course curriculum. Comparison between the rate of bystander CPR and bleeding control through hospital and emergency medical service (EMS) registries of the country and assessing its trend after 75%-80% completion of PLSP can further support the need for bystander CPR and bleeding control in low-resource settings.

## Conclusion
Empowering bystanders to perform CPR and bleeding control can prove to be an effective and sustainable step in decreasing associated preventable mortality, specifically through an increase in their self-efficacy. Given the six-month follow-up data in this study, biannual refresher courses for both CPR and bleeding control within bystander training can sustain their knowledge, confidence, self-efficacy, and skills. Further research can assess the impact of refresher training on long-term retention of these skills. As shown by our results, the education level of participants does not impact long-term retention. Therefore, such community training should be expanded to lesser privileged areas to maximize the impact of bystander response in OHCA and life-threatening bleeding. Strengthening national-level policies for bystander protection can further bolster confidence and reduce litigation fears, ultimately leading to a large-scale impact in reducing preventable mortality in LMICs.
