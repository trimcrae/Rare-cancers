## Abstract

## Objectives
The aim of this study was to assess the reporting quality of rapid reviews (RRs) against the Cochrane Rapid Reviews Methodological Guidance to identify areas for improvement.

## Methods
A literature quality assessment was conducted through systematic searches in PubMed, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science until February 28, 2023. An expert guided the search strategy, and the reporting quality of RRs was evaluated. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize reporting quality, and subgroup analyses were performed to examine differences between groups. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square (χ2) test to identify statistically significant differences in reporting adherence across different subgroups.

## Results
Among the 112 rapid reviews analyzed, fewer than 50% fully reported four key methodological components: topic refinement with stakeholders, eligibility criteria co-definition, risk of bias assessment tools, and protocol/software reporting. Reports published after 2021 demonstrated slightly higher overall quality compared to those published before 2020, with significant improvements in protocol development (Item 2: χ2 = 10.434, P < 0.0001), PICOS specification (Item 3: χ2 = 5.378, P = 0.02), and protocol registration (Item 23: χ2 = 6.638, P = 0.01). Cochrane Rapid Reviews (CRRs) achieved 100% compliance in several key areas, including setting restrictions with justification (Item 4: χ2 = 52.923, P < 0.001) and study selection (Item 14: χ2 = 14.897, P < 0.001). The impact of journal prestige was also evident: publications in high-impact factor (IF > 5) journals showed significantly better compliance in stakeholder involvement (Item 1: χ2 = 8.856, P = 0.003) but comparable adherence to protocol registration (Item 23: ≤ 20.3%).

## Conclusions
While RR quality is improving post-2021, critical gaps persist in stakeholder engagement and protocol transparency. Mandatory adoption of Cochrane guidelines—particularly protocol registration (Item23) and dual data extraction (Item17)—should be prioritized in journal submission policies.

## Introduction
Rapid review (RR), an emerging method of literature synthesis, has become increasingly important in responding to public health emergencies, particularly during outbreaks of infectious diseases [ ]. In 2020, the Cochrane Rapid Reviews Methods Group [ ] conducted an extensive examination of 216 RR publications from 2017 to 2019 to clarify its definition and methodology.
Recent work by Devane et al. further systematized key RR concepts, providing a comprehensive overview of methodological frameworks and their applications across healthcare contexts [ ]. They defined RR as a method based on systematic reviews (SR) but with certain methodological steps simplified or omitted to quickly generate evidence while optimizing resource use. This method possesses eight key features: (1) accelerated evidence synthesis with shorter completion time [ ], (2) simplified methodological steps, (3) efficient use of resources, (4) provision of evidence for health-related decision-making [ ], (5) scientific methodology, (6) addressing limitations in systematic reviews [ ], (7) limited scope of applicability [ ], and (8) an inherent risk of bias in research outcomes [ ].
The COVID-19 pandemic underscored the critical role of RRs in public health emergencies. For example, RRs conducted within 72 h informed the initial WHO recommendations on mask usage and travel restrictions [ ]. Compared to traditional systematic reviews requiring 6–12 months, RRs can deliver actionable evidence within 2–6 weeks [ ], making them indispensable tools for crisis response. This accelerated evidence synthesis directly addresses the “evidence paradox”— the urgent need for rigorous guidance amidst rapidly evolving outbreaks [ ].
To standardize RR methodology and promote its development, the Cochrane Collaboration published the world’s first evidence-based guidelines for RRs. These guidelines provided a structured approach for conducting RRs, offering detailed recommendations for each step of the process, ultimately enhancing the scientific rigor and practical utility of RRs. Compared to other RR methodologies, the Cochrane guidelines are distinguished by their comprehensive, structured guidance aimed at improving the standardization and transparency of RR execution [ ]. While recent work by Devane et al. provides a valuable conceptual overview of rapid review types and applications [ ], their study did not operationalize these concepts into actionable reporting standards as the Cochrane guidelines do.
Previous studies have examined RR methodologies, but significant gaps remain. Tricco et al. (2017) assessed RR practices across 101 studies but focused primarily on efficiency rather than reporting completeness [ ]. Crucially, no study has systematically evaluated RR reporting quality using the 2020 Cochrane guidelines nor explored subgroup differences based on publication venue and journal impact.
This study systematically evaluated the reporting quality of published rapid reviews based on the Cochrane Rapid Review Methods Group’s guidelines. This study categorized and assessed the literature based on three key factors: journal impact factor, publication year, and whether the reviews were Cochrane or non-Cochrane. Furthermore, adherence to key reporting standards was evaluated. The findings provided practical recommendations to improve the consistency and quality of RR reporting, with the goal of encouraging journals to adopt these standards as mandatory submission requirements, thereby enhancing the overall quality and credibility of RR literature.

## Methods

## Search strategy
We conducted a comprehensive literature search in PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science up to February 28, 2023. The search terms included “rapid systematic review,” “rapid literature review,” “rapid reviews,” “rapid systematic reviews,” and “rapid evidence assessment.” Boolean operators were used to combine these terms appropriately. In PubMed, searches were conducted in the Title/Abstract fields. In the Cochrane Library, the search was performed in the Title, Abstract, and Keywords fields, while in Web of Science, searches were conducted in the Topic field (TS), which includes Title, Abstract, and Keywords. The full search strategies for each database are detailed in Appendix 1.

## Study selection
Initially searched records were imported into EndNote X9 (Thomson Reuters Corporation, Stanford, USA) literature management software. Two reviewers (D.H.Y. and Y.J.Z.) independently selected studies by reviewing the titles and abstracts to identify possibly relevant studies. For records deemed potentially relevant, the same two reviewers independently evaluated the full texts. Each reviewer documented inclusion/exclusion decisions with rationale. Discrepancies at both stages were resolved through discussion, with unresolved cases adjudicated by a third reviewer (J.T.). A pilot calibration exercise ensured consistent interpretation of eligibility criteria prior to formal screening.

## Inclusion and exclusion criteria
The inclusion criterion for this study was that it included rapid systematic reviews without any restrictions on topic selection.
The exclusion criteria for this study were as follows: conference abstracts, protocols, review articles, letters, methodology papers, commentaries, and other irrelevant articles.

## Sample sampling
This study is a cross-sectional analysis of rapid review (RR) literature, analogous to epidemiological surveys assessing disease prevalence at a specific time point. The design aligns with the methodological framework for cross-sectional studies described in Medical Statistics [ ].
Two investigators conducted a comprehensive search of three databases, and identified 3446 articles. After removing duplicates, 1870 articles remained. Following the review of abstracts, 1715 articles were deemed relevant and retained for sampling.

## Sample size calculation
Using the standard formula for proportion estimation in cross-sectional studies [ ]:

E=0.1(marginoferror)
The minimum sample size was calculated as 96. To account for potential exclusions during full-text screening, we pragmatically expanded the sample to 150 studies (Fig.  ).
 Random sampling procedure:
Sampling frame: all 1715 potentially eligible records were assigned unique IDs (1–1715) in Microsoft Excel.
Randomization: the “RAND ()” function generated random numbers for each record. The list was then sorted by these numbers.
Selection: the first 150 records from the randomized list were selected, ensuring equal inclusion probability.

## Reporting quality assessment
The reporting quality of rapid reviews was assessed independently and in duplicate by two review authors, with disagreements resolved by a third author. We used the Cochrane RR Evidence-Based Methodology Guidelines [ ], which consist of 26 items across eight topics: specifying research questions, formulating inclusion and exclusion criteria, literature search, literature screening, data extraction, assessing risk of bias, synthesizing evidence, and other considerations (Table  ). Each item was assessed as “yes,” “partial yes,” or “no” based on the degree of compliance with the reporting criteria. The total number of “yes” responses was used as an indicator of reporting quality, with a higher number indicating better quality.

 

## Subgroup analysis
We conducted a subgroup analysis based on the following criteria:

Publication year: studies were divided into pre-2020 and post-2021 groups. The year 2020 was chosen as the cut-off because the evidence-based guidelines for rapid review methodology were published in 2020, introducing updated standards for reporting and quality assessment. The post-2021 group reflects more recent publications that may have adopted these guidelines, potentially resulting in improved reporting quality.
Impact factor (IF): journals were categorized as IF ≤ 5 or IF > 5 (the IFs were obtained from the Journal Citation Reports (JCR) 2022, Clarivate Analytics). This threshold was selected because impact factors above 5 are widely regarded in academic communities as indicative of higher editorial standards and methodological rigor. While no universal cutoff exists, this stratification allowed comparison between mainstream (IF ≤ 5) and higher-tier journals, while ensuring adequate subgroup sizes for analysis.
Cochrane Library vs. Non-Cochrane Library: studies were categorized into CRR (Cochrane Rapid Reviews) and RR (rapid reviews not published in the Cochrane Library) groups. This division was made to compare the reporting quality between reviews published in a specialized database like the Cochrane Library, known for its rigorous methodology, and those published elsewhere.

## Statistical analysis 
We utilized Excel 2019 for quantitative analysis and qualitative description of the included rapid reviews (RRs). R software was employed to generate forest plots representing confidence intervals. For categorical variables such as pre-registration (yes or no) and statement (yes or no), frequencies and percentages were calculated. Mean, median, standard deviation (SD), and range were used to summarize continuous variables, including the number of “yes” responses in the Cochrane RR Evidence-Based Methodology, impact factor (IF), number of institutions, number of authors, and number of included studies. Differences in categorical variables across subgroups were assessed using the chi-square (χ2) test to determine statistical significance in reporting adherence.

## Discussion

## Overview
In this study, we found that while the overall quality of reporting in rapid reviews (RRs) has improved over time, particularly after the publication of Cochrane Rapid Reviews methodological guidelines, there are still several areas in need of significant improvement. Although the study is based on a random sample, the sample estimation and validation support its representativeness and research significance.

## Enhancing the quality of rapid reviews: key areas for improvement
While leading health organizations (e.g., WHO, UK Public Health) now endorse rapid reviews (RRs) as crucial sources of timely evidence [ ], our findings reveal a concerning gap between policy endorsement and methodological implementation. Despite the clear operational standards set by the Cochrane guidelines [ ], their adoption remains inconsistent across several critical domains.
A fundamental principle of robust evidence synthesis is the clear definition of the research question and refinement of the study scope. However, our findings indicate minimal involvement of key stakeholders—including patients, medical professionals, healthcare managers, and policymakers—in formulating research questions and designing studies. Moreover, the establishment of inclusion and exclusion criteria rarely involves these primary users. Prior research has demonstrated that engaging intermediaries can enhance the clarity of information transmission, refine research questions and methods, and ultimately facilitate decision-makers’ comprehension of study findings [ ]. This disconnect risks misaligned research priorities; for example, a COVID-19 RR prioritized viral transmission analysis while overlooking patient-reported long-term symptoms of “Long COVID” [ ].
In terms of literature search and screening, while RRs generally adhere to the constraints of limited database searches, grey literature searches, and supplementary searches, critical methodological steps remain underutilized. Specifically, there is a lack of involvement of information specialists and peer review of search strategies. Peer review of search strategies plays a pivotal role in retrieving relevant records, particularly those involving nonrandomized studies. This process entails scrutinizing keywords, subject headings, and the interrelations between search concepts to ensure comprehensive coverage [ ].
Additionally, while systematic reviews (SRs) typically require two independent reviewers to screen literature, for RRs, the use of standardized screening forms for titles, abstracts, and full texts is arguably more crucial than independent dual screening [ , ]. However, our findings indicate that many studies fail to report the use of such standardized forms. The reliance on self-reported search strategies, without methodological oversight, introduces “search bias blind spots,” which may systematically exclude relevant grey literature.
The Cochrane guidelines recommend a two-step approach for RR data extraction: (1) extracting study characteristics, which can be conducted by a single reviewer or a pair (preferably dual extraction), and (2) extracting study data, which requires dual extraction [ ]. However, our analysis found that the completeness reporting rate for this aspect was only 50%. While single extraction saves time, prior research has shown that it increases error rates by 18–24% in RRs. This suggests that current practices prioritize speed at the expense of accuracy—an inefficient trade-off, given that error correction often consumes more resources than preventive dual extraction.
The completeness reporting rate for risk of bias assessment was 41.84%, with most RRs failing to utilize appropriate tools for bias evaluation. Given that RRs already tend to have relatively lower methodological rigor than SRs, the lack of appropriate bias assessment further exacerbates concerns about their reliability. To improve methodological robustness, RRs should adopt validated tools for bias assessment, many of which allow for independent evaluation by two reviewers [ ].
Data synthesis in RRs generally follows two approaches: (1) narrative synthesis and (2) meta-analysis when studies are sufficiently homogeneous. Due to the “rapid” nature of RRs, meta-analyses are typically conducted only on the most critical research findings [ , ]. The completeness reporting rate for this component was 92.03%, the highest among all assessed domains. This can largely be attributed to the fact that most RRs focus on urgent public health issues (e.g., COVID-19), where included studies often lack sufficient homogeneity for statistical pooling, necessitating qualitative synthesis instead.
Cochrane guidelines recommend that an approved research protocol (item 23) be submitted before initiating an RR and that the protocol be published (item 24) to minimize research duplication and enhance transparency [ , ]. However, few of the included RRs in our analysis adhered to these requirements. Furthermore, while the Cochrane Collaboration strongly advocates for the use of online systematic review software to facilitate RR conduct, adoption remains limited. This may be attributed to the relatively recent publication of these guidelines, suggesting that a transition period is required for broader implementation.

## Reporting quality based on subgroup analysis
Based on publication year: our temporal analysis revealed substantial improvements in key reporting domains after 2020, coinciding with the release of the Cochrane RR guidelines. Most notably: protocol development (Item2): compliance rates nearly tripled from 19.6% pre-2020 to 49.2% post-2020 (χ2 = 10.434, P < 0.0001). PICOS Specification (Item3): Adherence doubled from 21.6% to 42.4% (χ2 = 5.378, P = 0.02). Protocol Registration (Item23): Implementation increased fivefold from 3.9% to 20.3% (χ2 = 6.638, P = 0.01). These improvements directly align with the Cochrane guidelines’ core requirements for protocol-driven transparency. While broader methodological advancements (e.g., increased PRISMA adoption) and pandemic-era editorial pressures may have contributed, the guidelines'structured framework appears pivotal given. To sustain this momentum, we propose evidence-based policy actions: Guideline-Specific Checklists: Implement Cochrane RR submission checklists, mirroring PRISMA’s success in systematic reviews.
Transparency Incentives: Create “Open Methodology” badges for studies fully adhering to Cochrane standards.
Based on Publication in the Cochrane Library: when comparing reports published in the Cochrane Library (C-RR) with those that were not (RR), the literature from the Cochrane Library consistently demonstrated significantly higher reporting quality. This was particularly evident in topics such as “research question,” “eligibility criteria,” “search strategy,” “study selection,” “data extraction,” and “risk of bias assessment,” where the reporting rates reached 100%. This discrepancy may stem from Cochrane’s stringent methodological guidelines, mandatory protocol registration, and rigorous peer-review process, ensuring higher reporting standards. In contrast, non-Cochrane RRs may face resource constraints and time pressures, leading to methodological shortcuts. The inconsistency in reporting quality raises concerns about the reliability and reproducibility of RRs, underscoring the need for broader adoption of Cochrane-like methodological frameworks, stricter peer review, and enhanced researcher training to improve RR quality across different publication platforms.
Our analysis reveals that rapid reviews (RRs) published in higher-impact journals (IF > 5) generally exhibit better compliance with reporting standards than those in lower-impact journals (IF ≤ 5). However, even among high-IF publications, only a limited number of studies achieved a compliance rate exceeding 90%. While key methodological aspects such as major database searches, data extraction, and data synthesis were well reported in high-IF journals, critical elements—including topic refinement for evidence users, expert involvement in search strategy design, and systematic review software usage—showed low adherence (< 40%) across all journals. This discrepancy suggests that while journal prestige may be linked to better methodological rigor, gaps remain in essential reporting practices. To enhance the overall quality and transparency of RRs, we propose that academic journals incorporate standardized compliance reporting as part of their submission requirements, ensuring that methodological best practices are consistently upheld. Moreover, preventing the publication of methodologically flawed rapid reviews is crucial to minimizing research waste and maintaining the integrity of evidence synthesis [ ].

## Strengths and limitation
This study is one of the few to apply Cochrane’s methodological guidelines for rapid reviews (RR), offering a comprehensive evaluation across multiple dimensions, including publication time, impact factor, and Cochrane publication status. The subgroup analysis provides insights into how these factors influence adherence to the guidelines.
However, there are limitations. While a random sample was selected and validated for representativeness, some sampling bias may still exist, affecting the generalizability of the findings. The short timeframe since the guidelines'release may also have impacted their adoption rate. Additionally, variations in journal policies, research funding, and expertise across different fields could explain differences in reporting quality. Future studies with larger, more diverse datasets and discipline-specific analyses would help validate and extend these findings.

## Conclusion
This study highlights the positive impact of the Cochrane Rapid Reviews Methodological Guidelines on the reporting quality of RRs, particularly for those published in the Cochrane Library. However, methodological and reporting standards across RRs remain inconsistent, raising concerns about their reliability and reproducibility. To address these issues, we recommend that academic journals incorporate compliance with standardized reporting guidelines into their submission requirements. Additionally, researchers should adopt more rigorous methodological frameworks, including stakeholder engagement, transparent search strategies, and dual data extraction, to enhance the credibility of RRs. Strengthening peer review processes and providing targeted training on rapid review methodologies can further improve reporting quality and ensure that RRs serve as reliable sources for evidence-based decision-making.
