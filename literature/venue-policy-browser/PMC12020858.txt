## Introduction
1.1

## Social determinants of health
The Social Determinants of Health (SDH) are non-medical conditions in the environments that impact people's health. The World Health Organization defines SDH as the conditions in which people “are born, grow, live, work, and age” [ ]. The SDH framework provides a foundation for understanding health outcomes by showing how conditions such as unemployment, education, health services, income, and the built environment shape a person's health [ , ]. Education is specifically considered the primary and most modifiable social determinant impacting health, as it influences economic opportunities, mental health, and housing, all of which contribute to people's overall health and well-being [ ].
The role that education plays within the SDH is multifaceted. Those with higher educational attainment often secure better financial opportunities, leading to higher earnings. These increased earnings can contribute to improved built environments, which in turn can have a positive impact on health outcomes [ ]. In contrast, limited education has an inverse effect on a person's finances resulting in a series of adverse outcomes such as increased stress, mental health issues, and limited quality of health resources and care [ ]. Thus, examining the perceived value of pursuing higher education within the context of the SDH framework is valuable, particularly since these perceptions influence how people view the link between education, career prospects, and mental health.

1.2

## Importance of using social determinants of health framework
The SDH framework was chosen because it offers a holistic lens that situates education within broader socioeconomic, health, and policy contexts, rather than assessing it in isolation. This approach acknowledges the intersecting structural factors—such as income, community resources, and access to healthcare—that shape both educational experiences and overall well-being.

1.3

## Perceptions about higher education
As the cost of living continues to rise along with the cost of higher education, there has been growing skepticism regarding the net value of obtaining a college degree [ ]. This is evident in surveys highlighting the ambivalence many people feel about the worth of a college degree [ , , ]. Traditionally, a college degree has been hailed as the crowning achievement necessary to gain financial security, yet recent concerns about student debt and the quality of job prospects have led many to criticize and reevaluate its worth. Contrary to popular beliefs, research suggests that debt does not influence career choice [ ]. These sentiments are not new, as the research by Brouse et al. (2010) emphasized the interconnection between perceptions of college, financial challenges, career uncertainty, and how these combined factors influence decisions about pursuing higher education [ ]. This reassessment has implications for public health as people make decisions in hopes of positively impacting their financial stability and mental health [ ]. As such, examining the perceptions of a college degree within the SDH framework will provide public health professionals with insights into how beliefs about a college degree impact determinants that influence broader health outcomes.

1.4

## TikTok and perception sharing
TikTok has become a search engine, as explained by research highlighting its use among Gen Z for discovering information through keyword searches [ ]. The platform has a diverse and immense user base, growing daily, with over 1.5 billion active users worldwide [ ]. It also serves as a primary platform for young adults aged 18–24, where college-related topics are prominently discussed and reflected upon [ ]. In addition to its role as a search engine, TikTok serves as a venue for users to share their personal experiences—both positive and negative—seek guidance, and find support for their pre-existing perspectives [ ]. Research indicates that this collective sharing of experiences on social media not only shapes perceptions but also reinforces attitudes, amplifying narratives that align with one's pre-existing views [ ]. This can create an echo chamber whereby perceptions of the value of a college degree are continually reinforced, potentially limiting users from considering broader views and leading to judgments based on misrepresentations and oversimplifications.
It is important to note that content on social media platforms like TikTok is anecdotal and thus may not accurately reflect the long-term financial, career, and mental health benefits of obtaining a college degree. Additionally, the mental health implications of whether or not to pursue college is often a topic of discussion, with users openly sharing stress, anxiety, and depression associated with academic and financial pressures. As these discussions become more widespread, they contribute to a broader understanding of the financial and psychological costs associated with earning a college degree [ ].

1.5

## Secondary objectives
In addition to understanding how perceptions of college degrees are shaped on TikTok within the SDH framework, this study aims to examine the broader public health implications of these perceptions. These may include mental health outcomes, financial stress, and policy-level considerations tied to educational attainment. Demographic patterns in the videos are explored, providing insights into how various subgroups may differ in their views and informing tailored interventions or support services.

1.6

## Gaps and research aim
While prior studies have explored perceptions of higher education, they largely overlook how platforms like TikTok shape and amplify public discourse within an SDH framework. Existing surveys and polls provide insights into public views on the short- and long-term value of a college education [ , , ]. However, these studies overlook how these views are discussed and amplified on social media platforms, along with the associated health implications. TikTok, with over 1.5 billion active users and an algorithmic reinforcement of popular themes, provides a unique opportunity to analyze public perceptions of education within a global youth demographic [ , ]. It is increasingly being used as a search engine, making it an ideal platform for understanding public discourse and interactions surrounding the value of a college education [ , ]. Thus, this research aims to explore how people perceive the value of college on TikTok, with a specific focus on identifying public health concerns reflected in these posts and how they relate to the SDH framework.

## Methods
This descriptive, cross-sectional content analysis, modeled after the methodological approach used in Basch et al. (2022) [ ], was conducted in November 2024 to examine the top 100 TikTok videos found under the keyword “is college degree worth it.” This long-tail keyword was selected, as other keywords to examine people's perception of a college degree, such as “college degree value,” “university degree value,” and “higher education degree value,” did not yield a minimum of 100 videos. The top 100 videos were selected after excluding duplicates, irrelevant content, and non-English videos, resulting in the omission of three videos. The videos were analyzed for metadata, which included engagement metrics such as likes, comments, shares, and saves.
The SDH framework was used to guide the analysis to better understand creators' and consumers' perceptions of a college degree and how those perceptions intersect with various social and economic factors that influence health outcomes. These factors included themes such as Employment Opportunities, Income Potential, Debt and Financial Stress, Economic Mobility, Education Access, Education Quality, Education Literacy, Alternatives, Networks, Social Capital, Discrimination, Health Literacy, Insurance, Geographic, Work Environment, and Community Resource. For example, social capital was coded for comments such as, ‘you are taken more seriously when you have a degree.’ Discrimination was coded when users referenced bias in employment, such as, ‘A degree doesn't stop discrimination.’
This study involved two separate content analyses. The first content analysis focused on the characteristics of the videos and was also evaluated first for sentiments (positive, negative, or neutral) and the SDH framework themes. The second analysis focused on coding the comment threads of the 100 TikTok videos for the same SDH framework themes. Data were aggregated in a Google spreadsheet. The metadata (likes, comments, shares, saves) was coded as raw numbers, and the mention of sentiments and themes were dichotomously coded with 0 (absence) and 1 (presence). There were two researchers involved in data collection and coding. One coded the video content (AJ), and the other coded the characteristics of the comments (ED). The videos were subsequently evaluated for inter-rater coder reliability. Using Excel's randomization function, 10 % of the data was randomly selected. Two researchers independently coded the selected data. Any discrepancies were reviewed collaboratively, referencing the codebook definitions. When needed, the codebook was updated to clarify definitions and codes. After resolving discrepancies, inter-rater reliability was assessed using percentage agreement, achieving over 90 % consistency.
A descriptive analysis of the videos and comment thread characteristics was conducted in a Google spreadsheet for total count and frequency distributions. Further analyses were conducted in SPSS 29. The metadata was tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests. All variables (likes, comments, shares, saves) were found to have non-normal distributions (p < 0.001). As such, the Mann-Whitney U test was used to analyze differences in engagement metrics (e.g., likes, comments, saves, shares) between videos that mentioned specific themes and those that did not. Additionally, the relationship between themes mentioned in the comment threads of the videos and the total number of likes of those videos was examined. Accordingly, the Mann-Whitney U was used again to test the difference in the number of likes between videos whose comments included specific themes and those that did not. The analyses were conducted using SPSS 29. This research did not involve contact with human participants and thus did not meet the criteria for review by the IRB at York College of the City University of New York.

## Results
3.1

## Engagement metrics
A total of 100 TikTok videos were analyzed. These videos collectively received 1,786,567 likes, 25,055 comments, 190,557 saves, and 61,865 shares.

3.2

## Prevalent themes
The analysis of sentiments reported that the content in the majority of videos represented a positive sentiment (51 %) with the remaining either negative (27 %) or neutral (22 %). The video themes with the highest percentage of mentions were “Employment Opportunities” (76 %), followed by “Economic Mobility” (68 %), “Education Quality” (65 %), and “Income Potential” (64 %). Themes that were among the minor mentions included “Health Literacy” (4 %), “Insurance” (5 %), and “Work Environment” (7 %).

3.3

## Statistical analysis of engagement
When examining the significance of the theme to the engagement metrics, the theme of “Debt Financial Stress” was significantly associated with higher likes (p = 0.003), saves (p = 0.033), and shares (p = 0.049). Similarly, “Education Quality” was significantly associated with higher likes (p = 0.003), comments (p < 0.001), saves (p = 0.023), and shares (p = 0.028). The remaining themes were not statistically significant. provides a detailed breakdown of all the video themes, their frequency as percentages, and their contribution to total engagement metrics (likes, comments, saves, and shares), including 95 % confidence intervals to show the range of expected engagement values. 
The comment threads of the 100 TikTok videos analyzed revealed that “Education Quality” was the most frequently mentioned theme (60 %) followed by “Employment Opportunities” (58 %). The remaining themes of “Community Resources” (3 %), and “Discrimination” and “Geographic” (4 %) were in the minority.
Videos with comments mentioning “Employment Opportunities” (62.54 vs. 33.87, p < 0.001, r = 0.687), “Income Potential” (67.39 vs. 39.24, p < 0.001, r = 0.586), “Education Quality” (58.78 vs. 38.08, p < 0.001, r = 0.514), “Work Environment” (75.34 vs. 44.67, p < 0.001, r = 0.515), “Education Access” (65.11 vs. 43.67, p < 0.001, r = 0.342), and “Education Literacy” (65.20 vs. 39.41, p < 0.001, r = 0.668) showed significant differences in mean rank of likes with higher engagement in the comment threads of the videos discussing these themes.
“Economic Mobility” (66.43 vs. 46.01, p = 0.004, r = 0.446), “Social Capital” (68.36 vs. 46.58, p = 0.004, r = 0.345), “Geographic” (91.50 vs. 48.79, p = 0.004, r = 0.239), and “Alternatives” (60.46 vs. 44.90, p = 0.010, r = 0.414) had moderate significance in mean rank. The mean rank of likes for “Debt and Financial Stress” (61.69 vs. 45.93, p = 0.014, r = 0.399) and “Networks” (65.37 vs. 47.01, p = 0.013, r = 0.371) and “Community Resource” (90.33 vs. 29.27, p = 0.016, r = 0.238) was low in significance. In contrast, themes such as “Discrimination” showed no significant differences in likes (p > 0.05), whereas themes such as “Health Literacy” and “Insurance” were not present in the comment threads. shows the frequency of comment themes and their association with video likes, including mean ranks and p-values.

## Discussion
The results of this study examined the value of a college degree within the context of the Social Determinants of Health (SDH) framework. Consistent with Brouse et al. (2010) [ ], our findings indicate that financial concerns and career prospects, with themes such as Economic Mobility, Employment Opportunities, Income Potential, and Education Quality, receive higher engagement, potentially sidelining broader health-related benefits. People were more likely to comment, save, and share videos addressing these topics, aligning with existing research supporting higher education's multifaceted role in influencing health outcomes by improving economic opportunities, mental health, and overall quality of life [ , ]. There was significant engagement with videos addressing Debt and Financial Stress and Education Quality, which aligns with the broader skepticism about the costs of college and the perceived return on investment. This echoes existing research indicating public concerns about rising tuition and student debt [ ].
The themes emphasized in the comments, particularly Employment Opportunities and Social Capital, reinforce the audience's interest in interacting with content related to financial stability and career advancement. This suggests that TikTok users view these discussions as an opportunity to validate their concerns and explore pathways to economic mobility. It is also noteworthy that the videos analyzed were nearly evenly split between positive content and the combination of negative and neutral sentiments. This highlights the importance of further research to better understand how these perceptions are shaped and their broader implications for public health, particularly within the SDH framework.
It was surprising to note that health themes were not significantly present in discussions about the value of college education, especially given that education is a primary social determinant of health. Research consistently demonstrates that higher levels of education are linked to various health benefits, including increased likelihood of seeking preventive health care [ ], higher engagement in health-positive behaviors [ ], better access to employer-related health benefits [ ], and improved overall health outcomes and life expectancy [ ]. These well-established connections suggest there could be a relationship between public perceptions of higher education and anticipated health outcomes. However, the absence of SDH themes in social media videos and discussions regarding the value of a college education suggests a potential disconnect. Future research could explore whether individuals consider SDH-related benefits—such as health resources, access, and literacy—when evaluating higher education prior to enrollment.
College is often emphasized as a place where people can procure credentials that increase their baseline economic stability. While income is inextricably linked to health, the myopic narrative that securing gainful employment and expanding one's social network are the primary benefits of attending college may be a disservice. Ohmann criticizes colleges for being vehicles for social class reproduction against a backdrop of the erosion of academic freedom and the rise of vocational curricula [ ]. Given the diminishing economic returns of a college education in light of the United States debt crisis and the advent of artificial intelligence (AI), colleges and universities need to emphasize benefits beyond economic returns, such as SDH-related outcomes, to maintain enrollment.
While attending college has been associated with potential long-term health benefits, the stress of student debt, anxiety about job prospects, and rising costs often overshadow these advantages. Listening to people's concerns about affordability, recalibrating curricula to align with students' needs, giving greater support, and offering alternatives in the form of certificate programs may help make the health benefits of higher education more accessible. Stakeholders should leverage TikTok's influence to promote narratives highlighting education's non-economic benefits represented by other areas of the SDH.
These results align with existing literature on social media's role in shaping and amplifying perceptions. TikTok, as a platform, serves as a venue for users to share personal experiences and seek validation, often reinforcing existing views within an echo chamber [ ]. The high levels of engagement with videos and comments addressing financial stress and economic mobility suggest that these topics may be particularly effective at sparking meaningful dialogue and interaction among users. However, the anecdotal nature of TikTok content [ ] may also limit the depth of discussion about broader health implications.
The absence of certain SDH themes despite their irrefutable connection to holistic well-being reflects how the framework was imposed by the researchers rather than being an inherent part of the original content. This shows that the SDH framework is not how TikTok users conceptualize the value of their education. Nonetheless, it provides an analytical perspective that highlights the health implications of quality education.
4.1

## Limitations and future research
The cross-sectional nature of this research, with data collected within a concentrated timeframe, limits the ability to observe how these trends may evolve over time [ , ]. Additionally, TikTok's constantly changing algorithm and its dynamic engagement trends make it challenging to control the videos shown to different users and draw consistent conclusions. Thus, while TikTok's keyword search is meant to show a consistent set of videos and is less personalized than the For You Page, some personalization may still occur based on location, interactions, and preferences. Another limitation is that we do not know demographic information about the content creators or commenters, which would deepen our analysis. Research shows that factors such as race/ethnicity and even college preparedness shape perceptions of college and college attendance [ ].
The TikTok algorithm can cultivate echo chambers, where users predominantly encounter content that resonates with their existing beliefs or preferences. Over time, such curation can skew public discourse, normalizing specific narratives. While we must acknowledge the potential impact of the algorithm, there is no concrete way to isolate and attenuate its effects. In turn, the user-generated narratives of people from untraceable backgrounds affect generalizability. However, by analyzing themes, engagement patterns, and sentiment, researchers can identify dominant concerns, misinformation trends, and social influences that shape public attitudes, informing future studies and policy discussions.
Future research should focus on monitoring how these engagement themes change over time and use this as a foundation for primary research to better understand whether and how college experiences impact specific health outcomes. This information is particularly valuable for colleges during a period of declining enrollment, as it could help institutions better engage with prospective students. Furthermore, these insights could benefit public health by helping professionals design targeted advertisements and campaigns on social media platforms like TikTok. Such strategies could raise awareness and educate users about ways to improve their health outcomes, particularly among young adults who are highly engaged on these platforms. We suggest a longitudinal, multi-platform study to assess shifting perceptions over time and to include the demographic differences represented on other social media channels.
