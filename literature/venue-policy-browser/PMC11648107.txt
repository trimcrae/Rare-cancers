## Abstract
This study employs fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA) to examine 46 Chinese non-governmental organizations (NGOs) and identify the influential factors and developmental trajectories critical for NGO advancement. By integrating the AGIL functional analysis model, the research uncovers that no single factor—be it public expectations, digital technology, governmental support, cross-sector collaboration, key actors, or organizational culture—predominates in fostering advanced development. Instead, it is the combination of these elements that stimulates progress. Five unique pathways for NGO advancement are mapped: internal development, leadership-driven initiatives, strategic external support leveraging, societal adaptation, and an omnibus development approach. Cross-sector collaboration is highlighted as a pivotal factor that enhances NGO networking and facilitates resource sharing and co-creation of value. The study emphasizes the need for proactive public policy engagement, governmental acknowledgment, and fulfillment of public expectations. It underscores the importance of leadership and the cultivation of a robust organizational culture aligned with the entity's mission. This research enriches the theoretical understanding of NGO evolution and provides actionable strategies for NGOs to navigate the complexities of the social sector.
Keywords: Non-governmental organizations, NGO, Structural functionalism, AGIL model, Fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis, fsQCA

## Introduction
Non-governmental organizations (NGOs) are pivotal in addressing societal challenges and enhancing public welfare. In China, the NGO sector has witnessed significant growth and diversification, yet there is a notable divergence in the developmental trajectories of NGOs, with some emerging as leaders in public welfare and others struggling with credibility and sustainability. Understanding the factors that drive these divergent paths is essential for enhancing the effectiveness of the third sector and informing policy-making. This study aims to fill the research gap by employing fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA) to examine 46 Chinese NGOs and identify the influential factors and developmental trajectories critical for NGO advancement.
The importance of this research lies in its pioneering approach to integrating a broad spectrum of influential factors within a cohesive analytical framework. By applying fsQCA, we uncover the underlying factors contributing to the varied development trajectories of NGOs in China and the interplay among these factors. This study's findings are designed to equip NGOs with actionable insights to amplify their influence and ensure their sustainability, thereby contributing to the theoretical understanding of NGO evolution and providing strategies for NGOs to navigate the complexities of the social sector.

2.

## Literature review
The AGIL model within structural functionalism theory posits that for an organization to survive and thrive, it must fulfill the social adaptation function, goal attainment function, integration function, and latent pattern maintenance function. For NGOs to achieve these functions, they require a high degree of adaptation to institutional, social, and technological environments, as well as the full utilization of their core competencies. Prior research and discussions on these aspects have laid a literature foundation for this study to identify significant factors (variables) affecting the development of NGOs, facilitating qualitative comparative research.
2.1.

## Institutional environment
The institutional environment is a critical determinant that shapes the operations and behaviors of organizations and individuals [ ]. The influence of government policies and regulations is profound, directly affecting the establishment, management, and effectiveness of NGOs. Globally, the relationships between states and nonprofit sectors, including NGOs, are highly variable and context-specific. They range from being adversarial to cooperative, reflecting the historical, cultural, and policy nuances that significantly impact the development, financing, and autonomy of the nonprofit sector [ ]. Legal frameworks that facilitate processes like registration, funding, and operation are essential in nurturing a conducive environment for NGOs, whereas restrictive policies can hinder their growth and activity [ ]. The regulatory environment, which encompasses tax laws, accounting standards, and reporting requirements, significantly influences the way NGOs function and how they are perceived by the public [ ]. Effective collaboration and communication between the government and civil society are vital for creating a supportive policy environment that can strengthen NGOs and foster a robust relationship with the state [ ]. In certain Canadian cities, for instance, NGOs' impact is heavily influenced by their relationship with local governments, characterized by trust, communication, and collaboration—crucial for sharing information, coordinating resources, and aligning goals [ ]. Conversely, in Iran, while NGOs are legally permitted to operate, the government's understanding and support for their capabilities and needs are often limited, leading to skepticism and affecting cooperation and communication between NGOs and government officials [ ]. The status of Iranian NGOs is closely linked to government policies, where the government's active role and influence are evident [ ]. NGOs can achieve legitimacy by establishing effective communication and relationship management with key government stakeholders [ ].
In China, the institutional environment for NGO development is exhibiting a progressive relaxation, prompting a strategic shift in management approaches. This transformation is steering clear from the historical paradigm of administrative absorption of societal functions [ ] and the graduated controls mandated by the government [ ], towards a model of reciprocal engagement. This new model is defined by its emphasis on closeness, dialogue, mutual assistance, and collaborative initiatives [ ]. Despite the seemingly logical trend of increasing the delegation of public services to NGOs in China, uncertainties remain about the government's continuous involvement in this domain [ ]. Within this scenario of intensified interaction and collaboration between government and civil society, the dynamics and mechanisms of government support to NGOs require more in-depth scrutiny.
At the micro level, the influence of political connections and government support on the developmental trajectories of NGOs is substantial. The existing literature overwhelmingly underscores the impact of political connections on NGO development. These connections, which represent direct ties with political figures or institutions, can profoundly influence the prospects for political and social collaboration and the availability of resources for NGOs [ ]. They may grant NGOs access to crucial resources, a platform in policy-making discourses, or a competitive edge in tackling specific issues. However, political connections can have a dual impact; while they may bolster financial stability and foster collaborative networks [ ], they might also impinge upon organizational autonomy and the equilibrium between external pressures and internal objectives and values [ ].
Contrasting the substantial body of research on political connections, there is a noticeable scarcity of academic exploration into the role of government support in NGO development. This study zeroes in on grassroots NGOs, which often have scant political connections during their nascent and developmental phases, aiming to probe deeper into the implications of government support. In contrast to political connections that highlight direct relationships, government support focuses more on the official assistance or resources extended by governmental bodies to NGOs. This encompasses funding, policy inclinations, and public service agreements, potentially enhancing the organizational resilience and service delivery proficiency of NGOs, without inherently involving the direct political influence associated with political connections.
The goal is to fill the research lacuna regarding how government support influences NGO development, especially in the case of grassroots NGOs that might be more reliant on formal governmental aid instead of informal political alliances. By differentiating between the structured assistance provided by government support and the potential benefits derived from political connections, this study aspires to present a more refined comprehension of the diverse avenues through which NGOs can attain developmental success.

2.2.

## Social environment
NGOs bear the mantle of addressing societal challenges and fostering the public interest. They operate within a dynamic and intricate environment that is closely interwoven with societal resources and engages with a spectrum of stakeholders, including the public, media, and corporations [ ]. The efficacy of NGOs in participatory governance is contingent upon their seamless integration within the tapestry of China's political, cultural, and social landscape [ ]. As Chinese NGOs evolve, they exhibit "social-oriented characteristics" and "organization-oriented characteristics," indicating their dual focus on adjusting to societal shifts and enhancing their structural and functional mechanisms to more effectively serve societal and public interests [ ].
Meeting public expectations lies at the heart of NGO success. Studies indicate that an NGO's reputation and its multifaceted social capital are instrumental in strengthening crowdfunding endeavors and securing the attention of donors [ ]. To broaden their developmental prospects, NGOs must embrace a service-oriented approach within a framework of professionalism, address public demands, and provide services that earn recognition, fostering "multiple accountability" mechanisms for both donors and beneficiaries [ ].
Forming robust partnerships with local communities, and other NGOs is essential for NGOs, as these alliances promote the blending of knowledge, spur organizational innovation, enable resource sharing, mitigate risks, and encourage shared responsibility, all in the service of bolstering societal welfare [ , ]. The research underscores a positive link between the extensiveness of an organization's partnership network and its overall performance and capacity for innovation [ ]. Cross-sector collaboration is crucial for ensuring the ongoing flow, exchange, internal development, and transformation of resources within NGOs. Beyond their collaboration with governmental entities, NGOs also engage in a variety of cross-sector partnerships with societal actors, including businesses and media outlets. The financial backing and volunteer support from corporations, aligned under a shared mission, are significant elements of NGO-corporate collaboration [ , ]. Moreover, media coverage can profoundly influence the success of crowdfunding campaigns initiated by NGOs [ ].

2.3.

## Technological environment
The advent of the Internet and the spread of digital technology have incited sweeping societal changes and have become a linchpin in the evolution of NGOs. The Internet, notably, has broadened the outreach of organizations and branched out their fundraising avenues [ ], ironing out communication between different entities and fostering comprehensive stakeholder engagement. This sea change has redrafted the arena of political-social collaboration and participatory governance, enabling organizations to utilize real-time data for more insightful decision-making [ ] and developing advantageous public relations networks [ ]. NGOs that have adeptly harnessed and implemented digital strategies have seen notable escalations in their organizational development. In the epoch of digital transformation, even smaller NGOs must integrate advanced tools into their strategic development to boost their visibility and communication prowess [ ]. The maturation of the Internet-based public welfare ecosystem has progressively cleared the path for more intensive digital collaboration among NGOs and related institutions [ ].
However, the adoption of digital technology, while replete with promise, is not exempt from challenges [ ], especially for organizations with limited digital resources [ ]. In this context, social media platforms have become essential for marketing and promotional activities [ ]. In a milieu brimming with competition and where resources are sparse, NGOs are fortifying their resilience through the tactical employment of social media. This strategy is aimed at scaling up donations, enhancing visual presence, reinvigorating membership, raising awareness, and improving the quality of event and service content. It also aims to strengthen ties with the public, gather feedback on services, and stimulate community involvement [ ]. By increasing public visibility and engaging stakeholders, these platforms act as channels for fundraising and sponsorship, providing a buffer against crisis impacts, and spreading vital information [ , ]. The strategic use of social media significantly magnifies the visibility of NGO initiatives, raises community awareness and participation, and guarantees that services are tailored to community needs [ ].

2.4.

## Internal governance elements
Internal governance elements, such as organizational culture, ethical values, mission, and vision, play a critical role in defining the operational dynamics of NGOs. A robust organizational culture serves as the cornerstone, encouraging members to uphold principles of fairness, impartiality, honesty, and trustworthiness. This fosters standardized operations and minimizes dependence on informal systems, thereby maintaining integrity and consistency within the NGO. Ethical values not only guide the moral conduct and social responsibility of the organization but also significantly contribute to its financial health and economic sustainability. When NGO management prioritizes ethical values like honesty, transparency, responsibility, and fairness as cornerstones of success, these values positively influence the organization's financial well-being [ ]. A well-defined mission and vision act as a moral compass, fostering cohesion and consensus among members and cultivating goodwill among external stakeholders, including volunteers, donors, service recipients, and the broader society. A clear and compelling mission and vision are indispensable for effective resource mobilization and attracting additional funding. However, any ambiguity in formulating a mission and vision can hinder these efforts, potentially limiting the organization's financial prospects [ ]. NGOs often grapple with the challenge of reconciling their mission with the necessity of generating sufficient revenue, which may lead to conflicts between mission fulfillment and operational sustainability [ ]. Regardless of the pressing issues of financial constraints, human-resource challenges, competition, and organizational performance, NGO leaders must recognize that developing and articulating an updated rationale and a compelling vision is paramount for their future success [ ].
Against this backdrop of internal governance elements, the influence of key actors within NGOs further dictates the organization's capacity to navigate challenges and achieve its objectives. The impact of key actors, especially internal organizational leaders, is vital to the success of NGOs, encompassing the sustenance of operations, decision-making processes, and the cultivation of synergistic relationships within the organizational environment [ ]. These leaders often play an instrumental role in leveraging their networks to spearhead impactful campaign initiatives, underscoring the importance of relational networks within Chinese society and the concept of "governance by talents" [ ]. Professionalism among team members is of utmost importance, ensuring unwavering adherence to the organizational ethos, rules, and objectives [ ]. This professionalism, in conjunction with the strategic utilization of leaders' networks, highlights the indispensable role of key actors in NGO operations. Thus, the pivotal role of key actors, in both leadership and team roles, cannot be overstated.
In conclusion, the sustainability of NGOs is intricately linked to their internal governance structures and the broader institutional environment [ ]. It is crucial to acknowledge that the caliber of internal management within an organization can profoundly influence its external cooperative endeavors. Discord among NGO leaders and other internal organizational maladies can spill over into inter-organizational cooperation, consequently impairing the efficacy of collaborative efforts between organizations [ ].
Although previous studies have pinpointed several pivotal factors, they exhibit a degree of fragmentation and do not concentrate on the nuanced state of civil NGO development in China. They also fall short of conducting comprehensive research within a multi-case comparative framework. While the individual impact of these factors is significant, their collective influence is paramount for a thorough mapping of the NGO development trajectory. Acknowledging the critical role each factor plays, it is essential to consider them as key conditional variables within an integrated and holistic analytical model. This methodology ensures a nuanced understanding of the multifaceted dynamics propelling NGO evolution.
By employing Qualitative Comparative Analysis (QCA), our exhaustive examination seeks to ascertain the likelihood of these influential factors manifesting and the diverse combinations they form along various developmental pathways, ultimately leading to the high-level development of NGOs. Through rigorous examination of quintessential cases that exemplify these pathways, we aim to unravel the complex interdependencies among the factors driving such developmental narratives. This systematic approach is designed to provide a granular and nuanced typological portrayal of NGO development in China, offering a multidimensional grasp of the synergistic forces in play, and illuminating the pathways that pave the way for the prosperity and sustainability of these organizations within the complex tapestry of society.

## Materials and methods
The theoretical foundation and analytical structure of this study are anchored in the AGIL functional analysis model, which originates from structural functionalism theory, along with prevailing scholarly consensus from existing literature. These elements guide the identification of condition variables critical to the analysis of NGOs' development. The selection of cases encompassed 46 NGOs from China, chosen for their varied scales of development, fields of operation, and the socio-economic contexts they inhabit, ensuring a representative cross-section for research.
3.1.

## Structural functionalism theory
Structural functionalism, as articulated by Talcott Parsons in "The Social System," presents the four-system (AGIL) model as part of his encompassing systems theory framework. Parsons suggests that every organization establishes specific subsystems to fulfill four fundamental functions: adaptation, goal attainment, integration, and latent pattern maintenance, which are essential for survival and growth [ ]. The adaptation function requires the system to adeptly respond to environmental changes and secure necessary resources. The goal attainment function demands the system's ability to define and pursue objectives while mobilizing human and material resources, vital for self-renewal and sustainable development. The integration function highlights the need for cohesive internal collaboration to prevent fragmentation and conflict, promoting systemic harmony and efficacy. Lastly, the latent pattern maintenance function insists on the system's stability and the preservation of defining characteristics, necessitating a normative framework that includes both informal symbols, concepts, interests, and formal institutional norms.

3.2.

## Analytical framework
The analytical framework, constructed upon Parsons' AGIL model and enriched by scholarly consensus and the empirical realities of NGOs in China, identifies the influencing factors that shape NGOs' development (refer to ). This framework aims to balance theoretical flexibility with practical interpretability, thus enabling its application in specific contexts. The identification of these influential factors is fundamental to qualitative case analysis, where these variables are used as the basis for configuration analysis and the exploration of developmental pathways.
 Firstly, "Public Expectation" and "Digital Technology" are identified as key condition variables through the lens of the social adaptation function. As systemic entities, NGOs must nimbly respond to the external environment and promptly secure the necessary resources for their development. Unlike governmental welfare organizations, NGOs typically follow a bottom-up developmental trajectory. Therefore, their capacity to meet public expectations, earn public recognition, and attract support from donors, volunteers, and beneficiaries is a critical factor in their advancement. Moreover, in the digital age, organizations are increasingly required to adopt digital technology to achieve digital empowerment.
Secondly, "Government Support" and "Cross-sector Collaboration" are recognized as condition variables from the perspective of the goal achievement function. NGOs are fundamentally aimed at providing public welfare services and promoting the public welfare ethos, which necessitates the skillful mobilization of both internal and external resources to reach their objectives. Among the array of external resources, government support not only provides financial resources and service projects for NGOs but also confers political legitimacy, which is essential for gaining social legitimacy. Thus, government support is a key factor in the advanced development of NGOs. Collaboration across sectors with businesses, media, universities, intermediaries, and other civil society actors is equally vital for garnering social support, including donations and volunteerism. Furthermore, China's social governance model, which differs from Western paradigms such as that of the United States, highlights the importance of treating government support as a separate variable from cross-sector collaboration. This delineation allows for a clearer comparison with social support, clarifying the relative importance of each factor in driving the development of NGOs in China.
Thirdly, the "Key Actor" is selected as a condition variable from the viewpoint of the resource integration function. Often, NGOs originate from the initiative and mobilization efforts of key actors, with formal norms and informal principles that sustain organizational stability frequently being shaped by these influential figures. The embodiment of an NGO's values, mission articulation, and strategic positioning always depends on the central role played by key actors. Charismatic leaders often lead well-known NGOs, playing a crucial role in driving organizational progress.
Fourthly, "Organizational Culture" is designated as a condition variable from the angle of the latent pattern maintenance function. Symbols, concepts, and missions form the cornerstone of an organization, with the establishment of NGOs often stemming from groups with shared values and ideals, thereby forming the foundation for social legitimacy. Moreover, the long-term sustainability of NGOs requires adherence to established rules, regulations, and institutional norms such as organizational purpose, mission, internal protocols, and governance mechanisms, which ensure ongoing operational effectiveness after formation.

3.3.

## Selection of research methodology
Qualitative Comparative Analysis (QCA), first introduced by Charles C. Ragin in 1987, is a research methodology that adeptly combines case-oriented and variable-oriented approaches. It offers a holistic strategy that effectively harnesses both qualitative and quantitative research methodologies [ ]. The academic discourse on fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA) is experiencing significant growth, with a rising number of researchers adopting this configurational analysis technique to unravel the complexities of various phenomena [ ].
The QCA methodology is exceptionally well-suited for the analysis of multifactor configurations, such as the elucidation of NGO development trajectories. In this study, we amassed 46 valid cases. While this number may not satisfy the volume criteria for traditional quantitative analysis, it is perfectly suited to the requirements of QCA. The application of fsQCA is particularly apt for scenarios where raw data cannot be easily classified into binary categories. It employs three-value, five-value, and six-value fuzzy processing, which maintains a high degree of fidelity to the original data while reducing the subjectivity associated with variable assignment.
FsQCA identifies the sufficient combination of conditions that lead to an outcome by merging qualitative and quantitative evaluations, thus forging a link between qualitative and quantitative research approaches [ ]. A pivotal component of fsQCA is calibration, which translates data onto a [0, 1] scale, facilitating a sophisticated interpretation of variations and enabling an exact placement of cases to one another [ ].
In our investigation, we implemented Ragin's "indirect calibration method" for the calibration of both condition and outcome variables. This process transformed raw data into fuzzy-set values that mirror the extent to which each condition is satisfied. For outcome variables, NGOs that received a grade of 3A or above from the Ministry of Civil Affairs of China were designated a value of 1, signifying a high level of development. NGOs that achieved a grade of 3A or passed a qualified annual inspection were given a value of 0.5, whereas those below 3A or with a basic qualification/non-qualification were assigned a value of 0. Condition variables were calibrated employing a mix of four-value, five-value, and six-value fuzzy-set techniques, customized to fit the context of each variable. For instance, public expectations were calibrated using a four-value method, digital technology using a five-value method, and organizational culture using a six-value method, ensuring a detailed representation of partial condition fulfillment.

3.4.

## Selection of case samples
QCA offers a robust methodology for conducting cross-case analyses [ ]. The selection of 46 NGOs was guided by theoretical sampling, adhering to principles of homogeneity, diversity, comparability, and completeness.
Homogeneity: To ensure homogeneity, the selection criteria for NGOs were centered on shared organizational characteristics—non-official, civilian, grassroots, and independent—which align with the conventional concept of the third sector, despite their varied areas of focus. Furthermore, all selected NGOs engaged in annual inspections and formal assessments, demonstrating their dedication to regulatory compliance and transparency. The selection process deliberately prioritized economically developed regions in China, such as Beijing, Tianjin, Shanghai, Zhejiang, and Jiangsu, which are known for their similar levels of economic prosperity and a dense, highly active civilian NGO presence. This strategic selection ensures a sample that reflects not only the diversity of NGO focuses but also the impact of vibrant economic environments on NGO operations. To enhance representativeness, the sample also included NGOs from less economically developed regions, including Hunan, Anhui, and Fujian.
It is also crucial to highlight that China, as a unitary state, maintains a high degree of consistency in the local political environments that shape the development of NGOs. These organizations generally function within the regulatory framework provided by the People's Republic of China's Charity Law and other relevant legislation, as well as under the unified policies set by civil affairs departments nationwide. This consistency in the regulatory context provides a common baseline for NGOs, despite their geographical diversity, thus facilitating a balanced and representative analysis within our study.
Diversity: The NGOs selected for this study encompass a broad spectrum of sizes and operational domains, ensuring a rich diversity in the types of services they provide and the socio-economic contexts in which they function. Their areas of focus are eclectic, spanning a wide array of critical social issues including, but not limited to, education development, poverty alleviation, rural child growth education, juvenile protection, emergency rescue, health education, volunteer service, cultural and artistic exchanges, habitat conservation, river protection, special group assistance, psychological support, spiritual care, and community building.
Completeness: The selection process placed a premium on NGOs with transparent information disclosure practices, ensuring that a wealth of data, sufficient for variable analysis, was readily available and verifiable. This criterion was indispensable for the in-depth analysis necessitated by the QCA methodology.
Balance: A judicious balance between the number of cases and condition variables was meticulously upheld to satisfy the methodological demands of fsQCA. With six condition variables identified for this study, the selection of 46 cases not only satisfies but surpasses the recommended thresholds for achieving the most favorable outcomes, as delineated in .
 Adhering to the principles of case selection outlined, a total of 46 NGOs were carefully chosen as case samples. These samples were sourced from a variety of databases, including the China Public Welfare Knowledge Base, China Development Brief, and Public Administration Degree Case Database, as well as from the official websites of the organizations themselves and empirical investigations. This comprehensive approach is detailed in , ensuring a rich and diverse dataset for the study.
 
3.5.

## Setting and assignment of outcome variables
Since the Ministry of Civil Affairs began assessing social organizations in 2007, there has been a marked enhancement in the scientific rigor, standardization, objectivity, comprehensiveness, and authority of the assessment process in China. Over the past decade, the assessment grades of social organizations, including NGOs, have become a critical benchmark for measuring their developmental status and social credibility. These grades also serve as an essential reference for governmental departments when reallocating functions and procuring services from these organizations. Thus, the assessment grades have been adopted and assigned values to measure the developmental progress of NGOs.
As per the Administrative Measures for the Assessment of Social Organizations, the assessment outcomes are stratified into five grades: 5A, 4A, 3A, 2A, and 1A, in descending order of distinction. Organizations that achieve a grade of 3A or above are given priority consideration for assuming governmental functions, securing government-sponsored services, and receiving governmental recognition. Moreover, foundations, charitable organizations, and other public welfare entities that attain a grade of 3A or higher may apply for pre-tax deduction eligibility for public welfare contributions, according to regulations. Social organizations that receive a grade of 4A or higher may be eligible for a streamlined annual inspection process. Consequently, it is appropriate to establish the 3A grade as the threshold for differentiation.
However, not all NGOs are assessed. Assessment participation is dependent on the successful completion of the annual inspection. Organizations are categorized into one of three outcomes from the annual inspection: qualified, basically qualified, or unqualified. For this study, the findings from the 2022 annual inspections were selected for analysis.
In conclusion, as shown in , a three-value methodology is utilized to determine the assignment criteria for NGOs' developmental outcomes: organizations that achieve a grade of 3A or above are assigned a value of 1, those that receive a grade of 3A or pass the annual inspection are given a value of 0.5, and those that score below 3A or are classified as basic qualification/non-qualification in the annual inspection are assigned a value of 0.
 
3.6.

## Condition variable settings and assignments
We have chosen public expectations, digital technology, government support, cross-sector collaboration, key actors, and organizational culture as the pivotal variables for this study. This selection is fundamentally grounded in the structural functionalism theory, particularly the AGIL model, which offers a holistic framework emphasizing the essential functions that an organization must fulfill to ensure its survival and growth: adaptation, goal attainment, integration, and latent pattern maintenance. Each of the selected condition variables is aligned with one or more of these functions.
Public expectations (PE) and digital technology (DT) are recognized as crucial for the adaptation function. They empower NGOs to navigate environmental changes and capitalize on technological advancements to fuel their growth. Government support (GS) and cross-sector collaboration (CC) are identified as key for the goal attainment function, equipping NGOs with vital resources and collaborative frameworks necessary to realize their objectives. Key actors (KA) play a significant role in the integration function, nurturing cohesion and unity within the organization. Lastly, organizational culture (OC) is essential for the latent pattern maintenance function, safeguarding the organization's identity and core values.
The incorporation of these variables into our analytical framework facilitates a sophisticated understanding of the myriad factors that shape the intricate development trajectories of NGOs.
3.6.1.

## Public expectations (marked as PE)
Non-governmental organizations (NGOs) originate from and are devoted to the public, making the fulfillment of societal expectations for an enhanced quality of life an earnest imperative. When an NGO aligns with these public expectations, it naturally earns the support of the community through financial contributions and utilization of its services. Consequently, quantifiable criteria such as the proportion of donation income to total income and the volume of public welfare projects are utilized as metrics to assess public expectations of NGOs. The benchmark for these criteria is set at the sample mean, with 80 % for the donation income ratio and 10 for the number of public service projects, serving as the quantitative threshold. Additionally, the public welfare initiatives conducted by NGOs are pivotal in satisfying public expectations, quantified by the proportion of public welfare expenditures to total expenditures, with a sample mean of 93 %.
Based on these quantitative findings, public expectations are categorized into three dimensions for measurement: the proportion of donation income to total income, the average annual number of public welfare projects implemented, and the proportion of public welfare expenditures to total expenditures. Employing a four-valued fuzzy set method, values are assigned as follows: a value of 1 is given if all three dimensions are satisfied; a value of 0.67 if two dimensions are met; a value of 0.33 if only one dimension is met; and a value of 0 if none of the conditions are met, thereby providing a nuanced assessment of the NGOs' alignment with public expectations.

3.6.2.

## Digital technology (marked as DT)
Digital technology has become an essential catalyst for the advancement of NGOs, known for its interactivity and openness. The digital infrastructure of NGOs spans four key dimensions: firstly, the use of the Internet to launch fundraising campaigns; secondly, the use of online platforms to facilitate intra-organizational collaboration, such as conducting virtual meetings and utilizing online document management tools; thirdly, the establishment of a comprehensive digital framework, including the organization's official website, public platforms, microblogging, and short video platforms, all of which should be operational; and fourthly, the maintenance of dedicated technical staff, with specific departments or roles, including full-time technical personnel. A five-point assessment method is applied to allocate values: a score of 1 indicates that all four conditions are met, 0.75 signifies that any three conditions are satisfied, 0.5 means that any two conditions are complied with, 0.25 indicates that only one condition is adhered to, and 0 implies that none of the specified conditions are fulfilled.

3.6.3.

## Government support (marked as GS)
The trajectory of NGO development within China is significantly influenced by the extent of governmental support they garner. During the fledgling stages of development, the intensity of governmental engagement is typically measured by factors such as the administrative stature of NGO executives and the source and character of the NGOs' premises. Yet, as NGOs with governmental ties experience transformations and as the entities themselves evolve in conjunction with government-NGO partnerships, the parameters of governmental backing diversify to include a spectrum of elements. These range from funding for procurement and public welfare initiatives to financial aid, venue support, manpower allocation, technical support, and favorable policy environments.
As a result, the benchmarks for gauging governmental support are outlined across four critical dimensions: Firstly, the potential for NGOs to access government procurement and public welfare funding; secondly, the NGOs' qualification for receiving financial allocations from governmental bodies; thirdly, the NGOs' eligibility for tax reliefs or incentives offered by the government; and fourthly, the NGOs' official registration status at the municipal level or above. A detailed five-tier rating system is implemented for the assignment of values: an optimal score of 1 is awarded for meeting all four criteria, a rating of 0.75 is given for meeting three of the criteria, a score of 0.5 is for meeting two, 0.25 is for satisfying a single condition, and a score of 0 is assigned for failing to meet any of the four stipulated conditions.

3.6.4.

## Cross-sector collaboration (marked as CC)
NGOs in China enhance their resourcefulness and overcome challenges by actively forging cross-sector collaborations with a diverse array of partners, including businesses, community groups, fellow NGOs, academic institutions, and other stakeholders. These strategic alliances serve a dual purpose: they not only bolster the NGOs' resource pool but also pave the way for the introduction of innovative service paradigms and operational frameworks. Since government support is categorized as a separate variable, the cross-sector engagements of NGOs are characterized by four key dimensions: Firstly, collaborative efforts with businesses aimed at service delivery or securing donations and sponsorships within the past year; Secondly, joint ventures with grassroots organizations to implement public welfare initiatives; Thirdly, cooperative efforts with other social organizations to carry out public welfare projects; Lastly, partnerships with academic institutions to foster industry-academia-research endeavors in the preceding year.
A meticulous five-point scale is utilized to quantify the effectiveness of these collaborations: A score of 1 is assigned for the comprehensive fulfillment of all four dimensions, a rating of 0.75 is given for meeting criteria in any three areas, a score of 0.5 is for satisfying conditions in two areas, 0.25 is for fulfilling a single criterion, and a score of 0 is for a lack of alignment with any of the four conditions, thereby providing a nuanced assessment of NGO cross-sector engagement.

3.6.5.

## Key actors (marked as KA)
The efficacy of NGOs in China is heavily contingent upon the capabilities of key figures within their ranks, with leadership playing an indispensable role in guaranteeing the organizations' long-term sustainability [ ]. Reflecting the distinctive features of Chinese NGOs, the assessment of key actors' competence is conducted across a quartet of critical dimensions: it begins with an evaluation of whether these pivotal actors have previously held significant administrative posts or have demonstrated administrative acumen, then examines whether they possess a wealth of experience in business management spanning over five years, subsequently assesses their immersion in public welfare initiatives for a minimum of half a decade, and finally takes into account the diversity of their professional backgrounds.
In the quantification of these attributes, a nuanced five-tier rating system is applied: the pinnacle rating of 1 is reserved for those who meet all four criteria, indicating a comprehensive embodiment of the desired competencies; a score of 0.75 is accorded for meeting any three of the conditions, denoting substantial, yet not complete, fulfillment; a rating of 0.5 is given for satisfaction in two areas, signifying a moderate level of competence; 0.25 is allotted for the fulfillment of a single criterion, indicating a basic degree of competence; and a rating of 0 is designated for an absence of alignment with any of the stipulated conditions, pointing to a lack of the necessary competencies. This meticulous evaluation ensures a granular and comprehensive assessment of the key actors' contributions to the NGO's overall effectiveness and sustainability.

3.6.6.

## Analysis of single condition
Under the auspices of the fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA) methodology, we perform a necessity test on the condition variables utilizing fsQCA software. This critical examination is designed to verify that no condition variable is so intrinsically linked to the outcome variable as to be considered a necessary determinant. The presence of a necessary condition suggests an invariable association with the outcome, which, if identified, would warrant the exclusion of that variable from further analysis to avoid skewing the results.
The necessity of conditions is gauged through two principal metrics: consistency and coverage. Consistency evaluates how frequently the condition's presence predicts the presence of the outcome, while coverage measures the extent to which the condition's influence spans across all cases with the given outcome. Both criteria are indispensable for a robust assessment of necessity.
This meticulous process is emblematic of the nuanced approach fsQCA brings to social science research, allowing for a deeper understanding of causal complexities. By ensuring that our condition variables are not merely necessary but also sufficiently explanatory, we enhance the reliability and validity of our analysis, bringing us closer to unraveling the intricate web of factors that contribute to the development trajectories of NGOs.

## Configuration analysis of condition variables
Qualitative Comparative Analysis (QCA) employs Boolean algebra and Boolean minimization algorithms to identify patterns of multiple conjunctural causations and to logically and comprehensively simplify complex data structures. This process yields three types of solutions: the Complex Solution, which excludes any logical residuals; the Parsimonious Solution, which integrates all logical residuals; and the Intermediate Solution, which selectively incorporates theoretically plausible logical residuals [ ]. The Intermediate Solution, which includes meaningful logical residuals, is considered superior to both the Complex and Parsimonious Solutions and is typically seen as the most representative explanation. Therefore, the Intermediate Solution is adopted for analysis, in conjunction with the Parsimonious Solution, to distinguish between core and peripheral conditions. Conditions that are present in both the Intermediate and Parsimonious Solutions are considered core, while those found only in the Intermediate Solution are deemed peripheral. Ultimately, five variable configuration results that contribute to achieving a high level of NGO development were derived ( ).

## The evolving regulatory environment for NGOs in China
There is a palpable concern regarding the regulatory environment for NGOs globally, with regions like Tanzania witnessing a constriction of civic space that significantly hampers advocacy NGOs. The trajectory of governmental supervision for NGOs in China has transitioned from rigid control to a more nuanced, category-specific regulation. This shift has precipitated substantial growth opportunities for philanthropic NGOs. The reduction of entry barriers has ushered in an era of inclusive policies, offering tax relief, public financial subsidies, and collaborative opportunities with government entities in public service delivery. This burgeoning openness and supportive policy framework have been instrumental in the remarkable advancement of China's third sector. Within this dynamic landscape, NGOs have evolved from survival-centric strategies to a more diversified approach.

## The societal adaptability of Chinese NGOs: meeting public expectations and embracing digital technology
While not constituting the core elements in any of the developmental pathways, public expectations and digital technology serve as influential peripheral conditions. Specifically, public expectations materialize as a peripheral condition in Pathway 4, which focuses on societal adaptation, and Pathway 5, which emphasizes comprehensive development. This reflects the NGOs' capacity to adapt to societal contexts. Similarly, digital technology emerges as a peripheral condition in Pathway 3, centered on seeking external support, and in Pathway 4, which also focuses on societal adaptation. Here, digital technology plays a crucial role in facilitating both internal and external linkages, enhancing public participation, and fulfilling public expectations. These observations suggest that Chinese grassroots NGOs must improve their responsiveness to societal needs, meet public expectations, and achieve digital empowerment.
Extending the application of structural functionalism theory, particularly the AGIL framework, our study indicates that high-performing NGOs in China have adeptly fulfilled the goal attainment function, integration function, and latent pattern maintenance function, aligning well with the theoretical model. However, there is a notable shortfall in the realization of the adaptation function, particularly the social adaptation aspect. Although Chinese NGOs excel in cross-sector collaboration with businesses, universities, and other social entities, they often fall short in meeting public expectations, responding to service beneficiaries' needs, and leveraging digital technology for technological empowerment—key social functions.
Despite these shortcomings, some NGOs have managed to attain a high level of development. We interpret this phenomenon as an indication that the development of NGOs in China is still in an expansionary phase, with their intensive development and social functions requiring further enhancement. This represents a significant finding of our study and highlights areas for future improvement in NGO development. It suggests that while NGOs in China align well with the structural-functionalist perspective in terms of internal cohesion and goal achievement, external adaptation, especially in response to societal demands and technological integration, is an area that necessitates greater attention and development.
Value congruence, community trust, and meeting the needs of constituents are prerequisites for the social orientation and adaptability of NGOs. However, NGOs may encounter value conflicts with residents and challenges in establishing community trust in specific contexts [ ]. Moreover, NGOs, which are largely donor-dependent, often face a discrepancy between their activities and the needs of their constituents [ ]. In such cases, NGOs must reconcile these value clashes and secure community trust to achieve successful operation and development outcomes. They also need to balance donor intentions with the fulfillment of beneficiaries' needs.
In embracing digital technology and achieving digital empowerment, NGOs still have deficiencies. Not only are NGOs in China slow to adopt digital technologies, but similar challenges exist in countries like the Netherlands, where NGOs are lagging in utilizing digital tools [ ]. Several factors contribute to this slow adoption, including the complexity of digital communication, digital risks, and the acceptance levels of the target audience, as well as the NGOs' capabilities. Firstly, increased digital outreach does not automatically lead to better accountability or stakeholder engagement. NGOs face the challenge of aligning the fluid and asymmetrical nature of digital communication with their need for structured accountability and ethical engagement. This challenge is exacerbated by limited resources, cultural barriers, and the complex integration of technology [ ]. Secondly, while digital technology empowers NGOs, it also introduces risks and challenges that can disrupt operations. To mitigate these risks, NGOs must strengthen their digital security, stay vigilant against misinformation, safeguard data carefully, and promote transparent, community-oriented practices [ ]. Thirdly, the digital literacy and language proficiency of the target population, trust, visual identity, and perceived ease of use are critical factors influencing stakeholders' acceptance of digital platforms that support NGO projects [ , ]. Lastly, from the organizational perspective, key elements such as leadership, entrepreneurship, agile management, co-creation, user-friendliness, and establishing a data-driven learning culture are essential for NGOs to harness digital transformation for social good [ ]. In the use of social media, the gaps in communication and marketing technical skills, lack of funding, deficiencies in leadership skills, and capacity limitations are challenges faced by NGOs [ ].

6.1.7.

## The configuration paths leading to NGOs’ high-level development
The study has identified five configuration paths leading to the high-level development of NGOs: internal construction, key actor-driven, seeking external support, adapting to societal changes, and comprehensive development. The diversity among these paths is evident, with some prioritizing internal development, others focusing on external support, and some striving for all-encompassing growth. Despite the diversity in specific strategic focuses, these paths are consistent in their overall strategy of integrating internal elements and external factors, relying on the organization's core competencies to seek external support. One explanation for the diversity in the high-level development paths of NGOs is that different NGOs are at various stages of development, thus forming distinct pathways.
The five development paths reflect the stage characteristics of Chinese civil society NGOs: the initial stage often relies on resource-driven strategies, such as key actor-driven or external support-oriented approaches. As NGOs mature, they shift towards internal construction and adapting to societal changes. In the maturity phase, NGOs tend to adopt comprehensive development strategies. These stage characteristics mirror the inherent diversity within China's NGO landscape.

6.2.

## Conclusions
The present study offers a nuanced perspective on the development of NGOs in China by applying the AGIL model through the lens of fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA). Our analysis reveals that NGOs can achieve advanced development through various pathways, even when they face challenges in fulfilling the social adaptation function during the early stages. This finding suggests a stage-based approach to NGO development, where immature NGOs can leverage alternative strategies to succeed, while mature NGOs enhance their social adaptation to ensure resilience and sustainability.
Our results are closely aligned with our initial research objectives, which aimed to uncover the influential factors and developmental trajectories critical for NGO advancement. The identification of five unique pathways to NGO advancement underscores the complexity of NGO evolution and the interplay among various factors. This finding directly addresses the research questions and builds upon existing literature by introducing a configurational approach that values the combination of conditions over singular factors.
While our study aligns with previous research that emphasizes the importance of goal attainment and integration, it also contrasts by demonstrating that social adaptation, though ideal, is not a strict prerequisite for achieving high-level development. This insight extends the AGIL model's applicability, particularly within the Chinese context.
Given the cross-sectional nature of our data, we acknowledge the limitations in inferring causality or tracking NGO development trends over time. Future research should employ longitudinal data and adopt comparative studies across different cultural and political contexts to build upon our conclusions. Additionally, exploring the long-term impact of digital technology on NGO development and the evolution of NGO-government relationships could provide valuable insights for practice and policy. Our study provides a solid foundation for further inquiry into the dynamics of NGO development and its implications for practice and policy.
