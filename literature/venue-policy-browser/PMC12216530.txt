## Abstract
Nurses working in Intensive Care Units are prone to burnout and low job satisfaction which can be caused by stress. This cross-sectional study aimed to quantify the magnitude of stress among new graduate nurses working in ICUs. A survey was conducted between May and July 2023 at the 11 adult and pediatric ICUs in King Saud Medical City, Riyadh (KSMC-R). Using convenience sampling, 189 participants were recruited. A significant difference was found in the mean total Perceived Stress Scale (PSS)-10 and Expanded Nursing Stress Scale (ENSS)- 10 scores between the males and females. In the subscales, significant differences were seen between the workload and gender, age group, and type of ICU and in the mean inadequate emotional preparation scores and age group and marital status, type of ICU (adult/pediatric), and the different types of ICU. The survey showed that the major factors that caused stress among the new graduate nurses were discrimination, problems with supervisors, workload, problems with peer support, uncertainty concerning treatment, and dealing with patients and their families.
Keywords: Stress, New graduate nurses, Intensive care units, Saudi Arabia

Subject terms: Health occupations, Health care, Health policy, Occupational health, Quality of life

## Introduction
In recent decades, stress and burnout in nursing have been studied to improve nurses retention and reduce nursing shortages because nursing is a very stressful job . As sources of stress that negatively affect their profession, nurses frequently cite of being overworked, unable to meet their patients’ requirements, underprepared, and unable to deal with the patient mortality rate . These stressors influence nurses worldwide and at all stages of their careers. However, for several reasons, most notably the practice-preparation gap new graduate nurses (NGNs) are more vulnerable. Transition shock, often seen in the adjustment period for new professionals, is a particular issue for NGNs . Personal, interpersonal, and organizational aspects all contribute to these transitory stressors along with pressures that are evident to the entire nursing community, such as a heavy workload, lack of staff, and lack of emotional support .
Many NGNs begin their careers in intensive care units (ICUs), where they experience unparalleled stress due to high patient acuities, technology, and other considerations. Furthermore, low to high levels of stress were thought by new nurses to be predominantly brought on by a lack of professional nursing abilities and a demanding workload. Rarely were the individual and organizational issues that might have contributed to their stress evaluated . Additionally, stress at work has been found to be a significant predictor of NGNs’ intention to resign , . Research has focused on the experiences of NGNs working in ICUs with workplace pressures. For instance, it has been discovered that nurses working in critical care units experienced severe work-related stress symptoms, with recently graduated nurses in these settings being especially sensitive . According to a different study, occupational stress and other stressors were considerable for newly licensed nurses working in ICUs .
Positive stress management is frequently perceived as challenging yet essential. If new nurses can adjust successfully to the challenging clinical environment and circumstances throughout the nurse residency program stage, this should be beneficial to their entire careers . Researchers and nursing supervisors have similar concerns about implementing stress management techniques that are tailored to the requirements of new nurses, and these issues merit further investigation . High expectations, excessive responsibility, and little authority have been highlighted as the primary stresses in nursing, which is acknowledged to be a stressful profession due to its complicated job demands and needs . The quality of life that is tied to nurses’ health is negatively impacted by job stress. Stress can also hinder nurses’ ability to provide care and inhibit these caring behaviors, which affect patients’ outcomes .
Compared to nurses working in other settings, nurses working in ICUs are more prone to burnout and low job satisfaction. Emotional weariness, depersonalization, and decreased personal achievement are the three characteristics of burnout. A high workload, staff shortages, a lack of support, and coping with death and dying were among the issues that a review found to contribute to burnout and low job satisfaction. Additionally, it was determined that organizational and individual characteristics affected the successful deployment of NGNs . While personality and clinical placements during their early education have been linked to personal traits, orientation, stability, workload, and the scientific culture of the unit are relevant elements . The main causes of work-related stress among the nurses were busyness, a lack of resources and support, and dealing with death and dying. According to the study, strategic plans and intervention programs are required to lessen the symptoms of stress among Saudi Arabian nurses .
In Saudi Arabia, NGNs working in ICUs must cope with a variety of stressors at work, including a heavy workload, a lack of staff, a lack of support, and dealing with death and dying. The effects of these pressures include burnout, a lack of job satisfaction, and anxiety at work . According to the requirements of new nurses, stress management techniques are advised. A dearth of studies exist that specifically address the experiences of NGNs working in ICUs, even though several studies have examined work-related stress and burnout among Saudi Arabian nurses. An in-depth investigation is required to comprehend the stresses and difficulties that this demographic faces. This study aimed to quantify the magnitude of stress among NGNs working in ICUs in Saudi Arabia.

## Methods

## Design
This study employed a cross-sectional design.

## Setting and sample
This study was conducted at King Saud Medical City, Riyadh (KSMC-R), King Saud Medical City (KSMC) is a Ministry of Health tertiary hospital which started to serve the people of Riyadh in 1956. Over the years, KSMC has played the role of the safety net for Riyadh City. KSMC receives more emergency cases than any other hospital in the kingdom. Currently, it has 1,400 bed capacity. KSMC-R contains 119 ICU beds, which can be categorized into two groups: adult ICUs and pediatric ICUs. The adult ICUs consist of the Medical, Surgical, Trauma, and Isolation and Infected Cases ICUs, Burn Unit, Obstetric Gynecology ICU, and Cardiac Care Units. The pediatric ICUs (PICUs) include PICU 1, PICU 2 and the Respiratory and Neonatal ICUs (publicportal.ksmc.ed.sa, 2024).
Convenience sampling was used to recruit 189 participants. The inclusion criteria for the self-administered survey were NGNs of Saudi nationality, male and female, 18 years of age and above, holding either a diploma or bachelor’s degree, working full-time or part-time, with less than one year’s experience working as a Registered Nurses (RN), who had completed the orientation programs and had worked independently in regard to patient care. Newly graduated RNs who had more than one year of work experience were excluded.

## Instruments
The questionnaire consisted of three sections: background information, the PSS-10 items, and the ENSS-57 items. The background was divided into three parts. The first part included demographic information, such as the respondents’ age, gender, marital status, and number of children. The second part covered the respondents’ education and employment history, and the third part included questions about the type of ICU and the shift work and working hours of the respondents.
The Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) is the first instrument in this study and used with no modifications, apart from being translated into classical Arabic. The PSS-10 is an adequate tool for measuring perceived stress in women with breast cancer and may be useful for identifying people at risk for psychological maladjustment , . A 5-point Likert scale was used by the respondents to rate their respective experiences and feelings within the last month. The frequency ratings were 0 = never, 1 = almost never, 2 = sometimes, 3 = fairly often, and 4 = very often. The total possible scores range between 0 and 40, with higher scores indicating greater overall stress.
The Expanded Nursing Stress Scale (ENSS-57) (Section C), the second instrument used for this study, contains 57 items and was used with no modifications, apart from being translated into classical Arabic. It contains 57 items in 9 subscales: death and dying, conflict with physicians, inadequate emotional preparation, problems relating to peers, problems relating to supervisors, workload, uncertainty concerning treatment, patients and their families, and discrimination , . The questionnaire was developed to allow the respondents to grade their responses using a 5-point scale: 0 = does not apply, 1 = never stressful, 2 = occasionally stressful, 3 = frequently stressful, and 4 = extremely stressful. Higher scores on the ENSS-57 indicate a more frequently experienced stressor.
An expert panel comprising of linguistic and content experts was used to validate the ENSS-57 and PSS-10 and confirm content validity. The expert panel of three experienced Saudi nurses with postgraduate qualifications was used before and after the translation process. All feedback and comments were discussed and considered accordingly. A list of content validity criteria that were used in the self-administered survey of new graduate nurses: questions were are clear, questions are easy to comprehend, questions are comprehensive and relevant to the purpose of the study, questions are appropriate in the context of the culture and religion of Saudi Arabia, length and ordering of the entire questionnaire is appropriate, no redundancy in the questions, absence of bias in either direction.
A number of studies have reported using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient to assess the internal consistency of the Arabic versions of both the PSS-10 and ENSS-57. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the PSS-10 typically ranged between 0.7 and 0.8, while for the ENSS-57 items ranged between 0.7 and 0.9 (Abu AlRub 2004, 2006; Al-Hassan & Wierenga 2000; Chaaya et al. 2010; Cohen et al. 1983; Hamdan-Mansour & Dawani 2007 2008; Hattar-Pollara & Dawani 2006; Hamaideh 2008; Kamal et al. 2012; Masa’Deh et al. 2013, 2017; Mohamed et al. 2011; Zaghloul 2008).
For the current study, the PSS-10 showed good reliability with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.83 while the ENSS 9 subscales afforded a Cronbach alpha coefficient ranging between 0.72 and 0.89, as summarised in Table  .
 A pilot study was conducted to ensure the quality and efficacy of the self-administered survey. The Arabic version of the questionnaire was distributed to 5 Saudi nurses who were allocated 10–15 min to complete. The nurses confirmed the clarity and brevity of the survey. Therefore, no changes or amendments were made to the questionnaire in the actual study.

## Data collection
A self-administered questionnaire in Arabic was distributed to the NGNs working at the adult and pediatric ICUs at King Saud Medical Center-Riyadh between May 2023 and July 2023.
A hard copy of the questionnaire in Arabic was distributed to all the new graduates working in the ICUs, as they were unable to access the Internet. Each sealed envelope contained a cover letter, an information sheet, and a questionnaire containing background information, the PSS-10, and the ENSS-57). The estimated time required to complete the questionnaire was approximately 10–15 min.

## Ethical consideration
This study was approved the Research Administration of the Minister of Health Ethics Committee in Riyadh in Saudi Arabia. Ethical approval was also sought from the Institutional Review Board at KSMC-R. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. A written informed consent was obtained from each participant. The researchers gave a detailed explanation of the purpose of the study, emphasizing the participants’ right to withdraw at any point. Strict measures were implemented to protect the privacy of the participants and ensure the confidentiality of all collected information.

## Results

## Perceived stress scale (PSS-10)
The PSS-10 instrument was used to quantify the levels of stress experienced by the respondents in the month prior to being surveyed, using the mean score and Standard Deviation (SD). The total perceived level of stress for the PSS-10 items was calculated using the overall total mean and SD (mean = 29.80, SD = 5.02) (see Table  ). As the total possible score for all items is 40, this equates to stress experienced fairly often.
 

## Expanded nursing stress scale (ENSS-57)
The 9 ENSS-57 subscales were ranked based on the highest to lowest mean and SD (Table  ). As the subscales have different numbers of items, the means were calculated as the overall means of the items in each subscale rather than the means of the subscales’ totals. Based on the ranking, the highest stressor was discrimination (mean = 3.12, SD = 0.99), the second highest was problems with supervisors (mean = 3.03, SD = 0.80), and the third highest was workload (mean = 2.90, SD = 0.78). The subscale with the lowest mean was conflict with physicians (mean = 2.48, SD = 0.88).
 

## Relationship between the PSS-10 items and demographics and work characteristics
Table  presents the results of the analysis of relationship between the level of stress indicated by the PSS-10 results and the demographic variables. A statistically significant difference was found in the mean PSS-10 total scores between males and females (p-value = 0.0044). The mean difference in the PSS-10 for the males compared to the females was 2.4 ( 2.4, 95% CI: 4.1, 0.8), indicating a higher frequency of stress in female respondents.
 Significant differences were also found between the full-time and part-time respondents (p-value = 0.03), indicating higher stress levels in the full-time respondents than in the part-time respondents, and in the respondents working 31–40 h per week compared to those working 41–45 h per week (p-value = 0.046), indicating higher stress levels in the respondents who worked longer hours.

## Relationship between the ENSS-57 subscales and the demographic variables
Table  illustrates the relationship between the ENSS-57 subscales and the demographic variables. A statistically significant difference was found between the mean uncertainty concerning treatment subscale score and gender (p = 0.03). The mean difference in the uncertainty concerning treatment subscale score for the females in comparison to the males was 2.04 (estimate = 2.04, 95% CI: 0.13, 3.9), indicating a higher frequency of stress in the female respondents.
 Significant differences were also seen between the workload and gender (p-value = 0.0280), age group (p-value = 0.0123), and type of ICU (p-value = 0.0125). The mean difference of the workload scores for the females compared to the males was 2.6 (estimate = 2.6, 95% CI: 0.3, 5.0), indicating a higher frequency of stress in the female respondents. The mean difference in workload for the age group 19–25 years compared to the 26–35 years age group was 1.4 (estimate = 1.4, 95% CI: 0.6, 3.4), indicating a higher frequency of stress among respondents within the 19–25 years age group. The mean difference for the workload scores with regard to the type of ICU was 3.5 for the respondents in the adult ICUs compared to those in the pediatric ICUs (estimate = 3.5, 95% CI: 0.8, 6.3).
A statistically significant difference was found in the mean inadequate emotional preparation scores with regards to age group (p-value = 0.0004). The mean difference for this subscale for the 19–25 years age group in comparison to the 26–35 years age group was 0.068 (estimate = 0.068, 95% CI: −0.65, 0.8).
In addition, significant differences were noted in the mean death and dying scores for marital status (p-value < 0.0001), the type of ICU (adult/pediatric) (p-value < 0.0001), and the different types of ICU (p-value < 0.0001). The mean difference for the married respondents compared to those who were single was 2.8 (estimate = 2.8, 95% CI: 1.0, 4.5), indicating a higher frequency of stress in the married nurses, while the mean difference for the respondents working in the adult ICUs compared to the pediatric ICUs was 3.8 (estimate = 3.8, 95% CI: 2.3, 5.4), indicating a higher frequency of stress among the respondents working in the adult ICUs, and the mean difference for respondents working in the trauma ICU compared to the medical ICU was 2.0 (estimate = 2.0, 95% CI: 0.3, 4.3).
Significant differences were noted in the mean patients and their families scores for marital status (p-value = 0.0277) and the type of ICU (p-value < 0.0001). The mean difference for the respondents who were single compared with those who were married was 0.32 (estimate = 0.32, 95% CI: 2.1, 1.5), indicating a higher frequency of stress among the married respondents, while the mean difference for the patients and families scores in the adult ICUs compared to the pediatric ICUs was 7.4 (estimate = 7.4, 95% CI: 5.9, 8.9).
A statistically significant difference was found in the mean discrimination scores for the number of months the respondents had been working in the ICUs (p-value = 0.0496). The mean difference for the respondents who had worked 0–3 months compared to 4–6 months was 1.8 (estimate= 1.8, 95% CI: 3.5, 0.2).
Similarly, significant differences were found in the mean problems with peer support scores and the number of months the respondents had been working in the ICUs (p-value = 0.0385) and in the peer support scores and number of dependents (p-value = 0.0024). The mean difference for the respondents who had worked 0–3 months compared to 4–6 months was 1.5 (estimate = 1.5, 95% CI: 3.5, 1.4), indicating a higher frequency of stress among the respondents who had worked 0–3 months, while the mean difference for the respondents with children compared to no children was 2.3 (estimate = 2.3, 95% CI: 0.7, 3.9), indicating a higher frequency of stress for the respondents with 1–2 children. No other significant differences were associated with the problem with peer support subscale and the rest of the demographic variables in this category.

## Discussion
This study aimed to quantify the magnitude of stress among NGNs working in ICUs. NGNs have not been able to fully integrate their theoretical knowledge with clinical practice. They require more experience and need support from other colleagues during the transition period from study to work. They are faced with challenges and a great amount of stress as they start their careers. Based on the results of the PSS-10 items, the NGNs felt nervous and stressed in the last month before the survey took place. This relates to the perceived helplessness factor, which measures an individual’s feeling of lack of control over circumstances . One essential trait that NGNs should possess is professional confidence in providing quality nursing care from simple to complex patient cases. However, as graduate nurses enter the workforce, some fail to exhibit this confidence. A research survey results found that that NGNs lacked professional confidence during their first year of practice in the hospital setting . In addition, fear associated with making mistakes in highly technological and complex nursing care for seriously ill patients may be the source of stress in ICU . As they enter the nursing workforce, some NGNs mistrust their own capabilities and experience, are doubtful about their level of performance, and may lack the ability to communicate with other nurses or with physicians and patients.
The angered because of things that were out of your control subscale, which was ranked high among the PSS-10 subscales, also falls under the perceived helplessness factor. This may be because NGNs are novices in what they are doing and may not be prepared for changes in the workplace. As emphasized in one article, anger can increase confidence under any circumstances if people manage emotions effectively . With so many changes in the workplace, it is important to channel anger in more positive ways and think about strategies in which NGNs can use this emotion as the motivation to develop their capabilities.
On the other hand, the NGNs’ perceived self-efficacy in the items “felt that you were on top of things” and “felt confident about your ability to handle your personal problem.” An earlier study revealed that male nurses had higher self-efficacy than female nurses . Males are more mature and confronting problems and finding solutions than females. The hospital setting is an important venue for developing employees’ self-efficacy. If they have self-efficacy, nurses are more likely to overcome failure and be more confident in dealing with difficult situations.
The findings from the present research highlighted that discrimination was the highest stressor among the 9 ENSS subscales. One of the biggest clinical difficulties nurses’ faces is discrimination, which may be a significant roadblock to socialization. This stressor can have negative effects on a person, including tiredness, demoralization, lack of professional commitment, conflict and tension at work, and resignation . Male nurses are also affected by this stressor. Men are far more underrepresented in the nursing profession in China, and they also encounter many difficulties related to working in a female-dominated profession .
The subscale with the third highest mean value was workload. As NGNs begin their professional role, they may encounter challenges such as not being able to meet deadlines. A previous study revealed that new nurses perceived low to moderate levels of stress, mainly from heavy workloads and lack of professional nursing competence , . Similarly, A high proportion of respondents who had trouble with their workload, which affected their ability to perform safe healthcare practices . It is important that employers understand what NGNs are going through during their transition from study to work. They must set realistic goals related to workloads and, in turn, support a positive transition.
It was revealed in the present study that perceived stress was higher in the female NGNs than in the male NGNs. Their various responsibilities in their daily lives may be the reason. As working women, female nurses balance responsibilities in their daily lives, such as raising children, being the caregiver of aging parents, and being the breadwinner , . In other words, having many roles can create a conflict between work and home roles that can lead to stress. It is important that a workplace setting uses gender-sensitive approaches that consider the preferences and interests of both sexes in preventing work-related stressors.
Higher perceived stress levels in the full-time respondents than in the part-time respondents were noted in this study. The results also revealed a high level of stress in those who worked longer hours. As they work longer hours in the hospital setting, they are more exposed to difficult situations, and this can be stressful to them . Employers hire full-time employees because it has more benefits to the organization compared to hiring part-timers. Some of the benefits are ease of scheduling meetings, perception of loyalty, and more work hours per person .
A significant difference was revealed in the mean uncertainty concerning treatment subscale regarding gender, which indicated higher stress levels for the females. A similar finding was found by a previous study which revealed a highly significant correlation between almost all stress domains, but the stressor with the highest mean was death and dying, followed by uncertainty concerning treatment, and inadequate preparation . However, the previous study did not tackle the correlation of these stressors to all the demographic variables of its respondents.
Higher stress levels were displayed among the respondents in the 19–25 years age group in this study in relation to inadequate emotional preparation. This can be explained by the view that younger staff encounter new challenges and workloads and may not be emotionally prepared compared to older staff in the ICU who are more experienced. Another study found that younger nurses had fewer work experience and higher levels of stress and burnout . Promotional and preventive interventions are needed to maintain nurses’ well-being.
This study also revealed that the married nurses had higher stress than the unmarried nurses in the death and dying and patients and their families’ subscales. Similarly, previous studies revealed that as patients lay dying, nurses thought that there was an insufficiency in the emotional and psychological support they could offer to patients and their families during the COVID-19 pandemic, and they therefore felt troubled , . However, previous research did not correlate the death and dying subscale with marital status. It was also revealed in a previous study that 70% of ICU nurses experienced difficult patients and families, which caused them stress . The reason may be that when ICU nurses are handling patients and their families, the fear of making mistakes and being insulted arises, which increases their stress levels on a different note, it was revealed that while the neonatal ICU nurses were stressed, they felt that they were more respected by peers and families, which contradicts the findings of the present study . No similar study was found revealing higher stress among trauma ICU nurses in terms of death and dying and patients and their families’ subscales.
ICU nurses were deemed more stressed than any other unit in the hospital in general. This was revealed in the present study and is like the findings of previous studies , , . The present study indicated that there was higher stress in relation to the discrimination subscales among the respondents who had worked for 0–3 months. This can be best explained by the fact that, since they are new to the clinical area, they lack expertise compared to experienced nurses. They may not feel that they are trusted during their early months. Similarly, one study revealed that ICU nurses in Australia and New Zealand reported a high level of discrimination in the last 12 months but did not always report it because they did not feel confident that the issue would be addressed and action taken . Contrary to the findings of the present study, A study stated that new nurses had moderate to high levels of stress at 4 to 8 months of employment and the lowest levels at 12 months .
The present study also revealed that the respondents who had been working for 0–3 months in the ICUs experienced problems with peer support. This stressor was also frequently experienced by nurses with 1–2 dependents. Peer support plays an important role in reducing the effect of stressors on emotional exhaustion, which can hinder the growth intention of employees in different fields. This finding agrees with a previous longitudinal study that found that new nurses experience stress with management and interpersonal relationships at the start of their employment . On a different note, the transition to parenthood is a difficult time for mothers’ mental health. In one study, it was revealed that peer support contributed to reducing nurses’ low mood and anxiety by overcoming feelings of isolation and empowerment, thus increasing their self-esteem, self-efficacy, and parenting competence .
Overall, to support NGNs’ mental and physical health and enhance the standard of patient care, it is critical to manage workplace pressures for nurses working in Saudi Arabian ICUs. To lower workplace pressures and foster a supportive work environment, legislators, healthcare institutions, and nurses must collaborate to create and implement successful interventions.

## Conclusion
The results from the survey comprise demographic variables, and the PSS-10 and ENSS-57 items. The aim of the survey was to provide a more global view of the levels of stress and identify the key stressors experienced by NGNs working in ICUs.ThePSS-10 was employed particularly to focus on measuring the level of stress while the ENSS-57 items were used to measure the factors that cause stress. The self-administered survey afforded results that were able to inform the next components of the project, namely the individual interviews with NGNs and the group discussion with nurse educators,
In particular, the survey highlighted a wide range of issues from the perspective of a group of respondents who were reasonably homogeneous, consisting of mainly females who were young and who work long hours. These nurses have limited experience working in extremely challenging and stressful environments such as ICUs. The survey showed that the major factors that caused stress among the NGNs were discrimination, problems with supervisors, workload, problems with peer support, uncertainty concerning treatment, and dealing with patients and their families.

## Recommendations
Herein, this research has contributed new knowledge regarding to the stressors experienced by Saudi new graduate nurses working in the ICUs. It could be used to guide policymakers in making decisions for future directions.
The Saudi new graduate nurses require continued administrative support particularly from the nurse educators and supervisors, a work environment that fosters open communication with the top management and appropriate training programs to deal with potentially stressful conditions in the ICUs.
It would be advisable to replicate this study in order to be able to make comparisons between Saudi new graduate nurses working in public hospitals in different regions of Saudi Arabia.
This study arose from an issue that is the consequence of a rapidly changing and quite unique environment. The nature of the problem, stress in new graduate nurses, required an evidence-based approach to designing a solution. Where little evidence exists, an investigation is warranted.
