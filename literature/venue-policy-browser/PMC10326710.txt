## Summary
Aging population grows faster and has great demand for mental healthcare in the Western Pacific Region. Within the continuum of holistic care framework, mental healthcare for older adults is conceptualized to promote people's mental wellbeing, the “positive” states of mental health. As social determinants accounts much for mental health outcomes, addressing these factors may benefit older adults mental wellbeing in natural settings. Social prescribing, emerged as an innovative approach linking medical and social care, has been observed to potentially benefit mental wellbeing of older adults. However, it remained uncertain how to successfully implement social prescribing schemes in realistic world, such as community. In this viewpoint, we discuss three key elements, i.e., stakeholders, contextual factors, and outcome measures, that might facilitate identification of appropriate implementation strategies. Besides, we argue that implementation research needs to be strengthened and supported, aiming to accumulate evidence for scaling up social prescribing schemes to promote older adults mental wellbeing in the population level. We also provide directions for future implementation research on social prescribing for mental healthcare among older adults in the Western Pacific Region.
Keywords: Social prescribing, Mental healthcare, Older adults, Implementation research

The proportion of older people is growing faster in the Western Pacific Region. There are more than 800 million people aged 65 and over worldwide and more than 270 million live in the Western Pacific Region. This number is expected to double by 2050. Population aging has significant societal impact on health and disability. As WHO stated, “no health without mental health,” mental health and social wellbeing problems, such as cognitive decline, depression, and loneliness, have been considered as major contributors to their disabilities, leading to an increasing dependency ratio in aging community. Therefore, there is an increasing need for improving mental health for older adults worldwide.
The International Psychogeriatric Association (IPA) has prioritized to build an integrated mental healthcare model especially in low- and middle-income countries. However, the response to mental healthcare needs is often fragmented and poorly coordinated, especially in Western Pacific Region. The IPA affiliates from the Western Pacific Region, such as mainland China, Hong Kong SAR, and Indonesia, also echoed that community-based mental healthcare may facilitate the accessibility of the service and increase the coverage of care service.
Since its inception in the UK, social prescribing has emerged as an approach that enables the link between medical and social care. , It has been gaining popularity internationally, including countries in the Western Pacific Region. In recent years, the World Health Organization Western Pacific Regional Office (WHO WPRO) has been instrumental in promoting social prescribing for aged care, including mental healthcare. However, the evidence of social prescribing in mental healthcare remains limited. In this viewpoint, we provide an overview of implementing social prescribing in mental healthcare, with a focus on implementation research needs related to core elements such as stakeholders, contextual factors, and outcome measures.
We begin the overview by conceptualizing mental healthcare within the continuum of holistic care framework. In this framework, the goal of mental healthcare is not merely alleviating the burden of mental illness. Rather, mental healthcare aims to promote a person's mental wellbeing, the “positive” states of mental health, as elevating mental wellbeing is increasingly considered as a therapeutic route to disease prevention as well as clinical and personal recovery. , We also articulate the rationale of addressing social determinants of mental wellbeing in older adults through social prescription. With a general review of the current state of implementation research on social prescribing, we noted a number of gaps in delivering social prescription for older adults mental wellbeing. Therefore, our view is that implementation research on social prescribing should be prioritized, aiming to accumulate evidence for further scale-up social prescribing schemes to promote older adults mental wellbeing in the population level.

## Conceptualizing mental healthcare in the continuum of holistic care framework
Mental health is a state of successful performance of mental function, resulting in productive activities, fulfilling relationships with people, and the ability to adapt to change and to cope with adversity. Traditionally, individuals are dichotomized as either mentally ill or presumed mental healthy from the psychiatric view of mental health. However, some individuals free of mental disorders do not feel healthy or function well. Particularly, a large proportion of older adults needs mental health services because of a mental health problem, such as feeling of depression, loneliness, and subjective cognitive decline, while there is no diagnosable disorder. , , Thus, from the view of a complete state model of health, providing continuous holistic care for older adults is necessary to address mental health challenges. Within this framework, scholars and service providers in mental healthcare must consider both mental illness and mental wellbeing. Besides, mental healthcare targeting improvement in mental wellbeing should be highlighted as a large body of scientific literature has demonstrated improvement in mental wellbeing is associated with reducing the risk of developing mental illness and with improving the chance of recovery in people with mental illness and physical illnesses. 

## Rationale of addressing social determinants of mental wellbeing through social prescription
Good mental wellbeing is fundamental to a person's quality of life and enables the person to experience life as meaningful. The literature on mental wellbeing mostly taps into subjective wellbeing, which is based on an individual's own internal evaluation of life quality, such as life satisfaction, positive emotion, and feeling of being respected. 
There is increasing evidence that social determinants explain much of the variance in mental health outcomes. For example, social isolation and shortage of social networks may increase the risk of loneliness, depression, and cognitive impairment , , ; social participation and connectedness are associated with mental health status , ; social engagement may increase the positive response to stress and enhance the feeling of being valued. , Lack of mental health literacy, inequity of access to healthcare for mental health problems, and stigma against mental illness may also contribute to poor outcome of mental wellbeing, in particular among those with depression, anxiety or cognitive impairment. Therefore, addressing social determinants may has its advantage in promoting mental wellbeing.
Though existing literature has demonstrated potential benefit of psychological interventions in improving mental wellbeing, substantial barriers to implement psychological interventions for older adults exist. One of these is the shortage of geriatric mental health workforce, especially in the Western Pacific Region. In the past decades, geriatric mental health professionals have recognized the significance of linking with primary care. However, the lack of effective coordination between the social and health sectors has been a great concern. Challenges in recruiting professionals across multiple disciplines to improve mental healthcare for older adults persist in various cultural contexts. Social prescribing, effective in linking persons with non-medical sources of support within the community, is viewed as a potential means of addressing mental health needs and enhancing mental wellbeing.
In a 15-year span of review on quantitative and qualitative studies, Chatterjee and colleagues noted the potential benefit of social prescribing schemes for mental wellbeing, yet the evidence of effectiveness remains limited. Recently, the WHO WPRO toolkit of social prescribing for older adults' mental health was piloted in a community in Shangrao, China. To implement social prescription, mental health professionals, social workers, community staff, and volunteers formulated a coordinated service team, and delivered person-centered mental healthcare, including assessing mental health needs and resources of social support, developing and implementing individualized mental healthcare plan, and evaluating the feedback of older adults. With a pre- and post-social prescribing control design, the study found that social prescribing significantly improved the cognitive performance of community elderly residents. Also, the change of cognitive performance was associated with the baseline level of social support utilization. However, limited use of quantitative methods, lack of control groups, and insufficient process evaluation are major gaps against establishing effective strategies for implementing social prescribing schemes. In addition, the stakeholders’ perspectives, including community participants, implementation teams, and policy makers, are not fully understood. This may hamper the understanding of how participants benefit from social prescribing. Therefore, pragmatic studies that blends implementation and effectiveness research and uses quasi-experimental study designs may present novel opportunities to examine the effectiveness of social prescribing schemes in community mental health for older adults.

## Implementation research on social prescribing as mental healthcare for older adults
In Western Pacific Region, most older adults live at home. Community settings have the potential to play an important role in the widespread application of social prescribing. Social prescribing is a person-centered and goal-oriented approach to improve people's quality of life. Therefore, promoting the effective social prescribing schemes to community settings may be the key to improve quality and effectiveness of mental healthcare for older adults. Implementation research may narrow the gap between discovering new knowledge and the application of social prescribing in mental health. , During the process of “translation to practice”, implementation research needs to be strengthened, aiming to address the systematic processes that are used to integrate social prescribing in community mental health service for older adults.

## Adopter and implementer issues
Seeking government support is crucial for sustaining and amplifying impact of social prescribing. The WHO WPRO considers recruiting local governmental leaders to formulate the social prescribing team as one of the critical steps. Policymakers and community opinion leaders are potential adopters of implementing social prescribing in community. Their attitudes towards aging, mental health and social prescribing may impact the organizational structure of social prescribing. Therefore, further research is needed to explore the adopters' characteristics and perspectives on social prescribing in community-based mental healthcare. Besides, Morse and colleagues highlighted the role of leadership and governance in implementing social prescribing worldwide. However, the studies on the governance models related to social prescribing for mental healthcare are limited. Further research is warranted to explore the appropriate governance model tailored to local health and social care system for older adults’ wellbeing.
As illustrated in , social prescribing has schemed as a type of person-centered service, where older adults are the center of the close-looped service. Wherever they seek mental healthcare, the relevant stakeholders will refer them to the other side of the care. Thus, a multidisciplinary team, termed as implementers, constitutes the major workforce for implementing social prescribing. Historically, supported referral is one of the major UK social prescribing schemes. The referral pathways focus on enabling mental health services users to identify and access support to meet their needs. As mental health professionals may not be aware of community social care resources or have no time on this, link workers who have knowledge of local organizations and social support resources can act a bridge between medical care and community psychosocial support. Therefore, ideal implementers of social prescribing for older adults include geriatric psychiatrists and nurses, social care providers (usually social workers and community staff), and link workers. , However, the feasibility of installing link workers remained uncertain. Cultural adaptation should be considered. For example, when social prescribing was piloted in Shangrao, the local mental healthcare system has not been integrated and it was not easy to identify a single person as link worker. The team customized the term link worker as link worker team, which consisted of community mental health staff and Lou Zhang (the leader of a residential quarter). No matter how link workers or link worker team are formulated, education and training are necessary for capacity building. Further research is needed to develop an effective capacity building program for link workers, especially for those who do not have sufficient knowledge of mental healthcare in Western Pacific Region.
 

## Contextual factors
As the interventions included in the social prescribing for mental health might reflect local cultural norms, beliefs, and values, more attention should be given to cultural adaptation. , In a systematic review, factors embedded in cultural context, such as personal perception and public awareness, are identified as potential barriers that may hinder the implementation of social prescribing. 
In Western Pacific Region, there also remains a strong stigma against mental and behavioral problems. Most older adults tend to delay the care-seeking for their mental health problems. Besides, the public literacy of mental health problems was very low, especially regarding optimal care. , These factors may affect the engagement of older adults in social prescribing for mental wellbeing. Therefore, further efforts should highlight the need to design and tailor social prescribing schemes to ensure mental healthcare equity for older adults. Besides, researchers should consider mixed-methods approaches to evaluate individual engagement with social prescribing, with special attention to people living in underrepresented and low-resource areas.
During the COVID-19 pandemic, social distancing and lack of substantial support may increase the vulnerability to mental health problems. , Previous studies have observed that linking medical and social care potentially benefited mental wellbeing of older adults. , Therefore, maximizing community function and providing psychosocial support within the neighborhood maybe provide optimal solutions for mental health and wellbeing for older adults during the post-pandemic era. However, the social care resources for psychosocial support remain very limited, especially in low- and middle-income countries. Therefore, more research is needed to understand how a multisectoral partnership facilitates the shift of community social care resources to mental healthcare.

## Outcome measures
Ultimately, implementing social prescribing schemes of mental healthcare aims to improve older adults’ mental wellbeing. Mental wellbeing is a complex concept. In addition to mood and cognitive function, it also includes self-efficacy, the feeling of respect and value, and social adaptation and achievements. In most community-based multidisciplinary program, mood and cognitive function are usually measured. , , Sometimes, life satisfaction, quality of life, and level of distress and burden are reported. , , Besides, wisdom has recently been lifted to the frontier in older adult mental health research, as it is not only originated from philosophy but also linked with social cognition and can be measured. , Therefore, developing a set of ecological measures for mental wellbeing outcome remains a priority in research on social prescribing.
As discussed earlier, previous studies on social prescribing schemes give sparse attention to external validity. The existing implementation evidence is largely descriptive. , Most strategies to implement social prescribing are “bundled” approaches because it is less understood which specific components are effective in community-based service. Therefore, different implementation strategies may generate different outcomes when the contextual conditions are considered. For example, when social prescribing interventions fail in a community mental healthcare venue, we must distinguish whether it is an intervention failure or implementation failure. To examining the potential intermediating effect of implementation strategies, using implementation outcomes to model success of social prescribing may be an area of interest in Western Pacific Region as social prescribing becomes more widespread.
Implementing social prescribing schemes are mostly behavioral, educational, and psychosocial, rather than technological. Such practice makes it challenging to specify and measure implementation outcomes, such as acceptability, adoption, feasibility, fidelity, implementation cost, and sustainability. Qualitative interview, quantitative survey, and record archival are most often used, though the robustness of these measurements remains uncertain due to lack of psychometric validation. In recent years, digital technology has been introduced to mental healthcare, with special attention to meet needs of those living in remote areas. Digital tools may offer new opportunities to create novel measures of implementation outcomes. For example, in community care settings, demanding fidelity during intensive interventions may offset the retention of study participants. Thus, measuring fidelity is critical for implementation process evaluation. , , A digital platform may enable to capture the participation in social prescribing interventions, and thus measure the implementation fidelity dimensions, such as adherence to the mental healthcare delivery and participant engagement. Besides, multi-morbidities among older adults are common. Digit technology may help to simplify the complicated referral system which may hinder the implementation of social prescribing. For example, a digital mental healthcare triage platform development project is ongoing in China. The triage platform is expected to produce a personalized psychosocial interventions list from data of computerized assessment and share the list among social prescribing implementation team. These efforts may facilitate utilization of collected data in the living environment and save workforce and time costs for implementing social prescribing schemes. , Qualitative studies could be used to understand perspectives of implementation team members and participants on the application of digital tools. Further research is needed to investigate the feasibility of digital technology in evaluating the implementation process.

## Conclusion
With its widespread worldwide, social prescribing has been considered as a potential effective way for older adults' mental wellbeing. Implementing social prescribing schemes in natural settings, such as community health and mental health venues is a key step to translate research findings of psychosocial interventions to practice. There is an urgent need to identify implementation strategies that maximize access to mental healthcare resources and promote older adults' mental wellbeing. However, implementation evidence on social prescribing for mental healthcare remains limited, especially in Western Pacific Region, because implementation research on social prescribing for mental wellbeing of older adults is in its infancy. , Therefore, continuous research investment in implementing social prescribing should be leveraged to accelerate the process that translates advanced knowledge and techniques of integrated psychosocial support into local practice and eventually supports policy development. Emerging efforts should place specific attention to addressing contextual condition, understanding stakeholders' perspectives, specifying outcomes measures, and studying effectiveness and success model when implementing social prescribing schemes for older adults’ mental wellbeing.
