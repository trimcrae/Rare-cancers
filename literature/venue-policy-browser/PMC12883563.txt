## ABSTRACT

## Aims
This study aims to investigate the barriers encountered by specialised nurses working in nurse‐led clinics at a tertiary hospital and to identify strategies for enhancing service delivery and operational management.

## Design
A descriptive qualitative study, conducted in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ).

## Methods
Purposeful sampling was employed to select 20 specialty nurses providing outpatient care at nurse‐led clinics in a tertiary hospital in Fujian Province, China. Semistructured in‐depth interviews were conducted, and data were analysed using conventional content analysis.

## Results
The strategies and barriers identified in nurse‐led clinics can be categorised into four main themes: (1) Challenges and Difficulties in Work, (2) Resource Support and Optimisation, (3) Advantages of Specialty Integration, and (4) Innovations and Development.

## Conclusions
The development of nurse‐led clinics requires risk protection and robust support, underscoring the importance of managerial attention and collaboration among healthcare professionals. Additionally, enriching professional content and implementing innovative service models are essential for the long‐term advancement of specialty nursing outpatient practice.

## Theme 1: Challenges and Difficulties in Work
4.2.1.1

## Limited Prescription Authorisation
The majority of participants expressed supportive attitudes toward granting nurse prescribing authority in outpatient care. They generally believed that prescribing authority could enhance nurses' practice safety and streamline the patient consultation process. Nurse prescribing authority refers to the right of nurses to prescribe medications and relevant tests in clinical practice (Hou  ). Granting prescribing authority enhances the safety of specialised nurses by allowing them to independently manage clinical tasks within their scope, reducing delays, minimising communication‐related errors, and improving continuity of patient care. Nurses' prescribing authority can provide patients with timely nursing interventions, help reduce costs, and decrease hospital readmissions. Prescribing authority can also enhance nurses' ability to implement evidence‐based practice, enabling them to provide patients with more comprehensive and high‐quality medical and healthcare services. Participant N13: ‘The question arises as to why we have to incur charges through medical professionals for handling many wounds that we can manage on our own’. Participant N1: ‘Some patients require auxiliary tests for their treatment, and often they are asked to run back and forth to the doctor's office for prescriptions. This process involves waiting in queues and can lead to a poor patient experience’.

4.2.1.2

## The Project Cannot Be Charged
During the interviews, it was found that some nursing procedures lack corresponding chargeable items, resulting in a mismatch between nursing value and pricing. This situation has an impact on the implementation of certain nursing techniques. Participant N3: ‘Developmental cognitive training for newborns has significant benefits for children's intellectual development. We are keen to offer this training in our clinic, yet obtaining the necessary funding for it has been challenging’. Participant N7: ‘In our specialised practice, besides conducting blood glucose testing, we also engage in extensive health education efforts. Despite providing thorough health education, there is currently no associated fee for these services. This indeed appears to be an inconsistency’.

4.2.1.3

## Incomplete Salary and Performance Evaluation
Compensation and benefits constitute the material foundation for job satisfaction among specialised nurses. The absence of a reasonable salary system and performance incentive mechanism prevents specialised nurses from receiving appropriate rewards and recognition, thereby diminishing their job satisfaction and loyalty. Participant N19: ‘An effective approach to boosting motivation is to provide departmental bonuses, subsidies for clinic work, and commissions. Highlighting the performance of nurse‐led clinics is crucial, ensuring that their incentives are notably superior to those of ordinary nurses. This strategy serves as a driving force for nurses to actively engage in clinic activities’.

4.2.1.4

## Operational Processes Encounter Obstacles
Although nurse‐led clinics are still in the exploratory stage of development, nursing staff have demonstrated considerable enthusiasm for this innovative practice model. However, during the operational process, various challenges have been encountered, including inadequate information systems, unclear nurse roles, and chaotic patient visit procedures. Participant N4: ‘The current major challenge I believe is our backend information system. We need to operate several systems during work, and sometimes the computers are very slow and laggy’. Participant N5: ‘We suggest appointing designated staff to handle payment matters, which would permit nursing staff to devote their full attention to PICC maintenance and other technical procedures, while also enhancing patient‐facing time’. Participant N20: ‘Many patients are unaware of the consultation process. Some arrive without registering, while others need to move between the doctor's office and the nursing clinic. This back and forth creates dissatisfaction among patients due to the inconvenience’.

## Theme 2: Resource Support and Optimisation
4.2.2.1

## Optimisation of Hardware Facilities
Inadequate hardware facilities present a hindrance to the smooth operation of nurse‐led practices, as proper equipment is essential for conducting specialised procedures effectively. Participant N12: ‘Inadequate support facilities are impeding the implementation of various projects within the specialised unit. While there is a desire to initiate numerous initiatives, the absence of corresponding equipment is preventing their execution’. Participant N18: ‘The department should also be equipped with emergency instruments and facilities to ensure collaborative and coordinated responses in case of urgent situations’.

4.2.2.2

## Insufficient Nursing Manpower Is a Challenge
The inadequate allocation of nursing manpower imposes physical and psychological pressure on outpatient specialist nurses, impeding their effective role performance. This situation can lead to the emergence of negative emotions and work fatigue, thereby compromising service quality and elevating the risk of medical incidents. Participant N14: ‘To foster genuine development in specialised outpatient clinics, the establishment of a comprehensive team is essential. This team of specialised nurses should encompass a sufficient number of members to ensure its effectiveness’. Participant N13: ‘The hospital's perception of our low outpatient volume has led to a shortage of staff allocation. Currently, I am unable to step away from the clinic to attend to additional tasks. If an additional team member could be assigned, I would have the flexibility to conduct patient education in the wards and accompany doctors during consultations. This would enable me to guide patients in need of treatment to the clinic effectively’. Participant N5: ‘At times, I feel overwhelmed and find it challenging to meet the demands. The daily volume of outpatient work is substantial, and it necessitates adequate staffing support. Additionally, there is a need for a selection process for personnel, ensuring a suitable team to manage the workload effectively’.

4.2.2.3

## Collaboration Among Various Healthcare Disciplines Is Not Deeply Established
There is a lack of teamwork between medical and nursing staff, resulting in a lack of effective collaboration. This absence of information, resources, and experience sharing has led to missed opportunities for providing better healthcare services, impacting the quality of patient care and increasing the risk of medical errors. Participant N2: ‘Collaboration between medical and nursing staff is crucial. Currently, our consultation schedules are not synchronised with those of doctors, which sometimes leads to difficulties in locating us when doctors need patients to be attended to by nursing staff’. Participant N15: ‘If doctors consistently maintain such an attitude of questioning your work, it undoubtedly becomes challenging to carry out your tasks effectively’. Participant N10: ‘Doctors and nurses can collaborate in conducting comprehensive assessments and analyzing patients’ health conditions. This collaborative assessment ensures making accurate decisions tailored to the individual circumstances of the patient, thereby avoiding unnecessary risks’.

4.2.3

## Theme 3: Advantages of Specialised Integration
4.2.3.1

## Facilitating the Establishment of Nurse‐Led Teams
The integration of nurse‐led clinics across departments facilitates the development of nurse‐led clinics and promotes multi‐disciplinary collaboration, providing an ideal platform and opportunity for the construction of nurse‐led teams. Participant N10: ‘In our specialised clinic, we have five nurses who are able to care for a substantial number of patients. With this setup, we have a continuous six‐day work schedule, which ensures a more seamless patient care experience. I also hope for more individuals to join us, as a larger team is more advantageous for the growth of our specialised clinic’. Participant N2: ‘I work in the neonatal care clinic, and the PICC (Peripherally Inserted Central Catheter) is right next door to my consultation room. During times when there are not many patients, I can visit the PICC clinic to learn more. This way, I can broaden my knowledge and gain more insights’. Participant N4: ‘Previously, each specialty was quite fragmented, but now it is more unified, and nurses have a stronger sense of belonging’.

4.2.3.2

## Enhancing the Patient Experience
The integration of nurse‐led clinics can enhance the patient's healthcare experience by providing comprehensive nursing services within a single clinic. This eliminates the need for frequent referrals or navigating between different medical institutions, reducing the time and effort patients need to invest and thereby enhancing convenience and satisfaction in seeking medical care. Participant N17: ‘For discharged patients who require postoperative care such as those with gastric tubes, urinary catheters, or tracheostomies, having a centralised nurse‐led clinic ensures that they know exactly where to go. This streamlined approach not only makes it more convenient for patients but also enhances their overall healthcare experience’.

4.2.4

## Theme 4: Innovative Development and Construction
4.2.4.1

## Enhance the Outreach Efforts
Insufficient promotion can lead to a reduction in patient's willingness to seek medical care and a decrease in patient flow. To enhance public awareness of nurse‐led clinics, it is essential to intensify our promotional efforts through various platforms and channels. Participant N1: ‘The nursing department has implemented a multi‐channel promotion strategy for the specialty clinic by utilizing social media platforms, short video content, television broadcasts, and distributing informational cards throughout hospital departments. Although these efforts have led to an increase in patient volume, the outcomes remain suboptimal due to persistent patient unawareness’. Participant N12: ‘Releasing a comprehensive guide to outpatient care within the hospital that outlines the services and advantages of nursing clinics can effectively educate patients about their existence. This approach encourages patients to actively opt for nursing clinics when seeking medical assistance’.

4.2.4.2

## Enhance the Depth and Breadth of Professional Expertise
Nurse‐led clinics represent the comprehensive strength of hospitals and the nursing discipline. Nursing clinics should break through established patterns, develop personalised approaches based on evidence‐based nursing and clinical practice, explore new directions, and continuously innovate to enhance both the depth and breadth of professional expertise. Participant N16: ‘Many patients with vascular diseases can undergo continuous management from admission to discharge to home care. We need to consider how to manage and track such patients through various channels’. Participant N19: ‘The hospital is preparing to launch “Internet + Nursing Services,” expanding the scope of nurse's roles through “online app appointments and offline services”’. Participant N6: ‘Clinics should be established according to professional needs. For example, we can also open clinics for stroke and pulmonary rehabilitation management, broadening the scope of specialised directions and diversifying professional service offerings’.

## Discussion
This study highlights the need to strengthen performance‐based compensation and establish legal and policy frameworks, such as granting prescribing rights, to support the sustainable development of nurse‐led clinics. It also identifies key challenges: insufficient performance‐based compensation, lack of prescribing rights, and inefficient operations, which align with the findings of Shi et al. ( ). Currently, nurse‐led clinics and health education services are generally provided free of charge. In China, most nurse‐led services are not independently billed; instead, these procedures are typically included under physician billing codes. This limitation affects nurse autonomy and service sustainability, highlighting the need for policy reforms to better recognise the value of nursing services. The absence of prescribing rights not only complicates nursing workflows but also leads to operational inefficiencies and potential resource wastage. It also negatively affects patients' healthcare experiences, as noted by Gao et al. ( ). Compared to nurse‐led clinics in the UK, US (Connolly and Cotter  ), and Australia, which operate under clearly defined scopes (e.g., limited prescribing, test ordering), integrated multidisciplinary teams, and explicit billing structures, China's NLCs are still evolving. Most are embedded within hospital systems, with limited autonomy in pricing, unclear governance, and restricted prescriptive authority. These systemic differences help explain the barriers we identified (e.g., workflow ambiguity, billing issues) and underscore the need for context‐specific strategies. Therefore, health authorities should establish laws, regulations, and policies regarding nursing prescription, defining the rights and obligations of nurses in prescribing and thus clarifying their legal status (Zhu et al.  ). Hospitals should actively apply for relevant nursing service charge items, rationalise the price‐value relationship of nursing services, and reflect the professional value of nurses. Research indicates that dissatisfaction with salary and performance allocation reduces the enthusiasm of specialised nurses (Guo et al.  ). Therefore, hospitals should safeguard the legitimate rights and interests of attending nurses in terms of salary, performance, and occupational safety. A reasonable performance evaluation system should be implemented to enhance incentives for specialised attending nurses, including career advancement and compensation. This study also demonstrates that inefficient operational processes result in low nursing efficiency, decreased nursing quality, and poor patient experiences. Therefore, hospitals should strengthen information technology development, optimise nursing outpatient service processes, enhance the efficiency of nursing attending work, and increase patient satisfaction.
Our findings indicate that inadequate hardware facilities directly hinder the delivery of specialised nursing services, consistent with findings by Xuan et al. ( ) and Hamari et al. ( ). Too few nurses reduce the effectiveness of specialty care and increase workloads and stress (Ding et al.  ; Norful et al.  ; Quesada‐Puga et al.  ). Nursing departments should regularly assess workload and technical risks to optimise staffing and develop specialised skills among teams. This process requires collaboration between departments to form physician‐nurse teams that support nurse‐led clinics. Hospital administrators should build integrated support systems to ensure adequate infrastructure for outpatient nursing services. Most participants in this study agreed that the long‐term success of nurse‐led clinics depends on teamwork between medical and nursing staff, as effective collaboration improves patient safety and outcomes (Chen et al.  ; Costello et al.  ; Fukami et al.  ). The expansion of nurse‐led clinics also requires strong leadership, physician cooperation, and effective cross‐disciplinary teamwork.
The current interviews revealed that all participants indicated that integrating nurse‐led clinics from various departments into a unified department is beneficial for the overall development of nurse‐led clinics. The establishment of nurse‐led clinics offers a new platform for specialised nurses, diversifies nursing service modalities, broadens the scope of nursing services, promotes the development of the nurse‐led brand, alleviates the burden on both patients and physicians, and ultimately enhances patient satisfaction. Therefore, specialised nurses should continually update their knowledge, strengthen practical skills, keep informed about the latest nursing technologies, actively engage in research training and academic exchange activities, and maintain the updating and enhancement of their professional knowledge (Davis and Glasgow  ). Specialised nurses should also pay attention to patient needs and feedback, develop personalised nursing plans based on patient conditions, continuously improve and optimise nursing service content, and meet patient expectations and demands.
In this study, participants were concerned that limited promotion of nurse‐led clinics leads to low patient awareness, fewer patient visits, and slower clinic development. Therefore, hospitals should strengthen efforts to promote and guide nurse‐led clinics by utilising various forms of new media and effectively publicising their services (Kim et al.  ). Additionally, nurse‐led clinics should leverage information technologies such as AI (Artificial Intelligence), 5G, and the Internet to innovate nursing service models, thereby expanding the scope and content of their services (Guo and Li  ). By assessing patient needs through baseline surveys, nurse‐led clinics can tailor their services accordingly and diversify service offerings. By implementing a comprehensive approach involving ‘online, offline, and hotline consultations’ and integrating ‘Internet + nursing services’, nurse‐led clinics can offer specialised home‐based services to discharged patients and those with limited mobility (Cui et al.  ). Collaborating with community medical institutions and social service organisations can facilitate joint health projects and community health education activities. Establishing patient support groups or online platforms for patient interactions can also enhance service quality and patient experience, ultimately fostering a more supportive nursing environment. To enhance policy relevance, we recommend pilot projects for nurse prescribing in selected regions, the establishment of standardised charging mechanisms for nursing services, and the implementation of cross‐disciplinary training programs to strengthen collaborative practice. In summary, innovating nursing service models, enriching professional content, and exploring new avenues for the development of nurse‐led clinics are essential strategies for future advancement. This study was conducted in a tertiary hospital in Fujian Province, which limits generalisability. To enhance transferability, we provided a thick description of the local NLC model, including centralised pricing, staffing policies, and nurse management. These features may be common in other urban tertiary hospitals in China but may differ in community‐based or rural hospitals. Moreover, policy variation across provinces may influence NLC implementation and scaling. Thus, while themes such as role ambiguity and charging barriers may resonate nationally, the proposed strategies may require adaptation in different contexts.

## Limitation
The participants in this study were exclusively from a single hospital, and due to limited resources and conditions, there may be certain limitations in the depth and breadth of the research. Although the findings provide valuable insights into NLC operations in a Chinese tertiary hospital, the transferability of results to other regions or healthcare settings may be influenced by local policies, cultural attitudes toward nursing roles, and institutional support structures. The development of nurse‐led clinics remains in its exploratory phase, necessitating the ongoing accumulation of experiences and lessons from nursing practice. In the future, researchers should further investigate the construction and management systems of nurse‐led clinics from multiple perspectives based on this foundation.

7

## Conclusion
This study conducted qualitative interviews with 20 nurses working in nurse‐led clinics to identify the obstacles they encounter in practice and their recommendations for the development of such clinics. Nurse‐led clinics provide a platform for nurses to demonstrate their value and foster a favourable patient care environment. However, nurses continue to face numerous challenges and issues in their clinical practice within these settings. To translate our findings into actionable strategies, we propose the following concrete recommendations: First, healthcare institutions and policymakers should consider launching pilot projects for nurse prescribing to explore its safety and effectiveness under supervision. Second, standardised charging mechanisms should be developed to clarify billing for services provided in nurse‐led clinics. Third, cross‐disciplinary training programs involving physicians, pharmacists, and nurses should be established to enhance mutual understanding and cooperation. These strategies could be implemented either at the hospital level or through local health policy initiatives, providing a feasible path for scaling up nurse‐led clinic models. This study provides valuable insights into the operational realities of NLCs in China. The identified barriers and proposed strategies offer an evidence‐based roadmap for clinicians, hospital administrators, and policymakers to enhance the development and efficacy of nurse‐led care models.
