## Objectives
Young Black men are under-represented in sexual health services and research, a condition likely magnified during COVID-19 shutdowns due to disruption of STI screening and treatment services. We examined the effect of incentivized peer referral (IPR) increasing peer referral among young Black men in a community-based chlamydia screening program.

## Methods
Young Black men in New Orleans, LA, age 15–26 years enrolled in a chlamydia screening program between 3/2018 and 5/2021 were included. Enrollees were provided with recruitment materials to distribute to peers. Starting July 28, 2020, enrollees were also offered a $5 incentive for each peer enrolled. Enrollment was compared before and after the incentivize peer referral program (IPR) was implemented using multiple time series analysis (MTSA).

## Results
The percentage of men referred by a peer was higher during IPR compared to pre-IPR (45.7% vs. 19.7%, p < 0.001). After the COVID-19 shutdown was lifted, there were 2.007 more recruitments per week (p = 0.044, 95% CI (0.0515, 3.964)) for IPR, compared to pre-IPR. Overall, there was a trending increase in recruitments in the IPR era relative to the pre-IPR era (0.0174 recruitments/week, p = 0.285, 95% CI (− 0.0146, 0.0493)) with less recruitment decay during IPR compared to pre-IPR.

## Conclusions
IPR may be an effective means of engaging young Black men in community-based STI research and prevention programs, particularly when clinic access is limited.

## Introduction
Chlamydia trachomatis (Ct) is the most common reportable sexually transmitted infection (STI) in the USA, with over 1.8 million cases reported in 2019 [ ]. The state of Louisiana currently ranks 3rd in Ct case rates, with a rate of 775.3 cases per 100,000 population, 40% more than the national rate [ ]. The greater New Orleans area has the third highest Ct diagnosis rate of all major metropolitan areas in the USA [ ].
Youth age 15–24 years old experience the highest burden of Ct infection, accounting for almost two-thirds (61.0%) of all reported US cases in 2019 [ ]. Black youth have the highest risk of Ct infection [ ], and young Black men age 15–19 have a case rate 9.1 times higher than that of their White counterparts [ ]. Young men in this age group, particularly young Black men, are also less likely than their female counterparts to attend regular health care visits and are less likely to be insured [ ]. This is of concern as most Ct infections are asymptomatic — thus, screening in non-clinic settings is important in Ct detection and control efforts. Current Ct screening guidelines are focused on young women ages 24 and younger, due to the serious sequelae that can occur from untreated Ct infections, including infertility, pelvic inflammatory disease, and ectopic pregnancy [ – ].
At present, CDC guidelines do not recommend routine screening for young men who have sex with women, stating feasibility of testing men as a reason [ ]. Because of this, efforts have been made to determine if there are benefits to increasing screening and treatment in young men. The Check It study is a novel, bundled, community-based seek, test, and treat Ct screening program for 15- to 26-year-old Black men in New Orleans who have sex with women [ ]. Check It was designed with the purpose of determining if community-based Ct screening of young Black men ages 15–26 in New Orleans could both address barriers to Ct screening and treatment in this group and decrease rates of Ct in young women in New Orleans [ ].
Engaging young Black men in medical services and research has long been a challenge, largely due to historical trauma and experienced racism in the US medical system [ ]. Young Black men disproportionately experience greater barriers to healthcare. These barriers exist at multiple levels, including structural (e.g., transportation, inconvenient clinic hours, costs), individual (e.g., competing priorities, lack of time, confidentiality/privacy concerns, stigma, fear, low-risk perception), cultural (e.g., feelings of invincibility, masculinity norms, reluctance to seek health care), discrimination (e.g., individual, structural), and interpersonal (e.g., upbringing, negative influencers) [ – ].
The Check It team worked closely with student leadership, faculty, and student health centers at the historically Black colleges and universities in New Orleans, where much study recruitment occurred; local Black-owned barber shops; local Black-led community organizations; and community members to promote the study. Focus groups were also held with community members and young Black people in the target age range in order to gain insight into the best ways to engender the trust of the community. One piece of feedback was that young Black men were more likely to enroll in the study if they were recruited and consented by people who were their peers. The Check It study coordinators and many of the graduate student research assistants were representative of the study community, and study participants were encouraged to refer their peers to the study as part of the study protocol to further establish trust and community.
Peer referral became especially important during the COVID-19 pandemic, as the usual barriers to STI testing and study recruitment were exacerbated by a 2-month, city-wide, shut-down, and continued restrictions on in-person events. For adolescents age 14–19 in the USA, disruptions to STI screening and treatment services between March 2020 and June 2020 were expected to have resulted in 27,659 (26.4%) missed chlamydia and 5,577 (16.5%) missed gonorrhea cases among all genders in that age group [ ]. These declines in STI screening were also strongly associated with increases in weekly positivity rates for both chlamydia and gonorrhea among adolescents age 14–19 [ ]. A study among six pediatric primary clinics in Philadelphia, PA, found that during the pandemic, monthly asymptomatic adolescent STI screenings decreased from 479 per month pre-pandemic to 329 per month during the pandemic, ultimately resulting in an estimated 159 missed cases, 23.8% of expected cases, among this particular population [ ]. Of course, these missed tests and cases have real-world implications, and missed STI cases have the potential to lead to significant consequences for individual health, health care costs, and STI epidemiology [ ]. This is even more of an issue for groups such as young Black men who have sex with women who are marginalized and face higher barriers to care and, thus higher STI disease burdens than their more privileged peers. Thus, innovative strategies were needed to engage these young people in STI screening and treatment services.
Incentivized peer referral (IPR) is a form of peer referral in which study participants are compensated for each peer they successfully recruit and who enroll into the study. IPR has been shown to be an effective means of recruitment for HIV studies and care among transgender women and men who have sex with men (MSM) [ – ]. A study conducted in King County, WA, in the early 2000s found that IPR was an effective means of identifying new HIV cases in MSM [ ]. In separate studies, uptake of HIV services was found to increase both among Black MSM and transgender women in the USA and MSM in China via IPR, in part because peer referral both helped to reduce social stigma associated with HIV and helped to tap into self-identity and trust through community [ , ]. Although the evidence suggests that IPR is effective in engaging MSM and transgender women with HIV services, less is known about the effect on recruitment for STI screening and treatment and among men who have sex with women, particularly young Black men. The purpose of this analysis is to determine if this addition of IPR was associated with an increase in peer recruitment and, therefore, if it is an effective means of engaging young Black men to participate in a community-based Ct screening and treatment program.

## Methods

## Population and Setting
IPR data was collected via the Check It study, a community-based Ct and gonorrhea (GC) screening program in New Orleans for young Black men between the ages of 15 and 26 who have sex with women. The Check It study staff recruited participants via both venue-based and social marketing strategies between May 2017 and May 2021. Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, Check It study staff visited community venues in the Greater New Orleans Area — specifically, barber shops, local historically black universities, community colleges, and recreational centers-— Monday through Saturday to recruit study participants. Throughout each year of the study, the Check It team also partnered with organizations and venues across New Orleans to organize recruitment at events such as concerts, university homecoming, and back-to-school events at local community colleges and universities. Study recruiters approached potential study participants at these venues and events, providing them with an informational palm card and a short explanation of the study. Interested individuals were then asked a short series of questions to determine their eligibility.
To be eligible for the study, participants had to meet the following eligibility criteria: have a penis, identify as African American or Black; be between the ages of 15–26 at enrollment; live or spend most of their time in Orleans Parish; report having had vaginal sex with a woman in their lifetime; be willing and able to consent to study activities; speak and understand English; did not take azithromycin in the 2 weeks prior to enrollment; and did not previously enroll in the Check It study. Participants were provided with $25 compensation, in the form of a gift card or service of equivalent value (e.g., haircut, event ticket), as well as palm cards to pass out to their friends and peers who might be interested and eligible to participate in the study. Peer recruitment was not monitored, peer recruiters did not consent any study participants, and peer recruitment was not compensated prior to the implementation of IPR in July 2020.
Peer referral data was collected between March 7, 2018, and May 28, 2021. Due to the COVID-19 pandemic citywide shut down, venue-based recruitment was halted March 23, 2020, temporarily. Limited field recruitment was reinitiated on May 16, 2020. Although venue-based recruitment resumed after New Orleans COVID stay-at-home orders were lifted, the remaining COVID restrictions and realities of the pandemic continued to render recruitment in the community a challenge. Using evidence of the effectiveness of IPR in other studies, the Check It protocol was revised to include IPR and was approved by Tulane University IRB on July 27, 2020.
IPR was conducted between July 28, 2020, and the final day of recruitment, May 28, 2021. Participants were offered $5 for each person they referred and who was successfully enrolled in the study. Subjects were considered to be successfully enrolled if they were consented and completed all study activities (survey and STI screening). To further encourage participants to refer friends into the study, this was marketed as “Refer 5 friends for $25 incentive” at recruitment events and on the Check It Instagram profile. New participants were introduced to IPR during study consent and enrollment and were asked if a friend or peer referred them to the study. Incentives ($5 per enrollee) were given to the referrer in the form of an electronic gift card. Previously enrolled participants who agreed to future contact were sent an IRB-approved communication via SMS or Instagram direct message describing the IPR program (Fig.  ). Check It study staff provided strategies for engaging peers that included communication implements such as text messages, printed materials, and sharing Check It study information on social media. 

## Statistical Analysis
Data were limited to that collected between March 7, 2018, the day that collection of peer referral data began, and May 28, 2021, the final day of enrollment. Descriptive statistics were performed for this subset of participants (Table ) using IBM SPSS Statistics 24 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, 2016). 
The aim of this analysis was to determine if the rate of peer recruitment in the Check It study increased after the implementation of IPR, compared to the rate of peer recruitment during the pre-IPR period of the study. IPR was implemented in the final 10 months of the study as a means of increasing enrollment during the COVID-19 pandemic. Prior to the implementation of IPR, peer referral was uncompensated, and between May and July 2020 recruitment strategies overall were adjusted to meet the requirements of the COVID-19 pandemic. Thus, there were three distinct time periods in this study: March 2018 to March 2020, pre-shut down and pre-IPR; May–July 2020, post-shut down and pre-implementation of IPR; and July 2020 to May 2021, post-shut down and post-implementation of IPR. Note that the period of March–May 2020 has been omitted, as no recruitments occurred during this time due to COVID-19 stay-at-home orders in New Orleans.
As there are pre- and post-intervention periods with multiple observations on the outcome variable recorded during both periods, a multiple time series analysis (MTSA), a variation of the interrupted time series analysis, was conducted. The interrupted time series analysis (ITSA) has increasingly been used to evaluate public health interventions such as IPR [ ]. ITSA and MTSA are particularly suited to the evaluation of interventions introduced to a population over a clearly defined period [ ]. Using the time series of an outcome of interest to determine an underlying trend, the introduction of an intervention at a specific time or multiple time points — which interrupts this trend — is evaluated to determine its impact on the outcome of interest [24 25]. Thus, this analysis is particularly suited to evaluate the IPR intervention. To account for the observed variability in enrollment, a 7-day rolling average was used, which allowed us to account for daily variation in recruitment numbers (e.g., weekdays vs weekends). In addition, the period from March 20 to May 16, 2020, was omitted as no recruitments occurred during this time due to COVID-19 shut-down measures. Participant recruitment site was not considered in this analysis, as the purpose of this study was to examine trends in recruitment post-COVID shutdown and post-IPR implementation. The analysis did not compare which method of recruitment was most effective or participant outcomes by recruitment type, as all recruitment venues were subject to similar recruitment limitations post-COVID shutdown. The MTSA was performed using Stata 15.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, 2021), utilizing the MTSA code created by Ariel Linden of Linden Consulting Group, LLC [ ].

## Results
Between March 7, 2018, and May 28, 2021, 1534 participants were enrolled into the Check It study. Of these participants, 335 (21.9%) had been referred to the study by another study participant (friend or peer). Participant characteristics are summarized in Table . The mean age was 20.1 years (S.D. 2.6). Participants’ mean number of female sex partners was 14.98 (S.D. 24.25) and 170 (11.1%) of participants tested positive for Ct in the Check It parent study. Thirty-seven participants reported having a less than high school education, 282 (18.5%) were current high school students, 570 (37.4%) had graduated high school, 627 (41.1%) reported having more than a high school education, and 10 (0.7%) reported having an education of “other” or refused to answer. Regarding living situation, most participants (837, 54.9%) reported living at home with at least one parent or guardian. For the remaining participants, 15 (1.0%) lived with a foster family, 36 (2.4%) lived at a friends’ home with at least one parent or guardian present, 243 (15.9%) lived on their own, either alone or with a roommate, 205 (13.4%) reported living in a dormitory, 137 (9.0%) lived at home with a parent but attended college or were working, 18 (1.2%) reported not having a regular place to live, 22 (1.4%) reported their living situation as “other,” and 12 (0.8%) refused to answer the question.
When comparing study participants who had not been referred by a friend or peer to those who were referred to the study by a friend or peer (Table ), those referred by a friend or peer were more likely to be Ct positive (p = 0.029), have post-high school education (p = 0.003), have multiple female partners in the last two months (p > 0.001), and live on their own or in a dorm, rather than with a parent (p < 0.001). There was no statistically significant difference in age between those who were and were not referred by a friend or peer. 
Participants (n = 1391) were enrolled between March 7, 2018, and March 20, 2020, at which time COVID-19 shut-down measures were enacted in New Orleans. In the post-shut-down period between May 17 and July 27, 2020, 13 participants enrolled in the study; it should be noted that peer recruitment was still uncompensated during this period and that only 2 of these participants (15.4%) were referred by a peer. Incentivized peer referral (IPR) was approved by Tulane IRB on July 27, 2020, and implemented on July 28, 2020. The remainder of the participants (129) were enrolled between July 28, 2020, and May 28, 2021, in the post-IPR implementation period. Of these 129 participants, 59 (45.7%) were referred by a study participant (friend or peer). In total, 79 study participants were compensated for successfully enrolling at least one friend or peer into the Check It study. As peer recruitment was not only affected by the introduction of the IPR intervention but also the COVID-19 pandemic, multiple group ITSA was conducted to compare recruitment across these three distinct periods. It should be noted that since no participants were enrolled during the lockdown period of March 20 to Mary 16, 2020, this period was omitted from the analysis.
MTSA was conducted to examine the impact of IPR on the rate of participant enrollment. MTSA modeling indicates an initial mean of 15.21 (p < 0.01, 95% CI (11.176, 19.247)) recruitments per week with a decline of 0.00567 (p = 0.222, 95% CI (− 0.0148, − 0.00347)) enrollments per week over the period before the COVID-19 stay at home orders were put in place. In the post-stay at home/pre-IPR period of May 17 to July 27, 2020, there was a sharp drop of − 9.091 (p < 0.001, 95% CI (− 12.855, − 5.326)) recruitments in the first week relative to the pre-pandemic period. Over this segment of the pre-IPR period, there was a further weekly decline of − 0.00992 recruitments per week (p = 0.547, 95% CI (− 0.0424, 0.0225)). In the post-IPR period of July 27, 2020 until the study’s end on May 28, 2021, there was an increase in the first week of 2.007 recruitments (p = 0.044, 95% CI (0.0515, 3.964)) per week followed by a slightly upward, though not significant trend of 0.0174 participants recruited per week (p = 0.285, 95% CI (− 0.0146, 0.0493)). ## Discussion
IPR had a slight positive impact on recruitment of young Black men who have sex with women into a chlamydia (Ct) and gonorrhea (GC) seek, test, and treat community program in New Orleans, LA. After declines in recruitment observed between the pre-COVID/pre-implementation of IPR and post-COVID-19 shutdown/pre-IPR periods, the implementation of IPR resulted in a small increase in participant recruitment rate (an initial jump of 2.007 recruitments and increasing at a rate of 0.0174 participants recruited per week). While these increases are modest, so too was the cost of IPR at just $5 per recruit. This positive trend is similar to those observed in other community-based studies which used IPR as a means of recruitment, even though those other studies were predominantly focused on HIV, not other STIs, and targeted young men who have sex with men (MSM) and transgender women [ – ].
Of note, young men who were referred by a friend or peer were more likely to be Ct positive (p = 0.029), have post-high school education (p = 0.003), have multiple female partners in the last 2 months (p > 0.001), and live on their own or in a dorm, rather than with a parent (p < 0.001). One possible explanation for this is that much of the study recruitment occurred at a local community college and HBCU campuses, where these young men were living on their own, perhaps for the first time, and had greater exposure to new partners and new sexual experiences, thus increasing their risk for Ct infection. The use of IPR might afford researchers a greater ability to engage college sexual-health networks than traditional venue-based recruitment alone and could also assist in recruiting men at higher risk for Ct.
These results suggest that IPR may be a useful strategy to employ, alongside other efforts, when trying to recruit and engage young Black men in STI research and care. This may be particularly useful in contexts in which in-person recruitment may be limited, including school closures, holidays, and in remote settings with limited access to STI testing and treatment centers. Employing IPR as a recruitment and engagement strategy has the potential to have great clinical and public health implications and could also indirectly contribute to a decrease in STI transmission to their female sexual partners [ ].
Among youth in particular, services referred by peers, especially remote and virtual services, may have become more appealing than in-person clinics. In addition, an increase in virtual opportunities for making money which arose due to the COVID-19 pandemic further justifies the benefits of IPR. Additionally, IPR has the potential to help increase engagement of young Black men in research.
There are a few COVID-19-related factors that should also be mentioned to help interpret the data. STI testing services in New Orleans that were limited during and after stay-at-home orders were in place, which may have incentivized participants to enroll in the study, particularly those who had symptoms. The loss of job opportunities in New Orleans during the first year of the pandemic may have made participants more likely to enroll and refer peers in order to collect the incentive money. Though the results observed in the post-IPR implementation period may have been impacted by the pandemic, it is important to acknowledge that the future of study recruitment is now inextricably linked to both COVID-19 and its consequences. Thus, as we continue to navigate the ongoing pandemic landscape, the impact of IPR on study recruitment is worth studying further.

## Limitations
The rate of participant recruitment was already declining before the COVID-19 pandemic, due in part to saturation at the community recruitment sites. Although the implementation of IPR as a method of recruitment had only a slight positive impact on recruitment in the Check It study, it should be noted that this method of recruitment was not implemented until the end of the study, and during a time when COVID-19 restrictions made study recruitment challenging. It is thus worth exploring IPR further in future studies as a potential method to combat decay in recruitment; IPR serves to increase the potential subject pool, whereas venue-based recruitment is subjected to a finite number of people frequenting venues.
IPR was employed instead of respondent driven sampling (RDS). RDS combines snowball sampling (i.e., where a participant recruits other participants) with mathematic considerations about network characteristics. Hence, after several waves of RDS, the resulting sample approximates a random sample [ ]. IPR, in contrast, yields a non-probability sample. However, there is some evidence to suggest that IPR may be a more effective recruitment strategy than RDS. An HIV study, which examined engagement of Black MSM and transgender women in HIV screening, found that incentivized peer referral of $5 was a more effective method of recruiting people who were HIV positive than RDS [ ].
As expected, the results observed in the analysis were further influenced by the COVID-19 pandemic. In the period between the start of stay-at-home orders in March 2020 and approval for IPR, recruitment in general was low. This was because the Check It study could no longer hold in-person recruitment events or readily recruit in the community; instead, individuals who were interested in participating had to make an appointment with a study recruiter, which meant that recruitment slowed significantly during this time. Although stay-at-home orders were lifted in May 2020, the recruitment team was still limited in recruitment activities, since many of our community partners remained closed or severely limited in service. No participants were recruited in the stay-at-home period of March 20 to May 16, 2020, and in the post-stay-at-home/pre-IPR period of May 17 to July 27, 2020, only 13 participants were enrolled. In the post-IPR period of July 28, 2020, through the end of the study on May 28, 2021, only 129 additional participants were enrolled. Although this was an improvement compared to the time between March 20 and July 27, 2020, these numbers and the results of the analysis suggest that a larger sample size is needed to better evaluate the impact that IPR had on recruitment. Moreover, a longer IPR implementation period would help us to determine if the decay seen in the pre-implementation of IPR would be seen in the post-implementation of IPR as well.
It should be mentioned that during the early months of the COVID-19 pandemic, the Check It study had a more captive online audience. Thus, the possibility that the uptick in peer recruitment during this time may also be attributed to online engagement and not solely the implementation of IPR. Again, further study of IPR is warranted to determine the effects of IPR on study recruitment, particularly of young Black men and other underserved populations.
Mistrust of research and medical institutions and investigators has been noted as the most significant barrier to research participation by the Black community [ ]. One of the consequences of institutions failing to meet the needs of and engage young Black men in research is that they are underrepresented, and other groups are overrepresented in studies. This underrepresentation, in turn, contributes to increased inequities and health disparities. Institutions and investigators must make many changes in research study design and implementation to mitigate this issue.

## Conclusion
Despite these limitations, the results of this study suggest that peer referral, specifically IPR, could serve as means of engaging young Black men in a community STI screening study. Individuals who are enrolled in a study that they trust may be more likely to refer their friends and peers, especially if they receive an incentive for doing so. In turn, individuals may be more likely to enroll in a study if they are referred by someone they trust. A study looking at the efficacy of monetary incentives and peer referral in promoting digital network-based secondary distribution of HIV self-testing among men who have sex with men in China determined that peer referral as a strategy taps into self-identity with a community and trust to provide services more effectively [ ]. Research among Black MSM in the USA suggests that one strategy to improve uptake of HIV services by this population is stronger social support and reduced stigma from peers. Involvement and acceptance by the community and peer involvement with recruitment, as shown in the Check It study, may be part of this strategy [ ]. A 2018 study that engaged Black MSM and transgender women in an HIV screening study found that study participants who were compensated $5 for each peer they recruited online or in person were more successful at recruitment of HIV positive peers than study participants who were directed to recruit peers via respondent driven sampling (RDS) [ ]. It should be noted that the investigators of that study determined that the $5 compensation was a potential limitation, as that amount may have been too small to successfully motivate study participants to refer peers into the study [ ]. An earlier study in King County, WA, in which MSM were compensated $20 for each peer they referred to an HIV study, in addition to a $20 compensation for receiving an HIV test, determined that incentivized peer referral was an effective means of identifying new cases of HIV in MSM [ ]. It is thus worth studying and implementing IPR in future research studies and outreach programs aimed at engaging young Black men and other underserved groups in STI screening and treatment services, especially when considering barriers caused by the COVID-19 pandemic and other potential, widespread disruptions to screening and care in the future.

Key Messages
What Is Already Known: Access to STI screening and treatment was drastically reduced during the first months of the COVID-19 pandemic. This disruption in screening and treatment services is particularly an issue for groups who already disproportionately experience barriers to STI services, such as young Black men.
What This Study Adds: Incentivized peer referral (IPR) was observed to contribute to an increase in recruitment of young Black men who have sex with women into the Check It study during the COVID-19 pandemic.
Implications of This Study: IPR may be useful in engaging young Black men and other underrepresented groups in public health research and programs during times of disruption to access to STI screening and services.
