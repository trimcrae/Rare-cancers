## Background
Patient and public involvement and engagement (PPIE) in healthcare research has gained international recognition given its role in enhancing research relevance, quality, and impact; however, empirical evidence from non-Western contexts, including Hong Kong, remains limited. This study investigated how patients and the public perceive PPIE in healthcare research within a newly formed Patient and Public Advisory Committee in a newly formed research group-Digital Oncology Care Enhancement Lab in Hong Kong.

## Methods
Eleven participants, including seven cancer patients, one caregiver, and three social workers with experience in cancer care, were purposively recruited during the committee’s first formal engagement meeting. Two focus groups were conducted concurrently in March 2025. Data were collected via semi-structured interviews. The sessions were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.

## Results
Four major themes were identified: (1) Experience and Understanding of PPIE, including limited prior exposure, conceptual uncertainty, and unfamiliarity with its practices; (2) Perceived Benefits of PPIE, including improved patient-centered care, empowerment in health self-management, research quality, institutional service, and public health governance; (3) Perceived Barriers to PPIE, including personal constraints (time, privacy, psychological burden), limited research literacy (knowledge gaps and language or communication challenges), restricted access to participants, and researcher-dominated environments; and (4) Perceived Facilitators of PPIE, including trust in researchers, transparency in research processes, self-efficacy in health management, shared gains, long-term collaboration, flexibility, training, and incentives.

## Conclusion
This study contributes to the literature on PPIE in East Asian contexts by highlighting the initial perceptions and anticipatory views of PPIE among a specific, oncology-focused cohort. The findings indicate the need to develop a shared understanding of PPIE among patients, healthcare researchers, and the public. The identified facilitators and barriers provide practical guidance for researchers to implement PPIE successfully in healthcare research within the oncology context.

This study explored how patients and public representatives in Hong Kong understand and experience being involved and engaged in healthcare research. While involving and engaging patients and public representatives in healthcare research is well established in many Western countries, it remains a relatively new approach in Hong Kong and other parts of East Asia. To promote involvement and engagement in healthcare research, a Patient and Public Advisory Committee was created as part of the Digital Oncology Care Enhancement Lab in Hong Kong. This lab focuses on improving cancer care through the use of digital tools. Members of the committee include cancer patients, caregivers, social workers and researchers. The committee aims to bring together lived experience and professional knowledge to enhance the relevance and quality of healthcare research. A group of eleven committee members took part in two focus group discussions. The study found that many participants were initially unfamiliar with the concept of being involved and engaged in research. However, once they understood it more fully, they recognised its value. They believed that involvement and engagement could contribute to more patient-centred care and empower people to better manage their own health. Participants also raised concerns, including feeling intimidated in research settings, challenges related to time and privacy, and difficulty understanding technical language. This study offers practical suggestions for researchers, such as using plain language, providing training, building trust, and creating inclusive spaces for open dialogue. Importantly, involvement should deliver real value to patients and the public, not only to researchers.

## Introduction

## Reflection on conventional researcher-led healthcare studies
Healthcare research has traditionally been led by academic or clinical investigators who define objectives, select methods, conduct analyses, and control dissemination [ , ]. While this ensures procedural consistency and methodological rigor, concerns remain about whether such studies truly reflect the priorities and lived experiences of patients and the public [ , ]. Researchers contribute theoretical and methodological expertise, yet their interpretations may diverge from the experiential knowledge of those directly affected by health conditions [ ]. This disconnect can weaken the practical relevance of research findings, result in less suitable outcome measures, and lead to the inefficient use of resources [ ]. Although patients are often involved through ethical procedures such as informed consent, data provision, or consultations, these forms of engagement are often limited in depth and continuity [ , ]. Patients are often positioned as informants rather than collaborators, with minimal influence over research questions, study design, analysis, or interpretation [ ]. As a result, their contributions may have only a modest impact. A reflection is needed on how researcher-led studies can be complemented by approaches that integrate patients and the public more meaningfully throughout research processes.

## Patient and public involvement and engagement (PPIE) in healthcare research
With growing recognition of the disconnect between research findings and the real-world needs of affected populations, more attention has been directed toward PPIE in healthcare research [ ]. PPIE refers to an active and sustained collaboration or partnership between researchers and individuals with lived experience throughout the research process [ , ]. PPIE consists of two components: “involvement” and “engagement”. “Involvement” refers to active collaboration in shaping research such as identifying priorities, co-designing methods and interpreting findings, shifting the model from research conducted on or for the public to research conducted with or by them [ ]. While “Involvement” effectively emphasizes active collaboration between researchers and the public during the research process, it does not fully capture the outward-facing, interactive elements of knowledge translation. To address this, the NIHR and the broader academic community have increasingly transitioned from PPI to PPIE [ , ]. The addition of the “Engagement” (E) element introduces a vital layer of two-way interaction. It refers to two-way interactions between researchers and patients or the public through activities such as sharing research findings, raising awareness, and facilitating access to the benefits of research, often via events, campaigns, or media communication [ , ]. The term “public” broadly includes carers, people who use health and social care services, social workers, and individuals from organizations representing service users [ ].
Evidence indicates that PPIE enhances research relevance, quality, and ethical integrity; supports more efficient resource use; enables the early identification of implementation challenges; and fosters researchers’ reflexivity [ – ]. Moreover, PPIE contributes to public health literacy and empowers individuals and communities to engage more actively in health-related matters [ , ]. Importantly, realising these benefits depends not only on researchers’ commitment to PPIE but also on the experiences, motivations, and readiness of the patients and public contributors themselves. Previous studies conducted indicate that patients and members of the public are generally willing to engage in research when they perceive their contributions as valued and consequential [ , , ]. Reported motivations include a desire to improve services for others, to make sense of personal illness experiences, and to gain a sense of purpose and agency [ ]. Participants have also described personal benefits from involvement, including increased confidence, expanded social networks, and an enhanced understanding of health and research processes [ , , ]. The literature has also recognised that meaningful PPIE requires considerable preparatory investment from both researchers and participants [ , , ]. From the perspective of patients and public representatives, the expectation that they contribute to activities such as research design, data analysis, or governance may be unfamiliar and, without adequate support, potentially disempowering rather than empowering [ , ]. Effective involvement therefore depends on clear communication about roles and expectations, accessible explanations of research processes, and structured capacity-building opportunities such as orientation sessions, mentoring, or co-learning workshops [ , ]. Without such conditions, PPIE risks remaining tokenistic, present in name but lacking the substance necessary for participants to exert genuine influence [ ].

## Global rise of PPIE in healthcare research and emerging developments in Hong Kong
Over the past decade, the application of PPIE in healthcare research has expanded, particularly in Western countries [ ]. In the United Kingdom, institutions such as the National Institute for Health and Care Research and the Medical Research Council have made PPIE an essential component of grant applications [ ]. In the United States, the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality and the Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute have actively promoted stakeholder engagement across the research cycle [ ]. PPIE has been applied across diverse domains, such as chronic diseases, ageing, mental health, and digital health innovation [ – ]. In Asia, adoption is increasing; countries such as Singapore, Malaysia, and Saudi Arabia have incorporated PPIE into research supported by policy and funding developments [ , , ]. Hong Kong is part of this emerging landscape [ ]. Its multicultural population and established healthcare system provide a foundation for developing inclusive and culturally appropriate approaches to PPIE. Recent health policy and service initiatives have emphasized the importance of patient experience and public involvement [ , ]. The Hong Kong Hospital Authority’s Patient Resource Centres reflect efforts to engage patients and caregivers in service development and research [ ]. While the term PPIE is not yet consistently used, related practices such as co-design, participatory research, and user-centred design are gaining increasing attention in Hong Kong [ – ]. Though PPIE research is emerging in Hong Kong, there remains a lack of empirical evidence on how patients and the public perceive PPIE, as well as the potential facilitators and barriers to the PPIE practice.
To support sustained PPIE in healthcare research in Hong Kong, a Patient and Public Advisory Committee (PAC) was established under the Jockey Club Science, Technology, Engineering and Mathematics Lab of Digital Oncology Care Enhancement (‘DOCE lab’) at the authors’ institution. The DOCE lab focuses on the integration of digital technologies to support personalised cancer care. The PAC brings together researchers, cancer survivors, caregivers, and representatives of community organisations. The objective of this PAC is to provide valuable insights to the research projects conducted in the DOCE Lab, which is a typical example of PPIE in practice.

## Study aim and research questions
Following the establishment of the PAC under the DOCE Lab in Hong Kong, this study aims to explore the experiences and understanding of PPIE from the perspectives of patients and public representatives within the PAC, including their views on its perceived benefits, as well as the barriers and facilitators to PPIE implementation in healthcare research. The study has the potential to inform the development of contextually appropriate PPIE frameworks and strategies that reflect local conditions especially in the cancer care field, based on the perspectives of the active members of the PAC of the DOCE lab. Identifying local facilitators and challenges can support more ethical, sustainable, and responsive research practices within the DOCE Lab, while providing a reference point for other regions in Asia seeking to implement PPIE in healthcare research. The following research questions are proposed:

What are the experiences and understandings of PPIE among patients and the public within the PAC?
What values or potential benefits of PPIE are perceived by patients and the public within the PAC?
What perceived barriers and facilitators influence the implementation of PPIE in healthcare research within the DOCE Lab?

## Materials & methods
A descriptive exploratory qualitative study was conducted in March 2025 using focus group interviews to explore the breadth of participants’ diverse perspectives and experiences. Given the limited existing research on this topic in Hong Kong, this approach was chosen to establish a broad, foundational understanding of local PPIE practices and perspectives. The study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [ ].

## Participant recruitment
We established a Patient and Public Advisory Committee (PAC) under our DOCE Lab, with the objective of providing patient-centred insights into the lab’s oncology research. Rather than issuing an open public call, we employed purposive sampling to recruit individuals whose lived or professional experiences with cancer made their perspectives directly relevant to our research context. We initially approached 12 cancer patient organisations in Hong Kong via email, phone, and social media. Five organisations accepted the invitation, while seven declined due to time constraints, lack of interest, or non-response. Each participating organisation was asked to nominate two to three representatives to attend the first PAC meeting. These individuals were nominated by their respective community organisations to represent the patient and public voice.
Eleven participants from five organisations participated in the focus group interviews. Participants were included if they were (1) aged ≥ 18, (2) able to speak Cantonese and have the cognitive capacity to communicate and share ideas. Prior to the interviews, participants completed a modified version of the My PPIE Profile developed by Shaping Our Lives to document their background, including gender, age, education, employment status, and experience related to cancer, research, and health work [ ]. Prior to the interviews, participants completed an adapted (modified) version of the My PPIE Profile developed by Shaping Our Lives to document their background [ ]. The adaptation was made to suit the local context and the objectives of this study: we retained core demographic items, removed non-essential items not relevant to our study procedures, and added items capturing participants’ experiences related to cancer and prior involvement in research, health-related work, and PPIE. Minor wording and formatting adjustments were also made to enhance clarity for Cantonese-speaking participants. The adapted form was used descriptively to characterise the sample and was not used for scoring or outcome measurement.

## Data collection
Based on existing evidence that two focus groups with 5–6 participants each are often sufficient to capture the majority of relevant thematic content, including the most common themes [ – ], the research team planned to conduct two in-person focus group interviews. Each group included a mix of participants from different backgrounds such as patients and various public representatives rather than being grouped by role, to encourage diverse perspectives and interaction across roles. The interviews were conducted concurrently in March 2025. Each focus group lasted 45 to 60 min. To accommodate the concurrent sessions, the groups were facilitated by two distinct pairs of researchers. Group 1 was led by WZL, an experienced PPIE researcher and co-facilitated by TYZ, a trained, Cantonese-speaking research assistant. Group 2 was led by MQL, a healthcare researcher with expertise in qualitative methods, and co-facilitated by SS, a similarly trained bilingual research assistant. To minimise variability and ensure consistency across the concurrent sessions, all four facilitators underwent a standardised briefing prior to data collection. Both pairs utilised an identical semi-structured topic guide and adhered to agreed-upon moderation protocols for a uniform approach to probing and participant engagement. A structured interview guide (see Appendix ), based on the Context and Implementation of Complex Interventions framework [ ], was developed by the research team. It consisted of four areas: (1) understanding and prior experience with research and PPIE; (2) facilitators of PPIE implementation; (3) barriers to PPIE implementation; and (4) expectations and suggestions for building long-term partnerships between researchers and patients/the public.
The focus group interviews were conducted in person immediately following the first PAC meeting, at the same venue and a nearby room, in order to reduce additional travel and scheduling burden for participants. Facilitators conducted the focus group interviews using a semi-structured topic guide. At the start of each session, the moderator explained the study’s purpose, obtained verbal confirmation to proceed with recording, and established ground rules (e.g., respectful interaction, one person speaking at a time, and encouragement of diverse views). The moderators used open-ended questions and follow-up probes to elicit examples and clarify meaning, and adjusted question wording and sequencing in response to participant input to capture emergent topics while maintaining coverage of the key domains. Given the mixed composition of the groups (patients, a caregiver, and social workers), the discussions were intentionally interactive. The research team was acutely aware of the inherent power dynamics between healthcare/social professionals and patients. To prevent the professional perspectives of the social workers from overshadowing the lived experiences of the patients, the moderators actively facilitated turn-taking. Specific strategies included directed prompting to specifically centre patient voices (e.g., “We’ve heard the organisational side, but I would love to hear how this feels from a patient’s perspective”) and gently redirecting the discussion when professionalised language dominated. We aimed to create a safe space where patients felt empowered to share their personal views alongside professionals. To minimise social desirability bias [ ], none of the facilitators had prior relationships with the participants. All sessions were audio-recorded for transcription and subsequent analysis. Field notes were also taken during and after each session to capture nonverbal cues, group dynamics, and contextual observations that supplemented the audio data. To promote balanced participation, the moderator employed facilitation strategies such as directed prompting and open invitations so that all participants had the opportunity to contribute meaningfully to the discussion.

## Data analysis
Reflexive thematic analysis was carried out using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework (2006) [ ]. Focus group discussions were audio-recorded and transcribed in Cantonese by two authors (WZL and MQL). Transcripts were then cross-checked for accuracy by two other authors (YL and MJL) and verified by two participants. Minor discrepancies identified during cross-checking, primarily involving the transcription of colloquial expressions and overlapping speech, were resolved through discussion and re-examination of the original audio recordings. Participant verification confirmed the accuracy of the transcripts, with only minor contextual clarifications offered. Each transcript was independently coded using a line-by-line approach by an author (WZL for Group 1 and MQL for Group 2) to identify key concepts and recurring patterns. The coders engaged in iterative discussions to reconcile differences and develop a shared codebook. While the initial phases of coding were strictly inductive and grounded in the participants’ semantic expressions, the subsequent development and interpretation of themes utilised an abductive approach. Specifically, during the later interpretive phases, we applied theoretical lenses including Confucian relational ethics and postcolonial structuralism to contextualise participants’ latent perceptions of power dynamics, authority gradients, and language hierarchies.
To address potential variability introduced by using two different facilitator pairs, the analytical process also included a comparative step. During the initial familiarisation and coding phases, the research team compared the transcripts from both concurrent groups to check for facilitator-driven differences in participant responsiveness, data depth, or topic focus. No substantive differences in data quality or content were found between the two groups. Themes and subthemes were collaboratively developed through regular meetings involving all authors to ensure consistency and internal coherence.
Since the study focused on capturing a broad range of perspectives from patients and public representatives, rather than conducting a subgroup-specific analysis, we evaluated the richness of the dataset based on the concepts of information power and data adequacy, rather than claiming absolute thematic saturation. Given the sample size of 11 participants encompassing diverse stakeholder roles and cancer types, claiming true meaning saturation would be an overstatement. However, after carefully reviewing, coding, and comparing all focus group transcripts, the research team determined that the data provided sufficient conceptual depth to address the study’s specific exploratory aims regarding initial PPIE perceptions. We observed that the core categories regarding foundational barriers and facilitators became highly recurrent by the ninth participant, with subsequent data elaborating on existing thematic content rather than introducing entirely new dimensions. Therefore, the recurrence and deepening of key ideas across both focus groups indicated that the data were adequately rich and robust to support the study’s findings [ , ]. Illustrative quotations were selected to reflect the participants’ perspectives.
All interviews were conducted and transcribed in Cantonese. To maintain translation transparency and conceptual equivalence, selected quotes were forward-translated into English by two bilingual research assistants (TYZ and SS). The translations were then independently back-checked by a bilingual author (YL). Discrepancies, particularly concerning abstract Western constructs like “involvement” and “engagement” which lack direct, universally understood colloquial equivalents in Cantonese, were resolved through team consensus to ensure that the English quotes accurately reflected the participants’ original sociocultural intent.

## Rigor and reflexivity
In alignment with the epistemological foundations of reflexive thematic analysis, we approached researcher subjectivity as an analytical resource rather than a bias to be eliminated. For a rigorous and transparent interpretive process, we engaged in continuous reflexive practices. This included the use of field notes and reflective journals to document analytical reasoning, monitor subjectivity, and maintain transparency throughout the research process. Two authors (WZL and MQL) independently coded the interview transcripts and collaboratively reviewed 80% of the data through line-by-line comparison to explore divergent interpretations, challenge each other’s assumptions, and develop a deeper understanding of the data [ , ]. The other authors reviewed these materials and held regular peer-debriefing sessions to examine how the coders’ backgrounds might influence interpretation, while an audit trail of coding decisions was maintained.
We actively reflected on our positionality as researchers embedded within the newly established DOCE Lab, which also served as the research site. We recognised that our dual roles as lab investigators and focus group facilitators introduced inherent power dynamics and potential social desirability bias, as participants might feel compelled to view the lab’s new PPIE initiatives favourably. To mitigate this, facilitators explicitly emphasised at the start of each session that critical and dissenting views were highly valued and essential to the research objectives. Furthermore, the analytical process was enriched by the inclusion of a co-author (JW), who contributed directly to the methodology and data interpretation from a patient perspective based on their lived experience, helping to challenge researcher-centric assumptions.
For the fidelity of the data foundation, transcript verification was conducted by sharing the verbatim transcripts with two participants, who confirmed their semantic accuracy. In alignment with reflexive thematic analysis, which views theme generation as an interpretive, researcher-led process, we did not conduct traditional member checking of the final theoretical themes. Instead, credibility was established through the aforementioned peer-debriefing sessions, audit trails, and the use of thick description. Thick description was used to enhance contextual understanding and to highlight how meaning was co-constructed within the focus group setting. Non-verbal behaviours such as nodding, hesitation, and changes in tone were documented, as they revealed latent dynamics not evident in the verbal data alone and added depth to the interpretation of themes related to trust, uncertainty, and agreement.

## Ethical consideration
This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the authors’ institution (Ref No: HSEARS20240829001-01) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The purpose of the study was clearly explained to the interviewees, and written informed consent was obtained in person prior to conducting the focus group interviews. Participants were free to withdraw at any time without penalty. To acknowledge participants’ time and effort, a monetary incentive (HK$200) was provided. Data confidentiality was upheld by restricting access exclusively to authorised personnel.

## Results

## Participants’ characteristics
A total of eleven individuals participated in the focus groups and completed the PPIE profile. Please refer to Table  for the study participants’ characteristics. Participants included 7 cancer patients (lung: 2; breast: 2; colorectal: 1; blood: 1; stomach: 1), 3 social workers (with 22, 10, and 5 years of experience, respectively), and one caregiver for a breast cancer patient. Six participants were aged 50–65, and five were between 30 and 45. Employment status included six employed, three retired, and two housewives. Four participants held a master’s degree, one held a bachelor’s degree, one had completed upper secondary education, two had completed lower secondary education, one had completed primary education, and one reported no formal education. Three reported prior experience involving research, and six had worked in the health sector.

 

## Themes and subthemes
Using reflexive thematic analysis, four primary themes and 15 subthemes were developed (see Appendix ). Themes were derived from recurring patterns across the full dataset, and the quoted extracts were selected for illustrative clarity. The analysis also distinguishes between the lived-experience perspectives of patients and the caregiver and the distinct professional and organisational perspectives contributed by the social workers.

## Theme 1: Experience and understanding of PPIE
Participants reported limited exposure to and familiarity with PPIE, resulting in misunderstandings of its definition and purpose.

Subtheme 1.1: Lack of PPIE experience

Most participants had no prior experience with healthcare research, particularly in relation to PPIE. When involvement did occur, it was generally limited to passive activities such as completing surveys or participating in basic consultations.

I’ve never had any experience being part of healthcare research before; this is all new to me.” [FG1, P3]
I’ve only ever answered a few surveys; I’ve never been asked to give input or be involved beyond that. [FG2, P4]

Subtheme 1.2: Limited understanding of PPIE

Many participants misunderstood the term “PPIE,” associating it with digital tools or service delivery. Conceptual clarity was limited among participants, particularly those with no prior research experience.

Does PPIE refer to designing some kind of electronic tool? Or a tool to help cancer patients? [FG1, P1]
Actually, I didn’t know what PPIE was. Since I didn’t understand it, I hesitated to join the study. I wasn’t sure what would be expected of me. [FG1, C1]

## Theme 2: Perceived benefits of PPIE
Despite limited familiarity, most participants expressed broad support for PPIE and identified multiple perceived benefits.

Subtheme 2.1: Promotion of patient-centred care

PPIE was seen as a practical extension of patient-centred care, allowing researchers to reflect patients’ lived experiences and address real-world needs more effectively.

We are in favor (of PPIE). Public involvement is definitely a good thing. We always talk about ‘patient-centered care’ and ‘people-centered healthcare’; this is aligned. [FG2, P7]

Subtheme 2.2: Patient and public empowerment in health management

Participants emphasised the value of experiential knowledge, positioning patients not as passive recipients but as reliable contributors to research and decision-making.

Patients are the ones most directly affected and who ultimately benefit; they are the ones who know the needs best. [FG2, P6]

Subtheme 2.3: Enhancement and facilitation of research rigor

Incorporating patient perspectives was perceived as essential to grounding research in clinical and social realities.

If you don’t ask patients, you’re just making assumptions. Each condition and patient is different-they live in different environments and face different challenges. Getting their input helps make the research more accurate and relevant. [FG2, P4]

Subtheme 2.4: Improvement of institutional services and reputation.
Representing a distinct organisational perspective, the social workers in this study emphasised that their institutions were more likely to support and prioritise PPIE when it was seen as contributing directly to organizational development or influencing broader policy agendas. Unlike patients, who focused on personal empowerment and clinical outcomes, the social workers framed PPIE as a strategic tool that could bring value to service delivery and institutional positioning.

If PPIE can bring positive outcomes like improving our service delivery, enhancing how the public sees our organization, or even influencing government policy, then our institution would be more committed to supporting it. When we see that it aligns with our goals and adds real value, it becomes easier to justify the time and resources needed for involvement. [FG2, SW2]

Subtheme 2.5: Enhancement of government support for public health

Participants, particularly cancer patients and social workers, perceived PPIE as being aligned with emerging participatory models of public health governance. They acknowledged its gradual recognition in local public health discourse.

It’s a new direction, reflecting co-creation approaches in public health and service design. I think if our government sees the value in this, it could lead to stronger support for more participatory public health initiatives. [FG2, P2]

I think it’s a relatively new concept, but it seems Hong Kong is starting to accept this in public health fields. [FG2, SW2]

## Theme 3: Perceived barriers to PPIE
Participants in this study including cancer patients, social workers, and a caregiver identified multiple constraints that limited the feasibility and quality of PPIE in healthcare research. The nature of these barriers differed between the personal challenges faced by patients and the systemic challenges faced by social workers.

Subtheme 3.1: Personal factors

Time Constraints
Ongoing medical appointments and fatigue, especially among those with chronic illnesses, made participation difficult:

They have many different follow-up appointments, blood tests, and investigations. It might be difficult to fit research in. Also, some may not have the energy to participate in providing so much data input. [FG1, P2]

Privacy Concern
Patients expressed concern over visibility and data disclosure.

There is a chance I would be selective about which data I’m willing to share and which I could open to all. [FG1, P2]

Psychological Burden
Recounting illness experiences triggered emotional discomfort, particularly for those with prior negative encounters in research.

It brings up the negative emotions, because I might have experienced setbacks or gaps where things didn’t turn out as I wanted. [FG1, P3]

Some patients described experiencing psychological burden in past research when they felt dehumanised or objectified. Being treated more like data sources than collaborators made them feel unimportant and emotionally disconnected from the research, which discouraged further participation.

It’s easy to feel like research turns you into a guinea pig, an experimental subject. You might not even enjoy the process. I felt like my only role was to recount my experience. I just felt like a test subject. [FG1, P2]

Subtheme 3.2: Limited research literacy

Some participants, particularly cancer patients and the caregiver, faced structural barriers such as limited educational opportunities or language-related apprehension, which made engagement with research feel inaccessible or intimidating.
Knowledge Gaps

To be honest, my knowledge and educational level is low. [FG1, C1]

Language Challenges
Older participants expressed apprehension about interacting in academic or English-dominant settings.

When older people hear the invitation, they may think whether they need to come to the university to meet professors? They get scared about the language: Will I understand? Do I need to speak English? [FG2, P6]

Subtheme 3.3: Restricted access to research participants and resources

This subtheme revealed a clear divergence between organisational and personal barriers. Social workers from smaller or independent patient-led organisations drove the discussion regarding systemic limitations, expressing that limited resources such as staffing, funding, and institutional backing made it difficult to access and engage research participants in a meaningful way. Even when there was interest in contributing to research, the lack of capacity and support often prevented them from initiating or sustaining involvement.

We’re just a small team, mostly volunteers. Without enough time, funding, or institutional support, it’s really difficult for us to take part in research or help connect with other potential participants…… We don’t have the networks or resources that bigger organisations have……it’s hard to reach people effectively on our own. [FG2, SW2]

In contrast, when discussing access barriers, some patients noted that personal feelings of isolation and social withdrawal further hindered their ability to engage in participation.

As a patient, just being able to come out and still hold a job already takes a lot of courage. I often feel that many patients in Hong Kong have withdrawn into themselves. [FG1, P1]

Subtheme 3.4: Researcher-dominant environment

Both patients and social workers in this study described the research environment as being influenced by hierarchical, top-down practices that limited the authenticity and depth of PPIE. They perceived that research processes were often predetermined by academic or institutional actors, with fewer opportunities for patients or community members to meaningfully contribute beyond tokenistic tasks.

Honestly, I personally feel this comes down to a cultural issue in Hong Kong, we simply don’t have this culture. It’s like everything is already pre-designed, and they researchers just give you a Google Form to mechanically fill out. Even when they talk about ‘PPIE,’ as an actual patient, you realise there is fundamentally no real involvement at all. [FG2, SW3]

## Primary theme 4: Perceived facilitators of PPIE
Participants identified several conditions that could support more inclusive and sustained involvement and engagement.
Subtheme 4.1: Trust, transparency, and effective communication with researchers
Ongoing communication and transparency regarding the use of participant input were seen as essential.

We want to know what happens after we share our insights; how it’s implemented or whether the direction is good and should be pursued further. [FG2, P4]

Subtheme 4.2: Shared gains and long-term collaboration between researchers and patients/the public

While patients and social workers highlighted the importance of mutual benefit, their specific goals differed depending on their roles. Patients emphasised altruism and peer support, focusing on how their lived experiences could yield shared gains for other patients:

We volunteer to use our lived experience as peers to help newly diagnosed patients……we help your research and ultimately help those truly in need. [FG2, P7]

Conversely, social workers approached shared gains from a structural standpoint, explicitly driving the focus towards long-term collaboration as an organisational goal:

As an organization, we value sustainability and social impact. So, exploring opportunities for longer-term collaboration would be beneficial. [FG1, SW1]

Subtheme 4.3: Self-efficacy in health management

PPIE utilisation was linked to greater confidence in navigating healthcare systems, enhanced understanding of health-related knowledge, and more active involvement in managing one’s health.

Whether the data we provided and the work we contributed can be practically implemented in real-life contexts (e.g. personal health management or health knowledge improvement or within the existing healthcare system) is key. If this can be achieved, it gives those involved a sense of accomplishment and encourages others around them to participate. [FG1, P2]

Subtheme 4.4: Training, incentives, and flexibility for patients and the public
Participants in this study especially cancer patients advocated for training to improve research literacy, flexible engagement formats (e.g., hybrid participation), and recognition of their contribution through incentives.

If training is offered, I’d want to understand the research workflow-how research is conducted or how it’s designed. [FG1, P2]
Of course hybrid (participation) would be ideal. [FG1, P1]
Incentives help. Coupons, for instance, are very practical. [FG1, P1]

## Discussion
This qualitative study explores the initial perceptions of PPIE among patients and public representatives within a newly formed oncology advisory committee in the Hong Kong context. Because participants were interviewed immediately following their first formal orientation to PPIE, the findings primarily capture anticipatory views, early-stage conceptualisations, and first impressions of PPIE among largely PPIE-naive individuals. The timing provides a unique lens into the immediate conceptual and structural barriers that must be overcome when introducing PPIE to new populations. The findings of this study also inform crucial aspects of facilitating PPIE in healthcare research processes and highlight a broader function of PPIE: its role in bridging research-derived knowledge and clinical practice, particularly in oncology and supportive cancer care where the gap between what is studied and what patients experience can be considerable.

## Limited exposure, conceptual ambiguity, and diverse perceived benefits of PPIE
As reflected in Theme 1, the majority of participants reported no prior involvement in healthcare research (Subtheme 1.1), and where involvement had occurred, it was confined to passive activities such as completing surveys or attending basic consultations, rather than contributing to research design or decision-making. Conceptual confusion was also evident (Subtheme 1.2); for instance, one participant associated PPIE with the development of electronic tools, while another reported hesitating to join the study because the term itself was unintelligible to them. There was little evidence of sustained partnerships with shared contributions or empowered decision-making in healthcare research. The findings may reflect a lack of a PPIE culture and a regional emphasis on collaborative roles in research, where patients and public representatives are seen not only as research subjects but as active contributors to research design, governance, and dissemination [ , ].
The reasons may lie in the absence of formal PPIE foundations, such as guidelines, funding mandates, or institutional policies in Hong Kong to promote or operationalise PPIE. Without such structural support, researchers may regard PPIE as optional or symbolic rather than integral to research quality and relevance [ , ]. The conceptual confusion observed among our participants underscores that the lack of culturally and linguistically accessible PPIE frameworks makes it difficult for patients and public representatives to understand what “involvement” or “engagement” means, how it differs from conventional participation, and what forms of contribution are expected or valued [ , ]. The unclear articulation of PPIE principles and concepts may also reinforce traditional power hierarchies, in which researchers maintain control over knowledge and decision-making processes [ , ]. Viewed through the lens of Confucian relational ethics, this dynamic is not simply a matter of inadequate information provision; it reflects a broader cultural orientation in which patients are socialised to trust and defer to professional authority rather than to question or co-direct the research process. When PPIE concepts remain undefined or inaccessible, these pre-existing hierarchical tendencies go unchallenged, making it more likely that involvement, where it occurs, remains tokenistic rather than substantive.
Despite their limited experience and understanding, participants articulated a strong belief in the value of incorporating PPIE into healthcare research. As developed in Theme 2, these perceived benefits spanned multiple dimensions. At the individual level, patients highlighted that their lived experiences of treatment, survivorship, and psychological distress constituted a form of experiential knowledge that could ground research in clinical reality (Subtheme 2.2). One participant stated explicitly that patients “know the needs best,” positioning themselves not as passive recipients but as authoritative contributors. Participants further linked PPIE to patient-centred care (Subtheme 2.1), with one participant noting the alignment between PPIE and the principles of “people-centred healthcare.” PPIE was also perceived as enhancing research rigour (Subtheme 2.3); participants argued that without patient input, researchers risk making assumptions that overlook the diversity of patients’ conditions and circumstances. These perceived benefits align with existing literature that indicates the value of PPIE in enhancing the quality, relevance, and impact of healthcare research [ , , ].
At the institutional level, public representatives, particularly social workers, framed PPIE as a strategic mechanism for organisational development (Subtheme 2.4). They believed institutional commitment to PPIE was more likely when it was seen as contributing to service improvement, public image, or policy influence. It is critical to note that themes regarding institutional reputation, restricted organisational access to participants, and long-term collaboration as an organisational goal were driven almost exclusively by the social workers. Their distinct professional lens highlights the systemic and resourcing concerns of community organisations, which differ from the personal, lived-experience concerns raised by patients. Participants also identified PPIE as aligned with emerging participatory models of public health governance in Hong Kong (Subtheme 2.5), suggesting a growing recognition of its relevance in the local policy landscape. These perspectives extend the focus beyond the individual experiences and research outcomes typically emphasised in the PPIE literature [ , , ]. Future efforts are needed to clarify the definition, scope, principles, and practices of PPIE for both researchers and public contributors.

## Notable barriers to PPIE in healthcare research
Theme 3 captured the multi-layered barriers perceived by participants, spanning personal, structural, and environmental dimensions. A notable barrier to the implementation of PPIE in research is the limited time and availability of patients and public representatives for sustained involvement, an issue also reported in studies conducted in Western contexts [ , , , ]. In our data, participants described how ongoing medical commitments such as follow-up appointments, blood tests, and investigations left limited time and energy for additional research activities (Subtheme 3.1). In Hong Kong, demanding work and family responsibilities often constrain individuals’ capacity to participate in unpaid activities, especially when the purpose of involvement is unclear or when they do not see how their contributions will lead to changes in services, policies, or outcomes that matter to them [ , ]. Sharing personal experiences, especially those related to illness or vulnerability, was also described as emotionally challenging. Participants in our study reported that recounting negative experiences triggered painful emotions associated with setbacks and unmet expectations (Subtheme 3.1). This may be influenced by cultural norms that value emotional restraint and privacy in Asia, including Hong Kong [ ], making open sharing uncomfortable, especially in focus group settings.
Another notable barrier for patients and public representatives is their unfamiliarity with research processes and the inaccessibility of academic or technical language. In our findings (Subtheme 3.2), a participant with a lower educational background explicitly acknowledged her limited research literacy, while older participants expressed apprehension about interacting in academic or English-dominant settings, with one participant vividly describing the intimidation of being expected to “come to the university to meet professors.” In Hong Kong, the dominance of English in research communication may create both linguistic and epistemic barriers for participants, particularly those with lower educational backgrounds or limited English proficiency [ ]. Even when translation is provided, discomfort persists due to unfamiliarity with research conventions and terminology, which can undermine confidence and limit contributions [ , ]. This reflects a broader issue of language hierarchies in global health and research systems, where English often becomes a gatekeeper to participation [ ]. From a postcolonial perspective, this barrier is not merely practical but structural: Hong Kong’s colonial history established English as the language of institutional authority, and this legacy persists in its academic and healthcare systems. Participants who lack English proficiency are therefore excluded not only linguistically but epistemically. Their knowledge and perspectives are rendered less visible within a system that privileges English-medium scholarship. Educational attainment and English fluency constitute forms of cultural capital that determine not only who gains access to research spaces but who feels entitled to speak within them. The participant’s description of intimidation at entering a university setting reflects an internalised sense of exclusion rooted in these structural inequalities, rather than a simple lack of confidence.
Furthermore, our findings highlighted that resource constraints at the organisational level constituted a structural barrier (Subtheme 3.3). Social workers from smaller, patient-led organisations described how limited staffing, funding, and institutional backing prevented them from initiating or sustaining meaningful involvement in research, even when interest existed. One social worker characterised their organisation as “just a small team, mostly volunteers,” lacking the networks and resources available to larger institutions. Patients also reported that social withdrawal and isolation further hindered their capacity to engage. Across much of Asia, including Hong Kong, research governance remains largely centralised and expert-led, meaning that equitable participation is often conditional and constrained [ , ]. It is also important to acknowledge that implementing PPIE places additional demands on researchers themselves. Meaningful involvement requires dedicated time for relationship-building, accessible communication, and iterative consultation, all of which extend project timelines and workloads. In contexts like Hong Kong where PPIE infrastructure and funding mechanisms are not yet established, these costs fall largely on individual researchers, which may discourage adoption, particularly among early-career academics facing competing institutional pressures [ , ]. Recognising these demands is essential to avoid framing PPIE solely as a moral obligation without addressing the practical conditions needed to sustain it.
Contextually responsive PPIE models are needed to address structural and institutional barriers. There is much to learn from established models in countries such as the United Kingdom, which has institutionalised PPIE through mechanisms like public involvement standards, the inclusion of lay co-applicants on grants, dedicated funding opportunities, training programmes for both researchers and patients/public representatives, and formal evaluation frameworks to assess impact [ , , ]. Such models have helped embed PPIE as a normative practice and establish accountability across the research process. Several well-established frameworks have been shown to support meaningful PPIE and could be adapted to fit the local sociocultural context. For example, the UK’s NIHR INVOLVE framework and the UK Standards for Public Involvement [ ] provide practical approaches such as role clarity, accessible communication, capacity building, and inclusive representation to overcome common barriers to meaningful involvement. To support PPIE implementation, multiple workshops, training sessions, and co-learning events have been held across the UK [ ] to facilitate skill development and foster collaborative research cultures. Adapting these models and practices to align with Hong Kong’s sociocultural and institutional context may support the development of a research environment that genuinely values and integrates patient and public views and contributions. This is especially pertinent in oncology and supportive cancer care, where translating research into practice demands sensitivity to the lived realities of patients navigating treatment and survivorship. Our findings show that participants brought experiential knowledge that exposed assumptions in researcher-driven designs about what patients need, how they prefer to receive support, and when. Without such input, interventions may risk being theoretically sound but practically misaligned with cancer survivors’ actual circumstances [ , , ].

## Notable facilitators of meaningful PPIE in healthcare research
Theme 4 identified several conditions that participants considered essential for supporting more inclusive and sustained PPIE. From the perspectives of patients and public representatives, clear communication, mutual respect, and transparency emerged as foundational to establishing trust and fostering authentic, equitable partnerships with researchers. In our data (Subtheme 4.1), participants expressed a desire not only to contribute their insights but also to understand how their input would be used, with one participant stating, “We want to know what happens after we share our insights.” These practices supported patients and public representatives in feeling genuinely valued, informed, and confident in their roles, which could enable more active and meaningful involvement throughout the research process [ , , ]. Transparent communication helps set clear expectations, define roles, and reduce power imbalances, which is especially important in Hong Kong where medical and research hierarchies are entrenched [ – ]. When participants understand how their input will be used in research decisions, they are more likely to feel a sense of legitimacy and ownership, which can strengthen their engagement in PPIE [ ].
Another notable facilitator is the alignment between participants’ motivations and the perceived value of their involvement. Our findings (Subtheme 4.2) revealed that both patients and social workers emphasised the importance of mutual benefit and sustained collaboration. Patients described their involvement as an extension of their peer support roles, volunteering their lived experience to help newly diagnosed patients while simultaneously contributing to research. Social workers framed long-term research partnerships as aligned with organisational goals of sustainability and social impact. In Hong Kong, where civic engagement in research remains limited, people are more willing to take part when they see that their contributions lead to tangible outcomes such as better patient care, more responsive services, or more inclusive policies [ , ]. Seeing real-world impact makes participation feel more relevant and reinforces the idea that lived experience and experiential knowledge matter [ , ]. For many patients, involvement also offers a sense of personal empowerment. It can help them make sense of their illness experiences and build confidence in navigating healthcare issues. Similar findings have been reported in other Asian contexts, where meaning-making and personal growth are often described as key reasons for getting involved [ , , ].
Likewise, one key facilitator is the use of clear, plain language and bilingual materials, which helps bridge differences in education levels and language backgrounds. In a setting like Hong Kong, where linguistic diversity is common, this kind of communication support is crucial. Beyond language, it is equally important to consider participants’ literacy levels, cultural expectations, and preferred ways of expressing themselves. When researchers use tools such as bilingual facilitation, plain-language summaries, and visual or verbal explanations, they make research more approachable and reduce the barriers that often exclude people from engaging fully [ , ]. These strategies can be understood as deliberate efforts to redistribute cultural capital within the research setting, making explicit what is typically assumed and lowering the epistemic threshold for contribution. In the Hong Kong context, where colonial language hierarchies continue to shape institutional norms, such measures are not optional accommodations but necessary structural interventions to counteract the exclusionary effects of English-dominant research cultures.
In addition, capacity-building activities such as participant training, peer mentoring, and orientation sessions are important. Our participants specifically advocated for training that would help them understand the research workflow, including how research is conducted and designed (Subtheme 4.4), suggesting that demystifying the research process is a precondition for confident participation. These initiatives can help participants understand research processes, articulate their perspectives with confidence, and feel equipped to contribute meaningfully [ , ]. From the perspective of Confucian relational ethics, such capacity-building efforts serve a dual function: they equip participants with the knowledge needed to engage, but they also signal a reconfiguration of the traditional hierarchical relationship between researcher and participant-one in which the researcher actively works to reduce the authority gradient rather than taking it for granted. Logistically feasible arrangements are also crucial, especially in Hong Kong’s high-pressure socio-economic context [ , ], where time constraints, caregiving duties, and demanding work schedules are common. The time, location, and format of engagement activities should therefore be flexible and participant-centred. Offering hybrid formats (e.g., online and in-person), rescheduling options, travel compensation, and childcare support are not simply conveniences, they are preconditions for active and sustainable involvement [ , ]. This translational value is already visible in our work. Through our PAC, patient input reshaped our digital intervention for supportive cancer care, for instance, contributors identified that clinical language and English-dominant interfaces would deter older Chinese-speaking survivors, prompting a shift to bilingual, plain-language content with culturally resonant design. These modifications emerged from lived experience rather than the literature alone, illustrating how PPIE transforms research knowledge into intervention features that reflect actual patient needs. Future research should examine how such approaches improve intervention uptake and outcomes in oncology settings.

## Study limitation
This qualitative study drew on a purposively selected, small sample of individuals with cancer-related experiences without interview input from healthcare professionals or researchers. Therefore, the findings may have limited transferability to individuals with other health conditions, cancer types, illness experiences or institutional settings in Hong Kong. The limited representation of caregivers is acknowledged, as only one participated in this study. Although this individual contributed meaningful views on the caregiving role in relation to involvement and engagement, future research may benefit from including more caregivers to better represent their perspectives. The use of focus groups may have affected how openly participants shared their views, as some individuals might have felt uncomfortable expressing disagreement or discussing personal topics in front of others. Focus groups are better suited to capturing the breadth of perspectives across participants rather than eliciting the in-depth views that individual interviews may afford. As such, certain personal experiences or complex viewpoints may not have been fully explored within the group setting. Future research should consider incorporating individual interviews alongside focus groups to enable deeper exploration of participants’ views and should involve a more diverse sample, including participants with a wider range of health conditions, as well as researchers, healthcare professionals, and members of the broader public, to inform the development of PPIE strategies relevant across different contexts. We also acknowledge that full member checking in future studies involving all participants reviewing thematic interpretations would have strengthened credibility further.

## Conclusion
This study reveals the foundational perceptions, anticipated benefits, and perceived barriers to PPIE among patients and public representatives at the inception of their involvement in an oncology research setting. The findings demonstrate that introducing PPIE into contexts with limited prior exposure requires more than simply extending invitations; it necessitates the dismantling of structural, linguistic, and cultural barriers. Rather than assuming that Western models of PPIE can be seamlessly imported, researchers need to proactively address conceptual ambiguity, linguistic hierarchies, and resource constraints. The study can inform healthcare researchers and practitioners in understanding how PPIE is interpreted upon initial exposure, as well as the key factors that support or hinder its initial implementation. It can also guide the development of more appropriate, sustainable, and contextually relevant strategies for involving and engaging patients and public representatives in healthcare research. For instance, healthcare researchers in Hong Kong or other Asian regions preparing to implement PPIE in their studies might begin by introducing plain-language training and educational materials to help build participants’ confidence prior to the start of research activities. Establishing non-hierarchical communication structures that emphasise trust and transparency can help create a safe environment in which participants feel comfortable expressing their views without fear of judgement or marginalisation. It is also important to frame engagement as a mutually beneficial process, with clear, tangible outcomes for participants such as improved health literacy or self-management skills rather than treating involvement solely as a means of data collection. Flexible engagement models should also be adopted, including varied meeting times, remote participation options, and appropriate incentives, to accommodate the diverse needs and constraints faced by patients or public representatives.
