High-resolution daily samples from a wastewater treatment plant (WWTP) which served one of the largest cities in Australia, Melbourne, were analysed for temporal trends in alcohol consumption under extended periods of COVID-19 restrictions in 2020. Melbourne experienced two major lockdowns in 2020, which divided the year of 2020 into five periods (pre-lockdown, first lockdown, between lockdown, second lockdown and post second-lockdown). In this study, daily sampling identified shifts in alcohol consumption during different periods of restrictions. Alcohol consumption in the first lockdown period, when bars closed and social and sports events ceased, was lower than pre-lockdown period. However, alcohol consumption was higher in the second lockdown period than the previous lockdown period. There were spikes in alcohol consumption at the start and end of each lockdown (except for post lockdown). For most of 2020, the usual weekday-weekend variations in alcohol consumption were less evident but there was a significant difference in alcohol consumption between weekdays and weekends after the second lockdown. This suggests that drinking patterns eventually returned to normal after the end of the second lockdown. This study demonstrates the usefulness of high-resolution wastewater sampling in evaluating the effects on alcohol consumption of social interventions in specific temporal locations.

## Introduction
During the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic many countries implemented lockdowns with social restrictions of varying severity to reduce virus transmission. These restrictions affected many activities related to alcohol consumption, such as the closure of clubs, bars, restaurants, ceasing sporting events and reducing the opportunities for social interaction. The overall impact of these lockdowns on alcohol consumption was difficult to predict. On the one hand, the shutdown of the bars and the cancellation of various social events at which alcohol is consumed would be expected to reduce consumption. On the other hand, if alcohol sales are permitted for off premises consumption, then any increase in mental stress caused by the lockdowns could increase consumption ( , ). A further complication is that the lockdowns could affect regular and irregular drinkers in different ways.
Melbourne is the second-most populous city in Australia and the capital of the state of Victoria. According to the population data from the National Wastewater Drug Monitoring Program from 2021 ( ), the population of Melbourne (5,061,000) accounted for almost 20 % of the population of Australia. In 2020, Melbourne experienced two lengthy lockdowns. The first was from March 20 until May 17 and was a nearly two-month lockdown, at the same time as the rest of Australia. The second, which began on July 7 and ended on October 28, was a nearly four-month lockdown that was unique to Melbourne in Australia, in 2020. In a city with a large population and two lengthy lockdowns, it is meaningful to investigate the specific impact of COVID-19 pandemic on people's consumption behaviour.
Surveys have assessed the impact of the COVID-19 related restrictions on alcohol sales ( ). According to data from the Australian Bureau of Statistics, a record $15.6 billion in sales was generated by the country's alcohol retailers in 2020, an increase of 26.7 % on sales in the year preceding the pandemic. By comparison, alcohol sales grew by $195 million between 2018 and 2019, equivalent to 6 % of the increase between 2019 and 2020. However, these sales increases were potentially offset by the significant decline in on-premise sales due to venue closures and other restrictions. In addition, few surveys indicated that the COVID-19 pandemic could reduce alcohol consumption in Australia ( ; ).
In the past decade, wastewater-based epidemiology (WBE) has become an effective approach for providing supplementary data on the temporal trend of alcohol ( ; ; ). WBE involves measuring trace concentrations of human metabolic excretion products in influent wastewater, in order to examine consumption behaviour at the population level. Due to the high-throughput feature of the WBE methodology, temporal consumption trends and variations during special events could be properly identified.
A small number of publications have assessed the impact of COVID-19 restrictions on community patterns of alcohol consumption. Alcohol consumption estimated by wastewater analysis in South Australia, for example, declined during COVID-19 restrictions ( ). The same results were observed in most Australian states and territories in wastewater research that extended the South Australia investigation ( ). A wastewater study in Austria also found that alcohol consumption dropped by about 20 % during the lockdown period ( ). By contrast COVID-19 restrictions had a limited impact on alcohol consumption in Spain and Portugal ( ) and during a daily sampling study in Belgium ( ).
However, in order to clearly describe the effects of COVID-19 lockdowns on alcohol intake in the general population and distinguish the specific effects of lockdown restrictions from the general effects of COVID-19 on social activities. Limitations from previous studies such as the small size of sample collection, short sampling time frame or limited target population needed to be avoided. The work presented here expands the sample size and provides higher resolution with daily wastewater samples for alcohol consumption in the whole year of 2020 in one of the largest Australian cities that experienced longer lockdowns than the rest of the country. The target catchment in this study considered to be unique and representative in light of the large population it contained, longer and more strict lockdowns it had been through in 2020.

## Materials and methods
2.1

## Chemicals and reagents
Gao's study showed that ethyl sulfate was much more stable than ethyl glucuronide in the rising main sewer, which indicated that ethyl sulfate was more suitable for using as biomarker ( ; ). Native ethyl sulfate (EtS) and deuterated ethyl sulfate-d5 (EtS-D5) standards were provided from Sigma Aldrich (Castle Hill, Australia). Liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LC–MS) grade methanol was provided from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Hydrochloric acid was provided from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Formic acid was provided from Fisher Chemical (Australia). Ultrapure water was obtained from a Milli-Q system (Merck Millipore, USA, 0.22 μm filter, 18.2 mΩ·cm−1).

2.2

## Sample collection
Melbourne was selected as a model city to evaluate the usefulness of high-resolution sampling of wastewater for identifying changes in alcohol consumption as it went through two lockdowns associated with the COVID-19 pandemic during 2020, where most Australian states and cities experienced only one national lockdown in March to April 2020.
Daily samples of influent wastewater (24-h composite) were collected using a flow-proportional auto-sampler at a wastewater treatment plant located in Melbourne, Australia from 27th Jan 2020 to 17th Jan 2021, Australia, which covered pre-lockdown (n = 53 days, 27th Jan–20th Mar), first lockdown (n = 57 days, 21st Mar–17th May), between lockdown (n = 49 days, 18th May–6th Jul), second lockdown (n = 114 days, 7th Jul–28th Oct) and post second-lockdown (n = 79 days, 29th Oct–17th Jan 2021), 356 samples in total. The total number of inhabitants in the catchment area was 2,307,569 ( ), about half of the population of Melbourne. After collection, 10 mL of wastewater samples were acidified to pH 2 by using 2 M HCl instantly and kept at −20 °C. Every 2.5–3 months the samples were shipped to the University of Queensland for storage and analysis.

2.3

## Sample pre-treatment and instrumental analysis
A validated direct injection method for the analysis of alcohol using LC-MS/MS was adapted and optimized in this study ( ). Briefly, wastewater samples were adjusted to pH = 2 upon collection, frozen and transported to the laboratory where they were stored frozen until preparation. To prepare they were defrosted, and then filtered using 0.2 μm cellulose syringe filters. The filtered sample (1 mL) was transferred into a 2 mL amber vial, and spiked with10 μL of 0.5 mg/L of internal standard (EtS-D5). After homogenization by vortex for 1 min, a liquid chromatography (Shimadzu Nexera HPLC) — tandem mass spectrometry (SCIEX, 5500-QTrap) (LC-MS/MS) was applied for the determination of ethyl sulphate concentrations. The injection volume was 10 μL. The chromatographic separation was achieved using an Atlantis column (3 μm T3 column, 2.1 × 150 mm) at 40 °C with flow rate at 0.5 mL/min. 0.1 % formic acid in Milli-Q water (A) and 0.1 % formic acid in methanol (B) were used as mobile phase. Mobile phase linear gradient was set to: 0 % B at the first 2 min and increased to 95 % B in 1 min and stayed for 2 min, then dropped to 0 % B and stayed for 3 min for equilibration. The mass spectrometer operated in multiple reaction monitoring mode, mass spectrometry parameters are shown in Table S1.

2.4

## QAQC
EtS was quantified using a 9-point calibration curve (0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100 μg/L) that was injected before and after each batch sequence. Every 10 samples, QA/QC samples were added to each batch sequence to monitor instrument performance. These included 10 μg/L-spiked wastewater samples, duplicate wastewater samples and blank samples of MilliQ water laced with EtS-D5. The calculated results of all calibration standards and QCs were within 10 % of the predicted values. LOD was calculated as 3 times the standard deviation of 7 injections of 0.1 μg/L standard, LOQ was calculated as 9 times of the standard deviation of 7 injections of 0.1 μg/L standard ( ).

2.5

## Data analysis
Per capita consumption of alcohol (VolAlcohol) was calculated from Eq. :(1)VolAlcoholmL/person/day=CEtS×F×fAlcoholP×ρAlcohol

In brief, CEtS represents the concentration of EtS (μg/L), F represents represent the total flow of the wastewater during the sampling period (ML/d), fAlcohol represents the correction factor of EtS after alcohol consumption which applied by the value of 4 × 103 ( ). P represents the population covered by the wastewater catchment. ρAlcohol represents the density of alcohol.
The main purpose of this study was to evaluate alcohol consumption behaviour change under COVID-19 restrictions compare to “pre-COVID-19” time. For this purpose, daily data was normalized to the average per capita consumption of alcohol in the first week of the daily dataset (27th Jan–2nd Feb), which was selected as the zero point. This period captured consumption prior to COVID-19 based impacts which were caused by the COVID-19 pandemic timeline in Australia.
Normalized consumption change of alcohol (Diffalcohol) was calculated from Eq. :(2)Diffalcohol%=Voldaily−VolzeroVolzero×100%

Voldaily represents the daily per capita consumption of alcohol and Volzero represents the average per capita consumption of alcohol in the first week of the daily dataset.
ANOVA with Tukey post-hoc analyses were used to conduct all statistical comparisons between periods and to determine statistical significance. The differences in consumption levels between COVID-19 lockdown periods and non-lockdown periods were assessed, and separate analyses were performed for each period in 2020. Some data did not conform to normality assumptions and so log transforms have been applied where necessary. Linear trends were fit to the data using linear regression to account for short (within-weeks) and longer term (between-weeks) temporal trends.

## Results and discussion
3.1

## Method performance
EtS-D5 (10 ng/mL) was used to create calibration curves at nine different concentrations (0.05–100 ng/mL) with a correlation coefficient (r2) of 0.9994. Method accuracy and precision were carried out with influent wastewater samples spiked with native at 10 ng/mL and duplicate samples. The accuracy of the method varied between 98 and 106 %, and precision (RSD %) was <10 %. The method's LOD = 0.2 ng/mL, LOQ = 0.5 ng/mL. All analysed samples had concentrations of EtS above LOQ and so were quantified.

3.2

## The effects of specific restrictions on daily alcohol consumption in 2020
EtS was detected in all samples and the proportion difference over time is shown in 
. With this high-resolution daily dataset, the effects of specific restrictions on specific days could be evaluated against changes in alcohol consumption. 
There were various restrictions put in place in Melbourne in 2020, some of which directly affected the alcohol retail market. For instance, on March 25th the two largest alcohol retailers (BWS and Dan Murphy's, which above 70 % of the market) announced restrictions on the amount of alcohol that could be purchased from their outlets nationwide, with the aim of preventing stockpiling. In the following week, the average daily consumption level decreased by 47 % compared to the preceding week. Consumption remained low until the Easter holiday period in April 2020, after which it returned to the pre-pandemic level, before again dropping by about 50 % again after the holiday was over. On April 28th, alcohol consumption slowly increased over the first two weeks, probably related to the lifting of alcohol retailers' restrictions on alcohol purchases. Weekly average consumption showed a 15 % increase eventually and then decreased by 10 % compared to the previous two weeks until the lockdown ended.
Other restrictions on social gatherings reduced the number and scope of events at which alcohol could be consumed. This included closing all bars and many drinking-related facilities. For example, the average alcohol consumption in the week before the first lockdown was 26 % higher than the zero point. However, once the lockdown began, the weekly consumption showed a 53 % decrease during the first week. By contrast, alcohol consumption increased in the first few days at the beginning of the second lockdown, increasing >50 % compared to the week before.

3.3

## Temporal trends in alcohol consumption
Average alcohol consumption before the first lockdown was the highest of the year, before any COVID-19 related restrictions were first enforced. During the first lockdown, the average alcohol consumption declined by 15 % compared to the pre-lockdown period. It was at its lowest level in the year between the two lockdowns, 39 % below the pre-pandemic normalized zero point. Compared to the period between the two lockdowns, average alcohol consumption increased 4 % during the second lockdown followed by 3 % decline after the second lockdown. Interestingly, a previous study indicated that alcohol consumption in South Australia decreased during lockdown ( ), which is consistent with the change in alcohol consumption during the first lockdown in this study. However, alcohol consumption was slightly higher during the second lockdown than before the lockdown began. This result indicates that one cannot extrapolate consumption patterns from the first lockdown to other later lockdowns.
The statistical analysis identified some variations in alcohol consumption over the various periods. Alcohol consumption during the pre-lockdown period was significantly higher than other time periods, with a P value < 0.0001 except for the first lockdown. Alcohol consumption during the first lockdown was significantly higher than the period between two lockdowns with the P value of 0.0483.
The first lockdown began with a decline in alcohol consumption and then slightly increased during the lockdown. After the first lockdown, alcohol consumption decreased with a linear slope of −0.2547. The second lockdown began with an immediate increase in alcohol consumption compared to the relatively low consumption at the end of July (between the two lockdown periods), and then declined gradually during the second lockdown period. Finally, alcohol consumption stayed low at the first week after the second lockdown ended, then increased significantly and remain relatively stable for the rest of the year.
There were some spikes in alcohol consumption at the start and end of each lockdown which could reflect people's reaction to the lockdown. For instance, March 20th, the last day before the first lockdown, had double the average alcohol consumption on that day. May 20th, the third day after the first lockdown ended had the highest consumption for the whole year. Moreover, after the second lockdown began, alcohol consumption was more than three times the average consumption value on July 9th, the second day of the lockdown.
We were also able to identify increases or decreases that coincided with public holidays, major sporting events, and the introduction and removal of restrictions. For example, an increase in consumption was observed on July 22nd with additional restrictions were announced during the second lockdown. There was no spike in alcohol consumption at the end of the second lockdown, as observed after the end of the first lockdown. Furthermore, a trend of higher alcohol consumption during public holidays could still be observed except for the Christmas period, on and around December 25th 2020. Large sporting events such as the Australian Football League (AFL) grand final and Melbourne Cup horse racing events were generally associated with increased alcohol consumption. The restrictions meant that patrons had to enjoy the events from their homes but there was an increase in alcohol consumption on these days.

Some differences between the two lockdowns provide information on the community's reaction to these lengthy COVID lockdowns. Even though average alcohol consumption during the first lockdown was lower than before the first lockdown period, there was an increasing trend in alcohol consumption during the first lockdown. The lower mean consumption during the first lockdown suggested that the restrictions initially reduced alcohol consumption but the rising trend in consumption during the first lockdown period suggests that behaviors changed during the period. Alcohol consumption fluctuated significantly during the early stages of the second lockdown.
Multiple studies indicated there were some associations between alcohol consumption and mental health issues, which were exacerbated during the COVID-19 pandemic ( ; ; ). The pandemic and associated restrictions imposed multiple stressors on individuals. As the duration of the restrictions wore on, the number of daily positive cases increased as a result of the economic costs of being unable to work, the perceived availability of alcohol, lack of social interactions involving alcohol, and the uncertainty about the outcome of the epidemic. These factors can change individuals' drinking behaviors, but it is important to note that wastewater can only observe the changes at the population level and these changes reflect the effects of multiple factors.
As shown in , the finding that alcohol consumption reduced significantly after the first COVID-19 restrictions were introduced was consistent with other wastewater-based epidemiology (WBE) research. For instance, Bade et al. and Boogaerts et al. found that alcohol consumption reduced dramatically when social facilities were closed and social events restricted ( ; ). In , 134 wastewater samples were retrospectively analysed from 20 sites in Australia from three different restriction status. In the study by Boogaerts, daily wastewater samples (n = 735) were collected since September 2019 at the wastewater treatment plant of the university city of Leuven (Belgium). Both studies focused mainly on one lockdown. These results suggest that the stay-at-home policies and quarantines reduced alcohol use in the catchment population, probably by decreasing the number of social occasions where alcohol could be consumed. However, alcohol consumption increased in subsequent lockdowns in this study after the start of the second lockdown. This could be a result of multiple ongoing social pressures on the community, which could change alcohol consumption behaviour. According to the Australian Bureau of Statistics analysis of the impact of the COVID-19 epidemic on employee jobs and income ( ), the June quarters witnessed employee job losses. The decline of 1.9 % in the June quarter of 2020 contrasts with a seasonal increase of 1.9 % in the June quarter of the previous year.
In accordance with the findings of Boogaerts et al., the present study found that lifting restrictions substantially increased alcohol consumption. For instance, on May 31st, the reopening of restaurants, cafes, and pubs in Melbourne led to a significant 31 % rise in alcohol consumption compared to the previous week.
Another potential reason for the shift of alcohol consumption could be seasonality of drinking. Based on previous studies ( ; ), alcohol consumption in Australia is higher in summer than winter. This seasonal variation could influence the observed trends. For instance, the decrease of alcohol consumption between the two lockdowns occurred during the cold winter period when consumption may decrease seasonally. Consumption increased with increasing temperatures in November and December. However, Qiuda et al. have found that there was no seasonal trend of alcohol consumption in multiple sites in Australia ( ), which agreed with the alcohol consumption data from the National Wastewater Drug Monitoring Program in Australia (Fig. S2). With support from sales data, it showed that people tend to drink more beer in summer and more wine in winter.

3.4

## Weekly variation
An ANOVA analysis of weekday and weekend alcohol consumption ( 
) found that there was no significant difference between weekday and weekend alcohol consumption for the majority of 2020, except for the post-lockdown period. also found that the COVID-19 restrictions had a substantial impact on alcohol consumption during weekends. However, Boogaerts' found that during the lockdown period in Leuven, people continued to drink more on weekends. This could potentially be due to the limited population and occupational diversity of the catchment. Interestingly, a significant difference between the amount of alcohol consumed on weekdays and weekends re-emerged after the end of the second lockdown. The amount of alcohol consumed on weekends was again significantly higher than on weekdays, as in previous years. This result indicated that drinking patterns had returned to normal after the end of the second lockdown. 

3.5

## Limitation
This study used the measurement of EtS in wastewater to estimate temporal trends in alcohol consumption during 2020 and assessed changes in consumption to COVID related lockdowns in this catchment. One of the limitations of WBE in general in many studies, and also in this study is that the observed trends were relied on a static catchment population, and assuming that the trends were not driven by changes in population. In contrast to many other countries from March 2020 onwards population movement in Australia was heavily restricted even during the periods when the lockdown was lifted. Approaches to use population markers for assessing changes in the population have been published but have their own limitations. For example, during restrictions one can expect that even BOD and per capita water consumption may be affected since many commercial businesses were also under lockdown restrictions.
Another limitation is that this study only covered samples from one catchment with very specific COVID restrictions. It may not be representative for the effect of restrictions elsewhere.
In addition, the common limitations of using wastewater-based epidemiology to assess trends have also been recognized. It does not provide information on the demographics and specific groups that may or may not have changed their consumption behaviour. One can expect that different population groups respond differently, some may increase consumption where others decrease, and our data only provide an average change.

4

## Conclusions
This study demonstrates that the impact of COVID-19 restrictions on alcohol consumption could be measured by WBE with a daily, high-resolution sampling frequency. We examined daily wastewater samples for alcohol in one large catchment which contains approximately 18 % of national population over a one-year period. Daily sampling can provide specific consumption changes continuously, producing a more detailed assessment of policy changes at different stages of the COVID-19 epidemic than previous WBE studies. Specifically, people's short-term reaction to COVID-19 restrictions on alcohol consumption was shown with patterns shifting within and between the two extended lockdowns. This dataset can also provide findings for government and health institutions to monitor and optimize alcohol consumption prevention, harm reduction, and treatment strategies.
