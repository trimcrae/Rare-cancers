The involvement of trainees in these endeavors helps to propagate knowledge, curiosity, and novelty in the rapidly evolving field of vascular surgery. Through mentorship, trainees learn how to ask clear research questions; develop appropriate, ethical, and rigorous methods; and perform statistical analyses in a manner that is reproducible and valid. The use of real-world databases, such as the Society for Vascular Surgery (SVS) Vascular Quality Initiative (VQI) and other national databases, gives trainees the ability to test an array of hypotheses, spanning from preventive care to technical analyses and outcomes research, to contribute knowledge and change practice patterns.

## The VQI
The VQI is the largest repository of multicenter prospectively collected real-world data in vascular surgery. It was created to enhance the safety, quality, effectiveness and cost of vascular interventions using the structure of a Patient Safety Organization and has been very effective at evaluating compliance with SVS practices. , The VQI was developed by Dr Jack Cronenwett after the 1994 SVS presidential address discussing data reporting in Ohio. In 2003, hospitals in New England began sharing data through the Vascular Study Group of New England (VSGNE) and by 2011, the group extended throughout the United States as the SVS VQI. Currently, the group has more than 1000 centers internationally and more than 1 million procedures, serving as the primary data source for 785 publications. With 14 registries, the database includes information on carotid endarterectomy and stenting, infrainguinal and suprainguinal bypasses, thoracic and complex endovascular aortic repair, dialysis access, amputations, varicose veins, inferior vena cava filters, vascular medicine, and venous stents. Data coordinators, or other clinicians/trainee and research staff, from each site enter granular information on center/surgeon, demographic, preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative variables, as well as long-term outcomes.

## The VQI for trainee-led research and QI initiatives
The SVS encourages trainees to utilize the database through programs like the Quality Fellowship in Training (FIT) national program and the VSGNE/New England Society for Vascular Surgery Vascular Research Fellowship. These programs expose residents and fellows to the Patient Safety Organization, providing educational lectures, mentorship, and participation in regional meetings, and ultimately culminating in a VQI-based project. The FIT program started in 2022, with 16 trainee and mentor pairings with a total of 47 pairings since its inception. Additionally, the program offers the Jack L. Cronenwett scholarship to interested applicants. Examples of trainee-led projects through the FIT program include an analysis of shared decision-making and outcomes among carotid endarterectomy and transcarotid artery revascularization patients, and the exploration of the concept of unfit for open repair in patients with an abdominal aortic aneurysm. 
Through fellowship-led and institutional trainee involvement, trainees are encouraged to pursue research questions of interest in an ethical and rigorous manner. Areas of interest may include regional or demographic disparities can be explored using requested variables, such as the rural-urban commuting area, area deprivation index, and distressed communities index, among other variables. In the era of artificial intelligence, the VQI serves as a powerful database for machine learning models. Importantly, the VQI encourages QI initiatives through the recognition of practice gaps identified from registry data analysis. , Such involvements have resulted in numerous trainee-led publications, such as an analysis of outcomes for lower extremity revascularizations in octogenarians with intermittent claudication and the association of travel distance on readmissions after complex aortic surgery. , 

## Other large databases
Examples of other large databases include the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP) and the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services claims data. The NSQIP offers data on several operations and contains millions of more cases; however, in contrast with the VQI, it lacks detailed vascular-related variables, and the follow-up period is limited to 30 days postoperatively, whereas the VQI follow-up extends for 9 to 15 months. Use of Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services claims data provides the most generalizable method of analyzing nationwide trends and facilitates with procedural cost and reimbursement analyses; however, it is limited to data available for persons aged 65 years or older, is subject to misclassified bias from claims data, and lacks granular anatomical and procedural data. As an alternative venue, the SVS provides the ability to link VQI data to longer term outcomes from Medicare through the Vascular Implant Surveillance and Interventional Outcomes Network. 

## Challenges and considerations
It is important for trainees to be aware of registry data limitations, because these factors may affect their data analysis and conclusions. Principally, the accuracy of large database outcomes may be compromised by systematic errors, such as selection bias, information bias, and confounding. Specific to the VQI, centers and physicians who opt to participate in a QI registry may not be nationally representative. Additionally, smaller community hospitals are not able to participate owing to prohibitive costs and human resources required to enter data. Long-term data are also limited. Aside from mortality, which is linked to the Social Security Disability Index, all other long-term outcomes are subject to loss to follow-up at the index center. Importantly, the VQI only includes patients who underwent a vascular operation (except for the vascular medicine registry); as such, the VQI does not allow for comparisons between operative and nonoperative patients or the study of the natural history of vascular diseases. As a result of differences in variable definitions and specific limitations inherent to each database, outcomes obtained from the VQI and the American College of Surgeons NSQIP have been found to differ significantly. Therefore, trainees should understand how to approach these limitations in the context of their research questions, methods, and data interpretation.

## Conclusions
The VQI and other large databases facilitate meaningful and impactful QI and research endeavors among vascular surgery trainees. They can provide unique opportunities to engage with real-world data and use it to contribute knowledge, advancements, and improved health care systems in vascular surgery. It is important for trainees to approach these databases critically and systematically, and to collaboration with experienced mentors to navigate the complexities of large database analyses and produce good quality research.
