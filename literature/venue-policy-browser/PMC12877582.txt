## Objectives
Falls are a major cause of disability and mortality in older adults. Studies on falls in this population have mainly been conducted in high income countries, and scant attention has been given to the problem in low and middle income countries, including South Africa. The aim of the study was to establish a rate for falls in older adults in South Africa.

## Design
A cross-sectional survey with a 12-month follow-up survey.

## Setting
Three purposively selected suburbs of Cape Town: Plumstead, Wynberg Central and Gugulethu.

## Participants
Eight hundred and thirty seven randomly sampled ambulant community-dwelling subjects aged ≥ 65 years grouped according to ethnicity in three sub-samples: black Africans, coloureds (people of mixed ancestry) and whites.

## Measurements
Data were collected on socio-demographic and health characteristics, and history of falls using a structured questionnaire and a protocol for physical assessments and measurements.

## Results
Of the total baseline (n=837) and follow-up (n=632) survey participants, 76.5% and 77.2 %were females with a mean (S.D) age of 74 years (6.4) and 75 years (6.2), respectively. Rates of 26.4% and 21.9% for falls and of 11% and 6.3% for recurrent falls, respectively, were calculated at baseline and follow-up. Fall rates differed by ethnic sub-sample at baseline: whites 42 %, coloureds 34.4% and black Africans 6.4 % (p=0.0005). Rates of 236, 406 and 354 falls per 1000 person years were calculated for men, women and both genders, respectively. Recurrent falls were more common in women than in men.

## Conclusion
Falls are a significant problem in older adults in South Africa. Effective management of falls and falls prevention strategies for older people in South Africa, need to be developed and implemented.

## Background
Falls are a major cause of morbidity and mortality in older individuals. Even in the absence of an injury, a fall has potential psychological consequences for an individual. The cost implications of falls in later life for individuals and society are substantial ( , ).
Retrospective and prospective studies conducted in community dwelling or non-institutionalized older populations in high income countries have reported at least one fall a year in 30 to 60 % of persons aged >65 years, increasing to 50 per 100 in the age group 80 years and over. Higher rates have been reported in women than in men ( , , ). Retrospective cross-sectional studies on falls reported slightly lower rates (35% or less) than studies conducted prospectively ( , , ). However, retrospective studies may underestimate fall rates due to poor subject recall ( , ). Although the prevalence rate of falls is generally high, considerable variation is noted in findings across studies and even within the same country. Differences in fall rates may be explained partially by heterogeneity in the studies in terms of study design, sample size, ethnicity, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and variant duration of follow-up.
The rate of falls in Japanese-American and native Japanese populations has been almost half that reported in studies in Caucasian populations of the US and Europe ( , , ). This difference is evident in both rural and urban dwellers ( ). Rates are similarly low (average 18%) in mainland China, and in Hong Kong, Singapore and Taiwan (range 15 to 34%) ( , ), when compared to rates reported in both prospective and retrospective studies in Caucasian populations.
In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) data on falls in older persons are largely reported as part of studies on general trauma. The studies are in general based on convenience samples such as hospital or clinic populations, and mainly take the form of a retrospective search of hospital records to assess epidemiology of fractures, particularly of the hip ( , , ), or of mortuary records for causes of domestic deaths ( ). Nonetheless, the studies have shown that individuals aged 60 years and over have a high incidence of low energy trauma fractures ( , , ), suffer increased disability following trauma ( ), and account for a high number of fall related injuries.
Prevalence rates reported in community-based studies in SSA are limited. In Nigeria, Bekibele and Gureje ( ) reported a prevalence rate of 23% in a cross-sectional study in subjects aged 65 years and older with a mean age of 75.2 years. In Umutara Province, Rwanda, Ntagungira ( ) reported a recurrent fall rate of 23.3% for a sample of older persons with a history of falls. The subjects were aged 60 years or older with a mean age of 68.8 years.
A comparative lack of knowledge on falls in low and middle income countries including South Africa limits understanding of the extent of the problem, and hampers health planning and policy to address and prevent falls in later life. The objective of this study was to establish a prevalence rate for falls and to determine risk factors for falls in an older South African population, and thereby to contribute to knowledge on falls in SSA countries, as well as in low and middle income countries broadly. The study was undertaken in an urban, multi-ethnic community dwelling older population. The article focuses on the prevalence of falls and how the rates were established.

## Methods

## Study design
The study was designed as a cross-sectional descriptive/analytic survey with a 12-month follow-up survey. Data were collected with the use of a specially structured questionnaire which included a protocol for physical assessments and measurements. The questionnaire, constructed in English, was translated into isiXhosa (the local African language) for administration in the black African sub-sample, and back translated and piloted. To allow for comparisons between study populations from three residential suburbs which constituted the sampling frame and three ethnic groups (black Africans, coloureds (people of mixed ancestry) and whites), or sub-samples at a level of significance of 5% and 80 % power, a sample of 280 was required from each suburb, with a total sample of 840.

## Setting and participants
The three purposively selected suburbs of Cape Town which were the setting for the study were: Plumstead (which has a majority white population), Wynberg Central (a majority coloured population) and Gugulethu (a majority black African population). In 2001 Plumstead had a total population of 18,660 with 2,864 persons (15%) aged 65 years and over; Wynberg had a population 12,821 with 1,410 persons (11%) in that age group; and Gugulethu had a population of 61,113 with 3,419 persons (5.6%) in the same age group ( ). The sampling frame thus represented suburbs with comparatively high proportions of older people as reported in the 2001 national census, the most recent census data available at the time the study was conducted.
Inclusion criteria for the study were: Age 65 years or older; resident in one of the selected suburbs; ability to walk independently, with or without a walking aid; and capacity to give informed consent to participate in the study. A structured questionnaire with a small number of open ended items was specially constructed for the data collection. Variables were grouped in five domains: 1) Socio-demographic characteristics; 2) housing and environmental factors; 3) history of falls in the previous 12 months; 4) physical and mental health status; and 5) functional status (performance measures and anthropometric measurements were performed according to a protocol).
Information was gathered on history of falls in the previous 12 months, at baseline and in the 12-month interval to follow-up. Recurrent falls were defined as having ≥ 2 falls in the previous 12-month period. Approval to conduct the study was obtained from the Ethics and Research Committee of the Faculty of Health Sciences at the University of Cape Town, #126/2008.
For the purpose of the study, and communicated to the study participants, a fall was defined as an episode in which a person unintentionally comes to rest on the ground, the floor or another lower level, with or without injury, and includes falls resulting from contributing factors such as syncope, but excludes falls due to a violent blow, an epileptic seizure, or a stroke ( ).

## Data management and statistical analysis
Data were summarized as median (IQR) or as counts (%). Fall rates were calculated as proportions (or per 1000 person years) in the longitudinal study. Patients were categorized as fallers and non-fallers at baseline and follow-up. Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between the two groups of fallers. Differences in characteristics between the groups were expressed as an odds ratio (OR): with a 95% confidence interval in order to facilitate comparisons within each variable category as well as between groups for all variables included in the analysis. Wald test statistic was computed to assess significance at 0.05 level of significance. Multinomial regression analysis was used to compare single and recurrent fallers with non-fallers. Data were analysed using SPSS software (IBM SPSS, Inc., Chicago Illinois).

## Results
Eight hundred and ninety six households with a person aged ≥ 65 years old were visited. Of these, 844 household residents were eligible and agreed to participate in the study and were recruited for the sample. Fifty-two older persons (males 50%, females 50%) refused to participate in the study at baseline recruitment. Of the 844 subjects recruited, seven were later excluded for not meeting all the study criteria. Eight hundred and thirty seven subjects (Gugulethu: n=283, 98% black Africans; Plumstead: n=188, 70% whites and 27% coloureds; and Wynberg: n=366, 93% coloureds) constituted the sample for the baseline survey. A small number of Indians (2.6%) were recruited at baseline, who mainly resided in Wynberg. The small percentage of Indians in the baseline sample did not warrant meaningful analyses of the data for these subjects. Six hundred and thirty two subjects (Gugulethu: n=181; Plumstead: n=143; and Wynberg: n=308) constituted the sample for the follow-up survey. Of the 205 subjects who could not be followed up, 50 had died, 78 refused to participate, 56 had relocated, and 21 were too ill to be interviewed. Females constituted 76.5% of the baseline sample and 77.2% of the follow-up sample. The mean (S.D) age was 74 years (6.4) and 75 years (6.2), respectively.
Demographic characteristics of the sample are shown in . In the baseline sample, 28 % had a primary level of education (< 8 years of schooling) and 5 % had received no formal schooling. Seventy eight per cent black Africans had worked as unskilled labour compared to 8.6% whites and 17.8% coloureds, p<0.001. Less than 15 % lived alone; the majority co-resided with family members. Three fifths of the subjects were widowed. The majority (77.8%) were recipients of a means-tested social (non-contributory) pension. Approximately 10 % of subjects reported poor health and the same proportion perceived their health to be worse than a year ago. Almost one in five (17%) subjects reported poor mobility and 13 % used a walking aid. The median for comorbid conditions was three (range 0-9) and the median of medications used was four (range 0-17). Chronic conditions commonly reported were hypertension (68%), arthritis (59%), cardiovascular disease (47%), diabetes (26%) and foot disorders (26%). 
Of the baseline sample, 221 (26.4%; 95% CI 23.5-29.5%) subjects reported having fallen in the previous year; 11% reported recurrent falls. A total of 421 falls were reported by the 221 participants, an average of 1.9 falls per person; 3.2 falls were reported per recurrent faller (292 falls in 92 participants, prevalence rate (PR) 11%; 95% CI, 9-13%).
Of the follow-up sample, 138 (21.8%; 95% CI 18.925.3%) subjects reported at least one fall in the 12-month interval; 6.3% reported recurrent falls. A total of 224 falls were reported; 1.6 falls per faller. Recurrent fallers at follow-up reported 3.3 falls per faller (133 falls reported by 40 participants, a fall rate of 6.3%; 95% CI, 4.6-8.3%). Women reported more recurrent falls than men. Of the 40 subjects who reported recurrent falls, only 7 (17.5%) were men. The overall fall rate on follow-up was 354 /1000 person years. The rate for men and women was 236/1000 and 406/1000 person years, respectively. The difference in fall rate between men and women was statistically significant (p=0.002). However, significantly different fall prevalence rates were calculated for the three ethnic groups: 42.9 % for whites, 34.4 % for coloureds, and a low 6.4 % for black Africans (p=0.0005) at baseline and 556, 405 and 111/1000 person years for whites, coloureds and black Africans in the follow-up survey, respectively.The total number of Indian subjects ( ) was too small for interpretation, but 8 (36.4%) reported a fall at baseline.
Of the 139 respondents who reported falling in the year following baseline, 75 (54.3%) were incident fallers - that is, they had not fallen prior to baseline, and 64 (45.7%) had reported falling prior to baseline ( ). No difference in age was found between non-fallers and fallers, or between non-fallers and recurrent fallers, at either baseline or follow-up. Being white or coloured, and having had a skilled, professional or managerial occupation were predictors of falls ( and ). Fall rates did not differ by gender at baseline or follow-up, but females had increased risk of recurrent falls at baseline ( and ). Falls in men occurred mainly outdoors (68.2% versus 49.7% for men and women, respectively, p=0.02); while those in women occurred mainly indoors (length of time spent outdoors by subjects was not recorded). Of falls that occurred in men outdoors, slightly more than half occurred away from the dwelling. 
Self-reported conditions as diagnosed by a health practitioner, hypertension, diabetes, poor memory and poor vision, were more common in black Africans than coloureds and whites; while cardiovascular disease, foot disorders, poor urine control and dizziness were common in both coloureds and whites. On univariate analysis, a number of clinical conditions were associated with a propensity to fall ( ). On multiple logistic regression analysis, compared to non-fallers, those who had fallen only once were likely to report cardiovascular disease and dizziness, while stroke, Parkinson’s disease, cardiovascular disease, poor mobility, poor urine control and dizziness were associated with recurrent falls ( ). The most common consequence of a fall was injury, mainly soft tissue injuries, bruises (> 60%), lacerations (25%) and fractures (14%). Of those who reported injury following a fall, only 42 per cent sought medical help for the injury. Other consequences of the fall were fear of falling again (54%), and not having recovered fully from the effects of the earlier fall (42%), as well as having to alter or forego certain activities the subject engaged in previously. 

## Discussion
To our knowledge, the study is the first multi-ethnic community based study with a one-year follow-up in an urban older population in SSA. Overall fall rates of 26.4% and 21.8% established at baseline and follow-up, respectively, are lower than rates reported in high income countries. However, the rates are similar to the rate reported in the retrospective study in Nigeria, 23% ( ), but considerably higher than rates reported in studies in Asian countries, where rates have ranged from 14% to 19.8% ( , , ), and in Latin America, a rate of 16.8% in Brazil ( ).
The rates in the present study are nevertheless similar to those found in certain community based studies conducted in Latin America: e.g. a fall rate of 27.6 to 29 % in Brazil (2628). The lower fall rate of 21.8% in the 12-month follow up in the present study may be attributable to differential attrition of those at higher risk of a fall, as demonstrated by the frailty of subjects lost to follow-up compared to those who were followed up. Those lost to follow-up were older than those who were followed-up (median age 76 years versus 73 years) and rated their health as poor at baseline.
Rates of recurrent falls in studies in middle income countries are similar to those in the present study, but range widely, even in reports on prospective studies: from 4.8 to 6 % in Hong Kong and Taiwan ( , ), to 11 % in Brazil ( ). Of note is that the majority of the studies, although community based, included subjects younger than those in the present study, i.e. age ≥ 60 years. Although the rate of recurrent falls at follow-up of 6.3% established in our study is lower than rates reported in high income countries, the rate of recurrent falls at baseline of 11 % is similar to rates reported in high income countries both prospectively (11.5 % in Canada) ( ) and retrospectively (10 % in Canada and USA) ( , ). The rate of recurrent falls does not vary significantly in different studies; presumably, recurrent falls affect frail individuals with multiple risk factors for falls whose phenotype and predisposition to falls may not differ significantly in different environments. In a prospective study in 2008 in Australia, Tiedemann and others ( ) reported far higher rates: 22 % of recurrent falls in a sample of subjects aged 74-98 years. However, the study may have included a large number of frail individuals. In a cross-sectional study in France, recurrent falls were reported in 19% of the sample with a mean age 72 years ( ). Factors such as study design, inclusion criteria and method of reporting are likely to impact outcomes. Some studies report recurrent falls as a proportion of the total sample, while others report recurrent falls as a proportion of fallers
Pointedly, fall rates, particularly recurrent falls, established in the present study were associated with self-reported chronic disease. Indeed, other studies ( ) have shown that fall incidence in community dwelling older adults is linked to the presence of chronic disease and that fall rate increases linearly with the number of chronic conditions present.
The multi-ethnic study population in the present study was socio-economically heterogeneous. Interestingly, the black African sub-sample which in the South African context is the most socio-economically deprived ethnic group reported comparatively few falls. The fall rate reported in the white sub-sample approximates rates found in high income countries, while the rate for the coloured sub-sample lies between those of the other two sub-samples. The multi-ethnicity of the sample may partly explain why the fall prevalence rate lies between rates established in Asia and those in high income countries. Studies in Asia have tended to be conducted in homogenous populations, such as Chinese in Hong Kong. A lower risk of falls has also been reported in studies in minority populations in high income countries, such as Japanese Americans - which low prevalence has been attributed to better neuromuscular function in this group ( , ).
It may be speculated that differences in propensity to fall are related to life style factors, present and past. An earlier hospital based study in South Africa on fracture incidence in older patients reported a low incidence of fractures following a fall in black African compared to white patients. This difference in incidence was explained by high levels of physical activity in the day-to-day lives of black Africans, particularly at younger ages ( , ). In the present study, the majority of black Africans (78%) had engaged in unskilled manual labour, compared to 8.6 % of whites and 17.3 % of coloureds. Differences in physical activity levels and life-styles, particularly in contexts of socio-economic disadvantage, may modify peak bone, and muscle mass and function ( ) and their rate of decline, which may support the maintenance of gait and balance, and decrease a propensity to fall in later life. However, reasons for the differences in fall rates in the ethnic sub-samples in the present study need to be explored in a future study.
In accordance with findings of studies on falls in high income countries ( , ), and in comparison with women, in the present study falls in men mainly occurred outdoors and away from the dwelling (56.7% versus 48.3% for men and women, respectively). Falls were thus largely determined by gender differences in types of activity in which the subjects engaged. Indoor falls occurred mainly in the bedroom and kitchen. The majority of falls occurred during day time, a period of maximum activity, particularly in an older population.
Limitations of the present study included an inability to collect censored data in the follow-up period; the nonavailability of telephones for interviews in this period; and non-use of diaries due to a low literacy level of some subjects and thus inability to record falls in a diary in the interval to follow-up. Strengths of the study may nonetheless lie in an increased case-mix as the study was conducted in subjects’ dwelling which enabled participation of those who may have encountered barriers due to frailty or poverty to travel to a central venue for physical assessment.
In conclusion, falls are a significant problem in South Africa’s older population. The differential fall rates calculated for the three ethnic sub-samples are of particular interest and warrant further investigation in future studies. An understanding of factors that contribute to different fall rates in other countries also with multi-ethnic populations will help to pinpoint causality and risk factors for falls in different settings, and serve to inform intervention to prevent falls.
