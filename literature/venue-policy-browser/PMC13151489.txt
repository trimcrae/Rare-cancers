## Abstract

## Introduction
Exposure to secondhand smoke is a significant public health concern. Our study assessed the level of implementation of smoke-free laws in restaurants in Karachi, Pakistan.

## Methods
We conducted a cross-sectional study in District East, Karachi. We randomly selected 40 restaurants based on their meal cost: 17 low (<400 PKR/meal), 12 medium (400–600 PKR/meal) and 11 high-end (>600 PKR/meal). We assessed tobacco control law implementation using an observational checklist that included questions about the presence of no-smoking signs, presence of smokers, people/children near smokers and remains of tobacco products and developed a compliance score for smoke-free laws: compliant (all criteria met), partially compliant (1–2 criteria met) and non-compliant (none met). We asked questions about knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding smoke-free laws with each restaurant’s staff. We reported frequencies and percentages for observations and questionnaire responses by restaurant category.

## Results
Of the 40 restaurants, 70% (n=28) were either non-compliant or partially compliant, with 82% of low-end and 67% of medium-end were either non-compliant or partially compliant. None of the high-end restaurants were non-compliant; however, 55% partially complied with the smoke-free laws. More than half (53%, n=21) had no no-smoking signs. Smokers were observed on terraces or near children in 20% (n=8) of restaurants, and ashtrays or cigarette packs/butts were found in 40% (n=16). Fifty per cent of restaurant staff (n=20) believed that there was no tobacco control law in Pakistan, only 10% (n=4) knew the particulars of the law and 18% (n=7) were aware of any penalties for violations. In addition, 60% (n=24) reported no visits from legislative or administrative bodies to enforce compliance.

## Conclusion
Our study reveals disparities in the compliance of smoke-free laws between low-end and high-end restaurants, highlighting the need to enforce these laws across all restaurant categories effectively.

Keywords: Secondhand smoke, Surveillance and monitoring, Smoking Caused Disease, Public policy, Disparities

## WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC
Despite the legislation of the Prohibition of Smoking in Enclosed Places and Protection of Non-Smokers Health Ordinance in Pakistan, compliance with these regulations remains inconsistent.

## WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
Our study reveals the poor implementation of smoke-free laws, with 70% of the restaurants being either non-compliant or partially compliant.
We also found a disparity in compliance between low-end and high-end restaurants, as 82% of low end and 67% of medium end were either non-compliant or partially compliant.
None of the high-end restaurants were non-compliant; however, 55% partially complied with the smoke-free laws.

## HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE, OR POLICY
Our study findings highlight that merely passing smoke-free legislation is inadequate, and proper implementation of these laws is crucial across all categories of restaurants.
Ensuring strict surveillance and enforcement of smoke-free laws is an effective strategy that could help achieve our goal of reducing exposure to secondhand smoke.

## Introduction
 Tobacco use poses a significant threat to public health, contributing to a range of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), such as chronic obstructive pulmonary diseases, cardiovascular diseases, diabetes and various cancers. Globally, tobacco use is attributable to approximately seven million deaths each year. While developed countries have seen a decline in tobacco use, low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs) are experiencing an alarming rise in consumption, with nearly 80% of the world’s smokers residing in LMICs. Pakistan is one of the countries where over 23 million people are active tobacco users, and about 100 000 premature deaths occur annually due to tobacco use. 
Exposure to secondhand smoke (SHS) also poses a significant risk, leading to over 41 000 deaths annually among non-smoking adults and 400 deaths in infants, as well as substantial economic losses totaling around US$5.6 billion due to decreased productivity. Many individuals, particularly non-smokers, are exposed to SHS in various settings, such as workplaces, homes, vehicles and public places, including restaurants. According to the Global Adult Tobacco Survey, approximately 21.2 million people in Pakistan are exposed to tobacco smoke at restaurants, with notably higher exposure in males (86.0%) and in rural areas (90.5%). 
The recognition of the need to legislate tobacco production, distribution and consumption in Pakistan dates back to 1965. This early legislative step laid the foundation for the legislation on the Cigarette (Printing or Warning) Ordinance No. LXXIII, 1979, and Prohibition of Smoking in Enclosed Places and Protection of Non-Smokers Health Ordinance No. LXXIV, 2002. Moreover, the country officially ratified the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (FCTC) in early 2005. The Ordinance No. LXXIV, 2002 aligns with FCTC Article 8, which mandates that managers or owners of public places display clear no-smoking signage both inside and outside premises and prohibit smoking by customers in smoke-free zones. Non-compliance with not displaying no-smoking signage results in penalties for the manager or owner, whereas active smoking in smoke-free zones imposed duty on smoker. Other provisions include removing ashtrays and permitting authorised officers to take measures such as issuing warnings, discontinuing services or contacting authorities to enforce compliance. However, there is no penalty for these provisions. 
In Pakistan, the regulation and implementation of tobacco control laws are primarily overseen by the Ministry of National Health Services, Regulations, and Coordination (NHSRC). The specific responsibilities and enforcement actions may vary by province but generally include the Provincial Health Departments, District Health Officers, Local Law Enforcement Agencies, including police and municipal authorities, and the Tobacco Control Cell, a specialised unit within the Ministry of NHSRC. Despite these efforts, weak policy implementation persists, raising concerns among national and international observers about Pakistan’s ability to achieve its commitment to implement and comply with the smoke-free laws. 
Several studies have been conducted in different regions to assess compliance with smoke-free policies at various public places, and they found that compliance with smoke-free laws may vary across regions as well as based on the type of public places observed. For instance, a study from Bangladesh assessed the level of compliance with smoke-free laws across 673 public places and found that the overall compliance was 52.7% in indoor venues and 26.5% in outdoor venues. Another study conducted in Nepal found that less compliance with the law is observed in public places (26%) (such as eateries, shopping centres and transportation facilities) compared with government office buildings (75%), highlighting the challenges in uniformly implementing tobacco control laws across different types of venues. 
A few studies from Pakistan have reported compliance related to tobacco consumption in public places. , For instance, a pilot study conducted in Karachi surveyed 91 restaurants and found that 58% (n=53) had open smoking areas, while 70% (n=64) displayed no smoking signs. A recent study (2022) conducted in five districts of Karachi reported that 66% of the venues, including public and private offices, health and educational institutions, markets and eateries, showed no compliance with indoor smoking regulations. Another study by Ahsan et al observed compliance with smoke-free policies at 10 different indoor and outdoor public places and found that amusement centres (44%; n=88) and restaurants (35%; n=153) exhibited the highest prevalence of active smoking. These findings highlight venues, especially those offering leisure or dining experiences, may struggle to enforce smoke-free regulations. The primary goal of implementing smoke-free policies in public places is to protect non-smokers equally from SHS exposure. However, literature found notable discrepancies in exposure to SHS among people based on their socioeconomic status. For instance, people living in affluent areas are less likely to be exposed to SHS than those living in poorer areas. This disparity may be due to the variability in enforcing smoke-free laws in public places from different socioeconomic backgrounds. Therefore, identifying and addressing this disparity are crucial to ensure the equitable implementation of smoke-free laws, protecting the health and well-being of all people regardless of their socioeconomic status. However, there is a lack of comprehensive studies explicitly assessing the level of implementation of SHS exposure reduction policies in restaurants in Pakistan, mainly how implementation varies across restaurants of different socioeconomic categories. Therefore, this study assessed the level of compliance with smoke-free laws by restaurants from different socioeconomic statuses in Karachi.

## Study objectives
The study objectives are to evaluate the compliance of smoke-free laws by assessing the proportion of compliant, partially compliant and non-compliant across restaurants and to assess the knowledge, attitude and practice of restaurant staff regarding implementing smoke-free laws.

## Methods
We conducted an observational cross-sectional study in the District East in Karachi, Pakistan, from December 2022 to February 2023. District East is one of the seven districts in Karachi, with a population of 1.92 million. The residents represent diverse socioeconomic backgrounds and belong to various ethnic groups, including Urdu-speaking, Sindhi, Pashto, Punjabi and others, contributing to its rich cultural diversity. We mapped all the restaurants in the District East of Karachi with indoor dining facilities, including cafes, roadside restaurants (Dhabas), sit-down and high-end restaurants, and restaurants in hotels using Open Map. We found 515 restaurants in the District East of Karachi, and out of these, we randomly selected 40 restaurants. We then categorised these 40 restaurants into low (<400 PKR/meal), medium (400–600 PKR/meal) and high end (>600 PKR/meal) based on the cost of meals at each restaurant. To enrol 40 restaurants, we approached 48 randomly selected restaurants, as eight managers declined to participate (seven from low-end and one from high-end restaurants). We assessed the level of implementation of tobacco control laws at each restaurant using two methods: a structured observational checklist and an interviewer-administered questionnaire with each restaurant’s staff. The observation checklist comprised five points regarding compliance with tobacco control laws in line with smoke-free policies in Pakistan as well as set by the WHO FCTC, including the presence of no-smoking signs, the presence of smokers (employees or public), the presence of people/children near active smokers or remains of tobacco products on restaurant premises. This checklist has also been used in other studies conducted in Pakistan. The survey questionnaire comprised questions related to knowledge, attitude and practices adapted from Khan et al, who assessed the knowledge of the general population in Karachi, Pakistan. The knowledge domain covered questions related to the restaurant’s staff knowledge about tobacco control laws in Pakistan, the attitude included the question of whether SHS has any effect on surrounding people, and practices asked questions about customers’ smoking practices inside the restaurants and whether any legislative or administrative body visits the restaurants to observe adherence to smoke-free law.
Two data collectors were trained to collect data using the observational checklist and the survey questionnaire. The data collectors were instructed to remain unobtrusive during their observations to minimise the Hawthorne effect. The data collectors visited the restaurants during afternoon/evening hours as it was the busiest time and majority people including families visit restaurants at this time. On entering a restaurant, the data collectors acted as regular customers, ordering food and completing the observational checklist while waiting and eating to minimise any influence on staff behaviour. After finishing the checklist, they introduced themselves to the restaurant manager, explained the study, and requested permission to interview one available staff member. Given that visits occurred during peak hours, data collectors interviewed any staff member who had at least 10 min to respond to the survey questionnaire. The data collectors provided complete study details to the restaurant staff before the interview, and written informed consent was obtained from those who agreed to participate. The data collection took approximately 20–30 min at each site to complete the observation checklist and survey questionnaire.

## Observation checklist scoring
We converted the observation checklist scores into three categories: compliant, non-compliant and partially compliant. A restaurant was considered compliant if it met all three conditions: signage for no-smoking/no tobacco displayed, no one (public or employee) was observed smoking, and no cigarette butt or ashtray was found. A restaurant was considered non-compliant if none of these were met. A restaurant was considered partially compliant if only 1–2 conditions were met. We adopted this scoring method from another study from Pakistan, which used a simple binary classification (compliant and non-compliant). However, we believed that introducing a partially compliant category would capture intermediate compliance and highlight improvement areas.

## Statistical analysis
We present frequencies and percentages for our observations related to smoke-free laws implementation, including the number of no-smoking signs, presence of smokers on terrace/balcony, presence of smokers close to people/children, presence of ashtrays and remains of tobacco products, and smoking by restaurant staff. We tabulated compliance categories (complaint, non-compliant and partially compliant) separately for the three restaurant categories (low, medium and high end). The knowledge, attitude and practice of restaurant staff regarding smoke-free laws are also reported as frequencies and percentages. The data were analysed using SPSS V.26.0.

## Results
The overall compliance of the restaurants was assessed based on the criteria defined using the observation checklist ( ). Of the 40 restaurants observed, the majority (70%, n=28) were either non-compliant or partially compliant with smoke-free laws, while only 30% (n=12) were fully compliant. Based on categories, 82% of low-end and 67% of medium-end restaurants were either non-compliant or partially compliant with smoke-free laws. None of the high-end restaurants were non-compliant; however, 55% partially complied with the smoke-free laws. The median score out of 5 was 2 (IQR 1).
 Out of 40 restaurants, 17 were low end, 12 were medium end and 11 were high end. More than half of the restaurants (53%, n=21) did not have any no-smoking signs, 35% (n=14) had 1–2 signs and only 12% (n=5) had three or more signs. We observed smokers on extended terraces/balconies and near children/people in 20% of the restaurants (n=8). Of these eight restaurants, four (29%) were low end, and three (25%) were medium end ( ). In addition, ashtrays or cigarette packs/butts were found in 40% of restaurants (n=16), with 10 (59%) of these being low-end restaurants. Moreover, smoking among restaurant employees was observed in only 18% (n=3) of low-end restaurants ( ).
 Regarding knowledge-based questions in the survey questionnaire, half of the participants (n=20) stated that there is no tobacco control law in Pakistan, with most of them being from the low end (53%, n=9) and medium end (58%, n=7). In addition, a quarter (n=10) were unaware of the existence of any such law. Only four respondents (10%) admitted that they knew the particulars of tobacco control law; none were from the low-end restaurants. Overall, only seven respondents (18%) were aware of any consequences for violating smoke-free public spaces regulations; among these, three (18%) were from low-end, one (8%) from medium-end and three (27%) from high-end restaurants ( ).
 The question regarding attitude assessed the perception of restaurant staff about the impact of SHS on surrounding people. Overall, 93% (n=37) perceived that SHS has some effect on people around them ( ).
Regarding practice questions, 40% (n=16) of the respondents admitted that it is common for customers to smoke inside restaurants, with the highest proportion reported from high end (55%, n=6) and the lowest from medium end (33%, n=4). More than half of the study participants (60%, n=24) admitted that no legislative or administrative body visited their restaurants to observe adherence to smoke-free laws, with the majority from medium end (75%, n=9), followed by low end (59%, n=10) and high end (45%, n=5) ( ).

## Discussion
This study evaluated the adherence to smoke-free laws across restaurants from different socioeconomic statuses in Karachi, Pakistan. Among the 40 restaurants surveyed, there were 17 low-end, 12 medium-end and 11 high-end restaurants. Out of these 40 restaurants observed, the majority (70%) were either non-compliant or partially compliant with the smoke-free laws. In addition, full compliance was highest among high-end restaurants (46%), whereas lowest among low-end restaurants (18%), followed by medium-end restaurants (33%). Furthermore, the study recorded low knowledge of restaurant staff regarding smoke-free laws, specifically from low-end and high-end restaurants.
Our study revealed only 30% overall compliance with smoke-free laws in the restaurants of the District East of Karachi. In addition, compliance was higher among high-end restaurants (45.5%) than low-end (17.6%) and medium-end (33.3%) restaurants. The disparity in compliance between high-end and low or medium-end restaurants may be due to financial differences, as high-end restaurants often have the resources to train staff and ensure adherence to regulations. Additionally, people visiting high-end restaurants expect a smoke-free environment, motivating owners and staff to comply with smoke-free laws and promote healthier settings. Likewise, literature from other regions reported overall compliance with smoke-free laws at different public places; however, none reported findings based on the socioeconomic status of these places. For instance, a study by Basnet et al from Nepal reported overall smoke-free legislation of 56.4%, another study from India reported an overall compliance of 84%, while a study from Bangladesh reported an overall compliance of 52.7% in indoor venues. 
Literature suggests that appropriate signages prohibiting smoking are associated with a higher likelihood of compliance with smoke-free laws. Our study found that more than half of restaurants (53%) did not have no-smoking/no-tobacco signage, with a notably higher proportion of restaurants from medium end to low end, ranging from 50% to 65%. This aligns with a previous study from Karachi, which reported that 70% of the 91 restaurants, banks and offices did not have no-smoking signs. Similarly, another Karachi-based study reported that no-smoking signs were displayed at only 45% (n=73) of eateries, while another study reported that only 5% of 434 Karachi restaurants had signs at the entrance, and only 6% had no-smoking signage displayed inside the venue. However, our study identified the disparities based on the socioeconomic status of the restaurants.
The presence of ashtrays and cigarette butts also reflects indoor smoking practices within restaurants. In the current study, we observed 40% of restaurants (n=16) with ashtrays or remains of tobacco products on their premises, with 59% (n=10) being the low-end restaurants. Another study from Karachi found a higher number of cigarette butts (87.5%) but a lower number of ashtrays (6.5%). This high prevalence could be due to the inclusion of outdoor areas and the boundary of the premises. Another reason may be that there is no penalty for managers or owners to have ashtrays on the premises; however, FCTC Article 8 strongly recommended imposing a duty on them.
We observed active smoking on the extended terrace and near children/people at 20% of restaurants (n=8), and all of them were from low-end and medium-end restaurants. In addition, employees were observed smoking at 8% (n=3) restaurants, all of which were low-end restaurants. In other studies from Pakistan, active smoking was also observed in 58% (n=53) of restaurants in one study and 35% (n=153) in another study. In contrast, an Indian study with high compliance with smoke-free laws reported an absence of active smoking in public places (98%, n=1031) and their owner/incharge (99%, n=1042). This is particularly important as, according to the 2002 Ordinance in Pakistan, smoking is banned in all restaurants and eateries to protect non-smokers from SHS exposure. However, active smoking by customers and restaurant staff on restaurant premises not only violates these laws but also underscores that passing smoke-free legislation alone is insufficient; effective enforcement of these laws is essential to achieve the intended health protections.
While assessing the knowledge, attitude and practice, we found a higher proportion of restaurant staff with a lack of knowledge regarding tobacco control law in Pakistan (75%), the particulars of tobacco control law (91%) and the consequential jurisdiction for violation of smoke-free public places (83%). The knowledge among restaurant staff also varied based on the restaurant type as most of the staff from low to medium end were unaware of tobacco control laws in Pakistan. However, the majority (93%) accepted some effects of SHS on surrounding people. Similarly, another study conducted in Karachi interviewed the general public (n=114) and found that about 54% were unaware of the smoking ban in public places; however, 90% expressed their interest in supporting the smoke-free laws. In contrast, a study conducted in Malaysia among restaurant staff found that more than 80% of respondents had good to excellent knowledge of tobacco control laws. These differences in knowledge could be due to the population’s educational level and the extent of public awareness campaigns in the country. Hence, our findings highlight the need for targeted education and awareness programmes for restaurant staff to improve knowledge and compliance with tobacco control laws.
Ensuring adherence to the laws depends on monitoring compliance by the legislative or administrative bodies. Our study found that about 60% of restaurant staff accepted no legislative or administrative body visits to observe compliance with the smoke-free law. We also found discrepancies in the monitoring by restaurant type as a higher proportion of high-end restaurant staff (45%) reported visits from legislative or administrative bodies to observe compliance with smoke-free laws than low-end (35%) and medium-end (17%) restaurants. This lack of monitoring could be one of the key reasons for the higher non-compliance rates of smoke-free laws observed in our study, especially in low and medium-end restaurants.
The strengths of the study include a random selection of restaurants. Our study was the first to evaluate compliance across restaurants of varying socioeconomic categories, revealing differences despite existing smoke-free legislation. Future qualitative studies can explore factors underlying these differences in implementing these laws. In addition, observing several compliance indicators and surveying restaurant staff regarding their knowledge, attitude and practice towards smoke-free laws were other major strengths of our study. Furthermore, we selected restaurants across various socioeconomic statuses; thus, our study findings are generalisable. There were some limitations associated with this observational study. We could not perform a statistical test to show differences in compliance and knowledge across restaurant categories due to a smaller sample and sparse data. However, we present and discuss differences in compliance and knowledge across restaurant categories based on the descriptive results to show any observable trends, considering their significance for future research with larger sample sizes. The demographic profiles of customers who visited restaurants might influence compliance due to variations in awareness or demand for smoke-free environments. However, this is beyond the scope of our study as our primary focus was on assessing compliance within the restaurant settings rather than on external factors, such as customer profiles, which would require additional resources. Future studies could address this by incorporating customer demographics to provide a deeper understanding of their role in shaping compliance patterns. Our study assessed compliance with smoke-free laws in restaurants only in Karachi; therefore, our findings may not be generalised to other cities in Pakistan. Another limitation could be the presence of social desirability bias due to the favourable responses of the restaurant staff to meet restaurant expectations. However, to mitigate this, we assured staff that their responses would be kept confidential, and the names of the restaurants and staff identities would remain anonymous to help create a safe environment for staff to provide more honest and accurate responses.

## Conclusion
Our study reveals inconsistent compliance with smoke-free laws across socioeconomic categories of restaurants in Pakistan, with a higher proportion (70%, n=28) being either non-compliant or only partially compliant. The disparity in compliance rates, particularly between low-end or medium-end and high-end restaurants, underscores that merely passing smoke-free legislation is insufficient. To protect the public, particularly non-smokers, from the harmful effects of SHS exposure, it is crucial that these laws are consistently enforced across all types of public spaces. Given the role of SHS exposure in contributing to rising rates of NCDs in Pakistan, our study findings call for urgent action by the Ministry of NHSRC and other entities that oversee tobacco control laws. By taking joint responsibility, these agencies can work towards uniform enforcement of smoke-free laws across all locations and socioeconomic settings. Furthermore, strengthening monitoring compliance, imposing penalties on violating smoke-free laws and promoting public awareness about SHS risks are essential steps in achieving a comprehensive reduction in SHS exposure and advancing public health protection nationwide.
