## ABSTRACT

## Introduction:
Falls are a major health concern for older adults, particularly those with cognitive impairment. Many factors contribute to the risk of falls, making it a complex issue to manage.

## Aim:
This systematic review aimed to identify and describe the implementation strategies used for fall prevention in community-dwelling older people with cognitive impairment.

## Eligibility criteria:
The review included studies on fall prevention interventions for community-dwelling adults aged > 50 years with cognitive impairment. Eligible studies were primary research, including experimental, quasi-experimental, qualitative, and mixed methods designs, with detailed implementation strategies. Systematic reviews, protocols, editorials, opinions, commentaries, and conference papers were excluded.

## Methods:
This review followed the JBI methodology for systematic reviews of effectiveness and was reported in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Five databases were searched for studies published in English after 2000: MEDLINE (EBSCOhost), CINAHL Ultimate (EBSCOhost), PsycINFO (EBSCOhost), Scopus, and Web of Science Core Collection. Data on implementation strategies and outcomes were extracted and synthesized using the ERIC framework and Proctor's implementation taxonomy.

## Results:
Twenty-one studies met the eligibility criteria. Twenty-four implementation strategies were identified, with 15 directly fitting the ERIC list and 9 additional strategies emerging. The most common strategies were engaging consumers (i.e., patients and family members or caregivers) (n = 13), adapting and tailoring to context (n = 9), and training and educating stakeholders (n = 8). Implementation outcomes were reported inconsistently across studies. Several implementation strategies were often used, but their combined effects on fall prevention outcomes were not clearly reported.

## Conclusions:
This review indicates that engaging consumers, particularly family members, was the most common implementation strategy to prevent falls. The review also highlights new strategies, such as providing culturally appropriate interventions and using dementia-trained instructors. Lastly, the review identifies the need for clearer reporting of implementation strategies and outcomes in future studies.

## INTRODUCTION
Falls are a major public health concern among older adults, especially those with cognitive impairment, as such individuals are more vulnerable to injuries and complications following a fall. This is especially the case in low- and middle-income countries. , Globally, the aging population is rapidly increasing, with projections estimating that by 2050, the number of people aged 60 years or older will reach 2.1 billion. Furthermore, more than 55 million people worldwide have dementia, and this number is expected to triple by 2050. 
As life expectancy increases, the prevalence of age-related conditions, such as dementia and cognitive impairment, also increases and can significantly reduce quality of life. Cognitive impairment not only impairs a person's ability to maintain balance and mobility, but also affects their capacity to understand and adhere to fall prevention interventions. Consequently, individuals with cognitive impairments face unique challenges in mitigating fall risks, which may require tailored interventions addressing not only physical but also cognitive needs.
A range of strategies has been developed and tested to prevent falls in older adults with cognitive impairment. However, the effectiveness of these strategies can vary depending on the implementation context, the involvement of caregivers, and the cognitive state of the participants. Implementation strategies are important for effectively facilitating fall prevention interventions in community-dwelling people. Fall prevention strategies require structured approaches to incorporate prevention programs into daily life. To be effective, such strategies require a multifaceted approach that includes education, environmental modifications, and individualized care plans. Studies have demonstrated the success of implementation strategies in reducing the rate of falls and the risk of falls in community-dwelling, cognitively healthy older people. , However, such studies rarely assess determinants or propose appropriate strategies, particularly for older adults with cognitive impairment. , , 
This systematic review aims to describe the implementation strategies used for fall prevention interventions in community-dwelling older people living with cognitive impairment in order to identify a comprehensive approach for preventing falls in this group of people.

## ELIGIBILITY CRITERIA
This review considered the following eligibility criteria. (1) Community-dwelling older people living with cognitive impairment. Community-dwelling is defined as not living in long-term care, residential or institutional settings, retirement homes, hospital, or in-patient settings. For this review specifically, older people are defined as people aged 50 years or above, from low- and middle-income countries. This is because people from those countries are known to have a shorter life expectancy. Cognitive impairment is considered present if it was identified through a clinical diagnosis, assessment using a validated tool, and/or a clear description by study authors. (2) Fall prevention intervention studies (both single- and multi-factorial interventions) that compare the intervention with any comparators or usual care. (3) Primary studies with experimental and quasi-experimental studies, feasibility studies of randomized controlled trials (RCTs), action research, qualitative studies, mixed methods studies, and pre-post studies with detailed descriptions of intervention implementation and implementation strategies. Systematic reviews, protocol papers, editorials, opinions, commentaries, and conference papers were excluded.

## METHODS
We followed the JBI methodology for systematic reviews of effectiveness and used the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement to report this review. No ethical approval was required, as systematic reviews do not involve human participants. This review was conducted in accordance with an a priori protocol and has been registered in PROSPERO (CRD4202454689).

## Search strategy
A three-step search strategy was developed with the help of a subject librarian. First, an initial limited search of MEDLINE (EBSCOhost) and CINAHL (EBSCOhost) was undertaken to identify articles on the topic. The text words contained in the titles and abstracts of relevant articles, and the index terms used to describe the articles, were used to develop a full search strategy for MEDLINE (EBSCOhost), CINAHL Ultimate (EBSCOhost), PsycINFO (EBSCOhost), Scopus, and Web of Science Core Collection. A sample search strategy is presented in Table . The search strategy, including all identified keywords and index terms, was adapted for each included database. A systematic search was conducted on May 16, 2024. The reference lists of all included studies were also searched for any additional studies.
 The review only included studies in English (due to the linguistic limitations of the authors), published after the year 2000. This date limitation was imposed, as implementation science is relatively new and relevant data on implementation strategies may not be available prior to 2000. 

## Study screening and selection
Following the search, all identified citations were collated and uploaded into EndNote (Clarivate Analytics, PA, USA) and duplicates were removed. All reviewers performed title and abstract screening of articles using Rayyan software (Qatar Computing Research Institute, Doha, Qatar). Reviewers (SG, PC, CB, KW, RN, SK) assessed eligible full-text articles in detail against the inclusion criteria. The reasons for exclusion of papers at full text that did not meet the eligibility criteria were recorded and reported in the systematic review. Any disagreements that arose between the reviewers at each stage of the selection process were resolved through discussion and/or by the third reviewer (DP).

## Critical appraisal
Eligible studies were divided among the reviewers (SG, PC, CB, KW, RN, SK) for quality appraisal. JBI critical appraisal checklists were used to appraise the quality of studies included in this review. The checklist for quasi-experimental studies consists of a set of 9 questions, while the checklist for RCTs consists of 13 questions. Each study was reviewed against the relevant JBI checklist and each question in the checklist was scored as Y (Yes), N (No), U (Unclear), or Not Applicable (NA). The sum of all scores across the checklist items (total score) was then used as an indicator of a study's overall methodological quality (see Appendix I). Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved with a third reviewer (DP) and/or through discussion. All studies were included in the review regardless of their quality, as quasi-experimental and quality improvement papers are likely to provide useful implementation data. 

## Data extraction and synthesis
A data extraction form was developed by the review team based on the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis to collect relevant information. Studies included in the review were divided between the reviewers (SG, PC, CB, KW, RN, SK) for data extraction. Extracted data included information on publication details (author, publication year, and country), study aim and design, participants (study population and sample size), intervention characteristics, implementation strategies, and outcomes (implementation and falls-related outcomes). Any discrepancies that arose were resolved through discussion.
One reviewer (RN) compiled all the extracted data in a single table. Synthesis of data related to implementation strategies and implementation outcomes, which were reported and presented in narrative format. The Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) framework was used to identify, define, and categorize the implementation strategies from the included studies. The ERIC framework is a seminal framework for implementation strategies and facilitates the standardization and understanding of such strategies. The framework consists of 73 defined strategies, clustered into 9 categories. The implementation strategies from all individual studies were coded into categories and characterized using the labels and definitions for the 73 distinct strategies. These were then synthesized under the 9 categories of the ERIC Framework. The implementation outcomes were reported using Proctor's implementation taxonomy. 

## RESULTS

## Study inclusion
From the total of 893 studies retrieved from the database search, 390 duplicate articles were removed. Of the 503 studies screened for titles and abstracts, 50 eligible studies were included for full-text review. After the elimination of full-text articles, a total of 21 studies was included in the review. The study selection process is shown in Figure .
 

## Methodological quality
Quality appraisal was conducted at the study level using the JBI critical appraisal tools. , Each study design has a specific tool to assess quality criteria, and the number of questions in each tool is specific to the study design. The detailed quality appraisal results are shown in Appendix I.
Among the 11 RCTs included in the review, the scores ranged from 8/13 (n = 1) to 13/13 (n = 1). The lowest scores were reported for the questions on whether those delivering treatment were blinded to treatment assignment and whether the participants were blinded to treatment assignment (45.5%). It was also noted that treatment groups were treated identically other than the intervention of interest in all the studies (100%) included in the review. All studies reported that participants were analyzed in the groups to which they were randomized, that outcomes were measured in the same way for treatment groups, that outcomes were measured in a reliable way, and that appropriate statistical analysis was used.
The quasi-experimental studies ranged in score from 1/9 to 9/9, with five studies scoring 9/9 and two scoring 8/9. Only one study used a control group, while 90% of the studies clearly identified and described the cause and the effect. All studies reported that the participants included in any comparisons received similar treatment, that multiple outcomes were measured both pre and post the intervention, that follow-up was conducted, that outcomes were measured in a reliable way, and that appropriate statistical analysis methods were used.

## Implementation strategies used for fall prevention interventions
The review identified 24 implementation strategies used in fall prevention interventions, with 15 directly fitting into the ERIC list of strategies. Nine additional strategies were identified and added to the ERIC list. These included providing once-off training, providing language support, providing culturally sensitive interventions, providing transportation, playing music during the intervention, using instructors trained in dementia, providing training on how to communicate with people with dementia, using technology to deliver interventions, and using a motivational approach. The number of discrete implementation strategies per study varied from one to six. Overviews of the implementation strategies and outcomes grouped under the ERIC subheadings are shown in Tables and .
 With the exception of one study, all studies used multiple implementation strategies to support the delivery of fall prevention interventions. One study reported implementation strategies in five categories, three studies in four categories, , , ten studies in three categories, , , , , , , , , , five studies in two categories, , , , , and one study in one category. The most frequent categories of implementation strategy were: engaging consumers (n = 13); adapting and tailoring to context (n = 9); training and educating stakeholders (n = 8); and using evaluative and iterative strategies and providing interactive assistance (n = 5). The results are organized according to this order.

## Engaging consumers
The most common implementation strategy in this group was to involve the patient/consumer and their family members in implementing the fall prevention intervention (n = 13). In this category, nine studies reported involving family members or caregivers, who either supported, supervised, or facilitated the implementation of the fall prevention intervention. , , , , , , – In three studies, carers were also engaged in recording data related to the intervention at home, , , while in three other studies, caregivers were involved in monitoring and ensuring the safety of an exercise intervention (including the implementation of home safety recommendations). , , Two studies used the strategy to prepare patients/consumers to be active participants, such as conducting an orientation session on the intervention and an initial home visit to set up the program and train the patient and their caregiver on how to use the program. , One study used a motivational approach to change behavior (e.g., social support, improving knowledge, goal-setting, and self-monitoring strategies), while another involved identifying and using an exercise buddy—usually the caregiver—to assist with the exercise program. 

## Adapting and tailoring to context
Nine studies used the strategy of tailoring strategies to this group. , , , , , , , , Tailored strategies included tailoring or modifying the interventions and programs to meet individual needs and preferences (of both patients and caregivers), including functional mobility requirements. Six new strategies outside the ERIC strategies were identified under this group. Two studies used instructors who had received dementia training , as well as training on communication with people with dementia. One study used strategies such as providing language support, culturally sensitive interventions, and provide transportation. Playing music during the intervention, as chosen by the participants, was used in one study. 

## Train and educate stakeholders
The three most common implementation strategies in this category included distributing educational materials (n = 8), training the trainer (n = 5), and providing ongoing consultation and ongoing training (n = 4). Of the eight studies that distributed educational materials, the participants in seven of these studies received exercise booklets with illustrations and instructions. , , , , , , In one study, the participants also received a booklet containing all training records and appointments. In another study, the participants received a poster with a graphical description of the exercises and a comprehensive training manual to support autonomous training. One study provided participants with YouTube video links of the prescribed exercises. A train-the-trainer strategy was used in five studies, , , , , of which four studies , , , focused on training staff (e.g., clinicians or physiotherapists or instructors responsible for delivering the intervention) on exercise interventions. The aim was to equip them with the necessary knowledge and skills to deliver the exercise program. Another study used a train-the-trainer strategy, allowing a caregiver to practice under the supervision of a trained instructor. 
Four studies that provided ongoing support did so either via (1) home visits, telephone calls, and emails, (2) telephone calls, , (3) video conferences, and home visits or telephone calls. This motivated and supported the participants and their caregivers to maintain the exercises, ensure safety, and check upon any issues with the intervention. Four studies also provided participants with ongoing training on the interventions, either via online or face-to-face classes to ensure implementation of the intervention. , , , A once-off training strategy was followed in two studies to train participants and caregivers on the use of the intervention. , Two studies created a learning collaborative , by using certified experts to implement the intervention and carry out exercise sessions in a suitable venue. One study sought to make the training dynamic by explaining and demonstrating the intervention before the participants performed it under supervision, while another conducted educational outreach. 

## Using evaluative and iterative strategies and providing interactive assistance
The strategy of purposely re-examining the implementation was used in five studies , , , , to review progress of the interventions. This was done either via telephone calls, technology, or a monthly refresher group class. Four studies used a patient and family feedback strategy , , , in which feedback was sought from the participants on the intervention and any negative effects. One study used technology via a mobile app to collect feedback on the performance of the fall prevention intervention. 

## Providing interactive assistance
Five studies focused on technology (e.g., mobile apps, online classes, video conferences) to support the implementation of a fall prevention intervention. , , , , Three studies provided clinical supervision to support the implementation. , , Clinical supervision included the lead instructor observing the other instructor teaching a class, an expert clinical gero-psychologist supervising the training of an instructor, or an experienced instructor under the supervision of a Tai Chi instructor facilitating group sessions.

The reported implementation outcomes were identified and synthesized according to Proctor's implementation taxonomy, including fidelity, feasibility, adoption, acceptability, and appropriateness. Eight studies assessed adoption, , , , , , , , measured as the percentage of participants completing the intervention. Six studies reported the feasibility of the intervention. , , , , , Similarly, six studies evaluated the acceptability of the intervention, reported as positive feedback and satisfaction. , , , , , Two studies evaluated the appropriateness of the intervention, , reported as the suitability of the intervention. ## DISCUSSION
This systematic review reports on the implementation strategies used for fall prevention interventions in community-dwelling older people with cognitive impairment. The ERIC framework and Proctor's implementation taxonomy were used to identify and synthesize the strategies and outcomes used in the studies included in this review.
Our findings indicate that 24 implementation strategies have been used for fall prevention interventions across the included studies. Involving patients and family members was the most common strategy, highlighting the need to support family caregivers to ensure successful implementation. Moreover, family members were commonly involved in supporting intervention delivery, particularly in cases where individuals with cognitive impairment had difficulty using technology, remembering steps, or following complex instructions. Tailoring strategies to the interventions and adapting context was the second most used strategy. This included modifying the interventions and programs to meet individual needs and preferences, such as functional mobility requirements. In addition, training and education were frequently used implementation strategies in this review, which aligns with the findings of Albasha et al., who also reported that training and education were commonly used in nursing home settings. We believe that training is important for both the participants as well as their caregivers or family members, as many interventions are delivered either online or via apps.
This review also included strategies that did not align with those listed in the ERIC framework, namely, providing language support or culturally appropriate/sensitive interventions. As in one study, music chosen by participants was played during the exercise session, which may have helped them to engage in the intervention. Another study used instructors who had been trained in dealing and communicating with people with dementia. It is imperative to understand how to communicate with people with dementia, particularly those who have advanced dementia. Strategies to support vulnerable populations (such as people with dementia) need to be studied and included in the ERIC framework.
Furthermore, strategies used to implement the interventions (e.g., education, exercise training courses, health care support) were mostly experimental research to assess the effects of the intervention. This research consisted of RCTs or quasi-experimental studies. Nine of ten RCTs were single-blinded to the outcome assessors. However, the study participants were not blinded to the intervention group allocation, and they were considered compliant if they attended the intervention or if they performed the non-intervention. , , , – , – There was one double-blinded RCT in which participants were equally allocated to the intervention group and the control group using an urn design. 
Theories, models, and frameworks are for tailoring implementation strategies and evaluating implementation efforts. , Of the 21 studies, only four tailored their interventions to the context using a theory, model, or framework. Meyer et al. applied the Knowledge to Action Framework (KTA) and the Transtheoretical Model of Behavior Change (TTM) to the fall prevention strategies. , In a subsequent study, the KTA framework was used to assess knowledge translation regarding fall risk prevention and the adoption of strategies. The motivational approach in one study was carried out using theory-based behavioral change techniques. Additionally, Wesson et al. applied Allen's Cognitive Disabilities Model to tailor fall prevention interventions. The gap in reporting makes replication and translation difficult, as the principles guiding the tailoring are unknown. This gap was also reported by other authors. , Hence, future studies should include a clear description of the theoretical basis of the intervention, as this may affect the application and the evaluation of the fall prevention strategies.
Reporting the implementation outcomes differed across the included studies, possibly because there was no specific focus on reporting those outcomes. Fidelity was the most commonly reported implementation outcome, while outcomes such as sustainability, penetration, and intervention cost were not reported. This highlights the need for future research to consider the sustainability and cost-effectiveness of implementation strategies. Most studies reported an insignificant decrease in fall rates and fall risk post intervention. However, the studies did not report whether these outcomes were the effect of the implementation strategies used. Thus, the findings of our review highlight the lack of consistency in reporting implementation strategies and outcomes. Consequently, it is difficult to conclude what strategies work well for fall prevention when planning for a fall prevention program. Future research should also investigate the relationship between the implementation strategies and the implementation outcomes.
The findings of this review must be interpreted with caution due to possible bias in the reporting of implementation outcomes. This is because the studies in this review used different scales and/or instruments to measure and report implementation and fall outcomes. Reporting bias may have also occurred due to family members and/or caregivers reporting the implementation outcomes. Furthermore, the inclusion of all studies in this review, regardless of their quality, may have affected the overall findings. For example, the overall quality of studies included in the review was high, although there was poor quality relative to blinding participants and assessors in RCTs. This is because the lowest scores were reported for the questions on whether those delivering treatment were blinded to treatment assignment and whether the participants were blinded to treatment assignment.
This review also reflects the differences in implementation strategies before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Train-the-trainer strategies and using technology to deliver interventions were frequently used in studies published after 2019 (during and post-pandemic). Conversely, the use of strategies such as tailoring and involving patient/consumer and family members declined. This may well show that changes in human behavior and technological advancements have been key factors influencing the development and application of fall prevention interventions. In addition, most of the studies included in this review used multiple implementation strategies to support the delivery of fall prevention interventions, although the mode and frequency of delivery of the strategies varied across the studies. However, none of the studies included reported the effect of multiple strategies used to support fall prevention outcomes, clearly highlighting the need for future studies to examine this area.
In addition to the recommendations discussed above, the findings of this review will be used to develop a sustainable fall prevention intervention for cognitively impaired community-dwelling older people living in Mahasarakham province in Thailand. This will involve the development of a protocol and program to reduce the risk and incidence of falls. Careful consideration will need to be given to the implementation strategies, as the program will be in a rural area of a developing country. For future implementation efforts, implementation strategies deployed in the real world should focus on comprehensive contextual planning, ongoing engagement of stakeholders throughout the implementation process, and ongoing evaluation and refinement of the strategies to ensure that they are appropriate to local needs and resource availability.

## Strengths and limitations
The strength of this study lies predominantly in its systematic and team-based approach to searching and analyzing the literature. The inclusion of numerous study designs ensured a more comprehensive perspective on fall prevention strategies. The degree of cognitive impairment varied in the study population, providing insights into the different stages of cognitive decline. Nonetheless, this review has several limitations. First, most studies were conducted in high-income countries, with the exception of one study which was conducted in Indonesia, an upper-middle-income country.
While the review focuses on fall prevention and associated strategies, most of the studies predominantly used exercise interventions. There was a lack of studies addressing policy-level interventions and other relevant factors, such as environmental support. Moreover, the absence of long-term follow-up in many studies makes it difficult to assess the sustainability of the interventions. Some studies relied on data from health care units, which may have led to selection bias. Moreover, some strategies may have overlapped due to unclear guidelines on assigning ERIC strategies. It is also not possible to determine whether the implementation outcomes were determined by the implementation strategies or the intervention itself. Additionally, the included studies reported the biological sex but not the gender of the participants. These details were not reported in the findings of this review. This limitation may affect the interpretation and application of the outcomes of the implementation strategies, particularly across demographic groups. Future studies should include both biological sex and gender in demographic reporting to ensure a more comprehensive understanding of the findings across diverse populations.

## CONCLUSIONS
This study presents the implementation strategies used for fall prevention interventions in community-dwelling older people with cognitive impairment. Several studies reported using multiple implementation strategies; however, none of them reported the effect of these strategies on outcomes. Engaging patients/consumers and family members was a commonly used implementation strategy to prevent falls. Several new strategies were also identified outside of ERIC list, including providing language support and culturally appropriate interventions as well as using instructors trained in communicating with people with dementia. This review also identified variations in the reporting of implementation strategies and outcomes. Therefore, future studies should focus on clear reporting of the strategies and outcomes to provide a better understanding of the effect of these strategies.
