## Abstract

## Background
Intrinsic capacity decline (the age-related reduction in the composite of physical and mental capacities) is prevalent among Chinese rural older adults.This study aimed to describe the perceptions of intrinsic capacity decline and the corresponding intervention needs among older adults residing in a rural village in northern China.

## Methods
A descriptive qualitative study was conducted. Semi-structured interviews were used to collect detailed accounts of the lived experiences of intrinsic capacity decline among 14 older adults in a rural Chinese village. Data were collected from September to December 2023 and were analyzed using NVivo 12 software. Inductive analysis was used to identify key themes.

## Results
Three main themes were identified: (1) Normalizing decline, which reflected participants’ acceptance of capacity loss as an inevitable part of aging, coupled with low awareness and perceived need for intervention; (2) The social embeddedness of health behaviors, highlighting how engagement in health practices was profoundly facilitated or hindered by family dynamics and peer networks; and (3) Navigating a support vacuum, underscoring the expressed need for accessible, credible information and professional guidance in the absence of systemic support.

## Conclusion
This study provides a detailed description of the multifaceted challenges faced by older adults in this specific rural northern Chinese setting. Participants described a process of intrinsic capacity decline that was often normalized due to a lack of knowledge, deeply influenced by social relationships, and occurred within a context of scarce systemic support. The findings underscore the necessity for interventions that are responsive to the described personal, social, and systemic contexts.

Keywords: Social-ecological systems theory, Rural older adults, Intrinsic capacity, Need, Qualitative research

## Introduction
By the end of 2023, the number of older adults with disability or partial disability in China had reached 45 million, with rural older adults accounting for 60.1% of this population [ ]. It is projected that by 2050, the number of rural older adults with disability or partial disability will reach 97.5 million [ ]. Enhancing and maintaining the intrinsic capacity of older adults is a key strategy for preventing and delaying disability and promoting healthy aging [ ]. Intrinsic capacity refers to the combination of all physical and mental abilities that an individual can utilize at any time, including cognitive, psychological, sensory functions, vitality, and activity across five domains, which can reflect the overall health status of the older adults [ ]. Results from a nationally representative sample survey indicate that the overall incidence rate of intrinsic capacity decline in China is 43%, and the incidence rate of intrinsic capacity decline among rural older adults is 1.36 times that of urban older adults [ ]. Due to factors such as urban-rural population mobility, a decline in family care capabilities, a decrease in economic purchasing power, and insufficient service provision, the care for disabled older adults in rural areas faces severe challenges [ ]. In rural China, aging is advancing quickly, with many older adults living alone or as “empty nesters.” Access to health services remains limited compared to cities, and primary care resources are often scarce. There are also few health programs focused on maintaining intrinsic capacity. These conditions directly influence how older adults experience and perceive decline in their abilities.Therefore, developing effective strategies to prevent and delay the disability of rural older adults is an urgent issue in the context of healthy aging.
Health promotion behaviors—including cognitive training, physical exercise, social interaction, and nutrition—effectively address intrinsic capacity decline, improving overall health and preventing disability [ , ]. Yet, empirical interventions targeting intrinsic capacity remain scarce in China, where older adult care in both community and rural settings continues to focus on disease management [ ]. To date, no study has provided a detailed description of rural older adults’ own perceptions of intrinsic capacity decline and their corresponding intervention needs. Qualitative methods are particularly suited to capture authentic rural older adults’ perceptions and to describe their intervention needs within specific cultural contexts. This study employed a descriptive qualitative design to achieve a comprehensive summary of the experiences and needs of rural older adults regarding intrinsic capacity decline, with minimal researcher interpretation. Conducted in a single rural village in northern China, this study offers an in-depth, context-specific exploration rather than a nationally representative overview. This approach aligns with the study’s aim to develop a detailed description of intervention needs from the participants’ own perspectives, supporting the design of culturally appropriate and practical interventions.
The Social-Ecological Systems Theory (SEST) provides a robust conceptual framework for understanding complex health behaviors within their environmental contexts, which emphasizes that individuals are nested within a series of interrelated environmental systems (such as family, community, society), and categorizes these system environments into microsystems, mesosystems, and macrosystems [ ]. Given the limited understanding of rural older adults’ own views on intrinsic capacity decline, a descriptive qualitative design was employed to systematically gather and provide a straightforward description of their perspectives and needs. This approach is well-suited to provide a comprehensive account of the phenomenon as experienced by the participants themselves. Following data analysis, the Social-Ecological Systems Theory was utilized as an interpretive lens to further understand and organize the emergent findings within a broader contextual framework.

## Methods

## Study design
This study employed a descriptive qualitative design. This approach aims to provide a rich, straightforward description of an experience or phenomenon using participants’ own words and perspectives, with minimal researcher interpretation. It is particularly useful for summarizing the “what” and “how” of an under-researched topic [ ]. In-depth, semi-structured interviews were conducted to capture the participants’ subjective perceptions and meanings. This study sought to identify and describe multidimensional intervention needs as expressed by the participants. The descriptive approach allowed us to prioritize the participants’ lived realities and their own accounts of their experiences.

## Sampling and recruitment of participants
A purposive sampling strategy was employed to ensure the selection of participants who could provide rich, relevant, and diverse insights into the lived experience of intrinsic capacity decline, consistent with the descriptive aims of the study.The study was carried out in Daqing, China, from September to December 2023. For the purpose of this study, “rural” was defined in accordance with China’s National Bureau of Statistics classification, referring to areas with agricultural-based economies, lower population density, and administrative designation as rural (non-urban) communities.We used purposive sampling to select older adults from Dongfeng Village. This village was selected because it possesses the characteristic features of a typical rural community(including relatively limited healthcare resources, where there’s just one village clinic with two health workers serving about 1,500 people, around 50% of working-age adults have left for jobs elsewhere, leaving many older adults behind, most households also rely on farming, which accounts for over 60% of their income.), making it representative of the target population under investigation. Inclusion criteria: ① Local permanent residents (registered as rural) who had lived continuously in the village for at least 5 years; ② Age ≥ 60 years; ③ Able to communicate effectively, as screened by the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) with a score ≥ 24, and without severe hearing or speech impairments; ④Provided informed consent and voluntarily participated in the study. Exclusion criteria: People with serious physical or mental illnesses were excluded if their condition would significantly limit interview participation or make it hard to distinguish disease effects from age-related decline.
The sample size and group proportions were guided by thematic saturation, in line with Guest et al.’s recommendations [ ]. The rigor and depth of the study were enhanced through several strategies. First, participant selection intentionally accounted for heterogeneity in key demographic characteristics (e.g., age, gender, marital status, education) as well as in living arrangements and health status. This involved purposively sampling both older adults co-residing with children and those living alone, and including individuals with chronic conditions as well as those in relatively good health. Second, an iterative approach was adopted, where data collection and analysis were conducted concurrently. Finally, a reflexive stance was maintained throughout the research process. All formal interviews were conducted using a semi-structured interview guide, which was developed in advance and piloted prior to the study. After 14 interviews, thematic saturation was reached as no new codes or themes were identified, and consensus was reached between coders that further interviews were unlikely to yield additional insights. Accordingly, 14 interviews were included in the final analysis.

## Ethics and data protection
This study received approval from the Ethics Review Committee of Harbin Medical University (HMUDQ20231116110) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after detailed explanation of study procedures. Comprehensive measures were implemented to ensure data confidentiality. All participants were assigned pseudonyms during transcription. Electronic data were stored on password-protected encrypted servers, while physical documents were secured in locked cabinets accessible only to authorized researchers. Specific protections were established for older participants, including: 1)explicitly emphasizing voluntary participation and unconditional withdrawal rights, and 2) continuous monitoring for emotional distress during interviews by a qualified psychotherapist, with established protocols for session management and support provision. To uphold methodological rigor, the study complied with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ), ensuring transparency in the reporting of the qualitative data [ ].

## Data collection
Data collection was conducted from September to December 2023, with individual interviews scheduled based on participants’ availability. All participants were initially contacted by phone to discuss the study’s purpose, significance, and procedures. Prior to each session, a suitable venue was arranged at either a local health clinic or village activity room. The semi-structured interview guide (Table ) was developed through a comprehensive process: it was initially drafted based on a literature review, then refined through consultations with two experts in geriatric nursing and qualitative research, and finally pilot-tested with two rural older adults to assess clarity, relevance, and cultural appropriateness. The interviewer, a qualified psychotherapist who had received specialized training in qualitative research methods, conducted all sessions in Chinese using this guide. The interview guide was developed to broadly explore participants’ experiences, understandings, and needs related to intrinsic capacity decline, aiming to elicit detailed descriptions from the participants. Throughout data collection, significant efforts were made to ensure cultural and contextual appropriateness. Interviews were conducted in the local Northeast Chinese dialect using culturally resonant terminology.
 Each interview followed a standardized protocol: the researcher began by reintroducing themselves and reiterating the study objectives before commencing the one-on-one, face-to-face session. All interviews were audio-recorded with complementary field notes taken to document non-verbal behaviors including gestures and facial expressions. The average interview duration was approximately 30–40 min per participant. In cases where participants showed emotional distress, the interview was temporarily paused with appropriate support provided, and continuation was determined by the participant’s preference. Following each interview, contact information was exchanged for subsequent member checking of transcribed materials, and participants were thanked for their contribution. This systematic approach ensured both methodological consistency and ethical integrity throughout the data collection period.

## Data analysis
The data were analyzed using inductive content analysis as described by Elo and Kyngäs [ ], which is consistent with the descriptive qualitative approach. The process involved: (1) Repeated reading of transcripts to achieve immersion; (2) Open coding of the text by highlighting meaning units; (3) Grouping codes into subcategories based on similarities and differences; (4) Abstracting subcategories into broader categories and main themes through discussion and consensus among the research team. The analysis focused on remaining close to the participants’ descriptions and organizing the data into a descriptive summary of their experiences and needs. NVivo 12 software was used to manage the data.
To ensure the rigor and trustworthiness of the qualitative analysis, several measures were implemented throughout the research process. First, all interview recordings were transcribed verbatim by two research team members, and the transcripts were cross-checked for accuracy. Second, the data analysis was conducted independently by two researchers who were trained in qualitative methods. They regularly discussed and reconciled any discrepancies in coding and theme development until a consensus was reached. When persistent discrepancies remained, a third researcher was consulted for adjudication. Third, a process of member checking was employed to validate the findings. A summary of the preliminary themes and interpretations was returned to six participants who were available and willing to provide feedback. Their comments and confirmations were incorporated into the final analysis to enhance the credibility of the results. The absence of requests for substantial revisions during member checking serves as evidence that the analysis accurately captured the participants’ perspectives.

## Researcher qualifications and reflexivity
The principal researcher, who conducted the interviews and led the qualitative analysis, is a qualified psychotherapist holding a Ph.D. in Nursing. She has completed specialized training in qualitative research methods, including workshops on qualitative inquiry and interviewing techniques. Prior to this study, the researcher had conducted two qualitative projects, demonstrating established competence in participant recruitment, in-depth interviewing, thematic analysis, and maintaining methodological consistency throughout the research process.
Researcher reflexivity was actively maintained throughout the study. As healthcare professionals in gerontological nursing, we acknowledge that our clinical background may influence data interpretation. To mitigate potential bias, all researchers maintained reflective journals to document preconceptions, used open-ended questions during interviews to avoid leading responses, and held regular team debriefings to critically examine how professional perspectives might shape analysis. These practices helped balance our academic stance with respect for participants’ lived experiences.

## Results

## Participant characteristics
This study included 14 participants from rural communities. The sample (N = 14) had a mean age of 71.79 (SD = 7.14) and included 6 males and 8 females. Eleven participants were married and 3 were widowed. Educational levels were primarily low, with 8 participants having primary school education or less, 5 having junior secondary, and 1 having senior secondary education. Eight participants had chronic diseases, and 6 did not. Regarding living arrangements, 5 co-resided with their adult children, while 9 did not.Data collection continued until theoretical saturation was achieved, which occurred after the 12th interview, as no new codes or themes emerged in the subsequent two interviews. This ensured that the identified themes adequately captured the range of perceptions and needs among the study population. Table  provides a detailed overview of their demographic profile.
 

## Themes
The analysis revealed a detailed description of the lived experience of intrinsic capacity decline as reported by the participants. The core of this experience was a sense of progressive loss that was often normalized yet permeated all aspects of their lives. Our findings are structured to reflect how this core experience manifests across different levels of the individual’s social ecology, presenting three overarching themes: (1) Normalizing Decline: A Lack of Awareness and Low Perceived Need; (2) The Social Embeddedness of Health Behaviors; and (3) Navigating a Support Vacuum: The Quest for Reliable Information and Guidance. The inter-rater reliability for the initial coding, measured using Cohen’s Kappa, was 0.82, indicating a high level of agreement (Fig. ).
 

## Theme 1: normalizing decline: a lack of awareness and low perceived need
This theme describes the individual-level barriers reported by participants, highlighting a fundamental lack of understanding about intrinsic capacity and a consequent lack of motivation for preventive actions.

## Sub-theme 1.1: misinterpreting decline as inevitable aging
Participants universally described a lack of awareness regarding the concept of intrinsic capacity, often interpreting its manifestations as an inevitable part of aging. As P7 stated: “Getting old means always having some ache or pain; we simply accept it as our lot.” This was compounded by limited literacy and access to information, as P1 noted, “I don’t know about this. I haven’t read books, I’m not literate.“Some participants, like P2, dismissed the concept entirely: “What is this ‘decline’? If I can eat, drink, and move around, why should I worry about it? Isn’t that just causing myself trouble?” P4: “For us older adults who haven’t had much schooling, understand little of what’s on TV, and no one explains things to us, we don’t know anything about our health and healthcare knowledge.” P14: “What is the decline in intrinsic capacity? I’ve never heard of this before. Is it just getting old? Not being able to walk?” P7: “This is what happens when you get old; it’s either discomfort here or there.”
Incorrect beliefs about etiology were common. Some attributed it to specific diseases. Older person P9 and P12 attribute the decline to diseases; P9: “This condition is related to the shrinkage of the cerebellum, right?” P12: “This condition must be related to high blood pressure. When the blood pressure is high, one becomes confused, can’t speak clearly, and can’t walk properly.” P3 and P6 believe that intrinsic capacity is related to psychology; P3: “People with a narrow mind and unsociable personality get sick easily when they encounter problems.” P6: “With my children not around, I get worried over every little cold or headache, and the psychological stress is high.” P5 attributes the decline to family life events: “The family has been unsettled in recent years, with my spouse’s poor health and frequent hospitalizations, which cost a lot of money, and then my grandson got sick. So, my decline in intrinsic capacity is purely from worry.” P2: “It’s mainly about personal quality. Those with poor physical quality tend to suffer from such conditions.”
The potential adverse outcomes were largely minimized by participants. The decline in intrinsic capacity has not received sufficient attention, and there is a lack of clear understanding of its consequences. P5: “This isn’t a big deal, we rural folks don’t have high demands, as long as we can eat and drink.” P7: “What impact does this (decline in intrinsic capacity) have? We’re old, and there aren’t any important things to do, so it’s fine if our bodies are a bit worse off.” P11: “It’s nothing, it doesn’t affect work, no need to worry about it, it might get better after some time.” P13: “I do feel that my body isn’t as good as before, but let it be, I don’t want to be a burden to my children, just muddle along.”

## Sub-theme 1.2: fatalistic attitudes and vague notions of prevention
A weak sense of urgency regarding intrinsic capacity prevention was prevalent among participants, coupled with vague or incorrect knowledge about effective health-promoting strategies.Fatalistic attitudes were common.“When a person gets old, it’s easy to have all sorts of problems; how can you prevent it? This thing can’t be prevented.” P7: “As you get older, some health issues may naturally arise, which is hard to completely avoid.” P3: “At such an advanced age, the body’s parts are all worn out; there’s no need to worry about it.”
Vague Notions of Prevention, some acknowledged that activities like exercise could be beneficial, their understanding was non-specific. P8 noted,“Exercising should be able to improve this intrinsic capacity, but how exactly should one exercise?” P7: “The general public doesn’t know much about these things; maintaining good living habits is enough. As for the specifics, I’m not very clear either.” P11: “I like to play cards for fun, I don’t know if it’s good for my health.”
Lack of access to credible information was described. P4 explained, “To be honest, in rural areas, few people understand these contents (prevention of intrinsic capacity decline); most people are not very concerned or knowledgeable about it.” P2: “I think it should be preventable, like regularly moving around and chatting with others. It would be nice if there was a senior activity room. Our large village doesn’t even have one such place. If you want to find a place to exercise your legs, there’s simply nowhere to go.”
These individual-level knowledge gaps and fatalistic attitudes were frequently contextualized within mesosystem and macrosystem deficiencies as described by the participants. Participants often attributed their lack of knowledge to the absence of community health initiatives (macrosystem) and limited discussions with family or peers who were equally uninformed (mesosystem).

## Theme 2: the social embeddedness of health behaviors
At this level, the findings describe how family and peers directly influence the health behaviors of rural older adults, serving as either facilitators or barriers according to participant accounts.

## Sub-theme 2.1: family dynamics: facilitation and burdens
Family members played a critical role in encouraging or hindering healthy practices, as reported by participants. In some cases, family provided positive facilitation. P5 described how her children actively supported her: “My kids always say I’m too fat and keep an eye on me, making sure I take a walk after eating. At first I resisted, but now I appreciate their concern.” P6: “My spouse used to cook with a lot of oil and preferred strong flavors, liking to add a lot of salt and sugar. Since I was diagnosed with this condition, we both started to eat more lightly.” P6: “My spouse passed away several years ago, and my children can’t be around all the time, so I just make do with meals.” P5: “My kids always say I’m too fat and keep an eye on me, making sure I take a walk after eating.” Some older adults described lacking social support and facing difficulties in promoting health behaviors.
Caregiving responsibilities could impede healthy behaviors. P3 reported, “I used to love dancing, but now that my grandson is with me and my son and daughter-in-law are out working, I really don’t have the time to dance anymore, I have to take care of the child every day.”

## Sub-theme 2.2: peer influence: companionship and loneliness
The presence of peers provided motivation and opportunity for engaging in health-promoting activities, while their absence led to isolation, as described by participants. Companionship was a key motivator. P14 said, “I go for a walk with my neighbors every night after dinner, we usually walk for about an hour or more, and if I don’t go for a walk on a particular day, I feel like something is missing.” P12: “In my daily life, I often ask my neighbors for basic health knowledge because I can’t ask a doctor at any time.”
Loneliness and Lack of Peers were commonly reported. P9 expressed,“There are too few older adults around me, either they have passed away or moved to the city, and I feel very lonely.” P2: “My old neighbors and friends, the ones I used to chat with every day, have all faded away over the past couple of years—some fell ill, others moved away. Now, inside and outside the house, I’m left alone, talking to walls, my words just echoing off them. I can’t even gather a group of four to play a hand of cards anymore.”
The influence of family and peers was not isolated but was often moderated by macrosystem factors as described by participants. For example, the outmigration of adult children (a macrosystem demographic trend) directly shaped family dynamics and available support, while the lack of community activity spaces (a macrosystem resource gap) limited opportunities for peer companionship.

## Theme 3: navigating a support vacuum: the quest for reliable information and guidance
This theme describes the participants’ expressed needs for support from broader community structures and healthcare systems, particularly in terms of accessible, credible, and practical information.

## Sub-theme 3.1: insufficient community-level health promotion
Participants reported a notable absence of organized health promotion initiatives tailored to their needs. There was a strong desire for community-led efforts. P2 suggested,“I think there should be more dissemination and promotion of this knowledge, so that the older adults can understand better how to improve their intrinsic capacity and reduce the burden on their children.” P11: “Of course, I want to know, at this age, good health is more important than anything else. Life is so good now, I want to live a few more years.”
Participants expressed willingness to follow professional advice. P7 stated, “If someone could freely tell us how to do it, we would follow it, and if it’s good for our health, it’s better than taking medicine.” P1: “If only the doctor would come to our village and show us what to do with their own hands, even if it’s just a few words of advice, I would definitely follow them. It’s surely better than groping in the dark by myself and taking medicines at random”. P5: “If only someone could professionally tell us precisely what to eat and how much to move, without us having to waste our hard-earned money, we old folks would certainly listen. What old person wouldn’t want to add a few more years to their life?”

## Sub-theme 3.2: the need for professional and tailored guidance
A clear distrust of generic or online information was evident, with a preference for guidance from healthcare professionals that was personalized to their specific circumstances. Participants valued credible sources. P1 commented on online information: “There are all sorts of things said online, I don’t know if they’re true or not.” P8 affirmed, “It’s more believable and reliable when a doctor of a major hospital speaks. P10: “It would be ideal if professional doctors could provide us with more information and help.”
A clear distrust of generic or online information was evident, with a preference for guidance from healthcare professionals that was personalized to their specific circumstances. P12 emphasized: “It would be best if someone could give on-site guidance, like how to exercise specifically for my high blood pressure. The village doctor only says ‘exercise more’ but never shows me how!” P5, who had leg problems, asked, “Can you tell me what kind of exercise I can do? For example, just sitting there and stretching my arms and legs, does that count as exercise?” Similarly, P12 wanted “on-site guidance, what kind of exercise is suitable for me with high blood pressure? The more detailed, the better.” P5: “It would be best if they could provide some suggestions based on our personal circumstances. I also know that exercise is important, but I have leg problems and can’t do intense exercises. Can you tell me what kind of exercise I can do? P11: “I like to play with walnuts in my hand, does that count as exercise, is it good for my health? Sometimes I don’t know what to do that is beneficial for my health.”

## Sub-theme 3.3: preferred channels for information dissemination
Participants identified a variety of desired formats for receiving health information. Traditional, interpersonal methods were favored. P5 suggested, “If someone from the village team could guide us every day, telling us how to eat and exercise, that would be great.” P2: “I think the village should promote more through broadcasts and such, which would be helpful to us older adults.” P3: “It’s still TV, everyone has a TV, and anyway, we usually watch TV when we have nothing to do.”
Use of Digital Technology was mentioned by some participants. P7: “I find mobile phones very convenient, I can watch them while lying on the kang (traditional Chinese bed).“P11: “It was my son who bought me this smartphone. At first, I only knew how to answer calls. But now, I can scan QR codes, check the health information group, and even lie in bed at night listening to doctors’ live streams about how to manage blood sugar. It’s incredibly convenient.”P3༚“Even with my poor eyesight, I just set the font to its largest size. Then, propped up against the kang, I scroll through short videos to learn how to massage my knees. It’s a whole lot easier than going to town to register for a hospital appointment.”
The expressed needs at the macrosystem level, such as for professional guidance, were direct responses to the identified gaps at the micro- and mesosystems as described by participants. The demand for tailored information, for example, stemmed from the recognition that generic advice failed to address individual health conditions (micro) and could not be effectively mediated through existing social networks (meso).

## Discussion
This descriptive qualitative study provides a detailed account of how rural older adults in China perceive intrinsic capacity decline and what support they need. Our findings describe a process that is often normalized due to a lack of knowledge, deeply influenced by social relationships, and occurring within a context of scarce systemic support. The three themes—“Normalizing Decline,” “The Social Embeddedness of Health Behaviors,” and “Navigating a Support Vacuum”—collectively describe a complex landscape of challenges.
The pervasive and limited understanding of intrinsic capacity among rural older adults, as described in our findings, particularly regarding its manifestations, etiology, and risk factors, reflects more than an individual knowledge gap—it signifies a systemic public health challenge. This phenomenon is rooted in a constellation of interacting factors. While low national awareness of intrinsic capacity is acknowledged [ ], the situation is markedly exacerbated in rural communities due to educational disparities and restricted access to credible health information [ ]. Crucially, the reliance on informal networks and unverified online sources for health information creates a precarious knowledge ecosystem where misinformation can thrive, further widening the gap between evidence-based practice and lay understanding [ ].
This knowledge deficit is compounded by the dynamic and often ambiguous nature of health science itself. As health recommendations continually evolve [ ], individuals with limited health literacy face significant challenges in discerning reliable information. While electronic health tools present a promising avenue for disseminating accurate information [ ], their effectiveness is contingent upon overcoming digital literacy barriers and the entrenched distrust of non-interpersonal sources in rural older adults. Furthermore, competing health priorities and familial responsibilities often relegate proactive health measures, such as intrinsic capacity promotion, to a secondary position [ ]. This suggests that interventions must not only address knowledge dissemination but also reframe intrinsic capacity as an integral component of overall health management, rather than a separate and optional endeavor.
The promotion of health behaviors emerges from this study not merely as a clinical recommendation but as a socially embedded process whose success depends critically on the mobilization of relational resources within the mesosystem. While the efficacy of health behaviors in enhancing intrinsic capacity is well-established [ ], our findings reveal that rural older adults’ engagement with such behaviors is fundamentally mediated by social structures. The identified needs for family involvement and peer companionship transcend simple “support” functions—they constitute the very ecosystem in which health behaviors acquire meaning and sustainability [ ]. This insight demands a critical re-examination of interventions that prioritize individual education without concurrently strengthening these social relays.
Social Cognitive Theory provides a valuable framework for understanding this dynamic, yet our data suggest its application in rural geriatric contexts requires greater nuance [ ]. The theory’s dual focus on personal and environmental factors proves particularly apt: the participants’ limited awareness of specific exercise methods reflects not just an information gap but a failure of the social learning environment [ ]. When older adults predominantly learn through informal observation and word-of-mouth, as our participants reported, the narrow range of their physical activities—typically limited to walking [ ]—becomes understandable. This represents a critical implementation gap between knowing that activity is beneficial and knowing how to engage in safe, effective, and varied exercises appropriate for declining intrinsic capacity.
The transformative potential of peer influence emerges as a particularly promising avenue for intervention [ ]. However, simply organizing group activities misses the deeper implication of our findings: the power of peers lies not only in providing companionship but in creating what might be termed “behavioral legitimacy.” When health behaviors are visibly practiced and endorsed by social counterparts, they transition from clinical prescriptions to culturally validated practices. This social authentication process may be especially crucial in populations where trust in external authorities is limited [ ].
Similarly, family influence operates through mechanisms more complex than simple reminders or supervision [ ]. Our data indicate that family members who understand IC decline can help reframe health behaviors not as additional burdens but as shared investments in maintaining intergenerational independence. This represents a shift from surveillance to collective efficacy—a distinction with profound implications for intervention design [ ]. Future programs should therefore aim not merely to “educate” family members but to position them as co-architects of sustainable behavioral change.
Ultimately, these findings challenge the conventional wisdom that behavior change proceeds from knowledge to attitude to practice. In the social ecology of rural aging, practice often emerges through relational reinforcement, with knowledge and attitude following thereafter [ ]. This suggests that the most effective interventions may be those that embed health behaviors within existing social structures while simultaneously building the knowledge framework to sustain them—a dual approach that honors the complexity of health behavior as both individual choice and social practice.
The identified need for professional guidance and behavioral companionship among rural older adults reflects fundamental limitations in existing healthcare systems’ capacity to address intrinsic capacity promotion. While cognitive behavioral interventions show theoretical promise in modifying health behaviors [ ], their conventional application remains constrained by individual-focused paradigms that fail to integrate the social and environmental supports necessary for sustained change. This limitation is particularly evident in rural contexts, where the absence of continuous professional support and culturally appropriate delivery mechanisms leads to rapid decline in intervention effectiveness [ ]. The challenge thus extends beyond mere knowledge transfer to creating institutional frameworks that can provide ongoing, context-sensitive support through integrated care models.
Current group health management approaches, though demonstrating efficacy in structured settings [ ], reveal a critical paradox when implemented in rural communities. The professionally dominated model inherently creates dependency relationships that undermine autonomous motivation, while simultaneously failing to leverage existing social networks that could provide natural sustainability [ ]. This structural mismatch is compounded by the shortage of healthcare resources in rural areas, where professional-led programs face practical barriers to scalability and long-term maintenance [ ]. To address these limitations, future interventions must reconceptualize group models to incorporate distributed leadership structures and community-based resources, creating hybrid systems that blend professional expertise with local social capital.
Transformative approaches should focus on developing multi-level support systems that synergistically combine health coaching techniques [ , ] with strengthened community infrastructure. The strategic grouping of participants by geographical proximity and health conditions, coupled with the empowerment of peer leaders, represents a promising direction for creating self-sustaining health promotion networks [ ]. However, to achieve scalable impact, these micro-level interventions must be supported by macro-level institutional innovations—including integrated care policies that recognize intrinsic capacity promotion as essential preventive care, funding mechanisms that support community health worker programs, and digital health platforms that extend professional reach while maintaining local relational support [ ]. Only through such systemic integration can we effectively address the dual challenge of meeting individual intervention needs while building sustainable rural health ecosystems capable of supporting active aging across diverse contexts.

## Implications for aging practice and policies
This study provides concrete implications for formulating effective practices and policies to support healthy aging in rural communities, based on the detailed descriptions provided by participants. At the practical level, multidimensional assessment tools should be developed, peer group activities integrating social and health components should be established, and family caregivers should be trained to recognize early declines in intrinsic capacity. At the policy level, it is essential to integrate intrinsic capacity promotion as a core component of community health programs, prioritize the construction of rural community activity centers, and incentivize interdisciplinary collaboration to build comprehensive support networks. These recommendations, derived from the actual needs of older adults, offer a roadmap for establishing a responsive support system that preserves intrinsic capacity and promotes healthy aging.

## Limitations and implications for future research
This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the research was conducted in a single village in Daqing City, China. Although thematic saturation was achieved, the sample size was relatively small and geographically restricted, which may limit the transferability of the findings to other rural areas with different socioeconomic or cultural backgrounds. The core challenges identified may be applicable to other Chinese rural areas facing similar macro-level factors. Second, as a qualitative study based on self-reported data, the findings are subject to potential recall and social desirability biases and are not intended for statistical generalization.Additionally, we acknowledge that Question 5 in the interview guide may have been somewhat leading, which could have influenced participants’ responses regarding their perceptions of health behaviors. Notwithstanding these constraints, the research offers foundational insights into intervention needs related to intrinsic capacity decline among rural older adults, as described by the participants themselves. Future work should prioritize the development of multi-component interventions grounded in the Social-Ecological Systems Theory, integrating health coaching and peer-support models. Subsequent studies are also needed to quantitatively validate these findings and evaluate the long-term effectiveness and cost-efficacy of such tailored approaches to inform public health strategies for healthy aging in rural China.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, this descriptive study outlines a clear set of challenges and needs related to intrinsic capacity decline from the perspective of rural older adults, based on their own accounts. To be effective, future interventions must simultaneously address the described lack of knowledge at the individual level, leverage the influential role of family and peers at the relational level, and establish accessible, professional support systems at the community level.
