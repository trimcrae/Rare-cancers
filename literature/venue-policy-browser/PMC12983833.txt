## Abstract

## Objectives
To compare clinical radiography training experiences (structure, resources, participation, feedback) and self-perceived competence/practice readiness between public and private radiography centres in Lagos State, Nigeria.

## Design
Comparative cross-sectional survey design from August to October 2025 using a validated self-administered questionnaire distributed in person during departmental seminars and clinical debriefings at University of Lagos-affiliated centres.

## Setting
Centre-based settings at public and private radiodiagnostic centres.

## Participants
A total of 260 final-year students and recent graduates, 130 each from public and private radiodiagnostic centres. Inclusion criteria included: age ≥18 years, with ≥6 months clinical exposure, from centres affiliated to the University of Lagos. All participants completed the self-administered questionnaire. There were no interventions.

## Primary and secondary outcome measures
The primary outcome was the self-perceived competence/practice readiness, and the secondary was participation, extent and feedback mechanisms, measured as planned without protocol deviations. All variables were measured using validated items in the questionnaire.

## Results
Private centres significantly outperformed public centres in hands-on practice and feedback, with higher self-perceived competence (mean 35.6±5.7 vs 32.8±6.4; p=0.001). There were no significant differences in training structure (p=0.78). Public centres reported higher patient loads (86.2% vs 68.5%; p=0.001) but lower equipment availability (47.7% vs 72.3%; p<0.001); while private centres reported higher participation (80.0% vs 74.6%; p=0.03), hands-on practice (76.2% vs 68.5%; p=0.009) and feedback (70.0% vs 60.0%; p=0.002). Centre type was independently associated with self-perceived competence (β=2.45, 95% CI 1.12 to 3.78). Challenges included public overcrowding (70.8% vs 40.8%; p<0.001) and private equipment downtime (51.5% vs 34.6%; p=0.009).

## Conclusions
Private centres were associated with higher self-perceived competence and readiness, better resources and feedback, while public centres offered greater patient volumes. Hybrid placements and targeted infrastructure investment are recommended to help address disparities in perceived readiness.

## STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY
The comparative cross-sectional design enabled efficient hypothesis testing using a standardised, validated questionnaire with high internal reliability (Cronbach’s α=0.85).
Stratified random sampling from Radiographers Registration Board of Nigeria-accredited centres with a powered sample size (80% power) minimised selection bias and ensured balanced representation.
Adjustment for potential confounders (eg, age, gender, posting duration) in multivariate regression analyses strengthened inference on predictors of outcomes.
Anonymous, voluntary participation with informed consent and ethical approval enhanced data confidentiality and reduced response bias.
This cross-sectional design does not enable causal inferences, and reliance on self-report data may introduce social desirability bias despite mitigation strategies.

## Introduction
 Radiography plays a central role in modern healthcare diagnostics, as it enables the non-invasive visualisation of internal structures to support accurate diagnosis, treatment planning and patient management. Globally, the increasing numbers of imaging examinations are attributed to advances in technology, to the ageing population (with more chronic diseases) and to the practice of defensive medicine. According to the Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey 2024, there was a marginal increase in antenatal care coverage from 61% in 2013 to 63% in 2024, which did not translate to increased antenatal imaging examinations, as persistent gaps in diagnostic access during antenatal care persist. Additionally, the rising trauma cases in Nigeria require diagnostic imaging for their management, and these highlight the growing demand for robust radiography services in Nigeria with an estimated population exceeding 255 million people. 
However, Nigeria’s healthcare system faces a profound shortage of diagnostic imaging personnel, which worsens the inequities in service delivery. While international guidelines estimate that 100–120 radiologists are acceptable for a million population, Idowu and Okedere estimated that there were 100 medical physicists, 300 radiologists and 1318 radiographers in Nigeria between 2015 and 2019, translating to approximately 0.4, 1.2 and 5.2 medical physicists, radiologists and radiographers per one million inhabitants. Thus, Nigeria is far from meeting the international recommendation for radiologists and abysmally lower than Saudi Arabia’s 147 per million, Europe’s average of 127 per million (ranging from Bulgaria’s 51 to Sweden’s 270 per million), and Ghana’s three radiologists per million inhabitants. 
The uneven distribution of clinical radiologists, with a particularly high concentration in national capitals and urban regions, has been observed in Lagos and leaves rural areas underserved. Beyond the multiple workforce challenges, the unavailability of constant electricity as well as the problems of equipment availability and repair contribute to diagnostic delays and poorer health outcomes in Nigeria, and these demoralise the healthcare staff, damage public trust and cause families to incur catastrophic costs through emergency referrals. 
Since clinical training forms the cornerstone of radiography education, bridging theoretical knowledge with practical skills is essential for graduate competency to improve Nigeria’s radiographer deficits. The Radiographers Registration Board of Nigeria (RRBN) mandates a 5 year Bachelor of Science programme in radiography, emphasising supervised clinical placements to foster proficiency in image acquisition, patient positioning, radiation safety and ethical practice for radiographers. The emigration of highly skilled healthcare professionals to high-income countries affects the training of radiography professionals. The quality of these trainings also varies significantly between public and private centres: the public healthcare system serves the majority of Nigerians but is weak due to lack of adequate funding, personnel and equipment. This gap is closely bridged by private healthcare facilities, which account for 70% of health services coverage in the country. In general, public centres (eg, government teaching hospitals) typically serve high patient volumes with variable funding/equipment, while private centres (eg, privately-owned specialised clinics) frequently offer better-resourced environments but lower case diversity.
Despite these significant disparities, there is a limited body of comparative evidence regarding training experiences within the public and private sectors. This study is the first to explicitly compare these two settings in Lagos, thereby addressing this evidence gap through the examination of five specific objectives, which were to (1) Assess the structure and organisation of clinical radiography training, (2) Evaluate the availability of qualified supervisors, modern equipment and patient load, (3) Examine the extent of student participation, hands-on practice and feedback mechanisms, (4) Compare self-perceived competence and practice. Readiness among students and recent graduates from both training environments and (5) Identify key challenges encountered during clinical postings in the public and private centres. It is expected that variations would be evident in resource accessibility and practical application, resulting in a disparity in perceived competence. While focused on Nigeria, the findings of this study will be applicable to broader challenges in low- and middle-income countries with mixed public-private healthcare systems, where resource disparities affect training equity and workforce readiness, relevant to similar contexts in sub-Saharan Africa and beyond.

## Methods

## Study design
A comparative cross-sectional descriptive design was used to assess and compare clinical radiography training experiences in public and private radiodiagnostic centres. The key elements included a survey-based approach to capture self-reported data on training structure, resources, participation, competence and challenges at a single point in time, allowing for hypothesis testing on the differences between centre types. The design was chosen for its efficiency in identifying disparities in graduate competency implications within resource-constrained settings like Nigeria.

## Setting
The study was conducted at four RRBN-accredited radiodiagnostic centres (two public teaching hospitals and two private diagnostic clinics) affiliated with the University of Lagos, College of Medicine, Idi-Araba, Lagos State, Nigeria. These four centres were stratify-selected from a total of 12 eligible RRBN-accredited centres in Lagos and exemplify a balanced representation of the high-capacity public institutions (such as prominent teaching hospitals) and specialised private facilities (such as upscale diagnostic clinics) prevalent in Lagos. All centres were located within metropolitan Lagos, reflecting the concentration of accredited training facilities in Nigeria’s commercial capital. Recruitment and data collection occurred between August and October 2025. No exposure or follow-up periods were involved, as the design focused on retrospective self-reports of recent clinical experiences.

## Participants
Eligibility criteria included final-year undergraduate radiography students from the University of Lagos who had completed at least 6 months of mandatory clinical postings in either public or private radiodiagnostic centres and recent graduates (within 1 year post-qualification) who had undergone similar training. Participants must have been aged 18 years and above, proficient in English and willing to provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria encompassed individuals with less than 6 months of clinical exposure, those from non-affiliated institutions or incomplete questionnaire responses. Participants reported the type of centre for their primary clinical training; current employment location was not collected, as the focus was on training-era experiences.
Participants were sourced from the Department of Radiography, University of Lagos, using departmental records and clinical placement logs maintained by the RRBN. Selection methods involved simple random selection of four centres, stratified by public (two) and private (two) from RRBN’s full accreditation and student allocation lists, followed by consecutive recruitment of participants during routine departmental seminars and clinical debriefings to reach 260 eligible and representative individuals (130 per centre type).

## Variables
The primary outcome was self-perceived competence and practice readiness, defined as students’ or graduates’ subjective rating of their ability to perform core radiographic tasks (eg, image acquisition, patient positioning, radiation safety) independently, measured on a 5-point Likert scale (1=not competent, 5=highly competent). Secondary outcomes included extent of student participation and feedback mechanisms (Likert-scaled satisfaction levels).
The main exposure was the type of training centre (public vs private, a binary categorical variable). Predictors comprised: (1) Structure and organisation of training (eg, scheduled rotations, yes/no), (2) Availability of qualified supervisors (ratio of students to supervisors, ordinal), with qualified supervisors defined as licensed, experienced radiographers so recognised by the RRBN to oversee trainee radiographers, (3) Modern equipment access (defined as access to CT and/or MRI, reflecting contemporary diagnostic capabilities beyond basic X-ray, with a yes/no self-report), (4) Patient load (daily case volume, categorical: low <20, medium 20–50, high >50), (5) Hands-on practice opportunities (frequency, Likert scale) and (6) Key challenges (eg, overcrowding, multiple-choice). Potential confounders included age (continuous, years), gender (binary) and duration of posting (continuous, months). Effect modifiers were prior academic performance (Grade Point Average (GPA), continuous) and motivation level (Likert scale).

## Data sources/measurement
Data were collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire with sections on biodata, training experiences and competence ratings. Sources included participant recall of clinical postings (verified against logbooks where possible).

## Questionnaire development and validation
The full questionnaire is provided in and was developed collaboratively by the research team, drawing on established literature and scales to ensure content validity and relevance to the study’s objectives. Items for self-perceived competence (10 items) and practice readiness were adapted from the Radiographers’ Competence Scale by Andersson et al, to assess proficiency in core radiographic tasks. There were also sections on training structure and organisation (seven items), availability of supervisors/equipment/patient load (nine items), extent of participation/hands-on practice/feedback (seven items), motivation (five items), and challenges (10 items). Demographic items (eg, posting duration) were standardised from RRBN guidelines. The questionnaire draft was reviewed by three content experts (two radiographers and one medical educator) for face and content validity.

## Pretest
Pretesting was conducted on 20 non-study final-year radiography students (not included in the main sample) in July 2025, assessing clarity, completion time and internal consistency (overall Cronbach’s α=0.85; subscale α: structure=0.82, availability=0.89, participation=0.84, competence=0.85). Test-retest reliability was evaluated over 2 weeks in a subsample (n=10; ICC=0.91). Based on pretest feedback, ambiguous items were reworded, and the final version achieved high acceptability (96.3% completion rate). All items used 5-point Likert scales (1=strongly disagree, 5=strongly agree) unless otherwise specified (eg, yes/no for equipment availability, categorical for patient load).

## Questionnaire administration
The questionnaires were administered in person as a paper-based tool during routine departmental seminars and clinical debriefings at the University of Lagos in 30–45 min sessions from August to October 2025. Participants received clear verbal instructions from trained research assistants (who had radiography backgrounds), completed it independently in a quiet venue, and returned it immediately for on-site verification of completeness (eg, checking for missing sections). Non-responders were gently prompted once. Assessment methods were identical across groups (public vs private), ensuring comparability through standardised questionnaire administration in English. No clinical observations or objective tests were used; all observations relied on self-report for feasibility.
Completed questionnaires were assigned unique alphanumeric codes (eg, P001 for public centre participant 1) on collection to ensure anonymity. Two independent data entry clerks (blinded to centre type) double-entered responses into a password-protected Microsoft Excel spreadsheet (V.365) using a predefined coding template (eg, Likert scores as numeric 1–5; categorical variables as 0/1). Discrepancies (n=2, <1% of entries) were resolved by a third reviewer from re-examination of the originals. The cleaned dataset was then imported into SPSS V.29 for analysis, with a 5% random subsample (n=13) re-verified for accuracy (100% concordance).

## Outcomes (self-perceived competence/practice readiness)
Assessed using items like “I feel confident in interpreting basic radiographs” rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Total scores ranged from 10 to 50, categorised as low (<25), moderate (25-35) and high (>35). The high category (>35) was chosen to align with the RRBN’s benchmark of >70% proficiency (35/50 points).

## Exposures/predictors
Were measured using closed-ended items (eg, “Was supervision structured? Yes/No”; “Availability of modern equipment, Yes/No”). Structured supervision was defined as “scheduled rotations, formal orientation, clear protocols and consistent oversight.” 

## Confounders/modifiers
Were demographic items (age, gender) from participant self-report and motivation using a 5-item Likert scale (α=0.78). 

## Bias
Potential sources of bias were addressed as follows: selection bias was minimised by stratification of centres (with equal public and private representation) and recruitment from a centralised departmental list to avoid over-sampling from accessible sites. Recall bias was mitigated by limiting reports to recent postings (<12 months) and pretesting the questionnaire (n=20 non-study participants) for clarity. Response bias was reduced through anonymous, voluntary participation with no incentives. Confounding was controlled by adjustment during the multivariate regression.

## Study size
The target population was approximately 650 final-year radiography students who have undergone 6 months of clinical postings, intern radiographers and radiographers deployed by the National Youth Service Corps to clinical centres (based on administrative data). The sample size calculation for this comparative cross-sectional study was conducted on WinPepi V.11.65 software (Abramson JH, Hadassah-Hebrew University, Jerusalem, Israel), to compare proportions of two independent groups, assuming a 5% significance level; 80% power; a ratio of public to private of 1; a proportion of radiographers in private (48.65%) or 0.4865 and public facilities (29.73%) or 0.2973 in a similar study in Nigeria; A design effect of 1.0; and factoring a 20% non-response, the minimum sample size was 260 (130 in public and 130 in private facilities).

## Quantitative variables
Quantitative variables (eg, Likert scale scores for competence and motivation) were treated as continuous for descriptive statistics (means, SD) and ordinal for inferential analyses. Scores were summarised for each domain (eg, supervision quality) and grouped into low, medium or high based on distribution to facilitate interpretation and hypothesis testing, for example, low competence <25th percentile to highlight at-risk thresholds (based on an overall distribution of 25th percentile approximating 25), following RRBN’s competency benchmarks requiring >70% proficiency. Categorical variables (eg, patient load) were collapsed into 3–4 groups to avoid sparsity, with rationale tied to clinical relevance (eg, high load >50 cases/day aligns with overcrowding thresholds in Nigerian studies).

## Statistical methods
All data analyses used SPSS V.29 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA). Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means±SD) were used to summarise biodata and variables. For the inferential analyses, χ2 tests were used for associations between categorical variables (eg, centre type vs participation extent) and independent t-tests for mean differences in continuous outcomes (eg, competence scores), with p<0.05 significance, and the regression model was used for confounding control (eg, age). The RRBN-accredited affiliation and self-reported centre type was used for stratification, as this directly reflects the dual-system exposure in Lagos, Nigeria.
For descriptive and inferential statistics, the normality of continuous variables (eg, competence scores) was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk tests (p>0.05 for all) and Q-Q plots; non-normal data were not transformed as medians yielded similar inferences. The associations used Pearson’s χ2 for categorical variables (with Yates’ continuity correction where cell counts were <5) and independent t-tests/Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables (using parametric or non-parametric as appropriate).
For the multivariate linear regression: in the primary analysis for predictors of competence scores, a hierarchical approach was employed by entering centre type (main exposure, binary coded as 0=public, 1=private) in Block 1, followed by confounders (age, gender (0=male, 1=female), posting duration) in Block 2. No interaction terms were included due to non-significance in preliminary tests (p>0.10).
The model assumptions were verified with linearity (scatterplots), homoscedasticity (residual plots), multicollinearity (Variance Inflation Factor (VIF)<2.5 for all predictors) and independence (Durbin-Watson=1.92). Outliers (n=3, >3 SD from the mean) were retained after Cook’s distance checks (<1.0). The final model (R²=0.28) was interpreted using unstandardised β coefficients with 95% CIs from robust standard errors. All analyses used two-tailed α=0.05, with no multiplicity adjustments as the hypotheses were pre-specified.
Missing data (<5% overall) were addressed by listwise deletion, and a sensitivity analysis (eg, comparing the characteristics of deleted cases to those retained) was conducted to verify that listwise deletion did not introduce substantial bias. Subgroup gender interactions were non-significant (p=0.45), avoiding multiplicity-driven overinterpretation.

## Participants recruitment and response
A total of 300 final-year radiography students and recent graduates from the University of Lagos were identified as potentially eligible based on departmental records. Of these, 280 were examined for eligibility through screening, with 20 excluded due to insufficient clinical exposure (<6 months) or non-affiliation with selected centres. Confirmed eligibility was met by 270 individuals, but 10 declined participation due to time constraints. Thus, 260 participants were included and completed the self-administered questionnaire, all of which were analysed, with a response rate of 96.3%. Further follow-up was not necessary, as the cross-sectional design precluded post-recruitment loss. The reasons for non-participation are summarised in the flow diagram ( ).

## Descriptive data – participant characteristics
Participant characteristics are presented in , by training centre type (public n=130; private n=130). The sample had more males (55.0%, n=143) with a mean age of 24.2±2.1 years. Of the 260 participants, 188 (72.3%) were final-year students, and 72 (27.7%) were qualified recent graduates (within 1 year), with equal distribution across centre types. Posting duration averaged 7.8±1.5 months, and prior GPA was balanced (mean=3.6±0.4). Potential confounders (eg, gender, age, posting duration) showed no significant baseline imbalances (p>0.05). Exposures included centre type (binary: public vs private) and variables like supervisor availability (ordinal: low/medium/high). Missing data were minimal: 1.2% (n=3) for age, 0.8% (n=2) for gender and <0.4% (n=1) for competence scores, handled using listwise deletion with no imputation needed due to low rates and no pattern.

## Outcome data
Primary outcomes focused on self-perceived competence (continuous score: range 10–50, higher=better) and practice readiness (binary: ready or not ready, based on score >35). Secondary outcomes included training structure (ordinal: poor or fair or good), supervisor/equipment availability (yes or no), participation extent (Likert: low or medium or high) and challenges (multiple response). Subgroup analyses by status (final year student or recent graduate) showed no significant interactions with main outcomes (p>0.10), so results were not further stratified. For the summary measures, the mean self-perceived competence score was 34.2±6.1 overall, with 77.3% (n=201) reporting readiness. The public centres showed lower mean self-perceived competence scores (32.8±6.4) compared with private (35.6±5.7). For the key exposures, 68.5% (n=178) reported good structure, 74.2% (n=193) had qualified supervisors – and supervisor availability was similar (70.8% public vs 77.7% private; p=0.17).

## Main results
The results are aligned with the five specific objectives, using unadjusted estimates from χ2 (categorical) and t-tests (continuous), with 95% CIs where applicable. Continuous variables (eg, competence scores) were not categorised for primary analyses but grouped into tertiles (low <30, medium 30–40, high >40) for interpretability, based on RRBN benchmarks (>70% proficiency threshold). No absolute risk translation was needed, as outcomes were cross-sectional competencies rather than time-bound events. Confounder-adjusted estimates using multivariate linear regression for competence are reported for Objective iv, adjusting for age, gender and posting duration (included due to theoretical confounding on experience).

## Structure and organisation of clinical radiography training
Of 260 participants, 178 (68.5%, 95% CI 62.5 to 74.1) rated structure as good (scheduled rotations, clear protocols). Private centres had similar ratings (89/130, 68.5%) to public (89/130, 68.5%), with no difference (χ²=0.12, p=0.94). shows the summaries of structure and organisation.
 shows there were no wide disparities in training organisation, suggesting that standardised RRBN guidelines were uniformly implemented, although the absolute levels indicate that there is room for improvement in the provision of clear protocols.

## Availability of qualified supervisors, modern equipment and patient load
Overall, qualified supervisors were available to 193 (74.2%, 95% CI 68.7 to 79.2); modern equipment (eg, CT/MRI) to 156 (60.0%) and high patient load (>50/day) in 201 (77.3%) as in . The public centres had higher loads (112/130, 86.2%) but less equipment (62/130, 47.7%), compared with private centres with lower loads (89/130, 68.5% load) and more equipment (94/130, 72.3% equipment), χ²=10.61–15.40, all p<0.001). While the private centres had a higher percentage of qualified supervisors (77.7%), compared with the public centres (70.8%), the difference was not statistically significant (p=0.17). The higher modern equipment access in private centres may support advanced skill exposure, while high patient load in public centres provides volume-based experience which are complementary strengths.
 As in , the public centres offer greater case volume for experiential learning, but private settings provide superior equipment access, highlighting infrastructure inequities with implications for specific competency experiences.

## Extent of student participation, hands-on practice and feedback mechanisms
Overall, there was a high participation in 201 (77.3%); hands-on practice in 188 (72.3%) and feedback received by 169 (65.0%). The private centres had higher participation (104/130, 80.0%) than the public centres (97/130, 74.6%), χ²=4.56–9.12, p=0.03) as displayed in .
 The private placements were associated with greater active involvement, hands-on practice and feedback, compared with public placements ( ); however, the public high-volume settings were associated with broader exposure.

## Self-perceived competence and practice readiness
Mean self-perceived competence scores differed significantly (t=−3.45, df=258, p=0.001; mean difference=−2.8, 95% CI −4.5 to −1.1), with higher scores in the private (35.6±5.7) than the public centres (32.8±6.4). Self-perceived practice readiness was operationalised as a binary variable with high self-perceived practice readiness as a total score of >35 (as in methods section), and was higher in private centres (112/130, 86.2%) compared with the public centres (89/130, 68.5%), χ²=10.61, p<0.001.
On bivariate analysis, posting duration (β=0.89, 95% CI 0.45 to 1.33, p<0.001) and female gender (β=1.21, 95% CI 0.34 to 2.08, p=0.007) were positively associated. Multivariate linear regression (adjusting for age, gender and posting duration; R²=0.28, F=24.6, p<0.001) showed centre type (private: β=2.45, 95% CI 1.12 to 3.78, p=0.001) as a significant predictor of perceived competence and practice readiness ( ).

## Key challenges during clinical postings
The top challenges during clinical postings, the factors were overcrowding (n=145, 55.8%), equipment downtime (n=112, 43.1%), and inadequate feedback (n=91, 35.0%). The public centres reported higher overcrowding (92/130, 70.8%), compared with private centres (53/130, 40.8%), χ²=21.4, p<0.001. However, the private centres reported more downtime (67/130, 51.5%) compared with the public centres (45/130, 34.6%); χ²=6.78, p=0.009 ( ). Thus, the public settings faced volume-related barriers, potentially diluting individualised training, while the private settings’ challenges centre on resource reliability – this result informed targeted interventions.
 

## Other analyses
Subgroup analyses (by gender) showed no interactions with competence (p=0.45). Sensitivity analyses excluding outliers (n=5 high scores) confirmed primary t-test (p=0.002) and regression (β=2.38, p=0.002) robustness. Thus, no sampling strategy adjustments were needed beyond stratification.

## Discussion
This comparative cross-sectional study’s results addressed five specific objectives to evaluate clinical radiography training disparities between public and private radiodiagnostic centres in Lagos, Nigeria, and their implications for graduate competency. The study’s 260 participants (public n=130; private n=130) had more males (55.0%, n=143) with a mean age of 24.2±2.1 years. A similar study of 40 final year students of radiography in Ibadan, Nigeria, reported a similar 80% response rate, had more males (52.5%), and the majority were in the 20–25 years age range, and another study of 42 radiography students also had more males (59.5%) and the majority in the 19–23 age group. These similarities possibly reflect the typical demographic profile of final-year radiography students in the universities.

## Structure and organisation of clinical radiography training
No significant differences were observed in the structure and organisation of training, rated as good across both settings (68.5% vs 68.5%), was not a significant predictor in regression, and was not interpreted as a key driver of perceived competence differences in this study. This finding was similar to another study of 100 radiographers in public and private centres in southwest Nigeria, which showed that radiographers working across both settings strictly followed the standard rules with clear protocols of the RRBN. These findings indicate uniform adherence to the RRBN guidelines across both public and private settings.

## The resource-competence link
In our study, the public centres significantly demonstrated superior patient load (>50/day, 86.2%) which facilitated greater case exposure but lagged in modern equipment availability (eg, CT/MRI, 47.7%), which the private centres excelled in. Compared with other studies of public centres, 52.4% students opined that X-ray equipment was inadequate in Ghana; and a qualitative study in Zimbabwe highlighted the scarcity of basic infrastructure for delivering radiology services in the studied hospitals. These patterns converge with our identification of infrastructure deficits in southwestern Nigeria but extend by quantifying public-private gradients, absent in prior local studies.
This study also revealed that private centres performed significantly higher in student participation, hands-on practice and feedback mechanisms, which imply enhanced experiential learning opportunities. Up to 68.5% in public centres were limited in hands-on opportunities, and our result was similar to another Nigerian study of 40 final year Leads University’s 52.5% radiography students having limited access to hands-on training at UCH Ibadan (a public centre) and 40% feeling poorly prepared for their future. These findings further highlight the need to combine both private and public settings for a well-rounded radiographer student/intern exposure. Competence is expected of newly qualified graduates, as the United Kingdom’s Health and Care Professions Council standard 13.35 expects performance of a broad range of CT examinations and standard imaging techniques, while Nigeria’s RRBN expects proficiency in general radiography and ability to perform and assist with CT and MRI scans. 
In this study, self-perceived competence and practice readiness were significantly higher in private settings, compared with the public centres. In general, the significantly higher private-centre self-perceived competence aligns with international findings on mentorship-driven progression, for example, Benner’s novice-to-expert model, where competence is one of the stages of skill acquisition deriving from practice with available resources and infrastructure. While statistically significant, the mean difference (2.8 points on 10–50 scale) is modest, and its practical or clinical relevance is uncertain without established minimal important difference for this adapted scale. Our finding that 31.5% participants felt inadequately prepared for their future in public centres was similar to the 40% Lead University participants feeling poorly prepared for their future. Uniquely, our study’s multivariate regression revealed centre type as an independent predictor of competence and readiness, after adjusting for age, gender and posting duration. Additionally, practice readiness self-perceptions may vary by future practice setting (eg, high-volume public vs specialised private); however, this was not assessed in this study and warrants future exploration.

## The systematic challenges
Centre-specific significant challenges with overcrowding prevalent in public centres were identified, which likely reflects greater student allocation per site and dilution of hands-on opportunities; however, exact ratios were not collected in this study. In the index study, equipment downtime was more prevalent in private centres; in contrast, a study on equipment downtimes in Kano State, Nigeria, reported that radiological equipment in Kano metropolis was associated with frequent breakdown and prolonged downtime across all the four government hospitals and one private diagnostic centre in the study. These findings collectively highlight complementary strengths, showing that the volume-driven exposure in public settings and the resource-rich skill refinement in private centres could both potentially shape graduate competency experiences.

## Limitations
This study’s cross-sectional design cannot make causal inferences; it captures perceptions at one point, which may be improved by longitudinally tracking the evolution of competency. The self-reports may potentially inflate scores due to social desirability, although the validated subscale (Cronbach’s α=0.72–0.85) mitigated some subjectivity, and this bias likely operates unidirectionally upward, with a modest magnitude. As self-perceived competence does not equate to objectively measured clinical performance, future studies should incorporate objective assessments (eg, supervisor-rated clinical evaluations or Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs)). Selection bias was partially addressed using stratified sampling (n=130 per centre), but recruitment through one university may under-represent diverse socioeconomic profiles, potentially biasing toward urban participants. The student-to-site ratios were not modelled, potentially confounding hands-on opportunities from overcrowding; thus, future work should use objective metrics (eg, equipment inventory or student allocation logs).

## Generalisability
The generalisability of these findings was moderately constrained by the urban Lagos focus, encompassing University of Lagos affiliates and the four RRBN-accredited centres, which may not extrapolate to rural or non-teaching facilities. Participant demographics (mean age 24.2 years, 55% male) reflect typical radiography cohorts but limit applicability to older graduates or gender-diverse groups. Externally, the results are relevant for other sub-Saharan African contexts with dual public-private training systems, supporting transnational policy on equitable placements.

## Conclusion
This comparative cross-sectional study highlighted critical disparities and synergies in clinical radiography training across public and private radiography centres in Lagos, Nigeria, and directly addressed the aim of assessing associations with graduate self-reported competence and practice readiness. The results included that while public facilities offered foundational case diversity, private environments were associated with higher self-perceived competence through mentorship and technology, and centre type was an independent predictor of self-perceived competence and readiness. While the findings suggest associations between training centre type and self-perceived readiness, the RRBN should consider piloting hybrid placements to maximise the gains of the scale of public centres and the excellence of private mentorship, and investing in targeted infrastructure and objective competency assessment tools to better evaluate graduate preparedness.
