## Abstract

## Background and Purpose
The European Academy of Neurology (EAN) was a merger from two parent societies: the European Neurological Association (ENS, founded in 1986) and the European Federation of Neurological Societies (EFNS, founded in 1987).

## Methods
This article was written by nine former presidents, three of whom were also founders of the ENS, and is based on recollections and documents. It follows up on a review of the ENS history stored in the EAN archive.

## Results
The first European society (ENS) was founded by eight individual European academic clinician‐neuroscientists aiming at joining with other qualified European neuroscientists on an individual membership basis. After 1990 members were also invited from behind the former Iron Curtain. A principal goal was holding neurology meetings (700 participants in 1988 and over 3000 in 2010), promoting collaborative research projects with exchange of junior neuroscientists, and providing teaching and education independent from nationality. Health politics were not part of the agenda. The executive boards (4‐year term) were staffed with academic scientists from all subspecialties of neurology. Numerous bursaries and fellowships were established for junior neurologists. The impact of ENS members on research activities of young investigators was appreciated by academia at large. After years of negotiations ENS and EFNS joint efforts resulted in forming the EAN covering all fields of neurology and neuroscience under one roof.

## Conclusion
The basic principles of the ENS were successfully integrated into the new EAN in particular documented by the number of individual members rising to over 4000 in 2024.

The European Neurological Society (ENS) was founded in 1986. Twenty‐eight years later, in 2014, the merger of the ENS with the European Federation of Neurological Societies (EFNS) took place. This article briefly reviews the context in which the ENS was founded, and its evolution over almost three decades until the European Academy of Neurology (EAN) was launched.

## EUROPEAN NEUROLOGY BEFORE 1986
National societies of neurology or neuropsychiatry arose in most countries in the second half of the 19th century and first half of the 20th century. The first International Neurological Congress took place in 1931 in Bern [ ]. The following meetings took place in London (1935), Copenhagen (1939), Paris (1949) and Lisbon (1953) [ ]. In 1957 the World Federation of Neurology was founded, shaping international connections. The World Congress of Neurology became a new meeting ground for neurologists worldwide.
The need for a European society devoted to the entire field of neurology arose in the 1980s, triggered on one side by the great advances in experimental and clinical neurosciences and on the other side to counteract the fragmentation in neurology caused by the creation of different subspecialties.

## THE EUROPEAN NEUROLOGICAL SOCIETY (1986–2014)

## The foundation
The foundation of the ENS was initiated through the energy and vision of Gérard Said in Paris in 1984. After many discussions with potential co‐founders the decision to prepare the foundation of the ENS was made. The ENS was modelled on the American Academy of Neurology (AAN) promoting scientific interactions of clinician neuroscientists with a major teaching component included. After interviews of other potential candidates the final decision to start a first European society was taken by Gérard Said, P.K. Thomas and Anita Harding in early 1986. A group of eight European academic neurologists from France, the UK, Belgium, Switzerland and Germany formed the first and self‐elected Directing Board of the ENS in Paris in the fall of 1986: P.K. Thomas (President); G. Franck (Vice‐President), P. Rondot (Vice‐President), A.E. Harding (Director of the Teaching Programme), W.I. McDonald (Director of the Scientific Programme), G. Said (Secretary General), A. Steck (Treasurer), K.V. Toyka (Director of the Membership Committee). A common denominator of the founders was working experience in an academic medical environment in the USA and an individual membership of the AAN.
The plan also included having a slim administration, saving costs and time. The principal goal was to foster Europe‐wide collaboration in both neuroscience and clinical studies in the field of neurology. This became a very successful track over the years, generating multiple joint grant applications, publications and exchange of personnel between laboratories and clinical departments. Not all senior academic neurologists within the national societies were happy with the new ENS and some even expressed disapproval. Fortunately, the excellent performance of the young ENS generated wide acceptance over the following years.
During 1987 the EFNS was founded in Vienna encouraged and supported by the World Federation of Neurology. Here the members were drawn from the national societies of neurology, including from countries behind the Iron Curtain. Some of the founding ENS members had friendly contacts with Franz Gerstenbrand in Innsbruck, Austria, who had co‐founded the young EFNS. In 1986 Gerstenbrand tried to convince the ENS founding members to unite with the EFNS. As it turned out there was a misunderstanding about the ENS structure, which in fact was a supranational organization of individual members with a neuroscience background. Moreover, the ENS was reluctant to have politically elected neurology leaders in the USSR and its ‘brother states’.
The history of the EFNS is to be reviewed in more detail by Jes Olesen, one of its founders, in a separate article of this special issue [ ].

## The meetings
The principal activity of the ENS focused on meetings inviting clinicians and scientists from all over Europe. The first meeting of the ENS took place in Nice, France, in 1988 (Figure  ) organized by Gérard Said with the help of his family, and with support from other founding members. The number of participants was <900.
 The meetings were initially held biannually and later annually. The number of participants increased every year and so did the financial support the ENS received from membership fees and the—initially small—support from pharmaceutical companies. Two features of the ENS activities were unique: the ENS supported many educational and research activities including travel grants for young neurologists (about 100 grants per congress) and a growing number of fellowships for clinical and experimental research. Applications for travel grants and fellowships were reviewed by a special committee. Registration fees and travel costs for neurologists coming from low‐income countries were waived or very low and the same applied to invited speakers at symposia and teaching courses.
The last independent meeting of the ENS took place in 2013 in Barcelona. It was attended by 3614 participants from 92 countries. The highest number of attendees came from France, Germany and Switzerland. The programme included 22 teaching courses, 24 oral sessions and nine workshops. The number of abstracts was 958 (the highest number of 1066 had been reached in 2008).

## The ENS journal
The Executive Committee (EC) of the ENS decided in 1987 that the society should have a permanent contract with an established neurology journal. Two publishers, Karger (Basel) and Springer‐Steinkopf (Heidelberg), were approached. Negotiations were successful with the latter and the Journal of Neurology became the ENS journal.
The ENS chief editors were A. Compston (UK, 1989–1998), J. van Gijn (NL, 1994–2000), D. Miller (UK, 1999–2008), T. Brandt (Germany, 2001–2010) and G. Said (France, 2009–2011). In 2012, the editors of the Journal of Neurology Massimo Filippi (Italy) and Michael Strupp (Germany) were appointed by the last EC of the ENS, and they were later joined by a third editor, Roger A. Barker (UK).

## The finances
The first meeting in Nice generated modest revenues mostly from registration fees, whilst the costs amounted to about 100,000 Swiss Francs. Support of pharmaceutical companies for this meeting was very limited, making up only 5% of the revenues.
In 1990 AKM in Basel became the official professional congress organizer of the ENS meetings. Under the careful leadership of Andreas Steck, treasurer of the ENS for almost 20 years, and the skilled team of AKM including Sabine Geiger, Sabine Adam and Julia Bicher, the ENS developed very healthy finances. Later AKM was sold to its successor Congrex Switzerland.
In 2012 the operational income of the ENS had grown to 968,000 EUR (75% of the Lisbon Congress in 2011), whilst the operational expenditures had increased to 732,000 EUR. A financial report was presented by the treasurer at each annual business meeting. The ENS supported over the years several educational and research activities including travel grants and bursaries for young neurologists and fellowships for clinical and experimental research. Registration fees, hotel accommodation and travel costs for speakers at symposia and teaching courses were waived or financially supported by the ENS.

## The bodies of the ENS and its principal goals
The EC was the governing board of the society. The general policy and the scientific and teaching programmes were determined by the EC. It consisted of up to 14 members, including the President, Past‐President, Treasurer, Director of the Teaching Programme and Director of the Scientific Programme. Members were elected for 4 years at the Business Meeting which was held during the ENS meetings. The ENS had two General Secretaries, Gerard Said (1986–2008) and Gustave Moonen (2009–2014), who organized the meetings of the EC and of the congresses together with the professional congress organizer which guaranteed the continuity of the ENS.
Great care was taken that EC members were experienced and renowned clinician scientists with a substantial scientific impact specialized in different fields. From 1994, Presidents were in office for 1 year with additional EC membership years as president elect and as past president. One principal activity of the EC was generating the programme for the meetings and naming experienced speakers during their business meetings. After the Iron Curtain had fallen the EC specifically addressed the neuroscientists in countries having become newly independent nations. Moreover, the fellowship programme was extended, inviting individual neurologists from these countries to become members of the ENS.
In the second decade of the ENS, subcommittees were inaugurated by the EC in order to deal with all major subspecialties of neurology. These subcommittees advised the EC by suggesting the congress theme and various innovative subjects, symposia, teaching courses, workshops and speakers for future meetings (in 2013 there were 23 subcommittees).
The extended meetings of the EC took place in Paris every December during which the main activities were discussed and fine tuning of future annual meetings took place. All these business meetings were characterized by lively, intense, open‐minded and straightforward discussions. These working days ended with the culinary highlights of a late dinner, during which—typically in a quiet and relaxed atmosphere—a personal exchange took place and long‐term friendships were created.
The ENS had a total of 22 presidents. In 2013 all past presidents were elected as honorary members of the ENS and the first Board of the EAN ruled that these would receive equivalent benefits as did EAN honorary members (Figure  ).
 

## The liaison with other societies
The first formal liaison was formed with the AAN in 2006 supporting mutual membership options and even allowing ENS members to become a board member of the AAN (the first two liaison AAN members were John Newson‐Davis and Klaus V. Toyka). Ever since, board members of the AAN were invited to the ENS meetings and vice versa, and this was continued in the EAN.

## Preparations to form the EAN in 2014
The World Federation of Neurology and the EFNS approached the ENS in 1989 to establish closer collaborations between the two European societies. A second attempt was made in 2000 in Milan by the three successive presidents (past, present and elect) of the ENS and of the EFNS. After some difficult negotiations it was not clear for the ENS how to achieve a useful policy in uniting the two neurological societies: individual membership (ENS) versus a federation of national societies (EFNS) with largely different structures within this multinational federation. The opinions of a few leaders on both sides were against the idea of merging the societies at this time. Consequently, in 2001 the EC of the ENS voted against a merger of the two societies at that time.
After some years of talks, several joint ventures with the EFNS were started: European board examination in neurology, European guidelines, and mutual support of the UK‐based eBrain teaching platform. A new momentum was triggered when the support of the pharmaceutical companies decreased. Their intent was clearly expressed, preferring to have just one European society. Moreover, the increasing number of subspecialty meetings created another challenge for traditional general neurology meetings. In 2005 at the Vienna meeting, these aspects were discussed at the business meeting. Soon thereafter the ENS EC elected a task force with former and present EC members headed by General Secretary G. Moonen who wrote a formal proposal for the years to come that was accepted.
In 2005–2006 new discussions between the ENS and EFNS began. Following the initiative of leaders of the EFNS (Jacques de Reuck) and the ENS (Gustave Moonen) a transition task force was created in 2009 at the EFNS congress in Florence. The task force included Claudio Bassetti (who later became the last president of the ENS in 2013), José Ferro and Gustave Moonen for the ENS and Detlef Kömpf, Jacques de Reuck and Gunhild Waldemaar for the EFNS. The formal agreement between ENS and EFNS was signed by EFNS president Richard Hughes and by ENS president Zohar Argov in 2011, during the EFNS meeting in Budapest [ ]. The task force had worked over 5 years to prepare the unification of the two societies in 2014. The work included the outline of a constitution of the EAN, the creation of the bylaws, the definition of the bodies of the new society, as well as other financial, legal and practical matters [ ]. One crucial point for the merger was the agreement that the first General Assembly of the EAN should have 45 individual ENS members and 45 delegates from the European national societies to guarantee a balanced representation of the two original societies.
In 2014 the first joint congress of both European societies took place in Istanbul. At that time the new bylaws and the merger were accepted by the new Assembly of Delegates and the EAN was officially launched at this joint meeting of the ENS and EFNS. The first EAN Congress was planned in Berlin and at that time the ‘mother’ societies no longer existed.
In 2015, all former presidents were asked to describe their views when looking back on the ENS. All these were collected and can be seen in the EAN Archive [ ] (https://www.ean.org/home/organisation/archive/ens‐archive). Besides the History of the ENS file from 2015 there is also a booklet showing the many activities at the first EAN congress in Berlin including the History Walk at the congress venue that can be downloaded using the same link [ ].
