## Abstract

## Introduction
Alcohol‐related harms place a significant burden on the Australian economy and health‐care system. The current study aims to: (i) explore the relationship between blood alcohol concentration (BAC) and self‐estimated blood alcohol concentration (EBAC); and (ii) determine whether BAC underestimation is related to an increased risk of experiencing physical aggression, verbal aggression, drink driving, ejection from a venue or refusal of service.

## Methods
Patron street surveys were conducted in four night‐time entertainment precincts (NEP) across Queensland, Australia, between June 2016 and November 2018. Participants (N = 2144) reported on their EBAC and recent negative or harmful experiences in NEPs. BAC was measured via a breathalyser. Paired samples t tests were conducted to determine if patrons' BAC and EBAC were significantly different. Logistic regressions were conducted to examine whether BAC underestimation is a significant predictor of harm and negative outcomes.

## Results
Analyses identified a small, significant difference between patron's BAC and EBAC. Patrons with a low BAC were more likely to overestimate their BAC, while patrons with higher BACs were more likely to underestimate their BAC. Moderately intoxicated patrons had the most precise BAC estimations. Patrons with a high BAC and who underestimated their intoxication level were more likely to have been recently ejected from licensed venues, compared to accurate estimators or over‐estimators.

## Discussion and Conclusions
Overall, patrons are poor evaluators of their own intoxication level, which may have implications for their experiences in NEPs. The findings highlight the need for improved BAC education for patrons.

## DISCUSSION
The aim of the current study was to investigate the relationship between actual BAC and EBAC and to establish whether inaccurate BAC estimates were associated with harm. The findings supported the first hypothesis, as there were notable differences between patron's actual BAC and estimated BAC, particularly at higher levels of intoxication. The findings of the current study also partly supported the second hypothesis, as patrons who underestimated their BAC were significantly more likely to have a recent history of being ejected from a licensed premises. However, BAC underestimation did not increase the likelihood of being refused service, experiencing physical and verbal aggression or drink‐driving.
When examining the sample as a whole, the difference between patron's actual BAC and EBAC was trivial. However, when patrons were separated into categories based on their BAC level, several meaningful differences emerged. Those who were sober, slightly intoxicated and moderately intoxicated were more likely to overestimate their BAC. Although the magnitude of overestimation was substantially smaller in the moderately intoxicated patrons, which indicated that they had more precise estimates. In contrast, intoxicated and heavily intoxicated patrons (i.e., BAC >0.079 g/dl) were more likely to underestimate their BAC. This relationship trajectory was largely consistent with previous findings [ , ]. The contradictory findings on the relationship between BAC and EBAC in earlier research may have, in part, been due to researchers grouping all levels of intoxication together. This oversimplification may have inadvertently resulted in researchers overlooking the complex dynamics of the relationship at different BAC levels.
Unexpectedly, sober patrons were not the most accurate estimators of intoxication level. Rather, those who were moderately intoxicated (i.e., BAC of 0.050 to 0.079 g/dl) had on average, the most precise BAC estimates. One study determined that patrons who ‘pre‐drank’ before arriving at the bar were more likely to overestimate their BAC as they did not account for the amount of time that had passed during their drinking session [ ]. A similar phenomenon may explain why individuals with no alcohol in their system assumed that they were not completely sober. If patrons only consumed a limited number of drinks over the course of the night, they may have vastly underestimated the rate at which their body metabolised the alcohol, resulting in overestimation.
Moreover, research suggests that at a BAC of 0.050 g/dl, people become less vigilant and experience impaired psychomotor skills and drowsiness [ ]. Moderately intoxicated patrons may have relatively accurate BAC estimates as they are simultaneously experiencing numerous physiological changes. Furthermore, in Australia, the legal driving limit is under 0.050 g/dl. Information available to the general public pertaining to BAC tends to focus exclusively on this level (e.g., drinking one standard drink per hour should keep you below the legal limit [ ];). As such, this is likely the intoxication level patrons are most familiar with. Finally, patrons who were heavily intoxicated procured the largest discrepancy between BAC and EBAC. In accordance with prior research findings, higher BrAC or BACs were associated with substantial underestimation, suggesting that patron's sensitivity to increasing intoxication levels diminishes significantly [ , ].
In contrast to previous research findings, those who underestimated their BAC were not more likely to have driven under the influence of alcohol compared to those who accurately or overestimated their intoxication. Existing literature focuses on the impacts of BAC underestimation on risk‐taking while driving (i.e., in a driving simulator [ ];). One plausible explanation of the current study's findings may be that BAC underestimation does not affect the decision to engage in drink‐driving, rather, it may only influence risk‐taking and impairment while driving. Prior research has identified that impulsivity, increased objective intoxication, drug‐use and gender are significant predictors of drink‐driving [ , ]. These factors evidently play a more pivotal role in individuals' engagement in drink‐driving compared to inaccurate BAC estimates. Further, there is likely a proportion of participants that will always take public transport or Ubers/taxis when going out to drink (i.e., never drive), resulting in a floor effect.
In relation to the impact of inaccurate BAC estimates on aggression, BAC underestimation did not increase the likelihood of having recently experienced either physical or verbal aggression. As previously mentioned, the relationship between aggression and subjective intoxication is unclear. There are more salient findings in the existing literature pertaining to the relationship between alcohol use and aggression. Heavy episodic drinking and trait aggression are important explanatory factors in this relationship [ , , ]. It is therefore likely that the experience of alcohol‐related aggression is more contingent upon the acute pharmacological effects of alcohol and dispositional aggression than on subjective intoxication.
In relation to the second hypothesis, while patrons who underestimated their BAC were not more likely to have been refused service, they were considerably more likely to have a history of being ejected from licensed venues. This follows logically from the findings that those who underestimated their BAC were the most intoxicated patrons. It also corresponds to the findings identified by Droste et al. [ ]. As patron's intoxication level continues to increase, they become less able to identify their level of impairment. Therefore the venue's responsibility regarding safe consumption and patron monitoring becomes more important.

## 
Implications

Patrons are also typically unaware of the alcohol concentration in different beverages, their rate of consumption, metabolism and the corresponding effect on BAC [ ]. Despite this, patrons are expected to understand their level of intoxication and make decisions accordingly. While BAC education in Australia is typically focused on the legal drink‐driving limit, research suggests that social drinkers can learn to successfully gauge their level of intoxication [ ]. This can be achieved by a combination of internal (paying attention to cues at different BAC levels) and external (BAC education) training. At a community level, improved BAC education may lessen the burden placed on venue staff and emergency services as it could improve consumer's ability to monitor their own intoxication. At an individual level, more targeted BAC education interventions may be beneficial in reducing alcohol misuse and related harm (e.g., Quikfix [ ];). The Quikfix program reduces the risk of future alcohol‐related harm by providing targeted support and education to young people that have experienced harm on nights out.

4.2

## 
Strengths and limitations

The current study has four clear limitations. First, the measure of underestimation was based on a single night whereas outcome measures were based on patrons' experiences in the previous 3 months. This potentially weakens the relationship between BAC underestimation and harm in the current study. Second, there was a substantial amount of missing data which may have reduced the representativeness of the sample. Third, while the survey had a good response rate, there were a proportion of patrons who refused to partake in the survey, who may be intrinsically different to participants [ ]. Finally, this form of interview can be subject to self‐presentation bias. Despite these limitations, this study had a number of strengths including: data collection method (i.e., street surveys in the natural drinking environment), recruitment strategy (resulting in a random sample) and the diverse range of Australian NEPs included.

5

## CONCLUSIONS
Patrons tend to be poor evaluators of their own intoxication level, especially at higher levels of BAC. The findings from this study highlight the need for improved BAC education at both an individual and community level. While inaccurate BAC estimates were not associated with the experience of harm, this relationship should be further investigated in future research by examining harms that occur on the same night that patrons estimate their BAC.
