## Abstract

## Introduction
Headache is a common complaint encountered in primary care. In Indonesia, however, only migraine and tension-type headache are expected to be fully managed by general practitioners (GPs). Effective management requires a comprehensive understanding of headache diagnosis, treatment, and appropriate referral in primary care settings. Educational interventions for GPs are therefore essential to address existing unmet needs. This study aims to conduct a needs assessment to identify barriers in headache management in primary care and to determine gaps that can inform the development of targeted interventions.

## Methods
We conducted in-depth interviews with headache stakeholders and focus group discussions involving practitioners from government-owned and privately-owned primary care facilities. Verbatim was analyzed and categorized into themes. The themes from in-depth interviews and focused group discussions are compared and synthesized to make recommendations for the module development.

## Results
Several critical gaps were identified. First, history taking was acknowledged as essential but was often constrained by time-limited consultations and lack of structured diagnostic tools. Second, while practitioners recognized the importance of headache red flags, their application in clinical decision-making was inconsistent, leading to uncertainty in referral thresholds. Third, knowledge of secondary headache disorders and medication-overuse headache (MOH) was limited; MOH was frequently unrecognized as a distinct clinical entity. Fourth, the use of international headache guidelines and standardized diagnostic criteria was minimal, mainly due to perceived complexity and limited relevance to local practice. Based on these findings, stakeholders and practitioners agreed that priority module components should include: (1) a focused history-taking guide, with approach based on red flags for secondary headache, (2) simplified diagnostic algorithms (3) focused section on MOH and management, and (4) guidance on treatment choices aligned with locally available medications. Interactive, case-based workshops, delivered offline or in hybrid formats, were preferred.

## Conclusion
This study provides evidence regarding barriers in headache diagnosis, treatment and referral in Indonesian primary care and translates them into concrete educational priorities. A GP headache learning module should emphasize practical diagnostic strategies, early identification of secondary headache and MOH, and guideline implementation adapted to local resource constraints and consultation realities. Our findings offer a structured framework for developing focused continuing medical education to strengthen headache management in Indonesia.

## Introduction
Headache is one of the most frequent complaints in primary care, significantly causing disability and reducing quality of life [ – ]. According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2019 study, Indonesia ranks among the top four countries worldwide in terms of migraine incidence. Globally, India, China, the United States of America, and Indonesia together accounted for 43.6% of all incident migraine cases. This substantial burden highlights migraine as a major public health issue in Indonesia and underscores the need to strengthen headache management, particularly at the primary care level [ ]. In Indonesia, patients with headaches are managed through a tiered referral system under the national health insurance scheme, where the majority of the cases are financially covered with that scheme. Primary care facilities serve as the first point of contact, where GPs perform initial assessment, rule out red flags, and provide basic treatment. When further evaluation or specialist care is required, patients are referred to secondary or tertiary healthcare facilities, including hospitals with neurologists or headache specialists for advanced diagnosis and management. Its role in the first point of contact, to initially diagnose, treat and make decisions to refer if necessary, highlights the important role of primary care in headache care in Indonesia.
The importance of empowering primary care is also acknowledged globally, aligned with the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) on health and well-being [ ]. Given that about 90% of headache cases worldwide are diagnosed and treated in primary care, effective management at this level is crucial [ ]. The SDG 3 has been interpreted in the framework of headache, with a focus on preventing chronicity, reducing medication overuse, and strengthening healthcare systems through education and integrated care pathways. However, although the role of primary care in headache is crucial, many general practitioners (GPs) have limited knowledge of headache diagnosis and management [ ]. To address such gaps, WHO and the global campaign Lifting The Burden developed the Aids to the management of headache disorders in primary care, a practical guideline focusing on migraine, tension-type headache (TTH), cluster headache, and medication-overuse headache (MOH) [ ]. 
In Indonesia, the 2012 Competency Standards for Doctors require GPs to diagnose and manage migraine and TTH, and to initiate early management for cluster headaches. Yet, in practice, headache management remains suboptimal, reflected by underutilization of diagnostic tools and drugs such as headache diaries and preventive medications [ ]. Furthermore, MOH is not included in the current GPs competency framework.
To address these gaps in primary care, evidence shows that structured headache education for primary care physicians may improve knowledge and practice [ – ]. In this study, we aimed to understand enabling and limiting factors across different layers of the healthcare systems (stakeholders and practitioners) to frame the gaps in headache diagnosis, management, and referral for further intervention in Indonesia. To our knowledge, this is the first structured needs assessment for headache education in Indonesian primary care, providing essential insights to guide future educational and system-level interventions.

## Methods

## Study design and population
This study was conducted in Jakarta, Indonesia. Neurologists, primary care specialists, GPs, medical education experts, health professionals, and ministerial stakeholders represented key stakeholders in headache, module development, and primary care services.
We conducted (a) in-depth interviews (IDIs) with 6 experts from professional organizations and colleges related to headache and primary care, and (b) a focus group discussion (FGD) with 11 GPs practicing in government and private primary care. IDI participants were selected from the head of the headache working group, continuing professional development (CPD) coordinators, and medical collegium representatives. FGD participants were recruited using purposive sampling through primary healthcare facilities collaborating with our university. Facilities were selected to ensure geographic distribution across Jakarta and representation of both public and private sectors. Practicing GPs were identified via healthcare facility coordinators based on predefined inclusion criteria and invited to participate voluntarily, to capture diverse clinical perspectives and reduce potential selection bias. Although the number of participants was limited, this study employed purposive sampling to capture perspectives from key stakeholders and frontline GPs directly involved in headache care and medical education. Jakarta was selected as an initial study setting because it represents a referral-dense urban context where national guidelines and CPD programs are first implemented.
The IDI and FGD questions were developed by experts (Supplement and ). A specific segment of FGD questions on systematic reporting of headache characteristics was separately developed, tested with a mock-up FGD with 7 GPs, and finalized before inclusion with other questions.
The research team acknowledged the potential influence of interviewer characteristics on data collection. IDIs and FGDs were conducted by neurologists and a primary care specialist with clinical and educational experience in headache management. While this professional background is beneficial in facilitating the clinical context, it also carried the potential to influence participant responses. To mitigate this, interviewers adopted a neutral, open-ended questioning approach and gave a short briefing at the beginning of the session explaining ground rules and that there were no correct or expected answers. No supervisory or evaluative relationships existed between interviewers and participants. Discussions regarding reflexivity, particularly how the headache clinical perspectives and prior knowledge might shape coding and theme development, were conducted within the research team during analysis.

## Data collection and analysis
The general flow for our data collection and analysis are as follows: (1) determination of the characteristics of experts to be approached; (2) contacting and scheduling experts; (3) conducting interview through video conference; (4) transcription of interview verbatim; (5) categorization of subtheme and main theme of the verbatim of each subject; and (6) summary of main theme from all subjects.
Data were collected in July-August 2025 and were broadly categorized into expert insights (collected through IDIs) and practitioner insights (collected through FGDs). The duration of IDIs and FGDs was 1 h and 1.5 h, respectively, without any repeat sessions. A mock-up FGD involving seven GPs was conducted solely as a pilot exercise to test and refine the FGD question guide. FGDs were conducted in two batches to ensure manageable group size and to facilitate balanced participation among GPs. Both FGDs followed the same semi-structured discussion guide and were moderated by the same team of neurologists to maintain consistency in facilitation and topic coverage. No substantive differences in interaction patterns or thematic content were observed between the two groups, and data from both batches were analyzed together as a single dataset.
The data collection process followed an iterative process, i.e. when interview results showed a statement that needed to be clarified by other parties, then another resource person for IDIs was added and contacted. Transcribed verbatims are put into a table based on the Steps for Coding And Theorization methods [ ]. Noteworthy words or phrases from the text were identified, then paraphrased. After that, concepts are identified out of the text and themes as well constructs in considerations of context are developed.
IDIs were conducted by 2 interviewers, with credentials as neurologist and primary care specialist. FGDs were moderated by neurologists. No prior relationship was established between FGDs moderators and participants. All IDIs participants were given a brief introduction to the researcher’s profile, the general aim of all research steps, their particular role in the specific part of the needs assessment, as well as the current role of the interviewer. Additionally, participants were informed that their participation in the study was voluntary, and they were provided informed consent, agreeing to the entire process, including being recorded. They were also made aware that they could withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences or impact on their work. Similarly, all FGDs participants were given the ground rules for discussion, asked to fill out a demographic questionnaire prior to the start of the discussion, and reminded of their right to withdraw at any stage of the process. We complied with the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) reporting guidelines checklist. IDIs questions consist of nine questions, which starts with how continuing professional development (CPD) is prepared in general and then narrows to CPD in headache and specifically for GPs (Supplement ). Questions for FGDs cover factors associated in establishing diagnosis, administering treatment, referring, and monitoring patients with headache while practicing in primary care. Each main question was developed into probing questions (Supplement ).
Sampling saturation was determined when successive IDIs and FGDs resulted in no substantively new codes, themes, or explanatory concepts related to headache diagnosis, treatment, referral, and educational needs in primary care. The relatively small number of participants was appropriate given the narrow, well-defined study aim and the inclusion of experts as participants with direct roles in headache care delivery, referral decision-making, and curriculum development. Consistency of themes across professional groups (neurologists, primary care specialists, and GPs) and data sources (IDIs and FGDs) further supported that thematic saturation had been achieved. We ensured the rigor of this study through several strategies. We conducted member checking by sending the verbatim transcripts of IDIs and FGDs to participants for confirmation. Investigator triangulation was applied by involving two different researchers in conducting and transcribing the interviews to provide both clinical and contextual perspectives.

## Results

## IDIs results
IDIs results showed that national competency standards play an important role in governing CPD topics. However, educational topics in headache were perceived to be less considered compared to other neurological disorders such as epilepsy and stroke. Among all headache topics, which are already limited, participants felt that migraine was the most commonly discussed.
IDI participants also described the importance of GPs in recognizing headache red flags, particularly by thorough history taking to differentiate primary and secondary headaches. Identified pitfalls experienced by GPs include inadequate history taking and prescribing pattern without doing proper reassessment. Therefore, reassessment through repeated evaluation is important to ensure proper diagnosis and management. In addition, issues in referral also exists, including concerns regarding lack of recognition of secondary causes that may lead to delayed emergency referrals. Thus, CPD should importantly focus on refining clinical skills in history taking and physical examination as well as understanding algorithms for diagnostic decision making to avoid missing red flags.
Practical skill-based learning methods are also perceived to be the appropriate learning methods to recognize red flags, choose medications, and properly refer. In addition, cultural competence also plays an important role along with the clinical skills, including performing effective communication, acknowledging biopsychosocial aspects, being culturally sensitive, and treating the patient holistically.
In addition, disease burden in the community also plays an important role in determining CPD topics. Topics such as tuberculosis, reproductive health, and chronic diseases are developed as CPD topics based on the fact that there were national health programs due their public health concerns. Headache, although not currently in the national health program, is commonly found and can serve as an entry sign for a more serious condition. CPD in headache can serve to disseminate information regarding new updates in international guidelines to keep GPs update to appropriately diagnose and treat. IDI participants viewed the most effective format for headache CPD is workshop, through combining theory and hands-on practice. Workshop can facilitate more interaction in developing clinical reasoning through case-based learning. However, formats aside, participants also noted that financial barriers exist in accessing CPD, particularly in GPs. One proposed solution was to develop a workshop in an online format.
All participants agreed that headache CPD was expected to result in the following impact for GPs, i.e. to enhance diagnostic and management skills as well as to properly refer and monitor.

## Knowledge-related limitations
Regarding barriers to diagnosis, limited knowledge particularly in differentiating primary and secondary headache is a problem in primary care. Unfamiliarity with the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3) is an issue that emerged regarding the barriers of diagnosis. GPs primarily use International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes for disease classification and often use generic terms such as “cephalgia” to code for headache. The use of generic terms for headache was perceived by GPs to result in underrecognition of specific conditions such as MOH and to categorize headache in broad categories like tension-type or migraine. The large number of patients faced by GPs also contribute to the use of generic terms, which was also acknowledged to increase the risk of recognizing atypical headache phenotypes or identifying secondary headaches.
Besides barriers in diagnosis, barriers in treatment is also an issue in primary care. Unfamiliarity with headache clinical practice guidelines and treatment protocols leads to practice variations. Participants stated the need for easy-to-use, focused guidelines designed specifially for primary care that take into account the urgency to perform efficient assessment with high volume of patients. Therefore, CPD plays an important role to disseminate information regarding up-to-date, practical guidelines beyond frequently found cases such as tension-type headache and migraine.
Regarding monitoring, participants noted that structured criteria is useful for practice in primary care. However, instead of a long checklist, a simplified tool consisting of key points and questions to ask for monitoring was considered more feasible with current conditions in primary care.

## Patient- and facility-related limitations
Variations in headache description by patients sometimes served as a barrier in history taking. In addition, a high patient volume and limited time for patient encounters made it difficult to explore red flags through history taking. Physical examinations are also difficult to perform in such a limited duration. GPs had to choose to do focused assessments, such as only cranial nerve evaluations as opposed to a complete neurological physical examination although basic equipment such as an examination bed is available.

## Drug-related limitations
Restricted availability of drugs is also acknowledged by participants as barriers in management. GPs can only prescribe drugs theoretically available in the national formulary and practically available in the pharmacy unit of the respective primary care facility. In other words, medications could only be prescribed if it is listed within the primary care system. Participants also felt that their prescribing pattern was determined more by availability of drugs rather than their clinical judgement tailored to what the patient needed. In addition, drugs can also be prescribed for a maximum of three days regardless of clinical need, so that patients who received acute medications such as paracetamol or natrium diclofenac might be inadequately treated. GPs also rarely initiated preventive treatment in primary care even for patients who came with a history of chronic headache.

## Referral-related limitations
Reasons for headache referrals mainly consist of comorbidities, progressive symptoms, focal neurological deficit, poor treatment response, or limited drug availability. Headache diagnoses stated for referral were based on what is available on ICD-10 and often nonspecific. A general diagnosis of having secondary headache was often made, which reflected lack of certainty and the need of referral to hospital for diagnostic confirmation.

## Proposed CPD format for diagnosis, treatment and referral
Regarding CPD format, participants preferred offline courses, with an objective of refining clinical reasoning. For diagnosis, a set of questions for effective history taking is considered useful. The role of headache diary was mentioned in the mockup FGD but not in the actual FGD. For treatment, the desired content was clinical guidelines adapted to national formulary and conditions in primary healthcare settings.

## Discussion
Although headache disorders are one of the most disabling conditions globally, suboptimal care remains a problem in primary care, particularly in low and middle income countries. Our study provides the first structured needs assessment of headache education in Indonesian primary care, combining insights from practitioners, academics, and policymakers. Our results showed that there are consistent gaps observed in knowledge and skills for headache diagnosis, management, and referral, which were also intertwined with problems outside knowledge including infrastructure, drug availability, and patient load, to name a few. Therefore, a structured educational intervention aligned international guidelines such as the Aids to the Management of Headache Disorders in Primary Care, a practical guide published by Lifting The Burden (LTB) is needed [ ]. ## Diagnostic and clinical competency gaps
Participants of IDIs and FGDs stated that problems in history taking and red flag recognition are important diagnostic gaps. Problems in delivering comprehensive history taking include high patient volume, limited consultation time, and lack of structured diagnostic and red flags assessment tools, which often leads to overgeneralized diagnosis that was not evidence-based and unidentified red flags. In addition, participants often described that headaches in their patients are frequently due hypertension or psychiatric conditions, which reflects unfamiliarity, partial understanding, and application of the ICHD-3. The partial understanding of participants are not limited to the secondary headache concept, but also the terminology used by participants regarding headache description. Although vertigo is a different clinical entity from headache, several participants identified “vertigo” as a frequently encountered headache presentation (Table  ). This reflects a discrepancy between interpretation of clinical symptoms, i.e. vestibular symptoms from headache disorders, in primary care.
Similar challenges have also been reported globally. A study from the UK found that 70% of headache cases received no diagnostic label, and only 6% were classified as secondary headaches [ ]. Likewise, studies from Saudi Arabia and the United States identified diagnostic uncertainty, besides medication overuse and limited patient–clinician collaboration as barriers in providing headache care [ , ]. The consistent findings between our results and the existing evidence from other countries suggested that our diagnostic gaps in headache diagnosis also exist globally and there is a need for educational strategies adapted to local context to improve diagnostic capacity of GPs.

## Therapeutic gaps and medication use
Our results showed that therapeutic gaps exist in both the availability of acute and preventive treatment in primary care as well as the knowledge to use them. Available acute treatments in primary care included nonspecific ones such as paracetamol and NSAIDs (e.g. ibuprofen and natrium diclofenac), whereas available preventive options include propranolol and amitriptyline. Though available treatments are aligned with national (such as the national formulary and national guidelines for primary care) and international (WHO Model List of Essential Medicines) guidelines, barriers such as limited drug range and unfamiliarity with available protocols lead to suboptimal care [ – ]. Sumatriptan, a specific acute medication recommended by the IHS as an essential medicine, is absent from the National Formulary though available in Indonesia, but only in either secondary or tertiary care. Therefore, due to its absence in the National Formulary, its access is not covered by the national health insurance program. The limited access to sumatriptan in Indonesian primary care, which is considered expensive relative to other acute medications, is also observed in many low- and middle-income countries [ ]. More recent migraine-specific therapy, including monoclonal antibodies directed against calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) and CGRP receptor antagonists, have not been added to the national formulary and therefore remain unavailable in primary care. Interventional procedures such as the use of botulinum toxin for the prevention of chronic migraine is not available in primary care, and currently only in referral facilities, i.e. specialistic care. Therefore, primary care heavily relies on nonspecific analgesics for acute treatment.
For preventive treatment, our results showed that there is inconsistent availability of preventive drugs, i.e. beta blockers are more frequently found compared to antiseizure medications in our sampled primary care. In addition to limited formulary availability at primary care facilities, prescribing restriction, with a duration of maximum of three days within the national health insurance program also contributed to the underuse of headache preventive treatments. The inconsistent availability is also aggravated by the lack of knowledge for the preventive therapy indications. Some practitioners believed they should not be used to prevent headaches, reflecting limited confidence and inadequate training. Moreover, medication procurement in primary care facilities, especially those owned by the government, follows the national formulary and is largely driven by utilization patterns coordinated through local health offices, potentially perpetuating a cycle of low demand, limited availability, and continued underuse of preventive therapies. Similar findings have been reported internationally. European survey found that 82% of GPs did not refer chronic migraine patients to specialists, and only 55% received preventive therapy [ ]. This highlights regarding education, persistent misconceptions, concerns about side effects, and limited patient adherence contribute to the underuse of preventive management and missed opportunities for early intervention [ ]. 
The discrepancy between expectations of stakeholders regarding how preventive management should be used in primary care and practitioner insights of very limited use in practice highlights an implementation gap, emphasizing the need for educational programs with adapted guidelines that reflect formulary realities and provide practical alternatives.

## Awareness of MOH
Limited awareness of MOH is also a major finding in our studies. Although early recognition and management of MOH in primary care are was stated as something that should be done in primary care by international guidelines such as the Aids for the management of headache disorders in primary care, MOH is absent from Indonesia’s medical competence standards and practice guidelines for GPs [ , , ]. The lack of MOH inclusion in undergraduate studies and competence standards, followed by the lack of CPD programs on MOH described by our stakeholders, were linear with results from our FGD participants stating that they were unfamiliar with the diagnosis and treatment of MOH. The unfamiliarity with MOH was also consistent with findings from the literature, such as a study in Turkey showing that half of’ medical doctors sampled from a university hospital were unaware of MOH [ ]. Our findings of the unfamiliarity of our subjects with MOH align with the SDG 3 goal of reducing medication overuse in acute management of primary headaches and promoting education of health care professionals. Our module, which intends to strengthen knowledge in primary care and hopefully serves as basis to integrate MOH management into national guidelines and competence standards are essential steps to address this gap [ ]. 

## Monitoring, follow-up, and holistic care
Although participants recognized the potentially valuable non-pharmacological aspects of headache management such as headache diaries, lifestyle modification, and patient education, these were rarely described as part of routine practice. The biopsychosocial framework was not explicitly mentioned in the FGDs, which reflects limited awareness or application in primary care. The challenges in history taking, symptom misclassification, under-recognition of MOH, and lack of structured follow-up faced by our participants highlights the importance of incorporating a biopsychosocial perspective, as migraine and other chronic headaches are influenced not only by biological but also by psychological (e.g., stress, coping) and social determinants (e.g., work environment, sleep, and social support) [ – ]. Addressing these factors through behavioral, lifestyle, and pharmacological strategies can improve engagement and reduce progression to chronic headache or MOH.

## Educational and systemic implications
The gaps addressed above emphasized the need for practical educational interventions tailored to primary care realities. The preferred activities are a combination of theory with practice, with a focus on case-based learning, stepwise management and referral algorithms. Incorporation of online format is also suggested, with recorded sessions and electronic references to adapt with heavy workloads of GPs and support. These suggestions reinforce the relevance of hybrid and asynchronous learning formats in previous literature, which have been shown to improve accessibility and engagement in continuing medical education [ , ]. 
International experiences support these approaches. A study in Estonia showed that only two days of structured headache-care instruction significantly improved diagnostic accuracy and reduced unnecessary referrals of GPs [ ]. Similarly, the Yekaterinburg Headache Initiative (YHI) in Russia successfully educated GPs in a three-tier care model and comprehensive education for healthcare providers and the public. Together, these global examples offer practical models for strengthening headache management in Indonesia [ ]. 

## Global relevance and policy alignment
The Global Campaign against Headache, an initiative led by the Lifting the Burden and European Headache Foundation and endorsed by the World Health Organization (WHO), advocates for improved headache care worldwide. A publication of the campaign, the Aids to the management of headache disorders in primary care, covers guidelines, diagnostic tools, outcome measures, and patient education materials. The publication includes translation protocols for cultural adaptation and has been implemented in other countries [ ]. A translation work in Indonesia is on progress. Our needs assessment revealed unmet needs in primary care, including limited diagnostic confidence, drug indications, drug options, and consultation time. Therefore, many headache diagnoses are overgeneralized, with a high dependence on higher-level facilities due to diagnostic uncertainties. Our findings provide locally relevant evidence to guide the development of a culturally adapted educational module consistent with global standards, such as those set by the Global Campaign against Headache [ ]. 
By identifying these gaps, Indonesia and similar countries can strengthen their primary care response to headache disorders. Gaps identified in this study serve as an important basis for integrating structured headache education into CPD programs, updating competency standards to include MOH, and embedding outcome monitoring tools that significantly improve patient care and support strategic referrals.

## Strengths and limitations
This study represents the first formal needs assessment on headache education in Indonesian primary care. The use of triangulation, drawing data from neurologists and primary care practitioners across public and private facilities enhanced validity and depth. Participants represented diverse institutions and professional organizations, providing a broad perspective. However, because all participants were based in urban Jakarta, findings may not fully capture challenges faced in rural or remote settings, where resources and service structures differ. Future research should explore regional variations and evaluate the impact of implemented educational interventions.

## Conclusion
Our needs assessment found critical gaps in the understanding of secondary headaches, diagnostic accuracy, treatment approaches, and appropriate referral pathways for headache in primary care. In response to these findings, which identified specific and actionable gaps in primary care headache management, the planned CPD module will incorporate (1) structured history-taking tools and red-flag checklists for rapid diagnostic assessment, (2) case-based workshops to strengthen clinical reasoning and referral decisions, (3) concise treatment algorithms aligned with the national formulary, and (4) practical monitoring tools such as simplified headache diaries for follow-up in busy clinics. The content will be locally adapted to ensure relevance in a busy everyday practice. This approach aims to bridge the knowledge gaps identified and improve headache management in primary care settings.
Our findings identified vital gaps of headache management in primary care, notably in recognizing MOH and secondary headache, enhancing diagnostic accuracy, treatment approaches, and navigating referral pathway. In response to these findings, the planned CPD module will include: (1) structured history-taking tools and rapid red-flag assessment, (2) case-based clinical reasoning and referral confidence workshops, (3) concise and formulary-aligned treatment algorithms, and (4) practical follow-up and monitoring tools such as headache diaries. Cultural adaptation and workflow-sensitive adjustment will be implemented to improve diagnostic and referral confidence in resource-constrained primary care.

This study highlights barriers affecting headache care in Indonesian primary care, including limited diagnostic tools, short consultation times, and restricted medication availability. By identifying these practical constraints, the findings provide evidence to inform the design of targeted CPD programs that strengthen early recognition of serious headache conditions, promote rational medication use, and optimize referral efficiency. These improvements have potential to reduce avoidable disability from headache disorders and unnecessary burden on secondary and tertiary healthcare facilities.
