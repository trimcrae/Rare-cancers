## Abstract
Pharmacometrics (PMX) in Africa has transitioned from foundational capacity‐building to job creation, scientific leadership, regulatory influence, and health innovation. To accelerate this progress, the 2025 Pharmacometrics Africa Conference (PMXAC‐2025) convened key stakeholders to consolidate the continental PMX community, review progress in local capacity, and define priorities for African‐led quantitative pharmacology and regulation. Held as a hybrid event in Kampala, Uganda, PMXAC‐2025 featured plenary sessions, technical workshops, and poster presentations. A pre‐conference nlmixr2 workshop strengthened open‐source modeling expertise, while eight presentations and 32 posters facilitated discussions on capacity development, regulatory integration, and methodological innovation. The conference brought together 108 delegates, including 40 online participants from 14 countries (six African), reflecting strong global engagement. Regulatory representation (4.6%) demonstrated growing interdisciplinary collaboration. Sessions highlighted PMX as central to evidence‐based healthcare, with applications in regulatory harmonization, patient‐centered dosing, and equitable access. Presentations on model‐based bioequivalence (MBBE), physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling for safely extrapolating drug exposure in complex, understudied populations such as pregnant women and children, and model‐based meta‐analysis (MBMA) illustrated data‐driven solutions for resource‐limited settings. Emphasis on open‐source tools reinforced technical equity, while career discussions promoted mentorship and cross‐border collaboration. A closing panel outlined eight priorities for sustained progress, including funding, leadership, and integrated multi‐sector partnerships. PMXAC‐2025 signaled Africa's transition toward PMX self‐reliance and innovation, uniting continental and diaspora experts, advancing open science, and setting a roadmap for sustainable, model‐informed drug development across the continent. To sustain this momentum, establishing PMXAC‐2025 as a regular annual event was strongly recommended.

The field of PMX in Africa has evolved tremendously in the last two decades, from a small group in Durban, South Africa, to training workshops in several African countries focusing on addressing critical public health needs. Following this, the establishment of the PMX group at the University of Cape Town extended the global impact as they characterized the pharmacokinetics of drugs used in the treatment of major endemic diseases, such as tuberculosis, malaria, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection. This foundation was strengthened by several training initiatives across many African countries, paving the way for the formal launch of Pharmacometrics Africa (PMX Africa) in 2018 and its hosting of the Third World Conference on Pharmacometrics. The establishment of vibrant regional chapters in South Africa, Uganda, Kenya, Nigeria, and Tunisia has expanded the influence of the science. This progress facilitated the implementation of PMX, as evidenced by a high research output, primarily utilizing Population Pharmacokinetics (PopPK) modeling to study the endemic diseases in the region. However, greater efforts are needed to increase the number of trained pharmacometricians and retain them in the region. Innovative programs like the Applied Pharmacometrics Training (APT) Fellowship have successfully leveraged remote access to computational hubs, circumventing logistical hurdles and demonstrating a viable model for building local expertise. This approach not only addresses the continent's specific health needs but also creates a new opportunity for African institutions to become centers of excellence for outsourced PMX work from the global north, starting with data generated on the continent. 

Despite significant progress in African PMX, the continent continues to face structural gaps that limit wider impact. First, there is a training‐to‐employment disconnect. While programs such as the APT fellowship have trained a growing cadre of competent scientists, the absence of a robust local pharmaceutical research and development (R&D) sector forces many of these early‐career experts to seek employment in high‐income countries, perpetuating brain drain. Moreover, there is a population‐specific data inequity: Africa remains under‐represented in global clinical research, hosting only 1.1% of all clinical trials while bearing nearly a quarter of the world's disease burden. This imbalance results in scientific inferences that fail to account for Africa's unique genetic diversity and co‐infection dynamics. These add to the motivation for model‐informed approaches that ensure safe and effective dosing for African populations. , Furthermore, Africa faces a dual burden of infectious and non‐communicable diseases, necessitating the use of complex combination therapies that increase the risk of clinically significant drug–drug interactions. Together, these challenges highlight the need for a cohesive, continent‐wide ecosystem for drug discovery and development, positioning PMX as a key enabler of scientific and health inclusivity.
The inaugural Pharmacometrics Africa Conference (PMXAC‐2025) was conceived to consolidate Africa's growing PMX community, address the structural gaps highlighted above, strengthen regulatory engagement, and define a unified agenda for model‐informed science on the continent. The conference brought together key stakeholders to review progress in local capacity development and set priorities. PMXAC‐2025 provided a platform for knowledge exchange on cutting‐edge research, while also fostering professional networks and initiating collaborations between African researchers, clinicians, and their global counterparts. The conference represents a key milestone for PMX in Africa because it signals a strategic shift from capacity building to scientific leadership. By centralizing dialogue among regulators, academic innovators, and industry partners, the conference established a blueprint for applying PMX to local health priorities. In this mini review, we address the question: “What critical gaps persist in Africa's pharmacometrics ecosystem, and why does PMXAC‐2025 represent a pivotal milestone in addressing them?” We do so by summarizing the PMX methods showcased in the conference, including approaches that enhance regulatory efficiency in the review of bioavailability and bioequivalence (BA‐BE) studies, as well as techniques such as model‐based meta‐analysis (MBMA) and physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling. We aim for these insights to help define future priorities for African pharmacometricians and their global collaborators.

## PMXAC‐2025: CONVENING AFRICA'S TALENT AND SETTING THE AGENDA
The hybrid inaugural PMXAC‐2025 was held in September 2025 at the Infectious Diseases Institute (IDI, Makerere University College of Health Sciences) in Kampala, Uganda, under the theme “Celebrating a New Era for Pharmacometrics in Africa: Building on Global Momentum.” The event brought together 108 delegates, including 40 online participants, from 14 countries (six African) (Figure
 ). The meeting reflected strong regional and international engagement, connecting Africa's PMX community with partners in Europe, North America, and Asia. Based on country of residence, the conference was predominantly African, with 75 (69.4%) of attendees residing on the continent. A significant number of international participants were African nationals actively engaged in the continental initiative, highlighting the success of Pharmacometrics Africa in recapturing and mobilizing diaspora talent. The high proportion of African nationals (n = 85, 78.7%), regardless of residence, signaled a unified community with a shared vision for advancing African‐led science and highlighted the conference's role as a platform for knowledge exchange, mentorship, and collaboration across borders. Delegates represented a broad range of professional sectors: the public sector (academia and non‐profit organizations) formed the majority (56.5%), followed by the private sector, including pharmaceutical and consulting firms (20.4%). Students accounted for 18.5%, reflecting the emphasis on training and mentorship. Furthermore, the presence of representatives from NRAs (4.6%) signaled an important contribution toward integrating PMX into regulatory frameworks across the continent.
 

## STRENGTHENING CAPACITY THROUGH OPEN SCIENCE
A central feature of PMXAC‐2025 was its emphasis on open science as a foundation for equitable capacity building. A one‐day pre‐conference workshop on nlmixr2 (https://nlmixr2.org/) was held at the conference venue (IDI, Kampala, Uganda). The workshop advanced participants' proficiency in nonlinear mixed‐effects modeling using open‐source tools that are comparable to commercial PMX software. Thirty‐seven pharmacometricians with moderate to advanced experience were trained in model development, simulation, and interpretation.
The program introduced complementary R packages, including rxode2, PopED, posologyr, nonmem2rx, monolix2rx, and babelmixr2, demonstrating how open‐source platforms can support reproducible, cost‐free PMX analyses. The sessions also demonstrated real‐world applications of nlmixr2 in regulatory and academic contexts, reinforcing the credibility of open‐source approaches for model‐informed drug development.
The workshop was co‐facilitated by onsite instructors from Pharmacometrics Africa and virtual experts from the nlmixr2 development team. Pharmacometrics Africa instructors had earlier received training from the nlmixr2 development team; this “train‐the‐trainer” approach is chosen to support a self‐reliant ecosystem where local researchers can independently conduct advanced modeling and mentor the next generation within their regions.
Beyond technical training, the session emphasized the importance of community‐driven learning, data sharing, and transparency, core principles of open science that promote inclusivity and sustainability. By centering the conference around open‐source PMX tools, PMXAC‐2025 demonstrated a sustained commitment to technical equity, empowering African scientists to build advanced modeling expertise without prohibitive software costs and to participate fully in global PMX innovation.

## SCIENTIFIC DISCOURSE AND STRATEGIC PILLARS
The scientific sessions at PMXAC‐2025 highlighted PMX as a cornerstone of evidence‐based drug development, bridging research, regulation, and clinical practice across Africa. The conference was structured into three sessions featuring eight presentations delivered by 13 expert speakers (https://pmxafrica.org/conference/programme‐2025/) sourced from all potential work environments for pharmacometricians: academia, industry, regulatory agencies, and philanthropic global health institutions. This ensured both global relevance and direct content applicability to the African context and priorities.
Catriona Waitt (University of Liverpool, Infectious Diseases Institute, Uganda) set the tone for the conference with her presentation on the History of Pharmacometrics (PMX) in Africa. She chronicled the discipline's growth from the early use of NONMEM in Durban in 1988 to the launch of Pharmacometrics Africa in 2018, culminating in the continent hosting the World Conference on Pharmacometrics in Cape Town in 2022 (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hJq‐EMgCjB8). She said, “This development has progressed from an era of visionary leaders and initial short courses to a contemporary focus on establishing regional hubs.” She outlined challenges confronting the continent as genetic diversity, the prevalence of endemic diseases, alongside health system factors and substandard and falsified medicines. Integrating PMX into the African health system can bridge the gap. Her forward‐looking vision: “African‐led trials, African priorities, African funding, African products, and African predictions” captured the conference's aspiration for self‐sustaining, continent‐driven quantitative science.
The proceedings demonstrated a unified commitment to deploying quantitative science to address Africa's pressing healthcare challenges, emphasizing local capacity, regulatory advancement, and patient‐centered care. The sessions highlighted that PMX is an important tool to build trust and achieve health coverage in Africa. Within this framework, three key methodological domains: Model‐Based Bioequivalence (MBBE), Physiologically Based Pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling, and Model‐Based Meta‐Analysis (MBMA) emerged as important tools for strengthening data‐driven clinical and regulatory decision‐making.
Improving the efficiency of bioavailability and bioequivalence (BA‐BE) assessments is a regulatory priority in Africa, particularly given that generic medicines are estimated to account for over 80–95% of all medicines on the continent. Presentations in the second session collectively demonstrated how relatively simple computational tools can play a vital role in ensuring the safety, quality, and timely availability of affordable medicines. MBBE was presented as a scientifically robust alternative to conventional BA–BE studies, capable of reducing trial costs, duration, and sample size while maintaining regulatory rigor. 

More broadly, model‐informed approaches were shown to address Africa's practical challenges, such as limited infrastructure, smaller study populations, and resource constraints, by enabling simulation‐based assessments that minimize the number of subjects, bioanalytical samples, and clinical trial iterations required. Mahmoud Ali Afifi (Certara) positioned MBBE as a strategic enabler of regulatory efficiency, facilitating faster and more cost‐effective approvals of complex generic medicines while maintaining patient safety and scientific integrity. However, the success of these approaches depends on increased inclusion of model‐informed evidence within regulatory dossiers. Achieving this shift will require significant investment in regulatory training and the updating of regional guidelines. Encouragingly, this is an area where Pharmacometrics Africa has already established strong working relationships with African regulatory agencies. In this regard, a dedicated pre‐conference workshop was co‐hosted with the South African Health Products Regulatory Agency (SAHPRA) focusing on the digital transformation of medicines regulation in Africa (https://www.sahpra.org.za/event/dha‐side‐event‐digital‐transformation‐of‐medicines‐regulation‐in‐africa‐engagement/).
PBPK modeling was highlighted for its ability to predict and extrapolate drug exposure in understudied populations, particularly pregnant women and children, where conventional clinical studies are often infeasible or unethical. PBPK provides a mechanistic framework for navigating the substantial genetic diversity of African populations, including polymorphisms in genes such as CYP2B6 or CYP2D6, which profoundly affect the metabolism of essential drugs for HIV, TB, and malaria. In her presentation, Karen Rowland Yeo (Certara) emphasized PBPK's potential to guide dose optimisation across diverse African populations by integrating region‐specific physiological characteristics and genetic variability. This approach supports safe, effective, and increasingly personalized therapy, even in the absence of large local datasets. Importantly, PBPK modeling has evolved beyond a purely technical tool to become an ethical imperative for patient safety, addressing critical evidence gaps in populations historically excluded from drug development. African‐led PBPK research is at the forefront of correcting this imbalance by deliberately incorporating African genetic diversity, thereby ensuring biologically appropriate dosing for highly metabolized drugs and advancing therapeutic equity across the continent. Despite its predictive power, a major scientific challenge remains: the scarcity of continent‐specific clinical and physiological data to inform virtual population models. Many PBPK platforms rely on physiological and genomic parameters derived from non‐African cohorts; applying these to African settings without local baseline physiological parameters (such as organ volumes or enzyme expression levels) can lead to predictive bias. Generating region‐specific physiological datasets therefore represents a research priority.
MBMA was presented as a resource‐efficient strategy for generating robust clinical evidence in data‐limited environments, an essential consideration given that Africa currently hosts only 1.1% of global trials. By synthesizing evidence from existing trials, MBMA enables continuous benefit–risk assessment without the need for costly new studies, offering particular value in settings with constrained infrastructure and funding. , In his presentation, Innocent Gerald Asiimwe (University of Liverpool) demonstrated how pooling available trial data through MBMA can provide a cost‐effective and scientifically rigorous foundation for evidence‐based decision‐making. When integrated with PBPK modeling, MBMA offers a complementary framework that strengthens predictive accuracy and supports regulatory evaluation under the Model‐Informed Drug Development (MIDD) paradigm. Together, these modeling approaches enable African regulators and scientists to contextualize global evidence, develop locally relevant dosing strategies, and accelerate equitable access to essential and innovative medicines. However, the routine application of MBMA remains constrained by limited data transparency and the absence of Africa‐based data‐sharing platforms. Addressing these gaps will be critical for overcoming cross‐border data sovereignty challenges and for building trust among academic, industry, and regulatory stakeholders.
The keynote lecture by Steven Kern, Executive Director of Global Health Labs, an innovation laboratory working with the Gates Foundation, argued that the immense clinical and regulatory needs in Africa create profound opportunities. He outlined three core imperatives that PMX in Africa must fulfill to realize her potential: confidence in the science to build trust; clear and effective communication that translates complex technical concepts into actionable information for stakeholders; and implementation strategies that amplify existing system strengths. He further envisioned an artificial intelligence (AI)‐enabled future in which PMX expertise evolves into predictive systems capable of supporting personalized dosing in diseases such as tuberculosis and malaria. Complementing these perspectives, Bill Denney (Human Predictions, LLC) demonstrated how open‐source tools such as PKNCA and replicateBE can streamline and standardize BA–BE calculations, while Mahlodi Moropa (SAHPRA) demonstrated the application of AI and large language models (LLMs) to automate dossier reviews and enhance regulatory efficiency. Together with insights from the closing panel discussion led by Dan Hartman (Gates Foundation), these contributions emphasized the central role of PMX in advancing Africa's regulatory harmonization agenda under the African Medicines Agency (AMA) and the African Medicines Regulatory Harmonization (AMRH) program.
Delegates at PMXAC‐2025 further explored how PMX methods could be integrated into the operational workflows of African medicines regulatory agencies and the AMA. The deployment of MBBE, for example, was discussed as a means of accelerating market access for biosimilar medicines, , by providing resource‐efficient predictions of pharmacokinetic profiles, reducing the need for extensive and costly comparative clinical trials, and streamlining regulatory review processes. In parallel, the demonstrated utility of AI and LLMs for BA–BE dossier assessment (presented by SAHPRA) suggests that, if adopted by the AMA, these technologies could offer a pragmatic and innovative solution to capacity constraints by automating initial review and triage processes. Such approaches have the potential to directly reduce the protracted registration timelines that have historically delayed access to critical medicines. 

With ongoing progress toward operationalizing the AMA and the demonstrated success of the AMRH programme, the application of PMX‐driven analyses and AI‐assisted regulatory review to streamline the approval of innovative medicines and biosimilars across borders represents a realistic near‐term opportunity. However, documented progress to date remains largely limited to pilot initiatives and software demonstrations. A key example is SAHPRA, which has begun exploring the use of LLMs to automate dossier triage and review, a pragmatic step toward reducing registration backlogs and improving regulatory efficiency. This progress was also highlighted during the pre‐conference regulatory meeting co‐hosted by SAHPRA and Pharmacometrics Africa.
Crucially, we note that we are not currently aware of publicly documented examples of pharmacometrics‐supported regulatory decisions, such as model‐informed dosing changes or approvals, that have been independently reviewed and endorsed by African national regulatory authorities (NRAs). It is likely that modeling analyses have been considered as part of reliance‐based reviews when dossiers were previously assessed by WHO‐listed reference authorities in high‐income settings. Establishing transparent, Africa‐led case studies of PMX‐informed regulatory decision‐making therefore represents an important next step in advancing regulatory confidence and ownership across the continent.

## BUILDING THE NEXT GENERATION
Another defining strength of PMXAC‐2025 was its focus on nurturing early‐career scientists and expanding pathways for professional growth in PMX across Africa. The poster session, which featured 32 research presentations, was dominated by students and early‐career pharmacometricians. The 32 scientific posters spanned seven thematic categories, reflecting both the diversity and growing depth of emerging PMX research across Africa (Table
 ). The largest thematic group (13 posters) focused on population pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics (PK/PD), addressing dose optimization for infectious diseases such as HIV, tuberculosis, and malaria, as well as for non‐communicable diseases (NCDs). Collectively, these contributions demonstrate a marked maturation of the African PMX landscape, characterized by a transition from largely descriptive analyses toward predictive, mechanistic, and regulatory‐oriented science. While dose optimisation for endemic infectious diseases remains a central priority, the expanding focus on NCDs reflects the epidemiological transition underway in many African countries, where these conditions are assuming increasing prominence. The conference posters also highlighted the adoption of open‐source tools. Several presentations described the development and validation of workflows using platforms such as nlmixr2 (https://nlmixr2.org/), an open‐source package for population PK/PD modeling and PKNCA (https://cran.r‐project.org/package=PKNCA) for non‐compartmental pharmacokinetic analysis, demonstrating performance comparable to commercial software while avoiding prohibitive licensing costs.
 Further evidence of methodological adoption was seen in posters incorporating PBPK modeling (six posters) and quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP) approaches that integrated genetic polymorphisms to refine drug exposure predictions across diverse populations. In addition, exploratory applications of AI and LLMs to automate PMX workflows and regulatory dossier review point toward an emerging, digitally enabled regulatory future. Taken together, these trends illustrate that African pharmacometricians are not only addressing pressing local health priorities, but are also positioning themselves as strategic contributors to global scientific and regulatory innovation.
To recognize and incentivize excellence, eight posters received awards across categories, including the Outstanding Young Scientist Poster Award, the Outstanding “Other” Poster Award, and the Delegates' Choice Award. Winners were offered cash prizes and opportunities to attend international meetings, including the Uppsala Pharmacometrics Summer School (Sweden), the American Society for Clinical Pharmacology and Therapeutics (ASCPT) 2026 Annual Meeting (USA), and the World Congress of Basic and Clinical Pharmacology (WCP) 2026 (Australia), reinforcing global exposure and mentorship for African scientists. Complementing this, 25 travel grants (20 of which supported early‐career researchers) enabled on‐site participation in Kampala, representing a significant investment in equitable access and capacity building.
Career development also featured prominently in the scientific program. In a dedicated session, Aida Kawuma (Uganda) shared her journey from pharmacy school to international PMX consulting, emphasizing the importance of mentorship, flexibility, and perseverance. Complementary video narratives from Linda Chaba (Kenya) and Eliford Kitabi (Tanzania) illustrated how young scientists can balance academic and regulatory roles while maintaining active engagement with African science. Collectively, these experiences highlighted the importance of sustained mentorship and regional networks that connect African and diaspora pharmacometricians.

## STRATEGIC PRIORITIES FOR THE FUTURE
The closing panel at PMXAC‐2025, chaired by Dan Hartman (Gates Foundation) and supported by regional and international experts, focused on defining actionable strategies to sustain Africa's growing PMX momentum. The discussion emphasized that Africa now stands at a turning point, moving from capacity building to exercising scientific leadership, and that achieving this transition requires coordinated investment, institutional support, and continuity of effort (https://pmxafrica.org/conference/programme‐2025/).
The panel identified eight strategic priorities to guide the next phase of Pharmacometrics Africa (Table
 ). These priorities span leadership development for policy impact, strengthening regulatory science through digital and AI‐enabled solutions, building a virtual pan‐African pharmacometrics platform, expanding training and global exposure opportunities, and establishing robust systems for monitoring and evaluation of progress across the discipline.
 Among these, several priorities were identified as near‐term, high‐feasibility opportunities for the Pharmacometrics Africa network e.g., expanding global exposure and career pathways for African talent; ensuring world‐class quantitative analyses for African‐led clinical trials; and implementing a robust monitoring and evaluation (M&E) framework to track impact and build trust across regional chapters.
In contrast, priorities such as designing Africa's digital regulatory future, advancing regulatory reliance mechanisms, and establishing a secure, virtual pan‐African PMX analysis platform will require sustained multi‐institutional coordination, including formal partnerships with pan‐African bodies such as the African Medicines Agency (AMA) and NRAs with potential governance and implementation challenges.
Collectively, these strategic priorities aim to reposition Africa from a passive recipient to a self‐reliant partner in global health innovation, embedding quantitative pharmacology within African regulatory and clinical ecosystems. The panel underscored that success in PMX will depend not only on technical capability but also on the creation of enabling ecosystems that support innovation, policy translation, and equitable access to scientific opportunity. In this context, establishing PMXAC as a regular annual forum was strongly endorsed as a mechanism for sustaining momentum, reviewing progress, and fostering a connected and self‐reliant African PMX community.

## CONCLUSIONS
Pharmacometrics in Africa is undergoing a pivotal transformation: from a phase focused on foundational capacity building to one defined by job creation, scientific leadership, regulatory influence, and innovation. The scientific insights shared during the conference underscore that quantitative pharmacology is no longer merely a supportive tool but a strategic bridge for accelerating continental healthcare development. By leveraging methodologies such as PBPK and MBBE, researchers and regulators can better address Africa's unique genetic landscape and prioritize dosing for historically understudied populations. This marks a shift away from the extrapolation of global data toward a model‐informed evidence base that is fit for African patients.
Looking ahead, the sustainability of African PMX will depend on the creation of meaningful employment opportunities and career pathways and the retention of local expertise. The ultimate goal is a self‐sustaining ecosystem characterized by African‐led clinical trials, regional production of essential medicines, and scientific leadership capable of translating complex quantitative models into actionable health policy. By maintaining the momentum generated by this inaugural meeting and establishing regular forums for cross‐sector collaboration, Africa is poised to become not merely a consumer of global pharmacological knowledge, but a strategic hub of global health innovation.
The inaugural PMXAC‐2025, therefore, served as a powerful catalyst for this transition, uniting stakeholders from academia, industry, and regulatory agencies around a shared vision for model‐informed, African‐led healthcare. Establishing PMXAC as a regular annual forum for knowledge exchange and progress review will be critical to sustaining momentum, strengthening accountability, and positioning Africa as a strategic contributor to global PMX innovation.
