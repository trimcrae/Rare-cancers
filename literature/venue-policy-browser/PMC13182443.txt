The Care Quality Commission (CQC) and recent Birth Trauma Inquiry have also raised concerns about maternity service quality, highlighting deficits in empathy and kindness. Relatedly, data from recent CQC Maternity Surveys and the National Health Service (NHS) Staff Survey indicated that maternity services would benefit from enhanced empathy. These problems were more acute for women from minority and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, which may help to explain the higher rates of morbidity and mortality in Black, Asian or socioeconomically disadvantaged women in maternity care. 
Empathy in the healthcare setting involves listening, understanding someone’s situation and feelings, caring and helping, all while respecting personal boundaries. Research shows that empathy can improve patient satisfaction, reduce pain and anxiety and reduce the risk of practitioner burnout while improving their job satisfaction. Research also shows that empathy can be taught through structured, interactive educational approaches that combine experiential learning (such as role-play and reflection on patient experiences), explicit teaching of communication skills and opportunities for feedback on empathic skills rather than through didactic teaching alone. Effective training could, therefore, solve the problems associated with lack of empathy in maternity services. Indeed, evidence suggests that empathy training for midwives improves empathy and, subsequently, patient satisfaction. However, training alone is insufficient. Practitioner burnout and system-level factors can make it difficult for practitioners to express empathy, and adding training to an already busy workload may backfire by increasing stress. 
Improving empathy between patients and practitioners may, therefore, require addressing system-level factors that create conditions required for empathy between patients and practitioners to thrive. Indeed, empathic systems are increasingly being recognised as necessary to achieve empathy between healthcare professionals and patients. A systems-based approach is supported by the CQC, and by research showing that it can improve patient outcomes. System-level interventions can include organisation-wide empathy training for staff, teamwork (the effectiveness of empathy training depends on how well healthcare teams work together) as well as other system-level barriers ranging from adequate staffing, safe spaces to raise concerns and empathic leadership. 
Some of these factors have been developed and delivered as training for healthcare professionals. Relatedly, a model of system empathy was recently developed. According to this model, system empathy includes factors ranging from individual-level consideration (such as staff well-being and patient education) to empathic leadership, customer service training for reception staff, healing spaces and using appropriate technology that reduces the administrative burden on healthcare practitioners. This model was implemented at the Montreal Children’s Hospital in Canada in 2020, and subsequently in a UK NHS emergency services department. In both cases, implementation of the model helped staff to prioritise actionable steps to improving empathic healthcare in their settings, for example, by embedding patient satisfaction with care measures and streamlining booking appointments. 
Despite this work, three relevant gaps remain. First, an intervention aimed at improving empathy between team members has not been developed despite evidence showing that empathic team working affects patient outcomes. Second, interventions to enhance empathy towards patients have not been applied in the maternity setting, despite evidence supporting their efficacy in other healthcare contexts. Third, interventions aimed at enhancing empathy towards patients, between team members, and at the system level have not been combined; this is a major gap given that combining them together has the potential to increase the impact on patient and practitioner outcomes. 

## Objective
To assess the feasibility and acceptability of a complex intervention to improve empathy within a UK-based maternity unit and to generate pilot data to inform future controlled evaluation.

## Methods

## Study design
We conducted a mixed-methods feasibility study incorporating pilot implementation of a complex intervention, using a before-and-after design with multiple cohorts and no control group. Some of our data (including participant satisfaction and stated intention to change behaviour), which was collected postintervention only. We used relevant elements of the Transparent Reporting of Evaluations with Non-randomised Designs (TREND) guidelines to report the study. 

## Context
We implemented our initiative and conducted its evaluation across the maternity services at a UK NHS Trust, which serves one million residents and employs 17 000 staff. At the time of writing, the maternity services oversee approximately 10 000 births annually across three sites: two hospital-based sites and one standalone, low risk unit. In addition, the maternity services offer a community midwifery service, including a homebirth team.
In the CQC Maternity Survey, the maternity services scored lower than most other NHS Trusts in communication, addressing concerns and providing kind and understanding care. CQC reports published in 2024 rated two sites as ‘requires improvement’ in safety and leadership, while in the 2023 NHS Staff Survey, the NHS Trust’s women’s and children’s services scored below the Trust average for all aspects of staff experience. As such, this NHS Trust was selected as it provided an opportune setting to implement and evaluate interventions aimed at implementing a culture of empathy within maternity care.

## Theoretical framework
We drew on complexity theory to underpin our intervention. This helped us to think about the maternity services as a complex adaptive system, with different moving parts that interact in a non-linear fashion in conditions that are far from equilibrium. We thought about the history of the maternity services, including embedded power relations, hierarchies and ways of working. With this in mind, our intervention sought to enhance empathy at the individual-level, team-level and system-level by encouraging the development of ‘bottom-up’ strategies and solutions, rather than relying on ‘top down’ initiatives, which could exacerbate the problems related to hierarchical structures. We also used theories aimed at explaining individual learning to underpin each component of the complex intervention, described in greater detail below.
To complement our use of complexity theory, we drew on the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR). CFIR provides a structured way to examine barriers to, and facilitators of, implementation, through five domains : (1) innovation characteristics (features of the intervention, including cost and complexity), (2) outer setting (external influences including patient needs and policy), (3) inner setting (contextual factors within the organisation, such as culture, leadership engagement and resources), (4) individuals (characteristics of the people involved) and (5) implementation process (strategies for implementing, including planning, engaging stakeholders and evaluating). Each domain is comprised of multiple constructs, which influence the success of the intervention implementation. We used CFIR to help us identify quantitative data to be collected for the evaluation of this study and also to help us analyse and interpret that data; this is described in greater detail in the sections below.

## Intervention
The complex nature of maternity contexts, the multifaceted nature of empathy and the need to address individual-level, team-level and system-level factors, necessitated a complex intervention. A complex intervention is one that contains multiple interacting components targeting different attitudes or behaviours, and which requires adaptation within different contexts due to the dynamic interaction between the intervention and its environment. 
We developed a complex intervention to enhance empathy in maternity care that included six training workshops and was delivered to maternity services staff between July 2024 and January 2025. It was delivered by two experienced facilitators with support from two maternity services staff members. The complex intervention was designed to be delivered, in the first instance, as a standalone intervention. The training focused on enhancing maternity services staffs’ skills for showing empathy to patients, empathic teamworking or making system-level changes. The workshops were based partly on previously delivered work aiming to increase empathy in healthcare, along with discussions (unpublished) with key stakeholders in the maternity services (patients, maternity services staff, leadership). Principles of effective small-group teaching were used to encourage constructive interaction and support the generation of meaningful approaches to enhancing empathy within the maternity services. Comprehensive descriptions of all workshops are available in and the complex intervention is summarised in .
 

## Enhancing empathy towards patients
Two 1-day workshops (one in-person, one online) aimed to improve empathy from maternity services staff towards patients. The workshops were based on evidence from systematic reviews, the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation—Behaviour (COM-B) model of behaviour change and the theory of reflective practice. Capability and opportunity were enhanced by teaching empathy skills, such as empathic body language, that require no extra time or resources to demonstrate in the real-world setting. Motivation to enhance empathy was encouraged by two short lectures on the evidence for empathy improving patient and practitioner outcomes, and asking participants to make a written commitment to change their behaviour. In addition to this teaching, small groups of 4–6 participants engaged in four exercises, including reflecting on patients’ experiences, discussing barriers to empathy, analysing video-recorded consultations using the Consultation and Relational Empathy measure, and roleplaying a maternity consultation.

## Enhancing empathy in interprofessional and intraprofessional teams
Two 1-day workshops (one in-person, one online) targeted empathic interprofessional and intraprofessional teamwork between maternity services colleagues, informed by evidence from a systematic review of maternity teamworking interventions. They were also underpinned by the nominal group technique (involving structured brainstorming and voting on the ‘best’ ideas) and prioritisation and commitment (encouraging participants to prioritise a small number of actions, which they explicitly commit to in writing). Small groups engaged in six exercises, including sharing their own experiences of empathic teamworking, understanding each other’s roles, exploring barriers and facilitators and selecting specific actions to improve empathic teamworking using the nominal group technique. 

## Enhancing empathy towards patients and in teams
A 1-day, face-to-face workshop combined elements of the empathy towards patients and empathic teamwork workshops. This workshop was designed and delivered to meet a demand for additional training from the maternity services. Evidence from previous systematic reviews and theories (including the nominal group technique, prioritisation and commitment and reflective practice) informed the workshop. Exercises from the empathy towards patients and empathic teamwork workshops were combined.

## Enhancing system-level empathy
A 1-day, face-to-face workshop, based on an established method tested at two different sites, aimed to enhance system-level empathy. Informed by the nominal group technique, prioritisation and commitment, deep interdisciplinarity (seeking engagement from all key stakeholders, including non-clinical and clinical staff, senior leadership and patients), blue sky thinking (encouraging consideration of creative, sometimes impractical, solutions to problems) and creating a sense of urgency (involving setting ambitious timelines to implement solutions), participants codeveloped structural enablers of empathic care. Participants took part in five exercises, including identifying barriers to empathic care, generating potential solutions and prioritising actions to improve empathy in their context within the next 12 months.

## Participant recruitment
All workshops were open to clinical and non-clinical maternity services staff, including healthcare assistants, maternity support workers, nursery nurses, medical staff, (student) midwives, (student) nurses, administrative staff, housekeepers, operations management staff, security staff and ward clerks. For the system-level empathy workshop, we purposefully selected senior leaders, along with patient representatives from the local Maternity and Neonatal Voices Partnership (MNVP) to take part. For all of the other workshops, we recruited participants using convenience sampling, advertising via posters and emails to all staff via the NHS Trust’s internal communication system.

## Evaluation
Consistent with established guidance for this type of intervention, and guided by the CFIR, we conducted both process and outcome pilot evaluations. Based on the ‘implementation’ construct within the ‘implementation process’ domain of the CFIR, the process evaluation examined implementation adoption, penetration and acceptability, considering how the intervention was delivered and received. Based on the ‘innovation’ construct within the ‘implementation process’ domain of the CFIR, the outcome evaluation assessed early trends in empathy, teamwork and patient satisfaction to explore potential system-level effects. We conducted the evaluation in two stages, which we described as the ‘interim evaluation’ and the ‘final evaluation’, to collect a mix of quantitative and qualitative pilot data from a variety of sources.

## Interim evaluation
The interim evaluation took part immediately after the workshops had been delivered and assessed participant satisfaction and stated intention to change behaviour. Participants were invited to complete a short online survey to evaluate their satisfaction with the workshop they attended, their confidence that it would enhance empathic teamworking, empathic behaviour towards patients or empathic systems, and their intention to change their behaviour, on 10-point Likert scales. Participants could also leave free-text comments about the workshops (see for the questions administered after each workshop).
Descriptive statistics summarised participants’ satisfaction with the workshops and their stated intention to change their behaviour. Free-text comments were synthesised using inductive thematic analysis, an approach selected for its theoretical flexibility. After conducting a largely inductive thematic analysis, we mapped our themes onto the relevant CFIR domains and constructs to help us organise and make sense of the data. As such, our final analytic approach was a blend of inductive and deductive methods. One author worked through the six phases of thematic analysis, engaging in discussion with other authors throughout the process to ensure the findings had resonance, and accurately represented participants’ comments. The authors engaged in personal reflexivity throughout the analysis by writing their thoughts and feelings about the data in a reflexive journal and using this as a basis for critical discussions with the rest of the research team to ensure they were not projecting their own assumptions about the data onto participants’ accounts. 

## Final evaluation
The final evaluation took place 1 month after all the workshops had been delivered and 7 months after delivery of the first workshop. All participants in each workshop were invited to participate. The aim of the final evaluation was to identify whether promising signals of change were present, as recommended in early-phase quality improvement evaluation. We, therefore, collected a mix of publicly available, routinely collected data about staff and patient satisfaction, along with data about perceptions of empathy in the maternity unit:
Staff satisfaction: baseline data were taken from the 2023 NHS Staff Survey for the NHS Trust’s women’s and children’s services. The evaluation questionnaire involved 12 targeted questions from the NHS Staff Survey 1 month after the final workshop. The questions pertain to teamworking; a key area for improvement identified by senior leaders at the NHS Trust (see for the questions). The average score across all 12 questions represented the NHS ‘People’s Promise’ theme of ‘We are a team’. 
Perceived change in empathy: one question assessed the extent to which staff believed the intervention had contributed to greater empathy in the maternity unit (see for the question).
Patient satisfaction: monthly data from the routinely administered, publicly available Friends and Family Test were collected from January 2023 to January 2025 to provide an overview of patient satisfaction with the maternity services throughout the duration of the intervention. This was supplemented by comparing data from the publicly available 2023 CQC Maternity Survey with data from the 2024 CQC Maternity Survey. 
System-level changes to enhance empathy: the progress of the implementation of the system-level changes prioritised by participants during the system-level empathy workshop was recorded.
Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarise staff satisfaction, perceived change in empathy within the maternity unit and patient satisfaction. An average staff satisfaction score was calculated from participants’ responses following the 2023 NHS Staff Survey. 

## Patient and public involvement
Three patients from the maternity unit’s MNVP contributed to the development of our complex intervention, were involved in the delivery of the intervention and informed the plan for its evaluation. Additionally, these patients supported the recruitment of other members of the MNVP to participate in the system-empathy workshop.

## Results

## Participant characteristics
In total, 177 staff from the NHS Trust maternity services took part in the intervention. summarises the number of participants in each workshop by job title. Participants represented each of the maternity services sites: 45% were from one hospital-based site, 25% were from the second hospital-based site, 19% were from the community midwifery team and 4% were from the midwife-led unit. The remaining 7% did not report their place of work.
 

## Interim evaluation results

## Participant satisfaction and stated intention to change behaviour
One-hundred and twenty-three of the 177 participants (70%) completed an online survey and consented to have their responses published as part of the interim evaluation. Of those participants who completed the survey, 110/123 (89%) rated their satisfaction with the workshops as an eight or higher on a 10-point scale. Most (112/123, 91%) rated their confidence that they could implement the learning from the workshops as an eight or higher on a 10-point scale, and 106/123 (86%) rated the likeliness that the workshops would lead to positive change (better teamwork, enhanced empathy towards patients, more empathic system) as an eight or higher on a 10-point scale. summarises participant satisfaction and stated intention to change behaviour by each workshop.
 

## Thematic analysis of free-text comments
Thematic analysis of the free-text data from the online survey generated four themes: (1) appreciation for intraprofessional and interprofessional interaction, (2) value of creating a relaxed and supportive learning environment, (3) enhanced ability to identify practical approaches to empathic healthcare and (4) desire for additional (mandatory) workshops. These themes reflected three CFIR domains: (1) inner setting, (2) innovation characteristics and (3) implementation process. Because some themes related to more than one CFIR domain, we chose to first present the results of our inductive thematic analysis, and to then describe the alignment of these themes to the CFIR. As such, provides an explanation of how we aligned each theme to the relevant CFIR domain and corresponding constructs.
 

## Appreciation for intraprofessional and interprofessional interaction
There was an overall theme of appreciation for the opportunity to interact with colleagues in different roles, professions and from different teams in the workshops. Participants explained that the workshops were ‘more interactive than any of their other training’ and that this afforded them the chance to ‘explore, in-depth, what empathy means’. Moreover, they described how they engaged in shared learning about approaches to the expression of empathy in practice. By allowing them to meet and build relationships with established and new colleagues, they thought that the workshops helped to enhance teamwork by ‘connecting…colleagues from different teams’.

## Value of creating a relaxed and supportive learning environment
Several participants added that the interactivity of the workshops was enhanced by the ‘relaxed atmosphere’ and supportive learning environment. They described feeling able to honestly and openly ‘share experiences among colleagues’ without judgement from other participants or from the facilitators. This ‘non-pressurised, supportive environment’ was, in part, attributed to the fact that the face-to-face workshops were held in a venue that was not located at the NHS Trust, yet was easily accessible and provided free refreshments.

## Enhanced ability to identify practical approaches to empathic healthcare
As a result of the interactivity and relaxed, supportive nature of the workshops, participants described having an enhanced ability to identify practical approaches to empathic healthcare. They explained that working within and across their teams, and with senior leaders from the organisation, helped them to expand their ‘understanding of the real-life barriers to empathic care’. This allowed them to collaboratively develop potential solutions to ‘address these barriers’ that were acceptable to most staff. To further improve the potential impact of the approaches developed in the workshops on empathy in the maternity unit, several participants called for ‘more patient involvement’.

## Desire for additional (mandatory) workshops
Despite being offered to a large number of staff, there was unanimous agreement that the intervention should be made available to all staff, and that ‘top-up sessions’ should be made available for those who had already completed it. However, participants disagreed on whether the workshops should be ‘made mandatory’ to capture those staff who were perceived as requiring empathy training, or whether this would have a negative impact on participant engagement.

## Final evaluation results

## Staff satisfaction
Twenty-one (12%) participants completed the online survey administered as part of the final evaluation to assess staff satisfaction, which was measured using questions from NHS Staff Survey. Baseline data from the 2023 NHS Staff Survey showed an average score of 6.8 out of 10 for the ‘People’s Promise’ theme of ‘we are a team’ for women’s and children’s services at the NHS Trust. In the final evaluation, maternity services staff provided an average score of 6.5 out of 10 on the ‘People’s Promise’ theme of ‘we are a team’.

## Perceived change in empathy
Twenty-one (12%) participants completed the online survey administered as part of the final evaluation to assess the perceived change in empathy within the maternity services following the delivery of the complex intervention. Sixteen participants (76%) rated their agreement that the intervention had contributed to greater empathy as a seven or higher on a 10-point scale.

## Patient satisfaction
Friends and Family Test data showed no significant change in patient satisfaction following delivery of the complex intervention. Data from the 2023 and 2024 CQC Maternity Surveys showed an increase in patient satisfaction in terms of the extent to which staff care for patients (from 8.0/10 in 2023 to 8.3/10 in 2024); a promising signal of change.

## System-level changes
Seven system-level changes were prioritised for implementation to enhance the empathy within the maternity services following the system-level empathy workshop and the related meeting with the Department of Health and Social Care (DHSC). The implementation status of each of these changes, 1 month after the delivery of all aspects of the complex intervention, is summarised in .
 To encourage implementation of some of the system-level changes, we facilitated a meeting between senior leaders from the NHS Trust and representatives from the DHSC to discuss implementing two recommendations from the Kirkup report : (1) a well-equipped coffee room with free coffee and (2) a dedicated Clinical Director role. Both are believed to be associated with improved patient safety, by providing safe spaces to recharge, improving continuity of leadership and, in turn, enhancing authority for meaningful improvements to services. 

## Discussion

## Summary of findings
This mixed-methods feasibility study with pilot implementation and evaluation of a theory-informed, empathy-enhancing complex intervention for 177 maternity services staff showed high participant satisfaction (89% rating ≥8/10) and confidence in applying learning (91% rating ≥8/10). Participants valued interprofessional interaction, supportive learning environments and practical strategies for empathic care, with strong interest in further training. The intervention helped to prioritise seven system-level changes requiring implementation. In the final evaluation, two promising signals of change were reported: a high percentage of participants (76%) perceived improvements in empathy (≥7/10), and CQC Maternity Survey data showed higher patient satisfaction than before the intervention, though the observational design of our study prevents causal inference.

## Comparison of findings with other evidence
Interpreted through an implementation-science lens, our intervention demonstrated sound acceptability and feasibility. Mapping the themes generated from participants’ free-text comments in the interim evaluation to CFIR domains and constructs shows that acceptability and feasibility were primarily supported by ‘innovation characteristics’, including adaptability, relational connections and the physical infrastructure and design of the training workshops. Adoption and penetration were reflected in the rapid uptake of team pledges across diverse staff groups. 
From a complexity theory perspective, the intervention produced non-linear and uneven impacts across outcomes and system levels. For example, high acceptability and strong individual engagement were observed alongside only modest short-term change in team-based measures. These patterns are consistent with complex adaptive systems, in which initial enthusiasm for an intervention may not fully translate into sustained change. It is also possible that these findings reflect the impact of introducing additional training in an already pressured service on staff stress and well-being. Nonetheless, influences on team perceptions reflect short-term effects and were based on observational data from a small percentage of participants (12%); this finding should, therefore, be interpreted tentatively, and may not reflect all participants’ experiences.
Our findings align with prior evidence that empathy-focused interventions, particularly those that incorporate interactive learning and practical application of empathy, can improve practitioners’ attitudes towards, and confidence in, providing empathic care. Unlike most interventions focused on individual behaviours, we applied a multilevel approach addressing individual, team and organisational factors. This allowed us to acknowledge the contextual factors that enable or constrain empathic care, but with greater emphasis on organisational elements.
As with previous research, participants emphasised the importance of supportive learning environments and interprofessional interaction for empathy-enhancing training. Indeed, the workshops were well received and participants reported increased self-efficacy to implement their learning. The latter finding is notable given that self-efficacy is a determinant of actual behaviour change, and that empathic behaviours improve patient and practitioner outcomes. Moreover, while participants appreciated the opportunity to reflect on patients’ lived experiences during the training, they called for greater patient involvement in future iterations of the intervention. This could be achieved by drawing on patient narratives in small-group exercises, or even involving trained patient facilitators within small groups, to ensure that the ideas generated by participants are patient-centred. 

## Strengths and limitations
The system-level focus is a strength of our complex intervention, addressing organisational barriers to empathic care that are often overlooked in empathy interventions. Another strength is our theoretically underpinned approach to our initiative, supported by established methodologies for complex interventions based on implementation-science, which enhances reproducibility and transferability. While other maternity contexts can reflect on the transferability of our findings to other contexts, the study was conducted in a single NHS Trust, and the findings are somewhat context specific. Local organisational culture, leadership engagement and workforce pressures are likely to have influenced both implementation and outcomes, limiting direct generalisability to other settings.
Limitations include potential selection bias. Given our convenience sampling approach, practitioners who might have benefitted most from the intervention may not have attended. This can be mitigated by repeating the training in forthcoming years. Relatedly, despite our efforts to recruit, non-clinical staff did not participate and few medical staff attended. Our sample of training participants also likely reflects a small subset of the total maternity services workforce, though the absence of publicly available Trust-wide maternity workforce figures prevents precise participation percentages. Moreover, the final evaluation was conducted after a short follow-up period, and response rates were low (12%), tempering the strength of the conclusions that can be drawn about the overall impact of our initiative on staff satisfaction and teamworking. Additionally, we conducted a pilot study and used before and after observational data in our evaluation, which can be subjected to bias and potential confounders, and cannot be used to infer causality. It is also possible reliance on self-reported and routinely collected survey data may not fully capture patient outcomes or experiences. 

## Implications for research and practice
While this study demonstrates the feasibility of our complex intervention, intervention optimisation (including with wider stakeholder engagement) and a future controlled study is required to provide a rigorous estimate of its effects on patient and practitioner wellbeing. Relatedly, future research could collect additional data on “harder” patient outcomes, for example, beyond routinely collected and publicly available data, to achieve more granular measurement of the effects of the intervention. More nuanced understandings of patient experiences following intervention implementation may also be achieved through more patient-centred methods, such as qualitative interviews. Moreover, research is needed to explore the long-term impacts and cost-effectiveness of the intervention for healthcare organisations. Long-term impact could be assessed by monitoring uptake and sustainability of system-level changes, along with routinely collected patient and practitioner outcome data to identify if and where further intervention is required. This could be achieved, for example, by using the Medical Research Council guidance for developing and testing complex interventions. Sustaining change will require further quality improvement work, using Plan-Do-Study-Act cycles to embed empathy into established professional development pathways. Finally, implementing any intervention will require systematic addressing of barriers and facilitators to change, for example, by using the CFIR. 

## Conclusion
This pilot study suggests that a multilevel intervention targeting empathy has the potential to achieve high participant satisfaction and to set in motion system-level organisational changes. While early feedback indicates staff enthusiasm, modest changes in team perception measures indicate that sustained efforts may be required and underscore empathy as a skill requiring ongoing development. Future research with control groups and longer follow-up is needed to assess sustainability, long-term impact and cost-effectiveness, and greater patient involvement could enhance relevance. As empathy remains vital to quality care and staff well-being, comprehensive interventions like this may contribute to fostering meaningful change.
