## Abstract

## Background
People from ethnically diverse backgrounds are underrepresented in clinical trials, reinforcing healthcare inequity. Co-production involves users of research (knowledge users) as partners in the research process. A premise of co-production is that it enhances ethicality of research by responding to the needs and preferences of those it is designed for while facilitating adoption and impact. Research reporting co-production in ethnically diverse communities is emerging; however, reporting of specific strategies to support effective co-production remains sparse, particularly when designing interventions for clinical trials. We draw on our experience of co-production with ethnically diverse communities to address this gap in the research literature using the exemplar, MOVE Together: Reduce falls, a co-produced intervention to support people aged over 65 years from ethnically diverse communities to develop exercise habits and reduce their risk of falls.

## Methods
Utilizing an integrated knowledge translation (IKT) approach to co-production, our team of 24 partners, including people aged over 65 years from ethnically diverse communities, representatives from ethnically diverse community groups and service providers developed our intervention over five stages. Two stages engaged 75 participants including 63 (84%) people aged over 65 years from three ethnically diverse communities (Chinese, Italian and Arabic-speaking) and 12 service providers. The remaining three stages involved the co-production research team.

## Results
Strong participant satisfaction (96%) and co-production team consensus regarding the readiness of our intervention for progression to clinical trial (100%) support the success of our approach. Strategies to optimize communication and uphold the principles of co-production were identified by our team as important to the process. Drawing on research evidence and our experience we highlight potential strategies to support alignment with the principles of co-production when co-producing with people from ethnically diverse communities. These include additional time and financial resources, tailored approaches to information provision and a culturally responsive approach.

## Conclusions
When co-producing interventions for clinical trials with ethnically diverse communities, co-production teams must be prepared for the additional resources necessary to ensure genuine partnership. In this exemplar, we describe an iterative approach to health research co-production that may inform future health research involving under-served populations.

## Introduction
People from ethnically diverse communities face extensive barriers to accessing healthcare. In English-speaking countries such as Australia, services designed for English-speaking community members, diverse cultural beliefs that may not align with mainstream health service provision, and a lack of culturally responsive care can prevent ethnically diverse communities’ engaging with healthcare [ ]. People from ethnically diverse communities are also under-represented in research, further exacerbating healthcare inequity [ ]. While recognizing that language is continually evolving [ , ], as is the use of alternative terminologies, in this paper, the term ethnically diverse refers to communities of people or their descendants from non-dominant and non-indigenous cultures of their country of residence.
Under-representation of ethnically diverse populations in clinical trials has received strong attention in research literature, with over 50 systematic reviews addressing methods to recruit or retain ethnically diverse participants [ ]. Strategies to increase the representation of ethnically diverse participants in clinical trials include partnering with respected community stakeholders and organizations, the inclusion of ethnically diverse staff as part of the research team, building trust, use of community-based strategies and designing trials at the convenience of participants [ ]. These strategies are also consistent with the principles of co-production. Integrated knowledge translation (IKT) is a co-production research approach that involves partnering with people who will use and apply outcomes from research throughout the research process as it endeavours to facilitate relevant, useable outputs [ , ]. Partners are involved in decision-making to the greatest extent possible [ ]. Applying co-production principles to design interventions for clinical trials is a promising approach to enhance engagement of ethnically diverse communities, and therefore equity, in healthcare.
Guidance for co-production with ethnically diverse communities and evidence for potential benefits are emerging [ , ]. Promisingly, research into co-production with ethnically diverse communities suggests successful strategies may include: challenging cultural norms and practice, peer-led community engagement, ethnically diverse community-lived experience advisory groups, bi-cultural workers and peer facilitators, inter-organization collaboration, financial remuneration, workshops designed to accommodate cultural needs and self-reflection [ , – ]. However, reported challenges of co-production with ethnically diverse communities include difficulties consistently recruiting across different communities, and difficulty recruiting participants requiring interpreters despite their availability [ ].
Currently, there is limited guidance specifically for co-production of interventions for clinical trials with ethnically diverse communities. Therefore, the objective of this paper is to draw on current research and the exemplar MOVE Together: Reduce falls to provide to identify and address the potential challenges of designing an intervention to be tested in a research context with ethnically diverse communities. We believe this is an important step to inform research policy and practice to facilitate health equity.

## Methods
We applied a five-stage integrated knowledge translation approach to co-production to facilitate meaningful and useable outcomes for end users [ , , ]. Our approach was seated within the paradigm of pragmatism which identified potential problems and actions to address these in the real-world context [ , ]. Methods of our five-stage approach are detailed below along with relevant contextual information which informed our methods.

## The MOVE Together: reduce falls project and context
The MOVE Together: Reduce falls programme was a co-produced intervention to reduce falls risk in people aged over 65 years from ethnically diverse communities. The project received funding from the Medical Research Future Fund (2022/MRF2015963), ethical approval from the Royal Melbourne Hospital Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC/88727/MH-2022) and relevant site-specific governance approvals. The intervention is currently being evaluated in a clinical trial (ACTRN12624000658516). Findings related to barriers, facilitators, feasibility and acceptability of exercise programmes to reduce falls in people aged over 65 years from ethnically diverse communities and details of the intervention developed are beyond the scope of this paper and will be reported separately. However, we have provided specific examples from our project to aid understanding of the nuances involved in co-production with people aged over 65 years from ethnically diverse communities.

## Developing the project: from concept to collaboration
Our co-production project formed the first phase of a collaborative programme of research to (i) co-produce, (ii) pilot and (iii) evaluate an intervention to support people over 65 years of age from ethnically diverse communities engaged in sustained tailored exercise to reduce falls risk. The overall project was conceptualized by the project lead, C.M.S., a physiotherapist with over 20 years of clinical experience, expertise in fall prevention and co-production [ , , ], and a professorial research position embedded within a healthcare organization that provides care to ethnically diverse communities. The exemplar in our paper presents Phase (i), the co-production of MOVE Together: Reduce falls, which precedes its evaluation via clinical trial.
Recognizing the gap in evidence regarding programmes that effectively support people aged over 65 years from ethnically diverse communities to develop exercise habits and reduce falls, CMS formed a diverse co-production team to optimize potential project outcomes and impact. C.M.S. had previous experience leading an IKT co-production project [ ] and recognized its potential to address this gap. Discussion with F.B., E.R. and M.C., who have expertise in falls prevention, culturally responsive research, co-production and implementation science, identified the team should include (i) people with a mix of academic, industry and lived experience and (ii) academics across various stages of career development to build researcher capacity and capability while maximizing the likelihood of successful funding and project completion. The research team grew as members identified people within their existing collaborative networks who addressed gaps in expertise and capacity. Team members expertise included co-production research, working with ethnically diverse communities, lived experience as an older ethnically diverse community member, ethnically diverse consumer advocates, professional language interpreters and service management, clinical experience working with ethnically diverse communities, falls prevention research, falls prevention clinical practice implementation research, biostatistics and economic evaluation (team member roles are clarified in Supplementary File 1).
Our project was initially conceptualized in April 2021. A research team of 18 members worked together over a 14-month period on four grant applications. During this time the team shared ideas, brainstormed and drew on their individual expertise to develop an innovative and feasible approach to the co-production of an intervention to reduce falls in older adults from ethnically diverse communities. C.M.S. met with potential members virtually (via phone/videoconferencing) prior to them joining the research team to discuss the project, their involvement in research team membership and answer questions. In keeping with a co-produced research approach, the project specifics were to be determined via a formal co-production process. However, to do this, the project concept had to be sufficiently developed to attract grant funding.

## Scope of the project
To ensure the project was completed in a timely and financially viable manner we limited the focus of our project to three ethnically diverse communities (Arabic-speaking, Italian and Chinese). These communities were selected for two reasons: (i) because Mandarin, Cantonese, Arabic and Italian are four of the top five languages other than English spoken in homes around Australia [ ] and (ii) because of the cultural diversity in the origins of these communities. This approach was taken to enhance the generalizability of our project outcomes and its potential for adaptation for ethnically diverse communities.
The project occurred over five stages (summarized in Fig.  ).
Stage 1: Start-up and planning involving the research co-production team (researchers and knowledge users). During this stage the research questions and approach for the remainder of the project were defined.
Stage 1 commenced once funding was secured. Over 2 months, three iterative 90-min workshops (a, b and c) were conducted to define our research approach and questions. Individual objectives of each workshop were: (a) define research questions, define intended project outcomes and discuss the approach; (b) outline the approach guided by a four-stage IKT framework [ ]; and (c) finalize key components of the project by ensuring agreement on all key decisions. Along with usual preparation material, infographics synthesizing relevant evidence were circulated prior to the workshop. This included evidence around exercise to reduce falls, behaviour change, exercise and physical activity in ethnically diverse communities [ – ]. This provided all team members with a shared understanding of the evidence-based elements that would inform intervention development. Prompt questions were used to generate respectful discussion and debate regarding the objectives of the workshops.
Stage 2: Identifying key elements of the intervention, involving knowledge user informants (participants in the study).
This stage involved broader engagement of knowledge-user informants as research participants (as opposed to partners in the research) [ ]. Knowledge-user informants included people with lived experience as an older person in an ethnically diverse community and service providers (e.g. physiotherapists or healthcare managers) who may be involved in delivery or support implementation of an exercise programme aimed at reducing falls in people aged over 65 years. Knowledge-user informants were involved on a consultative level to provide contextual insights and cultural expertise within the local context (metropolitan Melbourne, Australian), regarding what is needed in an intervention to build exercise habits in ethnically diverse people aged over 65 years to reduce falls. Older, ethnically diverse participants were recruited from local health services via clinicians working with people aged over 65 years from ethnically diverse communities, from ethnically diverse community groups where the research team held information sessions regarding the co-production project and via social media. Service providers were recruited by advertising the study via social media, relevant associations and organizations, special interest online groups, health service communications and social media posts by the research team or university website. Seed questions based on the theoretical domains framework [ – ] were used in workshops to prompt discussion about barriers and facilitators for engagement in exercise for falls prevention. Data from these workshops were critical to inform the development of the intervention and the programme theory, i.e., how the intervention was expected to achieve the desired outcomes [ ]. Language specific workshops (in Cantonese, Mandarin, Arabic and Italian) supported by interpreters, and bi-cultural community leaders were held for older, ethnically diverse community members. To minimize power differentials, separate workshops were held for service providers (e.g. healthcare managers, physiotherapists and allied health assistants). Mini workshops and individual interviews were also offered to optimize potential participants’ ability to participate when required.
Stage 3: Prototype development, involving the co-production team
This stage included one hybrid in-person/teleconference workshop with the research team and follow-up online meetings. Two new lived-experience team members identified in Stage 2 joined the research team in Stage 3, therefore Stage 1 infographic resources were recirculated to ensure a shared understanding of the evidence. Prior to the main workshops, lived experience team members met with member(s) of the research team with experience in co-production (C.M.S. and E.R.) to discuss the workshop structure and aims, identify strategies to optimize their ability to engage, address questions and identify support needed for attendance at the workshop (e.g. travel). In the week leading to the workshop, a summary of findings from Stage 2 was circulated, along with the workshop objectives and timeline. The main objectives were to determine key elements of the intervention using the team’s expertise and knowledge gained from Stage 2, with a focus on ensuring the intervention was feasible to deliver within the proposed budget and timelines.
Stage 4: Adaptation of the intervention prototype, involving the knowledge-user informants. This stage involved two parts (a and b):
Part (a) involved three prototype adaptation workshops with service providers and older, ethnically diverse knowledge-user informants. Workshops were conducted in English and included participants from multiple cultural communities; therefore, only people who did not require an interpreter were included. Workshops involved presentation of the intervention prototype including examples of resources used to support delivery. Seeding questions were used to explore the programme’s acceptability and feasibility, perceptions of participants’ motivation or ability to complete the programme along with barriers and facilitators to the programme’s implementation. Issues identified through the workshops were initially discussed by the research team’s operational staff (project lead, project manager and research assistants), who devised potential adaptations to address the issues. These were circulated by email to the broader research team for comment and discussion at the team’s monthly meeting for resolution.
Part (b) involved people aged over 65 years from ethnically diverse communities trialling the intervention prototype. Participants received one MOVE Together: Reduce falls session with an interpreter as required. To identify potential adaptions needed to optimize the prototype, participants completed a survey based on the Theoretical Framework of Acceptability Generic Questionnaire [ ] and semi-structured interviews focusing on the intervention’s acceptability, fidelity and feasibility [ , , ].
Stage 5: Consensus involving the co-production team. This stage was introduced as an outcome of Stage 1 to ensure consensus that the intervention was ready for clinical trial.
This workshop was conducted via video conference and used an adapted nominal group technique methodology for consensus [ – ]. A summary of Stage 4 findings with potential strategies to ameliorate threats to the success of the intervention was circulated in advance along with other preparation material. During the workshop, time was provided for the team to discuss findings and determine the adaptations needed to optimize the MOVE Together: Reduce falls programme. Team members were then given a brief period to consider if they agreed or disagreed that “the intervention was sufficiently developed to be tested in a pilot”, by completing an anonymous poll. An 80% agreement was set a priori as the minimum required for the intervention, in its current format, to progress to pilot testing.

Challenges experienced and strategies applied in our project were identified directly by the co-production team (authors) through our reflections, which have been narratively summarized. These reflections are supported by data from multiple sources including notes from debriefing about the co-production process, detailed background data (e.g. team meeting minutes, field notes and feedback emails) [ ] and the iterative feedback provided by our team who contributed to writing, editing and revision of this work.

## Co-production workshop outcomes
This section details how our co-production team worked together from project development through to the final prototype of the intervention ready for piloting. We highlight key challenges and strategies, summarized in Table  .

 In June 2022 the team were informed that the co-production grant had been awarded; the project officially began once the embargo was lifted in October 2022. This allowed additional clinicians to join the project as salaried research assistants and one postdoctoral researcher increased the time devoted to the project. Two people with lived experience joined the project part-way through the co-production process. They were both leaders of community groups involved in the early stage of the co-production process and were keen to be involved in the project in a larger way.
Challenges experienced while building our collaborative team during this time included the grant-related application processes and constraints, a lack of dedicated funding and optimizing communication across the team. As a result of some of these difficulties and time pressures, not all non-researchers were named on early grant applications. However, there was a clear understanding from all members of the team that team membership was inclusive of everyone who contributed.
To ensure transparency and offer opportunity for input from all team members into all areas of the project, once funding was received, overarching strategies were formally implemented including: regular meetings of whole research team (monthly), additional informal meetings with specific team members as required (e.g. people with lived experience) and weekly/fortnightly [ ] meetings about progress activities.
To overcome the challenges of distance and reduce time requirements for attendance, we used video conferencing for most research team meetings and workshops. Family member assistance and our research team supported our older, ethnically diverse team members to navigate the technology and software as required. Our team also reflects that video conferencing reduced non-verbal communication (particularly when using gallery view), which may be especially important to convey messages when team members do not share the same first language. Strategies that our team felt assisted with reading of non-verbal cues via video conference included pinning the main speaker to the screen to optimize visualization while they were speaking, and iterative summaries of workshop/meeting outcomes throughout the meeting to ensure a shared understanding of the discussion.
Stage 1: Start-up and planning, involving the research co-production team
We defined the research aims, questions and intended outcomes of the project. We then identified the who, what, how, when and where of Stages 2, 3 and 4 of the co-production process. Over the three workshops we reflected on decisions to ensure their consistency with the grant applications our team had developed to ensure the feasibility of the project. Recognition and respect of each team member’s specific expertise was essential to ensure the project design would support an evidence-based intervention (drawing on expertise of researchers) that was, relevant and implementable (drawing on the contextual expertise of people with lived experience as older, ethnically diverse community members and service providers). Adequate preparation and time for discussion facilitated shared understanding and agreement regarding key decisions within the team. Stage 1 workshops were completed prior to ethics submission, and therefore, prior to participant recruitment. Amelioration strategies to overcome potential challenges of the co-production process identified at this stage included the need to build relationships with ethno-specific community groups; engaging community venues; utilizing interpreters, culturally responsive staff and experienced facilitators; briefing and de-briefing for people involved in the running of the workshops; and participant remuneration to support workshops with ethnically diverse community members. Further, complicated participant information sheets and consent forms based on recommended templates from the ethics committees were identified by our team as a major barrier to participation owing to varying levels of literacy in preferred languages. To address this we developed brief information statements.
Stage 2: Identifying key elements of the intervention, involving knowledge-user informants.
Overall, 69 participants took part in over 11 workshops, including 5 workshops involving 58 people aged over 65 years from ethnically diverse (Chinese, Arabic-speaking and Italian) communities. All older participants were recruited from community sources despite efforts to recruit through healthcare services. In total, 39 (67%) people aged over 65 years from ethnically diverse communities indicated the need for an interpreter, and 45 (78%) were women. Six workshops involved 11 service provider participants (100% women).
The inclusion of bi-cultural workers in our project was particularly important for people for whom this was new and potentially overwhelming. Our team worked with community leaders to identify preferences for how catering was sourced. Travel to workshops was identified as an important barrier to workshops success. To support workshop participation, our team budgeted for taxi vouchers and gift cards to recognize participant contributions. However, a change in university procedures to systems which relied on use of technology and English language proficiency (i.e. use of Uber and e-gift cards) posed an unexpected challenge. Evidence that we had consulted extensively with stakeholders assisted with our exemption application to address this challenge, enabling the use of physical gift cards and taxi vouchers. Workshop/interview summaries that required translation delayed the member checking process. Despite efforts to optimize participation, six (8%) of those who consented could not participate: three due to other commitments and three people aged over 65 years from ethnically diverse communities could not participate owing to the travel distance required. Scheduling convenient service provider workshops proved challenging. Further details of strategies and challenges are included in Table  .
Stage 3: Prototype development, involving the co-production team
The 3-hour workshop involved 10 team members attending in person and four attending virtually. Ten team members were unable to attend; however, they were provided with the opportunity to provide feedback before and after the workshop. The outcomes of the meeting included resolution of conflicting preferences for elements of the intervention. Three expert subgroups were set up to further define key aspects of the intervention: one to define the resources needed to support people aged over 65 years from ethnically diverse communities; another to further develop the intervention’s structure and content, and identify elements required for intervention therapist training; and the final group to define strategies to promote the programme. Research team members were given the option to self-nominate preferences for groups, and the project lead also invited some team members to join the different subgroups to ensure an appropriate mix of expertise.
Challenges during this stage included workshop logistics related to an event requiring significant travel, the large amount of content to be addressed during this stage and optimizing communication across the team. Comprehensive workshop preparation, hybrid workshop attendance options, operational research team staff and a committed team who utilized their networks were important in this stage. An unexpected logistical challenge that occurred on the day of the Stage 3 workshops was a prolonged telecommunications outage affecting one of our country’s major providers of phone and internet services. The flow-on effect of this limited ability of some members to attend the workshop in person. To support transport for our ethnically diverse lived experience team members, our operational research team members communicated via available networks to rearrange transport and provide alternate contact details. One outstanding item not resolved in the main workshop was the name of the intervention. Naming the intervention posed additional challenges beyond finding an appropriate English title, as the team needed to consider how potential names translated in three different languages and their associated nuanced meanings. Expertise of team members with experience as people aged over 65 years in ethnically diverse communities and language services provided critical and timely information regarding the translation of the proposed names of the intervention to inform the groups final decision. Further details regarding challenges and strategies to ameliorate these are included in Table  .
Stage 4: Adaptation of the intervention protocol prototype, involving the knowledge-user informants
Part 4 (a): Adaptation workshops involved three workshops with two service providers, and eight people aged over 65 years from Chinese, Italian and Arabic-speaking communities. All but three participants had taken part in Stage 2. Four issues were identified that could impact the success of the intervention. Email correspondence and discussion at the regular team meeting confirmed the adaptations to be made to the prototype.
Part 4 (b) of the MOVE Together: Reduce falls programme involved six people aged over 65 years from ethnically diverse communities trialling the programme and one physiotherapist. Four people aged over 65 years (67%) of Stage 4 (b) participants had taken part in Stage 2, and two participants (33%) required an interpreter. Ethnically diverse people aged over 65 years who trialled the programme provided their views on the prototype. Participants put forward suggestions regarding the various elements of the intervention. While overall response to the programme was very positive, the ethicality of whether interventions should specifically target individual ethnic groups was raised. While the team determined that no adaptations to the intervention were required, they reflected that it was important to highlight that the aim of the programme was to specifically address known inequities in health care. The ultimate vision of the team was to develop a programme which was inclusive of people regardless of their cultural backgrounds; however, they recognized that (to date) the emphasis was to support engagement for people from Chinese, Arabic-speaking and Italian communities. While no major adaptations were made at this time, the outcomes of this stage informed the language to frame the programme.
The challenge of building a shared understanding of the ideas and opinions of knowledge user informants from different backgrounds (professional and cultural) within a workshop to achieve consensus was addressed through skilled facilitation and limiting the workshop to people who did not require interpreters. However, we were able to include people requiring interpreters in Part 4 (b) of this stage. Another challenge we experienced was the participation of service providers in the in-person workshops. While we offered options for a number of small workshops only two service providers participated in Stage 4 (a) workshops, highlighting the challenge of including service providers in longer workshops in community locations. Further details regarding challenges and strategies applied in this stage are outlined in Table  .
Stage 5: Consensus, involving the co-production team
The stage 5 workshop was attended by 18 (82%) team members. Discussion of the intervention did not lead to any further adaptations of the intervention. Overall, 17 took part in the anonymous voting. The facilitator of the workshop abstained because the voting system did not allow the facilitator to vote. All 17 votes agreed the intervention was ready for pilot trial.

## Knowledge user informant participation and experience
In total, 62 knowledge-user informants aged over 65 years from ethnically diverse communities provided consent to participate, and 58 did. Overall, 45 (78%) of the 58 who took part in workshops were female, 34% spoke Arabic, 19% spoke Cantonese, 16% spoke Mandarin and 31% spoke Italian. Further, 39 (67%) of these knowledge-user informants indicated they required an interpreter. Thirteen service providers consented including eight physiotherapists, an exercise physiologist, an allied health assistant, a health service manager, a general practitioner and a community liaison/integration professional. Eleven service providers attended workshops or interviews. Six of the service providers identified a language other than English as their primary language. Table provides a summary of the responses from the participant experience survey which included five questions, and each had four response options. Overall, the vast majority (≥90%) of 78 survey responses identified that the project aims were clearly explained, they were provided with support to participate and opportunity to share ideas and opinions, were satisfied with the workshop processes and that researchers respected the views of workshop participants.

 

## Recommendations and discussion
Our team of ethnically diverse community members, members of ethnically diverse organizations, clinicians and academic researchers set out in partnership to co-produce an intervention to support sustained exercise to reduce falls in older adults from ethnically diverse communities. Co-production alone does not guarantee engagement. For example, previous projects have failed to involve non-English speaking ethnically diverse participants [ ] despite resources for interpreters. The high satisfaction from participants and strong consensus within the co-production team suggests our rigorous approach to co-production was successful. However, the process did present challenges. Limited resources (e.g. time and money) and a lack of pre-existing processes and structures to support co-production have previously been reported as constraints to co-production efforts [ , , – ]. These constraints were ever-present in our project, and at times, meant pragmatic decisions were made to ensure completion. Thorough planning to identify potential challenges allowed us to prepare for many of these in advance. Furthermore, our engaged and committed team with diverse expertise across culture, language, community and healthcare ensured we could react quickly and appropriately when unforeseen challenges arose. Using an autoethnographic approach we have reflected on strategies that facilitated effective co-production in our study. Figure  summarizes our recommendations, and the details relating to our recommendations are provided below.
 

## Building and working with your team
When co-producing research, nurturing the researcher–knowledge-user relationship is as important as the scientific approach itself [ ]. The co-production principles of power sharing, recognition and respect of different sources of knowledge, communication and inclusiveness [ ] were important to nurture our team’s working relationship. Strategies used to support this were provided in the previous section (e.g. setting expectations, clear communication and feedback loops). On a practical level, clear, transparent processes (outlined in Table  ) were also important to facilitate the relationship because they helped build trust and provided opportunities for the team to identify areas of the research to which they wanted to contribute. We had a core operational team who had funded time to contribute to the project and took responsibility for progression, timelines, project administration and open and transparent communication. For our team, this was critical to the timely completion and maintaining relationships and connection of team members to the project.
Effective facilitation of co-production is challenging but essential for developing trust, empathy and collective commitment in co-production [ ]. Consistent with this research, our team identified that effective facilitation of team workshops was important to their success. This included giving time for all team members to share their experiences and ideas to achieve co-learning, shared understanding, and solutions to address needs of ethnically diverse communities involved in research. Given the time-limited workshops providing this space for people to share required pre-planning. Operationally, this meant the facilitator for the co-production team’s workshops would go into each workshop with a few prioritized key outcomes required to progress the project. The facilitator ensured there were mechanisms in place that were acceptable to the team for resolution of lower priority decisions that could not be covered in the workshop timeframe.
Distributing leadership within the co-production team is suggested to facilitate power sharing, for example, rotating meeting chairs and agenda settings [ ]. However, leadership requires additional time and, in some instances, training. Practically, the majority of members did not have the capacity to take on shared leadership roles and roles such as setting agendas and chairing meetings fell to team members whose roles were supported via grant funding (i.e. the operations team made up of clinicians and researchers). We believe that insisting research team members take on leadership responsibility would have compromised the diversity and expertise within the team. This highlights the tensions of collaborative research partnerships, for which there is no one-size-fits-all approach [ ].
We aimed to ensure key decisions were made by the co-production team; however, what is a key decision is subjective. In our case, wether a decision was deemed a ‘key decision’ was often determined by the operational (and paid) research team who spent the majority of time on the project and had the greatest opportunity to reflect on the project. While we know this process was feasible, we cannot be certain that this did not compromise optimization of power sharing. However, we would like to emphasize that power sharing does not equate to equal power, and that the IKT approach to co-production does not necessitate sharing of procedural responsibility, rather, the sharing of research decisions. Furthermore, we caution against an approach that would restrict partnership of knowledge users who do not have capacity to take on leadership roles, as this may come at the expense of limiting diversity in relevant expertise. This is particularly relevant when working with people from ethnically diverse communities, where potential partners may not feel confident reading or writing in the dominant language. In addition, experiences from research teams involved in ethnically diverse co-production suggest not all people aspire to take on substantial roles within research teams [ ]. We used strategies to optimize open communication (Table  ) to facilitate transparency, shared decisions and power.
Addressing power imbalance is a universal challenge of co-production [ ]. However, the context of the power imbalance may impact the strategies employed to ameliorate power imbalance [ ]. We did not identify any culturally specific strategies to address power imbalance. However, our team took a culturally responsive approach, which requires awareness, knowledge and skills within the team [ , ], and endeavoured to embed processes consistent with the preferences of our ethnically diverse team. Our team had a strong commitment to improving healthcare for people from ethnically diverse communities, and everyone had lived experience either working with ethnically diverse communities or as a member of an ethnically diverse community. Some of our team already had strong skills in cultural responsiveness (e.g. our language service manager, a team member whose position in an ethnically diverse advocacy organization directly supports culturally responsive care for older Australians) and provided important guidance and role modelling of cultural responsiveness. We tried to develop an environment which was respectful of cultural differences and encouraged people to be reflective of their own knowledge gaps and biases. We believe this culturally responsive approach was critical to facilitate power sharing within our team, for the development of effective relationships with our community networks and building trust within our workshops.

## Work with relevant institutions
Research and institutional structures (e.g. ethical approval, funding body requirements and university processes) have been identified as potential barriers to co-production research [ , ]. Our experience co-producing with ethnically diverse communities identified additional structural barriers. For example, we applied for a university exemption to support participant remuneration that was feasible and acceptable to our ethnically diverse participants. We gained ethical approval of brief information statements to complement traditional participant information statements to facilitate understanding and acceptability of study information provided to our ethnically diverse participants. We also experienced substantial time delays related to additional ethical and governance approval of translated resources. Our experience highlights the importance of working with institutions and ethics committees to advocate and negotiate to facilitate research that meets the needs of ethnically diverse communities.

## Translation and working with interpreters
Limited time and resources are challenges to co-production that have been previously reported [ , , ], and co-production with ethnically diverse communities may increase these demands [ ]. In our experience, additional time was required for institutional and ethical processes, logistical processes (i.e. booking interpreters, time for translation of workshops transcripts) and individual processes (additional time for consent and interpreting in some workshops). Time was also critical to building connections with ethnically diverse community groups, which was essential to the success of our project. Therefore, we recommend when planning co-production with ethnically diverse community groups to ensure adequate time is allowed to accommodate processes. Translation and interpreting require financial resources, which our team considered a non-negotiable cost of working effectively and inclusively with ethnically diverse communities. Consistent with our experience, previous research has identified translation may be a barrier to inclusion of people with limited English language skills [ ]. Therefore, ensuring adequate budget for translation should be a priority for ethnically diverse co-production. While our project used and benefited from certified translation to optimize communication, we would also echo previous research in co-production, which recommends review by community members to ensure language is pitched at the right level and minimize use of academic or technocratic language [ ].
Inadequate interpreting services could compromise outcomes of projects involving ethnically diverse communities [ ]. Professional interpreters are reported to improve clinical outcomes, satisfaction, reduce communication errors and improve disparities in healthcare utilization [ ]. Consistent with this, our project aimed to utilize Australian certified interpreters for all interpreting; we believe this improved the overall experience and accuracy of information throughout the project. In the United States, use of trained medical interpreters has been found to reduce communication error and improve patient satisfaction [ ]. While Australian certified interpreters were available in Cantonese, Mandarin, Italian and Arabic, for some languages, certification is not yet available, limiting the generalizability of this strategy. Furthermore, we still experienced challenges related to interpreting. While the vast majority of interpreting that took place in our project occurred in a professional manner, a minority of interpreters exhibited unprofessional behaviour. Our co-production team member with extensive experience interpreting and managing interpreting services was integral to navigating these issues and optimizing our use of interpreting services. Furthermore, this team member has helped our team develop guidance for the piloting stage of our intervention and clinical trial, including managing any issues and optimizing communication via interpreters. Guidance for working effectively with interpreters highlights the potential importance of briefing and debriefing with an interpreter [ ]. We found this strategy facilitated a shared understanding of the objectives of the encounter, rapport development, to make corrections (if required) and identify need for further information.

## Identifying bi-cultural workers
Involving bi-cultural workers may facilitate cultural responsiveness, foster connection and enhance communication and understanding when working with ethnically diverse communities [ – ]. We included bi-cultural workers in our project after some members of our team identified their inclusion as important to facilitate engagement of older, ethnically diverse community members. Bi-cultural workers face challenges and complexity in their roles including community expectation, as well as culture and value conflicts [ ]. Support and relevant training has been recommended for bi-cultural workers [ ]. Currently, there is no universal definition of the bi-cultural worker, and therefore, their specific role and skill requirements remain unclear. Our team spent time identifying bi-cultural workers for our study who had relevant expertise to ensure they met the needs of the project to ensure their successful integration into the project. In our project, it was important to clarify that the role of bi-cultural workers was to support participants, so that participants were comfortable and confident to share their ideas and opinions, as opposed to bi-cultural workers providing their own opinions on participants or community views. We believe the briefing of bi-cultural workers prior to workshops to clarify their roles and scope within the project contributed to achieving this outcome and their successful integration.

## Shared understanding of constraints
Our team identified that co-production within a research paradigm requires negotiation and concession. We believe building a shared understanding of project constraints enabled our research team to work effectively together. Our project involved the co-production of an intervention ready for a clinical pilot trial, therefore elements of the intervention had to be feasible for implementation within the realities of a clinical trials. We also had to comply with various criteria and conditions of funding and trial governance. Honest conversations and clear communication regarding the project and its aims facilitated this within our research team. However, we did identify that these conversations are also very important with our ethnically diverse community knowledge-user informants contributing to the project. We identified a mismatch between the timeline expectations for the intervention development between some of our knowledge-user informants from ethnically diverse communities. This highlights the importance of ensuring clear communication with knowledge user informants, who are not involved in the research team, to ensure they understand that rigorous intervention development takes time. Our lived experience research partners understood the time requirements of our project, and therefore emphasized the importance of developing strategies to maintain engagement with the ethnically diverse communities involved in the intervention co-production to support our ongoing working relationship as we move towards clinical trials.

## Limitations
Our evaluation highlights our success in providing a respectful, safe and productive co-production space, which produced an intervention ready for testing in a clinical trial. However, our approach did have limitations. Co-production projects must, at some point, limit the size of the co-production team to ensure genuine partnership. Pragmatic decisions may also be required to ensure project feasibility. This means that additional knowledge and expertise that may have further enhanced outcomes may have been missed. For example, our team did not include any people who did not speak English, as this would have increased the time and costs of the project, and we made the pragmatic decision that workshops in Stage 4 (a) would not be supported by interpreters. However, we were successful at engaging non-English speaking participants, who reported high levels of satisfaction and the perception that they were respected and heard during the process.
The challenges and strategies to facilitate co-design with ethnically diverse communities were identified by our co-production team who had first hand, in-depth contextual knowledge of the process. We believe this facilitated relevant and meaningful recommendations discussed in reference to existing literature. However, we cannot rule out that an independent evaluation external to our team may have had alternative findings.
We included three ethnically diverse communities with culturally (and geographically) diverse origins to maximize generalizability of our experience to ethnically diverse communities and recommendations for co-producing interventions with ethnically diverse communities. Given the importance of community and context, it is likely that working with other ethnically diverse communities will uncover further learnings. Indigenous communities were not included in this research because coproduction with indigenous communities requires the application of recommendations and consideration specific to these communities, for example those relating to indigenous data sovereignty [ , ]. Thus, our findings may not generalize to co-production with Indigenous communities. A comprehensive evaluation of co-produced research should be multifactorial, considering the impact external to the co-production team (systems, sustainment, people and group levels), and within the coproduction team (group processes, people and research processes levels) [ ]. Further evaluation of the implementability and effectiveness of the MOVE Together: Reduce falls programme is planned and will shed further light on the impact of the co-production process.

## Conclusions
Reports of co-production of interventions for clinical trials involving ethnically diverse community members are scarce. However, co-production with ethnically diverse communities has strong potential to address under-representation in research and facilitate health equity. Our exemplar highlights the potential importance of adequate time and resources, effective communication strategies, development of trust and ensuring a culturally responsive approach in co-production with ethnically diverse communities. Furthermore, we identified additional considerations when co-producing interventions for ethnically diverse community members within a research paradigm, including the need for a shared team understanding of project constraints and addressing structural barriers. We intend our reflections to encourage further discussion and research regarding optimization of the co-production of healthcare interventions with ethnically diverse communities for clinical trials and its impact on health equity.
