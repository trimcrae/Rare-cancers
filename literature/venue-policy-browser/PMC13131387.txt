## Abstract
Alcohol-serving venues in East Africa are high-risk environments for HIV transmission but few studies in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) have examined perceived HIV risk and sexual risk behaviors by alcohol use levels on a broader population of persons at alcohol-serving venues in the era of biomedical HIV prevention. We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of adults (≥ 18 years) at alcohol-serving venues in eight communities (~ 10,000 residents/community) in rural western Kenya and southwestern Uganda. We distributed recruitment cards to patrons and workers and invited them for free HIV screening at nearby health facilities, with same-day initiation of HIV pre- or post-exposure prophylaxis or antiretroviral therapy (ART) if eligible. Using generalized estimating equations with adjustment for age, sex, and country, we assessed whether increasing levels of alcohol use, as measured by the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test–Consumption (AUDIT-C), categorized as no/low use (< 4 for men, < 3 for women), medium use (4–5 for men, 3–5 for women), high use (6–7), and very high use (≥ 8) were predictive of perceived HIV risk (current or anticipated in the next three months) and four HIV-risk factors (i.e., sex with a partner with HIV or unknown HIV status, multiple sexual partners, transactional sex, and sexually transmitted infections) among persons without HIV. We also evaluated the association between AUDIT-C levels and untreated HIV (defined as newly diagnosed or previously diagnosed but out of care for ≥ 6 months) among persons with HIV and between AUDIT-C levels and HIV-risk factors among persons with untreated HIV. We distributed 9,375 recruitment cards at 480 venues: 7,744 (83%) adults participated in screening at health facilities (median age 34 years; 38% women; 91% patrons). Overall, 4,107 (53%) reported unhealthy alcohol use: 26% medium, 11% high, 16% very high. Of 6,124 (79%) persons who tested HIV-negative, 2,285 (37%) reported perceived HIV risk. Increasing alcohol use was significantly associated with higher odds of perceived HIV risk (medium use: adjusted odds ratio (aOR) = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.17–1.58, p < 0.001; high: aOR = 2.06, 95%CI: 1.71–2.47, p < 0.001; and very high: aOR = 2.07, 95%CI: 1.74–2.47, p < 0.001; vs. no/low alcohol use) as well as sexual risk factors. Of 1,620 (21%) PWH, 219 (14%) had untreated HIV: 141 (9%) newly diagnosed; 78 (5%) previously diagnosed, not on ART. Increasing levels of alcohol use were not associated with untreated HIV. Our findings highlight the ongoing need for interventions to reduce alcohol use and improve engagement in biomedical HIV prevention and treatment among adults at alcohol-serving venues in East Africa.
Keywords: Alcohol, HIV risk, Untreated HIV, Alcohol-serving venues

## Introduction
Alcohol use remains a significant public health problem, accounting for 4.6% of the global burden of disease and injury [ ]. Unhealthy alcohol use, defined as a pattern of drinking that increases an individual’s risk of negative health consequences [ ], is responsible for 2.6 million deaths every year [ ] and is a persistent public health concern in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). According to the Global Status Report on Alcohol and Health 2024, the African region had the highest-levels of alcohol-attributable deaths per 100,000 and an 8.8% relative increase in alcohol use disorders between 2010 and 2019 [ ].
Several studies have evaluated the intersection between alcohol use and HIV in SSA, including Kenya and Uganda, and have shown that alcohol use heightens the risk of HIV acquisition [ – ]. People with alcohol use are almost twice as likely to acquire HIV compared to those who do not drink alcohol [ , ]. Alcohol consumption can impair judgment and decrease inhibitions, thereby reducing the ability to negotiate safer sexual practices and increasing vulnerability to HIV infection [ ]. Thus, persons with alcohol use are more likely to engage in sexual behaviors that increase HIV risk, such as condomless sex [ , ], sex with multiple partners [ ] or partners of unknown HIV status, and transactional sex [ ]. The risk of HIV extends further to those who spend time at alcohol-serving venues, independent of alcohol use, because of the increased probability of meeting high-risk sexual partners [ ]. As such, alcohol-serving venues are potentially high-yield locations to identify persons at increased risk of HIV who would benefit from HIV prevention services.
Several studies across sub-Saharan Africa have demonstrated a dose-response relationship between alcohol use and HIV risk behaviors, with hazardous and harmful drinking consistently linked to increased unprotected sex, multiple sexual partners, higher exposure to sexual and physical violence, and significantly elevated HIV incidence [ , – ]. A 2007 systematic review summarized evidence from prior studies in sub-Saharan Africa and found that increasing levels of alcohol consumption are associated with increased sexual and HIV risk behaviors in an era pre-dating biomedical HIV prevention and universal antiretroviral treatment (ART) for persons with HIV [ ]. More recently, a study among fishing communities in central Uganda found a dose-response relationship between alcohol use and incident HIV [ ]. In these studies, alcohol use was determined in heterogeneous ways, including use of number of drinks per week [ ], number of drinking days, or past-month use [ , ]. Our study provides an updated evaluation of the association between levels of alcohol use determined by Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Test-Consumption (AUDIT-C), and perceived HIV risk and HIV sexual risk behaviors among a large sample of persons at alcohol serving venues in Kenya and Uganda in the era of biomedical HIV prevention. The AUDIT-C has been extensively validated in previous studies and shown to perform as well as the full AUDIT at detecting unhealthy alcohol use [ – ]. Our study also evaluates perceived HIV risk, which is a key determinant in the uptake and adherence to biomedical HIV prevention interventions, as individuals who do not view themselves as being at risk are less likely to utilize HIV prevention methods or services compared to those who recognize their susceptibility [ – ]. An updated understanding of how HIV risk – including perceived HIV risk – varies at different levels of alcohol use could guide the optimization of HIV prevention efforts, including alcohol-focused harm reduction, venue-based HIV screening with referral to biomedical HIV prevention services, condom distribution, and policy changes.
Alcohol use also negatively impacts HIV care cascade outcomes among persons with HIV (PWH). Persons with HIV and alcohol use are less likely to have been previously diagnosed; if diagnosed, they are less likely to be on ART, and if on ART, less likely to adhere to treatment [ ] or to be suppressed [ , ]. A study in central Uganda found that persons with HIV with medium to very high alcohol use had significantly lower odds of being on ART [ ]. Another study in Kenya and Uganda showed that alcohol use was associated with delays in ART initiation [ ]. Thus, a better understanding of the proportion of persons with untreated HIV (whether undiagnosed or with known HIV but out of care), and how risk of untreated HIV varies by level of alcohol use among persons who attend or work at alcohol-serving venues (i.e., outside of clinical settings) is needed to optimize HIV care cascade outcomes at a population level.
We examined the associations between levels of alcohol use (as determined by AUDIT-C screening), perceived HIV risk, untreated HIV, and four HIV-risk factors (i.e., sex with a partner with HIV or unknown HIV status, multiple sexual partners, transactional sex, and sexually transmitted infection (STI) diagnosis) among individuals recruited from alcohol-serving venues in rural western Kenya and southwestern Uganda. We hypothesized that increasing levels of alcohol use would be positively associated with greater perceived HIV risk among persons without HIV, greater risk of having untreated HIV among persons with HIV, and higher engagement in high-risk sexual behaviors in both groups.

## Methods

## Study Design and Setting
We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of baseline data (June 2023 to January 2025) from the ongoing Outreach and Prevention at Alcohol venues in East Africa (OPAL-East Africa) cluster randomized trial (NCT05862857). OPAL-East Africa aims to compare multi-disease (intervention) vs. HIV-focused (control) mobilization strategies to promote biomedical HIV prevention uptake among adults (≥ 18 years) at alcohol-serving venues in eight rural communities (with ~ 10,000 residents/community) in western Kenya and southwestern Uganda. At baseline, study staff identified and mapped all alcohol-serving venues, including bars, nightclubs, taverns, informal drinking dens, and any other locations (such as restaurants or lodges) in the community where commercial or locally produced alcohol is served across the eight communities. Common locally produced alcohol-containing drinks included busaa and changaa in Kenya, and tonto, malwa, and waragi in Uganda. After mapping and obtaining consent from venue owners, staff visited all participating venues, and distributed recruitment cards ( and ) to patrons or workers, estimated to be ≥ 18 years (the legal age for alcohol use in Kenya and Uganda), inviting them to the nearby government health facility for HIV screening (as well as screening for multiple conditions, if in the multi-disease trial arm).

## Data Collection and Procedures
Participants who presented to the health facility with a recruitment card and met the following inclusion criteria were consented and enrolled: 1) patron or worker, 2) ≥ 18 years, and 3) recruited at an alcohol-serving venue in a study community. Participants completed a 20-minute interviewer-administered questionnaire in their preferred language. The questionnaire assessed socio-demographic characteristics, self-reported HIV status, self-reported HIV risk, past-3-month sexual behaviors (including having sex with a partner with HIV or unknown HIV status (yes/no), multiple sexual partners (yes/no), and transactional sex (yes/no); and current or past-3-month STI diagnosis), and alcohol use with the AUDIT-C survey, modified for past 3-month use [ ]. Survey questions about past-3-month sexual risk behavior and STI history were introduced later, after recruitment activities had commenced.
Participants were first asked whether they had a known HIV diagnosis (yes/no). Those reporting a prior diagnosis were asked if they had attended an HIV clinic for care in the past six months (the maximum period before returning to the clinic in multi-month dispensing in Kenya and Uganda) using the question “Have you ever seen a doctor or nurse for HIV care at a clinic in the past 6 months?” Participants who reported a clinic visit within this timeframe were considered to be in care, received adherence counseling, and were encouraged to remain engaged in treatment. Those who reported no HIV clinic visit in the past six months were classified as previously diagnosed HIV-positive but out of care and were immediately and actively linked to HIV care for ART initiation or re-initiation at the screening facility or a facility of their choice.
Participants without a known HIV diagnosis were asked about current and anticipated HIV risk using the questions: “Do you think you are currently at risk for HIV infection?” (yes/no), and “Do you think you will be at risk for HIV infection in the next 3 months?” (yes/no). We classified participants who answered yes to either of these questions as having perceived HIV risk.
All participants without a prior HIV diagnosis were offered HIV rapid antibody testing using Ministry of Health (MoH)-validated test kits with pre-and post-test counseling, in accordance with national MoH HIV testing algorithms in Kenya or Uganda [ , ]. Participants who tested HIV positive were classified as newly diagnosed and were immediately linked to HIV care for ART initiation at the same health facility or another of their choice. Participants who tested HIV-negative were offered same-day biomedical HIV prevention services, including oral pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) or non-occupational post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP), at the screening health facility.

## Outcome and Predictor Variables
The goal was to evaluate how alcohol use predicted HIV outcomes: perceived HIV risk among persons without HIV, untreated HIV among persons with HIV, and HIV-specific risk factors in both groups. We defined perceived HIV risk as self-reported current or anticipated HIV risk in the next three months, and untreated HIV as newly diagnosed HIV or self-reported HIV-positive status with no HIV clinic visit in the past six months. In addition, we evaluated four specific HIV risk factors as outcomes, based on participant self-report of engaging in each of the following in the past three months: sex with a partner with HIV or unknown HIV status, multiple sexual partners, transactional sex, or current or past three-month diagnosis/treatment of an STI. The main predictor variable was level of alcohol use determined by modified AUDIT-C score [ ], and categorized as: none/low-risk alcohol use (< 4 men, < 3 women), medium (4–5 men, 3–5 women), high (6–7), and very high-level alcohol use (≥ 8), consistent with prior literature [ , ]. We classified AUDIT-C ≥ 3 for women and or ≥ 4 for men as unhealthy.

## Statistical Analysis
We used descriptive statistics to characterize the population that participated in facility-based HIV screening, overall and stratified by country. Variables included sex, age, participant category (patron or worker), alcohol serving venue type, AUDIT-C level, marital status (married and living together vs. other status), education (none/primary vs. higher education), occupation (farmer vs. others), mobility, and visiting more than one venue in the past month (yes/no). We classified type of alcohol-serving venue from which participants were recruited as “formal” if licensed to operate and “informal” if operating without a license. We considered participants mobile if they reported any days away from their study community in the past 3 months. We also used descriptive statistics to summarize outcomes, overall and by country.
Using generalized estimating equations [ – ]., we examined the associations between levels of alcohol use, perceived HIV risk and specific HIV risk factors among participants without HIV. We adjusted for established, demographic risk factors, including age, sex, and country to provide estimates of how alcohol use predicted perceived HIV risk. Secondary analyses were unadjusted. We implemented analogous analyses to evaluate the associations between alcohol use and untreated HIV among persons with HIV. Among persons with untreated HIV, we limited evaluations of alcohol use and HIV-risk factors to outcomes with sufficient support (≥ 10 outcomes per coefficient) and did not adjust [ ]. All analyses accounted for clustering by venue via a working independent correlation matrix and robust (sandwich-based) standard errors. All analyses pooled over trial arm and were restricted to observations with complete data on relevant outcome and AUDIT-C level. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1.

## Results
A total of 530 alcohol-serving venues were mapped: 96 (18%) in Kenya and 434 (82%) in Uganda. Of these, 527 (99%) agreed to participate. Forty-one (8%) of the participating venues closed prior to recruitment and no recruitment occurred at 6 (1%) venues where no patrons or workers were present during recruitment visits. Ultimately, recruitment occurred at 480 (91%) venues: 89 (19%) in Kenya and 391 (81%) in Uganda. From July 2023 to January 2025, a total of 9,375 recruitment cards were distributed, out of which 7,744 (83%) adults came to the study health facilities and participated in HIV screening: 4,042 (52%) in Kenya and 3,702 (48%) in Uganda. Their median age was 34 years (IQR: 26–43); 2,970 (38%) were female; 6,592 (85%) were recruited from an informal venue; 4,151/7,641 (54%) were married and living with their spouse, and 6,523/7,172 (91%) were patrons. Of 7,727 (99.8%) participants with AUDIT-C scores, 3,620 (47%) reported low or no alcohol use, and 4,107 (53%) reported unhealthy alcohol use, with levels of unhealthy alcohol use shown in .
 Of 7,744 participants screened for HIV, 1,620 (21%) were identified as PWH, with a higher prevalence observed in western Kenya (25%) compared to southwestern Uganda (16%). Among PWH, 141 (9%) were newly diagnosed, with a substantially higher proportion in Uganda (18%) than in Kenya (3%). An additional 78 individuals (5%) had a prior HIV diagnosis but were not currently engaged in care, with similar proportions in Kenya (4%) and Uganda (6%). Of participants with HIV, 1,401 (86%) were already in care, more commonly in Kenya (92%) than in Uganda (77%). Among 6,124 (79%) participants who screened HIV negative, 2,285 (37%) perceived themselves to have HIV risk: 41% in western Kenya and 33% in southwestern Uganda. Specific HIV risk factors over the 3-months prior to enrollment reported by participants who screened HIV negative were as follows. Overall, 1,980/6,104 (32%) participants reported sex with a partner of unknown HIV status (with a higher proportion in Kenya (37%) compared to Uganda (28%)); 923/3,375 (27%) participants reported multiple sexual partners (30% in Kenya and 26% in Uganda), and 537/3,377 (16%) participants reported transactional sex (10% in Kenya and 20% in Uganda). Overall, 275/3,376 (8%) of participants reported a past-3-month STI diagnosis (4% in Kenya and 11% in Uganda; ).
 

## Level of Alcohol Use and Perceived HIV Risk and Sexual Risk Behavior among Persons Without HIV
Increasing levels of alcohol use were significantly associated with higher odds of self-reported elevated HIV risk. Compared to persons with no or low alcohol use, those with medium alcohol use had 1.36 times higher odds of perceiving themselves to have HIV risk (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 1.36; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.17–1.58; p < 0.001). Persons with high alcohol use had 2.06 times higher odds (aOR = 2.06; 95% CI: 1.71–2.49; p < 0.001), and those with very high alcohol use had 2.07 times higher odds (aOR = 2.07; 95% CI: 1.74–2.47; p < 0.001) ( ). Unadjusted analyses were similar ( ).
 Similar associations were observed between levels of alcohol use and specific self-reported HIV risk factors among participants without HIV ( ; ). Compared to those with no or low alcohol use, participants with medium, high, and very high alcohol use had 1.28 (p = 0.001), 1.94 (p < 0.001), and 2.15 (p < 0.001) times higher odds, respectively, of having a sexual partner with HIV or unknown status over the past 3 months. Similarly, the odds of having multiple sexual partners over the past 3 months were 1.50 (p < 0.001), 2.89 (p < 0.001), and 3.06 (p < 0.001) times higher for those with medium, high, and very high alcohol use, respectively. For past-3-month transactional sex, the odds were 1.36 (p = 0.02), 2.83 (p < 0.001), and 3.58 (p < 0.001), respectively. Finally, participants with high and very high alcohol use had significantly increased odds of reporting a past-3-month sexually transmitted infection diagnosis, at 2.82 (p < 0.001) and 4.80 (p < 0.001), respectively. Unadjusted analyses were again similar ( ) ( ).
 

## Level of Alcohol Use and Untreated HIV
Among persons with HIV, alcohol use was not predictive of having untreated HIV ( ). In unadjusted analyses, high and very high levels of alcohol use were borderline significant: OR = 1.56, 95% CI: 0.98–2.48, p = 0.06 and OR = 1.47, 95%CI 0.97–2.21, p = 0.07, respectively. However, after adjusting for age, sex, and country, the odds of untreated HIV were firmly non-significant across all alcohol use categories compared to no/low alcohol use: medium (aOR = 1.12, 95% CI: 0.75–1.65, p = 0.58), high (aOR = 1.18, 95% CI: 0.74–1.91, p = 0.49), very high (aOR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.57–1.38, p = 0.59). Among the 219 persons with untreated HIV who responded to sexual risk factor survey questions, 102/166 (61%) reported sex with someone with HIV or unknown status, 49/81 (60%) reported multiple sexual partners, 31/80 (39%) reported engaging in transactional sex, and 21/81 (26%) reported an STI diagnosis. In unadjusted analyses, only high and very high alcohol use were significantly associated with higher odds of having multiple sex partners; however, the confidence intervals were wide ( ).

## Discussion
In this cross-sectional study of adult patrons and workers recruited from alcohol-serving venues in rural Kenya and Uganda, we found that increasing levels of alcohol use were significantly associated with perceived HIV risk and sexual behaviors that increase risk of HIV acquisition. We also found that a high proportion (14%) of persons with HIV recruited from alcohol-serving venues had untreated HIV. Overall, our findings are consistent with prior research [ , ] and indicate that alcohol-serving venues remain sites of elevated HIV transmission risk in the universal HIV treatment era, thus highlighting the urgent need to reach and engage this population in both HIV prevention and treatment services. Our data suggest that interventions to reduce alcohol use, even short of abstinence, may reduce risk of HIV infection among adults at alcohol-serving venues. We observed a trend in which self-reported HIV risk and related behavioral risk factors increased with higher levels of alcohol use, suggesting a potential dose–response relationship between alcohol consumption and HIV vulnerability in this population.
Our finding that increasing levels of alcohol use are associated with increasing perceived HIV risk and sexual risk factors adds to prior, extensive literature on the relationship between unhealthy alcohol use and increased risk of HIV due to sexual behaviors, including sex without a condom, transactional sex and multiple partners [ , , ], which facilitate HIV transmission. These results are consistent with several other studies. For example, a meta-analysis from 2010 found that alcohol consumption increased risk of incident HIV by 77% compared to no alcohol consumption among adults [ ]. In a more recent study from East Africa, the overall population attributable fraction of incident HIV infections due to alcohol use was 64.1% among adults living in fishing communities on Lake Victoria in Uganda [ ]. Alcohol use significantly increased the risk of incident HIV (with an adjusted risk ratio of 2.07 for HIV serocon-version) in the SEARCH universal test and treat in rural Kenya and Uganda [ ]. Our study adds to this literature by evaluating levels of alcohol use by AUDIT-C among adults without HIV at alcohol-serving venues, demonstrating that as level of alcohol use increased, so did HIV risk and sexual risk behaviors. We also found that a high proportion (≥ 60%) of persons with untreated HIV at alcohol-serving venues reported sex with multiple partners and partners of unknown HIV status. Together, our findings demonstrate the persistent need for HIV testing, biomedical prevention and HIV treatment services that reach adults at alcohol-serving venues in rural East Africa in the era of universal HIV treatment and effective HIV biomedical prevention.
We did not find an association between levels of alcohol use and untreated HIV in our study. This lack of association contrasts with multiple prior studies that have found an association between higher levels of alcohol use and worse HIV care cascade outcomes [ , , ]. For example, a population-level cross-sectional study in rural Kenya and Uganda found that persons with heavy alcohol use were 13% less likely to have been diagnosed with HIV, and if diagnosed, 7% less likely to be on ART, compared to non-alcohol users [ ]. Potential explanations for this lack of a significant association include a relatively small sample size of persons with untreated HIV (N = 219) in our study, the combination of newly diagnosed PWH and previously diagnosed but out of care PWH, or possible under-reporting of alcohol use by persons with HIV, which may have attenuated the association. Despite this lack of association, the overall level of unhealthy alcohol use among persons with untreated HIV was high (59%, compared to 52% among persons with treated HIV), demonstrating the need for alcohol reduction interventions in this population. Furthermore, as noted above, approximately 60% of adults with untreated HIV who responded to sexual risk survey questions reported high-risk sexual behavior, including unprotected sex and multiple partners and a quarter reported engaging in transactional sex, demonstrating the urgent need for improving viral suppression among persons with HIV at alcohol-serving venues in rural East Africa. Overall, the high proportion of adults with untreated HIV (14%) we identified highlights the ongoing need for outreach to alcohol-serving venues, even in settings such as western Kenya, that are nearing or exceeding the UNAIDS 95-95-95 HIV care cascade targets [ ].
Our study had limitations. First, our study was cross-sectional, precluding our ability to infer causality. Our goal was to evaluate how alcohol use predicted perceived risk among persons without HIV, untreated HIV among those with HIV and specific HIV risk factors in both groups. While our analyses adjusted for age, sex, and country, we did not evaluate all possible individual-level predictors of these outcomes and did not control for all possible confounders. Second, our measure of level of alcohol use was based on self-reported alcohol use, using an AUDIT-C and was subject to social-desirability and recall bias [ ]. However, the AUDIT-C is a standardized survey that has been previously validated and allows for cross-study comparison [ ]. In addition, the AUDIT-C is inexpensive, easy to administer and can easily be integrated into HIV programs to identify individuals at highest risk of HIV. Despite potential social-desirability bias, we found a high proportion (53%) of participants reported unhealthy alcohol use. Third, we conducted our analyses among adults who participated in health facility-based HIV screening after receiving recruitment cards at alcohol-serving venues. We did not include adults who received recruitment cards but did not come to the health facilities. Such individuals may have relatively lower health seeking behavior than those who linked and participated in health facility screening, and consequently, may be at higher risk of HIV acquisition or untreated HIV. Nonetheless, participation in screening after recruitment from alcohol-serving venues was high (83%) in our study, and relatively lower linkage among adults at higher risk of HIV acquisition or untreated HIV does not impact the internal validity of our findings. Lastly, we introduced questions on sexual behavior and STIs were after participant recruitment had begun; consequently, the number of responses among individuals with untreated HIV was limited, restricting the scope of analyses within this subgroup.
Our study also has several strengths. We conducted comprehensive mapping and recruitment at all alcohol-serving venues in the study communities, and a high proportion of adults encountered in the venues subsequently participated in health center screening, strengthening the generaliz-ability of our findings to adults at alcohol-serving venues. Our study successfully reached and recruited a population at elevated risk for HIV acquisition and transmission. HIV prevalence among participants was 25% in western Kenya and 16% in southwestern Uganda—substantially higher than national prevalence estimates in both countries [ , ]. Notably, this was observed despite the high proportion of men enrolled (62%), a group that typically has lower HIV prevalence than women [ , ]. Importantly, the study identified 141 individuals with newly diagnosed HIV and linked them to same-day initiation of antiretroviral therapy (ART). In addition, our study reached a high proportion of men (62%) – a group that tends to be less likely than women to come to health facilities for screening in rural East Africa [ – ].
In conclusion, increasing levels of alcohol use were significantly associated with perceived HIV risk and engagement in HIV risk behaviors among adults recruited from alcohol-serving venues in rural Kenya and Uganda. The high proportion of adults without HIV who reported HIV risk, and with untreated HIV who reported high-risk sexual behavior, suggest that alcohol-serving venues remain sites of increased HIV transmission in rural East Africa. Our findings highlight the ongoing need for interventions to reduce alcohol use and to improve engagement in biomedical HIV prevention and HIV treatment among adults at alcohol-serving venues in East Africa. Introducing or strengthening screening for alcohol use in primary care and community settings, including alcohol-serving venues, could help to identify individuals at heightened risk and link them to biomedical HIV prevention services, STI screening, and HIV treatment. Interventions that simultaneously target unhealthy alcohol use and sexual risk reduction and promote HIV biomedical prevention use could play a crucial role in reducing HIV transmission in this population.
