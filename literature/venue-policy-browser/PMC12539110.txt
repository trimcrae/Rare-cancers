## Abstract

## Background
Thirdhand smoke (THS) is an emerging public health issue, posing risks during pregnancy, including chronic diseases, fetal harm, and cognitive-behavioral disorders in children. This study aims to design, implement, and evaluate an educational intervention to improve the awareness, beliefs, and practices of pregnant women in relation to thirdhand smoke exposure.

## Methods
This study, conducted in 2023–2024, evaluates an educational intervention’s impact on pregnant women’s knowledge, beliefs, and practices regarding thirdhand smoke (THS) exposure. It involves 100 pregnant women from four healthcare centers in Isfahan, Iran, selected by geographic zoning and randomly assigned to intervention or control groups (50 per group) via coin toss, with 5% type I error and 80% power. Data collection uses the Beliefs About Thirdhand Smoke (BATHS) questionnaire and a researcher-designed questionnaire on THS knowledge and practices. The study has three phases:
Phase 1: psychometric evaluation of questionnaires and pre-test implementation
• Psychometric evaluation of questionnaires for validity and reliability: to ensure the validity and reliability of the questionnaires, two groups of pregnant women (20 participants in each group—intervention and control) will complete the questionnaires. In addition, the educational content will be evaluated by a panel of at least 10 experts using indices such as the Content Validity Index (CVI), Clarity and Simplicity Index, Attractiveness Index, and Practicality Index.
• Pre-test implementation: awareness, belief, and behavior questionnaires regarding third-hand smoke exposure will be completed by pregnant women attending health centers in Isfahan, with 50 participants in each of the intervention and control groups.
Phase 2: design and implementation of the educational intervention
Based on the pre-test results, an educational intervention will be designed and implemented. The intervention will be conducted in group sessions, both in-person (individual and face-to-face) and virtually via online platforms. The educational content will include scientific information about the dangers of third-hand smoke, preventive strategies, and behavior change methods.
Phase 3: Post-test and analysis: 
Post-test one month post-intervention using the same questionnaires. Data analysis with SPSS v23 includes t-tests, chi-square, Kolmogorov-Smirnov, and non-parametric tests (e.g., Mann-Whitney, Wilcoxon) for non-normal data. Trial registration: IRCT20250105064282N1 was completed on 08/04/2025.

## Conclusion
This study examines how educational interventions can improve pregnant women’s knowledge, beliefs, and practices about thirdhand smoke (THS). Tailored education, using combined group and individual approaches, can boost awareness of THS risks and encourage protective behaviors. Social support and tobacco control policies in public spaces can further enhance these effects, especially in communities with low awareness of tobacco-related harms.

## Introduction
Tobacco use remains a major public health challenge and a barrier to achieving the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals [ ]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) in 2024, tobacco consumption causes over 8 million deaths annually, with approximately 1.3 million attributed to secondhand smoke exposure [ ]. In Iran, it is estimated that over 20% of the adult population is exposed to secondhand or thirdhand smoke, significantly increasing the burden of non-communicable diseases [ ]. Both active and passive exposure to tobacco smoke is associated with chronic conditions such as cardiovascular diseases, cancers, and respiratory disorders [ ]. A 2023 study in the Middle East revealed that only 28% of pregnant women were aware of THS risks, and fewer than 15% adopted preventive measures such as frequent surface cleaning or improved ventilation [ ]. Similarly, a 2020 U.S. study found that approximately 40% of pregnant women were exposed to environments potentially contaminated with THS, even in the absence of active smoking [ ]. In developing countries, 50–60% of pregnant women are indirectly exposed to THS due to living with smokers or in contaminated environments [ ]. The concept of thirdhand smoke (THS), defined as residual nicotine and harmful chemical compounds from tobacco smoke that persist on surfaces like furniture, walls, clothing, and dust, has emerged as a novel public health threat [ ]. First introduced in 2006 and formally defined by Winickoff et al. in 2009, THS can enter the body through dermal contact, inhalation, or ingestion and remains stable in seemingly smoke-free environments [ , ]. A 2023 study demonstrated that THS compounds can persist indoors for up to six months, potentially transforming into novel carcinogenic substances through chemical reactions with environmental agents [ , ]. THS poses a hidden threat to pregnant women and their fetuses, contributing to adverse outcomes such as preterm birth, low birth weight, and developmental defects [ , ]. Enhancing knowledge and promoting protective behaviors among pregnant women can mitigate these risks. Evidence-based educational interventions, strengthened tobacco control policies in indoor settings, and inter-organizational collaboration are critical for reducing THS exposure and improving public health [ , ]. Pregnancy is a critical period where exposure to environmental factors like tobacco smoke can have irreversible consequences for maternal and fetal health. Exposure to tobacco smoke (active or passive) is linked to adverse outcomes, including low birth weight (LBW), preterm birth, infant respiratory infections, and fetal mortality [ , ]. A 2022 study reported an 18% increased risk of preterm birth and a reduction in mean birth weight by up to 200 g associated with THS exposure during pregnancy [ ]. Furthermore, recent research indicates that THS may impair fetal neurodevelopment, increasing the risk of cognitive and behavioral disorders in children [ , ]. For instance, Gonzalez et al. (2024) found that chronic THS exposure during pregnancy was associated with a 10–15% reduction in cognitive scores in children at age five [ ]. The adverse effects of THS on pregnancy include:

Preterm birth: A 2022 study reported an 18% increased risk of preterm birth (before 37 weeks) linked to THS exposure [ ].
Low birth weight (LBW): The same study noted a reduction in mean birth weight by up to 200 g, increasing risks of respiratory issues and developmental delays [ ].
Neurodevelopmental and cognitive impacts: chronic THS exposure during pregnancy is associated with a 10–15% reduction in cognitive scores in children aged five, particularly in attention and memory [ ]. A 2021 study reported a 12% increased risk of behavioral disorders, such as hyperactivity, linked to THS [ ].
Other complications: THS exposure is associated with a 15–20% increased risk of respiratory infections in infants during their first year, particularly among those whose mothers were exposed to THS-contaminated environments [ ]. Despite growing evidence, public awareness and protective behaviors against THS, particularly among pregnant women, remain inadequate [ ]. A 2023 Middle Eastern study found that only 28% of pregnant women were aware of THS risks, with less than 15% implementing preventive measures like frequent surface cleaning or proper ventilation [ ]. This awareness gap is exacerbated by a lack of targeted educational interventions. Studies by Ferrante et al. (2022) and Torres et al. (2023) demonstrate that behavior change theory-based interventions (e.g., Health Belief Model) can improve knowledge and protective behaviors, though such studies are primarily conducted in developed countries and less explored in resource-limited settings [ , ]. A 2024 study further indicated that hybrid (in-person and virtual) educational approaches are more effective than traditional methods in changing pregnant women’s behaviors [ ].

## Methods

## Design
This randomized controlled trial protocol will be conducted among pregnant women attending healthcare centers in Isfahan, Iran. Following ethical approval and necessary permissions, participants will be randomly selected, informed about the study’s objectives and procedures, and asked to provide written informed consent.

## Sample size

 
The study will include 100 pregnant women, randomly sampled. From 39 active urban comprehensive health centers in Isfahan, four centers will be selected based on geographic zoning (uptown and downtown). Two centers will be randomly assigned to the intervention or control group via coin toss. Assuming a 5% type I error and 80% power, and based on Kazemi et al.’s study, a sample size of 50 participants per group is calculated (Fig.  ) [ ]. 
 

## Participants
Eligible participants who consent and meet inclusion criteria will be enrolled.

## Inclusion criteria
Informed consent, attendance at Isfahan healthcare centers, being in the first or second trimester, no high-risk pregnancy, literacy, and access to the internet and a smartphone.

## Exclusion criteria
Absence from more than 10% of educational sessions, severe physical or mental illness, hospitalization, fetal loss, or withdrawal from the study. Women whose spouses smoke in their presence will also be excluded.

## Data collection tools
Data will be collected using a four-part questionnaire:

Demographic information.
Beliefs about THS (using the BATHS questionnaire).
Knowledge about THS (researcher-designed).
Practices related to THS (researcher-designed).

## Phase 1: questionnaire design and psychometric evaluation

Demographic questionnaire: includes questions on age (woman and spouse), education (woman and spouse), occupation (woman and spouse), economic status, pregnancy rank, miscarriage history, fetal sex, smoking status (woman and spouse), and household smoking restrictions.
BATHS questionnaire: designed by Haardorfer et al. (2017), this standardized tool includes nine Likert-scale questions (strongly disagree to strongly agree) with high internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.90) and test-retest reliability (> 0.70). Its Turkish version was psychometrically validated in 2021 [ ], and its Persian version was validated by Khodayarian et al. [ ].
Knowledge and practices questionnaire: researcher-designed, validated for content using Content Validity Ratio (CVR) and Content Validity Index (CVI) by a panel of experts. Qualitative content validity (e.g., grammar, word choice) will be assessed by 10 experts. Qualitative face validity will be evaluated by 10 experts and 20 pregnant women for simplicity, clarity, and relevance. Quantitative face validity will use a 5-point Likert scale to assess item importance (Table  ).

 

## Phase 2: implementation of the intervention
Pregnant women attending selected healthcare centers will complete the knowledge, beliefs, and practices questionnaires to establish baseline measures. This phase enables comparative analysis of the intervention’s effectiveness in subsequent phases.
The educational intervention will be designed based on pre-test results, using credible scientific sources (e.g., CDC, WHO) and relevant literature [Table  ]. Delivery methods will include in-person (group and face-to-face) and virtual sessions (via online platforms and SMS). The intervention group will receive the program, and the control group will receive the educational package post-study for ethical considerations. Post-test assessments will be conducted one month post-intervention using the same questionnaires.
 

## Data analysis
Data will be analyzed using SPSS version 23. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation for continuous variables; frequency, percentage for categorical variables) will be reported. Normality will be assessed with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Parametric tests (e.g., Pearson correlation, chi-square) will be used for normal data, and non-parametric tests (e.g., Mann-Whitney, Wilcoxon) for non-normal data. Questionnaire reliability will be evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha and test-retest methods.

## Outcome measures
Beliefs about third-hand smoke: This variable will be assessed with the BATHS Questionnaire.
Knowledge and practice about third-hand smoke: This variable will be assessed with the researcher-made questionnaire about knowledge and practice about third-hand smoke.

## Ethical considerations
The present study was initiated after obtaining ethics approval under code IR.SSU.SPH.REC.1403.032 from the Research Ethics Committee of Yazd University of Medical Sciences and securing necessary permissions. The trial is registered under number IRCT20250105064282N1 was completed on 08/04/2025. Prior to data collection, the study’s objectives and procedures were clearly explained to participants, and informed consent was obtained from all research units. Participation was entirely voluntary, with participants retaining the right to withdraw at any stage without negative consequences. Confidentiality and protection of participants’ personal information were prioritized, and access to individual results was provided upon request. Additionally, all ethical principles regarding the use of credible printed and electronic sources were fully observed.

## Discussion
This study aims to design and implement an educational intervention to enhance pregnant women’s knowledge, beliefs, and practices, thereby improving maternal and infant health and reducing the burden of tobacco-related diseases.
Numerous studies have explored knowledge, beliefs, and exposure to THS across populations. A 2018 Spanish study found that only 27% of parents with Children under three were aware of THS, but 86% acknowledged its harm to children after receiving information [ ]. Education level significantly influenced THS awareness, with university-educated parents showing greater knowledge A 2021 Kuwaiti study reported that parents with stricter household smoking bans held stronger beliefs about THS harms [ ]. In China (2014), smoke-free homes were associated with reduced secondhand smoke exposure among non-smokers [ ].
The BATHS scale, developed by Haardorfer et al. (2017), measures beliefs about THS persistence and health impacts, validated in various studies, including Turkey (2021) for pregnant women [ , ]. A 2022 Turkish study found that education and smoking status influenced THS awareness and attitudes, with higher-educated individuals less exposed [ ].
Socioeconomic factors significantly affect secondhand and thirdhand smoke exposure A 2016 Saudi study found that women with lower education and unemployed women faced higher secondhand smoke exposure [ ] In Spain (2014), urinary cotinine levels in non-smoking pregnant women exposed to secondhand smoke were primarily linked to spousal smoking at home, with public spaces, workplaces, and restaurants also contributing [ ]. Karimian et al. (2019) noted that older husbands, lower education, unplanned pregnancies, and unemployed husbands increased smoke exposure, with lower knowledge and perception scores among exposed women [ ].
Tobacco smoke exposure during pregnancy requires robust control policies A 2012 Iranian study found that only 31.5% of households completely banned smoking [ ], with over half of pregnant women exposed to smoke. Women’s beliefs about smoke’s harm to fetal health influenced household smoking restrictions [ ]. Karimian et al. (2019) demonstrated that educational programs reduced secondhand smoke exposure and spousal smoking, lowering medical costs [ ]. A 2023 study by Karimian et al. showed increased male self-efficacy post-education, supporting the effectiveness of tailored programs [ ].
Educational interventions have proven effective in reducing secondhand smoke exposure. Kazemi et al. (2012) in Isfahan showed that face-to-face education increased perceived susceptibility and severity, reducing exposure, though insufficient for smoke-free environments [ ]. Stanton et al. (2004) reported a 7.2% smoking cessation rate among men with pregnant partners post-intervention [ ]. Ong et al. (2014) found positive outcomes in clinical trials, though some lacked biochemical validation [ ]. Chi et al. (2016) showed group education significantly improved health beliefs, self-efficacy, and self-reported behaviors compared to individual education [ ]. Aleman et al. (2016) found counseling interventions less effective without family involvement [ ].
This study addresses a critical gap by focusing on THS-specific educational interventions for pregnant women, a relatively underexplored area, particularly in developing countries like Iran [ , ]. By integrating in-person and virtual education with behavior change theories (e.g., Health Belief Model, Theory of Planned Behavior), it aims to enhance accessibility and effectiveness. The findings could inform standardized tools for assessing THS-related beliefs and behaviors, support national and international policy development, and promote smoke-free environments, ultimately improving maternal and infant health.

## Study limitations
The study relies on self-reported data, which may introduce bias. Participant adherence to educational programs and external factors (e.g., family support, tobacco control policies) may influence outcomes. The absence of biochemical validation (e.g., cotinine levels) may reduce exposure assessment accuracy. Additionally, conducting the study in Isfahan may limit generalizability. Broader, multi-regional studies with mixed-methods approaches are recommended.

## Conclusion
The primary aim of this study is to investigate the consequences of THS exposure and identify effective interventions to reduce pregnant women’s exposure to this hazardous factor. THS, the residual toxic compounds from tobacco smoke lingering on surfaces, poses a serious threat to maternal and fetal health.

## Role of education and awareness
Education, particularly when evidence-based and supported by validated tools, significantly enhances knowledge, corrects attitudes, and improves preventive behaviors among pregnant women, reducing indirect exposure to tobacco-related pollutants.

## Effective educational interventions
Evidence suggests that group and individual educational interventions, especially involving spouses, are more effective in reducing smoke exposure and promoting healthy behaviors. Tailored educational methods aligned with cultural and social characteristics enhance intervention efficacy.

## Promoting protective behaviors
Education on THS’s environmental persistence and health impacts can significantly increase protective behaviors, such as avoiding contaminated environments. While public tobacco control policies are essential, family-level educational interventions have a deeper impact due to their proximity to individuals’ living environments.
Multi-level educational interventions, including individual and group counseling, leveraging modern communication technologies, and fostering inter-sectoral collaboration, play a critical role in reducing THS exposure. These efforts are particularly vital in communities with lower awareness of tobacco-related harms, significantly contributing to improved maternal and infant health.
