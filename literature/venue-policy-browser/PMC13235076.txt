## Background
Despite Singapore’s robust healthcare infrastructure, adults with intellectual disabilities (AWIDs) and caregivers face insufficient support. Singapore’s multicultural context, ageing population, and income inequality further complicate equitable care. These challenges underscore the need to reassess the healthcare system for this group. Therefore, this study aimed to: (1) catalogue barriers and facilitators affecting health system access and care-seeking for AWIDs, caregivers, and health professionals before intervention; and (2) provide recommendations to address the barriers and enhance facilitators.

## Methods
A qualitative study using focus groups with 19 sectoral professionals and life-event guided interviews with AWID–caregiver dyads or triads (16 AWIDs; 20 caregivers) was conducted to explore viewpoints on care-seeking and health system access. Applied thematic analysis was used in identifying individual or system-level barriers and facilitators through a social constructivist lens.

## Results
Barriers included individual-level challenges such as having problems when faced with sudden or accumulated changes and transitions and the struggle with insufficient financial resources, particularly affected low-income families and was compounded by insufficient subsidies. System-level challenges included the lack of specialised services for AWIDs, which contributed to mismatched care, while fragmented care and limited care provider capability complicated service delivery. Additionally, fear and distrust of the health system, exacerbated access to care-seeking. All barriers are expressed by both groups.
Conversely, a facilitator that enhanced individual-level support, such as the usage of informal networks, helped connect AWID and caregivers to appropriate care. System-level support was stressed by both groups including intersectoral collaboration to address complex issues, while the availability of paid support services improved health outcomes and access. Furthermore, unpaid community support organisations, provided essential social care through organised activities. The facilitators highlighted the potential for improving the healthcare landscape through collective efforts and community engagement.

## Conclusions
This study highlights the need for individual-level support, including involving more family members in caregiving, and system-level reforms such as integrated, specialised care, and enhanced advocacy. Across all levels, co-created care plans and regular check-ins are important to identify emerging issues and build trust. Implementing these strategies can help Singapore progress towards a more inclusive and responsive healthcare system for AWIDs.

## Background
Singapore is a compact city state, which boasts a globally renowned healthcare system recognised for achieving world-class health outcomes at a low cost [ ]. However, since its rapid development post-independence in 1965 [ ], it has also grappled with challenges in social well-being and equity, stemming from persistent income inequality [ , ], a rapidly ageing population straining support systems [ ], and rising costs [ ]. Singapore’s unique multicultural society, shaped by Confucianism and colonial legacies [ , ], further adds to the complexity of ensuring inclusion and cohesion. This intricate socio-political landscape is crucial for understanding the challenges faced by vulnerable groups like adults with intellectual disabilities (AWIDs).
Intellectual disability (ID) in Singapore is characterised by limitations in intellectual functioning and adaptive behaviour manifesting before age 18 [ ]. While the estimated prevalence in Asia ranges from 0.06%–1.3% [ ], specific local prevalence studies are limited [ ]. Available data indicates that approximately 1.5% of the resident population has sensory impairments, ID, and autism spectrum disorder [ ]. Although precise percentages of AWIDs living in different settings are not publicly available, Singapore’s policy prioritises “ageing in place” [ ] to delay premature institutionalisation. Consequently, most AWIDs reside at home with their families rather than in residential facilities. Although a relatively small demographic of the entire population, community-dwelling AWIDs pose a significant healthcare concern due to heightened health risks and the need for lifelong support to navigate barriers like communication and financial constraints [ , ]. The long-term and costly nature of caring for AWIDs often results in substantial caregiver burden due to the extensive duration of care [ , ].
Singapore addresses challenges faced by people with disabilities (PWDs) through the Enabling Masterplan (EMP) [ ], an evolving roadmap guided by the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities [ ]. The current EMP 2030 [ ] outlines a long-term vision for lifelong learning, independent living, and inclusive environments (e.g., accessible public transport). This approach, largely reflecting the “Many Helping Hands” philosophy, expects all societal sectors to contribute [ ]. While some of these initiatives cater to those with lesser support needs – e.g., people with minor physical disabilities who can live independently with support, AWIDs with complex needs are supported by a mix of community and residential services. These include Day Activity Centres, specialised outpatient care like IDHealth and the Movement for Intellectual Disability Singapore (MINDS) Clinic, and high-support Adult Disability Homes (e.g., Metta Home for the Disabled) for those unable to reside with family [ ].
Relatedly, Singapore’s disability policies, described as pragmatic [ ], primarily adhere to an individual or medical model, focusing on the “individual” rather than systemic environmental barriers. This leads to limited choice and control for disabled individuals, as professional expertise dictates access and criteria vary across programmes. For example, the state determines which treatments are “basic” and eligible for subsidies, effectively funnelling those who require financial assistance into specific ward classes or standardised care packages. While this ensures system-level sustainability, it places decision-making power in the hands of experts, limiting an individual’s autonomy to choose providers or treatments outside of these strictly defined, means-tested tracks [ ]. The overall policy framework also emphasises re-commodification [ ] - the shift toward market-based provision and individual payment for health services. By prioritising individual responsibility and labour market reliance over extensive state welfare, the system focuses on functional maintenance but may fail to fundamentally address broader societal barriers to inclusion.
While some research exists on specific health issues like oral health, comprehensive studies on the overall healthcare access and care-seeking experiences of community-dwelling AWIDs and their caregivers were still lacking. Prior research has highlighted the lack of comprehensive data on the needs of AWIDs in Singapore [ ]. The current impact of the healthcare policies and initiatives (mentioned above) remains unexplored. Therefore, the research gap lies in the lack of empirical evidence regarding how Singapore’s pragmatic stance, and the aforementioned policy mechanism - recommodification - impact the experiences of this population.
Understanding this research gap requires considering broader perspectives on how health systems function. Healthcare access, in this context, is defined as the availability, accessibility, and affordability of medical services [ ]. It is not merely a matter of service presence, but a result of the interaction between individual characteristics (e.g., health literacy), structural factors (e.g., geographic distribution), and systemic determinants (e.g., policy and financing). Though in a city-state such as Singapore, geographical location is rarely the primary barrier to care. Care-seeking is understood as the process of identifying potential health disorders and seeking professional care [ ]. For caregivers of AWIDs, this behaviour is often symptomatic, triggered by observed changes in the AWID’s baseline health or behaviour. Both access and care-seeking are further influenced by the broader environment; Fleuret (2018) [ ] suggests that health systems could be viewed as socially constructed, influenced by cultural values, social norms, interactions, and various actors and institutions. Utilising concepts like biopower and biopolitics, this perspective challenges the biomedical model’s objectivity, highlighting the significant impact of cultural perceptions and social determinants on health behaviours and access to care.
Acknowledging these persistent challenges, the identified research gap, and the influence of social constructs on health systems, our study sought to explore health service availability and provision in Singapore. We aimed to: (1) identify and catalogue barriers and facilitators affecting health system access and care-seeking for AWIDs, their caregivers, and health professional experts prior to their enrolment in a specialised service; and (2) provide recommendations to address these barriers and enhance facilitators.

## Methods

## Study design
This comparative qualitative study integrated Life-event Guided Interviews (LGIs), an approach innovated by the study team, with AWID-caregiver dyads or triads, and Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) with sector professionals. While this research utilises a subset of data from a broader evaluation of IDHealth, a specialised community-based service providing integrated health care for AWIDs in Singapore [ ], it is distinct in its specific analytical focus on healthcare access barriers and facilitators. To ensure methodological transparency, the study adhered to the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) guidelines [ ] (Supplementary Material  ).

## Recruitment, sampling strategy and data collection procedures
Using purposive sampling, we recruited 55 participants – 16 AWIDs, 20 caregivers and 19 sector professionals.

## Life-event guided interviews
Thirty-six participants were recruited and interviewed in dyad pairs from December 2023 to March 2024. The target sample size was determined by the principle of thematic saturation; recruitment only concluded once no new barriers or facilitators were identified.The study team ensured representation across gender, age groups, ID severity, and Community Health Assistance Scheme (CHAS) card status—a national health subsidy programme in Singapore that provides tiered coverage for private care services [ ]. The programme utilises three colour-coded tiers (Blue, Orange, and Green-corresponding to highest, middle and lowest subsidy amount) to indicate different subsidy levels based on annual household income or value of home, serving as a reliable proxy for household financial status [ ]. The sampling grid is shown in Table  . 
The participants were identified by IDHealth and recruited by the study team via phone call. The primary eligibility criterion was active enrolment in the IDHealth service for the AWID participant. Caregivers were eligible if they were the support person for an enrolled AWID. As study collaborators, IDHealth’s clinical involvement was instrumental in facilitating access to this hard-to-reach population. Following the expression of interest, the research team coordinated the scheduling of the interviews and collected consent and assent before the start of the interviews. In accordance with ethical guidelines, informed consent and socio-demographic information were obtained via the secure online platform Qualtrics (Provo, UT, USA) [ ]. Caregiver participants provided their own informed consent after reviewing the participant information sheet. For AWIDs, formal consent was granted by their legal representatives, while the AWIDs themselves provided individual assent via a dedicated digital checkbox.
Occasionally, more than one caregiver participated. These caregivers were only invited to participate in the interview if they expressed personal interest and provided informed consent. Inclusion was further predicated on the comfort and mutual agreement of both the AWID and the primary caregiver. The LGIs lasted an average of one to two hours, and they were primarily conducted in participants’ homes to ensure a familiar and comfortable environment; however, one dyad opted for an external location. Throughout each session, participant well-being was prioritised through explicit verbal reminders of the right to pause or skip questions, as well as continuous researcher monitoring of non-verbal cues and body language for signs of fatigue or distress. While other family members were sometimes present, interviews proceeded only if participants were comfortable; otherwise, they were paused for privacy. Only one pair declined due to scheduling issues, and replacements were recruited. No repeat LGIs were carried out.
The life-grid interview is a retrospective qualitative technique employing a “temporal reference system” to minimise recall bias by anchoring health events to a chronological grid of personal milestones [ – ]. While life-grids typically gather both quantitative and qualitative data across a lifespan, this study employed qualitative “life-event” approach, prioritising narrative context (the “what” and “why”) over specific dates (the “when”). The photovoice method [ , ] utilises photography as a visual prompt to help participants document lived realities and trigger memories that may be difficult to express verbally. By integrating these methods, our team innovated the LGI approach to visually map access and care-seeking trajectories.
While the broader IDHealth evaluation [ ] mapped experiences from one year before to one year after registration—the details of which are outlined in the extraction grid in Supplementary Material  —the present study focuses exclusively on the pre-enrolment period. Beyond the targeted one-year window, significant earlier events and recent experiences were recorded to provide a richer contextual background for the health-seeking behaviours identified in this study. This window was chosen to characterise the barriers and facilitators encountered when navigating the general healthcare system without specialised support. Although the post-enrolment portion of the timeline was used during the interview to help participants anchor events around a salient reference point (IDHealth enrolment), these data were not used to generate the themes reported here. This approach integrated clinical case notes with participant recall to establish a baseline of experiences prior to the intervention.
Participants selected photos beforehand to place on the timeline, aiding memory and prompting discussion. They were also encouraged to note or draw events and position photos within this timeframe. A mock-up of the visual mapping of the participant experience is available in Supplementary Material  . The semi-structured LGI topic guide was piloted with three AWID-caregiver dyad pairs (conducted by ZJH, FC, and MA). While the full topic guide included a post-enrolment component for the broader evaluation, the excerpt provided in Supplementary Material  focuses exclusively on the pre-enrolment questions relevant to this study’s objectives. The process resulted in the addition of one prompt regarding future care planning. The findings of the pilot were included in the final results.
Beyond confirming that the LGI assists in retrospectively organising and reflecting on well-being, the pilot and subsequent interviews demonstrated several methodological advantages for this population. First, the timeline and visual prompts helped participants locate healthcare encounters in relation to concrete milestones, reducing reliance on abstract narration and facilitating the discussion of sensitive topics. Second, these chronological prompts enabled AWIDs to participate directly where possible, rather than relying solely on caregiver proxy accounts, thereby enhancing rapport between participants and interviewers. Finally, the format facilitated the cross-checking of discussed events against clinical case notes, strengthening the accuracy of the reconstructed trajectories.
Each LGI was conducted by a lead interviewer accompanied by one or more observers trained in engaging with at-risk populations. To ensure analytical rigour, observers maintained reflexive field notes throughout the sessions. The research team was multi-ethnic (comprising European, Chinese, Malay, and Indian researchers), which facilitated linguistic and cultural alignment; interviews were conducted in English, Mandarin, or Malay as required by the participants’ preferences. All sessions were audio-recorded using digital recorders (Olympus DM-720; OM Digital Solutions Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) or secure Zoom Workplace accounts (Version 6.7.0, Zoom Video Communications, Inc., San Jose, CA, USA) provided by the National University of Singapore.

## Focus group discussions
Nineteen FGD participants were identified through IDHealth’s professional network, which included established contacts with key stakeholders in the health and disability sectors. Recruitment was managed by researcher FC, who contacted potential participants independently via email. Notably, all invited professionals consented to participate. To ensure a balanced representation of professionals working in the the biomedical, psychological and social domains of health, we targeted five to seven participants per group; this size was chosen to facilitate robust interaction while remaining small enough to be manageable. On 24 January 2024, three FGDs were held in a formal meeting room with professionals who had expertise and one to 30 years of experience working with AWID. Each FGD lasted approximately 50 min. The FGDs were conducted during a pre-arranged gathering of participants for a related research initiative (mentioned in the study design subsection). Given the high-level professional commitments and limited availability of these experts, data collection was designed as a series of comprehensive, one-time sessions, making repeat meetings logistically unfeasible. As with the LGI, the FGD topic guide (see Supplementary Material  ) only focused exclusively on the broader AWID healthcare landscape; questions pertaining to the IDHealth service evaluation were omitted to maintain a clear distinction between both pieces of work. Hence, the FGD topic guides were not piloted. Prior to the commencement of each FGD, all participants provided written informed consent and their socio-demographic information after a briefing on the study and their rights (i.e., their involvement was strictly voluntary and that providing candid professional opinions would have no impact on their working relationships with IDHealth or their own organisations.) Details of the sampling grid are provided in Table  . 
FGDs were audio-recorded using digital recorders and secure Zoom recording feature. Field notes were written by the facilitators of the FGDs (ZJH, FC, MA and LP) after the session.

## Research team and reflexivity
The research team, comprising graduate and postgraduate researchers of various genders from the same university and led by a post-doctoral researcher (ZJH) experienced in qualitative research and the life-grid approach, was driven by a commitment to addressing health inequalities using participatory methods. Two team members (FC and MA) were the main LGI contacts, while FC was the main FGD contact.
While the research team brought extensive experience in researching or working with the vulnerable populations—including older adults (FC, AYC), patients with traumatic brain injuries (ZJH), and youths (SA, LP, MA)—none of the core interviewers or analysts were employees of IDHealth. Two study collaborators (JT and CSL) were members of the IDHealth clinical team and facilitated population access; however, they were not involved in the data collection and analysis to maintain analytical independence. Furthermore, the lead interviewers engaged in preliminary consultations and clinical meeting observations with IDHealth to ensure specialised preparedness for working with AWIDs. Grounded in social constructivism - the principles of which emphasise meaning-making through interaction, historical and social understanding, and community-derived meaning [ ] – research team members (ZJH, FC, MA, LP, AYC, SA) occasionally shared experiences with participants to build rapport. This approach, employing perceptions [ ] and emphasising shared language through hermeneutics and logic [ ], illuminates contextual nuances in participant expression for this population.
Initial rapport was established through purposive communication during the recruitment of both LGI and FGD participants, thereby establishing procedural transparency prior to data collection. No prior professional or personal relationships existed between the study team and the participants. Although true anonymity was not possible as the researchers and participants were known to one another during the sessions, strict confidentiality was maintained throughout. Furthermore, for the FGDs, researchers established clear ground rules at the outset, emphasising that information shared within the group must remain confidential and should not be attributed to specific individuals outside the session.

## Data processing, analysis and presentation
To manage the large qualitative dataset efficiently, a dual-processing approach was adopted. All sessions were audio-recorded in their entirety using digital recorders and cloud secure server – Dropbox (San Francisco, California) [ ]. All LGI data were processed using expanded notes [ ], which involved a systematic, iterative two-step process: researchers developed detailed summaries, categorised by biopsychosocial health domains, and integrated target verbatim quotes from the recordings to capture critical participant narratives. This method was chosen to maintain contextual depth - such as non-verbal cues and environmental factors - that traditional transcripts may omit. In contrast, FGD data were manually transcribed verbatim by the research team (FC, MA, LP); no artificial intelligence (AI) software was utilised to ensure the accurate preservation of local dialects and technical context. The de-identified expanded notes are stored in a password-protected, institutional secure sever.
Both datasets were cleaned and de-identified before being imported into ATLAS.ti 24 (Berlin, Germany) [ ]. To minimise interpretation bias, interviewers wrote their expanded notes immediately post-session, using audio recordings for cross-verification. While participant validation (member checking) was not feasible, the team utilised reflexive field notes and collaborative cross-checking to refine the analysis.
The study employed applied thematic analysis [ ] - grounded in social constructivism - to develop relevant themes, integrating qualitative findings with contextual metadata to crystallise the final results. The analysis began with an inductive open-coding process; the first author initially coded the LGI data, while two analysts independently coded the FGD data to identify recurring patterns and concepts. The team maintained a dynamic coding framework by using an iterative versioning process within the ATLAS.ti to track and compare thematic developments across multiple stages of analysis.
Initially, themes were developed inductively and iteratively through constant comparison [ ] and regular team debriefings. These emergent themes were subsequently mapped onto a conceptual framework of barriers and facilitators, a process that occurred in tandem with the refinement of the study aims. The coding framework was therefore emergent rather than predefined a priori. To enhance analytical depth and validity, data from the LGIs and FGDs were triangulated to cross-verify AWIDs, caregivers, and health professionals’ perspectives. Themes were then further segmented into distinct levels of analysis (e.g., individual and systemic) to provide structured insights into the care-seeking journey. The resulting coding framework converged on the final themes and subthemes reported in the Results section and tables. All LGI and FGD findings are reported using pseudonyms to ensure participant confidentiality. Thematic saturation was reached prior to the end of coding, as no new conceptual categories or patterns emerged from the final transcripts.

## Results

## Sample description
We conducted three FGDs with 19 participants and 16 LGIs with 36 participants. Tables  and detail participant characteristics. The LGIs involved 16 AWIDs and 20 caregivers. Among the employed AWID participants, one had regular employment, and another worked in a sheltered workshop. Participants with mild ID participated verbally, whereas those with moderate to severe ID participated through drawing, writing, gesturing, or simply by being present. ## Reporting of findings
The results are structured around barriers and facilitators to healthcare access, with illustrative quotes presented in their original colloquial form - Singlish [ ]. Singlish is an English-based creole and liaison language characterised by unique syntactic patterns influenced by Malay, Chinese, and Tamil. These features include the frequent omission of subject and object pronouns, the absence of the copula “be” before adjectives, and the use of sentence-ending particles such as ah or lah. [ ] To ensure these findings remain accessible to an international audience, we removed minor filler words, sentence ending particles and added clarifying terms in brackets where the colloquial syntax might obscure the primary meaning. Additionally, omissions using […] were only applied when quotes became repetitive or deviated from the core theme. A non-Singaporean researcher from the team (non-co-author) also reviewed the quotes to ensure readability, and intent. Figures  and illustrate the thematic overlaps identified across participant groups. The findings are categorized into themes and sub-themes. For clarity, themes are presented in bold, while sub-themes are indicated in italics throughout the subsequent results section.

 

 

## Barriers

## Individual-level barriers
The most frequently cited barrier for most AWIDs and caregivers was having problems when faced with sudden or accumulated changes and transitions. While services might have mitigated some of these challenges, the primary complexities often resided within the internal dynamics and unique needs of the AWID, or family unit. Some caregivers shared their struggles as a family member stepping up to provide care, which may have led to non-adherence to the care plan due to the difference in management or treatment strategies between previous care providers:“When the maid left, I […] shift him from that room to the other room. […] Then he’s not happy… the changes ah… he’s not happy you see. His mood also.” – Rina, caregiver of moderate ID patient

For others, there was a web of interconnected problems following the accumulation of problems throughout the lives of AWIDs:“So at that time [when he was young], we didn’t know how to look for [specialist] doctors, […] After that, [he was] quite big already, then for some time [his health was not great].”– Alicia, caregiver of severe ID patient

Many professionals added that AWIDs and caregivers face challenges at every stage of the life-course as they struggle to find appropriate services for each stage of the life-course:“But the problem is Singapore […], we never look at their transition. […] After the SPED (Special education) school, […] they need to go to DACs (Day Activity Centres) and I realised my areas don’t have this too. So, the transition is merely impacting and stressing a lot of caregivers as well.” – Fiona, social profession

They additionally noted that the healthcare system does not provide several caregivers the necessary knowledge or navigational support to effectively engage with care providers:“From the point of view from the caregivers, […] when we refer them to psychology, [or] so-called healthcare sector. A lot of the time, we observe that […] they are very lost on how to interact with them, how to rightly present the issue. […] So, I feel that the caregivers, also the time when it deals with the healthcare, they are pretty helpless and clueless as to what to expect, what is it all about.”– Choon Gim, psychological profession

Insufficient financial resources emerged as another major obstacle, particularly for low-income families that face challenges accessing care - with many caregivers expressing difficulties in managing expenses due to AWIDs requiring more supplies, making it hard to make ends meet:“My late husband only brought back $2000 in income. We lived in difficult circumstances. It’s not that it was difficult per se, but it’s because Amira needed a lot of supplies, such as medicine, milk.”– Izati, caregiver of severe ID patient

Professionals further observed socioeconomic disparities, noting that while affluent families can better navigate the complex “biopsychosocial” requirements of care—such as funding and advocacy—those with fewer resources face a more fragmented and difficult service landscape:“Let’s say, the very poor people with ID and the very rich ones, and you can really see a very stark difference [in the care provided by this sector. It] is not just healthcare procedures, [it’s] social, […]funding, advocacy, and so many different elements coming together. It’s biopsychosocial.” — Daniel, biomedical profession

Both groups agreed on the lack of subsidies or funding for AWIDs and caregivers, though their perspectives on the barrier differ. Experts stressed that service costs should not be shifted onto AWIDs and caregivers:“When I’m doing home visit, it’s at least half a day we have to pack so many things, these are surgical items, and then we have to see the patient and we have to come back. And it doesn’t make sense for most private practitioners to do it unless you dump all the costs into that single patient, which totally doesn’t make sense.” – Daniel, biomedical profession

In contrast, AWIDs and caregivers highlighted their social challenges, such as AWIDs being permanent residents and ineligible for subsidies reserved for citizens:“CHAS card we are not qualified, because I found out that CHAS card is only for Singaporeans and she’s a PR (Permanent Resident) [in Singapore]. Ya so one thing, it’s difficult for us is because she is not a citizen, […] so it’s hard for me to like get her all the subsidies.” – Nora, caregiver of moderate ID patient

## System-level barriers
Due to the complex needs of the AWID population, the lack of specialised services in the Singapore health system is widely seen as a key barrier to care. Most AWIDs and caregivers recognised that individual needs vary:“Last five years ago I wanted to send her to the daycare, but the only problem is, okay. She has mainly been on the floor. So, daycare they do not transfer her to the floor or to the bed or whatsoever. […] So after asking around I realise that majority of the daycare have the same scenario.” – Nasir, caregiver of severe ID patient

However, most services employ a standardised approach that does not cater to specific needs. This often results in a care mismatch, leading to reluctance to seek help:“Social worker tried to help me but I didn’t stick with them, because I think I wasn’t comfortable, or [able to] express or talk to social worker. I didn’t want to face my feelings inside. […] I just stayed at home, went through a lot, kept everything inside.” – Vijay, patient with mild ID

Indeed, professionals concurred that complexity needs to be accounted for in the health system, particularly for AWIDs with severe impairments who require significant support:“I think that the services can maybe serve the mild, or those that are able to live independently. But there is still a group which are also higher needs complex cases, they really need greater support, especially the caregivers.” – Charlotte, social profession

Professionals further supplemented that specialised support services should be designed to engage the AWID’s wider support network - including family and community members - to empower them in providing more effective and sustainable care.:“The patient with ID doesn’t live alone, and often times, whether they live in the systems, […the] whole setting, you know, so broader network, and I think that’s very important. […] In the intervention, you need to involve all the people around them and the environment as well.” – Gabriel, biomedical profession

Among AWIDs and caregivers, being restricted to the home environment during the pandemic was a pressing problem–especially since services they could access were limited. This led to accumulating and unresolved issues:“This one, CORONA time, […] it made him very unwell. […] I call him to come to the doctor, but he don’t want to come. Everywhere he don’t want come. That time, he got depression, he got depression, [and] everyday he’s making noise.” – Shanti, caregiver of mild ID patient

In the absence of specialised services, participants had to navigate general health systems that may be ill-equipped for their needs. Participants further described fragmented care and limited care provider capability, where both groups expressed concerns about gaps such as the care professional’s lack of knowledge and misdiagnosis leading to inappropriate care:“Previously, he never truly received appropriate care. [If he has an illness], we would just go down to the clinic downstairs… […] Yeah, and they didn’t know how to care for him appropriately, they are all busy.” – Po Kam, caregivers of severe ID patient

Some caregivers noted instances where doctors or health service professionals were impatient or dismissive:“[A] few months ago, he had shingles. We went to see a GP, [and] the GP say, ‘Oh it’s okay, you got nothing’.” – Karoline, caregiver of moderate ID patient

Particularly, several professionals found that there appeared to be a lack of care professional training, leading to the inability of facilities to provide support for patients:“Because I think you can have […] good practitioners, but unfortunately, they may not understand that person with ID, right. […] I think what needs to happen is the awareness of ID, literally awareness of intellectual disability and not forgetting the various comorbidities that it comes about, right.” – Faarah, psychological profession

Distinctly, a few professionals pointed out challenges in communication with patients, mentioning communication barriers and patients’ difficulties in accurately expressing medical concerns:“So, [the patients] will only go to the GP if there’s a symptom, and most of our clients cannot even say [what they are experiencing]. If they’re not feeling well; they will just cry, but you don’t know why. […] Even if they’re having a fever; you wouldn’t know, unless you touch them.” – Martha, biomedical profession

AWIDs and caregivers also noted incomplete medical records during transitions between care levels, particularly when transitioning between different levels of care, such as moving from hospital to a private primary care:“Whenever [the patient] falls sick, we just visit a GP. But because GP doesn’t have that of a close follow up, does not have a history of his medical conditions and stuff like that, it’s actually very difficult. They’re only assessing him as and when he said stuff like that. And whenever we go to a hospital, everything just starts all over again.” – Elena, caregiver of severe ID patient

Experts also observed that organisations serving this population often work in silos, with inadequate linkages between them:“I was thinking the way that because of you know, [organisations are] being disconnected, […] so to get things going for the caregivers and the ID, […] anyone […] who’s holding a case has to do a lot more. And that’s very tedious for the sector. So, I think the caregivers themselves also find that they are out there trying to… you know, let everybody know their needs and things like that. […] So hopefully things can get a bit better if we are connected.” – La Him, psychological profession

Beyond fragmented care and limited care provide capability, these systemic problems cultivated fear and distrust of the health system. Many caregivers added that unfamiliar care professionals and care environment could cause patients to reject care:“Last time… we went to… the [hospital]. [The patient] went for a while there and she didn’t want to continue going there anymore. It’s been 10 years since we visited there. She’s very afraid when she goes there.” – Shan, caregiver of mild ID patient

Many professionals agreed, observing that such fears might lead to patients resisting assessment or intervention, making it difficult to diagnose and treat their health issues:“Even if [the GPs] did do a home visit, [the patients] are not cooperative; it’s very hard to determine the problem as well.” – Gabriel, biomedical profession

Additionally, caregiver participants also detected societal biases, and public perception during care-seeking may dissuade them from seeking care:“[The public] would give like, you know those judgmental stares and looks, and like, I got one time some people said not very good remarks on us [in the hospital], so I don’t really feel comfortable.” – Naaz, caregiver of moderate ID patient

Professionals have attributed barriers raised previously stem from issues such as underfunded healthcare infrastructure (e.g., specialised facilities, services, and trained professionals). Resources were also unevenly distributed, often prioritising individuals with milder ID (who usually require less extensive support) over those with more severe ID (who often have greater support needs):“I think, unfortunately, a lot of the efforts in Singapore to raise the quality of life of persons with disabilities, including in the health domain are very, very focused on those with higher functioning disabilities, whether that be those with the higher function end of autism spectrum, or those with milder forms of sensory or physical disabilities.” – Bryan, social profession

This can be attributed to a lack of advocacy for the ID population, as there is limited awareness of the challenges faced by this group:“Actually what, again, what is lacking is the advocacy work. Because of Covid there was such a big piece on mental health, but isn’t any advocacy work done to the invisible disability groups, which is the ID and the autism.” – Fiona, social profession

## Facilitators

## Individual-level facilitators
Caregivers reported that the usage of informal networks, such as word of mouth from acquaintances, helped link AWIDs and caregivers to appropriate care channels:“That time, I’ve tried to look for people like [doctor]. I have a friend who happened to have her contact details that time, so I managed to contact them.” – Ong Chip, caregiver of moderate ID patient

Although not all caregivers had access to a personal mobility device, those who did reported ease of transport, which facilitated social activities for AWIDs and their extended families:“Last time, I bought a car because […] I realise that we never have a family outing, it’s always been me and my mother, then my father [and the AWID patient] will be at home. […] So, we started going out. Wah, she (referring to the AWID patient) [was] happy.” – Nasir, caregiver of moderate ID patient

Experts emphasised the importance of the involvement of caregivers and other family members, advocating for a family-centred approach in implementing interventions:“I wish somehow there is a way, almost like a one-stop shop where everybody knows about the family and everyone knows who’s working with who, because to me this is the issue. Sometimes, we don’t even know who’s involved with the family.” – Faarah, psychological profession

## System-level facilitators
Most participants appeared to agree that the availability of paid support services has helped improve health outcomes and system access. Several caregivers and AWIDs observed improvements from specialised services after receiving care supported by formal care organisations:“Actually, when he first got there [to the organisation], he’s still in the wheelchair. Once he was in a normal wheelchair then he… will walk with the help of the doctor la. Then slowly, slowly, he doesn’t need to use already.” – Rina, caregiver of moderate ID patient

Paid help (e.g., foreign domestic workers) also assisted in easing the care burden:“Certain days, [the other caregiver] will go out […] to do grocery shopping, going around alone, knowing that my helper can do a very good job.” – Jermaine, caregiver of moderate ID patient

When patients were able to visit care professionals, experts noted that it contributed to improvements in the management of patients, such as receiving medical advice regarding their medications:“I think for those clients where we’ve actually managed to link up […] we had very fruitful discussions with the neurologist […] in terms of managing their medication in their day-to-day business […and] any issues where the caregiver was having concerns about medical advice so we could support from our end.” – Kimberly, psychological profession

Unpaid community support organisations also served as a major facilitator. Caregiver and AWID participants with strong community networks reported that ground-up community support have been crucial in providing social support and care. Volunteers have actively involved AWIDs and their caregivers in organised activities:“So, we have a group of volunteers who came over, […they] bring the families with [people] like Nora to the zoo trip. So, at that point of time I was working, […] so the volunteers befrienders were there to bring them around.” – Naaz, caregiver of moderate ID patient

Religious organisations have similarly been active in this space:“No, he came with me to the temple, he will do whatever work they give, and then he will be involved in volunteering. […] In the temple ah, everybody likes him.” – Shanti, caregiver of mild ID patient

A few experts also highlighted that intersectoral collaboration efforts, where professionals from various fields are “sitting together as a single line”, are beneficial in resolving problems:“We [are] involving doctors, therapists and the social workers sitting together as a single line [to solve problems].” – Andy, psychological profession

## Discussion
Using a social constructivist lens, this research identified healthcare access facilitators and barriers for AWIDs and their caregivers. Facilitators, like caregiver and family involvement rooted in cultural practices, significantly improved healthcare engagement. Barriers, such as insufficient financial resources, were not purely economic but were perceived differently across social contexts. This study enhances understanding by showing how socially constructed meanings influence healthcare decisions for AWIDs in Singapore, highlighting the interplay of individual experiences and systemic factors within the country’s developmental context. To bridge the pragmatic nature of Singapore’s disability policies with socioenvironmental considerations, the following sections explore the multi-level implications of these findings within the Singaporean context.
The findings highlight the potential for strategically utilizing individual-level resources within the “Many Helping Hands” approach. The data suggest that involving more family members in caregiving and the building of informal networks to target barriers related to specialised service gaps and the strain of sudden life transitions.
Crucial for AWIDs’ healthcare, caregivers, often older women as found in a previous Singaporean study [ ] and reflected in the current study sample where many caregivers were of advanced age, experience substantial physical and emotional strain. A Brazilian study highlights challenges concerning older caregivers and those with comorbidities [ ]. In an ageing society like Singapore, the results imply that involving more family members in caregiving may distribute responsibilities and help prevent burnout, addressing the specific concerns raised by our participants regarding changes and transitions. Familiarity with multiple caregivers and the AWID’s care routine can ease disruptions, particularly when a caregiver is absent. A Korean study supports this, emphasising ongoing support from others to enhance caregivers’ quality of life, and pointing toward the benefits of increased family participation and communication [ ]. In Singapore, Confucian values, which underscore familial responsibility, facilitate leveraging family and community structures that significantly influence caregiver well-being [ ]. However, the findings suggest that this model of family capacity building is not a replacement for state responsibility; rather, it appears to require increased government funding to fund programmes to enable and support caregivers ensure the sustainability of this approach.
In Singapore, building and leveraging informal support networks emerged as critical facilitator, potentially enhancing caregiver resilience through both supporting caregivers when there are accumulating challenges or transitions, and assisting them in navigating the fragmented health system. This aligns with research showing how larger informal networks reduce caregiver burden [ ] and offers new insight into Singapore’s collectivist culture, where strong family and community ties exist alongside significant caregiving demands. Caregivers benefit from community and peer support groups [ ]. Developing interpersonal communication and self-efficacy within these networks is is highlighted as important for improving coping mechanisms, as indicated by studies on dementia caregivers [ ]. Such approaches resonate with Singapore’s policy focus on community-based care and support for ageing populations, with the government expanding initiatives to promote healthy ageing and social inclusion [ ].
Beyond relying on the individual and the community, systemic, socio-environmental factors the results underscore the importance of addressing broader societal barriers to inclusion. The findings indicate that comprehensive and collaborative approaches are needed to improve services for AWIDs. The observation that existing programmes, such as the EMP, often do not adequately address complex cases [ ], points toward the necessity of integrated, specialised services are crucial, as research [ ] indicates that patients without tailored programmes have longer hospital stays. These services are vital to address the unique challenges faced by this demographic, particularly those with profound impairments [ , ]. To enhance the visibility and navigation of these services, the data highlight the potential role of care coordinators [ ] in their lives - specialised professionals (primarily nurses or case managers) who act as a central resource point to bridge gaps in care transitions. By integrating into both hospital and community networks, they facilitate personalised care, coach families in navigating fragmented pathways, and link patients to holistic medical and psychosocial support. Services like IDHealth [ ] exist in Singapore to fill up the gap. Nevertheless, our findings confirm concerns about service fragmentation, the gaps in care delivery - specifically the insufficient specialized training among healthcare professionals - and a shortage of specialised programmes, underscoring the need for policy changes that prioritise tailored care pathways.
Relatedly, multidisciplinary partnerships are presented as vital for holistic, integrated services. A systematic review suggests that cross-sector partnerships and collaborations with culturally specific organisations can reduce marginalisation [ ]. The study suggests that networks leveraging collective expertise are crucial for cohesive support systems and tackling fragmented services. A community care study [ ] suggests intersectoral collaboration was often seen as means for better supporting community-dwelling frail AWIDs. Continuity of care through shared responsibility and knowledge exchange is also key [ ]. While intersectoral collaboration, an identified facilitator, is advocated, our findings suggests that Singapore needs clearer protocols for data sharing and communication across agencies, and joint training programmes to address service fragmentation. These findings echo previous research in identifying organisational barriers in Singapore [ ], highlighting the importance for systemic reforms.
Financial barriers significantly impede access to essential services and basic needs, including healthcare and daily living expenses [ ]. Our findings underscore this as a primary obstacle, revealing differences in care quality based on socioeconomic status and distress among caregivers who have insufficient financial resources. Furthermore, the data highlight how narrow eligibility criteria, such as residency status, excluded many from vital subsidies like the CHAS card. Although subsidies are vital for mitigating income disparities, the current specificity of eligibility criteria, suggests a potential benefit in broadening schemes - such as the taxi subsidy scheme [ ] - to include individuals requiring private transport for vital non-vocational activities. However, the study implies that addressing individual affordability through subsidies is only one part of the solution; it must be coupled with adequate funding and resource allocation to address the broader underfunded healthcare infrastructure [ ]. Our findings highlight a need for continued investment to support innovation and service responsiveness within Singapore’s disability sector. Specifically, the data suggest that state-led support must include tangible incentives to support the caregivers, such as subsidised caregiver training [ ], adding more respite care allocations [ ] to better support caregivers, and financial assistance for lower-income caregivers [ ] to ensure the sustainability of the “Many Helping Hands” approach.
Singapore’s rapid economic growth has brought prosperity but also social inequalities, including persistent stigma against AWIDs. A 2023 report indicated a decline in positive attitudes towards people with disabilities compared to 2019 [ ]. Therefore, the results suggest that public education campaigns are crucial for changing perceptions and reducing stigma, but they should also serve as a vital link to resources by promoting the availability of care coordinators and specialised services. By raising awareness of these professional supports, such campaigns can support caregivers by fostering resilience and self-efficacy, helping families move from isolation to supported engagement. In Singapore, community organisations and influential figures (e.g., religious leaders) [ , ] also play a vital role in advocacy. This study suggests the value of targeted, culturally-tailored public education to help with fear and distrust of health system, and to build awareness of current infrastructure.
Ultimately, the study suggests that Singapore requires a blend of individual-focused and socio-environmental solutions - multi-level solutions that combine societal support with governmental efforts. These findings emphasise the value of co-created care plans and regular check-ins to understand specific problems, to refer them to appropriate specialised services, and identify any transitional issues or accumulating problems. For instance, routine check-ins foster understanding of AWID’s needs and their current access to services, aiding care providers to provide targeted care. Concurrently, the findings highlight the governmental responsibility for performance monitoring and data collection are crucial to assess specialised interventions [ , ]. In Singapore, integrating administrative datasets across sectors could provide valuable insights for targeted interventions and resource allocation, further illustrating this blend of top-down and grassroots strategies. These findings may prove useful in other high-income Asian nations facing similar concerns. Ultimately, this study contributes new evidence on the benefits of data-driven approaches to support AWIDs in Singapore, underscoring collaborative governance and culturally sensitive interventions across all societal levels.
The practical utility of these findings is supported by the study’s methodological approach, which successfully included AWIDs in the research process to enhance data authenticity. The innovative use of LGIs for retrospective data collection was a key strength, minimising recall bias and improving the research’s quality and depth. This study also contributes to the limited body of literature on the healthcare landscape for AWIDs and their caregivers in Singapore.
While these methods provided a robust foundation, several limitations must be acknowledged. Despite attempts to mitigate recall bias, a common pain point of retrospective studies [ ], some information may still have been forgotten by participants. Engaging with participants with severe ID proved difficult, necessitating reliance on caregiver responses and environmental observations. Additionally, the researchers’ positionality must be considered; the team’s social constructivist orientation facilitated an analysis of barriers and facilitators, but a lack of extensive prior professional experience with the AWID population among some team members may have influenced the interpretative process. This theoretical lens may have shaped the focus of the analysis, potentially prioritising certain social themes over others. Notwithstanding these limitations, methodological innovations and data triangulation provide a robust basis for our conclusions. However, transferability to different sociocultural contexts requires consideration and further research, especially given how these contexts can influence the experiences of AWIDs.

## Conclusions
Overall, this study provides insights into the current healthcare access and care-seeking landscape for AWIDs and their caregivers in Singapore. Through a social constructivist lens, it identifies both barriers and facilitators, emphasising the importance of support from individuals and communities, alongside the need broader socio-environmental system-level reforms, such as integrated, personalised care and enhanced advocacy to address the specific needs of this demographic. Implementing these strategies can help Singapore progress towards a more inclusive and effective healthcare system for AWIDs.
