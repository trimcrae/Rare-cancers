## Background
For older adults with subjective cognitive decline (SCD), physical activity is now recognized as an effective means to reduce the risk of Alzheimer’s disease. However, this population often exhibits lower levels of physical activity. This study aimed to establish physical activity promoting indicators system for older adults with SCD, providing comprehensive targets for interventions and promoting relevant policies.

## Method
A modified Delphi technique was used to seek opinions from experts about what should be used and prioritised as indicators of promoting physical activity in older adults with SCD. Expert consultations were conducted from January to March 2024. Data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0 and Excel software. Descriptive statistics were used for expert demographics, while coefficients were calculated to assess expert authority (Cr), and coordination (Kendall’s W). Weights for indicators were determined through the order diagram.

## Results
Based on a literature review and the Wuli-Shili-Renli system framework, we initially identified 59 indicators, comprising primary (3 dimensions), secondary (11 items), and tertiary (45 items) indicators. Fifteen expert panelists were invited to participate in the study. Of these, 11 out of 18 completed round 1 (61.1% response rate), all 11 completed round 2 (100.0% response rate), and all 11 completed the third and final round (100.0% response rate). Ultimately, consensus was reached on 3 primary, 9 secondary, and 44 tertiary indicators. The order diagram determined weights for primary indicators as follows: foundational system (0.4242), operational system (0.2879), and personnel system (0.2879).

## Conclusion
The system of 56 physical activity-promoting indicators constructed for older adults with SCD through the Delphi method provides theoretical support for policy formulation and allocation of funds to comprehensively promote physical activity behaviors among this population.

## Introduction
Subjective cognitive decline (SCD) is considered the preclinical stage of Alzheimer’s disease (AD), characterized by mild neuronal damage and cognitive impairment that does not meet the criteria for mild cognitive impairment (MCI) [ , ]. This reversible phase presents an optimal opportunity for intervention [ ]. The prevalence of SCD among older adults is noteworthy. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in the United States [ ], the prevalence of SCD is 9.9% among individuals aged 64 to 74, increasing to 14.3% among those aged 75 and above. A study in China involving 2689 participants found that 18.8% of adults aged 60 to 80 reported SCD [ ]. Increasingly, scholars advocate for early diagnosis and prevention, aiming to shift intervention timing to the preclinical stage of AD, making SCD a crucial intervention target. Among these, physical activity stands out as a promising non-pharmacological intervention [ ].
However, older adults with SCD often exhibit lower levels of physical activity [ , ], making it especially important to develop a scientifically feasible indicator system to guide clinical practice. This indicator system can provide a framework for healthcare professionals, including doctors, therapists, and nurses, to assess and promote physical activity levels among older adults with SCD. The system is suitable for use in clinical settings, community centers, and care facilities, where healthcare personnel can implement it through physical activity assessments, personalized exercise prescriptions, and ongoing monitoring. Additionally, government agencies and community organizations can utilize this indicator system to support public health initiatives aimed at reducing cognitive decline through increased physical activity. Healthcare professionals conduct regular assessments using this system to ensure the effectiveness of interventions and adjust them according to individual needs, achieving better intervention outcomes.
The importance of this system of physical activity promoting indicators extends beyond guiding clinical practice. Firstly, because the SCD stage is considered the preclinical phase of AD, interventions during this stage are effective in altering or delaying disease progression [ ]. Promoting physical activity reduces the risk of cognitive decline, positively impacting individuals’ quality of life and health status. Furthermore, the establishment of a system of physical activity promoting indicators supports structured and systematic approaches to promoting physical activity among older adults with SCD. By focusing on creating an effective framework for intervention, this system can provide specific guidance on strategies to encourage physical activity engagement, thus contributing to reducing the risk of cognitive decline and improving quality of life for older adults. Although studies have highlighted the financial burden of AD [ ], the focus of this system is not on assessing cost-effectiveness but rather on offering a practical approach to enhancing physical activity levels and health outcomes in this population. Moreover, constructing a system of physical activity promoting indicators contributes to advancing public health. With the aging population exacerbating the incidence of cognitive diseases like AD, these conditions have become significant public health challenges [ ]. By promoting physical activity among older adults with SCD, overall older health can be indirectly enhanced [ ], reducing the incidence of dementia and promoting healthy aging [ ].
Therefore, the objective of this study is to establish a scientifically feasible system of physical activity promoting indicators for older adults with SCD, aiming to improve their quality of life, support effective intervention strategies, and advance public health initiatives.

## Methods

## Identification of the advisory experts
The number of participants in Delphi method expert consultations usually ranges from 15 to 50 individuals [ ]. However, the exact determination of the number of expert consultations should be based on the characteristics of the research field and actual circumstances. Therefore, considering the content of the promotion system and the development of the field, this study, after discussions within the project team, determined the participation of a total of 18 experts. The inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) engaged in relevant professional work for more than 10 years, with rich theoretical knowledge or practical experience; (2) holding a master’s degree or above, or a senior professional title or above; (3) having high academic achievements in elderly cognitive care, physical activity, or health promotion; (4) interested in this study and available to participate in expert consultations. The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) failure to complete the questionnaire according to the content; (2) failure to complete and return the survey form within the specified time.

## Indicator system construction and questionnaire compilation
This study conducted a systematic literature review and theoretical research in the early stage and used content analysis [ ] to extract elements related to promoting physical activity in older adults with SCD. Subsequently, based on the Wuli-Shili-Renli system model, the study preliminarily determined the primary indicators based on the three dimensions of this model. The Wuli-Shili-Renli system model [ ], proposed by Chinese systems science experts Professors Gu Jifa and Zhu Zhichang in 1994 at the University of Hull, United Kingdom, has been recognized by the International Society for the Systems Sciences as an “integrated systemic methodology.” It serves not only as a methodology but also as a tool for addressing complex problems. In WSR, “Wu” refers to things, objects, and substance, emphasizing the foundational and objective existence. “Wuli” represents mechanisms and regularities that determine the workings and effects of “Wu.” “Shi” denotes learning, doing, and human engagement with the world, with “shili” focusing on operation and management. “Ren” represents people, human, and humanity, while “renli” pertains to the principles, structures, and patterns of intersubjective relations, emphasizing coordination and interaction among individuals. The core theory of WSR posits that systemic practical activities encompass the dynamic unity of the material world, systemic organization, and human beings. These activities should involve the interrelations among physical, operational, and human aspects.
In this study, the WSR theory provides a systematic approach to analyzing the needs and barriers to physical activity among older adults with SCD. The “Wuli” dimension emphasizes understanding and analyzing the physical aspects of physical activity in older adults with SCD, such as environmental conditions and exercise content, helping to identify the fundamental factors that influence their participation. The “Shili” dimension focuses on management and operational aspects, making it suitable for analyzing how to enhance the willingness and effectiveness of physical activity participation among older adults with SCD through optimized management and social support mechanisms. The “Renli” dimension emphasizes interactions and coordination among individuals, helping to understand the impact of social environment, family support, and community interactions on the physical activity of older adults with SCD. Through a coordinated analysis of these dimensions, the WSR framework offers a comprehensive perspective that integrates physiological, psychological, and social factors, exploring the multi-layered factors that promote physical activity in older adults with SCD. This integrated systemic methodology better explains the complex relationship between SCD and physical activity, enhancing the scientific rigor and theoretical guidance of the research.
Finally, based on the systematic literature review and theoretical research mentioned above, this study identified the WSR framework content indicators for promoting physical activity among older adults with SCD through coding and summarizing using content analysis. These indicators include 3 primary indicators, 11 secondary indicators, and 45 tertiary indicators, aiming to comprehensively cover the key elements of the Wuli, Shili, and Renli dimensions, serving as the main content for expert consultation. The questionnaire for expert consultation consists of three parts: (1) Guidance on the questionnaire content and research objectives. (2) Inviting experts to evaluate the textual content of the survey questionnaire. This part uses a 5-point Likert scale to evaluate the indicators. A score of 5 indicates very important, 4 indicates important, 3 indicates moderate, 2 indicates unimportant, and 1 indicates very unimportant. Experts can provide their opinions at the corresponding positions in the questionnaire. (3) Obtaining information from experts, including their age, education, professional title, research field, years of work, judgment basis for indicator importance (Ca) and familiarity with indicator content (Cs).

## Consultation process
The research team distributed the survey questionnaire (Supplementary file Table ) to the experts via email and reminded them to reply within 7 days. After the first round of consultation, the research team analyzed the questionnaire. Based on the selection criteria, expert opinions, and group discussions, the indicator system was supplemented, deleted, and modified to form the questionnaire for the second round of expert consultation. Based on the scores of each indicator, the mean, full score rate, and coefficient of variation for the allocation of importance were calculated. When the coefficient of variation < 0.15 and the mean of the importance allocation > 4.00 [ ], these elements could be included. If both requirements cannot be met simultaneously, the final selection of indicators was determined based on expert modification suggestions and discussions within the research team. Finally, in the third round of expert consultation, the order diagram was incorporated to allow experts to rank the importance of indicators. The order diagram was first proposed by American scholar Mutti in 1955 [ ], and its main principle is to calculate weights based on the relative importance of data, i.e., by comparing each item pairwise to ultimately evaluate the priority sequence and importance. For example, more important items are marked as “1”, equally important items are marked as “0.5”, and unimportant items are marked as “0”. When conducting pairwise comparisons, one cannot compare with oneself. The larger the number, the higher the relative importance.

## Statistical methods
Data were double-entered and analyzed using SPSS 27.0 software package and Excel software. First, descriptive statistics were conducted for expert demographics, including percentages and frequencies. Next, the expert authority coefficient (Cr) and expert coordination coefficient (Kendall’s W) were calculated. Cr was calculated based on the experts’ judgment criteria for item content (Ca) and their familiarity (Cs) using the formula Cr = (Cs + Ca) / 2 (a higher Cr value indicates a greater degree of expert authority, with Cr ≥ 0.70 being considered as a high level of authority). Kendall’s W ranges from 0 to 1, where a value closer to 1 indicates better consistency. After testing Kendall’s W with a Chi-square test, if P < 0.05, it indicates significance and the results are considered reliable [ ]. ; otherwise, they are not. Then, the importance of items was assessed by calculating the mean, standard deviation, and coefficient of variation (CV) [ ]. Items with a mean importance score of less than 4 and a coefficient of variation below 0.15 were excluded. Finally, the weights of primary and secondary indicators were determined using the order diagram method, while the weights of tertiary indicators were calculated using the proportional allocation method.

## Results

## Information from the experts
Of the 18 experts invited to participate in this Delphi study, 11 completed round 1 (61.1% response rate), 11 completed round 2 (100.0% response rate) and 11 completed round 3 (100.0% response rate). 63.6% of the experts hold doctoral degrees, and over 50% of the experts have 20 years or more of work experience. The areas of work spanned geriatric nursing (18.2%), sports rehabilitation and promotion (9.1%), health promotion (18.2%), sports science (9.1%), geriatrics (18.2%), management (18.2%), and sociology (9.1%). ## Reliability of the expert panel
In Table  , detailed coordination results of expert opinions are presented. The Cr in this study was 0.891, indicating high credibility. Across the three rounds of expert consultations, the Kendall’s W scores showed improvement, demonstrating increasing consensus. In the first round, the Kendall’s W scores for secondary and tertiary indicators were both 0.640 (P < 0.001). In the second round, these scores were 0.744 and 0.563, respectively (P < 0.001). By the third round, consensus was achieved among experts on the first- and second-level indicators, making a Kendall’s W score calculation unnecessary. For the tertiary indicators, most experts provided a consensus score of 5, though a few rated certain indicators as 4, resulting in a slightly lower Kendall’s W score but with statistically significant differences.

 

## Results of expert consultation

## Delphi round 1
In the first round of expert consultation, the statistical results for the first-level indicators showed that the scores for the Static System, Dynamic System, and Active System ranged between 4 and 5. The mean score for the Static System was 4.818, while the Dynamic System and Active System both had a mean score of 4.909, with low coefficients of variation (ranging from 0.062 to 0.084), indicating a high level of agreement among the experts. However, all indicators still required modifications based on expert feedback. Experts 1, 3, and 10 pointed out that the original names of the Static System and other terms did not accurately reflect the characteristics of the dimension indicators. Therefore, we revised the three first-level indicators to: Basic System (Wuli), Operational System (Shili), and Personnel System (Renli).
Additionally, there were substantial changes to the second-level and third-level indicators. Thus, we reorganized the content of the second-level and third-level indicators, taking into account the indicators’ means, standard deviations, and coefficients of variation. We also revisited the literature and incorporated expert opinions. During these revisions, we comprehensively considered the means, standard deviations, and coefficients of variation of the indicators, and consulted literature and discussions within the research team. The final content for the second round of expert consultation includes three first-level indicators, nine second-level indicators, and forty-four third-level indicators.

## Delphi round 2
In the second round of expert consultation, all experts reached a consensus on the first-level indicators, and all were retained. The statistical results for each first-level indicator showed that the scores for the basic system, operational system, and personnel system were all 5, with a standard deviation of 0.000 and a CV of 0.000. This indicates complete agreement among the experts on these indicators, with no need for further modification.
Most second-level indicators received unanimous approval from the experts, with scores of 5, a standard deviation of 0.000, and a CV of 0.000, leading to their retention. However, the “Integration of physical activity resources” had a score range from 3 to 5, with an average of 3.909, a standard deviation of 0.701, and a CV of 0.179, indicating a lack of consensus among experts, leading to its removal.
Most third-level indicators were highly rated by the experts, with scores of 5, a standard deviation of 0.000, and a CV of 0.000, resulting in their retention. These included indicators like “Frequency of physical activity”, “Intensity of physical activity”, and “Duration of physical activity”. Some indicators, despite slight variations in scores, remained within an acceptable range (e.g., CV ≤ 0.062), such as “Progression of physical activity”, and “Configuration of venue facilities”, and were therefore retained or modified. However, some indicators were removed due to inconsistent scores or higher coefficients of variation, such as “Limitation of sedentary behavior” (average score of 3.727, CV 0.269) and “Public transportation accessibility” (average score of 4.364, CV 0.185). Additionally, indicators like “Integration of technical resources” and “Integration of funding resources” were removed due to lower-than-expected scores (with averages ranging from 3.727 to 4.182) and differing expert opinions. Certain indicators were streamlined or merged based on expert recommendations, such as merging indicators like “National Government” to improve the clarity of the system. The final outcome included the retention, modification, removal, and merging of specific indicators, laying the foundation for the next round of expert consultation. The revised content ultimately includes three first-level indicators, nine second-level indicators, and forty-four third-level indicators.

## Delphi round 3
In the third round of expert consultation, all experts maintained their scores for the primary indicators, resulting in the retention of all primary indicators. The experts also reached a consensus on the scores for all secondary indicators, all rated as 5, leading to the retention of all secondary indicators. Additionally, the average scores of all tertiary indicators were above 4, with CV less than 0.15, indicating a high level of agreement among the experts. Therefore, all tertiary indicators were retained. Specifically, most indicators received a score of 5.000, with a standard deviation of 0.000 and a coefficient of variation of 0.000, reflecting complete consensus among the experts on these indicators. A few indicators had a mean score slightly below 5 (e.g., 4.818 or 4.909), but they still fell within the range of consistency, thus were also retained. Ultimately, these tertiary indicators formed a relatively comprehensive system for promoting physical activity.
As a result, the final promotion indicator system includes three first-level indicators, nine second-level indicators, and forty-four third-level indicators. ## Discussion
The promotion system developed in this study, based on the Wuli-Shili-Renli system model, consists of 3 primary indicators, 9 secondary indicators, and 44 tertiary indicators. The three primary indicators are Basic System, Operational System, and Personnel System, with respective weights of 0.4242, 0.2879, and 0.2879. The weights for the secondary indicators range from 0.0698 to 0.1440, while for the tertiary indicators, they range from 0.0147 to 0.0480.
Among the primary indicators, the Basic System (0.4242) holds the highest weight, indicating its paramount importance in promoting physical activity behavior among seniors with SCD. It includes four sub-dimensions: physical activity content, built environment for physical activity, policies or funding for physical activity, and accessibility of physical activity. These indicators possess fundamental and rigid characteristics, serving as the cornerstone for promoting physical activity. Currently, there is still a lack of specific physical activity content tailored for older adults with SCD, with most guidelines and consensus advocating standards similar to those recommended by the WHO, albeit with low-quality evidence [ ]. Therefore, this promotion system deliberately emphasizes the content of physical activity for seniors with SCD, aiming to facilitate the accumulation of evidence from targeted content summaries and randomized controlled trial interventions, providing a higher level of evidence for determining the optimal amount and form of physical activity for seniors with SCD.
The aspect of physical activity built environment primarily reflects the physical environment. A previous umbrella review [ ] also indicated that the physical environment and resources are the factors with the highest evidence quality scores affecting physical activity behavior among older adults with cognitive impairment, aligning with our study’s findings. As more older adults in China move into nursing homes, we stress the importance of the physical activity environment in these setting, such as the configuration of activity facilities. Researchers like Susanna Nordin [ ] pointed out that the physical environment is particularly important for supporting the activity and interaction of older adults residing in care settings. Marinda Henskens’ research [ ] explored the impact of environmental stimuli on the physical activity levels of residents with dementia in nursing homes through a review of empirical studies, indicating that music, home-like environments, and functional modifications positively affect residents’ physical activity levels, indirectly emphasizing the importance of the “Configuration of Activity Facilities in Nursing Homes” tertiary indicator.
Several studies have emphasized the importance of policy and funding investment in physical activity for older adults. Effective policies can provide more opportunities for older adults to engage in physical activities, including the construction of facilities, organization of events, and provision of training. Thus, we include indicators like “Number of Policies” and “Number of Departments Implementing Policies Jointly.” Proper implementation and supervision are essential to ensure older adults with SCD benefit from these policies and achieve healthy aging [ ].
Research by Yufang Zhang et al. [ ] highlighted that the availability and accessibility of stores/business services and parks/open spaces are positively correlated with physical activity among older adults, which corresponds to the sub-dimension indicator of “Accessibility of Physical Activity” in our study. Jiabin Yu et al. [ ] conducted a cross-sectional random sampling survey of 252 elderly participants in Yiwu City, collecting data using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire and Neighborhood Environment Walkability Scale. The analysis revealed that lower residential density, better aesthetic environment, and higher street connectivity would encourage older adults to engage more in leisure walking and recreational sports activities. In the sub-dimension of “Accessibility of Physical Activity,” we include the indicator of “Home-based Physical Activity,” emphasizing the importance of home-based physical activity for seniors with SCD. An increasing number of researchers [ , ] have found that home-based exercises can significantly improve physical activity, balance, mobility, and muscle strength in older adults, especially during epidemic outbreaks [ ].
In the promotion system, the operational system and personnel system indicators have equal weights, highlighting their shared importance. The operational system addresses the coordination and management of activities for seniors with SCD, while the personnel system centers on a “people-oriented” approach, involving both promoters and those being promoted. The collaboration of various promoting entities, such as medical departments, social organizations, communities, and families, is vital for providing comprehensive support. Families, as a readily accessible support network, play a particularly important role. Research by Schlenk et al. [ ] shows that support from close family members can significantly increase physical activity levels in older adults.
For the recipients of promotion, the focus is on enhancing their acceptance of physical activity through improved cognitive recognition and motivation. A systematic review [ ] highlighted that some older adults perceive physical activity as unnecessary or potentially harmful, making it essential to increase their awareness of its benefits. Participation experience and motivation are crucial, as they directly influence the willingness of seniors with SCD to engage in physical activity. Selecting appropriate activities and providing incentives can significantly enhance their participation.

## Limitations
Initially, we reached out to 18 experts, but only received responses from 11, which was below our anticipated number, potentially affecting the consultation’s precision. However, these 11 experts are all esteemed authorities in the field, which significantly compensates for the numerical deficit. Furthermore, while the experts represent diverse provinces and municipalities, regional disparities may still exist. Lastly, our early efforts primarily focused on research review, with plans to refine the indicator system through qualitative research in the future.

## Conclusions
Based on the Wuli-Shili-Renli system model and through Delphi expert consultation, we have developed a relatively comprehensive indicator system for promoting physical activity among older adults with SCD. This system consists of 3 primary indicators, 9 secondary indicators, and 44 tertiary indicators. The promoting indicator system can offer targeted guidance based on the weight of indicators for relevant departments to take corresponding measures to improve physical activity among older adults with SCD. Moreover, it can also provide new theories and strategies for other countries to develop their own evaluation index system for improving the effectiveness of physical activity interventions among older adults with SCD.
