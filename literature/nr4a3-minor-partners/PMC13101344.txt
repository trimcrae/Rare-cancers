## Abstract

## Background
Aging is a complex process characterized by the progressive decline of physiological functions. This process is accompanied by a series of genetic and epigenetic alterations. Epigenetic mechanisms, including histone modifications, chromatin remodeling, and RNA modifications, are critical due to their highly dynamic and plastic nature. These mechanisms play fundamental roles in the occurrence and progression of aging and age-related diseases.

## Main body
This review systematically summarizes the fundamental roles and molecular mechanisms of histone modifications (e.g., methylation and acylation such as acetylation), chromatin remodeling (encompassing heterochromatin formation, nuclear lamina association, alterations in chromatin accessibility and higher-order architecture, and TF-mediated regulation), and RNA modifications (such as m6A, m7G, and m5C) in the regulation of cellular senescence, organ functional decline, and age-related diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders, CVDs, and osteoporosis. We highlight the sophisticated interaction networks formed between these three epigenetic layers. Finally, we summarize potential anti-aging interventions targeting these mechanisms. These strategies include natural products, peptides, and specific small-molecule inhibitors or agonists. Emerging intervention methods and their potential safety considerations are also discussed.

## Conclusion
Histone modifications, chromatin remodeling, and RNA modifications constitute an interconnected and dynamically coordinated epigenetic network that synergistically regulates the aging process. A thorough elucidation of the regulatory mechanisms within this complex network will not only contribute to a fundamental understanding of the biological essence of aging, but also provide a critical theoretical foundation for the development of precise and efficient epigenetic interventions.

Keywords: Epigenetics, Aging, Histone modifications, RNA modifications, Chromatin remodeling

## Background
Aging is a complex biological process characterized by a progressive decline in physiological functions over time. This decline significantly increases the risk of age-related diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders, cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), and osteoporosis [ ]. In recent years, aging research has made significant progress. The hallmarks of aging have expanded from twelve to fourteen, incorporating “dysregulated extracellular matrix remodeling” and “circadian rhythm disruption” [ ]. The former refers to structural and chemical changes in the cellular scaffold that impair tissue integrity. Meanwhile, the latter signifies the desynchronization of internal biological clocks that regulate key metabolic and cellular rhythms.
Epigenetics regulates gene expression and cellular phenotypes without changing the DNA sequence. Its core layers include DNA methylation, histone modifications, chromatin remodeling, and RNA modifications [ ]. These layers are dynamically regulated by environmental signals throughout life. DNA methylation is a classic and well-supported aging hallmark. It has led to various “epigenetic clocks,” such as Horvath, Hannum, PhenoAge and GrimAge [ ]. These clocks assess biological age and disease risk. Although DNA methylation has demonstrated responsiveness to environmental and pharmacological interventions [ – ], its turnover dynamics are generally slower compared to histone and RNA modifications [ – ]. Due to their higher plasticity and faster dynamics, histone and RNA modifications may represent promising hubs for linking basic mechanisms to clinical interventions.
This review focuses on histone modifications, chromatin remodeling, and RNA modifications. These three layers have high plasticity and tight regulatory links. We systematically explain their roles and mechanisms in aging. We also explore their cross-regulatory networks. Furthermore, we summarize potential anti-aging strategies. Through an integrated “mechanism-network-intervention” perspective, we clarify how these layers maintain gene expression balance. We aim to promote their use in clinical practice, which includes biomarker development, patient stratification, and therapeutic monitoring. Ultimately, this provides a basis for preventing and treating age-related diseases.
To build an integrative framework, we systematically searched the PubMed database for relevant literature published between 2020 and 2025. The search strategy combined keywords such as RNA modification, histone modifications and chromatin remodeling with senescence, aging, age-related diseases, mitochondrial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, genomic instability, and telomere attrition. Literature selection prioritized high-quality research papers that report original experimental data. Review articles were also utilized to supplement background information and integrate key perspectives.

## Histone modifications
Histones are the core structural proteins of eukaryotic chromatin, including H1, H2A, H2B, H3, and H4. These proteins are responsible for packaging DNA into nucleosomes. Histone post-translational modifications (PTMs) refer to covalent chemical modifications that occur on histone amino acid residues. Common types of these modifications include methylation, acetylation, and lactylation. These modifications do not alter the underlying DNA sequence. Instead, they regulate chromatin structure and accessibility to influence gene transcription. Therefore, they constitute a core epigenetic regulatory mechanism. This review focuses on methylation and various acylation modifications that are closely coupled with metabolism. These include acetylation, crotonylation, lactylation, and succinylation (Table  ). Other modification types, such as ubiquitination and SUMOylation, are beyond the scope of this discussion.
 Histone modifications are dynamically regulated by three classes of proteins: “writers,” “erasers,” and “readers.” “Writers” are responsible for adding modifications, such as histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and methyltransferases. Conversely, “erasers” remove these modifications, including histone deacetylases (HDACs) and demethylases. “Readers,” such as bromodomain-containing proteins, recognize specific modification states to mediate downstream biological functions. These proteins collectively participate in a series of critical biological processes, including cell differentiation, development, and DNA repair. They also play significant roles in the occurrence of various diseases. An increasing body of evidence suggests that the dysregulation of epigenetic regulators related to histone modifications is a major susceptibility factor for aging and its associated diseases.

## The role of histone modifications in cellular senescence and age-related diseases

## Histone methylation
Histone methylation is a fundamental epigenetic mechanism involving the addition of methyl groups to lysine or arginine residues on histones H3 and H4. This modification is dynamically regulated by methyltransferases (such as HMTs) and demethylases (such as KDMs). Its activation or suppression effects on gene expression depend on the specific modified sites and their methylation states. For instance, H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 typically mark active transcription. Conversely, H3K9me2/3 and H3K27me3 mediate gene silencing. The establishment and maintenance of methylation patterns depend on S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) metabolism. Furthermore, these patterns are guided by non-coding RNAs and recognized by specific “reader” proteins.
H3K4me3 is one of the most extensively studied activation markers. It is catalyzed by six distinct COMPASS complexes containing homologous core proteins SET1A, SET1B, and MLL1–4 [ , ]. These complexes share core structural components such as WDR5, ASH2L, RBBP5 and DPY30 [ – ]. The homeostasis of H3K4me3 is essential for maintaining cellular functions. The loss of SETD1A leads to reduced H3K4me3 levels. This reduction subsequently upregulates p21 and p27 to induce cell cycle arrest and direct cellular senescence [ ]. Similarly, the depletion of ASH2L decreases global H3K4 methylation. This disruption impairs gene expression and cell cycle progression to promote aging [ ]. On the other hand, the excessive accumulation of H3K4me3 can also drive aging. For example, SMYD3 accelerates Angiotensin II-induced senescence by driving the local enrichment of H3K4me3 at the CDKN1A promoter [ ]. Additionally, alterations in the H3K4me3 landscape can improve lipid metabolism in offspring and delay aging. This suggests its potential role as a transgenerational epigenetic signal [ ]. These findings indicate that H3K4me3 changes can either be a cause of aging or a result of adaptive changes and intergenerational regulation.
H3K36me3 is another critical transcriptionally active histone modification. it plays a key role in maintaining homeostasis across various tissues. Its levels generally decline during the aging process. This decline leads directly to tissue-specific functional impairment and aging phenotypes. During bone formation, SETD2-mediated H3K36me3 deposition at the LBP gene promoter promotes osteogenic differentiation to counteract osteoporosis [ ]. In senescent chondrocytes of osteoarthritis, H3K36 methylation levels undergo a comprehensive decline. Specifically, H3K36me1/2 catalyzed by NSD1 activates the expression of the transcription factor Osr2. This process is vital for maintaining chondrocyte differentiation and joint homeostasis [ ]. In hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), the Tet1 protein coordinates the dual modifications of H3K27me3 (inhibitory) and H3K36me3 (activating) in a gene-specific manner. This coordination precisely regulates the expression of cell cycle inhibitors like p19, p21, and p27 to maintain HSC homeostasis. Hypercholesterolemia disrupts this balance and leads to HSC senescence [ ]. During brain aging, H3K36me3 in neurons significantly decreases. This decrease triggers DNA damage, disrupts DNA methylation, and inhibits Bdnf expression. These events collectively accelerate cognitive decline and neurodegeneration [ ]. In senescent human fibroblasts, the specific demethylases KDM4A/B are highly expressed to actively remove H3K36me3. This process leads to increased chromatin accessibility. It subsequently promotes the binding of pro-inflammatory transcription factors like AP-1 and NF-κB to activate SASP expression and drive inflammaging [ ]. These studies demonstrate that H3K36 methylation decreases during aging with distinct tissue-specific patterns in the nervous, skeletal, and hematopoietic systems. Furthermore, it works with other histone modifications-especially H3K27me3-to drive changes in chromatin openness and the activation of aging-related transcriptional programs.
H3K9me3 is a critical histone methylation mark for maintaining heterochromatin stability and silencing repetitive sequences. This mark is primarily catalyzed by methyltransferases such as SUV39H1. Its role during the aging process is highly context-dependent. Specifically, both abnormal increases and decreases in H3K9me3 levels can drive aging-related phenotypes. Regarding abnormal H3K9me3 elevation, age-related weakening of BDNF signaling in the brain induces increased H3K9me3 in the hippocampus. This elevation subsequently impairs synaptic function and leads to cognitive decline [ ]. In human mesenchymal stem cells, the loss of the demethylase KDM4B results in H3K9me3 accumulation. This process triggers the formation of senescence-associated heterochromatin foci to impair stem cell self-renewal and exacerbate osteoporosis [ ]. Furthermore, the age-related lncRNA PURPL promotes the expression of senescence-associated genes by regulating H3K9me3 deposition at specific loci, such as SERPINE1 and EGR1 [ ]. Conversely, abnormal decreases in H3K9me3 also contribute to aging. In neural progenitor cells, the downregulation of SUV39H1 reduces H3K9me3 levels. This reduction accelerates cellular senescence through the ALKBH5-DNMT3B axis [ ]. In hematopoietic stem cells, miR-125b overexpression decreases H3K9me3 by inhibiting SUV39H1. This mechanism subsequently impairs B-cell differentiation and promotes immunosenescence [ ]. In osteoarthritic cartilage, a global reduction in H3K9me3 leads to increased chromatin accessibility and transcriptional activation of endogenous retroviruses. These events trigger chronic inflammation and drive disease progression [ ]. In adult mice, the specific loss of H3K9me3 directly triggers progeroid programs. This evidence proves that the loss of its stability is an upstream cause of aging [ ]. In summary, global reductions in H3K9me3 drive genomic instability and aging. However, locus-specific H3K9me3 deposition leads to the silencing of key genes and functional dysregulation. It alters the output of specific genetic programs without disrupting the global chromatin structure.
H3K27me3 is another vital inhibitory mark catalyzed primarily by the PRC2 complex. In the aging brain, PRC2-mediated H3K27me3 modifications exhibit significant region-specific dynamic changes [ ]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, EZH2-mediated H3K27me3 suppresses cellular senescence by inhibiting the miR-139-5p promoter to upregulate TOP2A [ ]. In hepatic stellate cells, PRC2-EZH2-mediated H3K27me3 maintains cellular quiescence. Conversely, its demethylation promotes activation and senescence. Therefore, the balance between these states determines cellular fate [ ]. Notably, H3K27me3 reprogramming during aging involves RNA-chromatin interactions. The RNA methylation-related protein METTL14 can simultaneously recruit PRC2 and the demethylase KDM6B. This recruitment establishes and maintains “bivalent chromatin domains.” These domains feature the coexistence of H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 at the same promoter region to keep genes in a “poised” state for rapid activation [ ]. During aging, the plasticity of these domains gradually diminishes. They tend to undergo “biased dissociation” that leads to excessive gene silencing or abnormal activation [ ]. In this context, H3K27me3 changes represent both a consequence of aging and a feedback mechanism that shapes the aging phenotype.
In conclusion, evidence from physiological aging indicates that specific and targeted alterations in histone methylation are potent drivers of senescence. These changes particularly include the loss of H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 or the global reduction of H3K9me3. Once established, these alterations and broader methylation reprogramming become intrinsic components of the aging epigenome. They subsequently act as consequences to stabilize and exacerbate irreversible growth arrest and the secretory phenotype. This process ultimately creates a vicious cycle where causes and consequences are intricately intertwined.

## Histone acetylation
Histone acetylation is a classic post-translational modification that utilizes acetyl-CoA as the acyl donor. This process is catalyzed by HATs and can be reversed by HDACs and the Sirtuin family. Its homeostasis is precisely regulated by both enzymatic activity and cellular energy metabolic states. Acetylation neutralizes the positive charge of histone lysine residues. This action weakens the interaction between histones and negatively charged DNA to relax chromatin structure. Therefore, it serves as a key epigenetic mechanism for activating gene transcription.
Research indicates that the same histone acetylation modification can play opposite roles under different physiological or pathological contexts. Its function depends on the specific location, cell type, and overall epigenetic environment. H3K27ac is a classic marker of gene activation, and its dysregulation leads to aging or disease. In the endometrial stromal cells of middle-aged women, reduced H3K27ac levels lead to the downregulation of progesterone receptor (PGR) expression. This decline subsequently affects embryo implantation and pregnancy maintenance [ ]. In macrophages of mice with rheumatoid arthritis, the loss of RBM25 causes metabolic reprogramming. The resulting excess acetyl-CoA drives the abnormal accumulation of H3K27ac. This accumulation over-activates pro-inflammatory genes and triggers inflammation [ ]. Conversely, the overexpression of the transcription factor SOX5 in hMPCs can enhance the activity of H3K27ac-enriched enhancers. This process drives the expression of anti-aging genes like HMGB2 to achieve cellular rejuvenation. It has been successfully applied to alleviate osteoarthritis [ ]. These findings highlight the potential of targeting H3K27ac in treating age-related diseases.
H4K16ac also serves as an activation marker, but its abnormal elevation promotes pathological processes. In lung fibroblasts from aged mice and IPF patients, H4K16ac levels are significantly increased. Reducing the acetyltransferase Mof can lower the expression of pro-fibrotic genes and improve the fibrotic process [ ]. In aging Drosophila oocytes, decreased Sirt1 activity causes elevated H4K16ac levels. This elevation leads to abnormal chromosome condensation and segregation errors. Activating Sirt1 with SRT1720 significantly reduces these age-dependent chromosomal errors [ ].
Acetylation and methylation are not isolated from each other. The “give-and-take” between these two modifications on the same nucleosome often determines chromatin openness and gene fate. In oocyte aging models, the loss of HDAC1 leads to abnormally high H4K12ac accompanied by reduced H3K4me3 levels. This imbalance between acetylation and methylation is a key epigenetic hallmark of declining oocyte quality [ ]. Supplementing with the antioxidant melatonin can reverse these modification abnormalities and improve aging phenotypes [ ].
The Sirtuin family consists of NAD⁺-dependent deacylases. Their activity is regulated by intracellular NAD⁺ levels to link metabolic status closely with epigenetic regulation [ ]. Members of this family function by acting on different targets during aging. In cellular senescence, the downregulation of SIRT1 expression disrupts epigenetic homeostasis, such as H3K9ac levels. This disruption also impairs critical cellular functions like increasing ROS or damaging spindles and mitochondria. These events eventually lead to the decline of post-ovulatory oocyte quality [ ]. SIRT3 plays a critical role in maintaining mitochondrial function by removing acetyl groups from various mitochondrial proteins to regulate their activity [ ]. Meanwhile, SIRT4 functions in cellular stress responses and nutrient supply. Its dysfunction is associated with metabolic imbalance and potential accelerated aging [ ]. A decline in SIRT3 leads to mesenchymal stem cell senescence and bone loss. However, H2S can stabilize heterochromatin and mitochondria through S-sulfhydration to delay aging [ ]. SIRT4 inhibits GDH enzyme activity via ADP-ribosylation. Its age-related decline in expression causes α-ketoglutarate accumulation and promotes SASP secretion [ ]. These findings suggest that Sirtuin family members integrate energy metabolism and epigenetic signals through two complementary pathways: regulating chromatin modifications or mitochondrial function and metabolite production. These pathways collectively modulate the aging process.
Evidence from physiological aging indicates that histone acetylation plays a significant role in aging regulation. Through metabolic intervention or targeting modification enzymes, new strategies may be developed to delay aging and improve functional decline.

## Histone succinylation
Histone succinylation is a recently discovered post-translational modification that utilizes succinyl-CoA as the donor. Similar to acetylation, succinylation regulates chromatin structure and gene activity by altering the charge properties of histones. This modification is closely linked to cellular metabolic processes and is negatively regulated by the chromatin-associated protein CDYL. Research indicates that succinylation plays a complex, bidirectional regulatory role during the aging process.
Succinylation possesses bidirectional regulatory characteristics. It may promote inflammation and damage accumulation or exert protective effects under appropriate contexts. Its dynamic balance is therefore essential for healthy aging. During aging, the decline in CDYL expression or activity leads to the hypersuccinylation of replication protein A1 (RPA1). This over-modification impairs the efficiency of homologous recombination repair. Consequently, it results in the accumulation of DNA double-strand breaks and genomic instability to drive the emergence of aging phenotypes [ ]. In the monocytes and macrophages of myocardial infarction patients and mouse models, Hat1 catalyzes H3K23succ to alter chromatin accessibility. This change directly regulates the expression of pro-inflammatory genes [ ]. Furthermore, the lysine acetyltransferase KAT2A can catalyze H3K79succ to upregulate the expression of SAT2, a gene associated with polyamine metabolism. This upregulation triggers ferroptosis in renal cells and leads to kidney functional impairment [ ]. However, recent studies also suggest that high levels of histone succinylation may represent an epigenetic hallmark associated with longevity predisposition. Supplementing middle-aged mice with succinate selectively improved indicators related to motor function [ ]. These findings suggest that modulating succinylation levels through metabolic intervention may specifically delay certain functional declines. Such strategies possess potential value for extending healthspan.

## Histone crotonylation
Lysine crotonylation is a novel histone modification that was first discovered and named by Professor Yue Zhao’s team at the University of Chicago in 2011 [ ]. It is enriched in transcriptionally active chromatin regions and is generally considered a stronger and more specific gene activation marker than acetylation. The role of crotonylation in aging and related diseases is highly context-dependent and bidirectional. Specifically, it can exert protective effects or drive pathological processes.
Potential anti-aging and renoprotective effects involve metabolic enzymes and specific signaling pathways. In chronic kidney disease, H3K9cr in tubular cells mediated by the metabolic enzyme ACSS2 can inhibit the IL-1β signaling pathway. This specific modification alleviates macrophage activation and tubular cell senescence to delay renal fibrosis [ ]. Furthermore, supplementing with enzymatic poly-oligosaccharides (EPO) from marine algae significantly increases histone crotonylation levels in the intestinal cells of elderly diabetic models. This intervention effectively improves glucose metabolism disorders and delays tissue aging in aged individuals [ ].
Conversely, crotonylation also exhibits pro-aging and pathological effects in different tissues. In cardiomyocytes, a specific metabolic defect involving ECHS1 deficiency leads to a global increase in histone crotonylation. This widespread modification abnormally activates hypertrophy-related genes to directly promote the pathogenesis of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy associated with cardiac aging and failure [ ]. In neurons, the histone crotonylation reader dENL/AF9 serves as a negative regulator of aging. Knocking down dENL/AF9 can extend the lifespan of Drosophila [ ]. During the early stages of AD, the decreased expression of lncRNA NEAT1 leads to elevated H3K27 crotonylation and reduced H3K27 acetylation. This imbalance impairs the clearance of Aβ by glial cells through endocytosis and autophagy to exacerbate AD pathology [ , ].
In summary, the functional consequences of crotonylation are determined by the cellular environment and the nature of metabolic dysregulation. These factors include whether the modification is a regulated site-specific change or a global, defect-driven accumulation. These findings are based on evidence obtained within the context of physiological aging.

## Histone lactylation
Histone lactylation was discovered in 2019 and is directly regulated by the metabolite lactate [ ]. Lactate is no longer viewed merely as a byproduct of glycolysis. Its biological importance has been fundamentally reassessed. The role of lactylation in aging exhibits a distinct duality. This duality is profoundly influenced by tissue-specific effects, cell types, and the dosage or duration of metabolic perturbations, such as acute injury versus chronic stress.
Tissue and cell-type specific effects manifest across various systems. In the brain, activated microglia under aging or neurodegenerative contexts continuously accumulate lactate. This accumulation leads to elevated H3K18la levels. It subsequently activates the NF-κB pathway to drive the expression of inflammatory factors and exacerbate neuroinflammation [ ]. In the skin, macrophages in the aging microenvironment continuously uptake lactate through MCT1. This process catalyzes the production of H4K12la to activate TGF-β signaling in fibroblasts. Such activation results in excessive collagen synthesis and fibrosis [ ]. Simultaneously, this modification induces HDAC3 expression to form a negative feedback loop. This loop demonstrates an inherent repair mechanism [ ]. In the vasculature, long-term lipid peroxidation causes lactate accumulation in endothelial cells. This accumulation increases H3K18la to drive SNAI1 expression. This process induces endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition and functional impairment to promote atherosclerosis [ ]. Smooth muscle cells enhance glycolysis through NR4A3 under pathological stimuli. The resulting lactate increases H3K18la to recruit YTHDF2. This recruitment activates the osteogenic gene Phospho1 and drives abnormal vascular calcification [ ]. In the immune system, H3K18la configuration at the promoters of repair genes (such as IL10, LRG1, and VEGF-A) in M2 macrophages correlates positively with their polarization. This modification further promotes cardiac repair after infarction [ ]. In the liver, hexokinase 2 upregulation in hepatocytes facilitates lactate accumulation. This increases H3K18la levels at the promoters of pro-fibrotic genes to promote fibrosis [ ]. In the lungs, alveolar epithelial cells suffer acute injury from sepsis-related ARDS. Lactate sharply induces H3K18la to activate the EGR1/HPSE pathway. This sequence leads to the degradation of the glycocalyx barrier and lung injury [ ]. Pharmacological inhibition, such as targeting KAT2B, can block this lactylation modification to alleviate pathological damage [ ]. This suggests the repair potential of intervening in this pathway. In the hematopoietic system, TET2 deficiency in aged HSPCs helps maintain specific nuclear H3K9me3 and lactylation patterns. These patterns protect heterochromatin structure and inhibit retrovirus activation. Consequently, they maintain genomic stability and stem cell function [ ]. In skeletal muscle, H3K18la can be increased through lactate supplementation or hypoxia. This intervention successfully restores stem cell regenerative capacity and delays their exhaustion [ ].
The dosage and duration of metabolic perturbations significantly influence outcomes. Chronic and persistent metabolic stress, such as in brain aging [ ], skin fibrosis [ ], atherosclerosis [ ], and liver fibrosis [ ], drives pro-aging lactylation modifications through long-term, low-level lactate accumulation. In contrast, acute and severe metabolic disturbances, such as sepsis-related ARDS [ ], induce acute pathological lactylation through rapid and massive lactate production to cause acute tissue damage. Controlled lactate supplementation or hypoxia in skeletal muscle [ ], along with HDAC3-mediated feedback in the skin [ ], demonstrates the potential role of moderate and regulated lactylation in maintaining homeostasis or promoting repair.
In summary, the harmful effects of histone lactylation typically emerge during chronic and high-intensity metabolic perturbations. It drives pathological processes through cell-type specific over-modification. Examples include NF-κB activation in microglia, TGF-β activation in skin fibroblasts, and endothelial dysfunction in blood vessels. It also includes osteogenic gene activation in smooth muscle cells, pro-fibrotic signaling in hepatocytes, and glycocalyx degradation in lung epithelium. Conversely, lactylation may participate in repair and adaptation under controlled conditions or specific cellular states. These include promoting repair in M2 macrophages and inhibiting excessive fibrosis through the HDAC3 feedback loop. They also include maintaining heterochromatin in HSPCs and supporting stem cell regeneration in skeletal muscle. Understanding this spatiotemporal and dose-dependent nature is essential for developing precise intervention strategies for specific aging pathologies. It is important to note that some examples in this section, such as fibrosis, atherosclerosis and sepsis, are derived from disease models. Their applicability to normal physiological aging should be extrapolated with caution.
 Taken together, histone modifications exhibit multi-layered dynamic patterns during the aging process. These changes can be primarily categorized into two types: global alterations in modification levels and locus-specific epigenetic reprogramming. At the global level, the reduction of heterochromatin markers represented by H3K9me3 is a critical early event that drives aging. Its loss of stability directly leads to genomic instability, activation of retrotransposable elements, and chronic inflammation [ ]. Simultaneously, the local enrichment of specific activation markers (such as H3K4me3 and H3K27ac) at inflammation-related loci systematically promotes the activation and maintenance of the SASP.
Significant crosstalk and synergistic characteristics exist between different modifications. Metabolic reprogramming continuously provides substrates for methylation and various acylation modifications, such as acetylation, succinylation, crotonylation, and lactylation (Fig.  ). These modifications are therefore tightly coupled with cellular energy states and the microenvironment. The mutual regulation between chromatin accessibility and modification states constitutes a complex feedback network. For example, the loss of plasticity in bivalent domains accelerates the transcriptional dysregulation of aging-related genes. The destabilization of heterochromatin, the imbalance of inflammatory networks, and the remodeling of the epigenome by metabolic signals represent three interconnected themes. Together, these factors drive and define the epigenetic landscape of aging.
 

## Targeted histone modification intervention for aging
Targeting histone modifications to intervene in aging represents a critical direction within epigenetic regulation. Current research primarily focuses on reversible modifications such as histone acetylation. However, the development of clinical translation for these related intervention strategies remains uneven.
Regarding agonists, compounds targeting the Sirtuin family have demonstrated potential. For example, resveratrol and SRT2104 can improve metabolic function, maintain tissue homeostasis, and extend healthspan in mouse models [ , ]. Nevertheless, these STACs are limited in clinical trials by low bioavailability, poor pharmacokinetics, and insufficient target specificity. Consequently, they have failed to effectively replicate the benefits observed in animal models [ – ]. In contrast, research on HDAC inhibitors is more advanced. While pan-HDAC inhibitors (such as vorinostat and romidepsin) and selective inhibitors (such as chidamide) have received FDA approval for hematological malignancies, their application in anti-aging remains in the preclinical stage. Studies show that HDAC inhibitors like MS275, SAHA, ITF2357, and sodium butyrate can improve muscle mitochondrial function and reverse progeroid skin phenotypes in various aging models. They also protect cardiac function and prevent muscle atrophy [ , , , ]. Although animal results are encouraging, the efficacy and safety of HDAC inhibitors in human aging remain to be verified. Currently, lifestyle interventions closely related to epigenetic regulation remain the most practical strategies. For instance, exercise enhances DNA repair by altering histone lactylation levels in skeletal muscle to reverse age-related muscle decline [ ]. Additionally, dietary restriction can extend healthspan by reducing global histone acetylation levels [ ].
However, most small-molecule drugs, including agonists, inhibitors, and natural products, face common translational challenges. The primary issue is the lack of site specificity. Their global alteration of histone modifications may simultaneously activate beneficial anti-aging pathways and harmful oncogenic or inflammatory pathways. Their epigenetic effects are often byproducts of multi-target actions rather than results of precision design. Furthermore, strategies aimed at globally “relaxing” chromatin to activate silenced genes carry potential oncogenic risks. This risk arises because such strategies may simultaneously release the inhibition of proto-oncogenes. Moreover, the timing and reversibility of intervention are critical. It remains unclear whether researchers should implement long-term mild regulation early in life or short-term intensive intervention in old age. Additionally, whether interventions are reversible (such as using transient small-molecule inhibitors) directly affects the safety of long-term use.
To overcome these challenges, frontier research is progressing in two directions. The first direction involves developing programmed precision tools, such as site-specific epigenetic editing systems. The second direction explores epigenetic reprogramming. Recent studies indicate that short-term induction of reprogramming factors like OSKM can reset the “epigenetic clock” in vivo. By remodeling chromatin accessibility and histone modification profiles, this approach reverses age-related epigenetic disorders and restores cellular functions. Simultaneously, controlling the degree of reprogramming avoids the risk of teratomas. This provides a highly promising new strategy for anti-aging [ ].
In summary, anti-aging interventions targeting histone modifications show diverse potential in preclinical studies. However, their clinical translation is still limited by core challenges such as drug specificity, safety, and timing. Future breakthroughs will depend on developing precision tools with higher specificity, such as site-specific editors. It is also essential to deeply explore emerging strategies like controllable epigenetic reprogramming to achieve safe and effective aging interventions.

## Chromatin remodeling
Chromatin is the fundamental biological structure composed of DNA wrapped around histones, with the nucleosome serving as its basic unit. Each nucleosome core particle contains approximately 147 base pairs of DNA coiled around a histone octamer. Chromatin exists in two dynamic states: euchromatin and heterochromatin. These states are critical for cellular fate determination, gene expression regulation, and the maintenance of genomic stability.
Chromatin remodeling is primarily regulated by ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling complexes. Based on the homology of the ATPase domains in their catalytic subunits, these complexes are classified into four major families: SWI/SNF, ISWI, CHD, and INO80/SWR1. All these families utilize energy derived from ATP hydrolysis to alter chromatin structure. However, each family forms functionally specific complexes by recruiting different accessory subunits. These complexes participate extensively in various cellular processes, including gene expression regulation, three-dimensional genomic organization, and DNA replication and repair. Specifically, the ISWI and CHD families primarily maintain regular spacing and ordered arrangement of nucleosomes. In contrast, the SWI/SNF and INO80 families tend to actively disrupt this order. They create local chromatin accessibility by sliding or evicting nucleosomes or exchanging histone variants to precisely regulate downstream functions [ ].
During the aging process, the chromatin remodeling system undergoes functional dysregulation. This decline manifests as heterochromatin loss, decreased nucleosome density, redistribution of chromatin accessibility, and A/B compartment switching (Fig. ). These systemic changes drive the dysregulation of gene expression networks and genomic instability. Consequently, they lead to a progressive decline in cellular function [ ]. Understanding the functional patterns of chromatin remodeling in cellular senescence and related diseases can provide a theoretical foundation for targeted intervention strategies.
 

## The role of chromatin remodeling in cellular senescence

## Heterochromatin and nuclear lamina
The decompaction of heterochromatin structure and the loss of nuclear structural integrity are among the most significant changes in senescent cells. The resulting abnormally increased chromatin accessibility and dysregulated expression of aging-related genes, including the SASP, represent key epigenetic events driving cellular senescence.
In senescent human primary fibroblasts, TP53 activation leads to the downregulation of TRF2 expression. This process subsequently initiates the ATM signaling pathway to drive the release of KAP1 and the loss of Lamin B1. These events eventually cause the decompaction of pericentromeric heterochromatin and increase the risk of DNA damage and chromosomal rearrangement [ ]. Similarly, research in human mesenchymal stem cells indicates that SIRT3 maintains heterochromatin stability through interactions with Lamin B1 and KAP1. The deficiency of SIRT3 impairs the dissociation of lamina-associated domains from the nuclear envelope and increases chromatin accessibility. This leads to the aberrant transcription of repetitive elements and promotes cellular senescence [ ]. In Hutchinson-Gilford Progeria Syndrome, mutant lamin A acts as a pathological driver to disrupt nuclear lamina integrity. This disruption causes abnormal nuclear morphology, decreased mechanical strength, and increased overall chromatin accessibility. Consequently, it triggers the dysregulated expression of aging-related genes to impair cellular function and cause premature senescence in human fibroblasts [ ]. Apolipoprotein E (APOE) has been identified as a critical regulator of senescence in human mesenchymal progenitor cells. Through interaction with the autophagy-related protein LC3, the accumulation of APOE mediates the degradation of lamin proteins and the heterochromatin protein KAP1. This process disrupts the structure of lamina-associated domains, increases chromatin accessibility, and upregulates SASP gene expression to drive cellular senescence [ ]. Ribosomal protein RPL22 abnormally accumulates in the nucleolus during aging to trigger the degradation of HP1γ and KAP1. This degradation leads to the disintegration of heterochromatin structure and the abnormal activation of rRNA transcription, causing nucleolar dysfunction and hypertrophy [ ]. Furthermore, aging causes an abnormal spatial redistribution of heterochromatin originally located near the nuclear envelope. In aging hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, the demethylation activity of TET2 drives harmful spatial relocation of heterochromatin. Conversely, cells lacking TET2 maintain normal perinuclear localization of heterochromatin and gain a clonal expansion advantage within the aging environment [ ].

## Chromatin accessibility and 3D genome architecture
Cellular senescence is accompanied by a global redistribution of chromatin accessibility and the reorganization of three-dimensional genomic structures. These changes include compartment switching and the remodeling of local chromatin loops. These processes are primarily driven by dynamic alterations in histone modifications and the dysregulated binding capacity of key transcription factors. Ultimately, these factors disrupt the homeostasis of chromatin spatial structure and accessibility to cause the aberrant expression of aging-related genes and the establishment of senescence phenotypes.
In the senescence process of BJ fibroblasts, two histone modifications synergistically drive the senescence phenotype. The loss of H3K9me3 (Refer to the “Histone methylation” section) promotes local heterochromatin interactions and the formation of new chromatin loops. Meanwhile, the accumulation of H3K27me3 (Refer to the “Histone methylation” section) mediates large-scale compartment switching, specifically from the silent B compartment to the active A compartment. This reorganization process triggers a widespread redistribution of regions with increased or decreased accessibility across the entire genome. These changes cause abnormal gene expression to drive cellular senescence [ ]. AP-1 serves as a factor responsible for driving the reconstruction of chromatin accessibility in identified accessible regions (IARs). It is critical for reconstructing chromatin accessibility to promote cellular senescence in aging human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC) [ ]. In aging umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells, TEAD4 dissociates from its originally bound “senescence-activated accessible areas” (SAAs). This dissociation leads to a significant increase in chromatin accessibility within these regions. Such changes directly result in the upregulation of neighboring SASP gene expression [ ].

## Transcription factors and pioneer factor-mediated remodeling
Transcription factors and pioneer factors play dual core roles as “program setters” and “state regulators” during the aging process by mediating chromatin remodeling. Chromatin opening driven by the pioneer factor AP-1 serves as a fundamental driving force for the transition from youth to maturity. This factor adapts the organism to the functional requirements of maturity by moderately “breaking” overly specialized cellular identity programs. However, this “opening” program becomes continuously and excessively activated during aging to function as a form of “hijacking.” This persistent and unrestricted chromatin opening eventually disintegrates the gene regulatory networks essential for maintaining cellular identity and normal metabolism. Consequently, it leads to functional decline and the emergence of senescence phenotypes [ ].
In early life, transient transcriptional activation of the longevity-associated pioneer factor dFOXO can durably remodel chromatin states and gene expression programs through a “transcriptional memory” mechanism. This process depends on the chromatin remodeling complexes SWI/SNF and ISWI. Such remodeling alters the subsequent aging trajectory to eventually extend healthspan [ ]. Simultaneously, the activation of the transcription factor Xbp1 has been confirmed to initiate protective mechanisms during late aging stages in Drosophila models. This finding suggests that Xbp1 similarly possesses anti-aging potential [ ].

## Metabolism-chromatin coupling
Metabolism serves as the foundation of cellular function by providing energy and biosynthetic precursors. Furthermore, intermediate metabolites generated during metabolic processes can directly participate in the regulation of chromatin structure. These metabolites dynamically influence chromatin accessibility and gene expression by acting as substrates for enzymatic reactions in epigenetic modifications or by directly modulating the activity of chromatin remodeling complexes. Consequently, they play a critical role in the aging process.
S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) serves as the universal methyl donor for histone and DNA methylation. In aging human muscle stem cells, SAM is preferentially utilized for polyamine synthesis. This reallocation leads to a decline in global methylation levels and the erosion of heterochromatin structure. These changes subsequently exacerbate DNA damage and cell death [ ].
Acetyl-CoA functions as a key mitochondrion-derived metabolic signal. Its concentration fluctuations directly regulate chromatin states. In C. elegans, mild mitochondrial stress during early life leads to a decrease in Acetyl-CoA levels. This reduction promotes the nuclear entry of the NuRD complex and the transcription factor DVE-1. Such translocation enhances histone deacetylation and induces chromatin reorganization to eventually extend lifespan [ ].

## The role of chromatin remodeling in age-related diseases
Given its critical role in gene expression regulation, DNA repair, and the maintenance of genomic stability, the dysregulation of chromatin remodeling is closely linked to the occurrence and development of age-related diseases.

## Cardiac aging
During the process of cardiac aging in humans and mice, ATP-citrate lyase (ACLY) catalyzes the generation of acetyl-CoA. This process activates HATs and enhances the chromatin accessibility of pro-fibrotic genes to eventually exacerbate cardiac fibrosis [ ]. In age-related cardiac hypertrophy, the downregulation of MLF1 expression can reduce chromatin accessibility at the IL1B and p21 gene loci to alleviate pathological hypertrophy [ ].

## Hepatic aging and metabolic disorders
In aging mouse livers, the overexpression of MIER1 interferes with chromatin remodeling and inhibits the EIF2S pathway. Together, these effects weaken the regenerative capacity of hepatocytes [ ]. In mouse non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, RPA1 opens chromatin structures by recruiting chromatin remodeling factors. This increases the accessibility of lipid metabolism genes to promote hepatic lipid accumulation [ ]. In human Werner syndrome, the loss of the WRN gene triggers the abnormal upregulation of SMARCA5. This drives excessive chromatin opening in the liver to aggravate lipid deposition and metabolic dysfunction [ ].

## Neurodegenerative diseases
In human and mouse models of ALS/FTD, elevated expression of Su(var)3–9 methyltransferases increases H3K9me3 levels. This lead to chromatin compaction and the inhibition of TDP-43 expression to impair neuronal function [ ]. In AD, genomic regions harboring transposable elements (TEs) exhibit hypomethylation and increased chromatin accessibility. These changes lead to the upregulated expression of neurodegeneration-related markers to accelerate disease progression [ ].

## Pulmonary aging and fibrotic lesions
Alterations in chromatin structure represent a major manifestation in the pathogenesis of lung aging and fibrosis. Aging of the pulmonary vasculature in humans and mice is accompanied by a decline in the function of the transcription factor ERG. This leads to reduced chromatin accessibility and results in impaired endothelial repair capacity, exacerbated fibrosis, and deteriorated lung function [ ]. In a mouse model of bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis, widespread A/B compartment switching occurs. This process promotes the expression of SASP and pro-fibrotic genes to collectively drive disease progression [ ].
In summary, chromatin accessibility serves as a central regulatory hub for tissue aging and age-related diseases. Aging disrupts the balance between chromatin “opening” and “closing.” On a systemic level, this imbalance tends toward a pathological state that promotes degeneration, fibrosis, and functional loss. Precise intervention in the “on” and “off” states of chromatin represents a potential target for improving age-related diseases and achieving healthy aging. Notably, these findings primarily stems from disease model systems such as mouse and progeria models. The practical application in the complex and heterogeneous process of natural human aging remains speculative.

## Targeted chromatin remodeling intervention for aging
Intervention strategies targeting chromatin remodeling have become a key approach for delaying aging as our understanding of aging mechanisms deepens. As a central regulator of chromatin remodeling, the SWI/SNF complex controls gene expression programs by adjusting nucleosome positioning and chromatin accessibility. Consequently, it has become an ideal target for delaying aging and related pathological states [ ]. The function of this complex depends on its ATPase subunit and several accessory subunits. These components collectively determine its stability, targeting specificity, and remodeling activity. The molecular chaperone MLF2 is essential for the assembly and stability of the SWI/SNF complex. In mouse embryonic stem cells, MLF2 promotes the binding of the complex to chromatin to increase chromatin accessibility and gene expression. This mechanism maintains chromatin homeostasis and exerts anti-aging effects [ ]. Subunit composition also determines the functional specificity of SWI/SNF. For instance, Arid1a binds with transcription factors such as Jun/Fos during osteoclastogenesis to target the Siglec15 promoter region. This interaction promotes chromatin opening and activates gene expression to facilitate cell differentiation [ ]. Additionally, transcription factors such as ZNF410 can specifically guide SWI/SNF to CHD4 enhancer regions to support chromatin accessibility and activate gene expression [ ].
Strategies to delay aging through targeting chromatin remodeling also include epigenetic drugs. Histone deacetylase inhibitors, such as Trichostatin A, promote the appearance of activation markers like H3K27ac (Refer to the “Histone acetylation” section). This action inhibits abnormal chromatin remodeling to maintain the regenerative capacity of human fibroblasts and delay cellular senescence [ ]. Based on a similar pharmacological pattern, inhibiting the chromatin remodeler CHD8 improves brain aging in mice by reducing chromatin accessibility and suppressing abnormal astrocyte proliferation and neuroinflammation [ ]. Specific knockout of MRG15L serves as a secondary epigenetic regulator by enhancing the binding of H4K16ac. This effect maintains high chromatin accessibility for cell cycle regulators like CDK1 to promote cell proliferation and anti-aging effects [ ]. Inflammatory memory driven by IL-6/STAT3/JunB signaling establishes persistent H3K27ac markers and chromatin openness in endothelial cells. This process amplifies secondary inflammatory responses and organ dysfunction after sepsis, although JunB inhibition may alleviate this chronic inflammatory state [ ]. In the liver, the upregulation of BAZ2B during steatohepatitis caused by aging or metabolic dysfunction reduces the chromatin accessibility of PPARα target genes. This impairment damages lipid metabolism and promotes aging. Conversely, hepatocyte-specific BAZ2B knockout restores PPARα signaling and reverses the pathological state [ ]. Aging increases nuclear membrane tension in hematopoietic stem cells to activate RhoA protein. This activation leads to the loss of H3K9me2 and chromatin decompression while promoting inflammatory signaling. Pharmacological inhibition of RhoA can reverse these epigenetic and transcriptional defects. Therefore, this establishes a viable anti-aging target within the nuclear mechanics-epigenetics axis [ ].
Natural products and lifestyle interventions can also improve aging-related conditions by targeting chromatin remodeling. For example, caloric restriction supports the expression of the long non-coding RNA Caren to inhibit the upregulation of HINT1 protein and maintain BAF complex activity. This mechanism restores mitochondrial biosynthesis and improves cardiac function and exercise capacity [ ]. Caloric restriction and resveratrol can also reverse the downregulation of MLF1 expression. This action inhibits the MLF1-EP300 axis to prevent H3K27ac-mediated chromatin opening and the transcription of aging-related genes [ ]. Melatonin promotes osteogenesis by upregulating the activity of the histone methyltransferase NSD2. This shift alters the balance between H3K36me2 and H3K27me3 modifications to increase chromatin accessibility. Consequently, it promotes the osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells to counteract age-related bone loss [ ]. N-acetylspermidine improves intestinal barrier integrity and inhibits inflammation through PIM1 kinase inhibition. This effect upregulates HDAC2 to reduce chromatin accessibility and enhance α1,2-fucosylation [ ]. Ascorbic acid restores heterochromatin stability and improves mesenchymal stem cell activity in progeroid mice [ ]. Taurine restores heterochromatin levels in aged mice to improve physiological function [ ]. Furthermore, nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors like abacavir and lamivudine can block retrotransposon activation and inflammatory responses caused by heterochromatin loss [ , ].
For ATP-dependent remodelers, several small molecules or PROTACs are currently in preclinical or clinical studies for cancer therapy. For example, the dual SMARCA2/4 oral inhibitor FHD-286 is being evaluated in Phase I trials for AML [ ]. Selective SMARCA2 degraders like YDR1 and YD54 demonstrate good oral bioavailability and can significantly inhibit KRAS-mutant tumors [ ]. The BRD9 molecular glue degrader ZZ7 achieves cell-specific degradation in synovial sarcoma by binding to the DCAF16-E3 ligase with minimal impact on cardiomyocytes [ ]. However, whether compounds targeting ATP-dependent remodelers can counteract aging requires further research. Off-target effects of SWI/SNF inhibitors cause acute side effects like myelosuppression and neurotoxicity [ ]. More concerning is that long-term high-dose use may induce secondary tumors. New degraders of family VIII BD in SMARCA4/SMARCA2 and PBRM1, such as AU-24,118 and AU-15,330, are being tested [ , ]. However, long-term treatment may lead to BD mutations and overexpression of ABCB1 to promote drug resistance [ ]. Therefore, we emphasize the need for transient rather than chronic exposure and the use of isoform-biased degraders instead of catalytic inhibitors.
Cell-type-specific delivery strategies, such as nanoparticles and AAV, may be effective for targeting chromatin remodeling to improve aging. For instance, intravenous injection of LNP-siLPCAT1 prioritizes enrichment in aging mammary glands to dissociate the BAF complex and restore p21 chromatin openness [ ]. Other examples include liver-targeting strategies using AAV-AM capsids to enrich H3K36me3 and improve rAAV-HR efficiency [ ]. Neuro-targeting strategies using AAV vectors to knock down MORC3 can restore H3K4me3 levels and improve memory defects in APP/PS1 mice [ ]. Future research must further evaluate the long-term safety and clinical translational potential of different intervention strategies. Exploring the possibility of combination applications will be essential to achieve more precise and safe anti-aging interventions.

## RNA modifications
RNA modification refers to a series of evolutionarily conserved chemical modifications on RNA molecules, with over 170 different types identified to date [ ]. Similar to histone modifications, RNA modifications are dynamically reversible and are regulated by a sophisticated “writer-eraser-reader” system. These modifications profoundly regulate the fate and function of RNA at multiple levels. These levels include stability, subcellular localization, splicing, translation efficiency, and the activity of non-coding RNAs (Fig. ). Through these mechanisms, RNA modifications participate in maintaining cellular homeostasis and responding to environmental changes. Research indicates that the dysregulation of various RNA modifications, such as m6A and m5C, is closely associated with the aging process and related diseases. As summarized in Table , these modifications play critical roles by influencing the mRNA stability, translation, and protein level control of key genes within various signaling pathways.
 

## The role of RNA modifications in cellular senescence and age-related disease

## m6A modification
As the most prevalent and abundant internal chemical modification in mammalian mRNA, m6A methylation constitutes a sophisticated post-transcriptional regulatory network through its dynamic and reversible mechanisms of “Writers” (e.g., the METTL3-METTL14-WTAP core complex), “Erasers” (e.g., FTO and ALKBH5), and “Readers” (e.g., YTHDF and IGF2BP families). This network extensively participates in various physiological and pathological processes by influencing RNA splicing, nuclear export, stability, translation, and degradation. Particularly in aging and age-related diseases, m6A modification acts as a highly context-dependent “double-edged sword” with profound and complex regulatory effects.
On one hand, m6A modification can accelerate cellular senescence by activating specific pro-aging pathways. This mechanism primarily involves the activation of pro-aging transcriptional programs. For example, the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) inhibits the expression of the demethylase FTO. This leads to elevated m6A levels in the 3′UTR of the proto-oncogene FOS mRNA, which is recognized by the IGF2BP family to enhance its stability and translation efficiency. Consequently, this process activates the AP-1 pathway to promote cellular senescence [ ]. Furthermore, m6A modification maintains the abnormal survival of senescent cells. METTL3-mediated m6A modification in the CDS region of PTCHD4 mRNA stabilizes the transcript via IGF2BP1 to activate Wnt signaling [ ]. Additionally, it triggers mitochondrial-telomere axis cascade damage. In mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), decreased ALKBH5 expression leads to m6A accumulation on CYP1B1 mRNA. This is recognized by IGF2BP1 to upregulate CYP1B1 expression, causing mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS bursts that directly induce senescence [ ].
On the other hand, m6A modification can construct multi-layered anti-aging barriers. Its primary pathways include the maintenance of genomic stability. METTL3-mediated m6A modification of MIS12 mRNA is recognized and stabilized by IGF2BP2 to maintain normal MIS12 protein expression. This is essential for ensuring correct chromosomal segregation and preventing genomic instability to eventually delay senescence in human MSCs [ ]. Another pathway involves promoting telomere repair. METTL3 catalyzes the m6A modification of the telomere-associated lncRNA TERRA. Modified TERRA is recognized by the nuclear reader protein YTHDC1 to promote homologous recombination (HR) repair at telomeres. This maintains telomere stability and delays the progression of cellular senescence [ ]. Furthermore, it regulates stem cell self-renewal. In hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), YTHDC1 synergistically regulates the maturation of key microRNAs, such as miR-125b and let-7e, in a METTL3-independent manner with HP1BP3 protein. This maintains the quiescence and self-renewal of HSCs to effectively resist aging [ ]. Notably, the same modification enzymes, such as METTL3, can be pro- or anti-aging depending on the cell type, stressor, and target transcript.
Additionally, the role of m6A modification in age-related diseases is equally bidirectional and highly dependent on target genes, cell types, and pathological microenvironments. Taking the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway as an example, METTL3-mediated m6A modification of lncRNA H19 enhances its stability via IGF2BP2 in atherosclerosis. This activates the NLRP3 inflammasome and promotes endothelial cell pyroptosis to accelerate plaque formation [ ]. Conversely, in diabetic cardiomyopathy, METTL14 promotes the YTHDF2-dependent degradation of TINCR lncRNA via m6A modification. This relieves the stabilizing effect of TINCR on NLRP3 mRNA to inhibit myocardial inflammation and pyroptosis [ ]. This reveals that the m6A-NLRP3 axis serves as a common regulatory node across cardiovascular and metabolic diseases. Its final output is determined by the specific combination of Writers (METTL3 vs. METTL14), Readers (IGF2BP2 vs. YTHDF2), and target RNAs (H19 vs. TINCR).
Similarly, the regulation of autophagy and metabolic homeostasis by m6A also shows opposing influences. In non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, METTL3 enhances the translation of the autophagy inhibitor Rubicon mRNA via YTHDF1. This blocks autophagosome-lysosome fusion and exacerbates hepatic lipid accumulation [ ]. In contrast, METTL14-mediated m6A modification stabilizes Wnt1 mRNA in myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury models. This activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway to inhibit cardiomyocyte apoptosis and exert protective effects [ ]. These findings demonstrate that m6A precisely switches metabolic and survival signals by selectively modifying key pathway nodes and recruiting functionally distinct Reader proteins. These changes are mainly observed in disease models.

## m7G modification
N7-methylguanosine (m7G) refers to a chemical modification that adds a methyl group to the seventh nitrogen atom of guanosine (G). This modification is widely present in various types of RNA. Its dynamic regulation is collaboratively completed by three key enzyme systems. The METTL1–WDR4 complex is responsible for modifications in tRNA and internal sites of mRNA. The RNMT–RAM complex specifically catalyzes the formation of the mRNA 5′-cap structure. Meanwhile, reader proteins such as eIF4E recognize m7G in the cap structure to initiate the translation process. This modification plays a critical role in maintaining cellular protein homeostasis by stabilizing tRNA spatial conformation, enhancing mRNA stability, and increasing translation efficiency. Notably, m7G modification exhibits complex bidirectional regulatory characteristics during the progression of aging and age-related diseases.
In replicative senescent IMR90 human lung fibroblasts, the protein levels of METTL1 and WDR4 decline synchronously. This reduction leads to a decrease in the occupancy of internal m7G in tRNA. Unmodified tRNAs are recognized and cleared by the rapid tRNA degradation (RTD) pathway. This results in the depletion of the tRNA pool and ribosome stalling at the A-site. Consequently, these events simultaneously activate the cytoplasmic surveillance pathway ZAKα–p38/JNK and the integrated stress response pathway GCN2–eIF2α. These pathways trigger global translation inhibition and induce the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). As SP1 expression decreases with age, the transcription of METTL1 and WDR4 is further suppressed. This process forms a positive feedback loop characterized by “m7G loss, translational imbalance, and accelerated senescence” [ ].
During the osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), the METTL1–WDR4 complex introduces m7G modification into the coding region (CDS) of Runx2 mRNA. This modification significantly enhances the translation elongation rate to increase Runx2 protein abundance. Such enhancement accelerates the lineage-specific differentiation of BMSCs into osteoblasts [ ]. Conversely, in myocardial ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury models, the expression of METTL1 is upregulated. This leads to internal m7G deposition on the mRNA of the deubiquitinase CYLD to enhance its translation efficiency. Elevated CYLD protein levels then inhibit K63-linked ubiquitination. This inhibition activates the p53-dependent apoptotic program to exacerbate cardiomyocyte death and cardiac dysfunction [ ].
In summary, m7G modification ensures smooth translation by globally maintaining tRNA homeostasis to delay the aging process. In specific pathological microenvironments, its site-specific enhancement of key regulatory factors (such as Runx2 and CYLD) can rapidly switch between pro-repair or pro-injury molecular functions. Therefore, m7G provides a precisely manipulable epitranscriptomic node for intervening in geriatric diseases.

## m¹A modification
m1A (N1-methyladenosine) is a highly conserved methylation modification occurring at the N1 position of adenosine. It is enriched in the 5’UTR of mRNA and conserved domains of tRNA, rRNA, and lncRNA. This modification is installed by methyltransferases such as TRMT6/TRMT61A or TRMT10C, removed by demethylases like ALKBH1/FTO, and recognized by YTH domain proteins such as YTHDF1/YTHDC1 to regulate RNA stability, translation, and degradation. Under physiological and pathological conditions, this modification exhibits typical bidirectional regulatory features. Its biological effects depend on the type of target RNA, the spatial position of modified sites, and the state of the cellular microenvironment. It can exert protective effects by enhancing the translation efficiency of key survival or stemness-related transcripts. Conversely, it can also accelerate cellular senescence and neurodegenerative damage through abnormal deposition that activates toxic protein aggregation or inhibits the synthesis of essential mitochondrial components.
At the level of protective regulation, the expression of methyltransferases TRMT6 and TRMT61A is upregulated following T-cell receptor activation. These enzymes catalyze m1A formation at specific tRNA sites to significantly increase the translation elongation rate of MYC mRNA. This process ensures sufficient MYC protein levels to drive resting T cells from G0 into G1/S phase for clonal expansion and the maintenance of immune homeostasis [ ]. In hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), tRNA m1A modification enhances the translation efficiency of TSC1 mRNA to maintain TSC1 protein levels. This maintenance subsequently inhibits mTORC1 activity to protect the long-term self-renewal and multi-lineage differentiation potential of HSCs [ ]. Further studies on vascular homeostasis models show that m1A modification of conserved adenosines within circCDK17 inhibits its degradation. This action blocks the abnormal proliferation of pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells to effectively delay the pathological progression of pulmonary hypertension [ ].
At the level of damaging regulation, decreased expression of ALKBH3 in CAG repeat expansion models leads to TRMT61A-mediated m1A accumulation. TDP-43 preferentially binds to m1A-containing CAG repeat RNAs through its RNA recognition domain. This interaction triggers nuclear-to-cytoplasmic translocation and the formation of irreversible gel-like aggregates in the cytoplasm to induce a neurotoxic cascade [ ]. TRMT10C introduces m1A into mitochondrially encoded ND5 mRNA to directly inhibit its translation. This reduction in ND5 levels lowers complex I activity and triggers mitochondrial respiratory chain dysfunction to accelerate Alzheimer’s disease-related pathological phenotypes [ ].
Furthermore, the TRMT6/TRMT61A complex can participate in bidirectional regulation through non-catalytic mechanisms. It acts as a scaffold to facilitate angiogenin-mediated tRNA cleavage for the generation of 3’-tiRNA-Leu-CAG. This fragment upregulates key factors in apoptotic pathways to induce age-dependent apoptosis in HSCs. This suggests that the m1A enzyme complex itself possesses a switch function that determines cell survival or apoptosis [ ].
In summary, m1A modification functions through a three-dimensional regulatory network encompassing “site specificity, cell type, and pathological context.” While maintaining immune responses and protecting stem cell stemness and vascular homeostasis, it can also amplify damage in neurodegeneration and mitochondrial dysfunction. Therefore, it constitutes a typical bidirectional regulatory paradigm of the epitranscriptome.

## m5C modification
5-methylcytidine (m5C) is formed by transferring an active methyl group from methyl donors such as SAM to the fifth carbon atom of the cytosine ring. Its regulatory network is collaboratively completed by three classes of proteins. The NSUN family and DNMT2 are responsible for catalyzing deposition. The TET family and ALKBH1 perform reversible removal. Meanwhile, ALYREF, FMRP, and YBX1/2 recognize the modification and mediate downstream functions. m5C participates extensively in physiological and pathological processes such as embryonic development, tumorigenesis, and stem cell differentiation. It exhibits bidirectional regulatory effects in hematopoiesis, bone metabolism, and myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury.
In the hematopoietic system, m5C is indispensable for maintaining the functions of stem and progenitor cells. NSUN2 catalyzes m5C formation on a series of cell cycle-related mRNAs. These transcripts are subsequently recognized and bound by the molecular chaperone YBX1. This interaction significantly enhances transcript stability and inhibits degradation to safeguard hematopoietic homeostasis and regenerative potential [ ]. Regarding bone metabolism, m5C participates in the pathogenesis of osteoporosis by regulating cell death pathways. In osteoblasts, YBX1 recognizes m5C-modified ATF4 mRNA and upregulates its expression. ATF4 further induces FSP1 expression to inhibit ferroptosis. This process maintains osteogenic function and counteracts osteoporosis [ ].
Conversely, in mouse models of myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury, the interaction between piRNA and DNMT1 reduces the m5C levels of Atf7 mRNA. this reduction weakens the methylation-dependent stabilization effect and leads to increased ATF7 expression. Elevated ATF7 inhibits CHMP2A, which is a negative regulator of necroptosis. This inhibition subsequently promotes cardiomyocyte necroptosis and exacerbates tissue damage [ ]. In summary, m5C modification functions through site-specific and cellular environment-dependent manners. While protecting hematopoietic and osteogenic functions, it can also transform into a damage-amplifying signal during myocardial injury. This phenomenon reflects typical bidirectional regulatory characteristics of the epitranscriptome.

## ac4C modification
N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) is an acetylation modification occurring at the fourth nitrogen atom of the cytidine base in RNA molecules. This modification was first discovered in yeast tRNA through enzymatic digestion and mass spectrometry in 1966 [ ]. It possesses high evolutionary conservation and endogenicity in mammals and other eukaryotes and is widely present in tRNA, rRNA, and mRNA. To date, NAT10 and its homologs are the only enzymes confirmed to catalyze the formation of ac4C in RNA [ ]. Recent studies have further identified the eukaryotic translation elongation factor eEF2 as a “reader” for ac4C [ ]. ac4C plays multiple roles in RNA metabolism and functional regulation. When located in the mRNA coding region, it enhances translation elongation efficiency by promoting tRNA interaction. Conversely, when located in the 5’UTR, it may inhibit translation initiation [ ].
NAT10-mediated RNA ac4C is a key driver in various cellular senescence models. In senescent ovarian granulosa cells, the upregulation of NAT10 increases ac4C modification and the mRNA expression of RIPK3. This increase inhibits the Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant pathway and leads to oxidative damage [ ]. During doxorubicin-induced podocyte senescence, upregulated NAT10 promotes its interaction with TLR2. This interaction subsequently activates the P53-P21 pathway to induce DNA damage and cell cycle arrest [ ]. In HGPS mouse models, pharmacological inhibition or knockout of NAT10 significantly improves pathological features and extends healthy lifespan [ ]. These findings reveal that NAT10-mediated RNA ac4C can serve as a potential target for improving aging.
NAT10 plays a dual role in cardiac pathology, and its function is highly dependent on the specific context. In myocardial ischemia/reperfusion models, NAT10-mediated ac4C increases on Mybbp1a mRNA and promotes the stable expression of its transcript. This process subsequently inhibits SLC7A11 expression and leads to ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes [ ]. In heart failure models, decreased NAT10 activity leads to a reduction of ac4C on the histone methyltransferase Kmt5a mRNA and inhibits its expression. This reduction relieves the inhibition of the P53 signaling pathway and triggers cardiomyocyte apoptosis [ ].

## Application of RNA Modifications in Translational Medicine
Although we have gained a preliminary understanding of RNA modification changes during aging, critical challenges remain. The core goal of translational medicine is to transform these basic biological findings into effective means for delaying aging or treating age-related diseases. This involves utilizing the reversibility and targetability of RNA modifications to develop novel diagnostic tools and therapeutic drugs.

## As biomarkers for disease diagnosis and prognosis
Changes in m6A modification levels or the abnormal expression of its associated enzymes have been proven to be biomarkers for various age-related diseases. In synovial tissues, the m6A level of hsa_circ_0007259 is significantly elevated. This molecule binds to miR-21-5p via a ceRNA mechanism and activates the STAT3 pathway to participate in inflammation and immune regulation. Therefore, it can serve as a specific marker for rheumatoid arthritis [ ]. Additionally, the expression of the m6A reader protein YTHDF2 is significantly downregulated. This downregulation is closely related to macrophage polarization imbalance and immune microenvironment disorders. Clinical sample validation has confirmed that it can serve as a reliable diagnostic marker for OA synovitis [ ]. Co-expression network analysis further reveals that genes co-expressed with m6A regulators are significantly enriched in metabolic pathways and the MAPK/EGFR signaling axis. Most of these genes have been confirmed to participate in the pathogenesis of diabetes. This suggests that m6A modifications and their eQTL-SNPs can be used for T2DM risk prediction and early diagnosis [ ].

## As therapeutic targets for diseases
Natural products and traditional Chinese medicine formulas have been shown to improve age-related diseases by regulating RNA modifications. For example, Okra flavonoids inhibit the mettl3-mediated m6A modification of PTEN mRNA to suppress the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. This suppression significantly attenuates podocyte pyroptosis induced by high glucose [ ]. Mi Jiao Pian activates the acetyltransferase NAT10 to install ac4C modification on Runx2 mRNA. This installation enhances its translation to promote bone formation and resist osteoporosis [ ]. Linggui Zhugan Decoction reduces the m6A level of SOCS2 mRNA to weaken its negative regulation of the JAK-STAT pathway and alleviate hepatic steatosis [ ]. Furthermore, Compound Zhenzhu Tiaozhi (FTZ) inhibits the YTHDF2-mediated m6A modification of SIRT3 mRNA to improve diabetes-accelerated atherosclerosis [ ].
Small molecules and peptides have also made significant progress in improving age-related diseases by regulating RNA modifications. The GLP-1 receptor agonist exenatide restores m6A methylation levels via METTL3 to effectively improve H2O2-induced apoptosis in mouse pancreatic beta cells [ ]. Cycloleucine specifically inhibits the m6A modification of cGAS mRNA to reduce cGAS-STING inflammatory pathway activity and alleviate neuroinflammation [ ]. Melatonin upregulates the m6A methyltransferase component KIAA1429 to enhance the m6A modification of key genes in the PI3K/AKT pathway for protecting spermatogonia against sperm aging [ ]. Specific inhibitors targeting m6A regulators, such as the METTL3 inhibitor STC-15, have demonstrated good efficacy in anti-AML therapy and have entered clinical trials [ , ]. PROTAC-targeted FTO degraders like QP73, as well as FTO inhibitors like FB23 and its derivatives, have also shown effectiveness in the anti-tumor field during preclinical studies [ , ]. Recent research found that FB23 can improve obesity-induced cognitive dysfunction. This suggests that the application potential of FTO inhibitors has expanded from oncology to neurodegenerative diseases [ ]. Remodelin was the first reported NAT10 inhibitor [ ]. Preclinical studies have shown its protective effects on various age-related diseases. These effects include reducing cerebral infarction injury, promoting neurological recovery, and alleviating liver fibrosis by reducing the senescence and abnormal activation of hepatic stellate cells [ , ]. It also blocks osteoclast maturation and relieves osteoporosis [ ].
Overall, research on FTO inhibitors and NAT10 inhibitors remains primarily in the preclinical stage. A significant gap still exists between these studies and clinical translation. The clinical indications for METTL3 inhibitors currently under study are also predominantly focused on tumors. Future research will expand toward geriatric diseases with high incidence, such as neurodegenerative diseases, metabolic syndrome, and osteoarticular degenerative diseases. Simultaneously, researchers will explore the potential of RNA modification regulation in delaying overall aging and extending healthy lifespan.

## Translational safety considerations
Core modification enzymes such as METTL3, FTO, and NAT10 exhibit pleiotropic functions in development, metabolism, and oncology. Systemic regulation of these enzymes may interfere with normal tissue homeostasis [ , ]. Since these enzymes function across the entire transcriptome, their perturbation will cause global rather than site-specific changes in gene expression. Therefore, it is urgent to develop precise and context-dependent therapeutic strategies.

## Cross-talk between histone modification, chromatin remodeling and RNA modification
Epigenetic modifications at different levels form a tightly interconnected network through metabolites and various factors. These components collectively constitute the dynamic regulatory system of the “epitranscriptome-epigenome.” During the aging process, a bidirectional dynamic interaction network forms between RNA modifications, histone modifications, and chromatin remodeling (Fig. ). This network serves as one of the core mechanisms driving cellular homeostatic imbalance and functional decline [ , ].
 

## Regulation of histone modification and chromatin remodeling by RNA modification
RNA modifications can influence chromatin states by directly recruiting modification enzymes. METTL14 establishes H3K4me3/H3K27me3 bivalent chromatin domains in the promoter regions of target genes by recruiting PRC2 and KDM6B. The dynamic plasticity of these domains determines cell fate [ ]. Through its m6A catalytic activity, METTL3 promotes the establishment of heterochromatin markers such as H3K9me3 and H4K20me3 at endogenous retrovirus (IAP) sites to inhibit transposon activity [ ]. The m5C modification on chromatin-associated RNA (caRNA) is recognized by MBD6. This recognition recruits the PR-DUB complex to remove the inhibitory marker H2AK119ub and promote chromatin openness [ ]. Some RNA modification enzymes themselves also function as chromatin regulators. For example, METTL14 can directly bind to H3K27me3 regions and recruit the demethylase KDM6B to maintain the dynamic balance of epigenetic modifications [ ].
In addition to direct effects, RNA modifications can indirectly remodel histone modifications by regulating the expression of epigenetic enzymes. NAT10-mediated ac4C modification maintains the integrity of H3K9me3-marked heterochromatin by enhancing the mRNA stability of heterochromatin factors. This maintenance effectively suppresses aging-related heterochromatin erosion [ ]. Conversely, the ac4C modification of ANP32B mRNA triggers p53-dependent cellular senescence by promoting its translation to remodel the global H3K4me3/H3K27me3 landscape [ ].
In models of age-related diseases, RNA modifications often drive histone modifications by affecting metabolic genes and altering metabolic substrate concentrations. The m6A reader protein IGF2BP2 increases glucose metabolism and lactate production by stabilizing the mRNA of the key glycolytic gene ALDOA. This increase drives H3K18 lactylation to activate hepatic stellate cells and promote the occurrence of liver fibrosis [ ]. METTL3-mediated m6A modification of ACSS3 mRNA promotes the production of propionyl-CoA. This production induces H3K14/K23 propionylation and activates lipid synthesis to promote the formation of fatty liver [ ].

## Feedback regulation of RNA modification by histone modification and chromatin state
Histone modifications influence the distribution of RNA modifications by recruiting RNA modification enzymes. The transcriptional elongation marker H3K36me3 is directly recognized by METTL14. This recognition guides the m6A methyltransferase complex to perform co-transcriptional modification on nascent mRNA [ ]. Histone modifications can also directly regulate the transcription of RNA modification enzymes. Increased KMT2A-mediated H3K4me3 raises METTL3 expression. This increase subsequently reduces ATG4a stability through m6A to inhibit autophagy and accelerate aging [ ]. H3K18 lactylation can transcriptionally activate METTL3. This activation enhances m6A accumulation on ACSL4 mRNA to promote alveolar epithelial cell ferroptosis and accelerate lung injury [ ]. Additionally, H3K18 lactylation can transcriptionally activate the m5C methyltransferase NSUN2 to cause endothelial cell dysfunction [ ]. Higher-order chromatin structures can similarly affect RNA modification levels. The loss of linker histone H1 leads to loose chromatin. This relaxation causes a decrease in m6A modification levels of chromatin-associated lncRNAs and results in R-loop accumulation and genomic instability [ ].
In conclusion, these studies outline a dynamic and bidirectional regulatory network involving RNA modification, histone modification, and chromatin remodeling. On one hand, RNA modification regulates gene expression or remodels chromatin states by directly recruiting enzymes, regulating epigenetic enzyme expression, or altering histone modification substrate levels. On the other hand, histone modifications and chromatin structures influence the overall distribution and function of RNA modifications by recruiting or regulating the expression of RNA modification enzymes. Notably, modifications on non-coding RNAs such as lncRNAs can directly affect chromatin accessibility and transcriptional activity by changing their interaction with chromatin or their stability [ ]. While these mechanisms have been verified in fields such as cancer, such interaction mechanisms between RNA modification and chromatin in aging research still require comprehensive elucidation.

## Conclusion
Aging is a complex biological process driven by multiple molecular mechanisms, among which epigenetic regulation occupies a central position. This review systematically organizes the roles of three highly dynamic and interconnected epigenetic levels—histone modification, chromatin remodeling, and RNA modification—in aging and related diseases. Research indicates that these levels do not operate in isolation. Instead, they form a sophisticated bidirectional regulatory network through metabolites and enzyme systems to collaboratively determine chromatin states, gene expression programs, and cell fate. Together, they constitute the “epigenome-epitranscriptome” regulatory axis. The imbalance of this axis serves as a critical driver for the onset and progression of aging and associated pathologies. Currently, intervention strategies targeting these epigenetic mechanisms, such as small-molecule agonists/inhibitors (Table  ), natural products, epigenetic editing, and reprogramming, have shown potential in preclinical models to delay aging and improve disease phenotypes. However, their clinical translation still faces challenges including insufficient targeting specificity, potential carcinogenic risks, difficulty in determining intervention timing, and unclear long-term safety. Furthermore, it must be noted that the current body of evidence relies heavily on rodent and in vitro cell models, leaving significant gaps in direct human data and tissue-specific research. These limitations necessitate a cautious approach when interpreting existing findings and advancing their translation into clinical applications.
 Looking forward, efforts should focus on developing precision tools such as CRISPR/dCas9-based epigenetic editing systems, PROTAC degraders, and cell-type-specific delivery systems like AAV or nanoparticles. These tools aim to achieve targeted regulation of specific genes or chromatin regions while minimizing off-target effects. Simultaneously, it is essential to systematically evaluate the long-term safety of epigenetic interventions. Special attention should be given to their potential impact on genomic stability, tumor risk, and normal physiological functions. Furthermore, researchers should actively explore reversible and controllable epigenetic resetting strategies, such as “transient intervention” or “periodic regulation.”
