## Background
Dysfunctional mitochondria increase oxidative stress and inflammation, driving atherosclerosis. Understanding gene expression and regulatory mechanisms is crucial.

## Objective
This study aims to identify key mitochondrial dysfunction-related genes (MitoDEGs) associated with atherosclerotic plaque severity, elucidate their molecular mechanisms and immune-regulatory roles in disease progression, and validate pivotal biomarkers to provide mechanistic insights for personalized therapeutic strategies.

## Methods
We utilized eight atherosclerotic plaque datasets (human samples) from GEO and mitochondrial gene data from MitoCarta3.0. Lasso Regression and the Shap algorithm were employed to identify key differentially expressed mitochondrial genes (MitoDEGs) for model construction. Enriched pathways were analyzed using GO and KEGG databases, and protein–protein interactions were explored with STRING and Cytoscape. Experimental validation was conducted using atherosclerosis mouse models and HUVEC cell models.

## Results
This study identified distinct and shared mitochondrial dysfunction-related genes (MitoDEGs) in carotid and peripheral atherosclerotic plaques. Key carotid-specific MitoDEGs included HK3 and BID, while peripheral-specific ones included RAC2 and TCL1A. Two common MitoDEGs, GZMB and PMAIP1, were found in both plaque types. Enrichment analyses revealed novel associations with mitochondrial pathways including apoptosis, p53 signaling, hexose metabolism, and protein serine/threonine kinase regulation. Importantly, these MitoDEGs are mechanistically linked to mitochondrial outer membrane integrity and metabolic reprogramming. Experimental validation confirmed the upregulation of core MitoDEGs (CASP1, BID, PMAIP1, and GZMB), highlighting their critical roles in mitochondrial dysfunction during atherosclerosis progression.

## Conclusion
This study underscores the critical role of mitochondrial dysfunction, mediated by specific MitoDEGs, in atherosclerosis progression. The identified genes modulate both mitochondrial apoptotic pathways and immune cell infiltration, contributing to plaque severity. These shared and location-specific MitoDEGs offer novel mechanistic insights and represent potential therapeutic targets for intervening in plaque development and achieving personalized management of atherosclerotic disease.

## Introduction
Atherosclerosis is a fundamental pathological process that primarily contributes to cardiovascular diseases. Cardiovascular diseases have become a leading cause of death and disability globally [ ]. Atherosclerosis, as the core pathological basis of cardiovascular diseases, is closely related to mitochondrial dysfunction. In the early stages of lesion development, endothelial injury triggers the excessive production of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS), which oxidatively modifies low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL), promotes foam cell formation, and activates inflammasomes, exacerbating vascular wall inflammation and plaque instability [ ]. At the same time, mitophagy, as a key quality control mechanism, removes damaged mitochondria via the PINK1/Parkin pathway. Its dysfunction can lead to the accumulation of mitochondrial fragments and accelerate the expansion of the necrotic core in plaques [ , ]. Recent studies have further revealed that the dynamic interaction between mitochondria and the immune system further regulates the inflammatory response: mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) release can activate macrophages to polarize towards a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype through TLR9 signaling, promoting the secretion of IL-1β and TNF-α, thus forming a positive feedback inflammatory loop [ ], while mitochondrial metabolites (such as succinate) can enhance Th17 cell differentiation through the HIF-1α pathway, driving immune imbalance in atherosclerosis [ ]. These mechanisms collectively suggest the existence of a "mitochondria-inflammation axis," which may represent a potential target for intervention in atherosclerosis.
Plaques in different anatomical locations exhibit significant biological differences. The formation and stability of carotid artery plaques are heavily influenced by low shear stress and oscillatory shear stress, which favor the formation of unstable plaques with a thin fibrous cap and lipid accumulation. The rupture risk of these plaques is closely associated with the activation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) mediated by mitochondrial ROS [ , ]. Spatial single-cell transcriptomic analysis reveals that macrophage subsets in carotid atherosclerotic plaques highly express the mitochondrial DNA synthesis-activating gene VCAM-1, suggesting that abnormal mitochondrial DNA synthesis may drive the exacerbation of inflammation [ ]. In contrast, peripheral artery plaques (e.g., femoral artery plaques) are characterized by smooth muscle cell proliferation and calcification, and their progression is significantly associated with mitochondrial glycolytic metabolic reprogramming and downregulation of the fusion protein MFN2 [ ]. This spatial heterogeneity suggests that interventions targeting different plaque types need to be tailored to their specific mitochondrial pathways.
In recent years, machine learning has emerged as a powerful tool in the field of biomedical research. By applying machine learning algorithms, hidden patterns and correlations can be extracted from large-scale gene expression and clinical data [ ]. Constructing machine learning models can reveal the relationships between atherosclerosis and specific genes, providing personalized treatment recommendations in the form of predictive models [ ]. In particular, interpretable machine learning (IML) models offer explanations and understanding of the model's decision-making process. Compared to traditional black-box machine learning algorithms like deep neural networks, interpretable machine learning models can explain their decision rationale and reveal the contributions of input features to the output, providing scientific justifications for decision-making and aiding in the understanding of model-generated results [ ]. By identifying important features, we can gain a better understanding of the biological mechanisms underlying the disease and develop more accurate predictive models. IML is transforming the research paradigm of atherosclerosis by integrating multi-omics and clinical data. For example, the XGBoost model based on the SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) framework has been used to identify key features of cardiovascular diseases [ ]. Héctor et al. applied IML techniques by combining proteomics data with traditional risk factors, thereby improving the accuracy of cardiovascular disease risk prediction and identifying new biomarkers [ ].

Carotid Plaque: Refers to advanced plaque tissues from datasets GSE43292, GSE24495, GSE28829, GSE21545, and GSE163154. These plaques typically correspond to lipid-rich lesions classified as stage IV or above according to the Stary classification (e.g., [ ]).
Atherosclerotic Carotid Artery: Refers to carotid artery tissues from dataset GSE100927, which are derived from vessels affected by atherosclerosis, encompassing a range of lesions from early to advanced stages.
Peripheral Plaque: Refers to advanced plaque tissues from peripheral arteries (e.g., femoral artery) in datasets GSE24702 and GSE53274.
Atherosclerotic Peripheral Artery: Refers to tissues from peripheral arteries (e.g., femoral artery, popliteal artery) derived from dataset GSE100927, collected from vessels affected by atherosclerosis.

In this study, samples from the above groups were further subdivided based on the degree of immune infiltration (see " " section) to compare gene expression differences across various disease severities. We employed Python running on the Visual Studio Code editor, utilizing machine learning algorithms, for the purpose of mitigating batch effects, performing standardization, and handling missing values across diverse datasets from different platforms.
Missing value handling: Genes with missing values ≥ 20% were discarded. For genes with missing values less than 20%, the missing values were replaced with the mean expression value of that gene. The missing value imputation was performed using the SimpleImputer function from the sklearn impute module in Python.
The gene expression data were normalized before batch correction. Specifically, we used DESeq2's median of ratios method to normalize the raw counts intensities, thereby eliminating technical variations. Subsequently, we performed batch correction based on the Combat algorithm (from the sklearn) [ ]. To assess the effectiveness of normalization in removing batch effects, we employed Principal Component Analysis (PCA) using the decomposition module from the sklearn package in Python [ ]. This allowed us to evaluate whether the normalization of the data successfully achieved the desired goal of eliminating batch effects.

## Single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) and sample clustering
Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was performed using R software to calculate the enrichment scores and functions of 28 immune-related cell types in each sample [ – ]. Unsupervised machine learning algorithms (Hierarchical Clustering) were employed to cluster the samples into two groups. Based on the degree of immune cell infiltration, the samples included in this study were further divided into immunity-high group and immunity-low group. The Seaborn, Matplotlib, and Pandas packages were installed for data processing and visualization. Heatmaps and cluster maps, incorporating cluster analysis, were generated using the aforementioned packages. Parameters were adjusted to create heatmaps with different color palettes and clustering methods.

## Differential Gene Analysis (DGA)
Using the stats function from the scipy module, we obtained the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the immunity-high group and the immunity-low group. The DEGs were defined based on a fold change (FC) threshold, where genes with a fold change less than 0.8 were considered downregulated, and genes with a fold change greater than 1.2 were considered upregulated. The biological rationale for selecting fold-change thresholds of 1.2 for upregulation and 0.8 for downregulation lies in the balance between sensitivity and specificity. These thresholds ensure that the selected genes exhibit expression changes of sufficient biological significance, while minimizing the risk of including genes with insignificant fluctuations that may not reflect true biological effects. By using these thresholds, we can effectively identify genes that are genuinely involved in the relevant biological processes or disease mechanisms.
The statistical differences in gene expression between two groups of samples were compared using an independent samples t-test. For data with unequal variances, Welch's t-test was applied to correct for the differences, enhancing the robustness of the test. To control the risk of false positives caused by multiple hypothesis testing in high-throughput data, the original p-values were adjusted using False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction. The Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method was specifically employed for this adjustment. In this study, a significance threshold was set at FDR < 0.05 and the adjusted p-value < 0.05. Significant differentially expressed genes were filtered based on FDR < 0.05 and |log2FC|> 0.58. The results were visualized using a volcano plot created with the matplotlib.pyplot library in Python.

## Differentially Expressed Genes Related to Mitochondria (MitoDEGs)
To obtain differentially expressed genes related to mitochondria, we retrieved mitochondrial-related genes from the MitoCarta3.0 database. We selected the human species and downloaded the Human.MitoCarta3.0.xls dataset, which contains mitochondrial-related genes specific to humans. Using the intersect1d function from the NumPy package, we performed an intersection of the DEGs obtained from the four groups: carotid atherosclerotic plaque group, atherosclerotic carotid artery group, peripheral atherosclerotic plaque group, and atherosclerotic peripheral artery group. This intersection provides us with a set of DEGs that are specifically associated with mitochondria, referred to as MitoDEGs.

## Selection of Key Mitochondria-Related Differentially Expressed Genes (Key MitoDEGs)
To identify key genes most strongly associated with plaque immune infiltration from the preliminary MitoDEGs, we employed a workflow that combines two machine learning algorithms. First, we applied LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) regression for feature dimensionality reduction on the MitoDEGs from each sample group (e.g., the carotid plaque group). Using tenfold cross-validation, we selected the penalty coefficient (λ) that minimized classification error and retained genes with non-zero coefficients.
Next, the genes selected by LASSO were used as features to construct an XGBoost classification model (immune high vs. immune low). We then calculated the average SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) values for each gene in the XGBoost model using the BorutaShap module to quantify their importance in the model’s predictions. Finally, based on the SHAP value rankings, we selected the top-ranking genes (e.g., the top 12 for the carotid plaque group, the top 10 for the peripheral arterial plaque group) as the "key MitoDEGs" for further analysis. (Please refer to Figure “Graphic abstract”).
The Shap algorithm calculates the SHAP value to measure the impact of each feature (corresponding gene) on the dependent variable. It treats each feature as a contributor and calculates its contribution value, which is then summed to obtain the final model prediction [ , ]. This method ensures maintaining the integrity of the feature genes. Shap reflects the influence of each feature in every sample and its impact on the positive or negative direction of the final prediction. In this study, we utilized the Shap function from the BorutaShap module and merged it with the XGBoost model. This enabled us to further identify the key MitoDEGs, which were visualized in gene plots.

## Construction of prediction models
Using the key MitoDEGs we built prediction models to estimate the extent of the atherosclerosis severity. The following models were employed:LogisticRegression model from the sklearn.linear_model module.
XGBClassifier model from the xgboost module.

If there was a significant difference in sample sizes between the compared two groups (immunity-high group and immunity-low group), the SMOTE function from the imblearn.over_sampling module was utilized to balance the sample sizes between groups. The training and testing sets were split in a 3:7 ratio.
GridSearchCV model from the sklearn.model_selection module was used for hyperparameter tuning through a grid search. The model with the highest accuracy scores was selected. ROC curves were constructed, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated.
The prediction models were evaluated using the classification report function from the sklearn.metrics module, providing precision, recall, F1 score, and support for each model. Confusion matrix plots were generated using the ConfusionMatrixDisplay function to assess the precision of the constructed models.
Components of the prediction models include data preprocessing, feature selection, model training, parameter tuning, evaluation, and visualization.

## Intersection gene acquisition
The intersection of key MitoDEG sets between carotid arterial atherosclerotic plaques and peripheral arterial atherosclerotic plaques was obtained. A Venn diagram was generated using the matplotlib_venn library in Python.

## Exploration of enriched pathways for key MitoDEGs
To explore the enriched pathways for the key MitoDEGs, we used Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases. The identified key MitoDEGs were divided into two main groups: the carotid-derived group and the peripheral-derived group. The carotid-derived group consisted of the key MitoDEGs in carotid atherosclerotic plaques and atherosclerotic carotid arteries. The peripheral-derived group consisted of the key MitoDEGs in peripheral arterial plaques and atherosclerotic peripheral arteries.
We took the union of the two sub-groups within the carotid-derived group to obtain a set of key MitoDEGs specific to carotid sources. Similarly, we took the union of the two sub-groups within the peripheral-derived group to obtain a set of key MitoDEGs specific to peripheral sources.
Subsequently, we imported the two sets of the key MitoDEGs into the David database [ ]. After setting the species to Homo sapiens (human) and selecting Gene Ontology and KEGG-Pathways for functional analysis, we exported the results, which provided us with enriched pathways for the key MitoDEGs.

## Construction of PPI network and selection of key biomarkers
The selected key MitoDEGs from the previous step were investigated using the String database (https://www.string-db.org). We utilized the "Multiple proteins" option to perform the analysis of protein–protein interactions (PPI) and uploaded the sets of key MitoDEGs. The species was set to Homo sapiens to ensure human-specific interactions and the score was set to medium (0.4).
By executing this analysis, we constructed a protein–protein interaction network (PPI network) that represents the interactions among the key MitoDEGs. To identify key proteins within the PPI network, we used the MCODE plugin within Cytoscape. This allowed us to extract and rank the key proteins from the carotid-derived and peripheral-derived groups of the key MitoDEGs.

## In vivo and in vitro validations
To validate the key genes identified through bioinformatic analysis, we employed widely recognized in vivo and in vitro models of atherosclerosis. The ApoE⁻/⁻ mouse model fed a high-fat diet serves as a classic in vivo system for studying atherogenesis, recapitulating several critical features of human disease, including lipid accumulation, inflammatory cell infiltration, and plaque formation [ , ]. For in vitro validation, we utilized the angiotensin II (Ang II)-treated human umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVEC) model. Ang II, a key effector of the renin-angiotensin system, has been extensively documented to induce endothelial oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and apoptosis, thereby simulating early-stage endothelial dysfunction in atherosclerosis [ , ]. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0, and GraphPad Prism 9.0 was used for data visualization. The data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). To evaluate differences between groups, unpaired t-test was performed. In cases where variances were unequal, Welch's test was utilized, followed by Dunnett's T3 test for post-hoc pairwise comparisons. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Antibody information were list as follows: Caspase-1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-56036, dilution 1:200), GZMB (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-8022, dilution 1:200), BID (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-56025, dilution 1:200), PMAIP1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-56169, dilution 1:200), and GAPDH (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-59540, dilution 1:500).

## In vivo validation
Ten SPF-grade C57 mice were obtained from Liaoning Chansheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (License NO.: SCXK (Liao) 2020–001), with a weight of (20 ± 2) g. Another ten SPF-grade ApoE-/- mice were obtained from Henan Skebess Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (License NO.: SCXK (Yu) 2020–0005), with a weight of (20 ± 2) g. The mice were housed at the Animal Experimental Center of the Affiliated Hospital of Liaoning University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (temperature: 20–24℃, humidity: 45–55%). Each cage contained 5 mice. The C57 mice served as the control group, while the ApoE-/- mice served as the atherosclerosis group. The mice underwent one week of adaptation feeding. The control group was fed with regular maintenance feed, while the atherosclerosis group was fed with atherosclerosis feed for 12 weeks. The atherosclerosis feed was purchased from Xiao Shu You Tai (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and contained 21% fat, 1.25% cholesterol, and 19.5% casein. This animal experiment was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Liaoning University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Approval NO.: 201,922), and the principles of the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement) for animal experiments were followed to ensure humane treatment. This study employs an exploratory experimental design to identify potential biological signals and trends. While an N = 3 sample size is commonly used in in vivo research. In this study, mouse aortic arch tissues were processed for histological analysis using both paraffin- and frozen-sectioning methods. For paraffin sections, tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, infiltrated and embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm, and baked at 66 °C. HE staining was performed with hematoxylin and eosin, and sections were mounted with neutral balsam for morphological observation (nuclei blue, cytoplasm and collagen pink). For frozen sections, tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (> 24 h), dehydrated in graded sucrose solutions, embedded in OCT, rapidly frozen, and sectioned at 8 μm. Oil Red O staining was carried out using freshly filtered working solution (8–10 min, protected from light), differentiated in 60% isopropanol, and counterstained with hematoxylin; sections were mounted with glycerin gelatin to visualize lipid deposition (lipid droplets orange‑red to bright red, nuclei blue). Quantitative analysis was performed with ImageJ software: HE‑stained sections were used to measure aortic wall thickness and luminal area, while Oil Red O‑stained sections were thresholded to determine the percentage of positive area indicative of lipid deposition. At least three non‑overlapping fields per section were randomly selected, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

## In vitro validation
The human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were obtained from Shanghai Zhongqiao Xinzhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (DFSC-EC-01). HUVECs were rapidly thawed in a 37℃ water bath, transferred in a sterile manner to a cell culture flask, and cultured in ECM medium containing 5% FBS at 37℃ under 5% CO2. The cells were observed for growth status, with the medium being changed every 3 days. When the cells reached subconfluency, they were passaged using trypsin containing EDTA. Cells from the 3rd to 5th passages, exhibiting good growth, were selected for the experiments. The cells were allowed to reach 80% confluency on the culture dish before initiating the experimental procedures. To simulate in vitro atherosclerosis conditions, we established a cell model by inducing HUVEC damage with Ang Ⅱ, a method previously validated in our research group [ ]. The experiments consisted of two groups: 1) Control group and 2) AngⅡ group: The cells were treated with a complete medium containing 700 nmol/L AngⅡ for 24 h. After 24 h, cells from both control and model groups were collected and centrifuged to prepare protein samples. Western blot analysis was performed to detect expression differences of Caspase-1, GZMB, BID, and PMAIP1 across groups, with data analyzed using unpaired t-tests. For in vitro validation, we performed three biological replicates.

## Results

## Data download and processing
The procedure of this whole work was shown in the analysis flow chart (Supplementary Fig. 1). The downloaded datasets include carotid atherosclerotic plaques (N = 343), peripheral atherosclerotic plaques (N = 299), atherosclerotic carotid arteries (N = 29), and atherosclerotic peripheral arteries (N = 40). As the carotid and peripheral arterial samples were derived from a single dataset rather than multiple datasets, there was no batch effect between the two arterial datasets. The PCA results indicated successful removal of the batch effect for carotid atherosclerotic plaque datasets and peripheral atherosclerotic plaque datasets. The sample grouping and relevant information are presented in Table  , while the PCA plots are shown in Fig.  A, B, C and D. The corrected PCA scatter plot shows an increased overlap in sample distribution between batches, indicating a more uniform mixing of samples from different batches. Cluster analysis revealed that the silhouette coefficient decreased from 0.632 to 0.102 (for carotid atherosclerotic plaque datasets) and from 3.209 to 0.562 (for peripheral atherosclerotic plaque datasets), suggesting a reduction in batch-specific clustering phenomena. 
The substantial sample sizes across multiple datasets provided a solid foundation for robust statistical analysis. However, integrating data from different sources introduces technical variations (batch effects) that can confound true biological signals. Therefore, we performed rigorous batch effect correction to ensure that the observed differences in gene expression in subsequent analyses were attributable to biological factors related to atherosclerosis severity rather than technical artifacts.

## Single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) and sample clustering
Given the central role of chronic inflammation and immune cell involvement in the initiation and progression of atherosclerosis, we sought to characterize the immune microenvironment within our plaque and arterial samples. To achieve this, we employed ssGSEA to quantify the relative abundance of 28 immune cell types in each individual sample.
Nie et al. conducted GSEA analysis on GSE43292, GSE28829, and GSE100927 datasets, which revealed significant differences in the levels of immune cell infiltration and immune cell functionality between carotid atherosclerotic plaques and control samples, early and advanced carotid plaque samples, carotid atherosclerotic arteries and control samples, as well as bleeding and non-bleeding plaques. The development of atherosclerotic plaques was found to be closely associated with immune cell infiltration and their functions, as confirmed by PCA and tSNE analysis [ ]. Thus, in this study, the degree of immune cell infiltration was used as the basis for sample grouping, further categorizing atherosclerotic plaques and arteries into immunity-high and immunity-low groups.
Figure  A displayed the extent of immunocyte infiltration in carotid artery atherosclerotic plaques. The Carotid plaque group consists of five datasets: GSE43292, GSE24495 derived from carotid artery plaques, and GSE28829, GSE21545, GSE163154 derived from atheroma plaques. Atheroma plaque refers to arterial plaques classified as stage IV and above according to the Stary classification [ ]. The heatmap showed a generally high level of immunocyte infiltration in both carotid plaque (264/311 immunity high samples, 47/311 immunity low samples) and atheroma plaque, with higher infiltration observed in atheroma plaque (32/32 immunity high samples). 
Figure  B represented the degree of immunocyte infiltration in peripheral artery atherosclerotic plaques. The Peripheral plaque group included two datasets: GSE24702 from peripheral plaques and GSE53274 from human femoral artery atherosclerotic lesions. The heatmap revealed a higher level of immunocyte infiltration in peripheral artery plaques (153/294 immunity high samples, 141/294 immunity low samples) compared to human femoral artery atherosclerotic lesions (5/5 immunity low samples).
The atherosclerotic carotid artery group and the atherosclerotic peripheral artery group shared one dataset, GSE100927.
Figure  C displayed the immunocyte infiltration level in atherosclerotic carotid artery. Overall, the immunocyte infiltration level in atherosclerotic carotid artery was relatively low, with a few regions showing higher infiltration (8/29 immunity high samples, 21/29 immunity low samples).
Figure  D illustrated the immunocyte infiltration level in the atherosclerotic peripheral artery group, including atherosclerotic femoral artery samples and atherosclerotic infra-popliteal artery samples. The femoral artery samples generally exhibited higher immunocyte infiltration (18/26 immunity high samples, 8/26 immunity low samples), while the infra-popliteal artery samples displayed a lower extent of immunocyte infiltration (5/14 immunity high samples, 9/14 immunity low samples).
These results indicated that the more severe the arterial atherosclerosis, the greater the degree of immunocyte infiltration. Additionally, the degree of immunocyte infiltration is also influenced by the location of the plaques. Collectively, these findings corroborate the immune-centric nature of atherosclerosis and validate the use of immune infiltration degree as a practical and biologically relevant surrogate for stratifying disease severity in subsequent analyses.

## Differential Gene Analysis (DGA)
We identified 4012 DEGs in the carotid atherosclerotic plaque group, with 3167 upregulated and 845 downregulated genes (Fig.  A). In the peripheral atherosclerotic plaque group, 2921 DEGs were identified, including 2196 upregulated and 725 downregulated genes (Fig.  B). In the carotid atherosclerotic artery group, 12 DEGs were found, with all 12 genes being upregulated and none being downregulated (Fig.  C). Similarly, in the peripheral atherosclerotic artery group, 14 DEGs were identified, all of which were upregulated with no downregulated genes (Fig.  D). Detailed information on the differentially expressed genes can be found in Table  . 
This comprehensive differential expression analysis aimed to distill the vast transcriptomic data into a focused set of genes most strongly associated with the heightened inflammatory state that characterizes severe atherosclerosis. The thousands of DEGs identified underscore the profound transcriptional reprogramming that occurs in the diseased vasculature.

## Differentially Expressed Genes Related to Mitochondria (MitoDEGs)
Mitochondrial dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a key contributor to oxidative stress, inflammation, and cell death in atherosclerosis. To hone in on this specific pathological axis, we intersected our list of DEGs with a comprehensive database of human mitochondrial genes (MitoCarta3.0). This strategic filtering allowed us to identify a subset of DEGs with direct mitochondrial associations (MitoDEGs), thereby linking our transcriptional findings to a central hub of cellular metabolism and quality control.
We obtained four sets of MitoDEGs. Specifically, the carotid atherosclerotic plaque group had 232 MitoDEGs, with 187 upregulated and 45 downregulated genes. The peripheral atherosclerotic plaque group had 178 MitoDEGs, among which 159 were upregulated and 19 were downregulated. There was one upregulated MitoDEG of carotid atherosclerotic arteries, while peripheral atherosclerotic arteries also had one upregulated MitoDEG, please see Table  . 

## Selection of key MitoDEGs
While the MitoDEGs list was more focused, it still contained numerous genes. To further prioritize the most impactful and non-redundant features for model construction and biological interpretation, we employed a machine learning-based feature selection pipeline. This approach moves beyond mere statistical significance by identifying a compact set of genes that collectively possess strong predictive power for the high-immune phenotype.
By utilizing Lasso algorithm, we have identified 12 MitoDEGs in carotid atherosclerotic plaque group (PGR, BID, HK3, PMAIP1, SLC4A11, NDUFB3, MSR1, GK3P, PNPLA3, GZMB, ARRB2, CYP27B1) as shown in Fig.  A, and 10 MitoDEGs in peripheral atherosclerotic plaque group (CASP1, RAB32, ALAS2, FGR, PIM1, TCL1A, SNCA, PMAIP1, GZMB, RAC2) as shown in Fig.  B We have ranked these MitoDEGs based on their significance and created a graphical representation of their interdependencies. No further gene selection is required for atherosclerotic carotid artery and atherosclerotic peripheral artery as we only identified one key MitoDEG for each source. 

## Key MitoDEGs in carotid atherosclerotic plaque
In the ranking of MitoDEGs in carotid atherosclerotic plaque (Fig.  C, genes ranked in descending order of importance), PGR, BID, HK3, PMAIP1, and SLC4A11 emerged as the top five genes with significant impact on the prediction model of high immune carotid artery atherosclerotic plaque. It is noteworthy that increasing PGR expression is associated with a decrease in the degree of immune cell infiltration and a reduced risk of developing carotid artery atherosclerotic plaques. Conversely, higher expressions of BID, HK3, PMAIP1, SLC4A11, NDUFB3, MSR1, GK3P, PNPLA3, GZMB, ARRB2, and CYP27B1 are linked to an increased degree of immune cell infiltration and an elevated risk of carotid artery atherosclerotic plaques.
In the interdependency network of MitoDEGs in carotid atherosclerotic plaque (Fig.  D), the farther apart the symbols representing gene pairs are from the axes, the stronger their mutual dependence. Conversely, if the symbols are closer to the coordinate axes, it indicates weaker interdependence between the genes. The analysis results demonstrated that strong interdependencies were observed between PGR and BID, PGR and HK3, as well as PMAIP1 and MSR1.

## Key MitoDEGs in peripheral atherosclerotic plaque
In the ranking of MitoDEGs in peripheral atherosclerotic plaque (Fig.  E, genes ranked in descending order of importance), RAC2, GZMB, PMAIP1, SNCA, and TCL1A emerged as the top five genes with significant impact on the prediction model of high immune peripheral artery atherosclerotic plaque. It is noteworthy that higher expressions of RAC2, GZMB, PMAIP1, SNCA, TCL1A, PIM1, FGR, ALAS2, RAB32, and CASP1 are linked to an increased degree of immune cell infiltration and an elevated risk of peripheral atherosclerotic plaque.
As shown in the inter-dependency network of MitoDEGs in peripheral atherosclerotic plaque (Fig.  F), RAC2 exhibited strong interdependence with SNCA, GZMB, PMAIP1, TCL1A, ALAS2, and PIM1. SNCA shown strong interdependence with GZMB, TCL1A, and PIM1. GZMB exhibited strong interdependence with ALAS2. PMAIP1 demonstrated strong interdependence with ALAS2 and PIM1. TCL1A displayed strong interdependence with PIM1.

## Construction of prediction models
To quantitatively validate the predictive power of the identified key MitoDEGs for assessing atherosclerosis severity, we constructed machine learning models. The primary goal of this modeling was not to develop a new clinical diagnostic tool, but to provide robust, data-driven evidence that the expression patterns of our key MitoDEGs can effectively stratify samples based on the degree of immune infiltration—a key proxy for disease severity. Strong model performance would thereby reinforce the biological and clinical relevance of our mitochondrial gene signature.

## Carotid atherosclerotic plaque severity model
In this study, we compared two different machine learning algorithms for prediction tasks. The XGBoost model performed the best, with an AUC of 0.822, indicating its excellent discriminative ability. Although the logistic regression model is relatively simple, it still demonstrated robust predictive performance (an AUC of 0.715), highlighting that a well-tuned baseline model can also achieve satisfactory results. The XGBoost demonstrated the best predictive performance (see Table  and Fig.  A, B and C). 

## Peripheral atherosclerotic plaque severity model
The XGBoost model performed the best, with an AUC of 0.882, indicating its excellent discriminative ability. And the logistic regression model also demonstrated robust predictive performance (an AUC of 0.857). The XGBoost demonstrated the best predictive performance (see Table  and Fig.  D, E and F).

## Intersection gene acquisition
The intersection of carotid atherosclerotic plaques MitoDEG sets and peripheral atherosclerotic plaques MitoDEG sets resulted in two common genes, namely GZMB and PMAIP1 (see Supplementary Fig. 1).

## Exploration of enriched pathways for key MitoDEGs
To decipher the potential biological roles and coordinated functions of the key MitoDEGs beyond their individual actions, we performed functional enrichment analysis. This systematic approach allows us to determine whether these genes collectively participate in specific biological processes, molecular functions, or signaling pathways, thereby providing a higher-level, mechanistic understanding of their involvement in atherosclerosis.
We identified a total of 11 carotid-derived MitoDEGs (1 from atherosclerotic carotid artery and 10 from carotid plaque severity model) and 13 peripheral-derived MitoDEGs (1 from atherosclerotic peripheral artery and 12 from peripheral plaque severity model). Among them, three genes were repeated, resulting in a total of 21 MitoDEGs. Enrichment analysis of the MitoDEGs was performed using GO and KEGG pathways.
After performing enrichment analysis, we identified three significantly different KEGG pathways for key MitoDEGs of carotid artery plaque severity, including “Apoptosis” and the “p53 signaling pathway”. Additionally, we found 15 GO pathways, which consisted of nine Biological Process (BP) pathways including “hexose metabolic process” and “regulation of epithelial cell proliferation”, three Cellular Component (CC) pathways including the “mitochondrial outer membrane”, and three Molecular Function (MF) pathways including “hexokinase activity”. Please refer to Table  and Fig.  B for more detailed information. 
We identified two significantly different KEGG pathways for key MitoDEGs of peripheral artery plaque severity, namely "positive regulation of peptidyl-serine phosphorylation" and the "Colorectal cancer pathway". Additionally, we found 22 GO pathways, which consisted of 12 Biological Process (BP) pathways including "positive regulation of protein serine/threonine kinase activity" and "apoptotic process", six Cellular Component (CC) pathways including "cytosol" and "mitochondrion", and four Molecular Function (MF) pathways, including "protein binding" and "protein kinase binding". For more detailed information, please refer to Table  and Fig.  C.

## Construction of PPI network and selection of key Markers
Proteins rarely function in isolation. To understand the functional relationships and potential collaborations among the key MitoDEGs at the protein level, we constructed a Protein–Protein Interaction network. This network analysis helps to visualize and identify core regulatory modules or highly interconnected "hub" genes, which are often of critical functional importance in driving disease-related processes.
The constructed PPI (protein–protein interaction) network revealed that out of the 21 Key MitoDEGs, 19 were upregulated (ALAS2, CASP1, FGR, GZMB, PIM1, PMAIP1, RAB32, RAC2, SDS, SNCA, TCL1A, ARRB2, BID, CYP27B1, HK3, MSR1, NDUFB3, PNPLA3, SLC4A11), 1 was downregulated (PGR), and 11 were found to be interconnected (NDUFB3, HK3, RAC2, PMAIP1, CASP1, GZMB, BID, FGR, ARRB2, SNCA, ALAS2). The GK3P gene was not detected, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2 The constructed PPI network was imported into Cytoscape, and cytohubba algorithm was employed for centrality analysis. Based on the cytohubba scores, as shown in Table  , the associated proteins were ranked according to their centrality strength. By tracing back the origin and function of each related gene, we identified four genes, CASP1, BID, PMAIP1, and GZMB, which were among the top 11 genes ranked by cytohubba scores and were most relevant to mitochondrial function and quality control. The selection of CASP1, BID, PMAIP1, and GZMB for subsequent experimental validation was based on a multi-faceted rationale. While all ranked highly by cytohubba algorithms, these specific genes were prioritized not only for their network centrality but also for their established biological roles as critical nodes bridging mitochondrial dysfunction and immune-inflammatory processes. CASP1 is a core executor of inflammasome-mediated inflammation, BID is a key integrator of death-receptor signaling and mitochondrial apoptosis, PMAIP1 (NOXA) directly induces mitochondrial permeabilization, and GZMB is a primary cytotoxic granzyme that cleaves and activates mitochondrial pro-apoptotic proteins like BID. This unique positioning makes them highly relevant for probing the immuno-metabolic crosstalk in atherosclerosis. These four genes were selected as our key marker genes of interest for further experimental validation.

As shown in Fig.  A, compared to the control group, the aortic Hematoxylin–Eosin (HE) Stain of mice in the model group reveals clear pathological changes consistent with atherosclerosis, including lumen stenosis, intimal thickening, and significant foam cell accumulation. These features align with the typical characteristics of advanced atherosclerotic lesions. Quantitative analysis of the aortic lesion area further indicates that the percentage of lesion area relative to the lumen area in the model group is significantly higher than in the control group (p = 0.0002, Fig.  A). Figure  B show the results of Oil Red O staining of the aorta in the different mouse groups. The staining of the aorta revealed a marked increase in lipid deposition in the Model group compared to the Control group (p = 0.035). 
In aorta and myocardium tissue, CASP1, BID, CASP1, and GZMB protein level in the model group were significantly higher than those in the control group (Fig.  C, D, E and F).

The Ang Ⅱ-induced injury in cell viability were shown in Fig. 32 (p < 0.001, Fig.  G). In AngⅡ injured HUVECs, CASP1, BID, CASP1, and GZMB protein level in the model group were significantly higher than those in the control group (p < 0.05, p < 0.01, Fig.  H and I).

## Discussion
Atherosclerosis (AS), occurring in the intimal layer of arterial blood vessel walls, is a chronic, progressive, inflammatory disease characterized by lipid accumulation. It serves as the pathological and pathophysiological basis for cardiovascular diseases, posing a significant threat to human health [ ].
Han et al. have confirmed through PCA and tSNE analysis that the included samples can be classified based on the degree of immune cell infiltration, and a higher degree of immune cell infiltration corresponds to a higher severity of atherosclerosis [ ]. Thus, in this study, the degree of immune cell infiltration was used to classify atherosclerotic plaques and arteries into immunity-high group and immunity-low group. Comprehensive analysis and comparison of differentially expressed genes were performed, considering variations in different locations and degrees of atherosclerosis. The results revealed 6933 differentially expressed genes between the immunity-high and immunity-low groups of atherosclerotic plaques. In atherosclerotic arteries, 27 differentially expressed genes were identified between the immunity-high and immunity-low groups.
In this study, we employed Lasso regression and Shap for the selection of key MitoDEGs. Shap aggregates the feature importance values to obtain the final prediction of the model [ ]. This technique avoids filtering out important differentially expressed genes in the carotid plaque group and peripheral artery plaque group, ensuring the integrity of the feature genes [ ]. Shap utilizes Shapley value to interpret the results of the prediction model. The major advantage of Shapley value is its ability to reflect the influence of each feature in each sample and the positive or negative effect of that influence on the final prediction outcome. Furthermore, Shap effectively reduces the running time by 80% via employing a sampling procedure that utilizes the smallest possible subsample of the available data at each iteration of the algorithm, addressing the issue of slow processing with large datasets (https://pypi.org/project/BorutaShap/).
In the construction of the atherosclerosis severity prediction model, we utilized various machine learning models such as Logistic Regression and XGBoost. Logistic Regression model displays the degree of association between variables, improves the likelihood of predicting outcomes, and controls the effects of confounding variables [ ]. XGBoost utilizes multiple learners to reduce model errors, handle missing values, and prevent overfitting of data [ ]. As the complexity of the models increases, we incorporated SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) interpretation to the XGBoost model, enhancing its interpretability and addressing the issue of poor model interpretability [ ]. We validated the performance of these prediction models using metrics such as accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and AUC (Area Under the Curve), providing support for the reliability of the model results.
Utilizing GO and KEGG databases allows us to gain insights into the biological processes, molecular functions, cellular components, and pathways related to the key MitoDEGs. Indeed, the pathways identified in this study—apoptosis, p53 signaling, hexose metabolism, and regulation of protein serine/threonine kinase activity—are intrinsically linked to immune cell infiltration, collectively shaping the inflammatory microenvironment in atherosclerosis. Apoptosis extends beyond mere cell death, playing a pivotal role in immune regulation by modulating the survival and turnover of leukocytes such as macrophages and T-cells within plaques, thereby influencing inflammatory persistence and plaque stability. The p53 signaling pathway, a critical stress-response mechanism, regulates immune cell function by inducing cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, or modulating inflammatory gene expression, thereby impacting the magnitude and character of leukocyte infiltration in atherosclerotic lesions. Hexose metabolism, particularly glycolysis, is fundamental for fulfilling the bioenergetic and biosynthetic demands of activated immune cells. Metabolic reprogramming towards glycolysis is essential for supporting pro-inflammatory functions and facilitating the migration of leukocytes into the vascular wall. Furthermore, protein serine/threonine kinases act as central signaling hubs that govern immune cell activation, migration, and cytokine production. Their orchestrated activity directly influences the ability of leukocytes to infiltrate tissues and propagate inflammatory responses. In summary, these pathways do not operate in isolation but form a complex, interconnected network that bridges cellular stress, metabolic adaptation, and immune activation. This nexus provides a compelling mechanistic explanation for the relationship between immune infiltration and atherosclerosis severity observed in our study and unveils novel therapeutic opportunities for targeted immunomodulation in atherosclerosis.
Analyzing the PPI network provides insights into how the key MitoDEGs interact and function together within cellular processes. Identifying the key proteins in these networks is crucial for understanding the underlying mechanisms and potential targets associated with atherosclerosis. The MCODE plugin in Cytoscape is used to identify highly connected key modules or core protein clusters from protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks. In our study, by using the MCODE plugin, we were able to extract and rank key proteins from the PPI networks of key MitoDEGs derived from both carotid and peripheral sources. By identifying these core proteins, we can not only gain a better understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying the related diseases but also provide important clues for the discovery of future biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets.
When mitochondrial quality control is impaired, mitochondrial fusion decreases, fission increases, and autophagy is enhanced, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and excessive mitochondrial inflammatory response. This exacerbates immune cell infiltration, promotes lipid deposition, and further intensifies the progression of atherosclerosis. Stable mitochondrial function and normal mitochondrial quality control mechanisms are of significant importance in the occurrence and development of atherosclerosis [ , ]. Therefore, we explored the association between mitochondrial-related genes and atherosclerosis, identifying differentially expressed genes associated with atherosclerosis and mitochondria. Utilizing machine learning techniques, we screened for key markers and further validated their differential expression in both in vivo and in vitro settings.
In our screened differentially expressed mitochondrial-related genes, BID, CASP1, GZMB, and PMAIP1 are closely associated with cellular apoptosis. BID, a member of the pro-apoptotic BCL-2 family, promotes mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP) and the release of cytochrome c by activating BAX/BAK or inhibiting anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family members, thereby facilitating cellular apoptosis [ ]. Oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) significantly upregulates BID, promoting both of these pathways and thus contributing to cellular apoptosis [ ]. Consequently, the upregulation of BID is associated with accelerated progression of atherosclerosis. PMAIP1, also belonging to the pro-apoptotic BCL-2 family [ ], activates BAX/BAK, leading to MOMP and cytochrome c release, consequently promoting cellular apoptosis [ ]. CASP1, a member of the caspase protease family, is known to be a key mediator of cellular apoptosis [ ]. CASP1 can cleave and activate BID at the Asp59 site, promoting the BID-mediated cellular apoptosis pathway [ ]. In the absence of BID, CASP1 can also trigger BID-independent cellular apoptosis, mainly relying on caspase-3 [ ]. Additionally, CASP1 has been shown to directly cleave parkin, inhibiting parkin-dependent mitochondrial autophagy and exacerbating mitochondrial damage and cellular apoptosis [ ]. Upon activation by inflammasomes, CASP1 cleaves and activates gasdermin proteins, causing their translocation to the cell membrane and formation of pores, ultimately leading to membrane rupture [ ]. GZMB is implicated in apoptosis of macrophages, smooth muscle cells, and endothelial cells, suggesting its potential contribution to the development of atherosclerotic plaques [ ], with elevated levels of GZMB in the aorta correlating with increased instability of plaques [ ]. Both human and mouse GZMB-mediated cellular apoptosis primarily occurs via the mitochondrial pathway [ ]. GZMB directly cleaves NDUFS1.2 and NDUFV1 in mitochondrial complex I, inducing ROS-dependent cellular apoptosis [ ]. Furthermore, BID serves as the preferred substrate for GZMB [ ]. Human GZMB cleaves BID at the Asp75 site, activating Bak/Bax and subsequently generating active caspase-3, resulting in cytochrome c release and subsequent cellular apoptosis [ ].
Additionally, RAC2 and NDUFB3 are both associated with the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). RAC2 GTPase can alter mitochondrial membrane potential, thereby modifying the electron flow in mitochondrial respiratory chain complex III (MRC-cIII) and leading to the production of high levels of ROS. These ROS, in turn, promote oxidative damage to mitochondrial DNA, triggering genomic instability [ ]. RAC1-dependent macrophage interleukin-1β expression is crucial in preventing progressive calcification, a key driver of vascular smooth muscle cell calcium deposition. RAC2 serves as a critical signaling sensor in inflammatory cells. It inhibits RAC1-dependent macrophage IL-1β expression to prevent vascular calcification and subsequently determines the extent of IL-1β-mediated atherosclerotic calcification [ ].
The NDUFB3 gene encodes a subunit of mitochondrial membrane respiratory chain NADH dehydrogenase (complex I), which is the first enzyme in the mitochondrial electron transport chain. This protein is localized in the inner mitochondrial membrane and is a component of the mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation mechanism. When NDUFB3 is up-regulated, it leads to excessive ROS production, thereby promoting the formation and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and subsequent cell pyroptosis, exacerbating the progression of atherosclerosis [ ].
Finally, we briefly elaborate on the genes in the PPI results that exhibit weaker associations with mitochondria and atherosclerosis. HK3 phosphorylates glucose to produce glucose-6-phosphate, which is the first step in most glucose metabolism pathways. Chronic social defeat (CSD) alters brain glucose metabolism and depletes HK3 in mitochondria-associated membranes (MAM). In CSD, the positive correlation between MAM, synaptic glucose, and HK3 is lost [ ]. MSR1, also known as Macrophage Scavenger Receptor 1, is primarily found on the surface of various types of macrophages and plays a critical role in inflammation in various pathophysiological processes. MSR1 can mediate the uptake of modified LDL by macrophages, leading to the accumulation of cholesterol within macrophages and contributing to atherosclerotic lesions [ ]. PIM1 can directly phosphorylate and inactivate the pro-apoptotic members of the Bcl-2 family, such as Bad, thereby suppressing apoptosis [ ]. The product of the ALAS2 gene is a red blood cell-specific mitochondrial enzyme. Defects in ALAS2 gene can result in X-linked sideroblastic anemia [ ]. Akt phosphorylates BAD to inhibit apoptosis [ ], while TCL1A acts as a novel Akt kinase coactivator, promoting Akt-induced apoptosis [ ]. FGR and HCK are two Src tyrosine kinases co-expressed in bone marrow cells, regulating cell adhesion and migration [ ]. The absence of HCK and FGR weakens atherosclerotic plaque formation by eliminating endothelial adhesion and migration, as well as reducing macrophage accumulation. Additionally, FGR serves as a convergence point for multiple cell receptor-mediated signaling pathways associated with atherosclerosis pathogenesis [ ]. SLC4A11 is present in the plasma membrane of corneal endothelial cells and mitochondrial membranes. Lack of SLC4A11 leads to excessive ROS production, mitochondrial damage, and cell apoptosis [ ]. The PNPLA3 protein plays a role in lipid metabolism, involving both synthesis and breakdown [ ]. CYP27B1 regulates the levels of biologically active vitamin D and plays a crucial role in calcium homeostasis. Mutations in CYP27B1 gene can lead to vitamin D-dependent rickets type 1 [ ]. PGR mediates the physiological actions of progesterone [ ]. ARRB2 is a member of the Arrestin/beta-Arrestin protein family, highly expressed in the central nervous system. SNCA is a member of the Synuclein protein family, and the deletion of this gene is associated with the pathogenesis of Parkinson's disease [ ].
We ultimately selected four genes, BID, CASP1, GZMB, and PMAIP1 as key markers to validate. Beyond mere statistical ranking, our final selection of CASP1, BID, PMAIP1, and GZMB for experimental validation was a deliberate strategy to target genes that sit at the functional nexus of mitochondrial integrity and immune activation. Although other candidates from our LASSO model exhibited strong predictive value, these four hubs were chosen because their well-documented functions directly contribute to the core pathological axes of AS: Caspase-1 inflammasome signaling (CASP1), the integration of extrinsic death signals with intrinsic mitochondrial apoptosis (BID), direct mitochondrial destabilization (PMAIP1), and immune-mediated cytotoxicity (GZMB). The fact that GZMB can activate BID, which in turn triggers mitochondrial apoptosis, exemplifies the synergistic interplay within this network. Our successful validation of their overexpression "in vivo" and "in vitro" supports the notion that this bioinformatics-guided, biology-informed selection strategy can help identify central mechanistic players, underscoring the value of interpreting computational findings through a lens of established biological pathways. When detecting protein expression levels in myocardium, aorta, and HUVECs (n = 3), the statistical power was calculated by inputting the mean and standard deviation of relative protein expression from the control group and model group using PASS.11 software. The results showed that the power of Casp1 and PMAIP1 in myocardial tissue was 0.99990 and 0.98071, respectively; that in aorta tissue was 0.53510 and 0.44390,respectively; and that in HUVECs was 0.86632 and 0.86504, respectively. The overall statistical power was good. This validation provide crucial evidence to support the relationship between key genes/proteins and atherosclerosis, aiding in a comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanisms.

## Limitations
This study has several limitations. First, obtaining healthy human coronary or carotid artery tissues is ethically and practically challenging, preventing direct comparison between normal arterial tissues and atherosclerotic lesions. We used varying degrees of immune infiltration as a surrogate indicator of atherosclerosis severity. While this represents an established methodology, future validation in clinical cohorts incorporating normal controls remains necessary. Second, the relatively small sample size of the available peripheral artery dataset (particularly the atherosclerotic peripheral artery group, N = 40) limits statistical power and may fail to detect some biologically significant genes. Therefore, conclusions regarding peripheral artery-specific MitoDEGs should be considered preliminary and require confirmation through larger-scale studies. Third, as this study primarily constitutes a bioinformatics discovery with preliminary experimental validation, we were unable to obtain corresponding patient serum samples for analysis. Measuring circulating levels of mitochondrial function, immune response, and inflammatory biomarkers associated with the key MitoDEGs holds significant importance for future clinical translation, representing a limitation that should be addressed in subsequent research. Fourth, the HUVECs used for in vitro validation are derived from umbilical veins and differ from arterial endothelial cells in hemodynamic forces and biological microenvironment, potentially limiting their ability to fully recapitulate all aspects of atherosclerosis. However, their accessibility and retention of core endothelial functions maintain their value as a tool for preliminary mechanistic exploration. Finally, this study primarily establishes associations between key MitoDEGs and atherosclerosis. Elucidating their precise causal roles and molecular mechanisms—such as determining their cell-type-specific expression within plaques through immunofluorescence co-localization, investigating their functions via genetic manipulation, and conducting more comprehensive mitochondrial functional assessments—represents an important direction for future research.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, this study systematically identified and validated key mitochondrial dysfunction-related genes (MitoDEGs) intricately linked to both immune infiltration and metabolic reprogramming in atherosclerotic plaque progression. By integrating multi-dataset bioinformatics analysis—including ssGSEA for immune characterization, rigorous differential expression screening, and advanced machine learning algorithms such as XGBoost and LASSO regression—we discerned critical MitoDEGs specific to carotid (e.g., BID, HK3) and peripheral (e.g., RAC2, TCL1A) plaques, alongside shared markers (GZMB and PMAIP1). Notably, our analyses revealed that elevated expression of these genes correlates significantly with enhanced immune infiltration and profound metabolic alterations, particularly in hexose metabolism and mitochondrial energy pathways.
The methodological strength of our study was evidenced by high predictive accuracy (AUCs up to 0.882) and successful batch effect mitigation, affirming the robustness of our computational framework. Functional enrichment further associated these MitoDEGs with apoptosis, p53 signaling, and immune-metabolic crosstalk, underscoring a synergistic interplay between mitochondrial dysfunction and metabolic reprogramming. Experimental validation in vivo and in vitro consistently confirmed the upregulation of central hub genes—CASP1, BID, PMAIP1, and GZMB—implicating them as drivers of both inflammatory and metabolic dysfunction in atherosclerosis.
Overall, our work provides insights into novel immune-metabolic mechanisms associated with plaque progression and demonstrates the utility of an integrative bioinformatics-experimental approach for identifying high-value candidate therapeutic targets. The emphasis on mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming provides a fresh perspective beyond conventional immune-centric narratives, offering new mechanistic avenues for targeted intervention in atherosclerosis.
