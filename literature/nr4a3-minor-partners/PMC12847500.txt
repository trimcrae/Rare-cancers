## Introduction
Solid tumors comprise the majority of global cancer incidences, accounting for over 95% of cases and remaining a predominant cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [ ]. While CAR-T cell therapy has achieved remarkable success in hematologic malignancies, its application in solid tumors has been limited, with no FDA-approved CAR-T products for these indications to date [ ].The challenges posed by solid tumors are multifaceted, encompassing both intrinsic and extrinsic barriers. On the intrinsic level, CAR-T cells in solid tumors often exhibit suboptimal functionality, including instability in CAR expression [ ], poor antigen recognition [ ], limited tumor infiltration [ ], and impaired persistence [ ]. These deficiencies collectively weaken the cell’s cytotoxic potential, undermining therapeutic efficacy [ ]. Extrinsically, the TME of solid tumors presents a complex and suppressive ecosystem [ ]. Characterized by hypoxia, metabolic dysregulation, and a dense stromal barrier, the TME actively limits CAR-T cell efficacy through immunosuppressive cytokines, immune checkpoint expression, and inhibitory metabolite [ ]. This environment fosters T cell exhaustion and anergy, effectively neutralizing antitumor immune responses.
To overcome these barriers in solid tumor, innovative approaches are required, encompassing advanced CAR expression and stabilization, strategies to improve CAR-T cell trafficking and persistence, and TME remodeling to create a more favorable immune milieu. Here, we synthesize recent progress in these areas and highlights future research opportunities aimed at unlocking the potential of CAR-T therapy for solid tumors.

## CAR transduction and stability
Achieving stable CAR expression is a critical determinant of CAR-T cell efficacy, particularly in the hostile microenvironment of solid tumors. Viral vectors like lentiviral and retroviral systems dominate due to their high efficiency and durable gene expression but are limited by insertional mutagenesis risks, high costs, and production inefficiencies, restricting scalability. Non-viral methods, including transposon systems (Sleeping Beauty, PiggyBac), mRNA electroporation, CRISPR-Cas9, and lipid nanoparticle delivery, address these issues by minimizing mutagenesis risks, shortening production, and enabling precise control of CAR expression. These approaches enhance safety, flexibility, and scalability, advancing CAR-T therapy against solid tumors and their trade-offs are summarized as below (Fig.  ). 

## Viral vector systems
Viral vectors have been instrumental in the evolution of CAR-T cell therapy, forming the cornerstone of numerous successful clinical trials. Among these, retroviral and lentiviral vectors are the most widely adopted due to their proven efficiency in delivering stable CAR expression. Additionally, adeno-associated virus (AAV) has been employed in the preparation of CAR-T cells due to its low immunogenicity and optimal transduction efficiency [ , ].

## Retrovirus
Retroviral vectors, classified as RNA viruses [ , ] possess the unique capability to integrate their genetic material into the host cell’s genome, ensuring long-term, stable expression of the introduced CAR genes [ – ]. This property makes them particularly valuable for sustained CAR expression in CAR-T cell therapies. The landmark work of M A Kay and colleagues in 1990 demonstrated the successful use of retroviruses to introduce exogenous genes into T cells, leading to promising clinical outcomes and establishing the foundation for modern CAR-T technology [ ]. In follow-up studies, retrovirus-mediated ex vivo CAR-T cells successfully treated nine patients with X-linked SCID (SCID-X1) and demonstrated durability in treating patients with active viraemia [ , ]. In recent years, γ-retroviruses have become a cornerstone in CAR-T cell production, especially for targeting hematologic malignancies. For example, CD19-targeted CAR-T cells, generated using γ-retroviral vectors and combined with chemotherapy, have shown a complete response (CR) rate of up to 88% in B cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) cases [ ]. This success underpins FDA-approved CAR-T products such as Yescarta and Tecartus (e.g., NCT01626495, NCT01029366).
However, retroviruses pose risks due to their preferential integration near transcriptional start sites and CpG islands, where long terminal repeat (LTR) enhancers may activate adjacent proto-oncogenes, raising concerns of oncogenic transformation [ ].

## Lentivirus
Lentiviral vectors exhibit a distinct preference for integrating into introns of transcriptionally active genes, reducing the likelihood of insertional oncogenesis compared to retroviral vectors [ ]. Third-generation self-inactivating (SIN) lentiviral vectors further mitigate this risk through the use of human-derived promoters [ ], such as EF-1α and phosphoglycerate kinase, which enhance both safety and efficiency. Unlike retroviral vectors, lentiviruses can efficiently transduce quiescent (non-dividing) T cells, an advantage that minimizes genotoxicity risks and broadens their applicability [ , ]. Furthermore, lentiviral vectors can carry larger genetic payloads, accommodating up to 6.5 kilobases of exogenous material, making them suitable for complex CAR designs [ – ]. These features have solidified lentiviral systems as the primary vector choice for many FDA-approved CAR-T products [ ]. In a preclinical study, Christian J. Buchholz et al. demonstrated for the first time the generation of human CD19 CAR-T cells directly in fully humanized mice using the lentiviral vector CD8-LV, which specifically targets human CD8 + cells [ ]. Byoung Y. Ryu’s VivoVec platform [ ], which employs third-generation SIN lentiviral vectors, achieved in vivo generation of CD20-targeted CAR-T cells, resulting in complete B cell depletion for up to 76 days [ ]. Despite these benefits, challenges such as production complexity and residual risks of mutagenesis remain, prompting continued efforts to optimize lentiviral vector design.

## Adeno-associated virus
AAV vectors have emerged as a significant innovation in CAR-T therapy, offering high transfection efficiency and a favorable safety profile. Unlike retroviral and lentiviral systems, AAV can transduce both dividing and non-dividing cells, making it highly versatile for gene delivery [ ]. To date, nearly 200 clinical trials using AAV have been completed or are underway (e.g., NCT06508346 and NCT06277427). Xilin Wu et al. demonstrated the efficacy of AAV in generating functional CAR-T cells in vivo, which successfully induced tumor regression in leukemia models [ ]. Sidi Chen and colleagues utilized AAV6 to deliver CRISPR-Cas12a systems for checkpoint knockout and homology-directed repair in CAR-T cells, streamlining their preparation process [ ]. Moreover, Pin-Yi Wang et al. leveraged AAV to deliver bispecific antibodies, achieving therapeutic expression for over a year with a single administration [ ]. While AAV offers numerous advantages, including controllable CAR expression and high production efficiency, challenges such as immunogenicity and scalability remain areas of active investigation [ ].
In summary, viral vectors are widely used for CAR transduction due to their high efficiency, yet they pose significant limitations, including immunogenicity, complex production, insertional mutagenesis risks, and high costs. These drawbacks have driven the development of non-viral delivery platforms, which are emerging as practical, effective, and safer alternatives in CAR-T cell therapy.

## Non-viral vector systems
Non-viral vector systems have gained increasing attention as next-generation platforms for CAR-T cell engineering. These systems, encompassing transposon technologies, electroporation methods, and nanoparticle-based carriers, offer several advantages over traditional viral vectors, including reduced genotoxicity risks, scalable production, and lower manufacturing costs [ – ].

## Transposon-based CAR gene transfer
Transposon-based systems, particularly the Sleeping Beauty (SB) transposon, have emerged as a robust non-viral strategy for CAR gene delivery into T cells [ ]. SB provides a non-viral approach for stable CAR integration with lower manufacturing complexity; however, SB-mediated insertion is not risk-free and warrants product-specific insertion-site analysis and long-term surveillance [ ]. Distinguished by its ability to carry substantial genetic payloads and its high safety profile, the SB transposon system has been validated as highly effective in producing genetically modified cells [ , ]. Importantly, the system facilitates precise genetic modification of T cells and allows for the integration of transgenes specifically into TA dinucleotide sequences in the host genome. This approach has demonstrated consistent efficacy in clinical applications, including the CARAMBA trial, which utilized SB-engineered CAR-T cells targeting SLAMF7 and confirmed their feasibility, safety, and antitumor potency [ ]. Atrial using donor-derived CD19 CAR-T cells engineered via SB reported complete remission in 46% of patients receiving the highest dose, highlighting its therapeutic potential [ ]. These results highlight the potential of transposon-based CAR gene transfer as a non-viral, effective method for engineering T cells to target and eliminate cancer cells [ ].

## Electroporation-based CAR generation
Electroporation, a widely used non-viral transfection method, enables the direct delivery of large and complex CAR constructs, as well as the simultaneous introduction of multiple genes [ ]. This approach offers significant advantages in CAR-T cell engineering, including scalability and operational simplicity. Essential components for electroporation, such as Cas9 protein, single-guide RNA (sgRNA), and CAR-encoding DNA templates, can be efficiently produced on a large scale, significantly reducing production costs and time compared to traditional methods [ ].
Recent advancements have highlighted the versatility and efficacy of electroporation in CAR-T cell generation. For instance, Shigeki Yagyu et al. employed electroporation to transfer CAR-carrying transposons into T cells, optimizing CAR-T production workflows and validating their safety in preclinical macaque models [ ]. These CAR-T cells exhibited robust tumor-killing capacity without significant toxicity or cytokine release syndrome, emphasizing their therapeutic potential. Similarly, Liu et al. utilized bio-orthogonal chemistry to generate Cas12a-crRNA complexes, which were then integrated into T cells via electroporation. This innovative approach facilitated precise CAR gene incorporation into the T cell genome, enhancing the efficiency of genetic modification [ ]. To further improve electroporation efficiency, Ying Zhang et al. optimized electroporation conditions using an iso-osmotic buffer, achieving a remarkable 20-fold increase in CAR-T cell yield compared to conventional protocols [ ]. In vivo studies indicate that these optimized CAR-T cells exhibit enhanced antitumor activity by inhibiting the STING signaling pathway through the production of 2′3′-cGAMP [ ].
Electroporation thus represents a promising non-viral strategy for CAR-T cell engineering, offering a balance of efficiency, scalability, and safety that is critical for advancing CAR-T therapies into broader clinical applications.

## Nanoparticle-based CAR encoding
Nanoparticles, first utilized in the late 1960s, have been engineered for delivering a wide range of biological and pharmaceutical materials to different cellular environments [ – ]. Their application in CAR gene delivery has demonstrated efficacy comparable to traditional viral vectors, making them a compelling alternative in T cell engineering [ – ]. Among the various types of nanoparticles, lipid nanoparticles (LNPs)—spherical vesicles composed of ionizable lipids—are the most widely utilized for CAR transduction due to their efficiency and versatility [ – ]. A notable advancement in this field was achieved in 2020 when Michael J. Mitchell et al. developed a specialized LNP, termed C14-4, capable of delivering CAR mRNA with high efficiency [ ]. This approach resulted in robust CAR expression in T cells while significantly reducing cytotoxic effects [ ]. Similarly, Joel G Rurik et al. used LNP-loaded mRNA to transiently generate antifibrotic CAR-T cells in vivo. These cells successfully targeted activated fibroblasts in a model of heart failure, demonstrating the potential of LNP technology to deliver CARs effectively, even beyond oncology [ ]. Argueta, S. et al. utilized a lipid nanoparticle (LNP) formulation to encapsulate mRNA encoding a GPC3-targeted chimeric antigen receptor (CAR), thereby in vivo programming bone marrow cells to express GPC3-specific chimeric antigen receptors [ ]. Efficacy and safety were demonstrated in preclinical studies using mice and monkeys.
While most CAR-T cell therapies in clinical trials have used viral vectors, non-viral strategies, including transposon systems, electroporation, and nanoparticle-based delivery, are rapidly gaining traction [ , ]. Transposon transfection is cost-effective and quick; electroporation supports large-scale, economical production; and nanoparticles enable faster, safer CAR-T cell preparation [ , , ]. These emerging technologies are poised to redefine CAR-T cell preparation, potentially establishing non-viral approaches as the standard for future clinical applications.

## CAR stabilization
The long-term stability of CAR-T cells are intrinsically linked to the structural design of the CAR. The CAR is composed of four critical domains: the extracellular antigen-binding domain, a spacer or hinge region, a transmembrane domain, and an intracellular signaling domain [ , ]. Optimizing these components is essential for enhancing antigen specificity, minimizing off-target effects, and improving the persistence of CAR-T cells in the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.
The antigen-binding domain, typically composed of single-chain variable fragments (scFvs), directly determines the affinity and specificity of CAR-T cells for their target antigens on tumor cells [ ]. For example, two preclinical mouse models demonstrate that adjusting CAR affinity via scFv can enhance antitumor activity and prolong survival through low affinity [ , ]. The hinge region, situated between the antigen-binding domain and the transmembrane domain, plays a key role in enabling CAR-T cells to adapt to different spatial configurations of tumor cell surface antigens [ , ]. The length and composition of the hinge region can also influence CAR-T cell activation and stability, with commonly used sequences derived from CD8, CD28, or immunoglobulin isotypes like IgG1 or IgG4 [ ]. The transmembrane domain anchors the CAR to the T cell membrane, ensuring proper positioning of the antigen-binding domain for efficient antigen recognition and binding [ , ]. This domain is typically derived from proteins such as CD3ζ, CD8α, or CD28, which promote stable CAR expression and effective signaling [ ]. Finally, the intracellular signaling domain, often derived from co-stimulatory molecules like 4-1BB or CD28, enhances T cell activation, expansion, and persistence. While 4-1BB triggers NF-κB signaling to promote cell survival and cytotoxicity [ ], CD28 activation leads to rapid expansion but can result in cytokine storms and multi-organ failure [ ]. Therefore, balancing the structural components of the CAR is essential to optimize antitumor efficacy while minimizing off-target effects and toxicity. Haopeng Wang et al. mutated lysine K to arginine R (CARKR), a ubiquitination site in the intracellular segment of CAR, which blocked the ubiquitination modification and lysosomal degradation of CAR, and enhanced the downstream signaling of 4-1BB, which facilitated the metabolic reprogramming of T cells, and achieved a ‘recyclable CAR’ [ ]. Ultimately, the design of the antigen-binding domain, hinge region, transmembrane domain, and intracellular signaling domain must be carefully tailored to maintain CAR stability and ensure effective tumor cell targeting.
While viral vectors remain the predominant platform for clinical CAR-T manufacturing, non-viral engineering strategies—including transposon systems, mRNA electroporation, and nanoparticle-based delivery—are rapidly gaining momentum as scalable alternatives. Viral vectors such as retroviral and lentiviral systems provide stable, high-level CAR expression essential for durable antitumor activity, but their clinical use is limited by insertional mutagenesis risk, high cost, complex manufacturing, and restricted feasibility for repeated dosing [ , ]. In contrast, non-viral approaches offer improved flexibility and safety: transposon platforms enable rapid and cost-effective genome integration, electroporation supports large-scale and economical CAR introduction, and lipid nanoparticle-based delivery allows transient or localized CAR expression that may enhance safety and tumor penetration [ , , ]. These viral and non-viral systems should be viewed as complementary rather than competitive, with viral vectors ensuring persistence and non-viral modalities offering precision, tunability, and potentially superior applicability in solid tumor microenvironments.

## Genetic modification to enhance CAR-T efficacy
Genetic engineering strategies are pivotal in enhancing CAR-T cell functionality, addressing both intrinsic cellular deficiencies and the multifaceted challenges posed by the solid tumor microenvironment. To address these challenges, innovative ex vivo strategies, including synthetic biology-edited CAR-T cells, on/off switch-CAR-T cells, and gene-modified CAR-T cells, are being actively investigated [ ] (Fig.  ). 

## Synthetic biology and logic-gated CAR-T system
Synthetic biology has emerged as a transformative force in CAR-T cell engineering, enabling the development of sophisticated control systems that enhance T cell precision, adaptability, and therapeutic efficacy. By incorporating programmable biological circuits, researchers have introduced innovative designs that allow CAR-T cells to respond dynamically to tumor-specific cues, thereby minimizing off-target effects and mitigating immune evasion mechanisms inherent in solid tumors [ ].
A notable breakthrough in this field is the synthetic Notch (synNotch) receptor system, which integrates tumor-specific recognition and downstream CAR activation within a dual-receptor framework [ ].This innovation, pioneered by Wendell A. Lim and colleagues, allows for more targeted and controlled CAR-T cell activity, addressing challenges such as immune evasion and systemic toxicity [ ]. Expanding on this approach, Dongfang Liu’s team developed a "logic-gated" synNotch receptor targeting both GPC3 and CD147, which selectively activates CAR-T cells in response to these dual tumor antigens, improving specificity and minimizing off-target effects [ ]. Further advancements have led to the design of the split, universal, and programmable CAR (ZipCAR) system by Jang Hwan Cho et al., which enables precise modulation of T cell activation through a universal receptor and tumor-targeting linker [ ]. Furthermore, the SynZifTR toolkit, introduced by Ahmad Khalil, enables the fine-tuning of CAR-T cells using programmable synthetic transcription factors, providing a versatile platform for optimizing T cell responses [ ]. In addition, Wendell A. Lim’s work on enhanced CAR-T cells equipped with secondary receptors that recognize tumor antigens and trigger interleukin-2 (IL-2) release has shown promising results in animal models [ ].
The integration of synthetic biology into CAR-T cell engineering is creating new opportunities for cancer therapy by offering more precise, adaptable, and effective treatments [ ].

## Reversible switching-driven CAR-T cell
Reversible switching mechanisms have opened new avenues in CAR-T cell engineering, offering dynamic activation in response to specific environmental cues, including hypoxia, thermal changes, and external chemical signals. These systems provide an additional safety layer by restricting CAR-T cell activity to the tumor microenvironment, thereby minimizing off-target effects and enhancing therapeutic precision in solid tumors [ ].
In preclinical studies, researchers integrated hypoxia-responsive elements (HREs) and oxygen-dependent degradation domains (ODDs) into CAR constructs by leveraging the hypoxic characteristics of solid tumors [ , ]. For example, Jianqing Xu et al. incorporated hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF-1α) with HRE and ODD to develop a hypoxia-responsive CAR-T platform, achieving enhanced specificity for tumor regions under low oxygen conditions [ ].Temperature-sensitive strategies further broaden the scope of control. Wang Yingxiao et al. utilized a hypersensitive heat shock promoter selected from a library and combined it with a CAR signal positive feedback loop to construct EchoBack-CAR T cells capable of sustained activation upon focused ultrasound (FUS) stimulation [ ]. Similarly, Yingxiao Wang et al. utilized focused ultrasound guided by MRI to activate CAR-T cells via thermal promoters [ ].
Furthermore, preclinical studies demonstrate that small molecule-based switches offer a highly flexible control mechanism [ ]. For instance, Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) serves as a pivotal component in a system where FITC-labeled tumor-targeting molecules interact with a CAR containing an anti-FITC scFv, forming pseudo-immune synapses between CAR-T cells and tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) [ ]. Wilson W. Wong et al. designed a multifunctional protease-regulated CAR-T therapy using the NS3 (non-structural protein 3) system, which can be used to regulate CAR expression with a variety of small molecule drugs (grazoprevir [GZV], danoprevir [DNV], etc.) [ ]. This strategy has been successfully applied to CAR-T cells targeting CD19, CD22, HER2, and other antigens, with similar studies exploring rapamycin, rimiducid, resveratrol, protac compounds, and dasatinib [ , ].
In summary, reversible switching mechanisms deliver a transformative solution for enhancing the precision, safety, and efficacy of CAR-T cell therapies. By integrating environmental and external control systems, these advancements pave the way for more targeted and adaptable cancer treatments with minimized off-target effects.

## Immune checkpoint disruption
Immune checkpoint disruption has emerged as a critical strategy to bolster CAR-T cell efficacy by counteracting tumor-driven immunosuppressive mechanisms. Checkpoint molecules such as PD-1, CTLA-4, TIM-3, and LAG-3 have been implicated in T cell exhaustion and dysfunction within the tumor microenvironment [ ]. Targeting these pathways through genetic engineering or antibody-based approaches can rejuvenate CAR-T cells, improving their persistence and antitumor activity in solid tumors [ , ].
In preclinical studies, a prominent approach involves utilizing CRISPR/Cas9 technology to enhance CAR-T cell function by silencing PD-1 expression, thereby mitigating CAR-T cell exhaustion and improving their persistence within tumors [ , ]. Complementary to this, other research has modified CAR-T cells to secrete a single-chain variable fragment (scFv) that blocks PD-1 locally within the tumor. This strategy ensures CAR-T cells are protected from PD-1 inhibition at the tumor site, maximizing efficacy while minimizing potential systemic side effects [ ]. Furthermore, Mitchell Ho et al. developed a PD-L1-targeted shark-derived CAR-T structure that disrupts PD-1/PD-L1 interactions, showing promising tumor regression effects in hepatocellular carcinoma models [ ]. Peng Li et al. further advanced these techniques by designing chimeric switch-type receptors for PD-L1 that convert inhibitory signals into activation signals, effectively enhancing CAR-T cell responses upon engagement with PD-1/PD-L1 [ ]. Encouragingly, in a clinical trial for lymphoma, non-viral site-specific integration of PD-1-CAR-T cells achieved an objective response rate (ORR) of 100% and a complete response rate (CR) of 85.7% [ ]. Although solid tumors have not yet seen such progress, future developments suggest similar outcomes may be possible.
Beyond PD-1, targeting additional immune checkpoints—such as LAG-3, TIM-3, and CTLA-4—has shown promise in augmenting CAR-T cell function [ , ]. For example, Carl H. June and colleagues utilized CRISPR-Cas9 to deplete CTLA-4 in CAR-T cells, leading to sustained CAR expression, increased T cell proliferation, and improved antitumor efficacy in preclinical models of leukemia and multiple myeloma [ ]. In another study, Joseph A. Fraietta et al. silenced transcription factors BLIMP1 and NR4A3, reducing TIM-3 expression on CAR-T cells, which subsequently increased tumor infiltration and reduced CAR-T cell senescence [ ]. In contrast, LAG-3 has been confirmed in numerous preclinical studies that blocking it can support anti-cancer immune responses and significantly inhibit tumor growth [ – ]. But recent studies have shown that LAG-3 therapy works better in combination with other therapies. For example, combining it with PD-1, a targeted immune checkpoint, to co-activate the immune response, has shown stronger therapeutic effects [ – ].
These findings underscore the potential of immune checkpoint targeting to mitigate CAR-T cell exhaustion and enhance effector functions [ ]. Recent advancements emphasize the importance of targeting immune checkpoints to counteract CAR-T cell exhaustion and enhance their efficacy. Newly identified checkpoints, such as NKG2A ligands [ ], TIGIT [ ], B7-H6 ligands [ ], and Galectin 3 [ ], offer novel opportunities for improving CAR-T therapies. Genetic engineering strategies focused on modulating these inhibitory pathways are poised to enhance antitumor immunity, contributing to more effective and durable therapeutic outcomes.

## Cytokine and chemokine augmentation
Cytokines and chemokines are essential modulators of T cell activation, expansion, and migration, making them valuable targets for enhancing CAR-T cell therapies. Genetic engineering strategies aimed at augmenting cytokine secretion or chemokine receptor expression have shown promise in overcoming the immunosuppressive barriers of the solid tumor microenvironment [ – ].
IL-12, a key pro-inflammatory cytokine, modulates the tumor microenvironment by activating cytotoxic T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, thereby amplifying antitumor responses [ , ]. In preclinical studies, CAR-T cells engineered to secrete IL-12 upon binding to the tumor demonstrated enhanced pro-inflammatory responses and superior tumor clearance capabilities. As evidenced by the design of Hinrich Abken and Hollie J. Pegram, this technology effectively overcame regulatory T cell-mediated suppression, achieving systemic clearance of B cell lymphoma [ ]. IL-2 and IL-15 are similarly critical, enhancing T cell proliferation and persistence [ ]. CAR-T cells designed to express IL-2 exhibit improved value-added viability and infiltration capacity [ ]. Furthermore, CAR-T cells engineered to co-express IL-7 and CCL19 have shown remarkable tumor clearance and resistance to lymphodepletion [ , ], while CAR-T cells engineered to secrete the cytokine IL-36γ can activate endogenous antigen-presenting cells and T cells, contributing to the development of secondary antitumor responses and exhibiting significantly improved expansion and persistence of CAR-T cells [ ]. Additionally, CAR-T cells which co-expressing IL-7 and CCL21 improved the efficacy of CAR-T cells in solid tumors without pre-emptive clearance of lymphocytes [ ]. Li Tang et al. combined mouse IL-4 with a mutated IgG2a Fc fragment (Fc-IL-4) to activate the STAT6 signaling pathway and the PI3K-AKT-mTOR axis, which enhanced the metabolism and viability of CD8+T cells, and achieved complete tumor clearance of 75% of mice in a relapsed leukemia model [ ].
For a long time, cytokines like IL-4 have been considered less friendly to cell therapy, but Rong Fan et al. demonstrated in a clinical trial of 82 pediatric patients and 6 healthy donors that the addition of IL-4 to the cell product manufacturing process or pre-treatment of prepared CAR-T cell products with IL-4 prior to infusion restored the effect of IL-4 in CAR-T cells with low type II cellular function. It showed antitumor effect with five patients exceeding 8 years of complete remission CR [ ]. Co-expression of IL-15, as demonstrated in clinical trials (e.g., NCT04377932), has led to superior tumor trafficking and peak expansion in GPC3 CAR-T cells [ ]. Beyond these, cytokines such as IL-27, IL-10, and IL-23 are emerging as promising candidates to synergize with CAR-T cells by promoting T cell proliferation and differentiation [ , , ]. These advancements underscore the versatility of cytokine engineering in optimizing CAR-T cell therapy for solid tumors, offering new avenues for addressing key therapeutic challenges.
In parallel, chemokine engineering has gained attention as a mean to improve CAR-T cell homing and infiltration within the TME. Tumors often exhibit abnormal chemokine gradients that hinder effective T cell migration. To counteract this, CAR-T cells have been engineered to express chemokine receptors matching the tumor’s chemokine profile [ – ]. For instance, CAR-T cells co-expressing CXCR5 or CXCR2 have shown enhanced homing to tumors and increased antitumor efficacy in preclinical studies [ ]. Similarly, mesothelin-specific CAR-T cells expressing CCR4 achieved heightened tumor specificity and pro-inflammatory cytokine production in mesothelioma and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [ ]. The expression of CX3CR1 on CAR-T cells has been shown to facilitate migration toward CX3CL1-expressing tumors, resulting in significant tumor regression [ ].
Collectively, these advances in cytokine and chemokine augmentation highlight their potential to overcome the barriers limiting CAR-T cell effectiveness, particularly in the treatment of solid tumors.

## In situ manipulation to enhance CAR-T efficacy
In situ manipulation strategies represent a transformative approach to overcoming the inherent limitations of CAR-T cell therapy in solid tumors, offering localized and sustained immune responses within the tumor microenvironment.

## Localized delivery of CAR-T cells
Localized delivery of CAR-T cells offers significant therapeutic advantages in solid tumor settings by directly addressing the limitations of systemic administration. These limitations include inefficient trafficking to tumor sites, off-target cytotoxicity, and reduced antigen specificity. By focusing on site-specific delivery, localized approaches aim to enhance CAR-T cell retention and functionality within the TME, thereby maximizing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicities [ , ].
Hydrogels have emerged as a versatile platform for CAR-T cell delivery due to their tunable physical and chemical properties, which can mimic the extracellular matrix (ECM) and provide a supportive microenvironment for T cell activation and proliferation. Their ability to encapsulate CAR-T cells alongside cytokines or therapeutic agents enhances localized immune responses and promotes sustained antitumor activity. In a series of preliminary clinical investigations, Eric A. Appel et al. developed a bespoke hydrogel that was integrated with CAR-T cells and cytokines. This integration resulted in a substantial enhancement in CAR-T cell proliferation and a notable promotion in systemic cancer cell elimination [ ]. Similarly, Zhuang Liu et al. utilized freeze-dried alginate hydrogels for dual delivery of metformin and CAR-T cells, leveraging metformin’s oxidative inhibitory effect on tumor cells to strengthen antitumor responses [ ]. In another study, Zhen Gu et al. created a biodegradable hydrogel reservoir encapsulating CAR-T cells targeting chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan 4 and anti-PD-L1 antibody, providing an optimized environment for CAR-T cell activity [ ]. These hydrogel-based delivery methods not only offer conducive environments for CAR-T cells but also help mitigate adverse reactions associated with CAR-T therapy.
Beyond hydrogels, other biomaterials are also being explored to improve CAR-T cell delivery and localization. Zhen Gu et al. transformed lyophilized lymph nodes into highly porous structures capable of accommodating T cells through a lyophilization process that facilitated efficient loading of CAR T cell suspensions [ , ]. Carl H June et al. utilized fibrin glue—a strong sealant and hemostatic agent—to deliver mesothelin-targeting CAR-T cells, effectively clearing residual cancer cells in xenograft models of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDA) and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) following incomplete tumor resections [ ]. Michael E. Coon et al. used a nickel-titanium thin film loaded with CAR-T cells that went directly to the tumor site, achieving a 232-fold increase in CAR-T cell peak density within tumor tissues compared to traditional T cell injections and resulting in 70% tumor clearance in treated animals [ ]. The novel methods and technologies described above have made significant progress in preclinical studies, and we look forward to seeing the results of clinical trials as soon as possible.

## In vivo generation of CAR-T cells
The in vivo generation of CAR-T cells marks a paradigm shift in immunotherapy, enabling the direct engineering of T cells within the patient’s body. This approach bypasses traditional ex vivo manufacturing processes—such as cell harvesting, genetic modification, and expansion—thereby reducing production time, minimizing costs, and simplifying clinical implementation.
In preclinical studies, Haig Aghajanian has developed a CAR-T cell therapy that has been modified to be transiently engineered. This therapy is generated in vivo through the injection of LNP-delivered mRNA, which effectively reprograms T cells to recognize cardiac fibrotic cells. The therapeutic intervention resulted in a reduction in fibrosis and a restoration of cardiac function in a mouse model of heart failure. This approach provides a new path to breakthroughs in CAR-T cell therapy [ ]. One of the most groundbreaking developments was announced by Shon Green et al., who introduced the VivoVec platform, a lentiviral vector-based system for in vivo T cell engineering [ ]. In preclinical studies, VivoVec enabled CAR-T cell generation in non-human primates, with engineered CAR-T cells persisting for over a month. Notably, on June 11, 2024, the platform achieved a significant milestone: VivoVec successfully cleared B cells in three non-human primates within seven days of infusion, with CAR-T cells comprising up to 65% of the total T cell population by day 10 [ ]. INT2104 is a novel in vivo CAR gene therapy that generates effector CAR-T and CAR-NK cells in vivo, targeting CD20-positive B cells for the treatment of B cell malignancies. Recent clinical trials have shown that INVISE, the first human Phase 1 clinical trial of INT2104, has completed the treatment of the world’s first patient, with systemic administration via intravenous infusion, without the need for lymphatic filtration or any special facilities or equipment. (e.g., NCT06539338).
Further innovations are expanding the toolkit for localized CAR-T cell generation. Jennifer A. Michael J Mitchell et al. developed an ionizable lipid nanoparticle (LNP) platform for simultaneous expression of therapeutic gene expression and RNA interference (RNAi)-mediated transient gene knockdown in T cells, and successfully constructed transiently PD-1-inhibited CAR-T cells in multiple platforms [ ]. Jennifer A Doudna et al. leveraged antibody-fragment-displaying, membrane-derived vesicles to deliver CRISPR-Cas9 components selectively to targeted immune cells. This technology facilitates precise in vivo genome editing through antibody-antigen interactions, enabling transient CAR-T cell production [ ]. Ying Liu et al. also have engineered toroidal-spiral particles (TSPs) that can encapsulate and deliver CAR-T cells directly to the tumor site, improving the cell’s ability to infiltrate and attack the cancer while reducing the systemic side effects often associated with traditional intravenous infusion methods [ ].The use of these materials as a localized delivery and generation device showcases an innovative strategy to improve the efficacy and safety of CAR-T cell therapies. (Fig.  ). 

## Modulating tumor cell components to boost CAR-T efficacy
The TME hampers CAR-T therapy by creating physical, vascular, and immune barriers. We summarized advanced strategies aimed at overcoming these obstacles by targeting critical TME components: cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), tumor vasculature, and immunosuppressive cells (Fig.  ). By modulating these factors, we seek to enhance CAR-T cell infiltration, restore antitumor immunity, and ultimately improve therapeutic outcomes in solid tumors. 

## Targeting CAFs
Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) contribute significantly to the formation of a dense ECM, which acts as a physical barrier impeding CAR-T cell infiltration in solid tumors. CAFs also play a key role in shaping the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by promoting tumor growth, angiogenesis, and immune evasion through the secretion of cytokines and growth factors [ ]. Fibroblast activation protein (FAP), a marker predominantly expressed by CAFs, has emerged as a promising therapeutic target. FAP promotes tumor progression by remodeling the ECM, facilitating angiogenesis, and suppressing antitumor immune responses. Targeting FAP can disrupt these processes, thereby enhancing CAR-T cell infiltration and efficacy in solid tumors [ ]. Consequently, targeting FAP has become a focus for removing these barriers and enhancing CAR-T cell efficacy [ ]. Several preclinical studies have investigated the ability of FAP-targeted therapies to enhance the infiltration and efficacy of CAR-T cells in solid tumors.
Astero Klampatsa et al. constructed FAP-targeted CAR-T cells in a pancreatic cancer model, observing a breakdown of the desmoplastic stroma and increased CAR-T cell recruitment to the tumor core [ ]. Meanwhile, Steven M Albelda. et al. developed a retroviral CAR construct targeting FAP in mice, demonstrating the safety and effectiveness of FAP-targeted CAR-T cells in reducing tumor growth within the tumor stroma[ ]. Furthermore, Saad S. Kenderian’s research in multiple myeloma (MM) highlighted that simultaneously targeting malignant plasma cells and CAFs can overcome MM’s resistance to CAR-T cells [ ]. These findings suggest that targeting CAFs, particularly through FAP, can effectively dismantle ECM barriers, thereby improve CAR-T cell infiltration and enhance therapeutic outcomes in solid tumors.

## Targeting tumor vasculature
The tumor vasculature serves as a critical barrier to CAR-T cell infiltration, with abnormal blood vessels contributing to hypoxia, immune evasion, and reduced drug delivery. Targeting tumor endothelial cells offers a dual benefit: disrupting the blood supply to induce tumor cell death and facilitating CAR-T cell infiltration by normalizing the vasculature [ ].
Early studies have shown that targeting VEGF receptors 1, -2 and-3 (VEGFR1, VEGFR2, VEGFR3) with CAR-T significantly inhibited the growth of different vascularized solid tumor types and prolonged survival in mice [ ]. Steven A. Rosenberg et al. developed CAR-T cells targeting VEGFR-2, achieving significant growth inhibition in five different types of murine vascularized tumors [ ]. Furthermore, co-targeting VEGFR2 along with tumor antigens using CAR-T cells has shown enhanced antitumor responses, suggesting the synergistic potential of dual-targeting strategies [ ]. The clinical application of bevacizumab, an anti-VEGF antibody, has offered a complementary approach. Bevacizumab induces vascular normalization, which enhances CAR-T cell infiltration. For example, combining bevacizumab with anti-GD2 CAR-T cells significantly improved their penetration into solid tumors, resulting in enhanced antitumor efficacy121. Prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA), which is expressed on the tumor vasculature of many solid tumors, has also emerged as a valuable target. Clinical trials demonstrated that PSMA-targeted CAR-T cells achieved more than a 30% reduction in prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels while improving CAR-T cell expansion and trafficking [ ]. Additionally, Steven P. Lee et al. identified CLEC14a, a glycoprotein highly expressed in the tumor vascular system, as a novel target. CLEC14a-specific CAR-T cells showed strong antitumor effects in three pancreatic cancer mouse models, further supporting the feasibility of vascular-targeted CAR-T therapies [ ].

## Targeting immune-suppressive cells
Myeloid-derived suppressor cells [MDSCs) and regulatory T cells (Tregs) are major immunosuppressive components of the TME, contributing to CAR-T cell dysfunction and tumor immune evasion. MDSCs inhibit both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell functions through the production of reactive oxygen species, nitric oxide, and immunosuppressive cytokines. Meanwhile, Tregs express inhibitory markers such as CTLA-4, PD-1, and LAG-3, further suppressing immune responses and promoting tumor progression [ ].
Dmitry Gabrilovich et al. showed that ATRA reduced the number of immature myeloid cells, significantly improved the immune response of CAR-T cells, and prolonged the survival time of mice [ ]. The use of all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) can reduce a patient’s MDSC cells by differentiating immature myeloid cells into mature dendritic cells [ ]. Targeting Treg cells, J Shimizu et al. showed that an anti-CD25 antibody depleting CD25+Treg cells in hormonal mice increases tumor-infiltrating CD8+T cells and enhances tumor eradication [ ].
Tregs are the main coordinator of immunosuppression in TME, inhibiting the efficiency of immune effectors by producing TGF-β (transforming growth factor-β) [ ]. Tregs also express key immune checkpoint molecules such as CTLA-4 and PD-1, making them attractive targets for therapeutic intervention. In preclinical studies, antibodies targeting CTLA-4 have been shown to enhance CAR-T cell-mediated antitumor responses, suggesting a promising strategy for overcoming Treg-mediated immune suppression [ ]. Aaron E Foster et al. removed the immunogenic hemagglutinin tag by modifying the dominant-negative TGF-β type II receptor (DNRII) to dramatically elevate resistance to tumor-derived TGF-β [ ] while other strategies include modifying the CAR-T cells themselves to convert inhibitory signals into activating signals. Alternatively, the CRISPR/Cas9 system was employed to block PD-1/PD-L1 signaling between CAR-T cells and tumor cells, as described above, which has been shown to not only improve the infiltration capacity of CAR-T cells, but also enhance their expansion capacity [ ]. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), particularly those of the M2 phenotype, also play a critical role in tumor progression and immune suppression [ ]. Daniel J Powell Jr et al. designed FRβ-CAR-T cells targeting M2-type macrophages in TAMs and showed that this targeting of immunosuppressive cells for removal can be, and is, achieved with significant antitumor efficacy [ ]. Additionally, the myeloid-trigger receptor 2 (TREM2), expressed predominantly on TAMs, is implicated in immune suppression [ ]. Xi Huang et al. developed a bispecific antibody (PD-1-TREM2 scFv), which, when expressed by CEA CAR-T cells, enhanced tumor tissue penetration and reprogrammed the TME to improve CAR-T cell efficacy, particularly in colorectal cancer models [ ].

## Combining CAR-T with other immunotherapies and strategies
Given the immunosuppressive nature of the tumor microenvironment and the potential for adaptive resistance, combination therapies integrating CAR-T cells with other immunomodulatory strategies have emerged as a promising avenue to enhance therapeutic efficacy. Rational combination therapies, including the use of oncolytic viruses (OVs), immune checkpoint inhibitors, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and target inhibitors, are being explored to augment the efficacy of CAR-T cell therapy [ – ]. (Fig.  ). 

## Oncolytic virus therapy
Ovs selectively target and lyse tumor cells, exploiting the vulnerabilities of cancer cells with specific oncogenic mutations. These viruses exploit cancer-specific vulnerabilities, inducing immunogenic cell death and releasing tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) that activate immune responses. Moreover, OVs can be engineered to express cytokines or chemokines that promote CAR-T cell recruitment, proliferation, and persistence [ ]. Richard Vile et al. were among the first to systematically investigate the interplay between OVs and CAR-T cells. For example, while lyssaviruses promoted inflammatory responses and antitumor immunity, the vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) expressing IFN-β induced type I interferon responses that caused T cell exhaustion, including in CAR-T cells [ ]. The study provides insights for subsequent scientists to further investigate combination therapy between CAR-T cells and lysosomal viruses. In a notable lymphoma study, Stephen J. Russell et al. engineered vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) to express the chemokine CXCL9, which significantly enhanced CAR-T cell infiltration and antitumor efficacy in preclinical models of multiple myeloma. This study highlights the potential of OVs to improve CAR-T cell trafficking and overcome the physical barriers of solid tumors [ ]. Other OVs, like Lysozyme VSV and Eutherozyme Virus, are also employed to remodel the TME, aiding in T cell recruitment and CAR-T cell efficacy [ ].
To maximize synergistic effects with CAR-T cells, ovarian cancer viruses were genetically engineered in preclinical studies to express cytokines that support T cell proliferation and long-term survival [ ]. For instance, Akseli Hemminki et al. constructed an oncolytic herpes simplex virus (HSV) engineered to express IL-12 and IL-4 in glioblastoma has been shown to promote the proliferation of CAR-T cells [ ]. In addition, Akseli Hemminki et al. created an oncolytic adenovirus encoding TNF-α and IL-2, which not only improves CAR-T cell activation but also reduces treatment-related toxicities [ ]. In cases where tumors lack CAR-T target antigens, innovative OV-based strategies have been developed. Steven M Albelda et al. created a lytic virus that expresses a truncated, non-signaling form of CD19 (CD19t), enabling tumor-selective targeting by CAR-T cells [ ]. This strategy has demonstrated effectiveness in various cancers, including triple-negative breast cancer, pancreatic cancer, and ovarian cancer [ ]. Additionally, Stephen H. Thorne et al. modified an oncolytic vaccinia virus to deliver CXCL11, a chemokine enhancing T cell infiltration in mesothelioma, thereby boosting CAR-T efficacy [ ]. These examples highlight the synergy between CAR-T therapy and OVs, offering a potent combined approach to cancer treatment.

## Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors
Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed cancer therapy by restoring T cell activity through the modulation of co-inhibitory and co-stimulatory signaling pathways [ ]. ICIs targeting molecules such as CTLA-4, PD-1, and PD-L1—approved by regulatory bodies like the FDA and NMPA—are widely employed to rejuvenate T cells suppressed by the tumor microenvironment [ ]. Exhausted CAR-T cells often express elevated levels of inhibitory receptors such as PD-1, TIM-3, and LAG-3, which can dampen their efficacy [ , ].Integrating ICI treatment with CAR-T therapy can reverse this CAR-T cell suppression, creating a synergistic effect [ – ]. Clinical studies have shown that adding PD-1 blockers to CAR-T therapy in patients with suboptimal responses to CD19 CAR-T therapy leads to improved outcomes and enhanced CAR-T cell persistence [ ]. Several preclinical and clinical trials have indicated that combining PD-L1/PD-1 blockade with CAR-T therapy synergistically improves treatment durability and efficacy [ – ].

## Tumor antigen vaccines
Tumor antigen vaccines represent a complementary strategy to CAR-T cell therapy, aiming to enhance antigen presentation and T cell activation. These vaccines can be based on proteins, RNA, DNA, or viral vectors and are designed to stimulate the immune system to recognize tumor-specific antigens [ ]. When combined with CAR-T cells, tumor antigen vaccines can boost T cell infiltration, improve antigen specificity, and enhance overall therapeutic efficacy [ ]. A notable example of this synergy is the study by Darrell J. Irvine, where the Amph-pepVIII vaccine demonstrated the ability to recruit and activate dendritic cells, thereby improving tumor antigen uptake and presentation. This enhanced antigen priming synergistically boosted the effects of CAR-T therapy in preclinical models [ ]. In another clinical trial, patients with relapsed or refractory advanced solid tumors positive for CLDN6 received a low dose of CLDN6-CAR-T cells followed by a CARvac vaccine. This combination achieved notable efficacy and safety, demonstrating the potential of antigen vaccines to complement CAR-T cell therapy [ ].

## Radiotherapy and chemotherapy
Radiotherapy is renowned for its dual role in cancer treatment: directly inducing tumor cell apoptosis while releasing tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which collectively stimulate specific immune responses against solid tumors [ ]. Clinical evidence suggests that administering radiotherapy before CAR-T cell infusion not only reduces tumor burden at primary sites but also enhances T cell trafficking to the tumor microenvironment [ ]. Furthermore, this approach is associated with milder cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and reduced neurotoxicity compared to treatments excluding radiotherapy [ ]. Patrick Roth et al. demonstrated enhanced long-term persistence and activity of CAR-T cells when combined with radiotherapy in a glioblastoma model [ ]. Systemic antitumor effects have also been observed; for example, in BCMA CAR-T clinical trials, no recurrences were reported within nine months of combination therapy [ ]. Similarly, a Phase II trial for B cell lymphoma reported a complete remission in a relapsed patient following radiotherapy, with a concurrent increase in CAR-T cell counts [ ].
Chemotherapy, another cornerstone of cancer treatment, is frequently employed as a preconditioning regimen prior to CAR-T cell infusion. Agents such as fludarabine and cyclophosphamide deplete existing lymphocytes to create a niche for CAR-T expansion while reducing immunosuppressive cell populations, including Tregs [ , ]. Furthermore, chemotherapy drugs like doxorubicin, fluorouracil, gemcitabine, and docetaxel selectively target immunosuppressive cells, thereby enhancing the efficacy of CAR-T therapy [ ]. Chemotherapy has also been shown to improve CAR-T cell infiltration into tumors. For instance, temozolomide and dacarbazine were found to increase the expression of chemokines such as CCL5, CXCL9, and CXCL10, facilitating T cell recruitment [ ]. Preclinical and clinical studies consistently demonstrate that combining chemotherapy or radiotherapy with CAR-T therapy yields superior antitumor effects compared to CAR-T therapy alone [ ].

## Discussion
As detailed in this review, substantial advancements have been made in overcoming the challenges posed by solid tumors through innovative CAR-T cell modifications and synergistic combination therapies. In Table  , we summarize the key outcomes and ongoing global clinical trials focused on CAR-T therapies for solid tumors. However, there remains a critical gap between the exceptional success of CAR-T therapies in hematological malignancies and their therapeutic potential in solid tumors. To translate these promising therapies into clinical practice more efficiently and ensure broader patient benefit, several key challenges must be addressed. One major bottleneck is the personalized and time-intensive manufacturing process of CAR-T cells, which limits scalability and timely patient access. Additionally, other essential questions, such as optimizing tumor-specific targeting and overcoming the suppressive tumor microenvironment, are actively being explored to enhance the clinical efficacy of CAR-T therapies for solid tumors. The current paradigm involves the autologous preparation of CAR-T cells, which can delay treatment for patients with rapidly progressing cancers. The development of "off-the-shelf" allogeneic CAR-T cells offers a promising solution to this limitation. These cells, derived from healthy donors or iPSCs, could streamline production and improve accessibility [ ]. Recent advances, such as Richard J O’Reilly et al.’s work with iPSC-derived oligodendrocyte progenitor cells, highlight the potential for scalable and low-toxicity cell therapies [ ].
 

## CAR-T efficacy prediction and toxicity management
Iven the risks and costs faced by patients receiving CAR-T therapy, biomarkers have emerged as a pressing imperative in prognosticating the efficacy of CAR-T therapy. Common methods to predict the efficacy of CAR-T include in vitro detection of CD4+/CD8+ ratio, levels of serum IL-2 or IFN-γ [ ]. Elevated serum IL-15 concentrations were reported to be correlated with the efficacy of CAR-T cells [ – ]. Immune synapse (IS) quality is defined as the interaction between immune cells and tumor cells, serving as a biomarker for CAR-T cell-mediated efficacy [ ]. Xiong et al. developed a novel approach to predict the effectiveness of CAR-modified cells by quantifying CAR-mediated immune synapse quality, potentially introducing a new parameter in the field of immunotherapy [ ]. CAR density is also reported to play a pivotal role in CAR-T cell activity, exerting a considerable influence on clinical response [ ]. In recent times, the advancement and widespread implementation of artificial intelligence (AI) have heralded a fresh metamorphosis in the domain of life sciences, encompassing the realm of CAR-T therapy. The screening of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) libraries, in conjunction with the utilization of machine learning, can guide the design and development of CAR-T cells, showcasing the desired phenotypic characteristics of CAR-T cells [ ]. A study employed AI to predict the manifestation of CRS in individuals undergoing CAR-T therapy [ ]. AI can assist in screening patients, designing trials, predicting patients likely to experience serious adverse event, and thus supporting clinical decision-making. Meanwhile bacteria-mediated cancer immunotherapy (BCIT) has made significant progress in cancer immunotherapy. Some bacterial strains have been tested in Phase 1–2 clinical trials, expanding the use of CAR cell therapy in solid tumors [ , ].
As more and more patients are treated with CAR-T cell therapy, more attention is being paid to managing the toxicity of this therapy. Management of acute toxicities like CRS and ICANS are effectively controlled, but management of rare toxicities like haematopenia and tumor lysis syndrome still require close attention [ ]. In future studies, in addition to the single-minded enhancement of the antitumor effect of CAR-T therapy, researchers should pay more attention to how to prevent and resolve the adverse reactions associated with CAR-T cell therapy without compromising the efficacy.

## Universal and off-the-shelf CAR-T
To overcome the manufacturing delays, variability, and cost associated with autologous CAR-T—limitations that are particularly detrimental for rapidly progressing solid tumors—universal and off-the-shelf platforms have gained increasing traction. Genome-edited allogeneic CAR-T has established clinical feasibility with TRAC-edited products demonstrating antitumor activity and manageable GVHD-sparing profiles in early trials [ ]. iPSC-derived NK and T cell products have also entered clinical testing, offering scalable, standardized cell sources with favorable early safety and preliminary efficacy signals [ , ]. However, translating these platforms to solid tumors requires overcoming additional hurdles not seen in hematologic settings. Host-versus-graft immune rejection remains a major barrier that limits persistence and therapeutic exposure; emerging strategies such as HLA-editing or immune-cloaking genomic modifications may extend persistence without full lymphodepletion [ ]. Moreover, solid tumors impose heightened requirements for chemotaxis, stromal penetration, and metabolic endurance, indicating that universal CAR-T for solid tumors may require multi-module engineering rather than simple allogeneic conversion. At a regulatory level, recent guidance has emphasized risk-proportionate long-term follow-up, genome-editing safety monitoring, and insertion-site characterization following rare reports of secondary T cell malignancies associated with integrating vectors [ ]. Thus, while universal platforms hold the greatest promise for scalable access, their success in solid malignancies will depend on simultaneously resolving immunologic compatibility and TME-specific functional constraints [ ].

## Safety oversight
To address safety as a primary translational concern, we now explicitly summarize current regulatory expectations and evidence that bear on solid tumor CAR-T development. The FDA’s guidance documents recommend risk-proportionate long-term follow-up for gene-modified cell products and provide CAR-T–specific considerations on manufacturing, vector qualification, replication-competent retrovirus testing, and product characterization [ ] [ ]. We emphasize that integrating vectors (gamma-retroviral and lentiviral) remain widely used because they support durable CAR expression, but their insertion-site preferences require product-specific insertion-site mapping and extended surveillance in clinical programs, particularly in light of recent analyses and case reports of secondary T cell malignancies following commercial CAR-T products [ ] [ ] [ ]. At the same time, the well-described syndromes of CRS and ICANS [ ] [ ] should not be assumed to present identically in solid tumor indications: heterogeneous antigen distribution, locoregional delivery, and cytokine sequestration by the tumor microenvironment can alter incidence, timing and clinical phenotype.
Building on this evidence base, we provide concise, indication-aware recommendations for early-phase solid tumor trials. Trialists should implement tumor site-adapted monitoring (organ-specific safety panels and focused neurologic assessments for CNS programs), prespecify conservative dose-escalation and stopping rules, and require product-appropriate RCR/RCA testing and insertion-site analyses when integrating systems are used. Where feasible, consider transient or non-integrating delivery modalities and orthogonal safety switches to reduce theoretical long-term genotoxic risk, and explicitly label statements as clinical versus preclinical when extrapolating hematologic CAR-T experience to solid tumors. These measures align with evolving regulatory expectations and recent literature on CAR-T toxicities and secondary malignancy risk, and they underscore that CRS, TLS, ICANS and IEC-HS must be managed with indication-specific vigilance rather than presumed to be fully generalizable.

## Antigen selection and heterogeneity
Unlike hematologic malignancies such as BCL or MM, which typically express lineage-restricted and relatively homogeneous surface antigens (e.g., CD19, BCMA), solid tumors are characterized by profound intratumorally and interpatient heterogeneity, dynamic antigen loss, and spatially compartmentalized expression patterns. This complexity often leads to incomplete targeting and antigen escape following single-antigen CAR-T therapy. To address these barriers, several complementary strategies have emerged. First, multi-target and logic-gated CAR designs (dual, tandem, or AND/OR configurations) improve tumor coverage while reducing on-target/off-tumor toxicity [ ]. Second, combinatorial approaches that integrate CAR-T with bispecific antibodies or oncolytic platforms can transiently broaden antigen recognition and promote epitope spreading [ ]. Third, synthetic biology tools enable tunable antigen density sensing or sequential antigen recognition to adaptively modulate CAR signaling [ ]. Fourth, precision target discovery using single-cell and spatial transcriptomics has revealed promising tumor-restricted candidates such as CLDN18.2, GPC3, [ ], and MUC16ecto, several of which are under early-phase clinical evaluation [ ] .Finally, local or regional delivery and on-demand CAR activation platforms can further mitigate off-tumor risk in antigens with partial normal-tissue expression [ ]. Together, these multi-antigen and context-aware engineering strategies offer a rational path toward overcoming antigen heterogeneity and improving precision and durability of CAR-T therapy in solid tumors.

## Prioritized design principles and an “ideal” CAR-T candidate for solid tumors
The strategies discussed in this review span multiple layers of CAR-T design, from gene transfer platforms and synthetic biology circuits to spatial delivery and combination regimens. For solid tumors, not all parameters are of equal importance. A pragmatic prioritization emerges from available preclinical and early clinical data. First, spatial access to the lesion—including chemokine-guided trafficking, locoregional or depot-based delivery, and modulation of ECM/CAF barriers—represents a primary bottleneck; without sufficient intratumoral accumulation, even optimally engineered CAR-T cells cannot exert durable control. Second, once CAR-T cells reach the tumor, functional persistence and resistance to exhaustion become decisive, favoring designs that combine calibrated co-stimulation and ITAM signaling with metabolic and cytokine circuits that preserve stem-like memory pools. Third, antigen recognition and safety logic must address antigen heterogeneity and on-target/off-tumor risks through dual-targeting, conditional activation, or safety switches. Fourth, TME reprogramming and rational combinations (e.g., with checkpoint inhibitors, oncolytic viruses, radiotherapy, or metabolic modulators) can convert an immune-excluded niche into a permissive one and broaden the impact of CAR-T beyond the initially targeted clone. Finally, manufacturability and delivery format—including non-viral and in vivo programming platforms—determine how feasibly these designs can be deployed at scale and repeatedly dosed in patients. In conclusion, CAR-T cell therapy holds immense promise for the treatment of solid tumors. By addressing the unique challenges posed by the tumor microenvironment and optimizing combination therapies, we can further improve the clinical application of CAR-T cells. Future research should focus on developing off-the-shelf CAR-T products, identifying predictive biomarkers, and utilizing AI to enhance therapy safety and efficacy. With continued advancements, CAR-T cell therapy could become a cornerstone treatment modality for solid tumors, offering new hope for cancer patients worldwide.
