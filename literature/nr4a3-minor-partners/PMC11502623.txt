## Abstract
Thermogenetics is a promising neuromodulation technique based on the use of heat-sensitive ion channels. However, on the way to its clinical application, a number of questions have to be addressed. First, to avoid immune response in future human applications, human ion channels should be studied as thermogenetic actuators. Second, heating levels necessary to activate these channels in vivo in brain tissue should be studied and cytotoxicity of these temperatures addressed. Third, the possibility and safety of chronic neuromodulation has to be demonstrated. In this study, we present a comprehensive framework for thermogenetic neuromodulation in vivo using the thermosensitive human ion channel hTRPV1. By targeting hTRPV1 expression to excitatory neurons of the mouse brain and activating them within a non-harmful temperature range with a fiber-coupled infrared laser, we not only induced neuronal firing and stimulated locomotion in mice, but also demonstrated that thermogenetics can be employed for repeated neuromodulation without causing evident brain tissue injury. Our results lay the foundation for the use of thermogenetic neuromodulation in brain research and therapy of neuropathologies.

## Introduction
Methodological approaches providing remote and non- or minimally-invasive neuromodulation in vivo are highly solicited techniques in fundamental neuroscience and are promising tools for the treatment of various neurological states. They facilitate our understanding on how the brain works by establishing a causative link between firing or silencing of certain neuronal circuits and behavioral outcomes on model organisms. Neuromodulation therapy based on these approaches will help to restore functional brain deficits such as loss of vision or alleviate neurological states such as epileptic seizures or parkinsonian motor impairments. A range of such approaches have been developed to date. Optogenetics uses light-gated ion channels or photo-sensitive ion pumps, which, when targeted to neurons, allow for optically induced depolarization or hyperpolarization of neuronal membranes [ – ]. Chemogenetics controls neuronal firing via binding of a small biologically inert molecule (a designer drug) to designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADDs), which modulate downstream signaling pathways to increase or decrease neuronal excitability [ – ].
Although these approaches have strengths enabling them to be essential tools for demystifying how the brain works, there are several shortcomings that prevent their application in therapy of brain pathologies. Optogenetics is based on opsins originating from algae and archaea, therefore these proteins can trigger an immune response in the host organism [ ]. Yet another issue for translation of optogenetics to the clinic is the necessity to irradiate relatively large brain areas with visible light wavelengths causing phototoxicity [ ].
DREADDs are designer human muscarinic receptors. However, when using them, the effect of neuromodulation depends on the rate of designer drug penetration into the brain from the blood stream and the rate of removal/decomposition. Recently, a novel DREADD ligand, deschloroclozapine, was demonstrated to produce rapid modulation of neuronal activity and behavior in mice and monkeys, including suppression of seizures [ , ]. Such progress renders chemogenetics based on DREADDs to be a promising tool for treatment of brain pathologies in humans when neuromodulation on a minute-timescale is sufficient. However, faster dynamics and more precise spatial resolution can only be achieved by physical stimulation methods such as light or sound.
Thermogenetics, based on the use of thermosensitive ion channels belonging to the superfamily of transient receptor potential (TRP) channels, may overcome most of the issues of the above mentioned techniques. Thermosensitive TRP channels are non-selective cation channels and are primarily expressed in sensory neurons and various types of skin, inflammatory, and mucosal cells [ , ]. Their primary role is to sense diverse noxious and physiological stimuli such as high and low temperatures, mechanical impact, pH, osmolarity, and a range of biologically active compounds (primarily plant alkaloids) [ ]. Targeting thermosensitive TRP channels to central nervous system (CNS) neurons, which do not express these channels under normal conditions [ ], was found to allow for modulation of neuronal activity and animal behaviors by heating or cooling, depending on the type of TRP channel used [ – ]. Ectopically expressed thermosensitive TRP channels can be activated by changes in ambient temperature, infrared (IR) laser exposure, radio-frequency magnetic-field heating of ferrite nanoparticles attached to target cells, or even focused ultrasound (FUS) treatment [ – ].
A thermosensitive ion channel appropriate for thermogenetics in mammalian species, including humans, would pass the following criteria. It should be able to respond to temperature changes and reside in the closed state at body temperature, typically ~ 37 °C for model rodents and humans; and the thermoactivation threshold of the thermosensitive ion channel should be around 40 °C to avoid harmful heating of cells. Mammalian TRP vanilloid member 1 (TRPV1) channels with thermoactivation thresholds close to 40 °C [ – ] are viable options for thermogenetics in mammals [ , ]. The use of the human TRPV1 (hTRPV1) channel [ , ] for thermogenetic neuromodulation would facilitate translation of this highly promising neurotechnology into medicine by avoiding a host immune response. Therefore, in this work, we target hTRPV1 into excitatory neurons of the mouse CNS, stimulate them directly by a fiber-coupled IR laser, and evaluate the effects of thermal stimulation on neuronal activity, mouse locomotor behavior, and brain tissue integrity during repeated stimulation.

## Materials and methods

## Creating vectors for expression of the thermosensitive human TRPV1 channel and the genetically encoded calcium sensor GCaMP6s
The plasmid pCAGGS-hTRPV1-p2A-tdTomato, carrying an open reading frame for expression of hTRPV1 under control of the cytomegalovirus enhancer fused to the chicken beta-actin promoter (CAG), was generated in two steps. First, p2A-tdTomato fusion from eolTRPA1-p2A-tdTomato construct [ ] was cloned into the pCAG_PSD95.FingR-eGFP-CCR5TC vector (a gift from Don Arnold; Addgene plasmid #46295; http://n2t.net/addgene:46295; RRID: Addgene_46295) with SmaI and Bsp1407I restriction sites. The open reading frame of the human thermosensitive TRPV1 channel (Horizon Dicovery, # OHS5894-202501188) was used as a template in a polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with following primers: F1 5′- gaattcggtaccgcgggcccgccaccatgaagaaatggagcag-3′ and R1 5′- agttagtagctccgcttcccgccttctccccggaagcggc-3′. The PCR product was fused upstream the p2A-tdTomato using AQUA cloning technology according to conventional protocol [ ].
The plasmid pAAV2-hSyn1-GCamP6s-p2A-tdTomato (hSyn1 = human Synapsin 1 promoter, p2A = self-cleaving peptide, and tdTomato = reporter red fluorescent protein tdTomato) was assembled with NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly Cloning Kit (NEB, #E5520S) using 25 bp overlapping primers for 4 PCR fragments obtained with Q5 High-Fidelity 2X Master Mix (NEB, #M0492L) from corresponding sources. The hSyn1 505 bp promoter (human synapsin I promoter; confers neuron-specific expression [ ] was amplified from hSyn1-tdTomato plasmid (a gift from Viviana Gradinaru, Addgene plasmid #104060; http://n2t.net/addgene:104060; RRID:Addgene_104060) with hSyn1-rev 5′-gatgatgagaacccatggtggcgcctgcgctctcaggcacgac-3′ and hSyn1-frw 5′-ctattaccatggctcgagagtgcaagtgggttttaggaccag-3′ primers. An adeno-associated virus serotype 2 Amp resistant backbone of 4616 bp was amplified from plasmid pAAV2-HyPer2-DAO-NES [ ] with AAV2-rev 5′- cttgcactctcgagccatggtaatagcgatgactaatacg -3′ and AAV2-frw 5′- acaagtaatgcctcgagcagcgctgctcgagagatctacgg-3′ primers. A GCaMP6s 1350 bp fragment was amplified from plasmid Tol2-elavl3-GCaMP6 (a gift from Misha Ahrens, Addgene plasmid #59531; http://n2t.net/addgene:59531; RRID: Addgene_59531) with GCaMP6s-frw 5′- cgccaccatgggttctcatcatcatcatcatcatggtatggctagc-3′ and GCaMP6s-rev 5′ gaagttagtagccttcgctgtcatcatttgtacaaact-3′ primers. A p2A-TdTomato 1488 bp insert was amplified from previously published caTRPA1-p2A-TdTomato construct [ ] with p2A-frw59 5’-gatgacagcgaaggctactaacttcagcctgctgaagc -3′ and TdTomato-rev60 5′- ccgtagatctctcgagcagcgctgctcgaggcattacttgtacagctcgtccatgccg-3′ primers. Positive colonies were selected with colony PCR of resuspended bacterial clones with hSyn1-cl-frw 5′- tcagcactgaaggcgcgctg-3′ and pAAV2-sc-rev 5′- gggccaggagaggcact-3′, expanded, and used for maxiprep plasmid preparation with Plasmid Maxi Kit (25) (QIAGEN, #12163).
The plasmid pAAV2-CaMKIIα-hTRPV1-flag (CaMKIIα = Ca2 + /calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II alpha promoter; flag = FLAG epitope) was created using the pAAV2-hSyn1-GCamP6s-P2A-tdTomato plasmid as a backbone sequence source. The CaMKIIα promoter was amplified from pAAV-CaMKIIa-mCherry plasmid (a gift from Karl Deisseroth; Addgene plasmid #114469; http://n2t.net/addgene:114469; RRID: Addgene_114469) with F_CamkII_Mlu 5′-tttacgcgtttaacattatggccttaggtcacttc-3′ and R_CamkII_Nhe 5′-tttgctagcgctgcccccagaactagg-3′ primers. The open reading frame of the human thermosensitive TRPV1 channel (Horizon Discovery, # OHS5894-202501188) was used as a template in a PCR with following primers: hTRPV1_Fw 5′-tatatgctagcgccaccatgaagaaatggagcagcacagac-3′, which adds Kozak (protein translation initiation site) and an NheI restriction site, and hTRPV1_Rv 5′-atatagctcgaggaattcttacttatcgtcgtcgtccttgtaatccttctccccggaagcgg-3′, which adds FLAG-peptide for subsequent immunohistochemical visualization of the expressed channel, and EcoRI + PspIX restriction sites. Escherichia coli XL1-Blue strain (Evrogen, #CC001) was used for the cloning, maintenance, and propagation of plasmids.
DNA fragments extraction from agarose gel was carried out using a Cleanup Standard kit (Evrogen, #BC022S). Plasmid Midiprep 2.0 kit (Evrogen, #BC124) was used for plasmid DNA extraction. Ligation was conducted using T4-DNA ligase (Evrogen, #LK001).
The created constructs were verified by restriction analysis with appropriate restriction enzyme sets and sequencing. Midipreps were prepared using the Plasmid Midiprep 2.0 kit (Evrogen, #BC124) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Then, the constructs pAAV2-CaMKIIa-hTRPV1-flag and pAAV2-hSyn1-GCamP6s-P2A-tdTomato were packaged into adeno-associated viral (AAV) particles. The constructs were packaged in AAV serotype 9 viral particles. The virus titers were 4.4E + 12 viral genomes (VG)/mL for AAV2/9-CaMKIIa-hTRPV1-flag and 4.07E + 12 VG/mL for AAV2/9-hSyn1-GCaMP6s-p2A-tdTomato.

## Culture and transfection of HEK293TN cell line
HEK293TN cells (gender: F, RRID: CVCL_UL49) were cultured in standard 25 cm2 flasks (SPL Life Sciences, #70025) in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Paneco, #C420E) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Biosera, #FB-1001), GlutaMAX (1:100) (Gibco, #2009511) and a penicillin–streptomycin mixture (1:100) (Gibco, #15140-122) at 37 °C in the ambient atmosphere with 5% CO2. The HEK293TN cell culture was tested for mycoplasma contamination using a MycoReport kit (Evrogen, #MR001) according to manufacturer’s guidelines. For thermal stimulation and real-time calcium imaging, HEK293TN cells (60,000 cells/mL) were seeded into 35-mm plastic dishes (SPL Life Sciences, #20035) and cultured in 2 mL of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, GlutaMAX (1:100) and a penicillin–streptomycin mixture (1:100) at 37 °C in the ambient atmosphere with 5% CO2. Plastic dishes were preliminarily coated with polyethylenimine (1 mg/mL) (Sigma-Aldrich, #BCCC2235) for 30 min at room temperature, and then washed with distillated water three times. Cells were transfected with a mixture of 1.25 µg DNA and 2.5 µL FuGene HD reagent (Promega, #E2311) pre-dissolved in 150 µL Opti-MEM (Gibco, #15140-122) per dish. Experimental cells were co-transfected with pCAGGS-hTRPV1-p2A-tdTomato (750 ng per dish) and pCS2 + CMV-GCaMP6s [ ] (500 ng per dish). Plasmid pCS2 + CMV-GCaMP6s carried an open reading frame for expression of genetically encoded calcium indicator GCaMP6s [ ] under control of human cytomegalovirus immediate early enhancer and promoter (CMV). Control cells were transfected with pCS2 + CMV-GCaMP6s (1.25 µg per dish) only. Cells underwent simultaneous thermal stimulation and real-time calcium imaging 18–20 h after transfection.

## Controlled heating and real-time calcium imaging of transfected HEK293TN cells
Prior to thermal stimulation and real-time calcium imaging, culture medium was replaced with 3 mL of the pre-heated Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS) (PanEco, #P020) supplemented with 10 mM HEPES-Na (PanEco, #F134). Then, a 35 mm dish with experimental or control cells was placed onto a stage of a Nikon ECLIPSE Ti2-E epi-fluorescence microscope equipped with a SPECTRA X light engine and a Photometrics BSI camera. The fluorescent signals of GCamp6s and tdTomato (control cells expressed GCaMP6s only) were acquired using a Nikon CFI S Plan Fluor LWD 20X (NA 0.7) objective and Nikon NIS-Elements software. GCaMP6s was excited at 470 nm, and its fluorescence was acquired within the spectral range 500–530 nm. tdTomato was excited at 555 nm, and its fluorescence was acquired within the spectral range 580–610 nm. We performed time-lapse imaging with 1 s interframe intervals for the subsequent measurement of changes in fluorescence of GCaMP6s in experimental (tdTomato positive) and control cells.
Controlled heating of cells during real-time calcium imaging was carried out by a custom heater/temperature control setup connected to a computer (Suppl. Fig. A, B). A nichrome wire and a temperature sensor were placed into a plastic dish with experimental or control cells at the sites adjacent to the imaged field of view. This ensured that imaged cells are appropriately heated and temperature at the site of real-time calcium imaging is properly measured. The heater/temperature control setup was operated by a custom software that uses a proportional–integral–derivative (PID) controller algorithm (Suppl. Fig. C). The coefficients that determine how much each component of the PID controller contributes to the output heating current were adjusted empirically in preliminary experiments emulating conditions in a 35 mm dish such as liquid volume and positioning of a nichrome wire and a temperature sensor. In our experiments, optimal PID coefficients should provide fast heating of cells from the baseline temperature of 37 °C up to desired temperatures of 39, 41, and 43 °C with error not exceeding 0.5 °C.
Using the software for the heater/temperature control setup, cells in the dish were maintained at the temperature of 37 °C during 10 min prior to beginning of time-lapse imaging. Then, time-lapse imaging and controlled heating of cells got started simultaneously. Cells were heated according to the following scheme: 37 °C for 60 s, 39 °C for 30 s, 37 °C for 90 s, 41 °C for 30 s, 37 °C for 90 s, 43 °C for 30 s, and 37 °C for 90 s. Desired increases of temperature were achieved within several seconds. Cooling of cells to the basal temperature of 37 °C occurred passively. Temperature was measured with the frequency of 10 Hz and recorded in a Microsoft Excel file format (.csv).

## Mice
Experiments were carried out using C57Bl/6 J mice (The Jackson Laboratory, #000664, RRID: IMSR_JAX:000664). Mice were maintained in groups (3–4 mice per cage) in the animal facility of Shemyakin-Ovchinnikov Institute of Bioorganic Chemistry. Mice were housed at an ambient temperature 22–24 °C with a 12-h light/dark cycle and had ad libitum access to food and water. All manipulations with animals were conducted according to the European Convention for the Protection of Vertebrate Animals used for Experimental and other Scientific Purposes (1986, ETS 123) and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (approvals no. 182 and 364).

## Stereotaxic viral vector delivery and implantation of a fiber-optic interface
AAV vectors were delivered to the CA1 area of the hippocampus (4 to 6-week-old male mice) or CnF (8-week-old male mice) by standard stereotaxic surgery. Briefly, mice were deeply anesthetized with 3% isoflurane (Baxter, #5AGG9621) in an induction chamber using a low-flow anesthesia system (SomnoSuite, Kent Scientific). Then, mice were placed onto the heating pad of the stereotaxic apparatus (digital stereotaxic instrument, Stoelting Co). Anesthesia was maintained by delivery of 1.5% isoflurane via inhalation mask. Body temperature was controlled by a rectal thermometer. Prior to incision, 0.25% bupivacaine (Ozon, #LP-003590) was injected subcutaneously (the total dose was less than 8 mg/kg) at the site of incision for local analgesia. After the skin on the mouse head was incised, connective tissue was removed using sterile cotton buds, and the surface of the skull was dried to facilitate identification of the bregma. The skull surface was aligned in anteroposterior and mediolateral directions. AAV vectors were injected into the mouse brain using a Hamilton microsyringe 75RN 5 µL (Hamilton, #7634‑01) equipped with a 33-gauge needle via holes drilled in the skull at the following stereotaxic coordinates: − 3.3 mm anteroposterior, ± 3.4 mm mediolateral (both hemispheres), and − 3.9/− 3.6/− 3.3 mm dorsoventral relative to the bregma for the CA1 area of the hippocampus [ , ] (Fig.  A); and − 4.8 mm anteroposterior, 1.1 mm mediolateral (half of mice received an AAV injection into the left hemisphere, and the rest of mice received an AAV injection into the right hemisphere), and − 2.75 mm dorsoventral relative to the bregma [ , ] for the CnF (Fig.  B). The AAV vector solution was delivered at 200 nL/min. Experimental mice (hTPRV1+ mice) were injected with an AAV mixture (1:1) containing AAV2/9-CaMKIIa-hTRPV1-flag and AAV2/9-hSyn1-GCaMP6s-p2A-tdTomato. Control mice were injected with AAV2/9-hSyn1-GCaMP6s-p2A-tdTomato dissolved in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (1:1). In the hippocampus, 400 nL of an AAV vector solution was injected at each point in the dorsoventral axis. In CnF, 1000 nL of an AAV vector solution was injected at a single point in the dorsoventral axis. After the intrahippocampal injection, the holes in the skull were sealed by the dental cement, the wound was closed, and mice were placed into individual cages and allowed for recovering for at least two weeks prior to electrophysiological recordings. Immediately after an AAV vector solution injection into CnF, a fiber-optic interface with the diameter of 220 µm [ , ] was implanted slightly above the site of the AAV delivery by slow deepening to the depth of 2.65 mm relatively to bregma. The ceramic pert of fiber-optic interface was fixed at the surface of the skull by the dental cement. After surgery, animals were placed into individual cages and allowed for recovering for at least two weeks prior thermogenetic stimulation and behavior recording.
 For analgesia, mice were intraperitoneally injected with ketoprofen (5 mg/kg) (Sandoz, #N013942/01) prior to surgery, and again 24 and 48 h after surgery. The recovery period also enabled sufficient expression of hTRPV1 and GCaMP6s to be reached.

## Electrophysiology and intracellular calcium imaging in acute brain slices
Mice that received AAV vector intrahippocampal injections were deeply anesthetized with 3% isoflurane and decapitated, and the brains were isolated from skulls. Transverse hippocampal 300 μm slices were prepared from the isolated brains using a Leica VT1200S vibrating blade microtome (Leica Microsystems). The slicing chamber contained an oxygenated ice-cold solution composed of (in mM): K-Gluconate, 140; N-(2-hydroxyethyl) piperazine-N′ -ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), 10; Na-Gluconate, 15; ethylene glycol-bis (2-aminoethyl)-N, N, N′, N′-tetra-acetic acid, 0.2; and NaCl, 4; pH 7.2. Slices were incubated for 30 min at 35 °C before being stored at room temperature in ACSF containing (in mM): NaCl, 125; NaHCO3, 25; KCl, 2.5; NaH2PO4, 1.25; MgCl2, 1; CaCl2, 2; and D-glucose, 25. The ACSF was continuously bubbled with 95% O2 and 5% CO2.
For experiments, brain slices were placed in a perfusion chamber of a Scientifica SliceScopePro 2000 microscope (RRID:SCR_018405; Scientifica, UK) and were continuously perfused with the oxygenated ACSF. Patch electrodes were pulled from hard borosilicate capillary glass (Sutter P-97/PC Pipette Puller (RRID:SCR_018636)). Electrodes were filled with a solution consisting of (in mM): K-gluconate, 140; HEPES, 10; NaCl, 8; MgATP, 4; MgGTP, 0.3; and phosphocreatine, 10 (pH 7.3 with KOH). CA1 pyramidal cells were visually identified using the IR-video microscope Scientifica SliceScopePro 2000 in differential interference contrast mode.
Whole-cell recordings were conducted in ACSF with a constant temperature of 35 °C in voltage-clamp mode using a Heka Elektronik EPC 10 USB Patch Clamp Amplifier (RRID:SCR_018399) and the Patchmaster software (RRID:SCR_000034) with a sampling rate of 100 μs and filtered at 3 kHz. Patched neurons were held at − 70 mV. GCaMP6s fluorescence was acquired using a water immersion Olympus LUMPLFLN40 × W objective with numerical aperture (NA) of 0.8. Image acquisition was carried out using the Scientifica SliceScopePro 2000 microscope equipped with a CoolLED pE-300ultra (RRID:SCR_021972) light source and a Hamamatsu Orca Flash 4.0 CMOS monochrome digital camera (Hamamatsu Photonics). Fluorescence signals were acquired and analyzed with the uManager free software (RRID:SCR_000415) at 20 fps. The CoolLED pE-300ultra (RRID:SCR_021972) light source was synchronized with Heka Elektronik EPC 10 USB Patch Clamp Amplifier via BNC-TTL output. The GCaMP6s fluorescence was excited and detected via a 49054 CoolLED GFP filtercube.
To assess average elevations of membrane potential (∆Vm) during heating periods, patch-clamp traces were filtered using a low-pass cutoff frequency 1 Hz Butterworth filter in the pClamp software (RRID:SCR_011323). For counting numbers of action potentials generated during heating periods, we used Event detection function in the pClamp software.

## The optical setup for thermal stimulation of principal hippocampal neurons in acute brain slices
A fiber coupled laser diode 4PN-117 (SemiNex) with an emission wavelength of 1375 nm and power up to 4.3 W was used as a source of the IR irradiation for heating brain slices (Fig.  B). The laser diode was mounted onto a thermo-electrically controlled (TEC) plate “264 TEC HP LaserMount” (Arroyo Instruments). The TEC plate was operated by a TEC driver TECSource 5305 (A.I.). A laser diode driver LaserSource 4320 (A.I.) provided laser diode current stabilization and control. For targeting the IR laser to a desired area in an acute slice, we used a PL520 (Thorlabs) laser diode with an emission wavelength of 520 nm. The visible 520 nm laser was connected closely to the end of a 200-μm FG200UEA optical fiber with NA of 0.22 (Thorlabs) by optical glue. The IR and visible laser beams were combined into the same optical pathway using 90:10 splitters TM105R2F2B (Thorlabs) for fibers with a diameter of 105 µm, NA of 0.22, and reduced OH content. For efficient transmission of the IR laser irradiation, it was fed into the Signal channel with a 90% power transfer efficiency. The splitter’s auxiliary output was used to monitor IR laser power by a photodiode. The main channel was connected to a 2 m FG105LCA optical fiber (Thorlabs) with a diameter of 105 µm and NA of 0.22. An increased bandwidth of the fiber within the IR range provided a 100-fold reduction in energy losses. All optical elements of the fiber were securely coupled to each other through connectors. The overall IR power transfer efficiency in the optical setup was 65%. To synchronize IR laser treatments, electrophysiological recordings, and calcium imaging, the laser diode driver LaserSource 4320 used for the control of IR laser power and the CoolLED pE-300ultra light source used for excitation of the calcium biosensor GCaMP6s were connected to Heka Elektronik EPC 10 USB Patch Clamp Amplifier via a BNC analogue output and a BNC-TTL output, respectively.
The flat endface of the 2 m FG105LCA optical fiber was placed above the slice with some angle to the slice surface. The heating IR 1375 nm heating laser was targeted to a desired site in an acute brain slice using a spot of the visible 520 nm laser. The heating/cooling protocol included six sequential 20 s IR laser treatments with the 40 s intervals. IR laser power was gradually enhanced by increasing the diode current from 0.5 to 1.0 A with a 0.1 A step.
Calibration of IR laser power by determination of temperatures reached at certain IR laser diode currents was conducted by monitoring of currents flowing through open microelectrode as previously described [ ]. Briefly, the ACSF in the perfusion chamber of the Scientifica SliceScopePro 2000 microscope was preheated up to 60 °C. Temperature of the ACSF was measured using a thermistor integrated in the Scientifica SliceScopePro 2000 microscope. The thermistor was placed into the perfusion chamber. Currents (I) flowing through open microelectrode at the constant voltage of 30 mV and corresponding temperatures (T) were measured while ACSF was passively cooled from 60 to 36 °C. Measured values were then used to build an Arrhenius plot. A linear approximation (y = mx + b, where y = ln(I) and x = T−1 (in K−1)) of these data by using the least squares method allowed for determination of an Arrhenius plot slope (m). Then, the calibrated microelectrode placed in the ACSF at the temperature of 35 °C (T0 = 308.15 K), and the baseline current (I0) was measured. The microelectrode underwent 20 s IR laser treatments with 40 s intervals. IR laser power was gradually enhanced by increasing the diode current from 0.5 to 1 A with a step of 0.1 A. Maximal currents (I) flowing through the open microelectrode were measured during periods when the IR laser was on. Temperatures reached at various IR laser power were then calculated by T = [1/T0 − R/Ea × ln(I/I0)]−1 as previously described [ ]. Here, R is the gas constant, Ea is the activation energy of electrolyte, and T0 and I0 are the baseline temperature of the ACSF and the corresponding electrode current, respectively. The value of (-R/Ea) is equal to (1/m), where (m) is a slope of the Arrhenius plot.

## Analysis of calcium imaging on cultured cells and acute brain slices
The image processing Fiji software (RRID: SCR_002285) was used to create quantitative datasets for determining the kinetics of the GCaMP6s signal in transfected HEK293TN cells and transduced hippocampal neurons.
For cultured cells, widefield image files in the Nikon nd2 format were imported into the Fiji software using Bio-formats importer. Files for experimental cultured cells expressing both hTRPV1-p2A-tdTomato and GCaMP6s contained images acquired in two channels: green—GCaMP6s, and red—tdTomato, whereas control cultured cells expressing GCaMP6s contained images acquired in one channel: green – GCaMP6s. The background was subtracted straightaway using the Sliding paraboloid item with a Rolling ball radius of 50.0 pixels. Cell bodies with GCaMP6s and tdTomato fluorescence were selected using the wand tool utility. The selections were used as regions of interest (ROIs) and added to the ROI manager of Fiji software. Then, channels were split, mean values of fluorescence intensity for selected ROIs were measured, and digitized fluorescence datasets for each ROI in the green channel were exported using the Multi measure tool. To normalize GCaMP6s fluorescence intensity data for a given ROI from time-lapse imaging, the minimal fluorescence intensity was determined within the time interval from 30 to 60 s prior to the cells underwent the first heating from 37 to 39 °C. Then, GCaMP6s fluorescence intensity values at every time-point for a given ROI were divided by this minimal fluorescence intensity to determine the kinetics of the GCaMP6s signal. Individual kinetic curves were averaged and combined with temperature measurements to provide a resultant response of experimental (hTRPV1⁺) or control cells to quick heating up to 39, 41, and 43 °C from the baseline temperature of 37 °C. Heat maps were created using the free web-enabled software Heatmapper [ ].
For neurons, widefield image files in the uManager tiff format were opened in the Fiji software. A patched GCaMP6s⁺ neuron and a field of view without the fluorescent signal from neuronal cell bodies was manually selected as ROIs. The selected ROIs were added to the ROI manager of Fiji software. Then, mean values of fluorescence intensity for selected ROIs were measured, and digitized fluorescence datasets for each ROI were exported using the Multi measure tool. Signal of the background ROI was subtracted from signal of the patched GCaMP6s⁺ neuron. To determine the kinetics of the GCaMP6s signal for a given patched neuron, GCaMP6s fluorescence intensity values at every time-point were divided by its fluorescence intensity in the first image of the time stack. Individual kinetic curves were combined with electrophysiological traces and temperature measurements to provide a resultant response of hTRPV1⁺ or control neurons maintained at the baseline temperature of 35 °C to heating by IR laser treatments.

## In vivo thermogenetic stimulation and fiber photometry
Prior to the first day of thermogenetic stimulation, mice were allowed for adaptation to the experimental conditions during two consecutive days (Fig.  A). Freely moving mice were manually restrained, the long optical fiber coupled to the optical stimulation/photometry setup was connected to the implanted fiber-optic interface, and mice were then placed into an arena (40 cm × 40 cm) for a 5 min habituation (Fig.  C). This optical stimulation/photometry setup provides simultaneous the IR laser irradiation and excitation and detection of the integral GCaMP6s signal (Fig.  C).
hTPRV1+ and control mice connected to the optical stimulation/photometry setup via an optical fiber were placed in the arena and allowed for adaptation for 5 min prior to the beginning of the experiment. Then, we started video recording of mouse behavior and capturing of the integral GCaMP6s signal. Each mouse underwent three sessions of stimulation with different IR laser powers: 20, 40, and 60 mW (Fig.  D). The stimulation was performed by continuous IR laser irradiation of the brain tissue. Each stimulation session consisted of three periods when the IR laser was on. The duration of the on-periods commonly was 20 s. If we observed that a mouse ran too fast in response to stimulation and there was a risk of getting tangled up in the optical fiber, we switched off the laser prior to a 20 s stimulation period over. The on-periods were separated by the 30-s off-periods. Experimental and control mice underwent to the same stimulation protocol for three consecutive days followed by euthanasia on the next day after the last stimulation. Their brains were collected for subsequent immunohistochemical analysis.
Locomotor activity of hTPRV1+ and control mice was evaluated in captured movies. Image processing was carried out using the Bonsai software (RRID:SCR_017218). An area containing the bottom of the arena was identified on each frame of a movie and cropped into a separate image. Then, it the image was smoothed with a spatial Gaussian filter with a kernel size corresponding to a size of a mouse. The latter allowed for blurring an optical fiber coupled to a mouse to eliminate its interference with an image of a mouse. Since a mouse is much darker than the arena, we used brightness threshold to find the position of the center of mass for each animal. Then, animal’s actual speed was determined by measuring changes in this position on sequential frames using the MATLAB software (RRID:SCR_001622). A final plot of animal’s actual speed was smoothed into a 1-s window by a moving average filter.
The time-course of the integral GCaMP6s signal in the CnF of hTPRV1+ and control mice was determined as follows. On each frame, an image of the optical fiber tip was cropped into a separate image, and the GCaMP6s signal was calculated by integration of signals from individual pixels. The mean value of the integral GCaMP6s signal F0 was determined for the time interval prior to the first round when the IR laser was on for a given thermal stimulation session. Then, GCaMP6s signals for a given thermal stimulation session were calculated as (F-F0)/F0, where F is an integral GCaMP6s signal for an individual time point, to determine the time-course of the integral GCaMP6s signal.
Mean values of animal’s actual speed and the integral GCaMP6s signal were calculated separately for the periods of time when the IR laser was on and the periods of time when the IR laser was off. Averaging of individual mean values of animal’s speed and the integral GCaMP6s signal provided resultant locomotor and calcium responses to the thermal stimulation with a given IR laser power. All calculations were conducted using the MATLAB software.
The relative speed increase was determined as the ratio of the average speed during periods when the IR laser was on to the average speed during periods when the IR laser was off for individual hTPRV1+ mice. Averaging of individual values enabled us to evaluate whether the stimulation effects of IR laser treatments remained stable accross at least three consecutive stimulation days.
To assess the potential anxiolytic effect of thermogenetic activation of CnF neurons, we determined the time spent by hTRPV1⁺ mice in the central area (20 cm × 20 cm) and near the walls of the arena for the periods when the IR laser was on and the periods when the IR laser was off. Then, we calculated ratios of the time spent by mice in the central area to the time spent by mice near the walls. Averaging of individual values allowed us to elucidate whether thermogenetic stimulation produced an anxiolytic effect in mice.
To verify the specificity of thermogenetic activation, separate cohorts of hTRPV1⁺ and control mice underwent a single stimulation session consisting of three 20 s periods when the IR laser was on (Fig.  A). The laser power was 40 mW. hTRPV1⁺ and control mice were sacrificed 90 min after completion of the stimulation session for subsequent analysis of c-Fos expression.
 

## The optical stimulation/photometry setup
The optical stimulation/photometry setup consisted of the following components (Fig.  C): 1342 nm: IR continuous wave laser with wavelength 1342 nm (CNI, MIL-III-1342-1W); Arduino: Arduino controller; BP1 and BP2: band pass filter (Thorlabs, MF525-39 and Chroma, ET480/20x); C: collimator (Thorlabs, F950FC-A); Camera: CMOS camera (Thorlabs, CC505MU); CMOS: CMOS camera (The Imaging Source, DMK 33UX226); DM1 and DM2: dichroic mirror (Thorlabs, DMLP490 and DMSP900); Fiber: optical fiber with core diameter 200 μm and NA = 0.22 (Thorlabs, FG200LEA); HWP: half wave plate (Thorlabs, WPH05M-1310); L1, L2, and L3: spherical lenses (Thorlabs, LSA01); LED: fiber coupled light emitting diode with central wavelength 470 nm (Thorlabs, M470F4) with a fiber (Thorlabs, M134L02); M1 and M2—dielectric mirror (Thorlabs, BB1-E02 and BB1-E04); Obj: microscope objective (Mitutoyo,10X Mitutoyo Plan Apochromat Objective, 480–1800 nm, NA = 0.26, WD = 30.5 mm, MY10X-823); PBSC: Polarizing Beamsplitter Cube (Thorlabs, PBS124).
For thermal stimulation, we used an IR laser at a wavelength of 1342 nm. The power of radiation was tuned by rotating the HWP in front of the PBSC. The IR radiation was expanded by a telescope based on L2 and L3 lenses and coupled into the optical fiber using mirrors and a microscope objective. A fiber coupled LED at a wavelength of 470 nm with a collimator was used to excite the calcium sensor GCaMP6s. The diode radiation was filtered out by a BP2 bandpass interference filter and combined with IR radiation using M1 and DM1 mirrors. The combined radiation was coupled to a core of a multimode optical fiber with a step-index profile with NA 0.22 and a core diameter of 200 μm. Average power of the LED radiation at the output of the fiber was 6 μW. Fluorescence of the calcium sensor GCaMP6s in the mouse brain was collected by the same optical fiber and transmitted backwards. It was separated from the excitation radiation, additionally filtered by the BP1 bandpass filter, and focused by the L1 lens on the sensor of the cooled CMOS (main CMOS) camera. An additional CMOS camera was used for video recording of freely moving mice. Both cameras were synchronized at 30 frames per second. The stimulation was controlled by an Arduino microplate. Synchronization of the camera and the IR laser stimulation was implemented by the image of additional LED on the edge of an open field.

## An implantable fiber-optic interface for in vivo thermogenetic stimulation
Implantable fiber-optic interfaces were prepared from step-index multimode fibers with a 200-μm pure-silica core (FG200LEA, Thorlabs, Newton, New Jersey). The fibers were finely processed for experiments using an in-house-built fiber-processing bench using in-house-tailored fiber components and fiberprocessing tools. As the first step of the fiber-preparation procedure, a factory-polished multimode patch cable was connected to a ceramic ferrule with a standard Lucent-connector-type ceramic sleeve. A short segment of bare optical fiber was then inserted inside the ceramic ferrule and fixed using a drop of UV-cured glue at the base of the ferrule. The length of each bare-fiber segment was chosen in such a way as to provide optical interrogation of a specific targeted area inside mouses brain. To this end, the length of each bare-fiber segment was adjusted with an accuracy of about 30 μm using a fiber cleaver (Thorlabs, XL411), driven by a computer-controlled motorized translation stage (Thorlabs, MT1/M-Z8) on an in-house-assembled fiber-cleavage frame. Transmission properties of each fiber probe were carefully characterized for calcium sensor pump radiation (470 nm LED), using a power meter (PM100D with an S120VC power sensor, Thorlabs). Only fiber probes with an overall transmission above 85% were used for implantation and subsequent measurements.

## Stainless steel head plate installation
For in vivo thermometry, we installed stainless steel head plates onto exposed skulls of three mice by a standard stereotactic surgery (for details see the item “ ”). Stainless steel head plates were attached to the surface of aligned and dried skulls by the dental cement, leaving the point with stereotactic coordinates − 4.8 mm anteroposterior and 1.1 mm mediolateral from bregma easily available. This point was marked for subsequent identification. After surgery, mice were returned to their home cages and allowed for recovery during a week with appropriate treatment for analgesia.

The optical setup for all-optical thermometry consisted of the following components (Fig.  A): 1342 nm: IR continuous wave laser with the wavelength of 1342 nm (CNI, MIL-III-1342-1W); 473 nm: continuous wave laser with wavelength 473 nm (Cobolt Blues 50 473); AOM: acousto-optical modulator (ISOMET IMAD -P80L -1.5); DM1 and DM2–dichroic mirror (Thorlabs, DMLP490 and DMSP900); Fiber: dual cladding optical fiber with core diameter 25 μm and NA = 0.07, and inner cladding diameter 250 and NA = 0.48 Fiber (P-25/250DC-PM Thorlabs); HWP: half wave plate (Thorlabs, WPH05M-1310), PBSC—Polarizing Beamsplitter Cube (Thorlabs, PBS124); L1, L2 and L3 -spherical lenses (Thorlabs, LSA01); LP: low pass filter (Thorlabs, FELH0500); M1 and M2: dielectric mirror (Thorlabs, BB1-E02 and BB1-E04); Obj: microscope objective (Mitutoyo,10X Mitutoyo Plan Apochromat Objective, 480–1800 nm, 0.26 NA, 30.5 mm WD, MY10X-823); PG: pulse generator (DG645, Stanford Research Systems); SM: spectrometer (OceanOptics USB2000).
 As in the experiment with thermogenetic modulation of mouse behavior, heating was carried out using continuous wave IR laser radiation with a wavelength of 1342 nm. The power of radiation was tuned by rotating the half-wave plate HWP in front of the polarizing beam splitter PBSC. The IR radiation expanded by a telescope was coupled into the inner cladding of a dual cladding optical fiber (Fig.  B). For carrying out fully optical thermometry, a diamond microcrystal with NV0 centers was attached to the core at the output endface of the dual cladding optical fiber (P-25/250DC-PM Thorlabs) in the cyanoacrylate glue vapor. The microcrystal had a diameter of approximately 30 µm. The dual cladding optical fiber had the core diameter of 25 μm and the numerical aperture of 0.07, and the inner-cladding diameter of 250 µm and the numerical aperture of 0.48.
Mice with preliminary installed stainless steel head plates received an intraperitoneal injection of ketoprofen (5 mg/kg) for analgesia and then were anesthetized with isoflurane (3% for induction and 1.5% for maintenance). Their heads were secured in a head fixer of a custom airlifted platform [ ]. We identified the earlier marked point on the skull, added a bupivacaine droplet, and drilled a hole in the skull. The dual cladding optical fiber with the attached diamond microcrystal was slowly deepened to the depth of 2.65 mm relatively to bregma. Then, anesthesia was interrupted, and mice with fixed heads were allowed for waking up. Awake mice were exposed to 20 s IR laser pulses with the laser power of 20, 40, and 60 mW (10 repeats per power value). The implanted fiber-optic probe was removed from the brain. Deeply anesthetized mice were sacrificed by decapitation. The dual cladding optical fiber with the attached diamond microcrystal was washed in saline and sterilized with 70% ethanol for the reuse. The repeated use of the same calibrated fiber-optic probe in several mice provided reproducibility of all-optical thermometry.

## Theoretical model
The theoretical model is similar to the one used earlier to calculate the thermal penalty for optogenetic stimulation [ ]. To analyze the heating in our thermogenetic stimulation protocol, we used mathematical modeling of IR radiation and heating transfer. The IR radiation transfer is analyzed in our model by means of Monte Carlo simulations with a Henyey–Greenstein scattering phase function [ , ]:
2pθ=121-g21+g2-2gcosθ3/2,In our modeling to mimic brain we used anisotropy parameter g = 0.96 and scattering length ls = 4.8 mm−1 [ ]. Light propagation in the brain tissue was modeled in cylindrical coordinates with absorbing ends. A cylinder had radius of 6 mm and thickness of 8 mm Cylindrical voxels were generate by space discretization with 5 µm steps. We also assumed that the optical fiber had a core diameter of 200 μm and numerical aperture of 0.2 and was placed to a depth of 1 mm from the brain surface. Then, photons propagated into the brain tissue at a random angle within the numerical aperture. When modeling, we used the absorption coefficient μa = 0.274 mm−1 [ , ]. In the spectral range around the wavelength corresponding to the IR laser line (1342 nm), the absorption and scattering lengths of water are noticeably less than those of brain tissues. Therefore, the calculations are resistant to small variations in these parameters and correspond to the predictions of stimulation.
For temperature calculations, we used heat diffusion equation in a cylinder with radius of 6 mm and thickness of 8 mm and with absorbing boundaries. Heat transfer in the brain tissue was treated as a diffusion process using the well-known modification of the diffusion equation [ , , ]:

## Immunohistochemistry and confocal imaging
Expression of hTPRV1 in acute brain slices and mouse brains, the inflammatory response of the brain tissue, and the appearance of a molecular marker of neuronal activity were evaluated by immunohistochemical staining and confocal microscopy. After electrophysiological recordings, acute brain slices were fixed for 24 h in 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.01 M phosphate buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) at 4 °C followed by three washings in PBS. Experimental and control mice subjected to in vivo thermal stimulation were deeply anesthetized by an intraperitoneal injection of a mixture of tiletamine/zolazepam/xylazine at doses of 40/40/20 mg/kg. Then, anesthetized mice underwent transcardiac perfusion with PBS followed by cold 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS (pH 7.4) according to standard procedures. Brains were extracted from skulls and postfixed overnight at 4 °C. On the following day, the brains were washed with PBS, and the hemispheres were sliced sagittally with a Leica VT1200S vibratome (Leica-Microsystems). Then, slices were incubated with the following primary antibodies overnight at room temperature: the rabbit anti-FLAG polyclonal antibody (1:500, Affinity, #T0053; RRID:AB_2843447); the chicken anti-GFP polyclonal antibody (1:500, Abcam, #ab13970; RRID:AB_300798), the rabbit anti-GFAP polyclonal antibody (1:1000, Abcam, #ab7260, RRID: AB_305808), the rabbit anti-Iba-1 monoclonal antibody (1:1000, Abcam, #ab178846, RRID:AB_2636859), and the guinea pig anti-c-Fos monoclonal antibody (Synaptic Systems, #226308, RRID:AB_2905595). ## Confocal image analysis
For evaluation of the inflammatory response, we determined the relative areas of GFAP and Iba-1 staining in hTRPV1⁺ and control mice. For each staining, we acquired Z-stacks from three slices per animal. Each Z-stack was acquired from the region (320 µm × 320 µm) underneath the site of implantation of the fiber-optic interface. Maximal projections obtained from Z-stacks were analyzed using the image processing software Fiji. Image thresholding was carried out by Huang’s fuzzy thresholding method [ ] using the Auto Threshold plugin. On segmented images (Fig.  A and ), percentages of GFAP or Iba-1 positive areas were calculated for three slices per animal followed by averaging.
 To assess specificity of neuronal activation, we quantified the numbers of c-Fos immunopositive cells in the ipsi- and contralateral CnFs of hTRPV1+ and control mice. On maximal projections of confocal Z-stacks, we chose a square area (461 µm × 461 µm) underneath the site of implantation of the fiber-optic interface for ipsilateral hemispheres and within the CnF for contralateral hemispheres. The CnF of the contralateral hemispheres was identified using the mouse brain atlas [ ]. Cells with c-Fos immunopositive nuclei were manually counted within this area. Cells adjacent to the left and bottom sides of the square were excluded from quantification. Neuronal activation was determined as the number of c-Fos immunopositive cells per mm2. For hTRPV1⁺ mice, we also assessed the percentage of cells with co-localization of c-Fos and hTRPV1 (revealed by the anti-FLAG antibody) underneath the site of implantation of the fiber-optic interface.

## Results

## Evaluation of the hTRPV1-based thermogenetic activation in vitro
To use human TRPV1 as a thermogenetic actuator, it is necessary to assess how strongly cells expressing hTRPV1 should be heated to elicit a marked increase of intracellular calcium. The thermoactivation threshold of hTRPV1 is 42–44 °C as earlier determined by recording the thermal activation current followed by analysis of temperature-current curves [ ]. Temperatures above 42 °C are generally considered as potentially harmful for mammalian tissues primarily because of potential protein denaturation. Therefore, we initially examined heating of HEK293TN cells co-transfected with a mixture of plasmids for expression of hTRPV1 and the genetically encoded calcium indicator GCaMP6s [ ]. tdTomato separated from hTRPV1 by a p2A self-cleavage site was used as a reporter of hTRPV1 expression. Control cells were transfected with a plasmid encoding GCaMP6s only.
Experimental (hTRPV1⁺) and control cells underwent simultaneous controlled heating and real-time calcium imaging (Suppl. Fig. A, B). Cells were maintained at the baseline temperature of 37 °C and exposed to three consecutive heating–cooling cycles to up to 39, 41, and 43 °C with 30 s duration of each heating period (Suppl. Fig. C). Quick heating of hTRPV1⁺ cells to the supposed subthreshold temperatures (39 and 41 °C) resulted in marked elevations of intracellular calcium as determined by changes in the GCaMP6s fluorescence (Fig.  A, Suppl. Fig. D, F, Supplementary Video ). Here and below, temperatures lower than the thermoactivation threshold of hTRPV1 determined previously [ ] are referred to as subthreshold temperatures. In sharp contrast, similar heating treatments of control cells led to a slight decrease of the GCaMP6s fluorescence (Fig.  B, Suppl. Fig. E, G, Supplementary Video ). This decrease occurred because GCaMP6s is a green fluorescent protein (GFP)-based calcium sensor [ ] and the fluorescence intensity of GFP is known to decline with temperature increases [ – ]. These observations enabled us to suggest that inward ion currents appearing as a consequence of quick heating of hTRPV1 to supposed subthreshold temperatures is sufficient to depolarize mammalian neurons strongly enough to initiate generation of action potentials, and, therefore, neuromodulation can be achieved within non-harmful temperature range.
 

## Thermogenetic stimulation of principal hippocampal neurons in acute brain slices
To examine whether mammalian CNS neurons can be thermogenetically stimulated using human TRPV1, we expressed hTRPV1-flag and GCaMP6s-p2A-tdTomato in mouse hippocampal neurons by adeno-associated viral (AAV) vector delivery (Fig.  A). Acute brain slices were used for thermal stimulation with simultaneous electrophysiological recording and calcium imaging. Since there was not a reporter for identification of hTRPV1-expressing neurons in living brain slices because of the limited AAV vector capacity, we assumed that most GCaMP6s⁺ pyramidal cells also expressed hTRPV1. Therefore, we carried out preliminary verification by calcium imaging of GCaMP6s⁺ CA1 pyramidal cells that responded to brief (10 s) heating by IR laser irradiation. Then, we subjected these cells to a heating/cooling protocol combined with cell-attached voltage-clamp recording and calcium imaging.
The experimental setup for thermogenetic stimulation of pyramidal cells in acute brain slices is shown in Fig.  B. The aligned beams of the 1375 nm heating laser and the 520 nm visible laser were delivered to brain slices using an optical fiber. The heating laser was targeted to the desired area in the slice using the visible laser (Fig.  C). Microelectrode targeting and calcium imaging were carried out using an upright epi-fluorescence microscope (Fig.  B, ). The brain slices were continuously perfused with oxygenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) at 35 °C. For thermal stimulation, patched cells were exposed to 20 s IR laser treatments with 40 s intervals. The IR laser power was gradually enhanced by increasing the diode current (Fig.  D, , lower graphs). The temperatures corresponding to the applied diode currents were determined in a separate calibration experiment by monitoring currents flowing through an open patch pipette as previously described [ ] (Suppl. Fig. A-D). Expression of hTRPV1 in brain slices of experimental mice was confirmed by immunohistochemical staining with an anti-FLAG antibody (Suppl. Fig. E, F).
Heating of neurons by IR laser elicited elevations of neuronal membrane potential (Vm) that were more evident in slices of hTRPV1⁺ mice (Fig.  D, , middle graphs, and F). With increases in IR laser power, the Vm elevations became higher reaching the action potential (AP) generation threshold in slices of hTRPV1⁺ mice but not in slices of control mice (Fig.  D, , middle graphs, F, and G). The GCaMP6s fluorescence declined in both hTRPV1⁺ and control mice when the IR laser was turned on. While the GCaMP6s fluorescence remained stably decreased within the heating period in control mice, it was elevated in hTRPV1⁺ mice (Fig.  D, , insets in upper graphs). We hypothesize that this intraneuronal calcium elevation is associated with heat-induced opening of ectopically expressed hTRPV1.
Heating above 38 °C from the baseline temperature of 35 °C evoked generation of AP trains that were accompanied by intraneuronal calcium bursts (Fig.  D, ). The amplitude of AP-related calcium bursts was much higher than the amplitude of intraneuronal calcium elevations associated heat-induced opening of ectopically expressed hTRPV1. This indicates that Ca2+ overload of neurons caused by expression and activation of hTRPV1 is unlikely.
In sum, experiments on acute brain slices demonstrated that mouse neurons expressing hTRPV1 can be stimulated by slight heating within the non-harmful temperature range.

## Thermogenetic modulation of mouse behavior
Next, we examined if thermogenetic stimulation of neuronal circuits in vivo using human TRPV1 can evoke behavioral effects. To this end, we expressed hTRPV1-flag and GCaMP6s-p2A-tdTomato in excitatory neurons of the mouse cuneiform nucleus (CnF) by delivery of AAVs (Fig.  A and ). CnF belongs to the mesencephalic locomotor region, and stimulation of CnF excitatory neurons has been demonstrated to initiate locomotion and induce running in freely behaving animals [ , – ]. Immediately after AAV injection, a fiber-optic interface was implanted so that its flat tip was 0.1 mm above the point of AAV delivery (Fig.  B). After a 14–18-day recovery period, mice were adapted to the experimental conditions during two consecutive days prior to the first day of stimulation (Fig.  A). For stimulation, awake mice connected to the optical stimulation/photometry setup by a long optical fiber (Fig.  C) were placed into the arena for a 5 min adaptation period. Then, we recorded their behavior and the integral GCaMP6s signal. Each stimulation session included three periods when the heating IR laser was on (Fig.  D). Heating periods of 20 s were separated by 30 s time intervals when the heating IR laser was off. Each mouse underwent three consecutive stimulation sessions, in which we gradually increased laser power: 20, 40, and 60 mW.
Naïve (not yet treated with the IR laser) mice of both the experimental and control groups displayed similar locomotor activity (Fig.  E). Hence, hTRPV1 expression in CnF excitatory neurons alone did not evidently affect mouse behavior. Five of seven hTPRV1+ mice stably exhibited evident locomotor responses to IR laser treatments, whereas no control mice displayed any behavioral response when the IR laser was on. During periods when the IR laser was off, both hTPRV1+ and control mice showed low locomotor activity with occasional acceleration of motion and a stable baseline signal of GCaMP6s (Fig.  F and ). When the IR laser was on, the hTPRV1+ mice exhibited increased locomotion with running gaits, and, at the same time, the integral GCaMP6s signal recorded in the stimulated brain area also elevated (Fig.  F, Supplementary Video ). Unlike the hTPRV1+ mice, control mice display neither apparent behavioral responses nor increases in the integral GCaMP6s signal during periods when the IR laser was on (Fig.  G, Supplementary Video ).
To study the possibility of chronic neuromodulation using hTRPV1, we analyzed data collected for three consecutive stimulation days. Data analysis was carried out by averaging the speed of mice and the normalized integral signal of the calcium sensor GCaMP6s for the periods when the IR laser was on and the periods when the IR laser was off. Analysis of the average speed of hTRPV1+ mice revealed that they exhibited a significant increase in this parameter during IR laser stimulation with a power of 40 and 60 mW (Fig.  A). Only on the third simulation day, we did observe a significant increase of the average speed when hTRPV1+ mice were stimulated with an IR laser power of 20 mW. To examine whether the observed increase is associated with sensitization of locomotor response by repeated stimulation, we compared the average speed of hTRPV1+ mice on all consecutive stimulation days separately for the periods when the IR laser was on and the periods when the IR laser was off. Statistical analysis did not show significant differences in the average speed for the periods when the IR laser was on (repeated measures ANOVA: N = 7 mice, F = 2.375, p = 0.1352), but revealed that spontaneous locomotor activity of hTRPV1+ mice during the periods when the IR laser was off was lower on the third stimulation day in comparison to the first stimulation day (repeated measures ANOVA: N = 7 mice, F = 6.395, p = 0.0129; Bonferroni post hoc test for stimulation day 1 vs. stimulation day 3: t = 3.520, p < 0.05) (Fig.  A). Habituation of mice in the arena explains this decrease of locomotor activity [ ]. Therefore, on the third day, the effect of thermogenetic stimulation on mouse behavior became more evident on the background of decreased spontaneous locomotor activity because of habituation. Mice of the control group did not display any increase of average speed during periods when the IR laser was on (Fig.  A). Similar to hTRPV1+ mice, control mice exhibited lower spontaneous locomotor activity during the periods when the IR laser was off on the third stimulation day in comparison to the first stimulation day (repeated measures ANOVA: N = 8 mice, F = 6.723, p = 0.0090; Bonferroni post hoc test for stimulation day 1 vs. stimulation day 3: t = 3.532, p < 0.01) (Fig.  A).
 Analysis of the averaged GCaMP6s signal revealed that even the lowest laser power stimulation (20 mW) significantly increased this parameter in hTRPV1+ mice on all stimulation days (Fig.  B). In control mice, heating of the brain tissue with the IR laser stably decreased the averaged GCaMP6s signal (Fig.  B).
To elucidate whether IR laser treatments evoked similar stimulation effects during three consecutive stimulation days, we compared the relative speed increase and the GCaMP6s signal elevation in hTRPV1+ mice between stimulation days for each laser power. The relative speed increase was determined as the ratio of the average speed during periods when the IR laser was on to the average speed during periods when the IR laser was off. There was not an evident decline in the stimulation effects of IR laser treatments on mouse locomotion (Suppl. Fig. A, Supplementary Table S4). Statistical analysis of the average GCaMP6s signal elevations did not reveal significant differences during three consecutive stimulation days (Suppl. Fig. B, Supplementary Table S5). In sum, these observations confirmed the possibility of chronic neuromodulation using hTRPV1.
The behavioral changes observed in our experiments could also be associated with an anxiolytic effect of thermogenetic activation of CnF neurons. To find out if thermogenetic activation of CnF neurons induced specifically locomotion and not an anxiolytic effect in hTRPV1⁺ mice, we assessed time spent by mice in the central area and near the walls of the arena during the periods the IR laser was on or off [ – ]. Quantification of the ratio of the time spent in the central area to the time spent at the periphery did not reveal significant differences in this behavioral parameter for the periods when the IR laser was on and the periods when the IR laser was off during all three stimulation days (Suppl. Fig. S4, Supplementary Table S6). Thus, thermogenetic activation of CnF neurons did not produce anxiolytic effects in hTRPV1⁺ mice.
Immunofluorescent staining with an anti-FLAG antibody confirmed the expression of hTRPV1 spreading around and underneath the track of the fiber-optic interface (Suppl. Fig. S5). Evaluation of DAPI nuclear staining in brain preparations of both hTRPV1+ and control mice revealed signs of inflammation around the site of viral delivery and fiber-optic interface implantation. Nevertheless, there was not evident brain tissue damage underneath the endface of the fiber-optic interface. We further assessed if there are differences in inflammatory responses between hTRPV1⁺ and control mice. To this end, brain slices were stained with antibodies against GFAP and Iba-1 for detection of astrocytes and microglia. Both cell types are known to participate in inflammatory responses and their morphological remodeling serves as a feature of neuroinflamation [ ]. On confocal images acquired from the site underneath the track of the fiber-optic interface (Fig.  ), we determined relative areas occupied by astrocytes and microglia [ ]. These parameters were approximately 50–60% higher in hTRPV1+ mice than in control mice. This finding indicated an increased level of neuroinflamation in hTRPV1+ mice.
To evaluate the selectivity of thermogenetic activation, we assessed the distribution of c-Fos, a molecular marker of neuronal activity [ – ], upon thermal stimulation. To this end, we delivered AAVs for expression of hTRPV1-flag and GCaMP6s-p2A-tdTomato in excitatory neurons of the CnF and implanted optic-fiber interfaces in a separate cohort of mice (Fig.  A). After a 14–18-day recovery period and two adaptation sessions on the two consecutive days prior to the day of stimulation, mice underwent a single stimulation session that included three periods when the heating IR laser was on. In this experiment, the IR laser power was 40 mW. We chose the value of 40 mW because thermal stimulation of mice at this laser power was found to induce an evident behavioral effect over a 3-day period. As in our previous experiment, heating periods of 20 s were separated by 30 s time intervals when the heating IR laser was off. After a stimulation session, mice were placed back to their home cages and sacrificed 90 min after completion of the stimulation session for subsequent analysis of c-Fos expression.
Similar to the experiment with repeated thermogenetic neuromodulation, thermal stimulation increased both the average speed and the GCaMP6s signal in hTRPV1+ mice but not in control mice (Fig.  B and ). However, the increase of the average speed in hTRPV1+ mice was not statistically significant probably because of the small number of animals in this group (N = 5). Significant elevation of the GCaMP6s signal confirmed thermogenetic activation of neurons in the brain of hTRPV1+ mice. Confocal imaging of brain slices co-stained with antibodies against FLAG and c-Fos showed an abundancy of c-Fos+ nuclei underneath and around the site of implantation of the fiber-optic interface in hTRPV1+ mice in comparison to control mice (Fig.  D). We further quantified c-Fos+ nuclei in a square area underneath the site of implantation of the fiber-optic interface for ipsilateral hemispheres (Fig.  E) and within the CnF for contralateral hemispheres. Quantification of c-Fos+ nuclei in the CnF of contralateral hemispheres enabled us to judge about possible unwanted effects of AAV delivery, implantation of the fiber-optic interface, and heating of brain tissue with the IR laser on c-Fos expression. Quantitative analysis revealed that hTRPV1+ mice contained a significantly higher number of c-Fos+ neurons in the CnF of the ipsilateral hemisphere in comparison to both hemispheres of control mice (Fig.  F). This confirmed that thermal stimulation by IR laser irradiation activated neurons in hTRPV1+ mice but not in control mice. Notably, numbers of c-Fos+ neurons did not differ between hemispheres of control mice. This observation argues that AAV delivery, implantation of the fiber-optic interface, and heating of brain tissue with the IR laser do not seem to produce effects on c-Fos expression. The number of c-Fos+ neurons in the contralateral CnF of hTRPV1+ mice was lower than the number of c-Fos+ neurons in the ipsilateral CnF but higher than numbers of c-Fos+ neurons in both hemispheres of control mice (Fig.  F). However, these differences were not statistically significant. This observation indicates indirect activation of neurons in this region via contralateral projections that provide coordination of locomotion [ ]. In the ipsilateral CnF of hTRPV1+ mice, we also determined that, on average, a half of hTRPV1+ cells had c-Fos+ nuclei, and a half of all c-Fos+ cells did not express hTRPV1 (Fig.  G and ). Thus, hTRPV1+ neurons fired non-identically upon thermal stimulation, and only a fraction of them fired at the rate enough to induce subsequent expression of c-Fos. In parallel, heat-induced activation of hTRPV1+ neurons in the CnF seems to implicate neighbor hTRPV1− neurons to form coordinated locomotive behavior. In sum, we demonstrated correlation between behavioral outcomes, changes in the GCaMP6s signal, and the c-Fos expression upon hTRPV1-mediated thermogenetic stimulation.

## In vivo thermometry
To assess temperatures reached during in vivo IR laser stimulation, we used a theoretical model allowing the dynamics of spatial temperature distribution in the brain tissue nearby the fiber tip to be revealed. The theoretical model was verified by determining heating kinetics using all-optical high-resolution temperature measurements in the brain of awake mice. The measurements were carried out by analyzing photoluminescence readout from neutral nitrogen-vacancy (NV0) color centers in a diamond.
Heads of anesthetized mice with a preliminarily installed stainless steel head plate were secured in the head fixer of a custom airlifted platform [ ] (Fig.  A). A dual cladding optical fiber with a 30 µm NV0 vacancy-containing diamond attached to its endface (Fig.  B) was inserted slightly above the CnF of anesthetized mice (Fig.  C). The photoluminescence of NV0 centers was excited by visible light transmitted through the fiber core, and then it was collected and conveyed to a spectrometer through by the same optical fiber. To avoid directly heating the diamond by IR radiation, it was delivered through the inner cladding for heating the brain tissue (Fig.  B). Spectrum approximation (Fig.  D) allowed for determination of the position of the zero phonon line (ZPLNV⁰) that was then correlated with a temperature change determined in a preliminary calibration experiment. Mice with an inserted optical fiber were allowed to wake up. Then, they were exposed to 20 s IR laser pulses with the laser power of 20, 40, and 60 mW (10 repeats per power value). Temperature kinetics was measured as ∆T (°C) at the endface of the optical fiber for each laser power. Experimental measurements were in good agreement with theoretically calculated curves and revealed that the 20 s IR laser pulses of 20, 40 and 60 mW increased the local temperature by roughly 3, 5, and 8 °C, respectively (Fig.  E).
In sum, thermometry in vivo demonstrated that the behavioral effects of thermogenetic neuronal stimulation by IR laser treatments through an implanted fiber-optic interface seem to be dependent on temperatures reached.

## Discussion
In the present study, we employed human TRPV1 as a thermogenetic tool and demonstrated that this tool allows for manipulation of neuronal firing and modulation of mouse locomotor behavior by heating. hTRPV1 targeted to mouse CNS neurons can be activated by IR laser-induced heating within a non-harmful temperature range. Heat-induced activation of ectopically expressed hTRPV1 elicits generation of action potential trains in neurons. Thermogenetic stimulation of neuronal networks in vivo by IR laser treatment via an implanted fiber-optic interface evokes evident locomotive behavior in awake freely moving mice, and the behavioral effects are stably reproduced in repeated stimulation sessions for at least three consecutive days. A key advantage of the developed thermogenetic tool is that it can be easily combined with in vivo calcium imaging to provide real-time control of neuromodulation in awake freely moving animals. This feature is useful for those experiments in which stimulation of certain neuronal circuits does not produce immediate behavioral effects. Furthermore, thermogentics is a promising technology for neuromodulation therapy, and the use of the human TRPV1 channel facilitates translation of this technology into medicine.
Our experiments on cultured cells and acute brain slices revealed that activation of hTRPV1 within the subthreshold temperature range increases intracellular calcium and depolarizes neuronal membrane enough to generate trains of action potentials. Patch-clamp recordings combined with calcium imaging in acute brain slices identify a hallmark of thermogenetic neurostimulation that had yet not been reported. Intraneuronal calcium elevations appearing due to heat-induced hTRPV1 activation are much lower than calcium bursts associated with neuronal firing. Hence, heat-induced activation of hTRPV1 within the subthreshold temperature range does not exert a massive calcium influx, and neurons have enough capacity to pump the TRPV1 activation-associated calcium fraction out of the cytosol. This finding suggests that thermogenetics is quite safe in terms of potential calcium cytotoxicity because of the low risk of cytotoxic calcium overload upon induced or non-specific activation of ectopically expressed hTRPV1.
The feasibility of long-term in vivo neuromodulation using thermogenetics had not yet been tested in vertebrate species. Previous studies have reported thermogenetic stimulation of vertebrate neurons in experiments with limited numbers of runs within a single day [ – ]. In this study, we demonstrate reproducible behavioral effects of repeated thermogenetic stimulation over three consecutive days. Observations in in vivo experiments led to the following conclusions critically important for subsequent translation of the hTRPV1-based thermogenetic tool into medicine. First, expression of hTRPV1 in the mouse brain per se does not evidently affect behavior. Second, the repeated heating of brain tissue by IR laser treatments used in our experiments does not seem to evoke brain tissue damage evident at the behavioral and histological levels. Third, thermogenetics shows promise as a tool to be used for long-term neuromodulation.
To be suitable for clinical applications, thermogenetics should allow for neuronal stimulation by heating within a non-harmful temperature range. Our data on thermometry in vivo reveal that temperature increases ∆T measured at the endface of the optical fiber for the laser power of 40 and 60 mW, when the behavioral effects of thermogenetic stimulation were evident, are approximately 5 and 8 °C, respectively. Given the core body temperature of mice is approximately 37 °C, such temperature increases are in the harmful temperature range. However, all experiments were carried out on mice with exposed skulls and an implanted fiber-optic interface attached to the skull surface. A previous study has reported that the surface of the neocortex in a cranial window preparation can have temperatures approximately 10 °C below the core body temperature [ ]. In our experiments, both the exposed skull and implanted fiber-optic interface definitely provide substantial heat loss by the brain. Therefore, the baseline temperature of the brain was certainly lower than 37 °C, and real temperatures reached upon IR laser treatments were lower than may be expected. Thus, combining the data on thermometry in vivo with observations on acute brain slices enables us to speculate that thermogenetic modulation of neuronal circuits in vivo can be performed by heating brain tissue within a non-harmful temperature range.
A critical property of all external stimulus-dependent synthetic biology tools is the timescale on which neuronal firing can be manipulated. This property determines the application range of such tools. Optogenetics allows for induction of synchronized neuronal firing at frequencies of up to several hundred Hz [ , ]. Chemogenetics provides neuromodulation at a minute-timescale and, unlike optogenetics, does not allow for induction of individual action potentials in neurons and synchronized neuronal firing [ , ]. Here, we studied neuronal firing and behavioral effects upon neuronal stimulation by continuous IR laser treatments lasting up to 20 s and did not test thermogenetic stimulation using IR laser pulse trains. In our previous studies, we reported that neurostimulation using IR laser pulse trains at frequencies up to 50 Hz exerts phase-locked responses in neurons in cell cultures and acute brain slices [ , ]. Hence, further studies are necessary to reveal if thermogenetic stimulation using IR laser pulse trains is able to induce synchronized neuronal firing in vivo.
Analysis of the distribution of c-Fos immunopositive neurons in the ipsi- and contralateral CnFs of hTRPV1+ and control mice confirmed our observations on locomotion of mice and changes in the GCaMP6s fluorescence upon thermal stimulation. Particularly, treatment with the IR laser does not induce neuronal firing in control mice whereas significantly elevates numbers of c-Fos cells in the ipsilateral CnF with the lesser effect on the contralateral CnF of hTRPV1+ mice. In the ipsilateral CnF of hTRPV1+ mice populations of c-Fos+ and hTRPV1+ neurons were revealed to overlay in a half. As we found in this study, unilateral thermal stimulation induced coordinated locomotion in hTRPV1+ mice. To produce such coordinated locomotion, specific patterns of neuronal activity should be formed in the CnFs of both hemispheres. Thus, neuronal firing in the CnFs depended on both direct thermal stimulation and self-generation of activity patterns by local neuronal circuits. This explains the non-exclusive expression of c-Fos in hTRPV1+ neurons of the ipsilateral CnF as well as the higher number of c-Fos+ neurons in the contralateral CnF of hTRPV1+ mice in comparison to control mice.
Evaluation of brain tissue integrity did not reveal evident heat-induced brain injury. However, we observed signs of an increased level of neuroinflammation in mice expressing hTRPV1 upon repeated thermal stimulation. There are several reasons why mice of the experimental group displayed this increased level of neuroinflammation. First, these mice had a doubled viral burden. Experimental mice received intracranial injections of an AAV vector mixture (1:1) for expression of hTRPV1 and GCaMP6s whereas control mice were injected with a single, half-dissolved AVV vector for expression of GCaMP6s only. Second, TRPV1 is known be a detector of pro-inflammatory molecules, and its activation induces inflammatory signaling pathways in the brain [ , , ]. Therefore, its overexpression in neurons may also contribute to the increased level of neuroinflammation in experimental mice. Third, the observed robust behavioral effects and c-Fos staining in mice with expression of hTRPV1 enable us to suggest strong activation of neurons upon thermal stimulation. Previously, neuronal hyperactivity has been reported to induce morphological remodeling of astrocytes and microglia as well [ , ]. Thus, contribution of the viral vector burden, per se expression of hTRPV1, and hTRPV1-mediated thermal activation of the inflammatory response of brain tissue should be carefully evaluated for translation of hTRPV1-based thermogenetic neuromodulation to practice.
Our findings enable us to highlight the following key features of hTRPV1-based thermogenetics and its shortcomings and advantages over opto- and chemogenetics. Thermogenetics allows control of neuronal activity with spatiotemporal resolution close to that provided by optogenetics. Control of neuronal activity with such spatiotemporal resolution is unavailable with chemogenetics. Heat induced activation of hTRPV1 causes not only cell membrane depolarization but also an increase in intracellular calcium. External stimulus-driven manipulation of intracellular calcium enables thermogenetics to be applied for controlling any calcium dependent processes, such as secretion or cell motility, in non-excitable cells. This extends the application range of thermogenetics both within and outside the nervous system. Thus, thermogenetics may serve as an alternative option for remote manipulation of intracellular calcium in those cases when light-or ligand-dependent control of calcium signaling using the existing opto- and chemogenetic tools [ – ] is complicated or not feasible. The use of hTRPV1 as a thermogenetic actuator in combination with non-invasive activation by FUS renders thermogenetics to be a technology with therapeutic translational potential. However, thermogenetics has two serious shortcomings. The first is the lack of thermosensitive inhibitory actuators that could elicit heat-induced silencing of neurons. This limits the potential application range. For instance, thermogenetics cannot be used for control of seizures by silencing principal excitatory neurons as can be done by optogenetics or chemogenetics [ , ]. The second is that hTRPV1 is a polymodal receptor able to sense, apart from heat, pH and a range of chemical compounds. Therefore, putative adverse effects of ectopic expression and non-heat activation of hTRPV1 should be carefully evaluated prior to translation to medicine.
Future clinical applications of thermogenetic neuromodulation require both introducing the gene encoding a thermo-TRP into specific brain areas or cell types and delivering heat. Gene transfer and heat delivery must be effective, specific, and safe. Current gene delivery approaches that can be translated to medicine are divided into two major classes: viral and non-viral. Non-viral approaches primarily use inorganic particles, polymeric structures, or their combinations as carriers to facilitate delivery of naked genetic material into cells. The major advantages of non-viral vectors are simple large-scale fabrication and reduced pathogenicy (reviewed in [ ]). However, the low efficiency of gene delivery, especially into CNS cells, limits the clinical application of non-viral vectors. Among viral gene transfer strategies, AAV vectors are the most promising for targeted gene expression in the CNS. AAV vectors of natural serotypes and engineered AAV capsids with enhanced trafficking through the blood–brain barrier were shown to provide effective gene transfer into various cell types of the CNS via intracerebral, intrathecal, and intravenous injections (reviewed in [ – ]). The use of promoters for targeted expression of hTRPV1 in glutamatergic excitatory neurons, various subtypes of GABAergic inhibitory neurons, dopaminergic, or cholinergic neurons [ – ] will allow activity of specific neuronal populations to be selectively modulated by heating. It may be possible to use neuronal cell type-specific thermogenetic neuromodulation to control epileptic seizures or relieve parkinsonian motor impairments.
The development of external stimulus-driven synthetic biology tools which use human-derived genetically encoded actuators and provide noninvasive neuromodulation will facilitate the future translation of such tools into medicine. An important conclusion of our study is that thermogenetic neurostimulation can be achieved by direct heating of hTPRV1 expressing neurons by IR light. Still, the approach of heat delivery using an implanted optical fiber is invasive. However, in future applications, we believe that FUS will be the method of choice for non-invasive energy delivery.
FUS produces both mechanical and thermal effects on biological tissues. Recent advances in the fabrication of miniature FUS transducers led to a series of studies that reported the use of various mechano- and thermosensitive proteins, including channels belonging to the TRP superfamily, in combination with FUS for noninvasive neuromodulation in vivo [ , – ]. Targeting hTRPV1 to specific neuronal populations in combination with a portable/implantable FUS device may become a promising noninvasive therapeutic tool for treating motor impairments or seizures, respectively, without the use of actuating agents such as nanoparticles.
In summary, neuromodulation using the human TRPV1 channel overcomes some disadvantages of opto- and chemogenetics and for the first time opens up possibilities for translation of thermogenetics into medicine in order to treat various neuropathologies.
