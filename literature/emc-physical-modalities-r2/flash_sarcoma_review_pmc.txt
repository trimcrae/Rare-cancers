 FLASH Radiotherapy: What can FLASH’s ultra high dose rate offer to the treatment of patients with sarcoma?

 Abstract

 FLASH is an emerging treatment paradigm in radiotherapy (RT) that utilizes ultra-high dose rates (UHDR; >40 Gy/s) of radiation delivery. Developing advances in technology support the delivery of UHDR using electron and proton systems, as well as by some ion beam units (e.g. carbon ion), while methods to achieve UHDR with photons are being investigated. The major advantage of FLASH RT is found in its ability to increase the therapeutic index for RT by shifting the dose response curve for normal tissue toxicity to higher doses. Numerous preclinical studies have been conducted to date of FLASH RT for murine sarcomas, alongside the investigation of its effects on relevant normal tissues of skin, muscle and bone. The tumor control achieved by FLASH RT of sarcoma models is indistinguishable from that attained by treatment with Standard RT to the same total dose. FLASH’s high dose rates are able mitigate the severity or incidence of RT side effects on normal tissues as evaluated by endpoints ranging from functional sparing to histological damage. Large animal studies and clinical trials of canine patients evidence skin sparing by FLASH vs Standard RT, but also caution against delivery of high single doses with FLASH that exceed those safely applied with Standard RT. Also, a human clinical trial has shown that FLASH RT can safely be delivered to bone metastasis. Thus, data to date support continued investigation toward clinical translation of FLASH RT for the treatment of patients with sarcoma. Toward this purpose, hypofractionated irradiation schemes are being investigated for FLASH effects on sarcoma and relevant normal tissues.

 Introduction

 For patients with sarcoma, radiotherapy (RT) plays an important role in both definitive and palliative disease management. As is the case with most cancer therapies, RT is intended to prevent malignant cells from dividing or spreading. However, RT necessarily also demonstrates toxicity to normal cells and tissues that causes treatment related side effects. This relationship between tumor response and normal tissue response is known as the therapeutic index, and an important goal in advancing RT is to find ways to separate the dose response curves for tumor and normal tissues ( Figure 1 ). The impact of broadening the therapeutic index in RT can result in vast improvements in tumor control, overall survival, and mitigation of toxicities, especially when RT is delivered over the course of several fractionated treatments 1 , 2 . This concept has guided the development of techniques such as intensity-modulated (x-ray/proton) RT (IMXT/IMPT) and stereotactic body RT (SBRT) that allow for more precise delivery of the bulk of the cytotoxic dose to tumors while sparing surrounding normal tissues. In addition to spatial control of RT therapeutic index, biological radiation response modifiers may further improve therapeutic index 3 . Finally, therapeutic index can be affected by temporal modulation of RT dose delivery, which is achieved by modulating factors such as the dose rate within a RT fraction, the dose per fraction or the time between fractions. Here, increasing the number of fractions and/or the time between fractions or reducing the dose rate (within the range of 0.001–1 Gy/s) for each fraction generally reduces normal tissue toxicity, although the effect on tumor control may depend on tumor type. However, as noted below, the effect of ultra-high dose rates (UHDR, above 40 Gy/s) on therapeutic index has recently received increasing attention.

 Despite our ability to target RT with sophisticated techniques, it can still lead to high rates of normal tissue toxicity that significantly affect the quality of life of patients. For patients with sarcomas, standard fractionation involves delivery of 2 Gy daily fractions, 5 days per week over 5–7 weeks for total doses of ~50 Gy and ~60 – 70 Gy in the neoadjuvant (preoperative) and adjuvant (post-operative) settings, respectively 4 , 5 . Normal tissue toxicity at these doses is significant. For example, in patients undergoing preoperative RT for extremity soft tissue sarcoma (STS), 11 – 48% will experience wound complications 5 , 6 , 7 , 15 – 31.5% will experience late complications such as fibrosis, joint stiffness or lymphedema 8 , 9 , and 2 – 10% will experience bone fracture 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 .

 In addition to having significant levels of toxicity, these standard fractionation schedules may increase the overall cost (opportunity and financial) for RT and reduce access to high level RT expertise 14 , 15 . With advanced RT delivery techniques that improve tumor imaging/targeting and spatial RT dose conformality to maintain therapeutic index, hypofractionated RT schedules (<7–10 fractions) are increasingly common for a range of malignancies including breast, lung and prostate cancers 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 . For patients with sarcoma, hypofractionated schedules can maintain high tumor control rates 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 . One study of 272 patients with STS receiving 5 Gy × 5 fractions pre-op, the 3-year overall survival was 72% and the local recurrence rate was 19% 22 . Nevertheless, 7% of patients required additional surgery for treatment of RT complications. Another study of 30 Gy in 5 fractions as neoadjuvant RT in patients with STS demonstrated a 2-year local failure rate of only 6% 25 . Nonetheless, the toxicity was similar to standard fractionation schedules with a wound complication rate of 32% and a late complication rate of 16%.

 While dose and fractionation have received attention in RT clinical trials, the dose rate within each fraction has been less well explored. Typical external beam RT is delivered at dose rates of ~0.003–0.1 Gray (Gy)/s. Reducing the dose rate to ~0.001 Gy/s for large fields such as in total body RT is used to reduce side effects 26 . Early studies in the effects of UHDR RT were performed with the thought that such dose rates might be less effective due to radiochemical depletion of oxygen in tissues. Indeed, reduced cell-killing effects of radiation was observed in cell culture studies, but the potential differences between tumor and normal tissue remained underexplored 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 . More recently, further studies of UHDR RT using laser-accelerated protons found no dose rate dependent effects using a variety of in vitro cell culture techniques that included monolayer and 3D/organoid cultures of both cancer and normal cell lines 31 , 32 , 33 . However, compelling evidence from in vivo studies in the past decade suggest that at dose rates >40 Gy/s (termed “FLASH” RT), RT therapeutic index is increased due to a decrease in normal tissue damage with maintenance of similar tumor control as compared to RT delivered at standard dose rates 34 . Here, we explore the preclinical and clinical evidence for the FLASH effect and discuss the potential clinical significance for FLASH RT in patients with sarcoma.

 FLASH Delivery Devices

 UHDR beams can be delivered with multiple types of radiation. Most in vivo research has been performed using electron and proton beams, but several different modalities have been used to demonstrate UHDR beam delivery.

 Electron UHDR beams have been delivered using clinical linear accelerators that have been modified for use in FLASH experiments as well as dedicated systems used for preclinical and clinical electron FLASH RT. Clinical systems have been modified by operating in photon mode while removing the tungsten target used to produce photon beams, which achieves a high intensity electron beam pulse. Dedicated systems include intraoperative radiotherapy devices (Mobitron IOR) 35 that have been modified, as well as preclinical systems such as the Oriatron 36 , 37 . These systems are capable of delivering electron FLASH radiotherapy at conventional energies, which are well-suited for shallow targets and mice. Recently, very high energy electrons (VHEE) have been of interest for their ability to provide UHDR electron beams that are capable of penetrating more deeply. These beams encompass energies of 50–250 MeV, which are suited to target deep-seated tumors. Clinically used linear accelerators (linacs) have been converted to deliver UHDR beams for preclinical studies 38 , 39 at energies of 9–20 MeV. The conversion process has been streamlined so the linac is FLASH-capable within 20 minutes, and all beam specifications for clinical treatment are met after reverting back to normal settings. 40 The beam structure of electron beams exhibits a pulse structure with pulse repetition rates of 5 to 182 Hz with the different delivery devices ( Figure 2a ). For FLASH-enabled electron devices, the instantaneous pulse dose rate is high (in the range of ~10 6 Gy/s) resulting in average dose rates up to 300 Gy/s.

 Proton FLASH radiotherapy studies have been conducted using isochronous cyclotrons as well as synchrotrons. Clinical proton therapy centers have been able to achieve proton currents capable of demonstrating the FLASH effect in in vivo systems. These proton systems typically produce beams with energies around 230 MeV or above, which are capable of targeting areas that are 32 cm in depth in water. Isochronous cyclotron systems are capable of proton beam currents that are several hundred times that of conventional pencil beam currents used for clinical treatments at frequencies ~100 MHz, resulting in a beam delivery that is considered quasi-continuous ( Figure 2b ). These cyclotrons have spatially modulated magnetic fields. The FLASH effect has been demonstrated using both the entrance region 41 , 42 , 43 and spread-out Bragg peak (SOBP) 44 of high energy proton beams. UHDR beams have also been demonstrated with synchrocyclotron systems 45 , 46 , in which the frequency of the applied electric field is changed, and proton beams are generated with a pulsed time structure with macro-pulse durations of microseconds and a few milliseconds for the repetition period ( Figure 2c ). Ultra-high beam current is limited by the number of protons in the macro-pulse. Synchrocyclotron systems can better modulate the pulse dose compared to isochronous cyclotrons and can be used as a platform to investigate how the time structure affects the FLASH effect in vivo .

 Clinical proton therapy systems typically deliver pencil beam scanning (PBS) techniques to cover whole targets with small pencil beams. While the majority of proton FLASH studies have been conducted with a single beam of small field sizes, PBS FLASH has been demonstrated in pre-clinical models 47 and has also been used in clinical gantry-based treatment rooms 48 .

 In particular, proton FLASH is a promising technology for rapid implementation of FLASH RT in deep seated sarcomas due to the increased penetrating power over clinical electron beams and the relative ease in adapting clinical proton cyclotron systems to deliver UHDR. The complex treatment sites indicated with sarcoma RT necessitate the use of conformal treatment fields to minimize normal tissue dose and toxicity. The use of multiple transmission 49 , 50 , 51 or stopping proton beams 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 to generate conformal dose distributions is under intense research. Additionally, the research community is looking closely at additional machine safety requirements due to inherent risks of delivering at UHDR and developing new dosimetry equipment 56 , 57 , 58 and machine QA protocols 59 , 60 .

 Ion-beam radiotherapy can also be used for highly conformal irradiation of tumors as they feature sharper lateral penumbra and Bragg peaks compared to proton beams. However, heavy ion beam depth dose curves suffer from a fragmentation tail that deposits a few percent of dose beyond the distal falloff. Ion beams may be beneficial due to their high linear energy transfer (LET) and relative biological effectiveness (RBE), that have potential for overcoming radioresistence. While there is not much FLASH data available using ion beams, groups have demonstrated carbon ion FLASH using a heavy ion synchroton accelerator with upgrades. The number of particles per spill was increased while decreasing the extraction time to achieve dose rates ~100 Gy/s for a small field size 61 , 62 , 63 .

 To-date there have been limited studies using photon x-rays for UHDR radiobiological studies. There are currently not many devices that are able to deliver x-ray beams at high dose rates. Synchrotron facilities have been able to achieve UHDR x-rays from a synchrotron light source with mean energies between 50–100 keV 64 , 65 . Typical clinical x-ray beams are generated using an electron beam impinging on a high atomic number target; however, this Bremsstrahlung process is very inefficient and limited due to the high heat deposition at the target. Groups have shown a preclinical irradiator assembled using high-capacity 150 kVp fluoroscopy x-ray sources with rotating anode technology in a parallel-opposed arrangement for uniform delivery in a small animal 66 , 67 , 68 . Other groups are developing new accelerator technology for the clinical translation of UHDR beams, an example of which is the Pluridirectional High-energy Agile Scanning Electronic Radiotherapy (PHASER) device 69 . This involves compact and power-efficient linear accelerator and radiofrequency sources with outputs that are hundreds of times the output of current medical linear accelerator technologies.

 Preclinical studies of FLASH RT for sarcoma

 As introduced above, to-date the UHDR of FLASH have primarily been studied to increase the therapeutic window of RT by sparing normal tissues. Thus, FLASH is effectively pushing the dose response curve for normal tissue damage to higher doses, thereby widening the differential between radiation’s damaging effects on tumor compared to normal tissues at a given dose. For its effective application, it is essential that FLASH delivery does not similarly shift the dose response of tumors by increasing the dose needed to achieve tumor control relative to that provided by Standard RT. Given this importance, many preclinical studies have compared tumor response to FLASH vs Standard RT across a broad range of tumor models 70 , 71 
 72 . The majority of these studies have found tumor response to FLASH RT to be indistinguishable from response to Standard RT.

 In studies of proton FLASH with the entrance (plateau) region of a 230 MeV beam, Velalopoulou et al. 73 investigated the RT response of established RIF murine tumors, propagated in the hind leg of female C3H/He mice. Tumors were grown either subcutaneously or intramuscularly (orthotopic), then irradiated with 30 Gy of FLASH (69 – 124 Gy/s) or Standard (0.39 – 0.65 Gy/s) dose rate using a double scatter system. Curves of growth delay (subcutaneous) and tumor regression (intramuscular) were indistinguishable between FLASH and Standard dose rate. Moreover, tumor response to 12 Gy of FLASH vs Standard proton RT could not be distinguished in studies of an additional sarcoma model, i.e., tumors propagated subcutaneously using cells isolated from GEMM model LSL-KrasG12D/wt;p53 FL/FL . Similar results were found in studies of pencil beam proton FLASH by Sorensen et al. 74 in female CDF1 mice bearing subcutaneous C3H mouse mammary carcinoma in their hind limb. Proton pencil beam scanning was conducted with a beam energy of 244 MeV for Standard RT and 250 MeV for FLASH RT, delivered at dose rates of 0.33 – 0.63 Gy/s (beam on duration of 95 – 173 s) and 71 – 89 Gy/s (beam on duration of 0.46 – 0.84 s), respectively. Tumor response across range of treatment doses was indistinguishable between the dose rates.

 Interestingly, in studies of Carbon FLASH conducted by Tinganelli et al. 75 , the delivery of 18 Gy by either FLASH or Standard RT could control the growth of LM8 osteosarcoma cells, propagated subcutaneously in the hind limb of female C3H/He mice; however, FLASH was significantly (~30%) more effective than Standard RT in reducing the size of residual tumor. Even more impressively, FLASH reduced spontaneous metastasis to the lung. This was observed as a highly significant reduction in the fraction of metastatic tissue, determined from histological sections of lung tissue from mice treated with FLASH vs Standard RT for their subcutaneous LM8 tumors. In these investigations, 240 MeV/n 12 C ion radiation was delivered to 18 ± 0.3 Gy in 150 ± 20 ms (~5 × 10 9 ions) for FLASH and in 1 min for Standard RT. The average FLASH dose rate (target dose/total irradiation time) was calculated as ~100 Gy/sec, but raster beam scanning created a local FLASH dose rate that was 2–3 times higher than this average. Computational models of heavy ion effects on tissue oxygenation 76 led the authors to suggest that FLASH RT with 12 C ion increased tumor oxygenation, resulting in more RT-created tumor damage. This hypothesis requires testing specific to the conditions investigated, but it should be noted that FLASH sparing of normal tissue damage (in murine muscle) was found relative to Standard RT in these studies.

 FLASH sparing of RT toxicities

 When delivering RT in treatment of sarcoma, normal tissues such as skin, muscle and bone are all at risk of radiation-induced side effects. Murine studies find that, comprehensively, the UHDR of FLASH RT can improve overall survival. For example, Velalopoulou et al. 73 has shown 30 Gy of FLASH proton radiotherapy to reduce mortality or morbidity-dictating euthanasia after irradiation of the murine hind leg in female C57BL/6 mice. Namely, FLASH proton therapy significantly increased median survival to a value not reached with the ~250 day observation period, compared to a median of 211 days after irradiation with standard proton therapy ( Figure 3 ).

 The ability for FLASH to mitigate the severity or incidence of toxicity-limiting side effects has been established through studies that evaluate endpoints ranging from functional sparing to histological damage. Functional sparing of skeletal and dermal tissues by FLASH RT has been documented in rodent studies of muscle or skin contracture. Cunningham et al. 47 evaluated pencil beam scanning in the plateau region of a 250 Mev proton beam in irradiation of the hind limb of female C57BL/6 mice to 35 Gy with FLASH or Standard RT. At dose rates of both 57 and 115 Gy/s, FLASH reduced muscle contraction during the observation period (3 – 12 weeks post RT) relative to Standard RT (1 Gy/s). Similarly, in studies of skin contracture, Zhang et al. 77 evaluated responses of hind leg skin in female FVB/N mice to FLASH (130 Gy/s) vs Standard (0.4 Gy/s) delivery of double scatter protons. Using a mixed effect model of response to doses of 25 Gy, 27 Gy and 30 Gy, FLASH delivery significantly reduced skin contracture by 15% compared to Standard delivery. A dose effect was apparent, modeled by an increase in contraction of 1.1%/Gy.

 Histological studies further document tissue sparing by FLASH RT, including additional evidence of dose effects. For example, Zhang et al. 77 reported 25 Gy of Standard RT to produce epidermal thickness almost twice that of FLASH irradiated tissue. At 27 Gy, they found less of a difference between FLASH and Standard RT, and at 30 Gy no difference in epidermal thickness was detected between the dose rates. Velalopoulou et al 73 have also documented histological endpoints spared by FLASH proton RT in the leg tissue of female C57BL/6 mice. At 27 days after radiotherapy, delivery via FLASH significantly reduced epidermal necrosis and hyperplasia, as well as both muscle atrophy and hair follicle atrophy. In conjunction with these endpoints, optical imaging of inflammatory myeloid cells detected less inflammation after FLASH vs Standard RT.

 Histological analyses of FLASH effect on skin have also been carried out in research studies of pigs exposed to electron RT (4.5 – 6 MeV). Vozenin et al. 37 irradiated 2.6 cm diameter fields on the back of a Gottingen mini pig at doses ranging from 22 −34 Gy. RT was delivered at Standard (~5 Gy/min) or FLASH (~300 Gy/s) dose rate. Acute toxicity was limited to the destruction of the hair follicles, which occurred only in fields treated with Standard dose rate. Fibronecrosis was found as a late toxicity, but again it was detected only with Standard treatment. Histological analyses at 36 weeks after Standard RT detected severe dermal remodeling and inflammatory infiltrate, as well as epithelial ulceration and hyperkeratosis at the wound’s edge. FLASH effects were noted only as some depilation at 36 week post treatment. In further studies by this group, Bley et al. 78 investigated the effects of FLASH field size in two Gottingen mini pigs. Standard RT was not evaluated in these studies of 31 Gy due to its previously detected toxicities. However, FLASH RT to 31 Gy delivered as before (10 pulses of 1.8 μs, 100 Hz, 100 ms) caused minimal toxicity, thus new studies were conducted at a FLASH RT dose of 31 Gy to investigate the effects of increasing field size. Electron beam parameters for FLASH delivery were 20 pulses at a frequency of 100 Hz and pulse width of 1.8 μs. Total treatment time was 190 ms with an instantaneous dose rate of 8.61 × 10 5 and mean dose rate of 163 Gy/s. Responses were compared between field sizes of 3.5 cm × 4.5 cm and 8 cm × 8 cm. Results found that field size had no effect on macroscopic acute toxicities and only depilation was noted as a subacute toxicity. However, late toxicities were found to increase as a function of field size. With smaller fields, cutaneous damage included erythema, ulceration, hyperkeratosis and skin contracture over 8 months of observation. With the larger field, damage progressed from telangiectasia to field-wide epithelial ulceration and the formation of necrotic scabs.

 The clinically relevant endpoint of dermatitis further establishes the tissue sparing benefits of FLASH RT. During electron (16 MeV) RT of the C57BL/6 (female) murine lung (right sided hemithoracic 2cm × 2cm field), Soto et al. 79 noted the macroscopic incidence and severity of ulceration in overlaying skin was less with FLASH than with Standard dose rate. RT was delivered in 5 μs pulses at FLASH vs Standard dose rates with respective beam parameters of 90 vs 72 Hz in frequency, 2.0 vs 0.00104 Gy /pulse, 4.0 × 10 5 vs 207 Gy/s in instantaneous dose rate, and 180 vs 0.0747 Gy/s in mean dose rate. As found in other studies, the differential in response to FLASH vs Standard RT differed as a function of dose over those (10 Gy, 16 Gy, 20 Gy, 30 Gy, 40 Gy) that were evaluated. FLASH delivery significantly decreased skin toxicity compared to Standard RT upon reaching a dose of 40 Gy.

 Others have similarly noted skin reaction to be spared when FLASH RT is delivered with protons. Singers Sorensen et al. 80 treated the right hind limb of CDF1 female mice with proton pencil beam scanning in the entrance plateau of the beam at energies of 244 MeV for Standard (dose rates of 0.35 – 0.4 Gy/s) and 250 MeV for FLASH (dose rates of 65 – 92 Gy/s) RT. A dose modifying factor was calculated as the ratio of doses needed to produce moist desquamation in 50% of mice treated with Standard compared to FLASH RT. This factor ranged from 1.44 to 1.58, meaning that 44 – 58% higher dose was needed to produce the same biological effect with FLASH compared to Standard RT. In studies performed at 35 Gy, Cunningham et al. 47 showed proton irradiation with FLASH delivery to significantly reduce skin toxicity (hind limb of female C57Bl/6 mice) compared to Standard RT ( Figure 4 ). This included significant delays in the development of moist desquamation and its faster resolution, alongside a less severe reaction. Velalopoulou et al. 73 also showed proton irradiation with 30 Gy of FLASH RT to reduce the severity of maximum dermatitis in the hind limb of female C57Bl/6 mice over an 8 month observation period, compared to 30 Gy of Standard RT. Moreover, they uniquely evaluated lymphedema as an endpoint, showing at 45 Gy that FLASH RT significantly reduced the severity of lymphedema compared to that resulting from Standard RT among mice that developed this toxicity.

 Finally, sparing FLASH of bone is relevant in radiotherapy applications to sarcoma, as well as other treatment indications. FLASH RT delivery to bone has been deemed safe in clinical trial of its application to painful extremity bone metastases (FAST- 01 trial) 48 , and an ongoing trial is evaluating safety for bone metastases to the thorax (FAST-02). Data by Velalopoulou et al. 73 suggests FLASH to create less damage and associated bone remodeling than Standard RT in the legs of treated C57BL/6 mice. Nonetheless, as discussed in more detail later, the detection of bone toxicity (osteonecrosis) in clinical studies of canine and feline patients establish it possible to exceed FLASH protection when delivering single high doses, especially in dosimetry hotspots 78 , 81 . This is not unexpected given the dose dependencies of FLASH sparing in other tissue types, such as skin as discussed above. It emphasizes the importance of dose conformity in FLASH RT and suggests that clinically used approaches such as fractionation should be evaluated for FLASH effect on tissues such as skin and bone.

 Elucidating mechanisms of FLASH RT sparing

 Much work is being done to understand the mechanisms by which FLASH produces its sparing effect in normal tissues, including those of the skin, muscle and bone. FLASH-induced differentials in DNA damage (including study of radical recombination), oxygen depletion, stem cell damage and vascular/immune responses have been studied to date as targets and mechanisms of FLASH RT effect in the field at large. 82 , 83 , 84 The possible contributions of several of these mechanisms to FLASH effects in skin have been evaluated alongside the above-described studies of FLASH RT tissue sparing.

 Evidence of stem cell sparing in the skin of FLASH RT-treated animals has been detected in murine and porcine tissues. Using the irradiation parameters described above, Vozenin et al. 37 evaluated hair follicle pathology in biopsies of porcine skin collected at 36 weeks after electron RT with FLASH or Standard dose rate. Compared to untreated skin, Standard RT dramatically reduced the number of hair follicles, while in contrast, such reductions were not found with FLASH RT. This was accompanied by the preservation of epidermal stem cells (CD34+) in the bulge region of the hair follicle of FLASH RT irradiated skin relative to Standard irradiated skin. Similar results were found in murine studies. Velalopoulou et al. 73 reported that at 18 days after 30 Gy of FLASH RT versus Standard RT to the hind limb of C57BL/6 mice, the skin of Standard treated animals contained significantly fewer Lgr6+ stem cells than did the skin of animals that received FLASH dose rate. Since sparing of skin stem cells by FLASH RT could support tissue repair and regeneration, these findings support a prevailing current of thought that differentials in wound repair may accompanying FLASH versus Standard dose rates.

 The oxygen depletion hypothesis of FLASH-mediated sparing is an area of much debate. This hypothesis states that FLASH dose rate causes rapid radiochemical oxygen depletion that outpaces oxygen resupply in tissues, thereby providing a protective effect. This effect is expected to enable FLASH protection of normal tissue, but not tumors, because oxygen depletion would be of less consequence and thus less protective in the already poorly oxygenated environment of a tumor. Indeed, in studies of RIF sarcoma tumors grown in C3H/He mice, Van Slyke et al. 85 reported the extent of oxygen depletion to be smaller and the time required for recovery to be longer in tumors compared to normal murine thigh muscle exposed to proton FLASH RT. After Standard RT, little oxygen depletion was detected in both tumor and normal muscle. Therefore, it seems that FLASH can promote some oxygen depletion in the in vivo environment, but the magnitude of this change may not solely explain the amount of protection that FLASH RT provides 85 , 86 , 87 . Moreover, current thought dictates that decreased production of reactive oxygen species during FLASH 88 should consider not only FLASH oxygen depletion, but also the process of radical self annihilation via recombination. This is thought to be promoted under FLASH conditions due to the production of high concentrations of radical species in a short time frame by FLASH RT 89 .

 In investigations of skin, Zhang et al. 77 studied the effects of tissue oxygenation on response to FLASH RT in female FVB/N mice exposed to FLASH (130 Gy/s) versus Standard (0.4 Gy/s) proton RT. Whereas FLASH RT significantly reduced skin contracture in these animals compared to Standard RT, in the presence of either hypoxia (induced by tourniquet of the leg) or hyperoxia (induced by breathing 100% oxygen) the FLASH benefit disappeared. The authors note that skin has a relatively low concentration of oxygen compared to other tissues, thus FLASH RT may be capable of consuming a large fraction of the available oxygen in skin to produce a protective effect. They rationalize that the FLASH benefit disappears under hyperoxic conditions because significant hypoxia may not be produced by FLASH due to higher initial oxygen concentration, while the benefit also disappears under severely hypoxic conditions because some oxygen is necessary for FLASH-mediated oxygen depletion to be relevant. Under the hypoxic conditions, higher doses of both FLASH and Standard RT were necessary to produce any damage, but it was similar between the doses rates, suggesting that levels of pre-existing hypoxia provided protection from damage by both modalities of delivery. Finally, it is also important to consider the benefits of oxygen depletion within specific regions of the tissue. Namely, stem cell niches are expected to be more susceptible to oxygen depletion given their comparatively hypoxic state relative to other tissue areas, thus FLASH RT could facilitate stem cell sparing via an oxygen depletion process 77 . Ongoing studies are needed to confirm or refute these expectations and better define the contribution of FLASH RT created oxygen depletion to its sparing effects across different tissue types and their subpopulations of cells.

 In other studies of FLASH RT of murine hind leg, there is clear evidence that FLASH RT alters cytokine production. Cunningham et al. 47 used a multiplex assay to measure levels of 12 cytokines in the blood of female C57Bl/6 mice at 12 weeks after irradiation of hind leg with FLASH versus Standard proton beam scanning. Results found CXCL-1 and G-CSF to be increased in Standard versus FLASH irradiated animals, whereas GM-CSF was less after Standard versus FLASH RT. They noted the GM-CSF to G-CSF ratio to be lower after Standard versus FLASH RT with observations that this ratio is found to inversely correlate with tissue toxicity in patients with cystic fibrosis. IL-6 levels were elevated by FLASH (but not by Standard RT), and TGFβ1 levels were significantly elevated in the plasma of Standard versus FLASH exposed mice at both 1 and 4 days after irradiation. Due to its association with RT-induced fibrosis, TGFβ1 levels are of high interest in study of FLASH versus Standard RT. Notably, Velalopoulou et al. 73 found TGFβ1 levels in the skin of both mice and canines to be elevated (relative to untreated skin) only by Standard and not by FLASH RT.

 Evidence of differences in cytokine production after FLASH versus Standard RT strongly point to potential for differentials in immune response as a function of dose rate. This is supported by the above-described findings of Tinganelli et al. 75 that carbon ion FLASH RT reduced the burden of lung metastases compared to Standard RT. In investigations of other tissue types, some investigators have identified cellular level differences in immune response to FLASH versus Standard RT 90 , 91 but much investigation remains to be done in this area.

 Finally, RNA-seq analyses by Velalopoulou et al. 73 of murine treated skin provide more broad insight into mechanisms by which FLASH provides its sparing effects. These results found proton FLASH, but not Standard RT to upregulate pathways related to tissue and vascular repair, such as anatomical structure and blood vessel morphogenesis. In contrast, Standard RT uniquely upregulated pathways associated with apoptosis and apoptotic signaling.

 Clinical studies of FLASH RT in spontaneously occurring tumors

 Prior to initiating larger clinical trials of FLASH RT in humans, pilot and feasibility trials are needed to both finalize the technological methods and logistics for clinical FLASH RT and begin investigating classical radiobiological parameters such as total dose delivered, fractionation, and partial volume irradiation effects in spontaneously occurring (clinical) tumors. In this regard, clinical trials using veterinary oncology patients represent an important tool that can provide potential clinical benefit to these animals while continuing to develop the extensive technical, biological and clinical knowledge necessary for FLASH implementation 92 . Such studies can also help to illustrate the potential long-term toxicities that may be difficult to observe using small animal models. Indeed, initial studies of electron FLASH-RT on cats with squamous cell cancer (SCC) of the nasal planum suggested that single fraction FLASH with doses of ~30 Gy could achieve tumor control with acceptable levels of acute toxicity 37 . Similar work using dogs with spontaneous cancers established that doses of 15–35 Gy electron FLASH could be delivered and were generally well tolerated, but also noted sub-acute mucosal toxicities that were slow to resolve in a dog that received 35 Gy for intranasal SCC 93 . Further studies of dogs with extremity osteosarcoma suggested that proton FLASH RT with doses up to 24 Gy might be safe and effective. In this study, dogs with extremity OSA were enrolled in a trial of preoperative RT (prior to standard of care limb amputation), in which they were randomized to receive proton FLASH-RT vs Standard dose rate proton RT in a single fraction with doses from 4–24 Gy. Histologic analyses suggest no difference in tumor necrosis between FLASH proton RT and Standard proton RT, while molecular analysis demonstrated that FLASH proton RT dramatically reduces induction of TGF-beta (fibrosis) in normal skin as compared to standard dose rate protons 73 .

 Additional studies in feline and canine head and neck cancers have demonstrate that single fraction doses ≥ 30 Gy might have significant late toxicities despite the FLASH dose rate. In a follow up study in cats with nasalplanum SCC, patients were randomized to receive 30 Gy in one single fraction using electron FLASH-RT vs standard dose rate, fractionated RT. This trial was prematurely interrupted due to maxillary bone necrosis, which occurred 9 to 15 months after FLASH electron RT in 3 of 7 cats (43%), as compared with 0 of 9 cats treated with a standard of care RT protocol 78 . In dogs with oral cavity tumors treated with 30–36 Gy electron FLASH RT, significant subacute and late toxicities were observed 81 . Here, 4/10 dogs experienced soft tissue ulceration at 1 month and 3/4 dogs who evaluable at 12 months had developed osteoradionecrosis. Follow up studies of electron or proton FLASH in dogs with head and neck cancer are ongoing or planned and are evaluating the potential of fractionated (2–3 fractions) FLASH-RT to provide for further increases in therapeutic index (Cengel, Børresen, personal communication).

 One human trial of relatively low dose FLASH proton RT has recently been completed. In this single arm trial at the Cincinnati Children’s/UC Health Proton Therapy Center, clinical workflow, feasibility, and early clinical outcomes were tested using a single 8 Gy FLASH proton RT treatment for patients with symptomatic bone metastases. Results of this trial demonstrate that FLASH proton RT can be delivered with acceptable safety parameters and produces expected levels of pain control as compared to historical 8 Gy standard treatment 48 .

 Conclusions

 Emerging research with the high dose rates of FLASH radiotherapy identifies its potential to reduce normal tissues toxicities, including the damage of tissues such as skin, muscle and bone that are relevant in RT of sarcomas as well as other solid cancers. This body of research incorporates study of mostly electron and proton FLASH, as well as selected study of carbon ion FLASH, which reflects the current state of available technology in the field. The accumulating body of literature provides consistent evidence that FLASH RT spares the structure and function of skeletal and dermal tissues without compromising therapeutic efficacy in response of numerous murine sarcoma models.

 Importantly, as a collective, these studies also provide direction in areas of ongoing investigative need. For example, is noteworthy that the vast majority of these studies were conducted in female mice, which emphasizes the need to also evaluate FLASH effect in male model systems. Indeed, recent evidence suggests that the FLASH effect may be even more pronounced in males 94 . The research further emphasizes that dose effects are evident in treatment with FLASH RT, as to be expected given that RT response is well established to be dose dependent. Consequently, absence of a FLASH effect may be found when either low doses produce mild damage 95 or high doses produce severe damage with both Standard and FLASH delivery. To this point, clinical studies in large animals have shown single high doses of FLASH to produce significant toxicities not unlike that expected from Standard RT. Thus, it is generally thought that higher FLASH total doses should utilize fractionated delivery to enable preservation of the FLASH benefit 96 , 97 . This fits well with the ongoing trends in clinical RT toward ultra-hypofractionated RT regimens. Indeed, FLASH RT may allow the safe extension of these regimens to larger tumors or tumors located near radiosensitive structures. Ongoing and future studies, including large animal and human clinical trials, will be needed to comprehensively evaluate fractionation schemes, alongside other delivery parameters, for FLASH RT of sarcomas (as well as other tumor types) and their associated normal tissues.

 Finally, it is noteworthy that a recent human clinical trial has established that FLASH can be delivered safely to patients, with the first of these trials conducted at sites of bone metastasis. These findings, together with the body of preclinical evidence in FLASH RT of sarcoma and skin support continued investigation toward clinical translation of FLASH’s high dose rate for the treatment of patients with sarcomas.
