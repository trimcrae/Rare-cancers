 Abstract

 Purpose

 Mesenchymal chondrosarcoma (MCS), a rare malignant tumor arising from cartilage-forming tissues, is known for its resistance to conventional therapies. This case report presents the first documented use of boron neutron capture therapy (BNCT) for treating a patient with chondrosarcoma.

 Methods and Materials

 The treatment was carried out using the NeuPex AB-BNCT system in combination with the boron compound NBB-001 (boronophenylalanine) and the NeuMANTA treatment planning system. Over the course of 2 BNCT sessions, the patient experienced significant tumor reduction and a favorable clinical response, with no severe adverse effects reported. The therapy was guided by 18 F-boronophenylalanine positron emission tomography-computed tomography imaging scans for planning and magnetic resonance imaging scans for outcome assessment.

 Results

 The targeted nature of BNCT resulted in a 45.8% reduction in the tumor's maximum dimension, achieving a partial response according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1. Most adverse events were mild (grades 1-2) and manageable, with only grade 3 mucositis observed, which was transient and resolved with supportive care.

 Conclusions

 This case study underscored the potential of BNCT as a promising treatment modality for MCS, particularly for tumors that are resistant to conventional radiation therapy. Further research is warranted to refine treatment protocols and explore the broader applicability of BNCT in managing MCS and other challenging malignancies.

 Introduction

 Mesenchymal chondrosarcoma (MCS) is a malignant mesenchymal tumor that originates in cartilage-forming tissues of the bones or soft tissues and is a relatively rare sarcoma, accounting for 0.2% of all adult cancers. This tumor is characterized by the production of cartilage matrices from neoplastic tissue lacking osteoid. MCS can occur at any age, but it is the most common primary bone cancer in adults, constituting 40% 1 of such cases. These tumors are typically found in the pelvis and proximal femur, with nearly 90% classified as low to intermediate grade. However, MCS is a rare, nonconventional, high-grade variant. Studies have shown that the 5- and 10-year overall survival rates for MCS are 51% and 43%, respectively, with a metastatic rate of 10.6% at presentation. 2 , 3 

 The effectiveness of chemotherapy in treating MCS remains uncertain because of the lack of prospective randomized trials. MCS is known for its resistance to conventional radiation therapy (RT); however, proton RT, either alone or combined with photon RT, has been associated with excellent local tumor control and long-term survival. 4 , 5 , 6 Additionally, carbon-ion RT has shown high local control rates in patients with skull base MCS and other unresectable MCS. 7 , 8 Surgical excision with negative margins is recommended for these tumors, though it can result in significant disability and reduced quality of life. 9 Recently, there has been a growing focus on avoiding amputation and preserving cosmetic and functional outcomes without compromising survival rates.

 Boron neutron capture therapy (BNCT) is a cell-selective cancer treatment. In this approach, nonradioactive 10 B atoms selectively accumulate in malignant cells surrounded by normal cells. When these atoms absorb low-energy (< 0.5 eV) thermal neutrons, they disintegrate into a recoil lithium nucleus ( 7 Li) and an alpha particle ( 4 He). The high-linear energy transfer properties of these particles can effectively induce malignant cell death. 10 BNCT is emerging as a promising modality for managing MCS because of its targeted RT nature, which has demonstrated high efficacy in radiobiology. Previous studies have indicated its effectiveness in treating osteosarcoma. 11 , 12 , 13 

 Recent research has highlighted that the uptake of boronophenylalanine (BPA), a commonly used boron compound in BNCT, is significantly mediated by L-type amino acid transporter-1 (LAT-1). 14 LAT-1 is known to be overexpressed in various malignant tumors, including MCS. Koshi et al 15 provide evidence that LAT-1 expression is elevated in MCS (50%), suggesting that these tumors may have a higher capacity for BPA uptake, thereby enhancing the efficacy of BPA-BNCT. This connection implies that MCS could be a particularly suitable candidate for BNCT, because the high LAT-1 expression may facilitate better boron delivery to the tumor cells, making the treatment more effective.

 This study reported the first application of BNCT in a patient with MCS, using the NeuPex 16 AB-BNCT system in combination with the boron compound NBB-001 (a.k.a. BPA) and the NeuMANTA 17 treatment planning system (TPS). Two BNCT sessions were administered, resulting in significant tumor reduction and a favorable clinical response with no serious adverse effects observed. The findings from this case suggest that BNCT could offer a novel and effective treatment option for MCS, particularly for tumors resistant to conventional RTs.

 The Patient with MCS

 In August 2018, patient G (the code is derived from the abbreviation of the patient’s surname and holds no additional significance), a 31-year-old Chinese male, presented to the hospital with numbness in his lower left lip. A 3-dimensional computed tomography (CT) scan revealed destruction of the mandibular bone and a space-occupying lesion in the left molar area. In October 2018, a partial mandibulectomy was performed to remove the tumor and a portion of the left mandible. Postoperative pathologic and immunohistochemical examinations identified the tumor as MCS, with a pathologic stage of pT4aN0M0 G3, corresponding to stage 3 according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer 8th edition staging system for soft tissue sarcoma of the head and neck. 18 

 The patient did not receive any postoperative adjuvant treatment and showed no recurrence during regular follow-up examinations until February 2021, when a follow-up positron emission tomography-CT (PET-CT) scan revealed a hypermetabolic mass in the mandible and gingival areas around the surgical site, suggesting local recurrence. Despite this finding, the patient declined a second surgery because of concerns about disfigurement, difficulty feeding and chewing, and impaired speech. Over the following year, the tumor gradually increased in size. In May 2022, the patient underwent anti-programmed death receptor1 monoclonal antibody (tislelizumab) treatment for 3 months, which failed to effectively inhibit tumor growth, reduce tumor size, or delay disease progression.

 In September 2022, the patient was admitted to the Cancer Center at Xiamen Humanity Hospital and underwent evaluation according to standard clinical protocols. Patient G declined a second surgery to preserve his quality of life and mental well-being. Conventional RT was deemed ineffective for treating such a large MCS tumor, and advanced options like proton or carbon-ion therapy were not available in Fujian Province or neighboring regions. A preliminary dose evaluation using BNCT TPS indicated that BNCT could be a promising alternative. Following detailed discussion and risk disclosure, the patient consented to participate in an investigator-initiated trial of BNCT (Chinese Clinical Trial Registry: ChiCTR2200066473). By this time, the tumor was rapidly progressing, with associated bleeding and an inability to chew. Physical examination was unremarkable except for the presence of the tumor, which had caused carcinelcosis, occupying most of the oral cavity and invading the floor of the mouth. An 18 F-fluorodeoxyglucose ( 18 F-FDG) PET-CT scan showed tumor 18 F-FDG uptake with a maximum standardized uptake value (SUV max ) of 10.3. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan revealed that the tumor’s longest diameter was 9.4 cm on day-16 relative to BNCT, growing to 10.8 cm by day-1, as depicted in Fig. 1A . The cancer was staged as rT4aN0M0.

 Materials and Methods

 The BNCT was conducted using the NeuPex AB-BNCT system, in conjunction with BPA (NBB-001), both developed by the Neuboron Medical Group. The treatment plan was formulated using the NeuMANTA TPS, which uses the Monte Carlo dose engine COMPASS (COMpact PArticle Simulation System). 19 

 The limited penetration depth of the BNCT epithermal neutron beam, which attenuates rapidly in hydrogen-rich materials, necessitated a 2-session irradiation strategy to effectively treat this large MCS tumor. Furthermore, in large-volume tumors with high boron-10 concentrations, a more pronounced neutron flux depression is anticipated because of the self-shielding effect, resulting in significantly reduced dose delivery to deeper regions. Although multiportal irradiation could potentially mitigate this challenge, current BNCT facilities rely on fixed beams, making it technically unfeasible to implement for this patient. To overcome this limitation, a 2-fraction approach was adopted, capitalizing on anticipated tumor shrinkage after the first session to enhance the subsequent dose to the deeper sections of the original gross tumor volume (GTV) along the beam path. Additionally, a 3-month interval between sessions was established to prioritize safety, allowing sufficient time for normal tissue repair.

 Before each BNCT session, the patient underwent MRI, CT, and 4-borono-2-[ 18 F]fluoro-phenylalanine ( 18 F-BPA) PET-CT scans. The uptake of 18 F-BPA was used to estimate the biodistribution of BPA within the tumor and normal tissues. 20 , 21 , 22 
 18 F-BPA is a PET scan tracer that shares a similar molecular structure and transport mechanism with BPA, predominantly through the LAT-1. LAT-1 is known to be overexpressed in various malignant tumors, including MCS, facilitating the selective accumulation of BPA in these cancer cells. This relationship allows 18 F-BPA PET scan imaging to serve as a noninvasive predictor of BPA distribution prior to BNCT for the studied MCS patient, providing crucial information on the potential efficacy of the treatment and minimizing the risk of radiation injury to surrounding normal tissues.

 In addition to 18 F-BPA PET, MRI and CT scans were also performed. MRI scans were used to contour the anatomical gross tumor volume (GTV anat ) and to monitor treatment outcomes, whereas 18 F-BPA PET-CT scans were used to determine the targeted tissue-to-blood ratio (TBR) of BPA uptake and to calculate the heterogeneous boron dose in GTV anat . 23 NeuMANTA constructed a voxel model based on CT scan images, using image fusion technology to incorporate MRI and 18 F-BPA PET scan data, ensuring accurate dose calculations.

 The first irradiation was performed in October 2022, and the second irradiation was conducted 3 months later. The NBB-001 was administered using the continuous infusion method, also known as the Ono method. The patient received 200 mg/kg BPA over 2 hours, after which the infusion rate was reduced to 100 mg/kg for the remainder of the irradiation session. The nominal BPA dose was described as 500 mg/kg. Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry was used to measure boron concentration in the blood, allowing for adjustments to irradiation time to ensure that the therapeutic dose remained within the physician-recommended safety limits.

 To maximize the dose delivered to GTV anat within an admissible irradiation time, the first treatment was performed with the patient in a sitting position, leaning against the beam collimator with the tumor partially enclosed. For the second BNCT, because the tumor had significantly shrunk, the patient was placed in a supine position and automatically positioned using a robotic arm. Long irradiation courses required careful consideration of the side effects on normal tissues. To ensure tolerable and recoverable side effects, the mucosa, defined as twice the blood SUV value from the 18 F-BPA PET scan image, was used to determine the irradiation time. The dose limitation for the mucosa for the individual treatments was set at D max ≤ 12 Gy-Eq (the maximum biologically equivalent dose allowed) and V 15 
 Gy-Eq ≤ 1% (indicating that < 1% of the mucosa volume could receive over 15 Gy-Eq).

 The dose and boron concentration dependence were corrected using a premodeled algorithm in the TPS and dose monitoring system. Based on the treatment plan, real-time beam monitoring, and measured boron concentration in the patient’s blood samples, the BNCT dose received by the patient was estimated. Contrast-enhanced MRI, 18 F-BPA-PET-CT, and 18 F-FDG-PET-CT scans were performed 3 months after each BNCT session. Additionally, 3 months after the second BNCT, an ultrasound-guided core-needle biopsy of the mandibular tumor was performed, and second-generation sequencing was used to analyze genome-wide gene expression profiling in the tumor tissue.

 From Fig. 2C , we can derive the D 80 for GTV anat —the dose received or exceeded by 80% of the GTV anat volume. The D 80 values were 8.46 Gy-Eq and 14.12 Gy-Eq for the first and second irradiations, respectively. The GTV anat volumes during the 2 irradiations were 387.5 and 201.8 cc, respectively, whereas the volumes receiving doses below 14 Gy-Eq (V 14 Gy-Eq ) did not exceed 48.6% (188.3 cc) and 19.6% (39.6 cc), respectively. Additionally, the dose homogeneity index (HI) was calculated from the DVH as the ratio between D 5 and D 95 , representing the uniformity of the dose distribution within the treated volume. An ideal HI value is 1, whereas a higher value indicates a more inhomogeneous dose distribution. In this treatment scenario, the HI values for the first and second irradiations were 4.74 and 2.52, respectively.

 Figure 3 displays the 2-dimensional dose distribution for the 2 sessions. The first BNCT irradiation revealed a significant inhomogeneous dose distribution, primarily because of the neutron flux distribution within the target region. This distribution is influenced by the geometry and size of the tumor, as well as by the self-shielding effect caused by the large number of boron-10 atoms accumulated in the tumor’s large size. This phenomenon was much less pronounced during the second irradiation because the tumor had already shrunk, and the TBR had decreased. Additionally, the maximum skin doses recorded for the first and second treatments were 10.2 and 13.4 Gy-Eq, whereas the mucosa doses were 11.44 and 16.74 Gy-Eq (with V 15 Gy-Eq,mucosa ≤ 0.9%, ie, 0.19 cc).

 Clinical observation

 The hemorrhage caused by the tumor ceased within 24 hours following the first BNCT session. Within a week after the procedure, a significant reduction in ulceration was observed, with whitish exudates present on the tumor’s surface. Regular follow-up imaging revealed that the tumor had shrunk 3 months after BNCT, with its maximum dimension reducing by 26.0%, from 108.8 to 80.5 mm. This reduction in tumor volume is illustrated in Fig. 1 and the MRI scan images shown in Fig. 4 . Based on MRI scan images, the GTV anat volume decreased by 47.9%, from 387.5 to 201.8 cc, between the first and second BNCT sessions. The tumor continued to regress, decreasing by an additional 4.69% (30.7% in total), with a maximum diameter of 75.4 mm on MRI scan images taken 3 months after the second BNCT session. According to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1, 24 the patient achieved a partial response (PR). At 150, 250, and 500 days after the second BNCT session, the tumor continued to decrease in size, with maximum diameters of 65.4 mm (−39.9%), 64.0 mm (−41.2%), and 59.0 mm (−45.8%) on MRI scan images, respectively.

 The results of the 18 F-BPA and 18 F-FDG PET scans are shown in Fig. 5 . Figure 5A illustrates the 18 F-BPA PET scan conducted before the first BNCT, where the SUV information serves as a reference for the macro-distribution of boron-10. The figures demonstrate that following the first BNCT, the lateral portions of the tumor closest to the skin exhibited low 18 F-FDG uptake and no 18 F-BPA uptake, indicating metabolic changes induced by the initial treatment. In contrast, the medial region, which had invaded the floor of the mouth, maintained high 18 F-BPA uptake. Additionally, 18 F-FDG-PET-CT scans showed medial tumor 18 F-FDG uptake with an SUV max of 8.6. Three months after the second BNCT, both the medial and lateral parts of the tumor displayed low 18 F-BPA uptake. The 18 F-FDG-PET-CT scan at this time revealed that the medial tumor 18 F-FDG uptake had significantly decreased, with an SUV max of 3.6. Ultrasound-guided core-needle biopsy confirmed the presence of MCS, and RNA sequencing identified an HEY1-NCOA2 fusion, supporting the pathologic diagnosis of MCS. In addition, the Ki67 expression was 10%, indicating reduced tumor proliferation and suggesting the efficacy of BNCT. However, 150 days after the second treatment, the 18 F-FDG-PET-CT scan showed tumor 18 F-FDG uptake with an SUV max of 6.2. Additionally, a high 18 F-FDG uptake lesion, measuring 1.6 cm in diameter, was detected in the tail of the pancreas, suggesting tumor metastasis, which was confirmed using a contrast-enhanced MRI scan.

 Adverse events were assessed using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events grading scales (version 5.0). Grades 1 to 2 adverse reactions primarily included alopecia, dermatitis, hyperamylasemia, hypogeusia, and nausea, with most resolving within 30 days. However, oral mucositis reached grade 3 severity following the second irradiation session, likely caused by cumulative effects and the higher mucosa dose during the second course. After the second course of treatment, mucositis resolved within a month, and symptoms such as alopecia, dysgeusia, and dry mouth had disappeared by the 60-day follow-up.

 Discussion

 TBR and dose performance

 The TBR frequency counts during the first irradiation session were highly inhomogeneous, leading to a strong dependence of the boron dose on the spatial distribution of boron-10. Consequently, a fixed TBR should not be uniformly applied, considering the tumor's large size and potential self-shielding effect. An inhomogeneous TBR must be used to ensure accurate dose calculation. The influence of tumor size and TBR was confirmed by the HI values from the 2 irradiations. The DVH and HI from the second irradiation were superior to those from the first, implying that either multiple irradiations were required for better tumor control or a multiportal irradiation approach was necessary to improve dose homogeneity and avoid cold spots.

 The difference in TBRs between the first and second BNCT sessions, from 1.92 to 1.45, may be attributed to decreased cell viability after the first treatment, leading to less active LAT-1 expression. This observation aligns with findings by Wang et al. 25 Furthermore, the results complied with the low Ki67 expression of the biopsy sample. Given the reduced TBR in the second irradiation, a multiportal irradiation strategy might be preferable. However, the tumor’s large size and its protrusion made this approach challenging because of the inevitable air gap between the beam exit and the tumor, resulting in reduced neutron flux. Therefore, a second irradiation was chosen.

 Traditionally, a score > 2.5 has been considered a key criterion for selecting patients for BNCT. However, in the case of Patient G, significant therapeutic effects were observed despite the TBR scores being 1.92 and 1.45 during the first and second irradiation sessions, respectively, which corresponded to D 80 values of 8.46 and 14.12 Gy-Eq. This outcome suggests that a lower threshold score of TBR might still be effective for treating MCS with BNCT. Nonetheless, further studies involving more cases are needed to establish a more precise selection criterion for BNCT in patients with MCS.

 Potential impact of high-linear energy transfer radiation on tumor control

 Typically, achieving effective tumor control for MCS requires a radiation dose of approximately 70 Gy. In this study, however, the delivered total D 80 dose to the MCS was only 22.58 Gy-Eq. This value corresponds to an equivalent dose in 2-Gy fractions of 41.4 Gy and a biologically effective dose of 49.78 Gy, assuming an α:β ratio of 10. These calculated an equivalent dose in 2-Gy fractions, and biologically effective dose values are significantly lower than the conventional therapeutic dose of 70 Gy.

 The observed clinical effectiveness despite these lower doses may suggest a beneficial impact associated with the high-linear energy transfer characteristics of BNCT. Such effectiveness might indicate a lower α:β ratio (for example, approximately 7.8) of BNCT for MCS. Alternatively, this discrepancy might reflect an underestimation of the compound biological effectiveness factor for BPA specific to MCS. However, because of the limitations inherent in a single-case study, further research involving a larger patient cohort is necessary to validate these hypotheses.

 Tumor response evaluation technique

 Evaluating treatment efficacy in the context of BNCT often relies on imaging techniques that capture changes in tumor size. The marked reduction in tumor volume and the attainment of a PR within 3 months posttreatment underscore the potential effectiveness of BNCT for managing MCS. Currently, RECIST v1.1 criteria are considered the gold standard for assessing disease progression or treatment response in solid tumors. However, RECIST v1.1 criteria may not always provide a comprehensive picture, especially for slow-growing, irregularly shaped tumors like MCS. These specific challenges might lead to an underestimation of the disease’s true extent. To enhance accuracy, integrating RECIST v1.1 criteria with volumetric analysis could offer a more nuanced approach to assessing tumor response. In addition, unlike traditional cytotoxic agents, BNCT’s clinical response patterns may involve complex phenomena such as pseudo-progression and delayed responses. Therefore, SUV max , as evaluated using PET scans, serves as an indicator of tumor uptake of fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG—sugar) and boronophenylalanine (BPA—amino acid), reflecting activity levels within tumor cells, and is an important tool for tumor surveillance. In this study, a discordance in tumor response evaluation between MRI and PET-CT scans was observed. This difference indicates that PET-CT scans could be a more effective surveillance method for monitoring patients receiving BNCT, because changes in metabolism may be detected more sensitively than alterations in tumor volume. Additionally, it points to the potential utility of PET as a follow-up tool for detecting MCS recurrence, given the tumor's known propensity for late recurrence. Further studies comparing 18 F-FDG-PET-CT, 18 F-FBPA-PET-CT, and MRI scans are warranted to substantiate these findings and refine the understanding of BNCT’s efficacy.

 Clinical findings

 Several preclinical studies and case reports have documented the safety and effectiveness of BNCT for specific sarcomas. For example, Futamura et al 11 reported successful outcomes with reactor-based BNCT for radiation-induced osteosarcoma, particularly when surgery and other modalities were not viable. Similarly, Fujimoto et al 26 demonstrated the potential of BNCT as an adjuvant therapy for synovial sarcoma. However, literature on the use of BNCT for MCS remains scarce. In this case study, Patient G underwent 2 BNCT sessions, resulting in significant tumor control, which has been maintained for 500 days after second BNCT at the time of this report. The treatment achieved a notable tumor regression of 45.8% after 2 courses, highlighting the remarkable efficacy of BNCT in this case.

 MCS can develop at any stage of life but are predominantly observed in the elderly. They are often distinguished from other sarcomas by a lower rate of distant metastasis and a better overall survival rate. Consequently, locoregional therapies play a significant role in treatment. The rarer subtypes, especially those in the head and neck, present particular challenges. Surgical interventions, though standard, often fall short of ensuring complete cures and may lead to disfigurement or significant physical and psychological distress, particularly in younger patients. This underscores the urgent need for innovative therapeutic options.

 BNCT has been used as both definitive and salvage therapy for head and neck tumors, with toxicities similar to those observed in conventional photon radiation. 27 , 28 , 29 An analysis of adverse events in the Japanese H&N BNCT trial (JHN002) 30 reported a range of symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, and fatigue, which resolved within 30 days. Prolonged symptoms included alopecia, dysgeusia, and dry mouth. Interestingly, alopecia was not associated with scalp inflammation and was also noted in our findings. Overall, the patient tolerated the 2 BNCT sessions, spaced 3 months apart, favorably, with manageable adverse events, consistent with findings from the JHN002 trial.

 This case report serves as a pioneering demonstration of BNCT’s effectiveness in treating MCS. Leveraging the NeuPex AB-BNCT system, NeuMANTA TPS, and NBB-001 (BPA), the successful application of BNCT was achieved. This study contributed valuable insights to the existing body of knowledge and may pave the way for broader applications of BNCT in treating various malignancies.

 Conclusion

 This study demonstrated the successful application of BNCT in treating a case of MCS, a rare and aggressive tumor known for its resistance to conventional therapies. Using the NeuPex AB-BNCT system in combination with the boron compound NBB-001 (BPA) and the NeuMANTA TPS, we observed significant tumor reduction, sustained local tumor control over 500 days, and a favorable clinical response with minimal adverse effects. The results indicate that BNCT offers a promising therapeutic option for managing MCS, especially in cases where traditional RT proves ineffective.

 The reduction in tumor size and TBR, as evidenced using MRI and PET/CT scan, along with the achievement of a PR according to RECIST 1.1 criteria, highlights the potential of BNCT as a targeted therapy. The treatment was well-tolerated, with manageable side effects, suggesting that BNCT could serve as a viable alternative or adjunct to existing treatment modalities for MCS.

 However, it is important to note that this study is based on a single case, and further research involving a larger cohort of patients is necessary to establish more robust criteria for patient selection and to optimize treatment protocols. Future research should investigate the incorporation of advanced imaging techniques to improve the precision of tumor response assessment, potentially offering enhanced screening for long-term outcomes following BNCT and better monitoring for MCS recurrence.

 In conclusion, BNCT represents a novel and effective approach for treating MCS, offering hope for improved outcomes in a patient population that currently has limited treatment options.
