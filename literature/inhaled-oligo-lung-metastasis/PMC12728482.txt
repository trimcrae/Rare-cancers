## Abstract

## Background
Brain metastasis, a leading cause of death in patients with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), arises from tumor cells adapting to the unique microenvironment of the brain through metabolic remodeling regulated by key oncogenes. Here, we aimed to determine the role of high mobility group protein box 3 (HMGB3) in regulating tumor cell metabolism to promote the progression and brain metastasis of LUAD.

## Methods
A LUAD cell model predisposed to brain metastasis was established, followed by differential gene expression analysis. HMGB3 expression was quantified via single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) and immunohistochemistry, with clinical relevance assessed in two retrospective cohorts: the primary LUAD and the LUAD brain metastasis cohorts. Gene enrichment analysis of scRNA‐seq and bulk RNA‐seq data, along with Western blotting, were performed to identify HMGB3‐associated pathways. Co‐immunoprecipitation combined with mass spectrometry was used to detect HMGB3‐interacting proteins. Gain‐of‐function, loss‐of‐function and rescue experiments targeting HMGB3 downstream pathways were conducted in vitro and in vivo.

## Results
HMGB3 expression was significantly elevated in both primary LUAD lesions and brain metastatic foci, and its upregulation was strongly associated with poor prognosis in LUAD patients, as well as in those with concomitant brain metastasis. HMGB3 enhanced the migration, invasion, and epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) capabilities of LUAD cells in vitro and promoted the development of brain metastasis in vivo. Mechanistically, HMGB3 recruited and interacted with single‐stranded DNA‐binding protein 1 (SSBP1), inducing its nuclear translocation and reprogramming mitochondrial metabolism. This process elevated cytoplasmic reactive oxygen species levels, which subsequently activated the phosphatidylinositol 3‐kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K‐Akt) signaling pathway through downregulating phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN), ultimately promoting tumor cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and EMT.

## Conclusions
This study demonstrated HMGB3 as a key regulator of the brain metastasis of LUAD, orchestrating tumor cells’ metabolic adaptation to the brain microenvironment through modulation of mitochondrial metabolism, thereby offering potential therapeutic targets for LUAD brain metastases.

## BACKGROUND
Brain metastasis occurs in approximately 10%–30% of patients with systemic malignancies, with particularly high incidence in cancers originating from the lung [ ], breast [ ], kidney [ ], and skin [ ]. Notably, nearly 50% of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) patients develop brain metastases, which dramatically elevates mortality [ ]. Despite advancements in diagnosis techniques and treatment strategies, persistent challenges such as drug resistance and neurological sequelae continue to contribute to high mortality and compromised long‐term outcomes in patients with LUAD. The molecular determinants that promote tumor aggressiveness and facilitate brain metastasis in patients with LUAD remain incompletely understood. Elucidating the underlying mechanisms driving the metastasis process may provide critical insights for the development of innovative therapeutic interventions.
It is well‐established that brain metastasis is driven by a multifaceted interplay of intrinsic factors, within cancer cells, encompassing genetic mutations [ ], epigenetic modifications [ ], and metabolic reprogramming [ , , ]. Multiple independent studies have consistently demonstrated the pivotal role of the phosphatidylinositol 3‐kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K‐Akt) signaling pathway in promoting brain metastasis across various cancer types, including lung cancer [ ], breast cancer [ ], and melanoma [ ]. Furthermore, microenvironment‐induced phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) loss mediated by exosomal microRNAs primes the outgrowth of brain metastases in breast cancer‐derived tumor cells [ ]. Protein biomarkers associated with the PI3K‐Akt pathway exhibit significantly higher expression levels in brain metastases than extracranial metastases [ ]. As a direct downstream effector of the PTEN‐PI3K‐Akt signaling axis, mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) is frequently activated in malignancies and serves as a central regulator of cell growth and metabolism. Specifically, mTOR signaling orchestrates the metabolism of amino acids, glucose, nucleotides, fatty acids, and lipids [ ].
Given the distinct cellular compositions and unique metabolic pools of the lung and brain tissues, metabolic reprogramming is essential for these brain‐metastatic tumor cells to survive and thrive in the brain [ ]. Several studies have highlighted that in the hypoxic brain metastatic microenvironment [ , ], tumor cells enhance lipid utilization through dynamin‐related protein 1 (DRP1)‐mediated mitochondrial metabolic remodeling [ , , ], thereby promoting their adaptation to the lipid‐rich and hypoxic brain environment. Furthermore, 3 additional studies have demonstrated the enrichment of mitochondrial metabolic remodeling features in brain metastases and utilized preclinical mouse models to underscore the potential therapeutic value of targeting mitochondrial metabolism in brain metastatic tumors [ , , ]. However, these studies have not comprehensively explored the upstream regulatory mechanisms of mitochondrial metabolic remodeling in brain metastases. The question of how tumor cells adapt to the unique metabolic pool of the brain microenvironment through mitochondrial metabolic remodeling remains largely unexplored.
High mobility group protein box 3 (HMGB3), a member of the high‐mobility group protein family, functions as a chromatin architectural protein that modulates DNA conformation to enhance transcription factor accessibility [ ]. Its DNA‐binding activity plays a critical regulatory role in fundamental cellular processes, including transcriptional regulation, chromatin remodeling, and DNA damage repair [ ]. Extensive research has identified HMGB3 as a ubiquitous oncogenic driver across multiple epithelial cancers, encompassing nasopharyngeal carcinoma [ ], prostate cancer [ ], cervical cancer [ ], pancreatic cancer [ ], small cell lung cancer [ ] and colorectal cancer [ ], with additional implications in therapeutic resistance [ , , ]. Nevertheless, the specific molecular mechanisms underlying HMGB3‐mediated promotion of brain metastases in LUAD remain to be fully elucidated.
To elucidate these mechanisms, we utilized intracardiac injection mouse models of brain metastasis and patient‐derived brain metastatic samples, complemented by deep sequencing analyses from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets for comprehensive analysis and validation. The experimental design integrated HMGB3‐mediated tumor phenotypes, mitochondrial functional assays, and interaction mechanisms to systematically assess HMGB3's role in mitochondrial metabolism and its coordination with single‐stranded DNA‐binding protein 1 (SSBP1)‐mediated activation of the PI3K‐Akt pathway.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1.

## Tissue specimens and clinicopathological data
In this retrospective analysis, tissue specimens and clinicopathological data were collected from 3 independent patient cohorts who were diagnosed at the FUSCC. This study adhered to the International Ethical Guidelines for Health‐related Research Involving Humans (https://cioms.ch/publications/product/international‐ethical‐guidelines‐for‐health‐related‐research‐involving‐humans/) and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (FUSCC; Shanghai, China; approval No. 050432‐4‐2307E).
The primary LUAD cohort was composed of 80 patients with available primary LUAD tumor tissues (with or without subsequent brain metastasis) who were diagnosed between January 2010 and October 2022. Tumor tissues and matched adjacent normal tissues were collected, formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded. These tissue blocks were used to construct tissue microarrays (TMAs) (Zhuoli Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). This cohort was primarily used to investigate HMGB3 expression in primary lesions and its prognostic significance. The overall survival (OS) of the primary LUAD cohort was defined as the time from the diagnosis of LUAD to death due to any cause.
In the paired cohort, matched samples of both primary LUAD and corresponding brain metastases were collected from 8 patients diagnosed between December 2015 and September 2020, primarily to compare HMGB3 expression between the 2 lesions within the same individual.
In the brain metastasis cohort, brain metastasis samples (without corresponding LUAD primary samples) were collected from 120 patients diagnosed with LUAD between December 2015 and September 2020. Paraffin‐embedded metastatic tissue sections (8 µm) were prepared without TMA reconstruction and used primarily to investigate HMGB3 expression in metastatic lesions and its prognostic value. The OS of the brain metastasis cohort was defined as the interval from the time of surgery to the time of death due to any cause.
In the second and third cohorts, patients were stratified into HMGB3‐low (no or weak staining) and HMGB3‐high (moderate or strong staining) groups based on immunohistochemical (IHC) evaluation. The inclusion and exclusion criteria for patient selection are presented in a Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials diagram (Supplementary Figure ), with detailed clinicopathological information for the 3 cohorts provided in Supplementary Tables .

2.2.

## Cell culture
The human LUAD lines A549 [Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS)+/epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)−/anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK)−] and H2030 (KRAS+/ EGFR−/ALK−) were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (Rockville, MD, USA). H2030 with high brain metastatic potential (H2030‐Lbrm) cells were isolated from the brains of mice in an experimental brain metastasis model established using H2030 cells and were subsequently cultured in vitro. The cells were maintained in RPMI‐1640 medium (C22400500BT, Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (A5669701, Gibco) at 37°C within a humidified incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) containing 5% CO2. Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were selected for assays and confirmed as mycoplasma‐free using the PlasmoTest™ Mycoplasma Detection Kit (cat# rep‐pt1, InvivoGen, San Diego, CA, USA). The confirmation process involved heat‐inactivation of supernatants (100°C, 15 min), co‐culture in detection medium, 16‐24 h incubation at 37°C, and assessment of contamination by colorimetric change (visual/OD655).

2.3.

## Cell transfection
Two HMGB3‐targeting small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), siHMGB3 #1 (5'‐GCAGAUAAAGUGCGCUAUGAU‐3') and siHMGB3 #2 (5'‐GCCGUAAUUGACACAUCUCUU‐3'), along with a negative control (siNC), were synthesized by Tsing Ke (Shanghai, China). The transfection experiments for siRNA were performed according to the manufacturer's guidelines for Lipo3000 (L3000075, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) as follows. Cells were seeded in 6‐well plates (3516, Corning, New York, NY, USA) at a density of 3×105 cells per well, reaching 70%–80% confluence. Transfection complexes, including 250 µL Opti‐MEM I (31985‐070, Gibco), 7 µL Lipofectamine 3000, and 15 pmol siRNA, were incubated for 10–20 min and added to each well. The medium was replaced after 6 h, and the cells were cultured for 48–72 h. HMGB3‐targeting small hairpin RNA (shHMGB3: 5′‐GCCTTCTTTGTGCAGACATGC‐3′) was cloned into the pFU‐GW‐012 vector (shNC), which was tagged with the firefly luciferase (luc) (GIDL0345249, Ji Kai Gene Chemical Technology, Shanghai, China) to instruct H2030‐luc, H2030‐Lbrm‐luc and H2030‐Lbrm‐shHMGB3‐luc for vivo assay. The pCDH‐CMV‐MCS‐EF1‐Luc‐T2A‐Puro lentiviral vector (V013570, NovoPro Bioscience Inc., Shanghai, China) was used as a control to construct HMGB3 and SSBP1 overexpression lentivirus. The lentivirus was packaged using a DNA transfection reagent (TF201201, Neofect Biotech, Beijing, China) in 293T cells by co‐transfection with the plasmids psPAX2 (12260, Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA) and pMD2.G (12259, Addgene). After 48 h, the viral supernatants were collected, filtered, and concentrated via ultracentrifugation (100,000 ×g, 4°C, 90 min). The concentrated virus was then used to infect LUAD cells using polybrene (8 µg/mL, TR‐1003, Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 48 h. Cells were subsequently cultured in fresh medium and selected with puromycin (2–10 µg/mL, A1113803, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Western blotting and quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction (qRT‐PCR) were employed to verify transfection efficiency.

2.4.

## Subcutaneous tumor‐bearing model
To assess the in vivo proliferative activity of HMGB3 in LUAD, we established a subcutaneous tumor‐bearing model. Cells were mixed with 100 µL phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS; G4202, Servicebio, Wuhan, Hubei, China) and an equal volume of Matrigel (354234, Corning), then subcutaneously inoculated into the right flank of BALB/c nude mice (1 × 106 cells per mouse). HMGB3‐knockdown cells were treated with SC79 (HY‐18749, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA), an Akt protein kinase activator, at a dose of 10 mg/kg, 3 times per week. The tumor size was measured every 2 days using calipers. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: volume = (length × width2) / 2.

2.5.

## Brain metastasis mouse models
All the mice were fed in the specific pathogen‐free (SPF) animal house, and all cells used were labeled with firefly luciferase. Mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation followed by cervical dislocation under isoflurane anesthesia when reaching individual endpoint criteria: tumor volume > 2,000 mm3 or tumor largest dimension > 20 mm. All animal studies followed the Fudan University's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee (Ethics Approval Number: FUSCC‐IACUC‐S2024‐0713).
A xenograft model of LUAD brain metastasis was generated using 4‐week‐old male BALB/c nude mice obtained from the Shanghai Jihui Laboratory Animal Care Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). To address potential injection failures and allow humane euthanasia of severely ill mice, 10–12 mice were assigned to each group, ensuring that 8 mice per group were used for the final analysis. Mice were anesthetized using 1% pentobarbital in PBS, fixed supine, and disinfected. The left ventricle was located by gently pressing the chest (second to third intercostal spaces) and marked. A 1 mL syringe was prepared with a 200 µL air gap, filled with 200 µL of cell suspension containing cells (H2030‐luc, H2030‐Lbrm‐luc, and H2030‐Lbrm‐shHMGB3‐luc) at a concentration of 2 × 105 cells/mL, and then air bubbles were removed. The syringe was inserted vertically 3–5 mm at the marked point; bright red blood flow confirmed left ventricle entry, and 100 µL of cell suspension was injected within 10 s. The needle was slowly withdrawn, and the bleeding was stopped by applying pressure. Mice were allowed to recover on a warming pad and housed in a SPF environment.
Following intraperitoneal administration of 150 mg/kg D‐luciferin (40902ES01, YEASEN, Shanghai, China), mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane (HY‐A0134, MedChemExpress) and imaged using the in vivo imaging system (IVIS) Spectrum (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) to assess brain metastasis. Upon brain metastasis verification at 6 weeks post‐injection, the mice underwent CO2 euthanasia followed by cervical dislocation to ensure death, and then underwent 75% ethanol disinfection. The brains were aseptically dissected, tumor macrodissected and tissues minced prior to enzymatic digestion with collagenase I/II (40507ES/40508ES, YEASEN) and trypsin (40126ES, YEASEN). The digested tissue was neutralized with complete medium, centrifuged at 100 ×g, 4°C for 5 min, then resuspended in complete medium, and plated in culture dishes (430167, Corning). Cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 24–48 h, after which tumor cells with parental morphology were observed, medium was replaced, and cells were expanded. Upon reaching a cell count of 1 × 106, the first‐generation LUAD brain metastasis cells (Lbrm1) underwent a second intracardiac injection using the established protocol. Following subsequent brain metastatic tumor formation, H2030‐Lbrm2 cells were established through primary culture under identical conditions. Until the fifth round, we continuously obtained H2030‐Lbrm3, H2030‐Lbrm4, and H2030‐Lbrm5. The final iteration H2030‐Lbrm5 was designated as H2030‐Lbrm and selected for in vitro experiments based on its superior brain metastatic potential. The brain metastasis rate of H2030 and H2030‐Lbrm cells was calculated by averaging the number of bioluminescence imaging‐positive brain metastases in 3 independent experiments, with 10 mice per group.

2.6.

## Available RNA‐sequencing (RNA‐seq) and proteomics data
Total RNA from H2030, H2030‐Lbrm, H2030‐Lbrm‐siNC, H2030‐Lbrm‐siHMGB3 #1 (with 3 biological replicates per sample) was extracted for RNA sequencing analysis. The initial RNA‐seq reads were processed using a standard analytical workflow for RNA‐seq [ ]. Single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) datasets were acquired from the National Center for Biotechnology Information Gene Expression Omnibus (NCBI GEO) database(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). The GSE131907 dataset comprised 208,506 cells from 58 samples of 44 patients with early and advanced‐stage LUAD [ ]. For our analysis, we utilized samples from the following categories: early‐stage LUAD (normal lung tissues, n = 11; LUAD tissues, n = 11), advanced‐stage LUAD (n = 4) and metastatic sites (lymph nodes, n = 7; pleural effusions, n = 5; brain metastases, n = 10). Ten normal lymph node samples were excluded from the study. The GSE123902 dataset transcriptionally profiled 41,384 single cells obtained from fresh surgical human samples of normal lung tissues (n = 4), primary LUAD (n = 8), brain metastasis (n = 3), bone metastasis (n = 1), and adrenal metastasis (n = 1) [ ]. We analyzed these scRNA‐seq data using the Seurat R package (version 4.3.0; https://CRAN.R‐project.org/package=Seurat). We integrated the sample analyses based on the mapped barcoded reads per cell to generate an aggregated matrix. Cells were filtered for quality if they exhibited over 200 expressed genes and a mitochondrial unique molecular identifier rate below 30%. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the FindMarkers function employing the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, adhering to the criteria of lnFC > 0.25, p < 0.05, and min.pct > 0.1.
The HMGB3 protein expression in normal and Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) LUAD tumor tissues was acquired from the University of Alabama interactive web resource (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/) [ ]. Bulk RNA‐seq data for primary LUAD and adjacent normal tissues were also obtained from GEO, including the GSE19188 [ ], GSE31210 [ ], and GSE63459 datasets [ ], for HMGB3 expression. Additionally, the LUAD datasets from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (http://cancergenome.nih.gov/), GSE31210 [ ], GSE72094 [ ], and GSE41271 [ ], were used for HMGB3 expression and survival analyses. These raw RNA‐seq reads were subsequently analyzed using a standard RNA‐seq analysis pipeline.

2.7.

## IHC
After dewaxing in xylene and rehydrated, paraffin‐embedded sections were stained with hematoxylin (C0107, Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 5 min and counterstained with eosin (G1100, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 1–3 min to histopathologically confirm malignancy, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with anti‐HMGB3 primary antibodies (1:200; 27465‐1‐AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, Hubei, China). Subsequently, the sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) goat anti‐rabbit immunoglobulin G (1:500; ab97051, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) at 20–25°C for 30 min, treated with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted for IHC analysis. Three board‐certified pathologists independently evaluated all hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and IHC slides using a dual‐phase quality control protocol [ ]. A consensus review of H&E whole‐slide images was performed to confirm specimen orientation and staining uniformity. Analysis of the TMA whole‐slide staining results revealed that HMGB3 exhibited uniform staining in most tumor tissues, with no significant spatial heterogeneity observed within individual specimens. A blinded assessment of HMGB3 expression was performed using the Remmele immunoreactivity score (IRS) system [ ] to calculate the composite scores [staining intensity (0–3) × positive cell percentage (0–4)]. Discordant cases (< 10%) underwent re‐evaluation with multi‐head microscopy until achieving ≥ 90% inter‐rater agreement. The final classifications were defined as negative (0), weak (1–4), moderate (5–8), or strong (9–12), with high/low expression thresholds at an IRS = 6.

2.8.

## Cell treatment
Cells were seeded at 3 × 105 cells/well in a 6‐well plate with RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS and incubated overnight at 37°C with 5% CO2. The next day, the medium was replaced with 2 mL serum‐free medium for 3 h of starvation. The PTEN inhibitor SF1670 (T6667, TargetMol, Wellesley Hills, MA, USA), dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), was then added at 200 nmol/L for the specified durations in each experiment. After 24 h, the medium containing SF1670 was replaced with RPMI‐1640 medium. Cyclosporine A (cycA) (T0945, TargetMol), which inhibits the nuclear translocation of SSBP1 [ ], was also dissolved in DMSO and administered to cells at 8 µmol/L for 2 h. DMSO was provided to the control cultures under the same treatment conditions. Cells with a density of 80%–90% were subjected to hypoxia induction for 24 h in an airtight Plexiglas chamber containing 5% oxygen and 95% nitrogen. Oxidative stress was simulated using 1 mmol/L hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and protein detection was performed at different time points after the addition of H2O2. SC79 was used at 0.01 mmol/L for 30 min. N‐Acetyl cysteine (NAC) (S0077, Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology) was added at a concentration of 5 mmol/L for a duration of 2 h to inhibit the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). These treatment assays were performed in triplicate for 3 times in targeted cells.

2.9.

## qRT‐PCR
Total RNA was isolated from the cells using NucleoZol (740404, Macherey‐Nagel, Düren, NRW, Germany). Complementary DNA was synthesized using 2 µg RNA and the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (RR036A, Takara, Tokyo, Japan) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. qRT‐PCR was performed on a QuantStudio7 Flex Real‐Time PCR System platform (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using SYBR premix Ex Taq (Tli RNaseH Plus; RR820Q, Takara) and 10 µmol/L primers, following the manufacturer's instructions. The qRT‐PCR protocol comprised an initial denaturation (95°C, 10 min) followed by 40 cycles of denaturation (95°C, 30 s), annealing (56°C, 30 s), and extension (72°C, 30 s). The reactions were performed in triplicate for 3 times to determine the average relative quantification values. All qRT‐PCR assays were performed in triplicate. The primer sequences utilized in this study are as follows: ACTB: F 5′‐CATGTACGTTGCTATCCAGGC‐3′, ACTB: R 5′‐CTCCTTAATGTCACGCACGAT‐3′, HMGB3: F 5′‐CCAAAGGGCAAGATGTCCG‐3′, HMGB3: R 5′‐TTGACAGGGACCTCTGGGTTT‐3′.

2.10.

## Cytoplasmic/nuclear protein extraction
The nuclear and cytoplasmic fractionation procedure for Western blotting analysis was performed using the Nuclear Extract Kit (40010, ACTIVE MOTIF, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The H2030‐Lbrm cell pellet was resuspended in 500 µL of 1 × Hypotonic Buffer and incubated on ice for 1 h to allow the cells to swell. Then, 25 µL of Detergent was added, followed by vortexing for 10 s. The mixture was centrifuged at 14,000 ×g for 30 s at 4°C. The supernatant contained the cytoplasmic proteins. The pellet was resuspended again in 500 µL of 1× Hypotonic Buffer and processed as described above. After centrifugation at 4°C, 14,000 ×g for 10 min, the supernatant was discarded to ensure complete removal of cytoplasmic proteins, leaving the nuclear fraction in the pellet. The successful separation was verified through Western blotting, utilizing Lamin A/C as a nuclear marker and α‐tubulin as a cytoplasmic marker. The experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3) in H2030‐Lbrm cell.

2.11.

## Western blotting
Whole protein extraction from the cell samples was performed using the total protein extraction kit (W034, Nanjing Jiancheng, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. The extracted proteins were separated using 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) and then transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (ISEQ00010, Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked with tris‐buffered saline (100 mmol/L Tris‐HCl, pH 7.5, 0.9% NaCl) containing 5% fat‐free milk (A600669, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) at room temperature for 1 h and subsequently incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Detailed information of all antibodies applied in Western blotting is provided in Supplementary Table . Each Western blotting experiment comprised at least 3 independent replicates. After three 5‐minute washes with Tris‐buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween‐20 (1247ML100, BioFroxx, Einhausen, BW, Germany), the membranes were incubated for 2 h at 20–25°C with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibody: anti‐rabbit (1:10,000; SA00001‐2, Proteintech) and anti‐mouse (1:10,000; SA00001‐1, Proteintech). Membranes were developed using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (SB‐WB011, Share‐Bio, Shanghai, China), and protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection system (ImageQuant, San Francisco, CA, USA). β‐Actin and vinculin were used as reference proteins to normalize variations in protein loading. Cytochrome c oxidase subunit IV (COX IV) was used as a mitochondrial protein loading control and detected using an anti‐COX IV antibody. α‐Tubulin served as the internal reference for cytoplasmic proteins, whereas lamin A/C was used as an internal reference for nuclear proteins.

2.12.

## Scratch wound healing assay
A scratch wound healing assay was performed to evaluate cell migration. Cells were seeded at 6 × 105 cells per well in a 6‐well plate (353046, Corning). After 24 h, a scratch was made using a 200 µL pipette tip, and cells were placed in a 37°C, 5% CO2 incubator. Live cell images were captured at 8‐h intervals for 48 h with a microscope (IX73, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Images were analyzed with the ImageJ software (2.1.0, National Institute of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA, https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/) [ ] to quantify the average extent of wound closure. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

2.13.

## Cell invasion and migration assays
Transwell assays were examined in 3 replicate experiments using a chamber (3422, Corning, USA). The membrane was coated with 50 µL Matrigel, which was diluted 1:10 in serum‐free medium (356234, Corning) for invasion assays, and left uncoated for migration assays. After drying, 200 µL of cell suspension was seeded in the upper chamber using serum‐free medium at 2 × 105 cells/mL for migration and 4 × 105 cells/mL for the invasion assay. A 500 µL serum‐supplemented medium was added per well in a 24‐well plate (353047, Corning) to serve as a chemoattractant. After 48 h, non‐migrated cells were removed using cotton swabs. The cells on the lower membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (G1101, Servicebio), stained with 0.1% crystal violet (C0121, Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology), and quantified using the ImageJ software employing the “Cell Counter” plugin after imaging with a IX73 microscope.

2.14.

## Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK8) assay
Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK8 reagent (C0037, Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology). Cells to be detected (8 × 103 cells/well) were seeded into 96‐well dishes (3364, Corning) containing 100 µL of complete medium and incubated at 37°C under 5% CO2 for 24 h with 5 replicate wells per group. For 5 consecutive days, 100 µL of CCK‐8 reagent (1:10 dilution) was added to each well, incubated at 37°C for 2 h, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (ELx 800, BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). Each CCK8 experiment was independently replicated at least 3 times.

2.15.

## Cell cycle analysis
The cell cycle staining was performed 3 independent experiments according to the manufacturer's instructions (CCS012, MultiSciences Biotech, Hangzhou, China). Cells (2 × 10⁵) were harvested, centrifuged at 100 ×g, 4°C for 5 min, and washed with 500 µL PBS through resuspension and recentrifugation. The supernatants were discarded after each centrifugation step. The pellet was treated with 1 mL DNA staining solution containing 10 µL permeabilization solution, then vortexed briefly for 5–10 s. After 30 min dark incubation at room temperature, the stained cells were acquired on a CytoFLEX S (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) using a minimal aspiration rate for fluorescence measurement, and the data were analyzed using the FlowJo V10 software (FLOWJO, Ashland, OR, USA).

2.16.

## Functional analysis by enrichment analysis
All statistical and enrichment analyses were conducted using the R programming language (version 4.2, http://CRAN.R‐project.org) [ ]. Differential expression analysis was performed with the limma package (v3.54.1) to identify DEGs, applying thresholds of false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and |log2‐fold change (FC)| > 1.
Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) Bioconductor package (2.0.4) was used to calculate the enrichment scores for the hallmark gene sets from Molecular Signature Database version 5.1 (https://www.gsea‐msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb). This analysis was applied across 2 independent datasets: the TCGA‐LUAD cohort (comparison of top vs. bottom 30% by HMGB3 expression) and a specific cluster from the single‐cell dataset GSE131907.
To evaluate the activation of pathways in LUAD tumor cells from primary and brain metastatic sites in GSE131907, we employed the Quantitative Set Analysis for Gene Expression (QuSAGE) via the Bioconductor package qusage (2.16.1, https://bioconductor.org/) in R language. This approach quantified the relative activation of hallmark gene sets from the Molecular Signature Database version 5.1.
Finally, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were conducted through the clusterProfiler R package (version 4.16.6) [ ]. Enriched terms were determined by Fisher's exact test, with significance thresholds set at p value < 0.05 and FDR < 0.1.

2.17.

## Co‐immunoprecipitation (co‐IP) and mass spectrometry
For the co‐IP experiments, cell lysis was performed using NP40 lysis buffer (P0013F, Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology) supplemented with 10% glycerol, a phosphorylated protease inhibitor cocktail (B15001, Bimake, Houston, TX, USA), and a protease inhibitor cocktail (B14001, Bimake). The cells were lysed on ice for 1 h. The lysate was then centrifuged at 13,400 ×g, 4°C for 10 min, and 10% of the supernatant was collected as the input control, while the remaining lysate was incubated with anti‐HMGB3 (3 µg, 27465‐1‐AP, Proteintech) and anti‐immunoglobulin G (3 µg, 3900, Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA, USA) at 4°C with continuous agitation overnight. Protein A/G beads (B23202, Selleck Chemicals, Houston, TX, USA) were washed 3 times with lysis buffer and then added to the antibody‐bound lysate and incubated for another 4 h at 4°C. After removing the supernatant, the beads were washed 3 times with lysis buffer, boiled, and resuspended in 50 µL of 1× SDS loading buffer. The experiment was repeated 3 times with consistent Co‐IP. The beads were then subjected to further analysis via mass spectrometry, and the immunoprecipitated proteins were subsequently detected via Western blotting analysis.

2.18.

## Immunofluorescence
Cell lines grown on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X‐100 for 5 min at 4°C. Following this, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The primary antibodies used were anti‐HMGB3 (1:100; PB9633, BOSTER, Pleasanton, CA, USA), anti‐SSBP1 (1:150; A6987, Abclonal, Woburn, MA, USA), and anti‐Translocase of the Outer Mitochondrial membrane 20 (TOM20) (1:200; 11802‐1‐AP, Proteintech). TOM20 was included as a positive control to validate the integrity of the mitochondrial staining. Subsequently, the slides were incubated with Alexa 488‐conjugated (1:1,000; S11223, Invitrogen) or Alexa 594‐conjugated secondary antibody (1:1,000; S11227, Invitrogen) for 40 min at room temperature, and the nuclei were counterstained with 4’,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (36308ES11, YEASEN). Immunofluorescence experiments were repeated in triplicate for validation. The images were captured using a Leica TCS SP5 II microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Hesse, Germany). Fluorescence intensity and colocalization analyses were performed using the ImageJ software.

2.19.

## Intracellular ROS
Intracellular ROS levels were measured by staining the cells with 5,6‐carboxy‐2′,7′‐dichlorofluoresceindiacetate (DCFH‐DA) using a ROS Detection Assay kit (S0033M, Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The cells were incubated with DCFH‐DA at a final concentration of 10 µmol/L in 1× buffer for 20 min at 37°C, and the fluorescence was measured using a CytoFLEX S with excitation and emission wavelengths set at 485 nm and 525 nm, respectively. In total, 10,000 events were recorded for each analysis. The experiment was repeated 3 times. Data were exported using the FlowJo software, and the intracellular ROS levels were expressed as the average 2′,7′‐dichlorofluorescein fluorescence intensity.

2.20.

## Measurement of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP)
JC‐1, also known as 5,5′,6,6′‐tetrachloro‐1,1′,3,3′‐tetraethylbenzimidazolo carbocyanine iodide, is a dye that exhibits a color shift from green to red in response to an increase in MMP. To measure the MMP of H2030 cells, we used the JC‐1 MMP assay kit (40706ES60, YEASEN) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The stained cells were washed twice with ice‐cold PBS and then incubated with JC‐1 solution at a final concentration of 20 µg/mL for 20 min at 37°C in the dark. Flow cytometry analysis was performed using excitation and emission wavelengths of 488/630 nm (red) and 488/530 nm (green) to detect the fluorescence. The assay was performed in triplicate, and the ratio of red to green fluorescence was used to calculate MMP.

2.21.

## Succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) assay
The SDH activity was measured using a Succinate Dehydrogenase Assay Kit (BC0955, Solarbio) according to the manufacturer's instructions, with the experiment being conducted in triplicate to ensure validation. Cells were grown to a density of 5 × 106 cells/mL in RPMI 1640 medium and incubated with or without 8 µmol/L cycA for 2 h. The cells were then digested and resuspended in 1 mL of SDH buffer, followed by ultrasonic cell lysis in an ice bath for 5 min. The suspension was centrifuged at 100 ×g, 4°C for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected for analysis. Enzyme activity was determined by the SDH‐dependent reduction of dichlorophenolindophenol and quantified as changes in absorbance at 600 nm at 2‐min intervals using a microplate reader (SYNERGYH1, BioTek).

2.22.

## Measurement of oxygen consumption rate (OCR)
The mitochondrial OCR was assessed using a Seahorse XF24 analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience Inc., North Billerica, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions for the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (103015‐100, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Experiments were performed in triplicate. Briefly, 100 µL of single‐cell suspension (105/mL) was incubated at 37°C until adherence, followed by adding of 150 µL of the medium and culturing for 24 h. For assay preparation, 175 µL of the medium was removed, the cells were rinsed twice with 600 µL Seahorse assay medium, and the volume was adjusted to 525 µL. The cell continuity was confirmed microscopically, and the cells were incubated in a non‐CO2 incubator for 1 h. Subsequently, 75 µL of inhibitors was added to each injection port: oligomycin (1 µmol/L), carbonyl cyanide 4‐(trifluoromethoxy) phenylhydrazone (2 mmol/L), and antimycin A (2 mmol/L). The OCR values were normalized to the total protein content of each sample.

2.23.

## Mitochondrial isolation
To explore the subcellular location of SSBP1, we separated the mitochondria and cytoplasm using a mitochondrial isolation kit (C3601, Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology). The experiment was performed in triplicate. Cells were collected at a density of 5 × 107 and resuspended in 2 mL of mitochondrial isolation reagent. After incubation on ice for 10–15 min and homogenization (10–30 strokes), the suspension was centrifuged at 600 ×g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube and centrifuged at 11,000 ×g for 10 min at 4°C, separating the mitochondria‐depleted cytoplasmic fraction (supernatant) from the mitochondrial fraction (sediment). The successful separation was confirmed by Western blotting using mitochondrial marker COX IV and cytoplasmic marker α‐tubulin, with no detection of COX IV in the cytoplasmic proteins.

2.24.

## Statistical analyses
Differences between 2 experimental groups were assessed by independent‐sample Student's t‐test, while comparisons involving 3 or more groups were conducted using one‐way or two‐way variance analysis. Overall survival (OS) was calculated from the date of cancer diagnosis to the date of death, considering all causes, and analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier survival curves with log‐rank testing for comparisons. During univariate screening, the Holm‐Bonferroni method controlled multiple comparisons (α = 0.05). Multivariate Cox modeling incorporated two‐step quality control: excluding variables with a variance inflation factor ≥ 10 and applying Bonferroni correction to confidence intervals.

3.

## RESULTS
3.1.

## HMGB3 emerged as a crucial determinant of the high brain metastatic phenotype in LUAD cells
The heterogeneity of metastatic cells has been documented in various cancers [ , ]. To construct a model of LUAD brain metastasis, we used BALB/c nude mice injected intraventricularly with H2030 cells. After 5 rounds of cycling screening, we developed a high brain metastatic cell line, H2030‐Lbrm, with a 50% metastasis rate (Figure  ). H&E staining confirmed that H2030‐Lbrm cells induced a significantly higher brain metastasis load compared to H2030 cells (Figure  ). Notably, the median OS of mice injected with H2030‐Lbrm cells was noticeably shorter than that of mice injected with H2030 cells (Figure  ). To substantiate our hypothesis that brain metastatic cells demonstrate greater metastatic capacity compared to primary cells, we assessed the proliferation, migration, and invasion capabilities of primary and H2030‐Lbrm cells using CCK8 assays, scratch tests, and Transwell assays. As anticipated, H2030‐Lbrm exhibited enhanced proliferation, migratory and invasive capacities in vitro compared with H2030 cells (Figure  ). Given that the epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) enhances proliferative ability, invasion, and metastatic potential [ ], we assessed the protein levels of EMT‐related markers in H2030 and H2030‐Lbrm cells. The results revealed a marked upregulation of zinc finger E‐Box binding homeobox 1 (ZEB1) and β‐catenin in H2030‐Lbrm cells, accompanied by elevated expression of the mesenchymal marker N‐cadherin, while the epithelial marker E‐cadherin was significantly downregulated (Figure  ).
 To identify pivotal genes associated with brain metastasis, we analyzed 2 datasets: 1,301 genes that were significantly upregulated in H2030‐Lbrm cells compared to H2030 cells in bulk RNA‐seq (H2030 vs. H2030‐Lbrm) data, and 358 genes that were markedly increased in brain metastatic tumor cells from the scRNA‐seq dataset GSE13190707. Intersection of the 2 DEG sets yielded 24 genes (Figure  ). Heat maps of the top 48 upregulated genes from both datasets revealed that HMGB3 was the most significantly upregulated gene. Furthermore, the specific overexpression of HMGB3 in brain metastases, compared to other extracranial metastatic lesions, was observed in the GSE131907 dataset (Figure  ). Additionally, this finding was further validated in an independent dataset GSE123902 (Figure  ).
We further analyzed the expression profile of HMGB3 in both brain metastatic specimens and brain metastatic mouse models. IHC results revealed that HMGB3 expression was notably higher in brain metastatic lesions compared to normal mouse brain tissue (Figure  ). Additionally, by evaluating the mRNA (Supplementary Figure ) and protein (Figure  ) expression of HMGB3 in H2030 and H2030‐Lbrm1‐5 cells, we observed a gradual upregulation of HMGB3 among these generations with increasing potential of brain metastasis. These consistent findings highlighted the prominent expression of HMGB3, indicating its potential as a pivotal factor in brain metastasis studies.

3.2.

## Elevated tumor HMGB3 expression was significantly associated with poorer OS in LUAD patients, especially in those with concomitant brain metastasis
To further substantiate the differential expression of HMGB3 between LUAD and brain metastases, IHC analysis of the Paired cohort (n = 8) revealed a significant upregulation of HMGB3 in brain metastatic lesions compared to paired primary LUAD lesions (p = 0.043) (Figure  ). In addition, IHC analysis of the Primary LUAD using HMGB3 antibody demonstrated significantly elevated protein level in 80 cases of LUAD tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (p = 0.005) (Figure  , Supplementary Figure ).
 To assess the prognostic significance of HMGB3 expression, we used 2 cohorts—the Primary LUAD cohort and the Brain metastasis cohort. In each cohort, patients were stratified to HMGB3‐low and HMGB3‐high groups based on IHC staining with representative images provided for illustration (Figure  ‐ ). The OS analysis showed that high HMGB3 expression, either in the primary LUAD site or in brain metastatic lesions, significantly predicted poor prognosis in patients with LUAD and those with brain metastases (Figure  ‐ ).
The associations between HMGB3 and clinical characteristics were investigated in both cohorts. In the Primary LUAD cohort (Table  ), high HMGB3 expression was associated with advanced N stage, M stage, and clinical stage (cTNM). In the Brain metastasis cohort (Table  ), elevated HMGB3 expression was significantly associated with severe peritumoral brain edema and postoperative radiotherapy. These findings indicated that HMGB3 expression was associated with disease progression in both primary LUAD lesions and brain metastases.
 Univariate and multivariate cox regression analyses revealed that elevated HMGB3 expression independently predicted poor prognosis in LUAD patients, and those with concomitant brain metastasis. In the Primary LUAD cohort (Table  ), cTNM stage IV and HMGB3 overexpression were prognostic indicators. In the Brain metastasis cohort (Table  ), significant prognostic predictors included age ≥ 65 years, extracerebral metastasis, high HMGB3 expression, absence of postoperative radiotherapy, and lack of targeted therapy.
 Building upon our investigation into HMGB3 expression in LUAD patients and those with LUAD brain metastasis, we extended our analysis to incorporate publicly accessible datasets to validate our IHC results. Proteomic analysis of the CPTAC‐LUAD dataset revealed that HMGB3 in tumor samples was significantly elevated compared with that in adjacent normal tissues (Figure  ). Transcriptomic analysis of the TCGA‐LUAD, GSE19188, GSE31210, and GSE63459 datasets consistently indicated an upregulation of HMGB3 in LUAD tissues (Figure  ). Additionally, the prognostic analyses utilizing the Kaplan‐Meier Plotter and the above GEO datasets revealed that elevated HMGB3 expression was associated with significantly reduced survival in LUAD (Figure  ).

3.3.

## HMGB3 promoted LUAD progression and brain metastasis in vitro and in vivo
Given the promising clinical implications of HMGB3 as a biomarker for tumor progression and prognosis in patients with LUAD and those with brain metastasis, as evidenced by our findings, we further investigated the pro‐tumorigenic phenotype and underlying molecular mechanisms of HMGB3 through in vitro cellular and in vivo animal models.
We initially examined the protein levels of EMT markers (β‐catenin, ZEB1, N‐cadherin, and E‐cadherin) in H2030‐Lbrm and A549 cells following HMGB3 knockdown, as well as in H2030‐Lbrm cells with HMGB3 overexpression. In these cell models, overexpression of HMGB3 significantly upregulated β‐catenin, ZEB1, and N‐cadherin, while downregulating E‐cadherin; conversely, HMGB3 knockdown increased E‐cadherin expression and reduced levels of β‐catenin, ZEB1, and N‐cadherin (Figure  ).
 In vitro experiments demonstrated that HMGB3 knockdown markedly suppressed the proliferation of H2030‐Lbrm and A549 cells (Figure  ). Scratch wound healing assays revealed that HMGB3 knockdown significantly impaired cell migration (Figure  ), while Transwell assays further confirmed a substantial decrease in the number of migrated and invaded cells upon HMGB3 knockdown (Figure  ).
Given the predominant nuclear localization of HMGB3 and its regulatory roles in chromatin organization and DNA structure [ ], we employed flow cytometry to assess its impact on the tumor cell cycle. HMGB3 knockdown resulted in an increased proportion of cells in the G1 phase and a concomitant decrease in the G2 phase in both cell lines (Figure  ). Notably, in H2030‐Lbrm cells, the S phase population was reduced, whereas it was elevated in A549 cells (Figure  ). These findings suggested that HMGB3 primarily modulated the G1 phase, with cell line‐specific effects on the S phase, underscoring its intricate role in cell cycle regulation and tumor progression.
We then assessed the effect of HMGB3 on LUAD growth in vivo in BALB/c nude mice. Consistent with the in vitro results, HMGB3‐knockdown tumors exhibited significantly slower growth rates and smaller weights compared to the controls (Figure  ). At the experimental endpoint (day 35), the volume of control tumors was 6 times greater than that of HMGB3‐knockdown tumors (Figure  ). Overall, these findings demonstrated that HMGB3 promoted proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro and accelerated LUAD growth in vivo.
Additionally, the brain metastasis rate in BALB/c nude mice injected with H2030‐Lbrm‐shHMGB3‐luc cells in the left ventricle was markedly lower than that in mice injected with H2030‐Lbrm‐shNC cells (p = 0.025, Figure  ). The median survival duration of mice in the NC group was 34 days, whereas the shHMGB3 group displayed significantly prolonged survival, exceeding 60 days (Figure  ).
Collectively, these findings highlighted the critical role of high HMGB3 expression in accelerating the progression of LUAD and establishing brain metastases, as substantiated by both in vitro and in vivo studies.

3.4.

## HMGB3 promoted brain metastasis in LUAD through PTEN‐PI3K‐Akt‐mediated EMT activation
To further elucidate the mechanistic underpinnings of HMGB3 in LUAD progression, we analyzed multiple datasets. In the TCGA‐LUAD cohort, patients were stratified into high and low HMGB3 expression groups based on the top and bottom 30% of HMGB3 expression levels. GSVA analysis revealed that samples with high HMGB3 expression were significantly enriched in biological processes such as the regulation of PTEN gene transcription, mTOR signaling, and mitochondrial biogenesis (Figure  ).
 In the GSE131907 dataset, GSVA analysis revealed a significant enrichment of oxidative phosphorylation in the brain metastasis‐specific cell cluster (Figure  ). Furthermore, pathways including mTOR signaling, oxidative phosphorylation, and the erythroblastic leukemia viral oncogene homolog (ErbB) signaling pathway were significantly upregulated in brain metastatic tumor cells compared to primary LUAD samples, implicating their potential roles in the progression of brain metastasis (Figure  ). We subsequently stratified brain metastatic tumor cells into HMGB3‐positive and ‐negative groups. KEGG enrichment analysis of the DEGs revealed significant enrichment of the PI3K‐Akt signaling pathway, oxidative phosphorylation, and mTOR signaling pathway in HMGB3‐positive cells (Figure  ). Additionally, HMGB3 knockdown was conducted in H2030‐Lbrm cells, and pathway analysis of DEGs revealed that HMGB3 depletion significantly suppressed the PI3K‐Akt signaling pathway (Figure  ). Therefore, we hypothesized that HMGB3 facilitated tumor progression and brain metastasis through the PTEN‐PI3K‐Akt signaling pathway. To validate this, we performed Western blotting analysis, which revealed decreased PTEN levels and increased expression of mTOR, phosphorylated Akt (Ser 473) and PI3K‐p85 (Tyr 458) in H2030‐Lbrm cells stably transfected with HMGB3, while the levels of PI3K‐p85 and total Akt (pan‐Akt) remained unaltered; in contrast, HMGB3 knockdown increased PTEN expression and inhibited the PI3K‐Akt pathway in H2030‐Lbrm and A549 cells (Figure  ).
To investigate whether HMGB3 modulates the PI3K‐Akt pathway through PTEN, we employed the PTEN inhibitor SF1670. HMGB3 knockdown significantly suppressed PI3K‐Akt signaling in both cell lines, an effect that was markedly reversed by PTEN inhibition (Figure  ). To further elucidate PTEN's role in HMGB3‐mediated EMT in LUAD cells, we analyzed epithelial and mesenchymal markers. As anticipated, PTEN inhibition upregulated epithelial markers and downregulated mesenchymal markers in HMGB3‐knockdown cells (Figure  ). Additionally, PTEN inhibition with SF1670 restored the migratory and invasive capacities of HMGB3‐knockdown H2030‐Lbrm and A549 cells (Figure  ).
 Collectively, these findings demonstrated that HMGB3 promoted EMT and LUAD brain metastasis by regulating the PTEN‐PI3K‐Akt signaling axis.

3.5.

## The HMGB3‐SSBP1 complex induced mitochondrial stress
To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying HMGB3's role in brain metastasis, we isolated proteins from H2030‐Lbrm cells and identified HMGB3‐interacting proteins using co‐IP and mass spectrometry (Supplementary Table ). Notably, KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment of nucleocytoplasmic transport among the potential HMGB3‐interacting proteins identified by mass spectrometry (Figure  ). Integrating the sequencing results with GSEA results, we observed that HMGB3 knockdown significantly reduced the nucleocytoplasmic transport in H2030‐Lbrm cells (Figure  ). Reciprocal co‐IP assays confirmed the interaction between HMGB3 and SSBP1 (Figure  ). SSBP1, a housekeeping gene involved in mitochondrial biogenesis and genome stability maintenance [ , ], was examined for colocalization with HMGB3 via IF. We found that HMGB3 and SSBP1 nuclear localization was significantly higher in H2030‐HMGB3‐oe cells, H2030‐Lbrm cells, and hypoxia‐treated H2030 cells than in control H2030 cells (Figure  ). Western blotting analysis of nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins from H2030‐Lbrm cells with either HMGB3 overexpression or knockdown demonstrated that HMGB3 overexpression elevated nuclear SSBP1 levels while reducing cytoplasmic SSBP1, whereas HMGB3 knockdown decreased SSBP1 in both nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments (Figure  ).
 cycA inhibits SSBP1 nuclear translocation by blocking mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) [ , ]. Studies indicate that proteins with a molecular weight below approximately 40‐60 kDa can passively diffuse into the nucleus [ ]. Due to its low molecular weight (17 kDa) and absence of a nuclear localization sequence, SSBP1 passively diffuses into the nucleus from the cytoplasm, with mPTP opening serving as the rate‐limiting step [ , ]. Thus, cycA is an effective tool for inhibiting SSBP1 translocation in the following cellular assays. To further elucidate the cycA's role in SSBP1 localization, we examined the cellular distribution of SSBP1. Using TOM20 as a mitochondrial marker, H2030 and H2030‐Lbrm cells were treated with cycA (8 µmol/L) for 24 h, with DMSO as a control. Immunofluorescence staining showed that cycA reversed the translocation of SSBP1 from the nucleus to the cytoplasm (Figure  ).
RNA‐seq data also revealed that mitochondrial metabolic processes were significantly enriched in H2030‐Lbrm cells (Figure  ). Importantly, QuSAGE analysis of 2 independent scRNA‐seq datasets, encompassing primary LUAD lesions and multiple distant metastatic sites, demonstrated significant enrichment of the “mitochondrial metabolic remodeling” hallmark in brain metastatic tumor cells, underscoring their organ‐specific characteristics (Supplementary Figure ).
Drawing on the findings of Tan et al. [ ] that SSBP1 translocated from mitochondria to the nucleus under heat shock‐induced proteotoxic stress to promote cell survival, we hypothesized that the hypoxic and metabolically stressed brain metastatic niche, similar to heat shock [ , ], may trigger SSBP1 depletion from mitochondria, resulting in mitochondrial dysfunction. To validate the mitochondrial metabolic status in brain metastatic tumor cells, we compared the mitochondrial function between H2030 and H2030‐Lbrm cells. H2030‐Lbrm cells showed a 1.4‐fold increase in ROS levels (Figure  ). They had lower basal OCR, but their maximal respiration and spare respiratory capacity were 1.4‐fold and 2.7‐fold higher than those of H2030 cells, respectively (Figure  ). This suggested that metastatic cells had enhanced energy capacity to cope with stress. Consistently, SDH activity in the H2030‐Lbrm cells was reduced by 1.8‐fold (Figure  ), indicating decreased tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle flux. JC‐1 staining also revealed a higher green fluorescence in H2030‐Lbrm cells (Figure  ), suggesting mitochondrial membrane potential dysfunction. Collectively, these results demonstrated that H2030‐Lbrm cells underwent metabolic reprogramming with mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction during brain metastasis.

3.6.

## The interaction between HMGB3 and SSBP1 activated the PI3K‐Akt pathway by inducing mitochondrial stress
Given that mitochondrial metabolic disorders frequently cause intracellular ROS imbalance [ ], and based on our hypothesis that HMGB3 remodels mitochondrial metabolism by affecting SSBP1 nuclear translocation, we quantified intracellular ROS levels using flow cytometry. The HMGB3‐overexpressing cells had about 8‐fold higher content than the vector control group (Figure  ). After 24 h of 8 µmol/L cycA treatment, the relative ROS level significantly decreased (Figure  ), indicating that HMGB3 modulates intracellular ROS levels via the nuclear translocation of SSBP1. Given the broad regulatory role of ROS in cellular signaling pathways, we employed the mitochondrial ROS inhibitor NAC [ ] to demonstrate that HMGB3's modulation of the PTEN‐PI3K‐Akt pathway was ROS‐dependent. NAC effectively reversed the HMGB3‐induced decrease in PTEN protein levels and concurrently attenuated the phosphorylation levels of Akt (Ser 473) and PI3K p85 (Tyr 458) (Figure  ), confirming that HMGB3 modulated this pathway through mitochondrial ROS signaling. Building on the above findings, we tested various cycA concentrations for their effects on PTEN protein levels by suppressing SSBP1 nuclear translocation‐induced mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction and ROS elevation. We determined 8 µmol/L to be optimal for rescue treatment (Figure  ). To mimic ROS conditions, H2030‐Lbrm cells were treated with 1 mmol/L H2O2 at 37°C, and we observed PTEN downregulation at 60 min post‐treatment (Figure  ). Additionally, cycA treatment reversed HMGB3‐induced S‐phase lengthening and G2‐M phase shortening (Figure  ) and inhibited the accelerated proliferation in HMGB3‐overexpressing H2030‐Lbrm cells (Figure  ).
 Collectively, we hypothesized that HMGB3 interacted with SSBP1, triggering mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction via SSBP1 nuclear translocation. This process elevated intracellular ROS levels, which regulated the PTEN‐PI3K‐Akt pathway and tumor cell phenotypic changes in a ROS‐dependent manner.
To elucidate the critical role of SSBP1 in this process, we examined whether the enhanced HMGB3‐SSBP1 interaction impaired mitochondrial metabolic function and cellular homeostasis. Using JC‐1 dye, we observed that H2030‐Lbrm cells overexpressing HMGB3 exhibited reduced red and increased green fluorescence, indicative of diminished mitochondrial membrane potential (Figure  ). Notably, cycA treatment mitigated this damage and restored red fluorescence levels (Figure  ). Furthermore, we demonstrated that HMGB3 overexpression led to a decline in SDH activity, reflecting impaired mitochondrial energy metabolism. Importantly, cycA treatment significantly restored SDH activity (Figure  ). By measuring OCR in H2030‐Lbrm‐vector and H2030‐Lbrm‐HMGB3‐oe cells with and without cycA, we discovered that HMGB3 promoted a metabolic shift from mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation to aerobic glycolysis, a shift reversed by cycA treatment (Figure  ). Given that DRP1 is essential for mitochondrial fission [ ] and translocates to the mitochondrial outer membrane when mitochondrial membrane potential is lost [ ], we investigated the effect of HMGB3 on DRP1 levels in the mitochondria by isolating mitochondrial and cytosolic proteins and performed Western blotting. Results showed that HMGB3 overexpression increased mitochondria DRP1 levels while concurrently downregulating its cytoplasmic expression (Figure  ). To confirm that HMGB3 mediated downstream signaling through SSBP1, H2030‐Lbrm‐shHMGB3 cells were treated with either a transfected SSBP1 plasmid or the Akt activator SC79. Western blotting analysis demonstrated that SSBP1 overexpression and SC79 treatment effectively restored the downregulation of the mTOR‐PI3K‐Akt signaling pathway induced by HMGB3 knockdown (Figure  ). Furthermore, the cells with SSBP1 overexpression or treated with SC79 exhibited enhanced migration and invasion in vitro (Figure  ‐ ) and restored brain metastasis in vivo compared to HMGB3‐knockdown cells (Figure  , Supplementary Figure ). In summary, these findings demonstrated that the HMGB3‐SSBP1 interaction triggered mitochondrial dysfunction, resulting in elevated ROS levels. This, in turn, downregulated PTEN in an ROS‐dependent manner, activating the PI3K‐Akt pathway and ultimately driving brain metastasis in LUAD (Figure  ).

## DISCUSSION
Brain metastases are highly lethal, and effective treatment remains a significant unmet clinical challenge. In this study, we demonstrated that HMGB3 promoted brain metastasis in LUAD. Mechanistically, HMGB3 interacted with SSBP1 to facilitate its nucleotranslocation, remodeling mitochondrial metabolism and elevating intracellular ROS levels. This, in turn, modulated the PTEN‐PI3K‐Akt signaling axis in an ROS‐dependent manner, thereby promoting brain metastasis in LUAD.
HMGB3 has been widely implicated in cancer progression and poor prognosis in multiple malignancies [ , , , , , ]. This study demonstrated that HMGB3 knockdown not only significantly inhibited brain metastasis in mice but also markedly reduced subcutaneous tumor burden, suggesting that HMGB3 plays a role in promoting tumor proliferation in both brain and primary LUAD contexts. Additionally, 2 other studies were particularly noteworthy: Kim et al. [ ] utilized scRNA‐seq to elucidate the mechanisms driving LUAD progression, demonstrating that HMGB3 expression escalates with tumor grade, implicating its potential role in LUAD advancement. Nevertheless, the specific functional roles and mechanistic underpinnings of HMGB3 remain unexplored in their study. Liu et al. [ ] employed large‐scale multi‐omics analyses to uncover mechanisms underlying small cell lung cancer (SCLC) progression, identifying HMGB3 as a prognostic marker for disease recurrence and advancement. Bioinformatics analyses further implicated HMGB3 in the activation of the PI3K‐Akt signaling pathway. However, the authors did not investigate the specific functional roles or mechanistic contributions of HMGB3 in greater detail. Given the distinct microenvironments and metabolic profiles of the lung and brain [ ], the molecular mechanisms by which HMGB3 promotes tumor proliferation in primary lung versus brain metastatic settings may vary, necessitating further investigation.
Furthermore, our study revealed that HMGB3 facilitated LUAD brain metastasis by suppressing of PTEN protein expression and subsequent activation of the PI3K‐Akt‐mTOR signaling pathway. This observation was also consistent with prior findings in colorectal cancer [ ], wherein HMGB3 inhibition attenuated PI3K‐Akt activation, underscoring its conserved oncogenic function. Furthermore, as previously mentioned, multiple studies have demonstrated that activation of the PTEN‐PI3K‐Akt‐mTOR signaling axis plays a pivotal role in the development and progression of brain metastatic tumors [ , , , ]. Our study provided evidence that elucidated the mechanisms underlying the activation of this oncogenic signaling pathway in brain metastasis.
To investigate the underlying mechanism by which HMGB3 activated the PTEN‐PI3K‐Akt‐mTOR signaling axis, we used mass spectrometry to identify SSBP1, a mitochondrial protein critical for maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis, as a key HMGB3‐interacting partner. Under physiological conditions, SSBP1 is primarily localized to mitochondria, which plays a crucial role in maintaining mitochondrial morphology and function [ ]. However, in malignancies, SSBP1 promotes tumorigenesis by sustaining mitochondrial integrity, driving oncogenic signaling, and inducing genomic instability, positioning it as a key biomarker and therapeutic target in various cancers [ , , , , ].
Previous work by Tan et al. [ ] demonstrated that SSBP1 underwent mitochondrial‐to‐nuclear translocation and modulated nuclear gene transcription in response to proteotoxic stress under adverse conditions, facilitating cellular survival during crises. In this study, confocal microscopy analysis revealed a pronounced redistribution of SSBP1 from mitochondria to the nucleus in HMGB3‐high tumor cells, as well as in H2030 primary cells under conditions of HMGB3 overexpression or hypoxia. We proposed that the brain metastatic niche, characterized by hypoxia [ ] and metabolic stress [ ] akin to heat shock, may induce SSBP1 depletion from mitochondria, leading to subsequent mitochondrial dysfunction.
Zhang et al. [ ] revealed that CHK1‐SSBP1‐ROS formed a nucleus‐to‐mitochondria ROS sensing pathway that coupled DNA damage response to the control of mitochondrial translation, potentially serving as a mechanism of resistance to platinum‐based agents. Mitochondria are the cellular powerhouses, playing an indispensable role in sustaining normal cellular function [ ]. Mitochondrial damage is mainly caused by increased ROS levels within the mitochondria. ROS can damage mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), impair the oxidative phosphorylation system, disrupt electron transfer, and reduce mitochondrial membrane potential. This damage further amplifies ROS production. Additionally, ROS extensively modulated cellular signaling pathways, such as the PTEN‐PI3K‐Akt axis and induced death through lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial dysfunction [ ]. Our study demonstrated that in brain metastatic tumor cells, HMGB3 recruited SSBP1 into the nucleus and interacts with it, causing an imbalance in SSBP1 distribution between the nucleus and cytoplasm. This imbalance subsequently induced mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction and elevated intracellular ROS levels. The increased ROS levels downregulate PTEN, thereby activating the PI3K‐Akt signaling pathway. This finding aligned with and substantiated the aforementioned mechanisms.
Elevated ROS levels exhibited dual effects on cell survival: moderate increases promote survival via signaling pathways (e.g., MAPK, PI3K‐Akt), while excessive levels induced death through lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial dysfunction [ ]. Given HMGB3 and SSBP1's roles in chromatin regulation [ , ], their nuclear interaction may modulate transcription of mitochondrial and antioxidant genes (e.g., TFAM, PGC1‐α, SOD1/2/3, NRF1/2) through chromatin remodeling and histone modification, potentially preventing ROS‐induced apoptosis, though further experimental validation is needed.
In this study, it was noteworthy that analysis of 2 independent datasets demonstrated brain metastases exhibit unique organ‐specific enrichment of mitochondrial metabolic remodeling, distinct from bone, adrenal, lymph node, and lung metastases (Supplementary Figure ). In the hypoxic brain metastatic microenvironment [ , ], tumor cells enhanced lipid utilization via DRP1‐mediated mitochondrial metabolic remodeling, adapting to the lipid‐rich, hypoxic niche [ , ]. Three studies further confirmed this enrichment and demonstrated the therapeutic potential of targeting mitochondrial metabolism in preclinical models [ , ]. However, the upstream regulatory mechanisms remain unclear. Our study identified key upstream mechanisms by which tumor cells adapted to the brain metastatic metabolic niche through mitochondrial remodeling, offering novel evidence for targeting HMGB3 and mitochondrial metabolism in brain metastasis prevention and therapy.
This study had several limitations. Due to historical technical constraints and inter‐institutional variability in assay methodologies, oncodriver data for the Primary LUAD cohort was incomplete. Future research should focus on LUAD patients with well‐defined pathological subtypes. Furthermore, although the H2030‐derived brain metastasis model exhibited robust experimental reproducibility, the utilization of a single cell line to establish the brain metastasis model may not adequately encompass the clinical heterogeneity observed among diverse primary tumor types and their respective molecular subtypes. Further extensive in vitro and in vivo validations are essential to elucidate HMGB3‐induced SSBP1 dynamics and its role in mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming during invasion‐metastasis cascades, as well as the HMGB3‐SSBP1 interaction‐mediated regulation of nuclear genes in maintaining redox homeostasis. This mechanism ensures the survival of brain metastatic tumor cells in the hypoxic and oxidative stress conditions of the metastatic niche. Given that HMGB3 may also promote tumor cell proliferation and survival in primary lung tumors, and considering the distinct microenvironments and metabolic pools of the lung and brain, the molecular mechanisms underlying HMGB3‐mediated tumor proliferation in primary lung versus brain metastatic contexts may differ. This warrants further investigation.

5.

## CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, this study revealed a mechanism by which HMGB3 promoted LUAD brain metastasis. We demonstrated that HMGB3‐mediated nuclear translocation of SSBP1, which induced mitochondrial metabolic remodeling, elevated ROS levels that inactivated PTEN and inhibited its function, thereby activated the PI3K‐Akt signaling pathway. Coordinated regulation of this mechanism provided a framework for understanding the high propensity for brain metastasis in tumors overexpressing HMGB3. These findings ultimately bridged the knowledge gap in targeting metabolic‐signaling dependencies in LUAD brain metastasis, thereby providing a roadmap for the development of brain metastasis‐specific diagnostics and therapies.
