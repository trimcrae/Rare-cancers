The role of BEX1 methylation in gliomas is not well understood, despite its potential implications for tumor biology and therapy. Investigating this relationship could uncover critical mechanisms underlying glioma pathogenesis and highlight therapeutic targets. This study aims to elucidate the specific mechanisms by which BEX1 methylation regulates the cell cycle and apoptosis in glioma. We conducted bioinformatics analyses to assess BEX1 expression differences in glioma using tissue samples, followed by validation through Western blot and qRT-PCR. Functional assays in glioma cell lines were performed, employing gene transfection and small molecule inhibitors to further explore BEX1’s role in the AKT/ERK/STAT3 signaling pathways. Our findings reveal that BEX1 is significantly downregulated in gliomas due to promoter methylation, which in turn activates the AKT/ERK/STAT3 signaling cascade, leading to alterations in cell cycle regulation and apoptosis. Targeting BEX1 methylation presents a promising therapeutic avenue for glioma treatment. This study provides valuable insights into the mechanisms of BEX1 in glioma, paving the way for clinical translation and further research.

## Introduction
Gliomas are a heterogeneous group of primary brain tumors, with glioblastoma multiforme being the most prevalent and aggressive subtype , . Epidemiological data indicate that gliomas account for approximately 30% of all central nervous system tumors, with a higher incidence observed in adults . These tumors exhibit complex pathological mechanisms characterized by abnormal cellular proliferation, invasion, and angiogenesis, often leading to poor prognoses , . Despite advancements in neuroimaging and molecular diagnostics, treatment options for gliomas remain limited, primarily involving surgical intervention, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy . The overall survival rates for high-grade gliomas, such as glioblastoma, remain dismal, highlighting the need for more effective therapeutic strategies . Furthermore, the underlying molecular mechanisms that drive glioma development and progression are not yet fully elucidated, creating a significant challenge in the field . Addressing these complexities and developing targeted therapies is essential to improve patient outcomes and advance the understanding of glioma biology.
Methylation, a key epigenetic modification, plays a crucial role in regulating gene expression and maintaining cellular homeostasis, particularly in the context of the cell cycle – . Aberrant methylation patterns can disrupt normal cell cycle regulation, leading to uncontrolled cellular proliferation and contributing to tumorigenesis . In various malignancies, including gliomas, altered methylation of critical tumor suppressor genes and oncogenes has been implicated in promoting tumor growth and progression. Specifically, in gliomas, dysregulation of methylation processes can influence the expression of genes that govern cell cycle checkpoints, apoptosis, and DNA repair mechanisms , . Recent studies have highlighted the significance of methylation changes in glioma subtypes, suggesting that these alterations may serve as potential biomarkers for diagnosis and prognosis. Moreover, targeting the methylation machinery presents a promising therapeutic strategy, with emerging research focusing on the development of drugs that can reverse abnormal methylation patterns , . This approach aims to restore normal cell cycle regulation and enhance the sensitivity of glioma cells to conventional therapies, ultimately improving patient outcomes , . As our understanding of the interplay between methylation and cell cycle regulation deepens, it becomes increasingly evident that these processes hold significant potential as therapeutic targets in glioma treatment.
BEX1 (Brain Expressed X-linked 1) is a protein that plays a significant role in various biological processes, including neuronal development and cell signaling . Its expression is primarily restricted to the brain, and it has been implicated in the regulation of cell proliferation and differentiation. Recent studies have highlighted the involvement of BEX1 in cancer biology, where altered expression levels have been associated with tumorigenesis in several malignancies, including breast, prostate, and lung cancers. The aberrant regulation of BEX1 appears to influence key pathways related to cell survival and apoptosis, contributing to the aggressive behavior of tumors , . Despite its emerging significance, research on BEX1 as a therapeutic target in gliomas remains relatively scarce. Understanding the specific mechanisms by which BEX1 interacts with methylation processes and regulates the cell cycle could provide critical insights into the tumorigenic processes of gliomas , . Moreover, elucidating the role of BEX1 in these pathways could pave the way for novel personalized therapeutic strategies, enhancing treatment efficacy and improving patient outcomes. As the field moves toward more individualized medicine, the exploration of BEX1’s involvement in tumor biology represents a promising avenue for future research, with the potential to inform targeted interventions based on the molecular characteristics of gliomas.

## Methods

## Bioinformatics analysis
GEPIA was employed to compare expression levels of the BEX1 gene between tumor and normal tissues derived from TCGA and GTEx databases, with high expression marked in red and low expression in green across multiple cancer types. This comprehensive analysis provided insights into the differential expression and methylation status of the BEX1 gene in the context of pan-cancer .
Survival analysis of the BEX1 gene was conducted using the MethSurv platform, applying univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models based on patient CpG methylation levels. Overall survival (OS) was assessed, illustrating survival differences between low and high methylation patient groups. The X-axis represents survival time in days, while the Y-axis indicates survival probability. Survival curves (red and blue) remained consistent irrespective of clinical factor inclusion, though the log-rank test p-values and hazard ratios (HR) were influenced by additional factors in the multivariate analysis, with group differences evaluated using the log-likelihood ratio.
Density plots were utilized to visualize critical thresholds for dichotomizing patient methylation levels, displaying β-values on the X-axis and the corresponding sample proportion on the Y-axis. Vertical lines indicated classification points, with the red numeral denoting the current cutoff for patient grouping to explore associations with query genes. Violin plots illustrated methylation differences among various sample groups based on clinical factors, highlighting the distribution, median, and interquartile ranges of CpG methylation in relation to patient characteristics, such as age and sex .
Transcriptomic expression data of BEX1 were retrieved from public glioma datasets (such as TCGA-LGG and TCGA-GBM) using the UCSC Xena browser and normalized to TPM values. Patients were grouped according to the WHO 2016 classification into low-grade (WHO II) and high-grade (WHO IV) gliomas. Statistical comparison was conducted using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. Data visualization was performed in GraphPad Prism 9.5 with significance thresholds set at p < 0.05.
Clinical and gene expression data for glioma patients were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Patients were grouped by IDH mutation status and MGMT promoter methylation status. Within each subgroup, Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated by dividing patients into high and low BEX1 expression cohorts based on the median expression level. Overall survival differences were assessed using the Log-rank test, and significance was set at p < 0.05. Analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 9.5.
Protein–protein interactions involving BEX1 were predicted using the STRING database (version 12.0; https://string-db.org/). The organism was set to Homo sapiens, and interaction sources included experimental data, text mining, co-expression, and curated databases. A confidence interaction score threshold of 0.4 (medium confidence) was applied. The resulting network was exported and visualized in high resolution for figure integration.

## Cell culture
The U-251 and LN229 glioma cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The medium was refreshed every 2–3 days, and cells were passaged at 80–90% confluence using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA solution. For experimental use, cells were seeded at the appropriate density and allowed to adhere overnight before further treatments or assays. All cell cultures were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination to ensure experimental reliability .

## Cell transfection and methodology for methylation
To overexpress the BEX1 gene in U251 and LN229 cell lines, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 10^5 cells per well and incubated overnight until they reached 70–80% confluence. The BEX1 expression plasmid was co-transfected with a lipofectamine-based transfection reagent (Lipofectamine 3000) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 2 µg of the plasmid DNA was mixed with 4 µL of transfection reagent in 200 µL of serum-free medium and incubated for 20 min to form transfection complexes. This mixture was then added to the cells and incubated for an additional 48 h. Following incubation, cells were harvested for further analysis, including quantitative qRT-PCR and Western blot, to confirm the successful overexpression of BEX1.
For gene silencing experiments, U-251 and LN229 cells were transfected with short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting BEX1. Transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For shRNA transfections, cells were incubated with shRNA-containing plasmids and a transfection reagent for 24 h, followed by selection with puromycin. For siRNA transfections, cells were exposed to siRNA and Lipofectamine 3000 for 48 h. Transfection efficiency and gene silencing were validated by qRT-PCR and Western blot to assess the knockdown of target genes at both mRNA and protein levels. All sequences targeting can be found in Supplementary Table .
To induce methylation in U251 and LN229 cell lines, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and cultured in standard growth medium until 70–80% confluence was achieved. Following this, cells were treated with S-adenosyl methionine (SAM) at a final concentration of 100 µM for 24, 48, and 72 hours . Control groups were treated with an equivalent volume of sterile saline. For demethylation experiments, cells were treated with 5-azacytidine (5-AzaC) at a concentration of 5 µM for 72 h, with daily medium replacement . For pathway inhibition, specific inhibitors were used at the following concentrations: LY294002 (PI3K inhibitor, 20 µM) U0126 (ERK inhibitor, 10 µM) and Stattic (STAT3 inhibitor, 5 µM) . Post-treatment, cells were harvested, and genomic DNA was extracted using a DNA extraction kit (Tiangen Biotech, Cat# DP304). Methylation levels were assessed using bisulfite sequencing and quantitative methylation-specific PCR (qMSP) to evaluate the effectiveness of SAM or 5-AzaC treatment in inducing gene-specific methylation changes.

## qRT-PCR
Reagents for qRT-PCR were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific, including the TRIzol reagent for RNA extraction, the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit for cDNA synthesis, and the PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix for qRT-PCR. Primers for target genes were designed using Primer-BLAST and synthesized by Sangon Biotech, ensuring optimal specificity and efficiency. Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using TRIzol, and 1 µg of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA. qRT-PCR was performed in triplicate using SYBR Green Master Mix on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System. GAPDH served as an internal control, and relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. All sequences can be found in Supplementary Table , and six experiments were performed .

## The experiment of colony formation
Reagents for the colony formation assay were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, including Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), and penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a low density (500 cells per well) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. After 10–14 days, colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.5% crystal violet. Colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted using ImageJ software. The experiment was performed in triplicate, and data were analyzed to assess cell proliferation and clonogenic potential . Colony counting and migration/invasion assay quantification were performed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Version 1.53t, https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/).

## The experiment of transwell
Reagents for the Transwell migration and invasion assays were purchased from Corning (Transwell chambers with 8.0 μm pores). For the migration assay, 2 × 10^4 U-251 or LN229 cells were suspended in serum-free DMEM and seeded into the upper chamber of the Transwell. For the invasion assay, the upper chamber was pre-coated with Matrigel (Corning). The lower chamber contained DMEM with 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2, cells that migrated or invaded through the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Cells were counted under a microscope in five random fields, and the experiment was performed in triplicate.

## Apoptotic rate assessed through flow cytometric analysis
Reagents for the apoptosis detection assay using flow cytometry were purchased from BD Biosciences, including the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit and 7-AAD for dead cell staining. U-251 and LN229 cells were treated according to experimental conditions and harvested by trypsinization. After washing twice with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), 1 × 10^5 cells were resuspended in 100 µL of binding buffer. Cells were stained with 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL of 7-AAD for 15 min in the dark at room temperature. Stained cells were then analyzed using a BD FACSCanto™ flow cytometer. The proportion of apoptotic cells (early and late apoptosis) was quantified, and data analysis was performed using FlowJo software. The experiment was conducted in triplicate. Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo™ software (BD Biosciences, Version 10.8, https://www.flowjo.com).

## Western blot
Reagents for Western blot were purchased from Abcam, including primary antibodies against BEX1, CyclinA, CyclinB, CyclinD, CyclinE, CDK1, Bcl-2, Bax, Cleaved-Caspase3, p-AKT, AKT, p-ERK, ERK, p-STAT3, STAT3 and β-ACTIN, as well as secondary antibodies conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP). Samples were lysated and prepared in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were quantified using the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using the ECL detection system (GE Healthcare) and imaged with a chemiluminescence imaging system (Bio-Rad). The experiment was performed in triplicate, and β-ACTIN was used as a loading control.

## Animal experiment and human samples collection
Reagents for the subcutaneous tumor formation assay were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (DMEM, PBS, and Matrigel). Nude mice (Balb/c) were anesthetized using isoflurane (Haver Lock, Cat# A0-1901) for all surgical and experimental procedures. Mice were placed in a closed chamber, and anesthesia was induced with 4–5% isoflurane in 100% oxygen at a flow rate of 1–2 L/min. Once the animals were fully anesthetized, the concentration was reduced to 1–2% isoflurane for maintenance. The depth of anesthesia was monitored by assessing the absence of the pedal reflex, and body temperature was maintained using a heated pad (Harvard Apparatus, Cat# 6875). Following the procedure, mice were allowed to recover in a warm environment and monitored until they regained normal activity. And Under anesthesia, nude mice (Balb/c) were subcutaneously injected with 1 × 10^6 LN229 and U-251 cells mixed with Matrigel (1:1) into the flanks. Tumor growth was monitored weekly, and tumor volumes were measured using calipers with the formula: volume = (length × width^2)/2. After 4 weeks, the mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation, and tumors were excised and measured size. Tumor growth and size data were analyzed to assess the effects of OE-BEX1, sh-BEX1, and SAM on tumorigenesis. The experiment was performed with a minimum of three mice per group, and all procedures were approved by the institutional animal care and use committee. ## The in vivo imaging and pulmonary metastasis
Reagents for the in vivo imaging and pulmonary metastasis assay were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (DMEM, FBS, and luciferase reagent). U-251 cells genetically modified to stably expressing luciferase were used. Nude mice (Balb/c) were tail-vein injected with 1 × 10^6 luciferase-expressing cells in 200 µL of DMEM. After 4 weeks, pulmonary metastases were assessed using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS Spectrum, PerkinElmer) following the administration of D-luciferin substrate. Mice were anesthetized, and bioluminescent imaging was performed to visualize and quantify lung metastases. Tumor burden and metastasis data were analyzed to evaluate the effects of STAT3in, sh-BEX1, and SAM on lung metastasis. The experiment was conducted with a minimum of six mice per group, and all procedures adhered to institutional animal care guidelines.

## Multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC)
Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections from human low-grade and high-grade glioma specimens were subjected to multiplex immunohistochemistry using the Opal™ 7-Color IHC Kit (Akoya Biosciences, Cat. No. NEL801001KT) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in AR6 buffer (Akoya Biosciences, Cat. No. AR600250ML). Sequential staining was performed using primary antibodies against p-STAT3 (CST, Cat. No. 9145, 1:100), p-ERK1/2 (CST, Cat. No. 4370, 1:200), p-AKT (CST, Cat. No. 4060, 1:100), CDK1 (Abcam, Cat. No. ab32094, 1:100), and BEX1 (Proteintech, Cat. No. 13608-1-AP, 1:100). Corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were followed by Opal fluorophore labeling, including Opal520 for BEX1. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Slides were scanned using the Vectra Polaris imaging system (Akoya Biosciences), and multispectral images were unmixed and analyzed with inForm software (Akoya Biosciences). All samples were processed in parallel under identical conditions. Image analysis was performed using inForm® Tissue Finder software (Akoya Biosciences, Version 2.4, https://www.akoyabio.com).

## Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay
To investigate the interaction between BEX1 and AKT, U251 and LN229 glioma cells were co-transfected with plasmids encoding FLAG-tagged BEX1 and HIS-tagged AKT using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher, Cat. No. L3000008) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After 48 h, cells were lysed in ice-cold IP lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher, Cat. No. 87787) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (CST, Cat. No. 5872). Equal amounts of total protein (500–800 µg) were incubated with anti-FLAG magnetic beads (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. M8823) overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation. The immunoprecipitates were washed, eluted, and subjected to SDS-PAGE followed by immunoblotting using anti-HIS antibody (Abcam, Cat. No. ab18184, 1:1000) to detect AKT. Reciprocal Co-IP was performed using anti-HIS magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher, Cat. No. 88836) and probed with anti-FLAG antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. F1804, 1:1000) to confirm the interaction. Input and IgG controls were included for validation. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times.

## Statistical analysis
Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.0 and GraphPad Prism version 9.0. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. For non-normally distributed data, the Mann-Whitney U test was used. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Bioinformatics analysis reveals low expression of BEX1 in glioma patients associated with aberrant methylation
Bioinformatics analyses play a crucial role in our research. In this study, we conducted a pan-cancer analysis, revealing that BEX1 is significantly downregulated in both high-grade and low-grade gliomas (Fig.  A). Subsequent survival analyses indicated a trend toward poorer disease-free survival and overall survival in cohorts with low BEX1 expression compared to those with high BEX1 expression (Fig.  B and C).

 

Given the importance of methylation modifications in tumor progression, we performed a methylation-related analysis. The results demonstrated that patients with low methylation levels had worse survival outcomes compared to those with high methylation levels (Fig.  D). To determine the optimal cutoff value for methylation stratification, we applied the maxstat algorithm, which identified the beta value threshold with the highest statistical separation between survival groups. The results of this stratification are visually presented in Fig.  E.
Violin plots illustrated the methylation differences between various sample groups based on clinical factors, emphasizing the distribution, median, and interquartile range of CpG methylation in relation to patient characteristics such as age and sex (Fig.  F).
To investigate the clinical relevance of BEX1 expression in glioma, we analyzed mRNA expression levels across glioma specimens of varying histological grades (n = 50). As shown in Fig.  G, BEX1 expression was significantly reduced in WHO grade IV gliomas compared to grade II tumors (p < 0.0001), suggesting a potential association between BEX1 downregulation and glioma malignancy progression.
Further stratified survival analysis demonstrated that among IDH-mutant glioma patients, those with high BEX1 expression had markedly longer overall survival compared to those with low BEX1 expression (Fig.  H, p < 0.01, Log-rank test). Similarly, in the subset of MGMT-methylated gliomas, high BEX1 expression was associated with significantly improved prognosis (Fig.  I, p < 0.05), indicating that BEX1 may serve as a favorable prognostic biomarker in molecularly defined glioma subtypes.
To explore potential mechanistic pathways, we constructed a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network centered on BEX1 using STRING (v12). The resulting interaction map identified AKT1, MAPK1 (ERK2), and STAT3 as putative BEX1-interacting proteins (Fig.  J). These connections were supported by curated database and experimental evidence with medium confidence (score ≥ 0.4), suggesting that BEX1 may exert biological functions through modulation of PI3K/AKT, MAPK, or STAT3 signaling pathways in glioma cells.

## BEX1 May induce apoptosis and inhibit proliferation and migration of glioma cells by regulating the cell cycle
Recent studies have linked tumor cell methylation to cell cycle regulation, exploring its implications for tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and apoptosis. However, the role of BEX1 methylation in these processes and its underlying mechanisms remain unclear. To investigate the functional role of BEX1 in glioma, we established stable BEX1-overexpressing U251 and LN229 cell lines using two independent constructs (OE-BEX1-1 and OE-BEX1-2). Western blot and qRT-PCR confirmed successful overexpression of BEX1 at both protein and transcript levels (Fig.  A–C).

 

Functionally, colony formation assays demonstrated a marked reduction in clonogenic potential in BEX1-overexpressing cells compared to negative controls (Fig.  D, J). Similarly, CCK-8 assays revealed that BEX1 overexpression significantly inhibited cell proliferation in both U251 and LN229 cells (Fig.  G). Transwell migration assays showed that BEX1 also suppressed cell migratory capacity (Fig.  F, K).
Flow cytometric analysis revealed an increase in apoptotic cells following BEX1 overexpression (Fig.  H, I), which was supported by the observed upregulation of pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and cleaved caspase-3 and downregulation of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 at both protein and mRNA levels (Fig.  L, O–P).
Further mechanistic investigation showed that BEX1 overexpression resulted in the downregulation of key cell cycle regulatory proteins, including Cyclin A, B, D, E, and CDK1, as demonstrated by Western blot and qRT-PCR in both glioma cell lines (Fig.  E, M–N), suggesting that BEX1 may induce cell cycle arrest in addition to promoting apoptosis.

## Methylation suppresses BEX1 expression and promotes glioma cell proliferation and migration
To investigate the epigenetic regulation of BEX1 in glioma, we first established BEX1-knockdown U251 and LN229 glioma cell lines using two independent shRNAs. Western blot and qRT-PCR analysis confirmed that both shRNAs effectively silenced BEX1 expression at the protein and mRNA levels, respectively (Fig.  A–C).

 

Next, to determine whether methylation modulates BEX1 expression, we treated glioma cells with increasing concentrations of S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), a methyl donor. Colony formation assays showed that while 200 µM SAM had minimal effect, 50 µM significantly enhanced clonogenicity in both cell lines (Fig.  D–E). Western blot analysis revealed a dose-dependent suppression of BEX1 expression with increasing SAM concentrations, accompanied by the upregulation of cell cycle regulators, including Cyclins A–E and CDK1 (Fig.  F). This was corroborated by qRT-PCR results, which showed similar transcriptional changes in both U251 and LN229 cells (Fig.  G–J).
To further confirm that methylation is responsible for BEX1 downregulation, we treated cells with the DNA methyltransferase inhibitor 5-Aza-2’-deoxycytidine (5-AzaC), either alone or in combination with SAM. Western blot analysis demonstrated that 5-AzaC alone restored BEX1 protein levels, and co-treatment with SAM and 5-AzaC partially rescued SAM-induced suppression (Fig.  K).
Transwell migration assays revealed that SAM treatment significantly enhanced glioma cell migration, whereas 5-AzaC inhibited migration. Notably, SAM + 5-AzaC co-treatment reduced the migratory capacity compared to SAM alone (Fig.  L), suggesting that BEX1 methylation promotes glioma cell motility. qRT-PCR further confirmed the trends in BEX1 expression under these treatment conditions (Fig.  M–N).

## Methylation suppresses BEX1 and activates the AKT/ERK/STAT3 signaling axis to regulate cell cycle progression in glioma cells
To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which BEX1 methylation influences glioma progression, we investigated the activation status of key signaling pathways following SAM-induced methylation, BEX1 knockdown, or overexpression. Western blot analysis revealed that SAM treatment significantly enhanced the phosphorylation of AKT, ERK1/2, and STAT3 in both U251 and LN229 cells, while BEX1 overexpression attenuated this activation. Conversely, BEX1 silencing further amplified SAM-induced pathway activation, indicating that BEX1 methylation may relieve inhibitory control over the AKT/ERK/STAT3 cascade (Fig.  A).

 

To further validate the signaling hierarchy, specific inhibitors targeting AKT (AKTi), ERK1/2 (ERK1/2i), and STAT3 (STAT3in) were applied under SAM treatment conditions. As shown in Fig.  B, inhibition of each pathway component reduced downstream protein phosphorylation and reversed SAM-induced upregulation of Cyclin A and CDK1, suggesting a functional linkage between BEX1 methylation and these signaling pathways.
Consistent with these findings, qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that SAM-induced methylation significantly suppressed BEX1 mRNA expression while upregulating the expression of CyclinA and CDK1. These effects were reversed by BEX1 overexpression and exacerbated by BEX1 knockdown (Fig.  C–F). Collectively, these results suggest that BEX1 methylation activates the AKT/ERK/STAT3 pathway to drive cell cycle progression in glioma cells.

## Methylation suppresses BEX1, promoting lung metastasis of glioma via STAT3-Related pathways
To investigate the regulatory effects of methylation and BEX1 protein on the malignant behaviors of glioma cells at the animal model level, we conducted subcutaneous tumor formation experiments. The results indicated that methylation significantly increased the tumor burden in the animals, while BEX1 knockdown further exacerbated this burden. Conversely, overexpression of BEX1 resulted in a marked reduction in tumor burden (Fig.  A), with relative quantification shown in Fig.  B.

 

Ki67 immunohistochemistry staining demonstrated a reduced proportion of proliferating tumor cells in the SAM-treated and OE-BEX1 groups, while BEX1 knockdown led to increased Ki67 + cell percentages despite SAM administration (Fig.  C–D), suggesting that BEX1 is required for the antiproliferative effect of SAM in vivo.
To assess the impact of methylation and BEX1 protein on distant metastasis of glioma cells, we performed in vivo imaging to evaluate pulmonary metastasis. The findings revealed that methylation significantly enhanced the lung metastatic capability of glioma cells. Further knockdown of BEX1 led to an additional increase in pulmonary metastasis, whereas inhibition of the STAT3 pathway using small molecule inhibitors mitigated this enhancement, resulting in a significant reduction in lung metastasis of glioma cells (Fig.  E), with relative quantification provided in Fig.  F.
Consistent with the in vivo findings, colony formation and CCK-8 proliferation assays in both U251 and LN229 cells confirmed that knockdown of BEX1 abolished the inhibitory effects of SAM, whereas STAT3 inhibition restored the suppressive phenotype (Fig.  G–K), highlighting the functional interplay between SAM, BEX1, and STAT3 in glioma growth regulation.

## Analysis of clinical glioma samples reveals BEX1 is downregulated in high-grade gliomas and interacts with AKT in glioma cells
We performed Western blot analysis to assess BEX1 protein expression in tumor tissues and adjacent non-tumor specimens from 12 glioma patients. The results revealed a significant decrease in BEX1 protein levels in the tumor tissues compared to the adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig.  A-F). Complementary qRT-PCR analysis confirmed that BEX1 mRNA levels were significantly decreased in tumor tissues relative to adjacent non-tumor samples in all 12 cases. These findings suggest that BEX1 may play a critical role in the development and progression of gliomas (Fig.  G-I).

 

Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed this observation. As shown in Fig.  J–K, BEX1 expression was markedly reduced in WHO grade IV glioma sections relative to WHO grade II, while levels of phosphorylated AKT (p-AKT), ERK (p-ERK), STAT3 (p-STAT3), and the cell cycle regulator CDK1 were significantly elevated in high-grade gliomas. Quantification of immunofluorescence intensity supported these findings, suggesting an inverse relationship between BEX1 expression and oncogenic signaling activation in glioma progression.
To explore potential mechanistic links, we performed co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays in LN229 and U251 cells co-transfected with Flag-BEX1 and His-AKT. As shown in Fig.  L, BEX1 was detected in the immunoprecipitates of AKT, indicating a physical interaction between BEX1 and AKT in glioma cells. These findings suggest that BEX1 may negatively regulate AKT pathway activation through direct protein-protein interaction.

## Discussion
The treatment of glioma presents a significant clinical challenge, primarily due to its aggressive nature and poor prognosis , , . While advancements in precision targeted therapies have transformed the management of various cancers, progress in glioma treatment has been notably slow , , . This stagnation can be attributed to the tumor’s inherent heterogeneity and the complex interactions within its microenvironment, which hinder the effectiveness of targeted approaches , , . Therefore, there is an urgent need to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms involved in glioma progression , . Methylation patterns, particularly those involving the BEX1 gene, have emerged as promising targets in this context. Understanding the role of BEX1 methylation in glioma could not only provide insights into its pathogenesis but also pave the way for novel therapeutic strategies that enhance treatment efficacy and patient outcomes .
Our results demonstrate a significant downregulation of BEX1 in glioma, associated with aberrant methylation patterns, corroborating findings from existing literature that highlight the crucial role of methylation in tumor progression , . Previous studies have shown that low BEX1 expression correlates with poor prognosis in various cancers, including glioma, suggesting that BEX1 may function as a tumor suppressor . Our bioinformatics analysis revealed that lower BEX1 levels are linked to adverse survival outcomes, emphasizing the potential of BEX1 as a biomarker for glioma prognosis.
Furthermore, our Western blot and qRT-PCR analyses confirm the reduced expression of BEX1 in tumor tissues, consistent with findings in other malignancies where methylation has been shown to silence tumor suppressor genes. This raises the possibility that therapeutic strategies aimed at demethylating agents might restore BEX1 expression, thereby inhibiting tumor cell proliferation and promoting apoptosis , , . These findings align with previous research indicating that reactivation of methylation-silenced genes can enhance the sensitivity of cancer cells to chemotherapy.
The functional assays conducted reveal that BEX1 overexpression significantly inhibits glioma cell proliferation and migration while promoting apoptosis. This aligns with studies suggesting that tumor suppressors, such as BEX1, can impede malignant behaviors by regulating the cell cycle and apoptotic pathways. Our results indicate that BEX1 impacts key cell cycle regulators, suggesting that methylation-induced repression of BEX1 leads to dysregulated cell cycle progression, further contributing to glioma malignancy.
Previous studies have reported diverse roles of BEX1 across different cancer types, underscoring its context-dependent function. In breast cancer, BEX1 has been shown to act as a tumor suppressor by inducing apoptosis through modulation of mitochondrial pathways and inhibition of BCL-2 expression . Conversely, in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), BEX1 has been implicated in promoting tumor progression via interaction with cytoskeletal proteins and enhancing cell motility suggesting a tissue-specific functional divergence. These findings highlight the importance of understanding BEX1’s molecular partners and regulatory networks in distinct tumor microenvironments.
In the context of glioma, our findings demonstrate a consistent downregulation of BEX1 expression, particularly in WHO grade IV tumors. Furthermore, stratified analyses revealed that this suppression is more pronounced in IDH wild-type gliomas compared to IDH-mutant subtypes, as supported by TCGA-based mRNA expression profiling and survival analyses. This molecular stratification not only reinforces the potential of BEX1 as a prognostic biomarker but also implies that its epigenetic silencing may be more relevant to the pathogenesis of aggressive, treatment-resistant glioma subtypes.
Together, these observations underscore the biological heterogeneity of BEX1 function and regulation across tumor types and within glioma molecular subgroups. Future studies incorporating single-cell transcriptomics and epigenomic profiling may further delineate the context-specific roles of BEX1, ultimately informing personalized therapeutic strategies.
Importantly, we also established that methylation not only suppresses BEX1 but also activates the AKT/ERK/STAT3 signaling pathways. This is particularly relevant as these pathways have been implicated in promoting cell survival and metastasis in gliomas. The persistent activation of these pathways in the presence of methylated BEX1 suggests a feedback mechanism whereby tumor cells can exploit aberrant signaling to enhance their survival and metastatic potential .
In vivo experiments further support our in vitro findings, demonstrating that BEX1 knockdown exacerbates tumor burden and enhances metastatic capability, which aligns with the notion that loss of tumor suppressor functions drives cancer progression. The attenuation of metastasis following STAT3 pathway inhibition indicates a critical role of this signaling cascade in mediating the effects of BEX1 on glioma aggressiveness.
While we fully acknowledge that targeted bisulfite sequencing represents a gold-standard method for assessing CpG methylation at single-base resolution, this technique presents notable technical and logistical challenges in the context of low-abundance tumor suppressor genes such as BEX1.
First, the design of specific bisulfite PCR primers within the dense CpG-rich regions of the BEX1 promoter is technically complex, particularly due to the high GC content and sequence redundancy. This often results in poor amplification efficiency and off-target effects . Additionally, bisulfite treatment itself leads to significant DNA degradation and fragmentation, which can compromise data quality, especially in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) glioma tissues , . Second, BEX1 has relatively low basal expression in normal brain tissue, and its promoter methylation status may be heterogeneously distributed among glioma cell subpopulations. Therefore, traditional bisulfite Sanger sequencing or methylation-specific PCR (MSP) may lack the sensitivity to resolve cell-specific epigenetic alterations in bulk samples . To address this limitation, we instead utilized integrative methylation data from public databases (e.g., TCGA, MethSurv) combined with functional modulation using methylating (SAM) and demethylating agents (5-AzaC) to provide indirect but functionally validated evidence of BEX1 epigenetic silencing. These results consistently demonstrated that methylation was negatively associated with BEX1 expression and glioma aggressiveness. Nevertheless, we fully agree that future studies incorporating high-resolution bisulfite sequencing (or nanopore-based methylome analysis) of the BEX1 promoter region will be valuable to delineate the precise CpG loci responsible for transcriptional repression and interpatient heterogeneity.
We also acknowledge the limitations of the in vivo models used in this study, which primarily included subcutaneous tumor formation and tail vein metastasis models. While these models are valuable for assessing the proliferative and metastatic potential of tumor cells, they do not fully recapitulate the infiltrative growth pattern and microenvironmental interactions characteristic of gliomas within the brain parenchyma. Consequently, the current findings may not completely reflect the biological role of BEX1 in the orthotopic brain microenvironment. In future studies, we plan to establish orthotopic implantation models with either BEX1 overexpression or knockdown, including humanized intracranial injection models and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models. These approaches will allow us to further investigate the functional role of BEX1 within the glioma microenvironment and provide more robust in vivo evidence to support its potential as a therapeutic target.
Overall, our study underscores the importance of BEX1 and its methylation status in glioma biology. Given the emerging evidence linking methylation to tumor behavior, further exploration of BEX1’s role as a therapeutic target is warranted. By elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying BEX1 regulation and function, we can develop novel strategies aimed at enhancing the efficacy of current glioma treatments and improving patient outcomes.

## Conclusions
In summary, our study reveals that BEX1 is significantly downregulated in glioma tissues, with its expression closely linked to aberrant methylation patterns. Functional assays demonstrate that BEX1 plays a critical role in inhibiting glioma cell proliferation and migration while promoting apoptosis, implicating it as a potential tumor suppressor. Moreover, methylation not only suppresses BEX1 expression but also activates key signaling pathways, including AKT/ERK/STAT3, which contribute to the malignant behavior of glioma cells. These findings underscore the necessity of further investigations into BEX1 as a therapeutic target, providing insights that may enhance treatment strategies for glioma patients.
