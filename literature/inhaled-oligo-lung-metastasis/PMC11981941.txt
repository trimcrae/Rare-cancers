## Abstract
The survival of malignant cells within tumours is often seen as depending on ruthless competition for nutrients and other resources , . Although competition is certainly critical for tumour evolution and cancer progression, cooperative interactions within tumours are also important, albeit poorly understood , . Cooperative populations at all levels of biological organization risk extinction if their population size falls below a critical tipping point , . Here we examined whether cooperation among tumour cells may be a potential therapeutic target. We identified a cooperative mechanism that enables tumour cells to proliferate under the amino acid-deprived conditions found in the tumour microenvironment. Disruption of this mechanism drove cultured tumour populations to the critical extinction point and resulted in a marked reduction in tumour growth in vivo. Mechanistically, we show that tumour cells collectively digest extracellular oligopeptides through the secretion of aminopeptidases. The resulting free amino acids benefit both aminopeptidase-secreting cells and neighbouring cells. We identified CNDP2 as the key enzyme that hydrolyses these peptides extracellularly, and loss of this aminopeptidase prevents tumour growth in vitro and in vivo. These data show that cooperative scavenging of nutrients is key to survival in the tumour microenvironment and reveal a targetable cancer vulnerability.
Subject terms: Cancer metabolism, Cancer microenvironment

Nutrient-starved tumour cells cooperate by secreting aminopeptidases that digest oligopeptides in the microenvironment, creating a shared pool of free amino acids.

## Main
Cell biologists have long established that cell growth is inhibited at low cell densities – (Fig. ). The positive effect of density on growth cannot be explained by purely competitive interactions, and it is a clear indication that individuals within the population cooperate—a phenomenon known in ecology as the Allee effect . Despite these observations, studies on the growth of cancer cells have almost entirely focus on how they compete. The role of cooperation in tumour evolution has previously been recognized , but most of these studies are either theoretical or depend on specific growth factor-mediated paracrine signal loops that offer narrow therapeutic targets , . We therefore sought to identify universal mechanisms of cell cooperation that may be targeted to stall and potentially collapse tumour populations.
 Challenging environments favour the emergence of cooperation in nature , . For example, emperor penguins form tight huddles to conserve heat during the gelid Antarctic winter, whereas this behaviour does not occur during warmer months (Fig. ). Similarly, starvation can drive microorganisms to display cooperative strategies that are absent or redundant under more favourable conditions , . As nutrient scarcity is a defining feature of the tumour microenvironment , , we reasoned that cooperative feeding mechanisms may be favoured by cancer evolution.
To determine the strength of cooperation, one can focus on individual or per capita growth rates. In populations that are driven solely by competition, per capita growth is maximized when their density is low as resources are in abundance (Fig. ). By contrast, cooperative populations show maximal per capita growth at intermediate densities—low enough to avoid strong competition but sufficiently high to enable individuals to interact and cooperate. As density decreases, cooperative populations approach their Allee threshold, below which the lack of cooperative interactions leads to a rapid population collapse (Fig. ). As shown in the example in Fig. , the growth of tumour cells illustrated here by A375 cells neatly follows the curved per capita growth of cooperative populations (Fig. ).
To test the relationship between cell cooperation and nutrient levels, we seeded a panel of tumour cell lines at different densities and gradually reduced the levels of specific nutrients. We used automated live microscopy and image analysis to track the growth rates of thousands of independent cell populations – (Extended Data Fig. and ). To formally quantify the strength of cooperation, we determined the Allee threshold—the minimal number of individuals required to survive under a specific environment , (Extended Data Fig. ). In the example of penguins, the Allee threshold—the size of the huddle—increases with colder temperatures , indicating a stronger need to cooperate (Fig. ). Deprivation of some nutrients, such as glucose, had no discernable effects on the Allee threshold, suggesting that cells do not use cooperation to cope with these conditions (Fig. ). By contrast, all cell types that we tested showed a strong need to cooperate when starved of glutamine—a non-essential amino acid that is almost universally required as a cell culture supplement (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ). We can thus conclude that cell cooperation is required to survive under glutamine-deprived environments.
 Cells normally uptake free amino acids via specific transporters in a cell-autonomous and thus competitive manner. If this was the sole mechanism by which cells obtain glutamine, limiting levels of this amino acid would benefit sparse populations—the opposite of what we observed. Starved tumour cells can also internalize entire proteins via macropinocytosis, and then obtain free amino acids through lysosomal digestion , . However, this process is also competitive and thus cannot explain our observations. We reasoned that the constant degradation of the extracellular matrix and other exacerbated proteolytic activities in tumours may generate significant quantities of oligopeptides as a by-product. Regardless of their origins, analyses of metabolomic data from skin, breast, lung, pancreatic and renal human cancers show that tumours are abundant in oligopeptides – (Extended Data Fig. ). For example, a study in patients with non-small cell lung cancer found that all but 6 out of the 361 dipeptides that the authors resolved and detected were increased in tumour samples (Fig. ). From the 27 glutamine-containing dipeptides that they detected, 26 were increased in tumours (Fig. ). By contrast, glutamine and other free amino acids are scarce in tumours . Despite the abundance of extracellular oligopeptides, it is unclear whether cancer cells can use them to compensate for amino acid deprivation.
We then explored whether tumour cells use extracellular oligopeptides cooperatively as a source of amino acids. We supplemented a panel of skin, breast and lung cancer cell line cultures with free glutamine or glutamine-containing dipeptides and compared cell growth to cultures with no glutamine at all. As expected, cells cultured without glutamine sources died rapidly, whereas glutamine-containing cultures grew well at a wide range of cell densities (Fig. , Extended Data Fig. and Supplementary Video  ). In marked contrast, sparse cell populations were unable to grow when supplemented with dipeptides and rapidly collapsed, whereas dense cell populations grew perfectly well under these same conditions (Fig. , Extended Data Fig. and Supplementary Video  ).
Among the dipeptides we tested, Ala-Gln was the preferred substrate for tumour cells (Extended Data Fig. ). Of note, Ala-Gln is commonly used as a glutamine alternative in cell culture, and it is one of the most abundant glutamine-containing dipeptides in lung tumour samples (Fig. ). We therefore used this dipeptide as a model system in most of our experiments, but we obtained the same qualitative results with other oligopeptides. For example, all X-Gln dipeptides provided a growth advantage to dense cultures but not to sparse populations (Extended Data Fig. ). We also found a similar effect when we supplemented glutamine-starved cultures with tryptone and casitone—complex mixtures of oligopeptides that lack free glutamine (Extended Data Fig. )—or in cultures supplemented with defined tripeptides or tetrapeptides (Extended Data Fig. ). All of the cell types that we studied were able to use oligopeptides to grow exclusively at high cell densities, suggesting that this may be a conserved cell-feeding mechanism (Extended Data Fig. ). We thus conclude that tumour cells cooperate to scavenge amino acids from extracellular oligopeptides.

## Cells digest peptides extracellularly
We then sought to understand how cells catabolize oligopeptides and at which step this process becomes cooperative. Inhibition of the specialized dipeptide and tripeptide transporters PEPT1 and PEPT2 did not affect cell growth in medium with Ala-Gln (Extended Data Fig. ). Similarly, inhibition of vesicle transport, macropinocytosis, lysosomal proteolysis or autophagy did not reduce the ability of dense populations to consume oligopeptides (Extended Data Fig. ). As inhibiting these major transport and intake mechanisms did not affect oligopeptide utilization, we speculated that tumour cells might instead cleave peptides extracellularly and import the resulting free amino acids as they accumulate outside the cells. To test this idea, we treated cells with low doses of the glutamine transporter inhibitors l-γ-glutamyl-p-nitroanilide (GPNA) or O-benzyl-l-serine (BenSer) so that they prevent cell growth under low glutamine levels but not in high glutamine levels (Extended Data Fig. ). Under these conditions, both inhibitors prevented the growth of cells in media supplemented with Ala-Gln, suggesting that glutamine transporters are needed in the scavenging of oligopeptides (Extended Data Fig. ). We thus thought of the following mechanism of cell cooperation: first, cancer cells release enzymes to the extracellular space, where they hydrolyse oligopeptides into free amino acids. Then, as the extracellular concentration of these amino acids increases, they become a ‘public good’ that all cells can exploit (Fig. ). In the final step—key in making this process dependent on density—cells uptake the free extracellular amino acids. Critically, the rates of transporter-mediated uptake of amino acids follow Michaelis–Menten kinetics (Extended Data Fig. ). This relationship means that cleavage of dipeptides still occurs at low cell densities, but the resulting free amino acids do not reach concentrations that are efficiently transported into cells, leading to the Allee effect. We combined these processes in a mechanistic mathematical model. Numerical simulations using experimentally validated parameters accurately captured the dynamics of populations seeded at various initial cell densities including the spontaneous emergence of the Allee effect (Fig. , and Supplementary Table ). Notably, this model does not explicitly implement the Allee effect; instead, it shows that the Allee threshold is an emergent property of the described biochemical processes.
 If our model is correct, conditioned media of dense populations should contain free amino acids and should rescue the growth of sparse cell populations. Indeed, we completely rescued the growth of sparse populations cultured without Gln by supplementation with conditioned media from dense populations cultured in Ala-Gln (Fig. , Extended Data Fig. and Supplementary Video  ). This rescue was not cell-type-specific, as, for example, sparse cultures of human A375 cells were rescued by conditioned media obtained from a diverse range of human or mouse cells (Extended Data Fig. ). Furthermore, conditioned media could still support the growth of sparse populations even after using a high-molecular-mass cutoff filter (3 kDa; Extended Data Fig. ).
We detected high levels of extracellular glutamine in dense cultures supplemented with different glutamine-containing oligopeptides (Fig. ). Notably, we did not detect free glutamine when measuring fresh media or in conditioned media from cultures supplemented with non-glutamine-containing peptides (Fig. ). These observations rule out the idea that free glutamine may arise from spontaneous peptide hydrolysis or be released from dying cells. In further support of the notion that glutamine obtained from dipeptides is the currency in this form of cell cooperation, cultures supplemented with different dipeptides accumulated varying amounts of free glutamine, and these levels were proportional to the growth that they stimulated (Extended Data Fig. ).
If amino acids accumulate extracellularly, the cleavage rate of peptides should be higher than the consumption rate of amino acids. To test this relationship, we used gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC/MS) to distinguish added 13C-labelled free glutamine from unlabelled glutamine produced by Ala-Gln cleavage. Using this approach, we determined that the cleavage rate of Ala-Gln was roughly twice as fast as the maximal consumption rate of glutamine, thus leading to the buildup of free amino acids (Fig. ). Together, our data demonstrate that cancer cells cooperate to mine amino acids from extracellular oligopeptides and provide a rationale for the long-standing observation of cell-density dependence in mammalian cell growth – .

## CNDP2 is required for peptide catabolism
To identify the enzymes responsible for the hydrolysis of extracellular oligopeptides, we first treated A375 cells with a panel of inhibitors targeting major protease families (Fig. ). We found that 2S,3R-(3-amino-2-hydroxy-4-phenylbutanoyl)-l-leucine (bestatin, also known as ubenimex) was the only compound that inhibited growth on oligopeptides while having no effects on cells growing on free amino acids. Bestatin is a competitive aminopeptidase inhibitor with low clinical toxicity. It is approved for use as a chemotherapy adjuvant in Japan, but it exhibits low antitumoural activity on its own .
 Bestatin prevented oligopeptide utilization in every cell type that we tested, including a large panel of skin, lung, breast, colon and pancreatic tumour cell lines (Extended Data Fig. ), non-transformed cell lines (Extended Data Fig. ) and primary bone-marrow-derived macrophages (Extended Data Fig. ). In all cases, we were able to rescue the effects of this inhibitor by adding free glutamine, thereby ruling out non-specific or toxic effects of bestatin (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ). As expected, there was no accumulation of glutamine in medium conditioned by cells treated with bestatin, and this medium could not rescue the growth of sparse populations (Extended Data Fig. ). We observed similar effects using 3D tumour organoids from dissociated mouse lung adenocarcinomas (Extended Data Fig. ).
 Bestatin also prevented the utilization of other oligopeptides. For example, leucine- or phenylalanine-containing oligopeptides rescued the growth of cultures starved of those essential amino acids and this rescue was inhibited by bestatin (Extended Data Fig. ). Similarly, tumour cells with Keap1 mutations—which rely on exogenous sources of glycine and serine , —were able to grow in media supplemented with oligopeptides containing these amino acids in an aminopeptidase-dependent manner (Extended Data Fig. ). Keap1-mutant cells are also more sensitive to reduced levels of other amino acids, including glutamine . Consistently, our experiments show that Keap1-mutant cells have a higher Allee threshold than isogenic cells with wild-type Keap1, indicating that loss of KEAP1 makes cells more reliant on cooperation (Extended Data Fig. ). As we show later, we took advantage of this feature to treat tumours in vivo.
Aminopeptidases form a group of proteins with more than 50 members in humans (Extended Data Fig. ). Our model suggests that the cooperative proteolysis occurs in the extracellular space. To specifically target extracellular aminopeptidases, we sought to reduce the cell permeability of bestatin by increasing its molecular mass and reducing its hydrophobicity. To achieve this effect, we synthetized a series of bestatin analogues with polyethylene glycol (PEG) side chains (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ). All of these analogues were as effective as regular bestatin at inhibiting growth on Ala-Gln and showed no effect on cells growing on Gln (Fig. ). These data support the notion that the cooperative aminopeptidase activity occurs in the extracellular space.
 We reasoned that if secreted, key cooperative peptidase(s) must be detectable in conditioned media. Using medium fractionation experiments, we confirmed the presence of bestatin-sensitive proteases in conditioned medium (Extended Data Fig. ). Liquid chromatography and mass spectrometry-based analysis of this conditioned medium detected 16 different aminopeptidases (Fig. ). We then conducted a targeted genetic screen, producing knockout cell lines for each of these proteases (Extended Data Fig. ). Using this screen, we found that a single aminopeptidase, CNDP2, was required for cell growth on oligopeptides (Extended Data Fig. ). The key role of CNDP2 was conserved, as a range of tumour cell lines were unable to grow on medium supplemented with Ala-Gln in both 2D (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ) and 3D (Extended Data Fig. ) cultures after CNDP2 loss of function. Of note, the re-expression of CNDP2 in Cndp2-knockout (KO) cells fully restored their ability to grow on Ala-Gln (Fig. ), and populations of cells overexpressing CNDP2 were more resilient to amino acid starvation and showed significantly lower Allee thresholds in culture (Extended Data Fig. ).
CNDP2 is a ubiquitously expressed and non-specific aminopeptidase . It is known as a cytosolic protein and lacks a canonical signal peptide. Despite its measurable intracellular localization, CNDP2 is also detected in human cerebrospinal fluid , plasma and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) of patients with lung cancer . Consistent with these patient data, we detected significant levels of CNDP2 in mouse plasma and BALF samples (Extended Data Fig. ).
We took additional steps to confirm that the cooperative aminopeptidase activity is catalysed by extracellular CNDP2. First, we detected CNDP2 in conditioned media from wild-type but not from Cndp2-KO cells (Fig. ). Notably, we did not detect the presence of other intracellular proteins such as voltage-dependent anion channel (VDAC) or actin, ruling out an unspecific leakage of intracellular proteins from non-viable cells. Second, if Ala-Gln and other dipeptides are catabolized intracellularly, they should accumulate inside Cndp2-KO cells. However, we were unable to detect intracellular levels of Ala-Gln in these cells (Extended Data Fig. ). Third, using recombinant CNDP2 protein we determined that the levels of endogenous CNDP2 secreted by wild-type tumour cells were sufficient to rescue the growth of Cndp2-KO cells (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ). Fourth, the addition of recombinant CNDP2 to culture media fully restored the growth of Cndp2-KO cells, and this rescue was inhibited by bestatin (Fig. ). Finally, conditioned medium obtained from Cndp2-KO cells did not rescue the growth of low-density populations of wild-type cells (Fig. , Extended Data Fig. and Supplementary Video  ). It is important to emphasize that this extracellular role of CNDP2 is not incompatible with known functions of intracellular CNDP2. However, the cooperative process that we describe here is independent from these other CNDP2 functions (Extended Data Fig. ). Altogether, we concluded that the extracellular pool of CNDP2 is necessary and sufficient for the cooperative digestion of extracellular oligopeptides.

## CNDP2 loss reduces tumour growth in vivo
We hypothesized that CNDP2-mediated cooperation may be particularly important in individuals with loss-of-function KEAP1 mutations. KEAP1 loss is the third most common alteration in patients with lung cancer . In the absence of KEAP1, cells increase glutathione synthesis and amino acid consumption . Although these changes protect tumours from reactive oxygen species , they also sensitize them to therapies that target amino acid metabolism and thus they may also be more responsive to the loss of CNDP2. Consistent with this prediction, our in vitro data showed that Keap1-KO cells relied more on cell cooperation than isogenic Keap1 wild-type cells (Extended Data Fig. ). CNDP2 is widely expressed in tumours, although it is rarely mutated (around 1% of all cancers ; Extended Data Fig. ). Analyses of data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) suggested that increased CNDP2 mRNA in patients with lung cancer correlated with worse survival (Extended Data Fig. ) and that CNDP2 gain of function significantly co-occurred with KEAP1 loss of function (Extended Data Fig. ). In the same study, we found that patients with alterations in both genes had the worst survival probability (Extended Data Fig. ), suggesting that there may be a clinical link between KEAP1 and CNDP2 alterations.
 To test whether CNDP2 inhibition in vivo reduces the growth of Keap1-mutant tumours, we injected mice with tumour cells derived from a genetically engineered mouse model of lung adenocarcinoma bearing Keap1 mutations (KrasG12D/+; Trp53−/−; Keap1−/−, also known as KPK lung) . After injection, we treated mice with bestatin or PBS for ten days and monitored tumour growth for another two to three weeks. Following previous studies using this KPK lung model , we placed mice on a glycine- and serine-deficient diet (−Gly/Ser) that reduces circulating levels of these and other amino acids (Extended Data Fig. ). In our experiments, bestatin had a significant antitumoural effect in vivo (Extended Data Fig. ) with no evident side effects (Extended Data Fig. ). The genetic loss of CNDP2 produced even more marked effects than bestatin treatment. A large portion of mice injected with Cndp2-KO cells did not develop palpable tumours for the duration of the experiment (Fig. ). The absence of tumours was specific to the loss of CNDP2, as its re-expression reproduced the growth dynamics of Cndp2-wild-type tumours (Fig. ). We thus concluded that the inhibition of aminopeptidases with bestatin or the absence of CNDP2 in mice fed with an amino acid-limiting diet reduces tumour growth.
 We estimated that the levels of glutamine-containing extracellular oligopeptides in BALF were of the same order of magnitude as those of free glutamine (Extended Data Fig. ). Thus, these oligopeptides can be a meaningful source of these nutrients. To specifically target the extracellular pool of CNDP2 in vivo, we performed a series of genetic and pharmacological experiments. First, we cloned the Cndp2 gene with or without the addition of an N-terminal nuclear localization signal (NLSSV40) or a secretion signal peptide (SSPIL-2) into lentiviral vectors (Extended Data Fig. ). After confirming that the chimeric CNDP2 proteins showed the expected subcellular localization (Fig. ), we tested whether they could rescue tumour growth of Cndp2-KO cells in vivo. Using the same tumour models described above, cells expressing the wild-type form of CNDP2 rapidly formed tumours resembling those formed by Cndp2-wild-type cells (Fig. ). We observed a similar rescue in cells expressing the secreted form of CNDP2 (SSPIL-2–CNDP2; Fig. ). By contrast, cells expressing the intracellularly enriched form of CNDP2 grew poorly in vivo (NLSSV40–CNDP2; Fig. ). As an orthogonal approach, we selected one of the polyethylene glycol-conjugated (PEGylated) bestatin analogues that we previously tested in vitro (Fig. ) and scaled up its production to treat mice in vivo. As all analogues had a similar potency, we chose the PEG12 linear modification owing to its relative ease of synthesis and the substantial increase in molecular mass. As shown in Fig. , this ‘PEGstatin’ analogue showed similar tumour-inhibitory effects as regular bestatin. Overall, we conclude that the cooperative catabolism of peptides is mediated by the extracellular pool of CNDP2 in vitro and in vivo.
Seeking to complement these experiments with a better preclinical tumour model, we tested the effects of bestatin on orthotopic lung tumours. In this model, we introduced the most common KEAP1 mutation observed in the clinic , in which an amino acid substitution (R470C) generates a dominant-negative form of KEAP1. As in our subcutaneous tumour experiments, we placed fully immunocompetent syngenetic mice on a −Gly/Ser diet and treated them with PBS or bestatin for 10 days. As shown in Fig. , the tumour burden in bestatin-treated mice was significantly reduced, suggesting that the inhibition of this cooperative mechanism may be a viable therapeutic approach—particularly for patients bearing tumours with KEAP1 mutations.

## Cooperative tumours are fitter in vivo
In nature, cooperation co-exists with competition. In the early example of penguins, the penguins may cooperate in the formation of huddles, but they still compete for food and mates. The interplay between cooperation and competition in tumour evolution is poorly understood, but the mechanism that we describe here presents an ideal system to study this problem. In our experiments, Cndp2-KO cells cannot cooperate but they take advantage of amino acids mined by cooperating Cndp2-wild-type cells (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ). In co-cultures supplemented with Ala-Gln, non-cooperators (Cndp2-KO cells) greatly increased their growth rates, whereas cooperators (Cndp2-wild-type cells) exhibited slower growth than when cultured on their own (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ). Studies of cooperative microorganism populations show that cheaters—cells that reap the benefits of cooperation without paying for its costs—often emerge and outcompete cooperative individuals – . By contrast, when we conducted long-term competition experiments between Cndp2-KO and wild-type cells, cooperators were much fitter and drove non-cooperators to extinction (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ). In control experiments, the fitness gain of cooperators disappeared in co-cultures supplemented with free Gln, or in co-cultures of two cooperator clones expressing different fluorophores (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ).
 The resilience of cooperators against non-cooperators in our experiment presented an apparent paradox , . A possible solution to this problem is if the benefits of cooperation only affect neighbouring cells , . When a cell divides in a solid tumour, the resulting daughter cells are likely to remain close to each other. If the parental cell is a cooperator, its progeny will form a beneficial neighbourhood of cooperators, whereas dividing non-cooperators would form a neighbourhood of non-cooperators, which brings no benefits. Consistent with this idea, we observed that non-cooperators positioned near cooperators grew up to ten times faster than those located further away from cooperators (Extended Data Fig. ). Similar to observations and predictions in other biological scenarios , , cooperating tumour cells formed distinct clonal patches. By contrast, non-cooperators did not form spatial patterns and grew intermingled between patches of cooperators (Extended Data Fig. ).
These data suggested that the relative fitness of wild-type and Cndp2-KO cells varied according to the ‘tumour geography’—the combination of local nutrient levels and the properties of neighbouring cells across different tumour regions. To test this idea, we grew cooperators and non-cooperators in a metabolic microenvironment chamber (MEMIC)—a microphysiological device that recapitulates the spatial gradients of metabolites found in the tumour microenvironment – (Fig. ). After a week of co-culture, the distributions of these clones were markedly different: cooperators and non-cooperators co-existed and formed clonal patches in well-nurtured regions (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ). However, nutrient-deprived regions of the MEMIC were dominated by cooperators, whereas non-cooperators almost disappeared (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ).
Finally, we tested whether these evolutionary dynamics and geographic effects remain true in vivo. We compared the growth of syngeneic tumours from Cndp2-wild-type and Cndp2-KO KP cells with tumours formed by a 1:1 mix of these cells. Although these mixed tumours initially grew slower than pure wild-type tumours, after around 20 days their volumes and growth rates were indistinguishable (Extended Data Fig. ). We speculated that mixed tumours initially grew slowly owing to the presence of non-cooperators, but over time cooperators took over the entire tumour population, resulting in a final tumour formed almost exclusively by Cndp2-wild-type cells. To test this idea, we studied the evolution of tumours formed by a 1:1 mix of cooperators (Cndp2-wild-type cells expressing mCherry) mixed in 1:1 with other cooperators (Cndp2-wild-type cells expressing YFP) or with non-cooperators (Cndp2-KO cells expressing YFP). Using flow cytometry and image analysis of histological samples, we observed that the proportion of non-cooperators decreased exponentially, with a half-life of approximately three days (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ). Consistent with our MEMIC experiments, the location and relative abundance of these different clones was determined by the local tumour geography. For example, the few non-cooperator cells present in late tumours were located almost exclusively in perivascular regions. By contrast, cooperators were present everywhere, including in regions with lower vascular density (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ).
We conclude that the cooperative catabolism of extracellular peptides is an evolutionary advantage for malignant clones in the tumour microenvironment. In our mouse models, inhibition of this process can markedly reduce tumour growth without major effects on overall mouse health. We thus propose that CNDP2-mediated cooperation can be an effective and specific target for cancer treatment.

## Discussion
Cell cooperation in cancer has been largely overlooked or assumed to require growth factor signalling , . Here we have described a growth factor-independent form of cell cooperation in which a secreted aminopeptidase, CNDP2, enables cells to tolerate amino acid starvation by cleaving extracellular peptides. Beyond its importance to cancer, to our knowledge, this is a previously unrecognized mechanism used by mammalian cells to scavenge nutrients. In contrast to transporter-mediated amino acid uptake and the macropinocytosis of whole proteins, the collective utilization of extracellular oligopeptides is a non-cell-autonomous and cooperative process. Although this form of cooperation is not exclusive to malignant cells, our data suggest that it is redundant for well-perfused healthy tissues that obtain sufficient nutrients from the blood. By contrast, the high demand for nutrients in cancer cells and the nutrient scarcity in the tumour microenvironment make this cooperative process is much more important for tumour cells, and enabled us to inhibit CNDP2 in mouse models and successfully reduce tumour growth without causing noticeable health effects.
Although other secreted aminopeptidases could theoretically have a similar role to CNDP2, we found CNDP2 to be particularly important in this process. Additional work will be needed to better understand its role in this form of cell cooperation. For example, CNDP2 lacks a conventional secretion signal peptide, and although many extracellular and plasma proteins are secreted via non-conventional mechanisms , we do not know the exact mechanism of CNDP2 secretion. Our data also show that the cooperative effect of CNDP2 secretion only benefits neighbouring cells, but we do not know how the activity or levels of CNDP2 are spatially restricted. Direct visualization of single CNDP2 molecules may help us to understand how this protein is distributed in the extracellular space and better understand the spatial distribution of its effects. Finally, it will be important to determine the specific cell death mechanisms triggered by CNDP2 inhibition. One possibility is that in the absence of CNDP2, cells do not have the required amino acids to synthesize sufficient amounts of glutathione, leading to the accumulation of toxic levels of reactive oxygen species. The known role of CNDP2 in glutathione metabolism and the increased sensitivity of Keap1-mutant tumours to its inhibition are consistent with this idea.
The cooperative feeding behaviour that we describe here is remarkably similar to the breakdown of sucrose by secreted hydrolases in yeast , . Although these mechanisms have different molecular bases, their conceptual similarities suggest that cooperation by extracellular digestion of nutrients may be a conserved evolutionary adaptation to challenging environments. Similar symbiotic interactions may lead to the co-existence of different tumour clones with distinctive metabolic specializations. As an example, a recent study has shown complementary metabolic features in different pancreatic adenocarcinoma cells . More broadly, our data highlight that ecological concepts such as the Allee effect can help us to better understand—and potentially control—the complex cell populations that form tumours.

## Methods

## Cell culture
A375, B16-F10, MDA-MB-231, MCF7, PyMT99, H1792, H1693, H1299, DLD1, HCT116 and MIA PaCa-2 cells were purchased from ATCC. Mouse KP and KPK cells were developed by T. Papagiannakopoulos and KPC cells were a gift from D. Bar-Sagi. All cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco 11965118) with 10% FBS unless otherwise noted, and grown in 37 °C, 5% CO2 humidified incubators. H1792, H1693 and H1299 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 (Gibco 11875119) supplemented with 10% FBS. Cells were authenticated by the ATCC FTA Sample Collection Kit for Human and Mouse Cell Authentication Service and confirmed as mycoplasma-negative. Cultures were supplemented with GlutaMAX (Gibco 35050061), l-glutamine (Gibco 25030081). Amino acids l-leucine, l-serine, glycine, l-methionine, l-lysine and l-arginine were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. We also supplemented cultures with Casitone (Gibco 225910) and Tryptone (Gibco 211705), both of which lack free glutamine . Ala-Leu, Ala-Gly, Ala-Ser and all glutamine-containing dipeptides were purchased from GenScript or AnaSpec.

## Lysine[Z(NO2)]-valine (LNV) synthesis
The Fmoc-protected dipeptide was formed by first activating Fmoc-Lys(pNZ)-OH with EDC (3-(ethyliminomethyleneamino)-N,N-dimethylpropan-1-amine) to form the pentafluorophenolate ester. Addition of a solution of valine and sodium carbonate in water provided the dipeptide, which was purified on silica with dichloromethane and methanol. Finally, Fmoc was removed using piperidine in acetonitrile, then precipitated in cold ether. The solid was dissolved in 3:1 acetonitrile:water and lyophilized, giving a fluffy white powder. UV, mass spectrometry and 1H-NMR spectra are consistent with reported data.

## High-throughput live microscopy
Cells were engineered to express nuclear localized fluorescent protein (H2B–YFP, Addgene #26000). Cells were seeded into 96-well culture plates at different densities and allowed to adhere overnight in glucose/glutamine-free DMEM (Gibco A1443001), 10% dialysed FBS (GeminiBio 100-108), 2 mM glutamine, 12.5 mM glucose (Sigma-Aldrich D9434) and antibiotic-antimycotic (Gibco 15240096). After 24 h, the plates were washed with PBS (Gibco 14040182) and medium was replaced with glucose/glutamine free DMEM, 1% dialysed FBS, 12.5 mM glucose, antibiotic-antimycotic and propidium iodide (MP Biomedicals 0219545810) to detect non-viable cells. These media were supplemented with 2 mM glutamine, 2 mM oligopeptides or neither. Population growth was tracked with an automated robotic microscope and incubator for up to 96 h (Cytation5/BioSpa8, BioTek). We used the same approach for Leu and Gly/Ser experiments but with DMEM containing Gln and missing these other amino acids.

## Conditioned media experiments
Two million cells were plated in 10 cm dishes in glucose/glutamine-free DMEM, 10% dialysed FBS, 25 mM glucose, and 2 mM glutamine to adhere. The next day, the medium was removed, the plates were washed with PBS, and the medium replaced with glucose/Gln-free DMEM, 2 mM Ala-Gln, and 12.5 mM glucose and cells were grown for 24–72 h. After confirming that cell viability was above 90% (measured by Trypan Blue, Thermo Scientific), conditioned media were collected and filtered using 0.2-μm filters. When needed, conditioned media were fractioned by Amicon Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filter Unit with 3 kDa cutoff (Millipore UFC900308) and spun at 4,000g for 40 min. To clean high molecular mass fractions, the filter was rinsed with PBS and centrifuged at 4,000g, 40 min twice. Protein fractions were analysed by LC/MS by the proteomics core at NYU Langone School of Medicine.
For cell-free enzymatic activity, protein fractions were collected as described above in duplicates. Half of the volume for each sample, were heat-inactivated at 100 °C for 1 h. Then, all samples were transferred to a new Eppendorf tube supplemented with glucose/glutamine free DMEM, 12.5 mM glucose, 2 mM Ala-Gln or 2 mM Ala-Gln and 60 μM bestatin without cells and incubated overnight at 37 °C as well as 4 °C for control. After incubation, 2,000 cells per well in 96-well plate were treated in 100 μl cell-free enzymatic media mixed with 100 μl glucose/glutamine free DMEM, 2% dialysed FBS, 12.5 mM glucose, propidium iodide and grown for 3 days. The growth of cell populations was tracked by high-throughput live microscopy and image analysis were used to count live (YFP-labelled) and dead cells (propidium iodide-stained). For glutamine measurements, experiments were conducted in triplicate and were analysed by YSI 2950D-3 Biochemistry Analyzer.

## Image analysis
Most of our in vitro experiments required tracking the growth of multiple cell populations using automated time-lapse imaging. This process generates many images—typically more than 20,000 per experiment—which required an efficient image analysis pipeline. To this end, we overhauled our image analysis algorithms , , and optimized to run on a graphics processing unit. In brief, our code uses natural intelligence and human learning to find, locate and count cells via the IDL Particle Tracking algorithm . This algorithm uses a bandpass filter to remove the background noise and then employs a peak detection routine to rapidly locate cells without the need of image segmentation. To calculate the areas between cells, we used the Delaunay triangulation to find the closest neighbours of each cell. Then, the area of these triangles was calculated using the ‘area of polygon’ (polyarea) function in Matlab. Local cell densities are approximated as the inverse of the local area. To quantify levels of fluorescence, we first binarized grayscale nuclei images (typically stained with Hoechst) to create nuclear masks of individual cells. Masks of adjacent cells were separated by a watershed transformation using the peaks from the IDL algorithm as ‘catchment basins’. We then gently expanded these areas so they would also cover cytosolic areas of cells. Finally, we used these masks to obtain per cell properties such as location, area and pixel values from each fluorescent channel. All these analyses routines were written in Matlab. The scripts for our adaptation of the IDL Particle Tracking algorithm, including a graphical user interface for users unfamiliar with Matlab, are available at 10.5281/zenodo.14297705 (ref. ).

## Metabolic tracing
Extracts dried in an evaporator (Genevac EZ-2 Elite) were resuspended by incubating with shaking at 30 °C for 2 h in 50 μl of 40 mg ml−1 methoxyamine hydrochloride in pyridine. Metabolites were further derivatized with 80 μl of N-methyl-N-(trimethylsilyl) trifluoroacetamide + 1% TCMS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 70 μl ethyl acetate (Sigma-Aldrich) and then incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Samples were analysed using an Agilent 7890 A Gas Chromatograph coupled to an Agilent 5977C mass selective detector. The gas chromatograph operated in splitless injection mode with constant helium gas flow at 1 ml min−1; 1 μl of derivatized metabolites was injected onto an HP-5ms column and the gas chromatograph oven temperature ramped from 60 to 290 °C over 25 min. Peaks representing compounds of interest were extracted and integrated using the MassHunter software v.B.08 (Agilent Technologies) and then normalized to both the internal standard (d-2-hydroxyglutaric-2,3,3,4,4-d5 acid (d5-2HG)) peak area and protein content of triplicate samples as determined by bicinchoninic acid assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The following ions were used for quantifying glutamine levels and 13C isotope distributions: glutamine, m/z 362–372; d5-2HG, m/z 354. Correction for natural isotope abundance was performed using IsoCor software v.1.0. All peaks were manually inspected and verified relative to known spectra for each metabolite. The concentration of glutamine in cell lysates, conditioned media and BALF was determined using a colorimetric Glutamine Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich MAK438) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Synthesis and validation of PEGylated forms of bestatin
We decided to add PEG chains to the 5-carboxylate group of bestatin, which has been previously modified without interfering with its inhibitory activity . In brief, diimide-activated Boc-leucine was coupled to PEG12-amine, followed by Boc removal with trifluoroacetic acid afforded the trifluoroacetic acid salt of the PEGylated leucine. This was coupled with a benzyloxycarbonyl (Cbz)-protected (2S,3R)-3-amino-2-hydroxy-4-phenylbutyric acid under non-racemizing conditions, using EDC and HOOBt (3-hydroxy-1,2,3-benzotriazin-4-one). After crude purification on silica, this mixture was acetylated with acetic anhydride in pyridine, which, after careful chromatography, provided the fully protected PEGylated bestatin. Hydrogenation followed by methanolysis to remove the Cbz and acetate, respectively, provided PEG-bestatin, which was purified on a low pressure C8 column, 30–70% MeCN/0.1% HCl (aq). To test these drugs, 20,000 lung KPK cells per well were seeded in 96-well plates and grown in glucose/glutamine free DMEM containing 12.5 mM glucose, propidium iodide (MP Biomedicals 0219545810) and 2 mM of either Gln or Ala-Gln. The 6 variants of PEGylated bestatin were added at different concentrations and cell growth was tracked over time as described before.

## Animal work and tumour models
Female C57BL/6J mice (JAX 000664) around 11 weeks old were placed onto control (Envigo TD.110839) or serine/glycine-deficient diet (−Ser/Gly diet, Envigo TD.160752). After 4 days, mice were inoculated with 5 × 104 mouse lung wild-type or Cndp2-KO KP (KrasG12D;
Trp53−/−) or KP Keap1-KO (KrasG12D;
Trp53−/−;
Keap1-KO) tumour cells in 100 μl of PBS (Gibco 14040182) intradermally, in the back and maintained in control or −Ser/Gly diet diet monitored daily. Next day, mice were started to be injected intraperitoneally with 250 mg kg−1 of bestatin-HCl (Cayman 70520), an equimolar PEGstatin solution (bestatin-PEG12, see above) or PBS every day for 10 days. Once tumours became palpable, measurements were performed with a calliper every 3 days and tumour volumes were calculated using the formula volume = (length × (width)2)/2. We considered mice free of tumours when tumours were undetectable or remained as small cysts (<20 mm3). Source data can be found in Supplementary Tables – . Mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation followed by cervical dislocation before tumours reached maximal permissible size (1,500 mm3 per tumour as defined by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC)) or ulceration. Researchers were not blinded to the experimental groups during in vivo treatments and measurements. All mouse experiments were performed according to National Institutes of Health’s Guide and were approved by the New York University Animal Welfare Committee.
For in vivo competition experiments, mice were placed in the −Ser/Gly diet and after 4 days, they were inoculated with 1:1 mix of 2 × 104 mouse lung wild-type or Cndp2-KO KP tumour cells in 100 μl of PBS (Gibco 14040182) intradermally and maintained in the −Ser/Gly diet. Tumours were collected at different time points (before reaching the maximum size defined by IACUC) and frozen for cryosections or dissociated for flow cytometry. Dissociated tumours were fixed with Cyto-Fast Fix/Perm Solution (Biolegend 750000133) for 20 min, centrifuged, and the pellet was washed with PBS. Then per cell mCherry and YFP values were determined via flow cytometry (Cytek Aurora) and the data were analysed in FlowJo.

## Orthotopic lung tumours
Cells derived from KrasG12D; Trp53−/− lung adenocarcinoma tumours, were engineered to express a dominant-negative Keap1 mutant (Keap1R470C) . After continuous selection with 8 µg ml−1 puromycin for 5 days, the cells were incubated without puromycin selection media. Following an overnight incubation, cells were trypsinized and filtered twice through a 70-µm strainer and then resuspended in sterile PBS to a concentration of 500,000 cells per ml. C57BL/6J mice were then injected with 200 µl of this cell suspension via the tail vein using a 27G needle. Tumours were then embedded, sectioned, and processed for haematoxylin and eosin staining. Entire tumour sections were then imaged using an Aperio AT2 slide scanner (Leica). Tumour burden was calculated as the percentage of the lung area taken by tumours using the Q-Path software. For each mouse, we average the tumour burden from five sections.

## Tumour collection and sectioning
Tumours were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Thermo Scientific) in PBS at 4 °C, overnight. The next day, tumours were transferred into 30% sucrose in PBS solution and incubated at 4 °C until tumours were sunk onto the bottom of the 1.5 ml centrifuge tubes. Tumours were embedded into a cryostat embedding medium (Tissue-Tek O.C.T. Compound) at −20 °C. Sections were taken as 15–20 μm thickness onto polysine-coated slides (Thermo Scientific J2800AMNZ) using a cryostat (CM8150, Leica). A barrier around the tissue was drawn with a liquid blocker pen (ImmunoPen; Calbiochem) to prevent solution leakage. Permeabilization was performed by incubating the sections in 0.1% Tween. Then, the sections were blocked for 30 min in 2.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). After blocking, the sections were incubated overnight with a primary antibody against CD31 to detect blood vessels (1:100, BD 550274). After washing the primary antibody with PBS, the sections were incubated with a secondary antibody (AlexaFluor 647; Thermo Fisher) diluted 1:1,000 in blocking solution for 1 h. For nuclear counterstaining, Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher, H1399, 1 μg ml−1) was added to the same secondary solution. After PBS washes, the sections were mounted using Fluoromount-G (Thermo Fisher) and stored in the dark and imaged within 1 or 2 days. Keyence BZ-X810 microscope was programmed to image consecutive image fields. These fields were stitched together using the BZ-X800 Analyzer software. Final images were exported as raw 16-bit tiffs and analysed in Matlab.

## Tumour organoid cultures
Tumours were excised from mice, immediately placed on DMEM with 10% FBS media in a 6-well plate, poked with a 25G needle syringe loaded with a protease mixture of 4 mg ml−1 collagenase IV, 2 U ml−1 dispase, and 2 U ml−1 DNase I (3 ml solution per tumour), and incubated 5 min at room temperature. Tumours were then cut into smaller chunks and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After incubation, two frosted glass slides were used to disrupt the tumours mechanically. Once all the tumour pieces were fully disrupted, cell suspension was filtered through 70-μm filters into a 50-ml Falcon tube and vortexed at high speed for 2–3 min. Then, the samples were centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 10 min. The pellets were resuspended and seeded into 10 cm plates with DMEM, 10% FBS with penicillin–streptomycin. After 48 h, the cells were trypsinized and re-suspended in ice-cold glucose/glutamine free DMEM, 1% FBS, 12.5 mM glucose and 5% growth factor- reduced Matrigel (Corning 356230). Two thousand cells in 100 μl were plated per well in a 96-well plate already coated and solidified with 35 μl per well growth factor-reduced Matrigel. The plate was incubated 1 h in 37 °C incubator and then 100 μl glucose/glutamine free DMEM, 1% dialysed FBS, 12.5 mM glucose, 5% growth factor reduced Matrigel with 2 mM glutamine, 2 mM Ala-Gln, or no glutamine and 60 μM bestatin were added. Medium was changed to fresh every two days and the cells were grown for ten days. Imaging was carried out in All-in-One Fluorescence Microscope (BZ-X810 Keyence).

## Generation of knockout cell lines by CRISPR–Cas9 gene editing
For Penta-KO cell lines, Anpep, Lnpep, Trhde and Rnpep single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) were first cloned into pX330 (Addgene 42230), lentiCRISPR-v2-neo (Addgene 98292), lentiCRISPR-v2-blasticidin (Addgene 98293), or lentiCRISPR-v2-hygromycin (Addgene 98291), respectively. These plasmids were linearized with BsmBI by T4 ligase (NEB). Then, sgRNAs targeting 12 different aminopeptidases were cloned individually into LentiCRISPR-v2-puro vector (Addgene 98290). The oligonucleotides used to generate sgRNAs were purchased from IDT and sequences are provided in Supplementary Table . For virus production, the sgRNA-expressing vector and lentiviral packaging vectors (VSV-G and Delta-8.9) were transfected into HEK 293T cells using Lipofectamine transfection reagent (Thermo Scientific L3000008). After 16–18 h, the transfection medium was replaced with fresh medium. Lentiviral supernatants were collected and filtered through a 0.45-μm filter 48 h after transfection. The supernatant was then mixed in a 1:1 ratio with fresh medium supplemented with polybrene (MilliporeSigma TR1003G) to a final concentration of 8 µg ml−1. This mixture was added to cells at 40–50% confluence in 6-well plates. After 48 h, transduced cells were transferred to a 10 cm plate followed by selection of desired antibiotics: puromycin (1 µg ml−1, Gibco A1113803) for 3–4 days, G418 (1 mg ml−1, Sigma-Aldrich A1720-1G) for 7 days, blasticidin (5 μg ml−1, InvivoGen ant-bl-05) for 5 days and hygromycin (0.5 mg ml−1, Invivogen ant-hg-1) for 7 days.
All Cndp2-KO cells were generated using the pX330 CRISPR vector. For point mutations, oligonucleotides for the sgRNA and the reverse complement sequences were synthesized and cloned into the pX330 vector. Cells were transfected with sgRNA-expressing vector using Lipofectamine. After 48 h, the cells were seeded as single cells per wells into 96 well plates. Cells were cultured as single clones for 2–3 weeks. Then, genomic DNA was isolated from each clone and the targeted region was amplified and analysed by DNA sequencing. Sequences of the primers used for this reaction are available in Supplementary Table . CNDP2 expression was also validated by immunoblotting. For Cndp2 deletion, sgRNAs targeting the beginning and the end of Cndp2 gene were designed following a previously described protocol and cloned individually into the pX330 vector. Cells were co-transfected with pX330-sgRNA1, pX330-sgRNA2 and a puromycin resistance gene containing vector. Cells were selected with puromycin for 4 days. Puromycin resistant cells were sorted into single cells to generate clones that were then screened for the deletion of Cndp2. The sgRNAs used for whole gene deletion are provided in Supplementary Table .

## Identification of CNDP2
As peptidases may have overlapping and redundant functions, we used CRISPR–Cas9 to generate a tetra-KO line (TKO) for 4 of the 16 secreted aminopeptidases detected in conditioned media. These cells were knockout for Trhde, Anpep, Lnpep and Rnpep. We then knocked out each of the other 16 genes generating 12 penta-KO lines. Finally, we grew each of these lines in media with glutamine or with Ala-Gln and we compared their growth to parentals, TKO and bestatin-treated cells.

## Cloning and testing of CNDP2 variants with signal peptides
To re-express CNDP2, we designed lentiviral vectors containing the mouse Cndp2 gene under the control of the CMV or PGK promoters and resistance to puromycin (lentiviral sequences can be found in Supplementary Table ). For constructs with signal peptides, we introduced a 6×-His tag on the C terminus of CNDP2 and the nuclear localization signal from SV40 or the signal peptide from IL-2 as N-terminus signal peptides. All vectors were synthetized by VectorBuilder. CNDP2 levels and localization were validated by western blotting against CNDP2 or by immunofluorescence using an anti-His tag antibody (1:400, Invitrogen R930-25). To inhibit the secretory pathway, we incubated cells with Brefeldin A (1:1,000, Biolegend 420601) for 5 h.

## Experiments with recombinant CNDP2
Twenty thousand lung KPK cells per well were seeded in 96-well plates and grown in glucose/glutamine-free DMEM containing 12.5 mM glucose, 2 mM Ala-Gln and propidium iodide (MP Biomedicals 0219545810). Recombinant CNDP2 protein (Sino Biological, Cat: 50246-M08B) was added to the media at different concentrations.

## Testing intracellular roles of CNDP2
To test the contribution of other roles of CNDP2 including glutathione processing – , and in the formation of lactate-amino acid pseudopeptides , , we added either oxidized glutathione (GSSG; 2 mM, Sigma-Aldrich G6654), reduced glutathione (2 mM Sigma-Aldrich G6013) or N-lactoylphenylalanine (Lac-Phe; 2 mM Sigma-Aldrich SMB01375) to cultures of Cndp2-KO cells. None of these compounds were able to rescue the growth of Cndp2-KO cells.
To test whether CNDP2 modulated the PI3K–mTOR pathway in our system, we compared the levels of phospho-AKT, AKT, phospho-mTOR and mTOR in wild-type and Cndp2-KO cells by western blot. We did not observe any changes in the levels of these proteins when comparing wild-type and Cndp2-KO cells.

## Western blotting
Protein lysates were collected in ice-cold RIPA buffer (0.05 M Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 0.15 M NaCl, 1% Triton-X 100, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) supplemented with freshly added protease inhibitors and centrifuged for 20 min at 14,000 rpm at 4 °C. For secreted proteins, the conditioned media were collected, and the proteins were concentrated using Amicon Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filter Unit with 3 kDa cutoff (Millipore Sigma UFC900308) by centrifuging for 1 h at 4,000g (4 °C) and obtained 200 μl of total sample. Protein concentrations of both lysates and concentrated conditioned media were measured by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Pierce, Thermo Scientific). Same amounts of protein were loaded on precast mini-PROTEAN TGX (Bio-Rad 4561095) gels followed by transfer to nitrocellulose membrane. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (CNDP2 1:1,000, Millipore Sigma HPA036898; CNDP2 1:500, Proteintech 14925-1-AP; actin 1:10,000, Cell Signaling 8H10D10; VDAC 1:1,000, Cell Signaling 4661; phospho-AKT 1:2,000, Cell Signaling 4060; AKT 1:2,000, Cell Signaling 2920; phospho-mTOR, 1:1,000, Cell Signaling 5536; mTOR 1:1,000, Cell Signaling 2983; α-tubulin 0.5 μg ml−1, Sigma-Aldrich T6199) overnight at 4 °C diluted in TBS-T supplemented with 5% BSA. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-linked secondary antibodies were used (HRP anti-mouse 1:10,000, Cell Signaling 7076; HRP anti-rabbit 1:8,000, Cell Signaling 7074). SuperSignal West Pico Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or 1-Shot Digital ECL Solution (Kindlebio) was used for detection. Protein bands were imaged using a camera image detection system (KwikQuant).

## Quantitative PCR with reverse transcription

## In vitro competition experiments
For in vitro competition experiments, lung KP Cndp2-WT cells were labelled with H2B–mCherry or with H2B–YFP and Cndp2-KO cells were labelled with H2B–YFP. Pairs of these cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a final concentration of 20,000 cells per well for all conditions. The initial ratios of cooperators:cooperators or cooperators:non-cooperators were 1:1, 1:10 and 1:100. Full DMEM containing Gln was used to seed the cells. Cells were grown overnight and then the full medium was replaced with medium containing either Gln or Ala-Gln. For short-term experiments, cells were grown for 4 days and imaged every 4 h. Population growth was tracked with an automated robotic microscope and incubator (BioTek Cytation 5 Imaging Reader/ BioSpa 8 Automated Incubator). For long-term experiments, cells were imaged once a day for 30 days. Whenever confluency was reached, cells were trypsinized and 10% of the cell suspension was re-seeded in full media containing Gln. The next day, the medium was replaced with medium containing either Gln or Ala-Gln. For both short- and long-term experiments, clones were identified and quantified using custom image analysis software.

## Statistics
We conducted at least three biological replicates of all experiments. Unless noted, we used two tailed Student’s t-tests to estimate P values between two conditions. In dot and line plots, error bars show s.d. from the mean. In box plots, centre lines show the median and box edges show 75th and 25th empirical quartiles of the data.
