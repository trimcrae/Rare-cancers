## Introduction
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common malignancies worldwide and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality . This high mortality rate is primarily driven by metastatic disease, particularly the spread of cancer to the liver . Once CRC metastasizes to distant organs, the prognosis worsens significantly, with 5-year survival rates falling below 20% . Notably, liver metastasis (LM) is the most frequent and lethal form of advanced CRC, accounting for the majority of CRC-related deaths , . These findings highlight the urgent need to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying CRC LM to develop more effective therapeutic strategies.
Chromosomal instability (CIN), the persistent occurrence of chromosome segregation errors leading to aneuploidy, is a defining hallmark of CRC and may serve as a crucial link between the tumor microenvironment (TME) and metastatic progression , . The majority of sporadic CRCs follow a CIN-driven evolutionary trajectory, marked by widespread karyotypic abnormalities that accumulate as tumors advance , . CIN fosters intratumoral genetic heterogeneity, enabling the selection of aggressive therapy-resistant clones, and is strongly associated with poor patient outcomes . Importantly, emerging evidence suggests that CIN is not merely a byproduct of tumor evolution but also an active driver of malignancy. Sustained chromosome missegregation has been shown to promote invasion and distant metastasis in experimental models . For example, chromosomally unstable cancer cells can activate inflammatory signaling cascades, such as the cGAS/STING/NF-κB pathway, in response to cytosolic missegregated DNA , , conferring pro-migratory and survival advantages. Clinically, CIN-high or-highly aneuploid CRCs are frequently associated with advanced disease stages and metastatic dissemination . These findings suggest that CIN is a fundamental driver of tumor progression and therapeutic resistance.
While CIN is known to elicit inflammatory responses within tumor cells, a compelling yet underexplored question is whether extrinsic signals from the tumor stroma can actively induce CIN in cancer cells. Chronic inflammation in the TME is a well-established driver of genomic instability, largely through the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and genotoxic stress that promotes DNA damage and mutagenesis in epithelial cells . Cancer-associated fibroblasts, key architects of the tumor stroma, are central to maintaining a proinflammatory and oxidative milieu. CAFs secrete factors, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), nitric oxide (NO), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), all of which can inflict oxidative DNA damage in neighboring cells . These observations suggest that CAF-derived signals may not only promote tumor invasion and survival but also compromise genomic integrity and contribute directly to CIN. However, definitive evidence demonstrating a causal link between CAF-secreted factors and CIN induction in CRC cells is lacking. Furthermore, the mechanistic pathways through which CAF-driven CIN accelerates metastatic progression are poorly understood. Addressing these gaps is essential to unravel the full spectrum of CAF function in CRC pathogenesis.
Given these observations, we investigated whether CAF-mediated signaling contributes to the induction of CIN and metastatic progression in CRC. We hypothesized that CAF-derived factors promote genomic instability in CRC cells, thereby fostering intratumoral heterogeneity and enabling the emergence of more aggressive metastasis-prone sub-clones. Using an integrative approach combining single-cell analyses and experimental models, we aimed to delineate the molecular mechanisms by which CAFs influence tumor genome integrity and metastatic behavior. Ultimately, our goal was to identify stromal signaling pathways that may serve as novel therapeutic targets to impede CIN-driven CRC progression.

## Results

## CIN-high malignant tumor subpopulations (C3/C4) are preferentially enriched in liver metastases
We used the GSE225857 dataset, which includes both immune and non-immune cells derived from CRC, liver metastases, and corresponding tissues, including adjacent normal colorectal tissue (NCT), adjacent normal liver tissue (NLT), LM CRC, and peripheral blood (PBL) (Supplementary Fig. ). Nine distinct cell types were identified: B cells, plasma B cells, cycling NK/T cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, mast cells, myeloid cells, NK/T cells, and tumor cells (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). Representation-over-expected (Roe) scores for all cell populations displayed the tissue-specific enrichment features of each cell populations (Supplementary Fig. ). Furthermore, tumor cells from both LCT and primary CRC samples were segregated into 14 distinct subpopulations (Fig. B, C and Supplementary Fig. ), characterized by unique gene expression signatures and functional enrichment (Supplementary Fig. ). For example, clusters C3 and C4 are associated with cell cycle regulation and chromosomal stability. Notably, tumor cell subclusters exhibiting elevated CNV scores were defined as malignant tumor cells compared with normal reference cells (Supplementary Fig. ). Moreover, we employed NMF across a range of parameter settings to extract robust expression programs of these tumor cell subpopulations (Fig. ). Each program was defined by the top 50 genes ranked by the NMF scores (Fig. ). Among these, MP1 was the most strongly enriched for genes related to cell cycle and chromosomal segregation. Focusing on CIN, we identified 22 hub genes within MP1 that were closely associated with CIN. PPI analysis revealed strong functional interconnectivity among these genes (Fig. ). Expression profiling revealed that CIN-associated genes were predominantly enriched in clusters C3 and C4 (Fig. ). Furthermore, the prevalence of clusters C3 and C4 was higher in LCT samples than in primary CRC samples, suggesting a preferential expansion of CIN-high subpopulations in metastatic sites. Roe analysis corroborated this finding, showing a distinct tissue preference for these clusters in the LCT group (Supplementary Fig. , D). Collectively, these results suggest that a specific subpopulation of tumor cells, defined by a CIN-enriched expression program, may play a critical role in driving CRC progression and LM. 

## CIN-high group was identified to have high metastasis and proliferation activity
To elucidate the role of CIN in CRC LM, we developed a novel CIN index by integrating the multidimensional scRNA-seq data. We first performed high-dimensional WGGNA (hdWGCNA) on the tumor cells using a soft-thresholding power of 7 (Supplementary Fig. ), which identified 11 distinct modules (Supplementary Fig. ). GO analysis revealed that the MEgreenyellow and MEpurple modules were significantly associated with CIN-related processes (Fig. ). Cross-referencing of these modules with tumor cluster-specific markers demonstrated strong, non-random associations between particular modules and tumor subpopulations (Fig. ), with ME greenyellow and ME purple notably enriched in clusters C3 and C4 (Fig. ). Correlation analysis among MEs showed the strongest correlation observed between the ME greenyellow and ME purple modules (Fig. ). To construct the CIN index, we integrated the results from hdWGCNA (MEgreenyellow and MEpurple), (NMF; MP1), and inferCNV analyses, along with CIN-related genes obtained from GeneCards (Fig. E, F). Based on the median CIN index score among the LCT samples, we stratified tumor cells into CIN-high and CIN-low groups for downstream analyses (Fig. ). Patients with LM exhibited significantly higher CIN index scores (Fig. ), indicating that CIN is a critical driver of metastatic potential. To further characterize the molecular differences between the CIN-high and CIN-low groups, we identified 450 DEGs with EMT-associated genes highlighted in red (Fig. and Supplementary Table ). Hallmark GSEA revealed that the CIN-high group was significantly enriched for pathways associated with cell cycle progression (Fig. ). KEGG further confirmed the significant involvement of the cell cycle, DNA replication, homologous recombination, base excision repair, and focal adhesion processes in the CIN-high group (Fig. ). Consistent results were obtained from the GSVA analysis (Fig. ). Together, these findings revealed that CIN-high tumor cells exhibit aggressive biological behavior characterized by increased proliferation, genomic instability, and metabolic reprogramming, potentially underpinning the metastatic progression of CRC to the liver. 

## CIN-high tumor cells were highly interacted with fibroblast in the TME
To systematically dissect intercellular communication within the TME, we employed CellChat analysis. Fibroblasts and endothelial cells exhibited the most extensive and robust interactions with other cell types (Supplementary Fig. ). Both cell types displayed the highest outgoing signaling strengths (Fig. ). Notably, fibroblasts and endothelial cells exhibited high-probability interactions with CIN-high tumor cells (Supplementary Fig. ), particularly via extracellular matrix (ECM)-related ligand-receptor pairs. Collectively, CIN-high tumor cells displayed intensified and frequent communication with fibroblasts and endothelial cells, largely mediated by ECM-related ligand-receptor pathways. This enhanced stromal-tumor crosstalk likely promotes CIN, accelerates tumor progression, and facilitates liver metastasis. Given this finding, we examined the differential abundance of their subpopulations between primary CRC and LCT samples. Endothelial cells expressing high levels of DLL4 and F02-fibroblasts characterized by elevated MCAM expression were significantly enriched in LCT samples (Fig. ). Additionally, subpopulations such as E02-endothelial (DLL4+), E06-endothelial (CLEC4G+), F02-fibroblast (MCAM+), and F04-fibroblast (C3+) were markedly expanded in the LCT group (Supplementary Fig. ). Survival analysis revealed that an elevated abundance of F01-fibroblasts (marked by MCAM) and F02-fibroblasts (marked by PRELP) was significantly associated with poorer overall survival (p < 0.05, Fig. C, D). Moreover, we stratified the LCT samples into high- and low-fibroblast groups based on the abundance of F01-fibroblast-MCAM and F02-fibroblast-PRELP, respectively. Differential gene expression analysis was performed between the high and low groups. DEGs were designated as fibroblast DEGs (Fig. E, F). Subsequently, we intersected these fibroblast DEGs with DEGs identified between the CIN-high and CIN-low tumor groups, identifying 132 overlapping genes (Fig. and Supplementary Table ). Among these, CCDC3 was predominantly expressed by CAFs and displayed significantly higher expression in LCT samples. Notably, the functional role of CCDC3 in CRC metastasis remains largely unknown. In addition, we found that elevated CCDC3 expression was significantly associated with poorer overall survival in TCGA datasets (Fig. ). Figure illustrates the process of constructing a biological knowledge graph using artificial intelligence. Thus, CCDC3 was selected for further investigation. CCDC3- specific biological knowledge graph network suggested that it was engaged in biological processes such as chromosomal instability and colorectal cancer liver metastasis through MAPK signaling pathway and nucleotide excision repair (Fig. J, K and Supplementary Fig. ). 

## CCDC3 derived from CIN-high CAFs enhances CRC malignancy
To elucidate the mechanisms driving CRC LM, we focused on the roles of CAFs. We conducted co-culture assays using CAFs derived from primary tumors (CAF) and tissue-resident CAFs (CAF-TR) along with CRC cell lines. Notably, CRC cells co-cultured with CAF-TR exhibited significantly enhanced migratory and invasive abilities compared with those co-cultured with primary CAFs (Fig. ). In addition, CAF-TR promoted higher proliferative capacity in CRC cells (Fig. ). These CAF-TR populations also markedly increased CIN, as indicated by elevated α-tubulin levels (Fig. ). Treatment of CRC cells with recombinant CCDC3 led to increased metastatic traits (Fig. ), enhanced proliferative capacity (Fig. ), and elevated α-tubulin expression, further confirming its role in promoting CIN (Fig. ). Specifically, CCDC3 was knocked out in CAF-TR cells and overexpressed in CAFs, followed by co-culture with CRC cells. High CCDC3 expression significantly enhanced CRC cell invasion (Fig. ), colony-forming ability (Fig. ), and CIN (Fig. ), whereas low CCDC3 levels in CAF-TR exerted the opposite effects. Finally, we compared endogenous CCDC3 expression levels between the CAF and CAF-TR populations and confirmed significantly higher expression in CAF-TR (Fig. ), further supporting the role of tissue-resident CAFs as a key source of CCDC3 in the TME and a potential driver of CRC progression and metastasis. 

## CCDC3 produces oncogenic effects via interacting with CXCR3 in CRC
Mechanistically, our data revealed that CCDC3 exerts oncogenic effects in CRC cells by interacting with the CXCR3 receptor. Co-IP assays demonstrated a physical association between CCDC3 and CXCR3 in CRC cells (Fig. ). CXCR3 silencing significantly reversed the CCDC3-induced increase in metastatic potential (Fig. ), cell proliferation (Fig. ), and CIN as indicated by α-tubulin expression (Fig. ). These findings confirmed that CXCR3 is essential for mediating the pro-tumorigenic effects of CCDC3. Furthermore, when CRC cells were co-cultured with CAF-TR, in which CCDC3 was silenced, CXCR3 knockdown suppressed the enhanced metastatic capability (Fig. ), proliferation (Fig. ), and CIN (Fig. ), otherwise induced by CCDC3. Conversely, co-culture with CAFs overexpressing CCDC3 reproduced these oncogenic effects, which were mitigated by CXCR3 knockdown (Fig. ). Collectively, these results provide compelling evidence that CCDC3 promotes metastasis, proliferation, and CIN in CRC cells by directly interacting with CXCR3, thus positioning the CCDC3-CXCR3 axis as a potential therapeutic target in chromosomally unstable CRC. 

## CXCR3 enhanced CDT1 expression by facilitating STAT3 phosphorylation
Given the established role of CXCR3 in activating multiple intracellular signaling cascades associated with cancer , we investigated the downstream effectors involved in CIN. We categorized CRC samples into high- and low-CIN score groups and performed differential expression analysis. In parallel, we conducted a separate differential expression analysis comparing LM samples to primary tumor samples to identify metastasis-associated DEGs (MET DEGs). Additionally, CIN-related genes were retrieved from the GeneCards database. By intersecting these three gene sets, CIN DEGs, MET DEGs, and CIN-associated genes, we identified 25 candidate hub genes that potentially link CIN to metastatic progression in CRC (Fig. and Supplementary Table ). Transcription factor analysis targeting the 25 CIN hub genes revealed that CXCR3 may enhance CDT1 expression by promoting STAT3 phosphorylation at the Tyr705 site. Supporting this, treatment with the STAT3 inhibitor LLL3 reduced the CDT1 protein levels in CRC cells (Fig. ). Then we cloned the CDT1 promoter sequence into a GFP reporter plasmid and co-transfected it with a STAT3 expression plasmid into 293 T cells. The significantly increased GFP fluorescence intensity confirmed that STAT3 enhanced CDT1 transcription (Fig. ). ChIP-PCR using a STAT3-specific antibody further demonstrated that STAT3 directly binds to the CDT1 promoter, specifically at the 458–474 bp region, as predicted (Fig. ). To validate this binding site, we introduced mutations at the 458–474 site of the CDT1 promoter and co-transfected the mutant construct with the STAT3 plasmid into 293 T cells, resulting in a marked reduction in GFP fluorescence compared to the wild-type promoter (Fig. ). To assess whether CXCR3 modulates STAT3 activation, we knocked down CXCR3 in CRC cells and observed a decrease in p-STAT3 (Tyr705) without affecting the total STAT3 protein levels, along with a concomitant reduction in CDT1 expression (Fig. ). Immunofluorescence analysis further revealed reduced nuclear localization of STAT3 following CXCR3 knockdown, indicating suppression of STAT3 activation and downstream CDT1 transcription (Fig. ). Conversely, overexpression of CXCR3 led to increased STAT3 phosphorylation and CDT1 upregulation (Fig. ), whereas STAT3 knockdown reversed these effects, including diminished nuclear STAT3 accumulation (Fig. ). DNA pulldown assays using a CDT1 promoter-derived probe confirmed STAT3’s direct binding to the CDT1 promoter (Fig. ). Notably, CXCR3 overexpression-induced STAT3 activation and CDT1 expression were abrogated by STAT3 knockdown (Fig. I, J), collectively demonstrating that CXCR3 promotes STAT3 phosphorylation, leading to the transcriptional activation of CDT1 in CRC.

## CCDC3 promotes CDT1 expression through activation of STAT3 signaling via CXCR3
Next, we found that treating CRC cells with recombinant CCDC3 protein led to upregulation of CDT1 expression and increased STAT3 phosphorylation levels (Fig. ). However, this CCDC3-induced enhancement was significantly attenuated when cells were co-treated with the STAT3 inhibitor LLL3, which not only reduced CDT1 expression and STAT3 phosphorylation (Fig. ) but also suppressed STAT3 nuclear localization (Fig. ). To further validate the dependency on CXCR3, we investigated the effect of CCDC3 in CXCR3-knockdown (CXCR3-KD) CRC cells, in which the ability of CCDC3 to induce CDT1 expression and STAT3 phosphorylation was substantially impaired (Fig. ), along with a reduction in nuclear STAT3 accumulation (Fig. ). Moreover, CRC cells co-cultured with CAF-TR cells subjected to CCDC3 knockdown (CAF-TR-CCDC3-KD) showed decreased levels of p-STAT3 and a diminished increase in CDT1 expression (Fig. ). Supporting this, the STAT3 inhibitor, LLL3, also suppressed the stimulatory effect of CAF-TR on CDT1 expression, reinforcing the centrality of STAT3 in this mechanism (Fig. ). Finally, co-culture of CXCR3-KD CRC cells with CAF-TR confirmed that CAF-TR regulated STAT3 activation and CDT1 expression in a CXCR3-dependent manner (Fig. ). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CCDC3 enhances CDT1 expression by activating STAT3 signaling through CXCR3, highlighting a critical axis within the TME that contributes to CIN progression in CRC. 

## CCDC3/CDT1 axis drives malignant phenotypes via CXCR3-STAT3 pathway
We further explored the role of CCDC3 in promoting malignant phenotypes in CRC through CDT1 and found that CDT1 knockdown significantly inhibited cellular migration, invasion (Fig. ), and proliferation (Fig. ), which were partially restored by co-culture with CAF-TR cells. A similar suppressive pattern was observed following treatment with the STAT3 inhibitor LLL3 (Fig. C, D), underscoring the involvement of STAT3 in mediating these effects. Additionally, silencing CDT1 partly reversed the CCDC3-induced increases in both invasive behavior (Fig. ) and proliferation rate (Fig. ), whereas STAT3 inhibition produced comparable outcomes (Fig. G, H), reinforcing the notion that CCDC3 exerts its oncogenic influence via the STAT3/CDT1 axis. Collectively, these findings indicate that the pro-tumorigenic effects of CAF-TR in CRC are largely driven by the secretion of CCDC3, which activates the CXCR3/STAT3/CDT1 signaling cascade to enhance cell motility, invasiveness, and proliferation. Importantly, disruption of any individual component within this pathway, whether CCDC3, STAT3, or CDT1, results in marked attenuation of malignant phenotypes, thereby identifying this signaling axis as a promising therapeutic target in the treatment of CRC. 

## In vivo validation confirms CCDC3/CXCR3/STAT3/CDT1 axis drives tumor growth and LM
To validate our in vitro observations, we conducted in vivo experiments using xenograft models in which recombinant CCDC3 protein or anti-CCDC3 antibody was administered via tail vein injection to both subcutaneous tumor and metastasis models. Administration of recombinant CCDC3 significantly enhanced primary tumor growth (Fig. ) and metastatic spread (Fig. ) of CRC cells, whereas treatment with an anti-CCDC3 antibody markedly suppressed tumor expansion (Fig. ) and LM (Fig. ). Analysis of tumor and metastatic liver tissues revealed that CCDC3 treatment elevated STAT3 phosphorylation and CDT1 expression at both primary (Fig. ) and metastatic sites (Fig. ), whereas anti-CCDC3 intervention led to reduced STAT3 activation and lower CDT1 levels. To further dissect this pathway, we employed CRC cells with enforced CDT1 overexpression (CDT1-OE) in mice via subcutaneous or tail vein injection, which significantly accelerated tumor growth (Fig. ) and LM (Fig. ). Notably, these pro-tumorigenic effects were effectively reversed by treatment with the STAT3 inhibitor LLL3, resulting in a reduced tumor burden and fewer metastatic lesions (Fig. E, F). Mechanistically, LLL3 administration suppressed STAT3 phosphorylation and CDT1 expression in both primary tumors (Fig. ) and metastatic liver tissues (Fig. ). Taken together, these in vivo findings confirm that CAF-derived CCDC3 drives CRC progression through the CXCR3/STAT3/CDT1 signaling axis, promoting both tumor growth and metastasis and highlight this pathway as a promising target for therapeutic intervention (Fig. ). ## Discussion
Our study identified a novel stromal-to-tumor signaling axis in CRC liver metastasis centered on the CCDC3 protein secreted by CAFs and the downstream CXCR3/STAT3/CDT1 pathway. We found that CCDC3 is abundantly secreted by CAFs in the tumor microenvironment and significantly enhances the invasive and metastatic behavior of CRC cells. Mechanistically, CCDC3 binds to the chemokine receptor CXCR3 on tumor cells, triggering robust phosphorylation of STAT3 and subsequently driving transcriptional upregulation of the DNA replication licensing factor CDT1. This signaling cascade promotes CIN and enhances the proliferative capacity of tumor cells, thereby facilitating liver colonization. Notably, blockade of CCDC3 or inhibition of CXCR3 or STAT3 in CRC cells abolished these pro-metastatic effects, underscoring the critical role of this axis in CRC metastasis. These findings expand the paradigm of CAFs as the active orchestrators of metastasis. Similar to our observations, recent studies have shown that SEMA3C secreted by CAFs binds to NRP2 in CRC cells to activate MAPK signaling and promote liver metastasis . Such evidence highlights that CAF-derived signals, such as SEMA3C and CCDC3, can directly license tumor cells for dissemination and outgrowth in distant organs.
CAFs are recognized as major tumor-promoting components of the TME. Single-cell profiling of CRC has revealed heterogeneous CAF subpopulations with distinct functions . Fibroblasts are abundant in the CRC stroma and typically drive tumor progression, although some specialized subsets may exert tumor-suppressive – . Moreover, single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses have revealed CRC-specific CAF phenotypes (e.g., FAP+ CAFs or inflammatory CAFs) that interact with immune cells and help establish an immune-excluded niche . In this context, our study demonstrated that CAF-derived CCDC3 directly activates a metastatic program in tumor cells, offering concrete insights into CAF-tumor crosstalk . Previous research has shown that liver metastasis-initiating cells in primary CRC engage CAFs via SEMA3C/NRP2 signaling, with CAF-secreted SEMA3C activating MAPK to drive colonization . Our data revealed a distinct ligand-receptor axis (CCDC3-CXCR3) through which stromal fibroblasts endowed CRC cells with enhanced metastatic competence. Collectively, CAF-secreted proteins, such as SEMA3C and CCDC3, or other proteins such as HGF , CXCL12 , and TGF-β , , act as non-cell-autonomous drivers of CRC metastasis by hijacking growth and survival pathways in tumor cells.
The central role of STAT3 in this pathway is consistent with its well-established role in CRC progression and metastasis. STAT3 is a critical transcriptional regulator of many cancers, including CRC . Large-scale analyses showed that STAT3 and its activated form, p-STAT3, are frequently elevated in CRC and are associated with advanced stage, metastasis, and poor survival , . Jeong et al. reported constitutive STAT3 activation in CRC cells, which drives epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), invasion, and stem-like properties via the target gene FOS . Our findings indicate that CAF-derived CCDC3 effectively hijacked this pro-tumorigenic STAT3 circuitry. Specifically, we showed that CCDC3 induces rapid phosphorylation of STAT3 at Tyr705, and that pharmacological inhibition of STAT3 abolishes CCDC3-induced tumor cell migration and survival. In line with these observations, Pennel et al. recently demonstrated that CRC patients with elevated JAK/STAT3 signaling, particularly in stromal-rich tumors, exhibit significantly worse outcomes and that JAK/STAT3 inhibition impairs CRC cell viability and growth . Our identification of STAT3 as a key effector of CCDC3 strengthens the concept that STAT3 is a convergence point for diverse prometastatic signals in CRC. It also suggests that STAT3-dependent transcriptional programs in this context include not only EMT and stemness, but also the upregulation of the DNA replication machinery (via CDT1) in response to stromal signaling.
CIN is a hallmark of most CRCs and is closely linked to tumor aggressiveness , . CDT1 is a licensing factor that governs DNA origin firing, and its dysregulation can trigger unscheduled DNA replication and genomic instability , . In mouse models, CDT1 overexpression in the colon drives excessive origin licensing, DNA damage, and tumor formation . In human CRC, elevated CDT1 levels are associated with microsatellite instability and poor differentiation . Our findings suggest that CAF-derived CCDC3, via STAT3 activation, increases CDT1 expression in CRC cells. This may establish a feed-forward loop, wherein elevated CDT1 induces replication stress and CIN, potentially enhancing metastatic adaptation. Interestingly, STAT3 has been implicated in the regulation of the cell cycle and DNA replication genes , and our results indicate that CCDC3-induced STAT3 activation directly controls CDT1 transcription in CRC cells, linking extracellular niche signals to intrinsic genomic instability. Thus, our study provides a mechanistic bridge between TME and one of the key biological drivers of CRC metastasis. Moreover, we noted that CCDC3 upregulation appears to be clinically relevant, as elevated expression is associated with poorer outcomes. To validate this observation, we analyzed independent TCGA cohorts and confirmed that high CCDC3 expression consistently correlates with adverse prognosis. These data support the potential utility of CCDC3 as a prognostic biomarker and align with the CCDC3-linked signaling programs identified in this study. An important next step for translation is protein-level validation in clinical specimens.
A surprising finding of our study is the pro-metastatic role of CXCR3 in tumor cells, which contrasts with recent evidence suggesting a protective role for CXCR3 in metastasis , . Traditionally, CXCR3 is known as a receptor expressed on immune cells (e.g., Th1 lymphocytes and NK cells), guiding them to inflamed tissues through CXCL9/10/11 signaling . Paradoxically, previous studies have shown that CRC cells express CXCR3 and migrate toward its ligands and that antagonizing CXCR3 reduces tumor cell migration and survival in vitro . A seminal study demonstrated that a small-molecule CXCR3 inhibitor (AMG487) dramatically suppressed lung metastases of colon carcinoma in mice, although it had minimal impact on liver metastases . Thus, CXCR3 is believed to mediate organ-specific metastatic tropism in CRC. More recently, Russo et al. reported that CXCR3 on NK cells enhances their infiltration and antitumor activity in CRC liver metastases; CXCR3-deficient NK cells failed to populate liver lesions, suggesting a protective immune function . Our data showed that tumor-intrinsic CXCR3 can be exploited by CAF-derived signals, such as CCDC3, to promote metastasis via STAT3 activation. In fact, blocking CXCR3 in CRC cells abolished CCDC3-induced STAT3 signaling and metastatic behavior. This dichotomy highlights the context-dependent nature of CXCR3 signaling: CXCR3-mediated chemokine gradients are essential for immune cell recruitment to the liver, and stromal activation of tumor-intrinsic CXCR3 triggers survival and dissemination programs. These findings have several important therapeutic implications. Direct CXCR3 antagonism may impair immune surveillance, even if it suppresses tumor invasiveness. Indeed, preclinical studies have shown that systemic CXCR3 inhibition markedly reduces lung, but not liver metastases . Our findings suggest that more selective approaches, such as neutralizing CCDC3 to block its interaction with CXCR3, could inhibit tumor spread without disrupting immune cell trafficking.
In conclusion, our study revealed a previously unrecognized mechanism by which the CRC stromal niche drives liver metastasis. We demonstrated that CAF-derived CCDC3 activates the CXCR3/STAT3 axis to upregulate CDT1 and induce genomic instability, thereby enhancing the metastatic potential of tumor cells. These findings align with emerging literature on the role of the tumor microenvironment in CRC, including CAF-tumor interactions (e.g., SEMA3C/NRP2 ) and JAK/STAT3 signaling in stromal-rich tumors . By integrating these insights, our study identified CCDC3 as a novel stromal mediator and potential therapeutic target in CRC. Targeting the CCDC3/CXCR3/STAT3/CDT1 axis may offer a promising strategy to prevent metastasis and improve outcomes in patients with aggressive CRC. Moreover, these findings underscore the importance of addressing both tumor-intrinsic and-microenvironmental signals in therapy design. Interventions that neutralize CAF-derived factors, block chemokine receptors, or inhibit STAT3 transcriptional activity may complement existing treatments and help counter the lethal process of liver metastasis in CRC.
A key next step toward translation will be validation at the protein level in well-annotated clinical specimens. In particular, immunohistochemical profiling of CCDC3 together with CXCR3 and activated STAT3 (p-STAT3) in patient tissues stratified by CIN status would help clarify whether the proposed CCDC3-CXCR3-STAT3 axis is recapitulated in vivo and whether CIN modifies pathway activation or its relationship to outcome. Although such CIN-stratified IHC analyses are not feasible within the scope of the current study, we explicitly acknowledge this as a limitation. Future studies incorporating independent clinical cohorts with matched CIN assessments and standardized IHC scoring will be important to confirm clinical utility, define the most informative biomarker combinations, and evaluate whether this signaling axis can support patient risk stratification or therapeutic targeting. Similarly, while the animal experiments in this study demonstrated consistency across multiple batches and post-hoc power analyses supported the current sample size as sufficient to detect significant between-group differences, we acknowledge that the sample size remains relatively limited. This design choice was made based on the pre-experimental effect size, animal ethics principles, and the exploratory nature of the preliminary investigation. We acknowledge that increasing the sample size would further enhance the robustness and generalizability of the results. Therefore, we explicitly list this as another limitation of the study. Additionally, the artificial intelligence knowledge graph approach employed in this study aims to construct an efficient and interpretable hypothesis generation engine for integrating multi-omics data and identifying potential core regulatory nodes (such as CCDC3). However, the current method still has room for improvement in terms of relationship prediction coverage and will integrate more complex deep learning models in the future.

## Methods

## Sc-RNA-seq analysis
The single-cell RNA-seq dataset including GSE225857 was obtained from the GEO database and analyzed using Seurat (v5.1.0) in R platform (v4.3.1). Expression matrix was normalized using LogNormalize (scale factor = 10,000) and percent.mt was regressed out during scaling. Batch effects across samples were corrected using Harmony (v1.2.0) on the top 10 principal components (dims = 1:10). The Harmony-corrected embeddings were used to construct the shared nearest-neighbor graph and to perform clustering at a resolution of 0.5. Dimensionality reduction was conducted using PCA, followed by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) for visualization. Cell clustering was performed using the graph-based Louvain algorithm. Cell types were annotated based on canonical marker genes for epithelial, immune, and stromal populations as described in a previous study . To identify malignant epithelial cells, large-scale copy number variations (CNVs) were inferred using inferCNV (v1.10) with non-malignant clusters (e.g., T cells, B cells, dendritic cells, and fibroblasts) serving as references. Relative gene expression levels were compared across chromosomes and smoothed profiles were generated to detect broad chromosomal gains and losses. Cells that exhibited widespread CNV events were classified as malignant. CIN was quantified for each cell as the proportion of the genome showing inferred alterations.

## Survival analysis
Gene expression microarray datasets and corresponding clinical information (GSE12945, GSE17538, GSE29621, GSE38832, GSE39582, and GSE41258) were obtained from the GEO database. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated using the R package Survival (v3.8), and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. Patients were dichotomized into high- and low-expression groups based on the average expression value as the cutoff. Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to estimate hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals.

## CIN index establishment
Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) was performed to identify gene modules associated with CIN using gene expression data derived from tumor cells in liver colorectal tumor (LCT) samples via hdWGCNA (v0.3.3). Highly variable genes were selected to construct a weighted gene correlation network (soft-thresholding power of β = 7, modules were detected with a minimum module size of 20). Module eigengenes were correlated with CIN scores, and CIN-associated modules were defined by correlation >0.3 and FDR < 0.01. Functional enrichment analysis was conducted to uncover the biological processes represented by each module. Non-negative matrix factorization was performed using GeneNMF (v0.9.2; cNMF implementation) with default parameters. The factorization rank (K) was evaluated over a range of 2–10, and the optimal rank was selected by maximizing the cophenetic correlation. Gene programs (meta-programs) were annotated by enrichment analysis, and the CIN-related program (MP1) was used for CIN index construction. A comprehensive CIN index was constructed by integrating genes from 3 sources: (i) the CIN-related cNMF gene program (MP1), (ii) CIN-associated hdWGCNA modules (e.g., MEpurple and MEgreenyellow), and (iii) a curated CIN gene list obtained from GeneCards (https://www.lifemapsc.com/gcsuite/gc_trial/). CIN scores were computed using Seurat’s AddModuleScore, and tumor cells/samples with CIN scores above the 50th percentile were classified as CIN-high, whereas those at or below the median were classified as CIN-low. This dichotomization was used for subsequent differential expression analyses.

## Cell communication analysis
To investigate intercellular communication among the major cell populations, including CIN-high and CIN-low tumor cells, immune cells, fibroblasts, and stromal cells, we employed the CellChat R package. Normalized gene expression data and cell cluster annotations were provided as inputs. CellChat inferred intercellular communication networks by computing the probability of ligand-receptor interactions based on a curated database of known signaling interactions. Statistically significant signaling pathways were identified through permutation testing and visualized using network and circle plots, allowing the comparison of communication strength and patterns across different cell types and CIN states.

## Differentially expression analysis and enrichment analysis
Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis was performed to compare CIN-high and CIN-low tumor cells using thresholds of adjusted p < 0.05, and |log2 fold change | > 0.5. The identified DEGs were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses using the clusterProfiler R package to determine associated biological functions. To further explore pathway activity, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the MSigDB Hallmark gene sets, and Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) was performed using KEGG pathways to evaluate pathway-level differences in CIN-high cells. Additionally, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to assess the prognostic relevance of fibroblast subpopulations in CRC patient outcomes (The transcriptome and clinical data utilized herein were sourced from TCGA).

## AI-enhanced methodology for constructing biological knowledge graphs
This study employs a systematic, multi-level AI-enhanced approach to construct a biological knowledge graph, aiming to integrate multi-source biomedical data and reveal gene regulatory networks. The method encompasses four primary stages: data preprocessing, entity recognition, graph construction, and reasoning analysis with validation assessment. The specific workflow is as follows:

## Step 1: data sources and preprocessing
Abstracts and full-text articles related to colorectal cancer (CRC), chromosomal instability (CIN), and liver metastasis were retrieved and downloaded from the PubMed database. Simultaneously, integrate structured knowledge from public biological databases, including STRING (protein interactions), BioGRID (genetic and protein interactions), KEGG/Reactome (pathway information), NCBI Gene (gene annotation), and CTD (gene-disease associations), to form a foundational knowledge base.

## Step 2: entity recognition and relationship extraction
(1) Named entity recognition: employed a BioBERT model fine-tuned on CRC-related literature to identify entities such as genes, proteins, pathways, diseases, and biological processes within text. Further standardized and disambiguated entities using the GNormPlus tool against HGNC and UniProt databases. (2) Relation extraction: employ a hybrid approach combining dependency parsing and predefined semantic schemas to extract entity-relationship types from sentences, such as “regulation,” “interaction,” “promotion,” and “inhibition.” Extracted results undergo manual annotation to create a training corpus for model optimization. (3) Literature-derived knowledge base construction: integrate extracted entity-relation triples with content from the aforementioned public databases to form an initial knowledge network.

## Step 3: knowledge graph construction and storage
The Neo4j graph database is used to store and manage the knowledge graph. Nodes represent biological entities (genes, proteins, pathways, diseases, etc.), while edges denote relationships between entities. Each edge includes attributes such as source database, confidence score, and supporting literature ID. During graph construction, original confidence scores from databases like STRING are retained, and relationships extracted from literature are assigned composite scores based on model probability and co-occurrence support.

## Step 4: graph analysis and reasoning methods
(1) Topological property calculation: utilize the NetworkX library to compute topological metrics such as degree centrality and betweenness centrality for nodes, identifying key regulatory nodes within the network. (2) Latent relationship prediction: employ Node2Vec for node embedding learning, predicting latent gene-phenotype associations based on vector similarity. Simultaneously, utilize rule-based meta-path reasoning to infer hidden relationships through multi-step paths like ‘gene-pathway-disease’. (3) Functional module detection: employ the Louvain community detection algorithm to identify highly interconnected subnetworks (functional modules) within the graph, revealing biological themes such as CIN-related regulatory clusters.

## Cell lines and culture
The CRC cell line HCT116 was cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Primary carcinoma-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) were isolated from de-identified CRC patient tissues (primary tumor and matched resected LM) under protocols approved by the Ethics Committee (NSFC-25-0303-166). Briefly, ~0.5 cm3 fresh tumor or metastatic liver tissue was finely minced and plated to facilitate fibroblast outgrowth, as previously described. Early passage CAF lines were designated as CAF (from primary tumor) and CAF-TRs (tumor-resident CAFs from LM) and maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. For the stimulation experiments, HCT116 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and allowed to reach 60–70% confluence over 24 h. The cells were serum-starved for 4 h before treatment. Recombinant human CCDC3 protein (R&D Systems, USA) was added to the culture medium at a final concentration of 100 ng/ml. The control group was administered an equivalent volume of PBS. To inhibit STAT3 signaling, cells were pretreated with the STAT3 inhibitor LLL3 (MedChemExpress, USA) at 10 μM for 1 h prior to CCDC3 stimulation. The corresponding control group was treated with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). After treatment, cells were incubated for an additional 24–48 h prior to downstream analyses.

## Cell migration, invasion, and colony formation assays
Transwell assays were performed to evaluate the migration and invasion capabilities of HCT116 cells. For migration assays, 2.5 × 10⁴ cells suspended in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chambers of Transwell inserts (8.0 μm pore size). For the invasion assays, the inserts were pre-coated with Matrigel to mimic the extracellular matrix. In both cases, a medium containing 20% FBS was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After 24 h, cells that had migrated or invaded the underside of the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.5% crystal violet. Cells were imaged and quantified using a light microscope. For colony formation assays, 500 HCT116 cells per well were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 10–14 days under standard conditions. Colonies were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.5% crystal violet, and those containing more than 50 cells were counted using a stereomicroscope.

## Western blotting
Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Equal amounts of total protein were separated using SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting CCDC3, CXCR3, CDT1, STAT3, phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3 Y705), and α-tubulin (all from Abcam) following the manufacturer’s instructions. After washing, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection reagent.

## Immunofluorescence
HCT116 cells cultured on glass coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Following blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against STAT3 or p-STAT3 Y705 at a 1:200 dilution. After washing, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. The coverslips were mounted using an antifade mounting medium, and fluorescence images were captured using a confocal laser-scanning microscope.

## Co-culture assays
Co-culture experiments were performed using Transwell inserts with 0.4 μm pore-size membranes (Corning), which permit the exchange of soluble factors while preventing direct cell-cell contact. HCT116 CRC cells (2 × 10⁵ cells in 1 mL DMEM with 5% FBS) were seeded in the lower chamber, and CAFs (1 × 10⁵ cells in 300 μL medium) were plated in the upper chamber. Co-cultures were maintained for 3–4 days. To investigate the role of CAF-derived CCDC3, genetically modified CAFs were used, either CCDC3 knockdown (shCCDC3) or overexpression. The control groups included wild-type CAFs and those transduced with non-targeting shRNA. Following the co-culture period, HCT116 cells from the lower chamber were harvested for downstream analyses including proliferation, migration, and gene expression profiling.

## Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
Co-immunoprecipitation assays were performed to validate the interactions between CCDC3 and CXCR3. Briefly, the cells were lysed in ice-cold lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein lysate were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-CCDC3, anti-CXCR3, or control IgG antibodies, followed by incubation with Protein A/G agarose beads for 2–4 h at 4 °C. After extensive washing to remove non-specifically bound proteins, the immune complexes were eluted by boiling in SDS sample buffer and subjected to SDS-PAGE. Proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes and probed with antibodies against the corresponding binding partners to assess co-precipitation by western blotting.

## Chromatin IP (ChIP)-PCR
ChIP was conducted using a commercial ChIP assay kit (Millipore), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. HCT116 cells, with or without CAF co-culture or cytokine treatment, were cross-linked with formaldehyde, lysed, and sonicated to shear chromatin into ~200–500 bp fragments. Chromatin extracts were immunoprecipitated overnight at 4 °C, using an anti-STAT3 antibody or normal IgG as a negative control. After reversal of cross-linking and DNA purification, enrichment of STAT3 at the CDT1 promoter region was assessed by PCR or quantitative PCR (qPCR), using specific primers flanking the STAT3-binding site within the CDT1 promoter.

## GFP reporter assay
To assess the STAT3-mediated transcriptional activation of the CDT1 promoter, a GFP reporter assay was conducted. The promoter region of human CDT1 was PCR-amplified and cloned upstream of the GFP coding sequence in the pEGFP-N1 plasmid. HEK293T cells were seeded in 24-well plates and co-transfected with the CDT1 promoter-GFP reporter construct and a STAT3 expression vector (pcDNA3.1-STAT3) using Lipofectamine 3000 following the manufacturer’s protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After 48 h, GFP expression was observed under a fluorescence microscope and quantified using a microplate reader (excitation/emission, 488/510 nm) to evaluate promoter activity.

## DNA pull-down assay
A DNA pull-down assay was performed to assess STAT3 binding to the CDT1 promoter. Biotin-labeled double-stranded oligonucleotides corresponding to the CDT1 promoter region containing the STAT3-binding site were synthesized and incubated with nuclear extracts from HCT116 cells at 4 °C for 2 h. The DNA-protein complexes were captured using streptavidin-agarose beads, washed extensively, and eluted. Bound proteins were analyzed by SDS-PAGE followed by western blotting using an anti-STAT3 antibody. As controls, non-specific (unrelated) biotinylated probes and competition assays with excess unlabeled CDT1 probes were included to confirm binding specificity.

## Animal assay
Six-week-old BALB/c nude mice were maintained under specific-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. Two in vivo CRC models were established: a subcutaneous xenograft model and an experimental metastasis model. For the xenograft model, HCT116 cells (5 × 10⁶ cells in 100 μL of PBS:Matrigel [1:1]) were injected subcutaneously into the dorsal flank of each mouse (n = 5 per group). Tumor dimensions were measured every 3–4 days using digital calipers, and tumor volume was calculated using the following formula: V = (L × W²)/2, where L is the length and W is the width. Once tumors reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were randomized into three groups and treated for 4 weeks with PBS (control), recombinant CCDC3 protein (20 μg/mouse via tail vein, once weekly), or anti-CCDC3 antibody (10 mg/kg, intraperitoneally, twice per week). At the study endpoint, the tumors were excised, weighed, and subjected to histological and molecular analyses.
For the experimental metastasis model, BALB/c nude mice (n = 3 per group) were injected via the tail vein with 1 × 10⁷ HCT116 cells suspended in 100 μL PBS. Six weeks post-injection, the mice were euthanized, and the liver and lungs were harvested, fixed, and examined for metastatic lesions. Metastatic burden was assessed by counting the visible surface nodules and performing histological analysis. The treatment groups received systemic administration of recombinant CCDC3 protein (20 μg/mouse, tail vein, once weekly) or anti-CCDC3 antibody (10 mg/kg, intraperitoneally, twice weekly), as per the xenograft protocol, to evaluate its effects on metastatic colonization. All the in vivo procedures were conducted under sterile conditions in a laminar flow hood. Isoflurane inhalation was used for anesthesia during cell injections and blood collection. The mice were monitored daily, and analgesics were administered if signs of distress or pain were observed. At the study endpoint, euthanasia was performed using CO2 asphyxiation, followed by cervical dislocation in accordance with the AVMA guidelines. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (Protocol No. FUSCC-IACUC-S2025-0329). The sample size for this animal study (n = 3–5 per group) was determined based on significant phenotypic differences observed in preliminary experiments, common sample sizes in prior studies of this nature, and adherence to the “3Rs” principle of animal ethics (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement). Preliminary experiments demonstrated substantial and consistent effect sizes in tumor growth and liver metastasis metrics between the CCDC3-treated group and the control group. Given this foundation, we adopted the minimum sample size necessary to detect significant differences while balancing scientific rigor with animal welfare. Subsequent statistical power analysis confirmed that this sample size possesses sufficient statistical power at a significance level of α = 0.05 to support the primary conclusions of this study.

## Statistical analysis
Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0). Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test. For experiments involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by appropriate post-hoc tests was applied. For data that did not conform to a normal distribution, nonparametric tests (Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test) were used. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Public datasets were processed and visualized using the R software (version 4.4.0). The statistical significance shown in Figure is denoted as p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), and p < 0.001 (***). To assess the statistical power of the experimental design, this study conducted a post hoc power analysis on key in vivo experimental results. Using G*Power 3.1 software, statistical power was calculated based on observed effect sizes (e.g., Cohen’s d) to ensure the reliability of primary conclusions given the sample size.
