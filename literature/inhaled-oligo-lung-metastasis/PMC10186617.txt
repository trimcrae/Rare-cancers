## Abstract
The use of nanoparticles (NPs) as a therapeutic delivery system has expanded markedly over the past decade, particularly regarding applications targeting the skin. The delivery of NP-based therapeutics to the skin requires special consideration owing to its role as both a physical and immunologic barrier, and specific technologies must not only take into consideration the target but also the pathway of delivery. The unique challenge this poses has been met with the development of a wide panel of NP-based technologies meant to precisely address these considerations. In this review article, we describe the application of NP-based technologies for drug delivery targeting the skin, summarize the types of NPs, and discuss the current landscape of NPs for skin cancer prevention and skin cancer treatment as well as future directions within these applications.

## Introduction
The use of nanoparticles (NPs) in various clinical applications has expanded vastly over the last decade ( ; ; ). The term NPs encompasses a wide variety of particle-based materials and formulations in the 1−500 nm diameter range that can improve the conventional delivery of therapeutics. NPs have been successfully used for delivery of various active agents, including a range of hydrophobic and hydrophilic small molecule agents, mAbs, nucleic acids, and proteins, to help overcome barriers in intracellular delivery and trafficking that were not previously possible through traditional modes of administration ( ). In addition, NPs have shown the capacity to enhance site-directed drug delivery by improving tissue drug levels and cellular uptake. Encapsulation of therapeutics in NPs may allow for flexible delivery and increases the stability and solubility of such actives with protection against hydrolytic or enzymatic degradation. Systemic administration of high doses of drugs is often limited by extravasation of the active into off-target sites, leading to higher toxicity and adverse effects ( ). By creating a depot for the cargoes, NPs also allow for a more low-dose, sustained, localized delivery, which may also decrease systemic side effects. This is especially advantageous for agents that show significant therapeutic effects that have previously been halted in preclinical or clinical assessment owing to systemic toxicity ( ). Furthermore, NPs can be engineered to promote transport across cell membranes and by including smart systems that target a specific tissue or vasculature. Such targeted delivery strategies can overcome issues with biodistribution first-pass metabolism and facilitate uptake by specific cell types ( ; ). This smart-engineering system allows for enhanced and targeted delivery to sites that were previously inaccessible to free drugs and molecules owing to their interactions.
NPs by most definitions encompass particle sizes ranging from 1 to 100 nm, but sizes up to 500 nm are utilized in research and application ( ). As a platform that interacts with multiple external factors such as fluidic forces and dynamics in addition to interactions with other biomolecules and cells, engineering the size of the NPs is an important consideration for targeted delivery to an intended site ( ; ; ). The size of NPs affects the cellular uptake pathways, that is, phagocytosis, macropinocytosis, and clathrin- or caveolin-mediated endocytosis ( ; ). Larger NPs may enter cells owing to direct transmembrane penetration, although it may harm cells owing to the large membrane pore created for entry. The optimal size of the NP depends on the location and intended target tissue, that is, the circulatory half-life, tumor permeability, and biodistribution, depending on the intended site. When administered intravenously, there is a size-dependent distribution of NPs to different organs depending on the filtration system within the organ itself; generally, particles linger the longest in the blood, liver, and spleen ( ).
Drug delivery with NPs often also allows for increased functionality and esthetics of the drugs ( ). Some NPs have been designed to incorporate a bioadhesive quality to increase interactive forces between the material and the tissue or cellular target material, including skin and mucous membranes, thereby increasing the specificity and persistence of the NPs ( ; ; ; ). For inorganic molecule delivery, NPs facilitate targeted delivery and can be engineered for increased bioadhesiveness, which presents several advantages over the currently available formulations targeted toward the skin ( ; ), including minimizing accumulation in off-target sites that may lead to adverse effects ( ). In this study, we will focus on the various challenges faced with application of NP-based technologies for drug delivery targeting the skin, the various types of NPs, the current landscape of NPs for skin cancer prevention and skin cancer treatment, and future directions.

## NPs in the Skin
The skin as a target for NP drug delivery requires special consideration because this tissue forms both a physical and immunologic barrier. It is an immunologically rich site with various resident immune populations, including epidermal Langerhans’ cells and dermal dendritic cells, skin-resident and skin-homing effector and regulatory T cells, and innate lymphoid cells ( ). The stratum corneum is the immediate barrier against the entry of NPs designed to target the skin; it is constituted by a thick matrix of dehydrated, dead keratinocytes within an ordered lipid layer. Depending on the method of administration and desired level of targeting, that is, topical application with transdermal delivery or direct injection to the site, NPs can be engineered to perform in synergy with the surrounding environment.
Transdermal delivery refers to when agents are delivered through the skin for systemic or local effect ( ; ) ( ). For typical drug formulations, penetrance and delivery into and through the stratum corneum and epidermis and into the dermis (and thus availability for systemic absorption) can be facilitated by excipient agents, including fatty acids, esters, alcohols, amines, and lipids that physiochemically alter the skin barrier ( ; ; ). NP-facilitated drug delivery to the skin, however, comes with a myriad of challenges because the skin has evolved to be highly protective against most naturally occurring environmental NPs (e.g., bacteria, dust, viral particles, or allergens) that do not readily penetrate the skin unless the skin barrier is disrupted by disease or specific interventions ( ; ). Topically applied NPs have been engineered for the specific transdermal and dermal delivery to target damaged skin, for example, compromised barrier function as seen in inflammatory skin diseases such as atopic dermatitis and psoriasis and genetically altered skin as seen in certain disorders of cornification or in aging skin. Other topical approaches aim to intrinsically and safely enhance skin permeability through the NP composition, often by disrupting the stratum corneum barrier by creating micropores ( ). For other topical or local delivery strategies, there may be other engineered NP surfaces or properties to better accommodate the target lesion, including cutaneous malignancy or damaged/wounded skin. For instance, the site of cutaneous tumors is more acidic and has receptors allowing localizable targeting with conjugated antibodies on NPs to target the tumor matrix. NPs engineered with bioadhesive properties can be delivered to a skin (or mucosal) surface or as a simple subcutaneous or peritumoral or intratumoral injection to improve skin-delivery strategies ( ).
 Depending on the agent and lesion or target of concern, the topical delivery of free agents in traditional vehicles may not achieve adequate penetration into deeper lesions or may require high concentrations and repetitive application, and NPs may offer advantages to address these challenges. For example, topical chemotherapy with 5-fluorouracil or an immunomodulator such as imiquimod can lead to local side effects such as skin irritancy and even ulceration as well as the risk of systemic absorption and toxicity that is increased by the necessary repetitive application ( ; ).
Despite the many design strategies, efficient and effective topical delivery of NPs in a clinical setting remains challenging. There is some evidence that there is skin penetration into the stratum spinosum and then to the dermis for particles that are smaller than 10 nm; however, there is little evidence of penetration with larger molecular sizes ( ; ). For particle sizes greater than 20 nm, the NPs may depot within pores and hair follicles and penetrate the perifollicular dermis, albeit usually without a more diffuse penetration into the dermis.

## Types of NPs

## Lipid-based NPs
Lipid-based NPs usually include spherical platforms with a lipid layer ( ). These lipid NPs can be binned largely into two types of NPs: liposomes and solid lipid NP (SLN) or lipid NP (LNP); liposomes contain at least one lipid bilayer, whereas LNPs are a spherical NP composed of an outer lipid layer that may not necessarily include a continuous lipid bilayer. For delivery and application to the skin, lipid-based NPs can be a favorable material because lipids are a key component of the stratum corneum layer and are fundamental in maintaining skin integrity and maintaining moisture. Liposomes and lipid NPs are essentially similar because they both are formed with micellar structures. However, liposomes are traditionally formed by phospholipid bilayer or materials that mimic the biological membranes, whereas LNPs may or may not have a lipid or engineered lipid contiguous bilayer with the active, ranging from drugs to peptides, in the particle core. The lipid-based NPs can be versatile in their composition because they can deliver hydrophobic, hydrophilic, and lipophilic molecules depending on the location of the entrapment of the molecule. Differential small molecule encapsulation within the same NP allows, for example, a conjugated outer shell to target a specific environment or tumor matrix with an inner core of therapeutic release upon contact with the surface.
 However, liposomes can have increased uptake through macro and micropinocytosis and endosomal uptake and faster clearance owing to their similarity in structure with physiological liposomes and vesicles ( ). There is also often rapid leakage of water-soluble drugs from the liposomal cores in the presence of blood components. Therefore, liposomes are also often created with surface modifications that include polymers, peptides, or other compatible materials to extend their circulations ( ).
LNPs typically contain cationic lipids, ionizable lipids, or other lipids within the outer shell that help encapsulate the drug or molecule of choice in their aqueous core. These other lipids are incorporated to improve stability and compatibility in the compartment, to enhance delivery to a specific site, and to enhance escape from endosomes. The cationic and ionizable lipids allow for easy complex formation with a charged material, such as a nucleic acid, and allow for endosomal escape. Additional components of LNPs include other supportive lipids, for example, cholesterol and phosphatidylcholine, which facilitate membrane fusion. The incorporation of lipids modified with polyethylene glycol (PEG) may be used to mitigate opsonization and reticuloendothelial clearance ( ). PEGylation is also a method used to increase the serum half-life of the conjugate after systemic administration and increase the therapeutic potential of the NP ( ). A derivative of SLNs is nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs), which are formed by both solid- and liquid-phase lipids and exhibit improved loading capacity and stability relative to SLNs.

## Polymer-based NPs
Various polymers have been used in NP formulation with the intent to control the size and properties of the NP, minimize the loss of drug and premature degradation of the NPs, and produce materials that are easily manufactured and stored. Polymeric outer shell can help to protect the NPs from protein absorption, including the clearance by opsonins, thereby prolonging blood circulation or target site persistence ( ). Despite their solid forms, polymers used in biomedical applications are biodegradable; most common polymers degrade by hydrolysis enzymatic cleavage in biological fluids, such that larger molecular-weight polymers become lower in molecular weight over time, thus allowing for controlled release of the therapeutic from the system and ultimately leading to complete disappearance of the polymer ( ). Polymeric NPs utilize polymer chains as the main NP agents, but as described earlier, polymers have also been commonly used as a modifying agent on drugs or on lipid NPs. The most commonly used surface polymers include polyglycerols, polycyanoacrylate, and PEG ( ). Micellar formation is also often employed using block copolymers—such as a block copolymer of polylactide-co-glycolide (PLGA) and PEG—which allows for the formation of NPs with differing inner core (PLGA) and surface (PEG) properties. This incorporates an amphiphilic polymer, formed by the conjugation of different polymers for formation of the micelle. Commonly used hydrophobic polymers include polylactic acid (PLA), polycaprolactone, and PLGA ( ).
Polymer NPs can encapsulate therapeutics of various hydrophobicity because the agents can be encapsulated in the core—which might be a multiphase matrix—or conjugated/adsorbed to the surface of the NP. The NP shape and therapeutic dispersion depend on the method of preparation ( ). Nanospheres are characterized by uniform dispersion of the therapeutic agent throughout the NP matrix, whereas the nanocapsules have the therapeutic encapsulated within a hollow core. The kinetics of drug release from polymer NPs depends on the drug, diffusion within the core matrix, pH and other features of the biological environment, and other erosion/diffusion processes ( ). The in vitro release rate of the therapeutic from polymer NPs is often studied to show a controlled and steady release, although the rate of release measured in vitro does not always predict the rate of release in the biological environment.
Polymer NPs, owing to their engineerability with various surface and core modifications, have been used to carry drugs, nucleic acids, and other small molecules. Delivery of a variety of nucleic acid therapeutics has been shown, including small interfering RNA (siRNA) ( ), mRNA (Suberi et al., 2022 ), and CRISPR Cas-9 gene editing machinery ( ). When loading these compounds in the core, charged polymers such as polyethylenimine ( ), polyamidoamine ( ), poly(beta-amino esters) ( ), or poly(amine-co-esters) ( ) are frequently used. Owing to their cationic nature, these polymers have also been shown to have some cytotoxicity to the surrounding cells when the vehicle delivery in vivo is observed with TUNEL stain ( ). This toxicity appears to be related to the excess cationic charge on the NP surface and can be dramatically reduced using cationic polymers with reduced charge density ( ) or shielding charge with surface polymers such as PEG ( ).
Many of these polymers have been extensively studied, including in clinical trials. Notable examples are PEG and PLA: there are already several Food and Drug Administration–approved therapeutics and clinical trials employing PEG-conjugated delivery of chemotherapy or immunomodulator such as recombinant human granulocyte colony-stimulating factor for solid tumors ( ; ; ). For instance, PEGylation of cytokines such as GM-CSF and IFNs has shown a significant increase in biological half-life in plasma ( ; ; ; ; ).

## Metal- and metal oxide–based NPs
Gold, silver, and copper are the most commonly used metals for the synthesis of metal-based NPs, which have been used in a variety of applications from biological labeling to therapeutic delivery ( ). In dermatologic and skin-based applications, metal-based particles, such as silver NPs, have been used for their effective antibacterial and antifungal properties ( ). Metal-based NP effectiveness and toxicity are intrinsically related to size, shape, and zeta potential. For example, several studies have indicated an increase in antibacterial properties of the NP with decreasing diameter, with particles around 10 nm showing the greatest antimicrobial effect ( ; ). Zeta potential can influence biocompatibility with the environment, and the change in the environment’s pH or ionic strength can also affect NP stability in the medium ( ). NP with positive zeta potential has been shown to be associated with increased hemolytic potential, inflammation, and recruitment of granulocytes in the lung ( ). Because these are inorganic engineerable materials, they can be more precisely engineered to have specific sizes and structures.
Gold-based NPs are well-studied and have a wide range of applications, including delivery of chemotherapy, peptide-based, and photosensitizing agents for photothermal therapy (PTT). PTT is a method of cancer treatment that incorporates a photothermal agent (PTA) in the NP to be released to tumors and induce cancer cell death through energy conversion. This leverages the phenomenon of surface plasmon resonance, where resonant conditions are achieved between the oscillations of valence electrons with the frequency of incident light. Through this resonance, there is an enhanced conversion of light energy to thermal energy because absorption to emission is large enough to generate heat. By localizing gold NPs with PTA, there can be targeted light therapy that induces cancer cell destruction with conversion of the light energy into heat. However, gold has been shown to cause acute and chronic changes in gene expression, hypothesized to be due to their affinity for DNA ( ). Silver NPs have similarly been used for purposes of photodynamics or PTT. In antitumor applications, silver particles offer the potential to provide a dual antitumoricidal effect with an encapsulated chemotherapeutic drug followed by irradiation of the administered site to induce cell death.
Metal oxide NPs such as zinc oxide (ZnO) and titanium oxide (TiO2) have been used as actives in sunscreen formulations owing to their capacity to both reflect and absorb UVR ( ). Although largely considered incapable of penetrating the stratum corneum, reduction of particle size through micronization or nanonization both improves performance and raises some concerns about whether size reduction might increase penetrance. There is also some evidence that both metal and metal oxide NPs may exert toxic effects through the generation of ROS, which may accelerate the aging of the skin and photocarcinogenicity ( ), as has been shown for some organic compound sunscreen agents. When ZnO and TiO2 structures are excited with UVR, electron excitation leads to the formation of radicals with surface-bound molecules ( ).
Owing to increasing concerns about the toxicity of metal-based NPs and their potential accumulation in sensitive organs, current research trends have started to shift from systemic and local injections of metal-based NPs ( ). In topical applications, several studies have found that penetration is below the level of detection and that compared with macro-sized particles, nano-sized ZnO and TiO2 do not exhibit an increased penetration potential ( ; ; ; ; ). However, concerns regarding the effects of inhalation in spray products have been raised given their potential to deposit along the airway down to the alveoli. Studies showing the safety of nanometal particles have yielded variable results, but they have consistently shown that inhaled NPs can be found in the lungs depending on the metal particulate type and that they can diffuse and translocate to other parts of the body ( ; ; ; ).

## Membrane/vesicle/polysaccharide
Some NPs are derived from natural polymers such as membranes of plants, viruses, or yeast and polysaccharide-derived materials such as lecithin, dextran, alginate, or chitosan. These NPs were created to take advantage of the natural properties of the plant polymers and improve the transport of the compounds in blood. Virus-like particles (VLPs) are defined as protein structures isolated from viruses lacking the infectious and genetic components of the mammalian host. These have been used in the context of cancer vaccines because the particles themselves can also serve as an adjuvant to enhance immunization. Viruses activate the host immune system owing to the presence of pathogen-associated molecular patterns that can be recognized by pattern-recognition receptors on various cells of the immune system, including resident dendritic cells, to activate a cascade of immune reactions and trigger immediate innate immune responses that are delayed and antigen specific. VLPs share this molecular structure without the toxicity and therefore can enhance immunogenicity when delivered to the host ( ).
Chitosan is an example of a natural polysaccharide. It is a cationic, linear polysaccharide that is made up of randomly distributed and forms of glucosamines and N-acetylglucosamines ( ). It is notably bioadhesive, mucoadhesive, and biodegradable and has been applied in several types of therapeutics for treatment of skin diseases owing to these properties. There is also evidence that chitosan supports skin reconstruction and wound healing ( ; ; ). Chitosan interacts with skin lipids owing to charge and hydrogen bonding with its amino and carboxyl groups, improving its potential as a topical drug carrier for improved diffusion through the skin layers ( ). With its positive charge, chitosan can be also conjugated with polyanions to form NPs with engineered properties ( ). These naturally derived NPs do not have the high purity and reproducibility associated with synthetic LNPs, polymer NPs, and metal-based NPs. For more synthetically derived NPs, conjugation with these proteins or vesicles has been done to improve cellular uptake ( ).

## Nps in Skin Cancer Prevention
Skin cancer remains the most commonly diagnosed cancer in the United States, resulting in an estimated cost of $8.1 billion dollars per year ( ). UVR is the major environmental factor for skin cancer development, with an estimated contribution to 65% of melanoma and 90% of nonmelanoma skin cancer cases, suggesting that the majority of cases are preventable by limiting the skin’s exposure to UVR ( ). In addition to wearing sun-protective clothing and avoiding sun exposure during the day’s peak exposure times, sunscreens can be an effective tool to protect against the development of skin cancer as well as acute sunburn and the effects of photoaging ( ; ). There are two main categories of sunscreen agents: organic (e.g., chemical) and inorganic (physical). Organic sunscreens work by absorbing UVR with subsequent dissipation of the energy. Inorganic sunscreens function primarily by reflecting and scattering UVR as well as through absorbance ( ). Whereas inorganic sunscreens protect against both UVA (320–400 nm) and UVB (290–320 nm), organic sunscreens typically have a more narrow window of protection covering primarily either UVA or UVB. Thus, two or more organic chemical sunscreen agents are often combined to achieve broad-spectrum coverage.
Ideal performance criteria for a sunscreen product include retention on the skin surface and photostability, and most of the NPs described in this section revolve around studies showing transdermal or topical delivery of the NPs. Organic sunscreens have been observed in human studies to permeate the skin barrier and enter the systemic circulation, with detection in urine, plasma, and human milk ( ; ; , ; ; ). This presents a concern, which is amplified by the potential of sunscreens to interact with endocrine receptors and generate downstream estrogenic, antiandrogenic, and antithyroid effects ( ; ). NPs offer a promising opportunity to enhance the desirable effects and minimize the potential concerns of organic sunscreen agents. , , include examples from the current literature of various sunscreens encapsulated in NPs. Encapsulation of sunscreens within LNPs ( ) can help to decrease their rate of release and ability to penetrate human skin ( ; ). SLNs were additionally shown to have better photoprotection abilities relative to their unencapsulated forms as well as improved retention in the stratum corneum layer ( ).
 NLCs, as described previously, evolved from SLNs in pursuit of improved encapsulation efficiency and stability in addition to decreased cytotoxicity ( ; ). NLCs have been shown to enhance sunscreen photostability and reduce skin irritation ( ; ; ; ). They have also shown the potential to enhance UV protection, possibly owing to their particulate nature, allowing a lower concentration of sunscreens to achieve the same photoprotective effect ( ; ; ). In one study, the incorporation of organic sunscreens into carnauba wax NLCs resulted in sun protection factor (SPF) values that were 45% higher than that of the corresponding nanoemulsion ( ). In another study, NLCs encapsulating oxybenzone were shown to increase SPF by more than six-fold while also achieving low skin irritation ( ).
NLCs have also been used in sunscreen formulations that are safer and biorenewable. In 2014, published a study in which NLCs were produced from rice bran oil and raspberry seed oil to encapsulate the organic sunscreen agents avobenzone (AVO) and octocrylene (OCR). The NLCs were able to encapsulate a high concentration of both agents (79% AVO and 90% OCR) and, when formulating these NLCs into creams, resulted in an overall sunscreen concentration of only 3.5% ( ). This formulation exhibited improved photoprotection and antioxidant activity ( ). Antioxidants have also been encapsulated within LNPs with one study showing that NLCs loaded with the antioxidant quercetin, which normally has low stability, exhibited significantly increased in vivo SPF ( ). In another study, a sunscreen formulation containing SLNs encapsulating the antioxidant silymarin, a flavonoid, was found to exhibit improved photoprotection. ( )
Inorganic NPs, such as nano-sized ZnO and TiO2, have been studied extensively as contributors to an optimized sunscreen formulation ( ). Their nano-sized forms have shown a heightened ability to absorb and scatter UVR as well as improved spreadability ( ; ). Given safety concerns surrounding inorganic NPs, attempts have been made to decrease any potential toxicity. One group of researchers coated ZnO NPs with chitosan and PEG, which was shown to improve ZnO stability as well as its photoprotective abilities ( ). Another study found that using ethylene glycol to modify the surface of TiO2 NPs reduced ROS generation while preserving its sunscreen performance ( ). ZnO NPs modified with peptides were found to reduce UVB-induced cellular toxicity and improve epidermal retention ( ). Aside from ZnO and TiO2, silvericronee (Ag) NPs have also shown photoprotective abilities, particularly against UVB-mediated skin damage in vitro and in vivo as well as oxidative damage ( , , ). Other metals that have been formulated as NPs include cerium and gold, both of which have shown promising results as potential components of sunscreen formulations ( ; , ; ). Notably, gold NPs are often loaded on polymer and composite carriers owing to their high cost and low stability. A combination of metals has also been used in an NP-based sunscreen formulation, as in one study that developed a nanocomposite of TiO2, Zn2TiO4, and silver that was shown to have a higher SPF than TiO2 NPs alone ( ).
Silica-based NPs have been explored as sunscreen formulations. Organosilica NPs have shown photoprotective abilities in vivo with minimal UVR-induced ROS generation ( ). One study encapsulating oxybenzone within mesoporous silica was shown to have enhanced SPF, improved photostability, and decreased skin permeation relative to the unencapsulated form ( ; ). Octinoxate was similarly encapsulated within mesoporous silica and found to have improved photoprotective abilities with an increase in SPF of 57% ( ).
The use of polymeric NPs in encapsulating sunscreen agents has been extensively studied ( ). Polymeric NPs have been shown to enhance photoprotective abilities in addition to improving the photostability of various sunscreen agents ( ; ; ). One study that encapsulated OCR in poly(D,L-lactide) NPs found that formulations containing the encapsulated sunscreen agent had improved photostability while maintaining UVB protective effects ( ). Polymeric NPs have also been shown to decrease skin accumulation of sunscreen agents and the potential of protection against UV-induced erythema ( ; ; ; ). Inorganic and organic sunscreen agents have been coencapsulated. One study coencapsulating ZnO NPs and OCR within poly(methyl methacrylate) nondegradable NPs exhibited high encapsulation efficiency and photoprotective properties ( ). Antioxidants have also been coencapsulated with sunscreen agents within polymeric NPs. In 2016, encapsulated OCR, AVO, and oxybenzone alongside the antioxidant α-tocopherol to improve the photostability of the sunscreen agents through the scavenging of ROS. Of note, polymers have also been used in sunscreens outside of NPs as film formers, which boost photoprotective properties by increasing water resistance and improving uniformity of application to the skin ( ).
Among the more recent generation of polymeric NPs are bioadhesive NPs (BNPs), particularly the NPs formed from block copolymers of PLA and hyperbranched polyglycerol (PLA-HPG). The bioadhesive property was achieved by converting vicinal diols on the surface of PLA-HPG NPs to aldehydes, which can readily bind amines in proteins through Schiff-base covalent bonding ( ), encapsulated padimate-O within biodegradable BNPs. These BNPs exhibited strong adherence to the stratum corneum and water-resistant properties in addition to reducing DNA damage. Building upon the work of , utilized the same BNP delivery system to coincorporate AVO and OCR, achieving a broad-spectrum sunscreen formulation again exhibiting water-resistant properties as well as effectiveness in protecting against UVR-induced erythema in a pilot clinical study.

## Nps in the Treatment of Skin Cancer
NPs have been used to treat various types of skin cancer, which can be divided into the two major groups of melanoma and nonmelanoma skin cancers (NMSCs). NMSCs are more common and include squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), basal cell carcinoma, Merkel cell carcinoma, and others. Melanoma presents a uniquely different challenge from other forms of skin cancer because it contains both driver and passenger mutations arising from UV irradiation ( ). Whereas primary in situ melanomas are relatively simple to manage with local resection, metastatic melanoma management remains a challenge: the 5-year survival rate with metastatic melanoma has not changed significantly in the past decades ( ). In metastatic melanoma, combination therapy is typically employed with chemotherapy; radiotherapy; immunotherapy, including anti–PD-1 inhibitors or anti–CTLA-1 inhibitors; and surgery ( ; ; ).
Tumor microenvironment (TME) shows a challenging therapeutic target because there is extensive restructuring in tumors with new vasculature, fluid pressure, and extracellular matrix density. Specifically, TMEs are transformed to accommodate the rapid growth of cancerous cells and their differential oxygen and nutritional needs with microenvironments of altered oxygenation, acidity, immunity, metabolism, innervation, and mechanism ( ). Therefore NPs targeting TME, delivered topically, intradermally, transdermally, or intratumorally, must have improved permeation and penetration with a successful accumulation of the drug in the tumor ( ; ). Hypoxia also plays a large role in tumorigenesis and shaping of the TME with increased angiogenesis in larger, solid tumors. In addition, TME is intrinsically acidic owing to the Warburg effect in which tumorigenic environments have extensive metabolic reprogramming with increased glucose consumption and dependence on lactic dehydrogenase pathway. The main challenge in therapeutic delivery for skin cancer treatment is to improve the selectivity of anticancer drugs for TME while sparing healthy cells.
When delivered systemically, larger molecules and NPs can accumulate in tumors through the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect; the EPR effect describes the concept in which leaky tumor vasculature and poor lymphatic clearance in tumors allow for accumulation of these molecules in the tumor ( ; ; ; ). The EPR effect is applicable in the intravenous injection of NPs; the longer the drugs are in circulation encapsulated in the NPs, they can extravasate into the tumors owing to the EPR effect ( ). Employing NPs for delivery can theoretically increase retention in tumor sites through EPR and allow better tolerability of such cytotoxic drugs while decreasing the necessity for large surgical reconstructions.
Topical and transdermal delivery of NPs is also frequently employed. As mentioned earlier, topical delivery is challenged by the dense stratum corneum requiring either an already damaged skin surface for the NPs absorption and delivery or the creation of micropores for the delivery of NPs ( ). Microneedles have also been employed for NP delivery because they can penetrate the epidermis and enter the dermis; these are typically patches with extremely fine needles loaded with NPs to painlessly penetrate the epidermis ( ; ; ). They are especially advantageous in cancer therapeutics because they can induce skin-homing immune cells and improve antitumor immunity ( ; ).
NP strategies for gene therapy, although beyond the scope of this review, have been extensively characterized, including for use in the treatment of solid tumors ( ; ; ; ; ). Briefly, NP-based carriers for siRNA, peptide-conjugated siRNA (peptide nucleic acid), gene, and other RNA interference have been used to leverage the capacity of NPs to protect such agents from hydrolytic and enzymatic cleavage ( ). The discussion of examples of particles for skin cancer treatment presented in the following section highlights recent works that go beyond encapsulation of a therapeutic with engineered properties that target the TME.

As previously mentioned, both topical and intratumoral delivery of NPs has been tried with lipid-based NPs ( ) ( ; ; ; ). For instance, resveratrol, which has shown in vitro and in vivo antioxidant and tumoricidal effects has shown limited clinical efficacy, most likely owing to its limited penetrance. and have shown that lipid-based NP encapsulation of resveratrol significantly improves its antitumor activity and improves skin penetration. have shown an engineered SLN to improve skin permeation of 5-fluorouracil and resveratrol, which have both been used clinically but with difficulty in penetrating the stratum corneum.
 Liposomes and LNPs have the capacity to incorporate several agents of different characteristics, including peptides and nucleic acids, with great stability. have employed conjugation of peptides to target tumor-associated antigens that are commonly expressed in human skin tumors. Cytokines, one of the first immunotherapies in cancer therapeutics, can also be delivered with decreased systemic toxicity in lipid-based NPs. When delivered as a systemic monotherapy, cytokines are significantly limited owing to facile degradation and dose-limiting toxicity and side effects. In a study by , LNPs encapsulating IL12 and IL27 cytokine mRNA were efficacious in low toxicity antitumor therapy against melanoma. Previously limited by the small therapeutic window, antitumoral cytokine IL-12 when paired with anti-inflammatory IL-27 led to significant antitumor responses in in vivo models without signs of systemic toxicity or inflammation. Finally, LNPs have also been used to show smaller segments of nucleic acids such as siRNA. aimed to overcome multidrug resistance and increase the therapeutic efficacy of paclitaxel, a well-studied chemotherapy agent, by codelivery with Bcl-2 siRNA. The encapsulation allowed for improved uptake of siRNA and showed cell cycle arrest after delivery of BCL-2 siRNA.
The capability of R 1,2-dioleoyl-3-trimethylammonium-propane as a carrier to deliver immunotherapy was studied by ( ). Using an LNP, modified viral or self-peptide Ags and melanoma-associated peptide Trp2 were loaded and delivered intratumorally in animal models of melanoma. One injection of formulated LNPs caused complete regression in various solid tumor models, including melanoma; there was significant induction of cytotoxic T cells to induce type I and type II IFN pathways to induce priming of antitumor immunity. By remodeling the TME and the immune microenvironment, they were able to achieve good combinatorial therapy and antitumor effect when LNP was administered with anti–PD-1.
Lipid-based NP surfaces are also often modified to improve tumor localization. studied the conjugation of hyaluronan (HA) to lipid-based NP. Effects of various low, medium, and high HA molecular weights conjugated to lipid NPs were studied to determine how the conjugation molecule characteristics would affect cell binding, immunogenicity, circulation time, and tumor localization. They had shown that small-molecular-weight hyaluronic acid was most effective in targeting tumors. HA-coated lipid NP showed high affinity toward HA receptors on tumor cells both in vitro and in vivo and against melanoma. Loading methotrexate into the HA lipid NPs showed enhanced therapeutic response in comparison with free methotrexate and lipid NP with methotrexate without HA conjugation.
Because of their synthetic versatility, high encapsulation efficiency, and stability, polymeric NPs have increased in popularity as new carriers for treatment of nonmelanoma and melanoma skin cancer ( ). have shown a polymeric NP with aldehyde modification on the surface that improved bioadhesion to the tumor matrix owing to formation of Schiff bonds. Camptothecin delivery through NPs increased intratumoral drug retention in addition to therapeutic efficacy against in vivo SCC: the aldehyde modification on the outer shell showed that bioadhesion to the tumor matrix significantly improved the antitumor effect and increased survival in preclinical model of SCC.
Coencapsulation of agents such as immunotherapy and chemotherapy is a common strategy in polymer NP drug delivery, providing a synergistic antitumor effect. Combining immunotherapy with chemotherapeutics can improve cancer therapy by providing a cell death signal, enhancing antitumor immunogenicity that is further potentiated with immune stimulation ( ; ; ). For instance, created a polymeric NP composed of PLGA to codeliver paclitaxel in conjunction with immunostimulant IL-2 to potentiate the immune tumor response in melanoma. Interestingly, coencapsulation of dual therapeutic methods significantly decreased tumor growth in comparison with two separate, individual NPs with encapsulated paclitaxel and IL-2. The cytokine responses significantly differed between single-drug and dual-drug encapsulation with a reduction of TGF-b and IL-10 levels and increased IFN-γ and IL-12 with combinatorial therapy compared with free agents and single-drug encapsulation. Coencapsulation showed therapeutic efficacy against metastatic melanoma spread with combinatorial NP with IL-2 and paclitaxel, outperforming free agents and single encapsulated NP agents.
PEGylation of NPs of different cores has also been employed to decrease early uptake and degradation. A study has shown that anchoring the surface of liposomes with PEGylation and conjugation with anti-CD137 can allow for rapid accumulation of the particles in tumors while decreasing systemic toxicity ( ). Although cytokine therapy with IL-2 had previously shown efficacy against several solid tumors, including melanoma, there was significant toxicity owing to cytokine storm through nonspecific, systemic lymphocyte stimulation. PEGylated liposomal delivery of CD137 and IL-2 fusion achieved significantly decreased tumor size and improved survival against in vivo melanoma and decreased metastatic melanoma spread.
PEGylation has been extensively employed for environment-responsive NP delivery. One study showed a TME-responsive PEGylated hyaluronic acid nanogel that would release drugs in hypoxia ( ). IL-12, a cytokine with antitumor activity and induction of innate and adaptive immunity through activation of NK cells and T cells and thus the release of IFN-γ, was encapsulated in this nanogel. Although a powerful immunostimulant with antitumor activity, systemic administration of IL-12 has been associated with significant side effects, which limits its current use in clinical settings. The release of particles and IL-12 in acidic and hypoxic conditions was demonstrated. In vivo studies showed the ability of the targeted delivery system to decrease systemic circulation and accumulation of IL-12 after administration, showing its antitumor efficacy and on-demand release in TME.
Metal-based NPs are often used for applications of photodynamic therapy (PDT) or PDT combined with chemotherapy in which NPs are injected or applied to the cutaneous site and irradiated with lasers locally ( ). In one application, Ag NPs were topically applied for PTT through a hydrogel patch to improve synthesis and bioadhesiveness ( ). For cutaneous skin malignancies, hydrogel patches have been effective for PTT delivery without necessitating intratumoral injection. A hydrogel film with BSA and silver NPs with low-dose laser irradiation provided effective energy for laser-induced cell death and impeded melanoma growth in vivo.
Thermo–chemotherapy delivery with mesoporous silica NPs has also been well-studied. However, given limitations with conventional metal NP related to nonspecific drug delivery, premature drug release, and lack of prolonged antitumor immunogenicity, pH-sensitive mesoporous silica-coated gold NPs were developed by . The group showed that pH-responsive, mesoporous silica-coated gold NPs loaded with doxorubicin can effectively deliver chemotherapy to acidic environments. When the tumor site was irradiated with a 655 nm laser at various time intervals and power densities, there was significant tumor growth inhibition with combination therapy, with both chemotherapy and laser irradiation significantly improving tumor growth inhibition in comparison with single therapy. This combination therapy showed effective induction of immunogenic cell death, maturation of antigen-presenting cells, and infiltration into primary and metastatic melanoma.
Organically derived NP cancer therapy has rapidly progressed in the last decade ( ). These are often used for the loading of immunotherapies because they potentiate well with virus-like NPs or those derived from vesicles/membranes. Indeed, there have already been clinical phase I/II studies in patients with stage II–IV melanoma to observe the effect of virus-like NPs loaded with type A CpG. In these studies, patients were injected with particles as vaccinations, either subcutaneously or intradermally, and induction of type I IFN along with maturation of dendritic cells and cytotoxic T-cell differentiation was studied ( ; ). Yeast, a common fungus with shown biosafety, has been studied as a potential immunotherapy for solid tumors. showed yeast cell wall–derived NPs of various sizes that had therapeutic efficiency for antitumor immune induction, antitumor efficacy in retardation of tumor growth, and treatment against metastatic melanoma. Notably, they showed that there was a significant improvement in antitumor efficacy with decreased NP size, with the smallest NPs (less than 100 nm) outperforming the same yeast-derived NPs but at larger sizes. In addition, in tumor and tumor-draining lymph node immune microenvironment studies, the yeast NPs were able to provide excellent antitumor efficiency in mice with cutaneous and metastatic melanoma when combined with PD-L1 blockade.

## Future Perspectives
In this review, the types of NPs that have been used or are being developed for therapeutic delivery in both skin cancer prevention and treatment applications were discussed. In the recent decade, there have been increasingly advanced and sophisticated NP designs with improved therapeutic loading and study of particle design for smart delivery. In skin cancer studies, NP encapsulation of therapeutics has all shown improved efficacy compared with free agents, with a significant decrease in systemic and local toxicity.
A major next step is to leverage the efficient loading capacity and controlled release for combinatorial therapeutic encapsulation. In skin cancer prevention applications, there is evidence showing the improved stability of the sun-protective agents with the addition of antioxidants. This combination approach is also important in the context of skin cancer. Combining immune stimulating agents with chemotherapeutics should lead to regulation and improvement of both therapies’ effectiveness, with a reduction in toxicity. Current studies that leverage both immunotherapy and chemotherapy in NP have shown significant antitumor efficacy in decreasing metastatic spread. Dual delivery allows a kill signal, derived from the chemotherapy’s local tumor cell destruction, to prime the immune cells that are supplemented by immunotherapy for anticancer immunity. In addition, NPs responsive to external and internal stimuli such as lasers, pH changes, and surface interaction are promising in the way they would better target tumors even in metastasis while sparing the healthy tissue.
However, although preclinical research on NP use has expanded dramatically, its use in clinical settings remains limited, and clinical use requires additional studies on patient response to NP delivery ( ). Obstacles also exist in manufacturing NPs at a commercial scale, where particular attention needs to be given to NP reproducibility and stability in clinical use. Still, even with these challenges, NP therapeutic delivery for skin cancer prevention and treatment has significant promise to improve skin cancer incidence and survivability, widening the populations that may benefit from previously less optimized therapeutics.
