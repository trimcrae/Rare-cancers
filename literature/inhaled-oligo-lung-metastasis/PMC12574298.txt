## Abstract

## Background

Nanoparticles (NPs) have emerged as highly efficient drug delivery vehicles. NPs are characterised by their ability to safeguard drugs, enhance stability, prolong durability, and facilitate targeted tissue delivery. Moreover, NPs can be customised to either stimulate or suppress immune responses while evading immune detection.

## Main body
An increasing body of research demonstrated the therapeutic advantages of delivering various substances via surface-engineered NPs particularly in targeting immune cells. A broad spectrum of nanomaterials has been recognised for their superior ability to elicit immune responses, thereby enhancing disease prevention and therapeutic outcomes in vivo. In this comprehensive review, we discuss the impact of bioresponsive NPs on immunostimulation, immunomodulation, and immunosuppression. Particular focus is placed on how the physiochemical properties of these NPs influence the interaction between immune cells and host tissues, thereby achieving the desired immune regulation to combat various human diseases while reducing immunotoxicity. Additionally, we examined immune-inspired and immune-mediated strategies for the targeted delivery of NPs to specific sites within the body. Finally, we highlight the importance of understanding and focusing on immune cell trafficking mechanisms, as deeper insights into these processes may guide the rational design and fabrication of NPs capable of specifically targeting altered immune pathways under pathological conditions. This review offers an in-depth understanding of the diverse roles of bioresponsive NPs in immune regulation and targeted drug delivery, aiming to advance therapeutic strategies for a wide range of human diseases.

## Conclusion
Nanomaterials possess significant immunomodulatory capabilities, enhancing both disease prevention and therapeutic efficacy in vivo, yet their clinical translation is hindered by complex fabrication methods and high production costs. Future efforts must focus on simplifying synthesis and improving cost-efficiency to facilitate their adoption in clinical practice.

## Background
The immune system provides a rapid and specific primary defence mechanism that prevents infections caused by pathogens, maintains the balance of symbiotic commensal flora, resolves sterile inflammation, and prevents tissue destruction, all while preserving the integrity of normal host tissue physiology [ – ]. Immune cells play a critical role in tissue restoration and offer immune memory-related long-term protection. Beyond protection from pathogens, the immune system is implicated in allergies, autoimmune diseases, graft rejection and regenerative processes, tumour surveillance, pathogenesis and progression, tissue repair, and wound healing. When the innate immune response is prompt and transient, recovery of health occurs swiftly. However, a dysregulated response can lead to exaggerated inflammatory conditions, which can contribute to severe acute illness, promote the development of long-term chronic complications and increase the risk of mortality [ – ]. Thus, modulating the immune system and its responses provides the therapeutic potential for addressing a broad spectrum of diseases [ – ].

The process of stimulating or augmenting the immune system to combat pathological conditions is referred to as immunotherapy [ ]. The capacity to regulate innate or adaptive immune responses either by suppression or stimulation, depending on the clinical requirement, is referred to as immunomodulation. This strategy has demonstrated significant therapeutic potential in various contexts, including the prevention and treatment of infections, the suppression of autoimmune and inflammatory disorders, and the activation of antitumour immunity in cancer patients [ – ]. Biologics with immunomodulatory properties achieve therapeutic effects by altering or leveraging immune responses and have demonstrated efficacy in treating various complex pathological conditions [ ]. A significant proportion of pharmaceuticals approved in the past decade are immunomodulatory biologics, which can be classified into three main categories based on their mechanism of action. The first category includes cytokine-based immunomodulatory biologics, which mimic, replace, or enhance endogenous cytokines, such as granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) and interferon-β (IFN-β), and are typically recombinant human proteins. The second category consists of antibody-based triggering biologics that bind to specific targets and activate immune cell signaling pathways, exemplified by rituximab (Rituxan/MabThera; Biogen Idec/Genentech/Roche) and muromonab-CD3. The third category involves blocking immunomodulatory biologics, which inhibit the function of immune-relevant targets, such as etanercept (Enbrel; Amgen/Pfizer) and omalizumab (Zolair; Genentech/Novartis) [ ]. These immunologics have revolutionised the therapy for various diseases, including cancer and autoimmune diseases [ ]. However, systemic immunomodulation carries the risk of potentially life-threatening adverse effects, including severe infections, malignancies, cytokine release syndrome, anaphylaxis, hypersensitivity, and immunogenicity [ , – ]. For instance, cancer immune checkpoint blockade therapy stimulates the activation and proliferation of T-cells but can also lead to diverse immune-related adverse events, including encephalitis, hepatitis, myocarditis, and pneumonitis [ ]. Similarly, administration of immunosuppressive drugs can extend allograft survival but significantly escalate the risk of opportunistic infections and malignancies [ ]. Nevertheless, developing novel and safer immunomodulatory biologics is imperative due to existing challenges, such as adverse reactions and therapy-induced immune exaggeration.

Nanoparticles (NPs) have gained recognition as highly effective platforms for drug delivery. They offer advantages such as protecting therapeutic agents, improving their stability, extending their durability, and enabling precise delivery to target tissues [ – ]. Moreover, “smart” or “stimuli responsive” or “bioresponsive” nanocarriers sensitive to one or more physiological signals or pathological abnormalities and respond or interact with them accordingly represent promising therapeutic systems for advancing next-generation precision medicine [ , ]. These intelligent nanomaterials are used for a range of biomedical applications, including controlled and targeted drug delivery, diagnostics, medical devices, regenerative medicine, and tissue engineering. Deviations in physiological parameters often serve as critical indicators of various diseases, including cancer, autoimmune disorders, degenerative conditions, infections, and cardiovascular diseases, making them valuable targets in the development of bioresponsive materials [ ]. These triggers can generally be divided into three major categories: organ-level triggers, triggers associated with pathological conditions, and triggers specific to cellular compartments. Stimuli employed for biomedical applications encompass internal triggers, such as pH, redox balance, specific or dysregulated enzymes, glucose levels, adenosine triphosphate (ATP) concentration, temperature, and shear force, as well as external triggers, including light, radiation, and sound [ , – ]. These smart carriers enhance bioactivity, stability, and controlled drug release kinetics, offering improved therapeutic efficacy and reduced side effects compared to traditional approaches by delivering drugs in response to specific pathophysiological stimuli [ , ].
In this review, we comprehensively examine recent advancements in bioresponsive nanomaterial-based strategies for modulating the spatiotemporal behaviour of immune cells as a therapeutic approach to manage chronic inflammatory diseases. Specifically, we discuss the diverse stimuli employed to direct these nanomaterials to inflamed tissue sites, with an emphasis on the design and engineering of nanomaterial constructs, as well as their pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles. Further, we explore the immunomodulatory characteristics inherent to these materials, discussing how they interact with the immune system to influence cellular activity and promote the resolution of inflammation. This review also provides in-depth details about the clinical trials and safety issues of these bioresponsive nanomaterials. In addition, this review provides an overview of the existing challenges that currently hinder the clinical application of these bioresponsive systems. Finally, we discuss future research directions and potential strategies to overcome these barriers, to accelerate the clinical translation of bioresponsive nanomaterial-based immunotherapies. Despite the abundance of reviews on bioresponsive NPs, a critical gap remains in linking their design principles to clinical translation. This review uniquely integrates emerging NPs engineering strategies with immune cell-targeted approaches, offering a fresh perspective on optimising therapeutic efficacy while minimising adverse effects. By addressing key challenges in NPs-mediated immunotherapy, including biodistribution, pharmacokinetics, and immune modulation, we provide a comprehensive roadmap for advancing nanomedicine toward clinical applications. This focus on translational potential and rational NPs design to appropriately target immune cells distinguishes this review from existing literature.

## From toxicity to precision: why NPs are the future of immunotherapy
Significant advancements have been made in the development of antibody-based and antibody-conjugated therapies, with clinical trials demonstrating their remarkable efficacy as both monotherapies and in combination with other treatments [ , ]. The approval of Provenge (sipuleucel-T) by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in April 2010 marked the first recognised success of active immunotherapy as an effective strategy, specifically for patients with advanced castrate-resistant prostate cancer [ , ]. The most frequently reported adverse events associated with Provenge included chills (54.1% vs. 12.5%), pyrexia (29.3% vs. 13.7%), headache (16.0% vs. 4.8%), influenza-like symptoms (9.8% vs. 3.6%), hypertension (7.4% vs. 3.0%), and excessive sweating (5.3% vs. 0.6%) [ ].
A major breakthrough occurred in 2011 when the FDA approved ipilimumab, an immune checkpoint inhibitor, for the treatment of unresectable metastatic melanoma, marking a pivotal moment in cancer immunotherapy [ , ]. The subsequent discovery of the programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) immune checkpoint pathway further transformed the field and remains one of the most successful immunotherapy strategies to date [ ].
Following this, the FDA has approved several monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), including atezolizumab, avelumab, cemiplimab, dostarlimab, durvalumab, nivolumab, and pembrolizumab, for the treatment of a wide range of cancers, such as breast, cervical, colorectal, endometrial, oesophageal, gastric, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, liver, lung, and urothelial cancers [ , ]. More recently, the FDA authorised the use of relatlimab, an immune checkpoint inhibitor targeting lymphocyte activation gene-3, making it the third checkpoint inhibitor available in clinical settings after cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) and PD-1 inhibitors [ , ]. While these immunotherapy advancements bring renewed “cure” hope for cancer patients and their families, the emergence of immune-related adverse events (irAEs) poses significant challenges to their clinical success. These adverse effects result from a complex interplay of intrinsic and extrinsic patient-specific factors, highlighting the need for continued research to improve safety and efficacy [ , ]. Indeed, when activated, the immune system exhibits remarkable potency by not only eliminating cancerous cells effectively but also potentially triggering long-term autoimmune responses. The irAEs, such as colitis, cytokine storm, myocarditis, pneumonitis, systemic toxicity, and other inflammatory complications, are frequently observed with these therapeutics [ – ]. These conditions can be severe, posing life-threatening risks and significantly diminishing patients’ quality of life. Systemic delivery of immunotherapeutic agents may disrupt immune homeostasis in non-target tissues, leading to unintended adverse effects [ , ]. Targeted drug delivery strategies offer a solution by concentrating immunostimulatory agents at specific cells or tissues, thereby reducing widespread distribution and minimising off-target toxicity. Additionally, the controlled release of therapeutic agents over an extended period allows for sustained local engagement with the target, preventing rapid saturation and unintended systemic exposure [ , ]. Recent advancements in nanotechnology and bioengineering have introduced innovative strategies to enhance the safety and effectiveness of cancer immunotherapy. Stimuli-responsive biomaterials enable a multifaceted approach to tumour treatment by improving immunotherapy responses such as inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD), modulating the tumour microenvironment (TME), and enhancing antigen presentation [ , ]. Additionally, these technologies help mitigate adverse effects by reducing immune-related toxicities associated with systemic administration, expanding delivery routes, and broadening the scope of immune checkpoint blockade therapies [ ]. Further details on these studies and their findings are provided in the following sections.

## Properties of materials used in bioresponsive nanoformulations
The properties of bioactive materials used in nanotherapeutics are of paramount significance, especially their purposive applications. These materials are primarily influenced by their physical structure, surface properties, nanotopography, and the controlled release of bioactive ions or molecules [ ]. Various materials are employed in the creation of bioactive NPs, including bio-ceramics like hydroxyapatite, polymer-nano-ceramic composites, and bioactive glass [ – ]. Studies have shown that incorporating acids like sialic acid (SA) into nanoformulations can significantly enhance the stability and longevity of these particles, even in the challenging physiological environment of the human body. These nanoformulations not only improve the durability of the particles but also play a crucial role in targeted drug delivery. Utilising redox-sensitive mechanisms enables the controlled release of therapeutic proteins, ensuring that the drugs reach the cancer cells more effectively. This targeted approach leads to better treatment outcomes, particularly in cancer therapies, by maximising efficacy and minimising side effects [ ]. Some specific sets of polymers, including poly lactic-co-glycolic acid (PLGA), are widely used in biomedical engineering because of their high plasticity and biocompatibility [ , ]. Various biomaterials, including polysaccharides like hyaluronic acid, chondroitin sulphate, and chitosan, are utilised in NP-based drug delivery systems (DDSs) due to their aptness to target specific receptors, control drug release, and penetrate tissues. Additionally, enzyme-responsive materials and peptide-functionalised nanovesicles, such as elastin-b-collagen-like peptides and integrin-targeting peptides, are being developed to enhance targeting and drug delivery to specific tissues, including tumours, through receptor-mediated and tissue-specific interactions [ ]. Another study investigated the synthesis of silver and gold NPs with biomolecules like tryptophan, tyrosine, curcumin, and epigallocatechin gallate. These biomaterials acted as reducing and stabilising agents, forming a surface corona on the NPs. The engineered surfaces exhibited free radical scavenging abilities and enzyme-mimic activities while demonstrating low cytotoxicity and genotoxicity in biological applications [ ].

## Types of bioresponsive NPs
Besides the diverse range of materials, there exists a wide spectrum of bioresponsive NPs. These include pH-responsive, enzyme-responsive, redox-responsive, temperature-responsive, magnetically-responsive, light-responsive, glucose-responsive, biomarker-responsive, and ultrasound-responsive NPs [ , , – ]. A thematic study presented pH-responsive, biodegradable hybrid NPs with a PLGA core and a bovine serum albumin shell, functionalised with the acidity-triggered rational membrane peptide to target cancer cells in acidic TME. These NPs effectively released chemotherapeutics within cancer cells, demonstrating potent anticancer activity without harming healthy tissue, making them a promising platform for targeted drug delivery [ ]. Similarly, another study has reported the development of pH-bioresponsive fluorinated peptide dendron-functionalised dextran NPs with core–shell architecture, demonstrating controlled, pH-dependent drug release with great biocompatibility. The NPs’ disassembly in acidic environments enabled targeted, on-demand intracellular drug delivery, highlighting their potential for advanced therapeutic applications [ ]. In another promising study, a temperature-responsive minoxidil NP-loaded microneedle system was developed that enhanced minoxidil solubility, stability, and skin absorption, significantly promoting hair regrowth with reduced dosage and side effects. The system’s switchable adhesion allowed for precise, on-demand application, improving both efficacy and ease of use [ ]. Another research finding elucidated NPs formed by encapsulating cytotoxic RNase A within disulphide-hybridised silica material (BS-NPs) and surface-imprinted with SA for tumour-targeting capabilities. It enabled redox-responsive protein release, enhancing cancer cell specificity and therapeutic efficacy [ ].
In another investigation, ultrasound-responsive NPs were developed for the treatment of atherosclerosis. Precisely, polybutylcyanoacrylate-based NPs, CDNPs, loaded with 2-hydroxypropyl-beta-cyclodextrin (CD), were designed for enhanced cholesterol crystal dissolution and atherosclerosis treatment. CDNP was demonstrated to improve CD delivery and cellular uptake and offered ultrasound-triggered dual imaging, elucidating therapeutic potential [ ]. Another intricate study reported a glucose-responsive nano system enabling rapid insulin release in response to elevated glucose levels, mimicking the body’s natural response, while the protective microgels provided long-lasting glycaemic control. This innovation holds the potential to minimise hypoglycaemic events and extend insulin efficacy, improving diabetes management [ ]. Similarly, elaborative research on enzyme-responsive NPs constructed from sulphate-β-cyclodextrin (SCD) and protamine showed disassembly specifically in the presence of trypsin, allowing controlled release of encapsulated substances. This system offered stable encapsulation and targeted release at sites with elevated trypsin activity [ ]. Along with single responsive NPs, there are advanced dual bioresponsive NPs as well. For instance, a study presented a dual-responsive nanosystem named BM@EP, designed for the detection and treatment of ulcerative colitis (UC), utilising a pH- and reactive oxygen species (ROS)-sensitive mechanism to release both a therapeutic flavonoid dihydromyricetin and a chromophore for near-infrared (NIR)-II imaging, enabling targeted drug delivery to inflamed colon tissue and facilitating real-time diagnosis and monitoring of UC progression [ ].

## Size
The bioactivity of nanomaterials is significantly influenced by particle size, with smaller NPs having larger surface areas, which enhances their biological interactions [ , ]. An investigation showed that 20 nm silver NPs exhibited greater cytotoxicity, particularly in metabolic activity and membrane damage, compared to larger particles. Additionally, these NPs exhibited greater cytotoxicity toward fibroblasts compared to silver ions highlighting the size- and cell-type-dependent nature of silver NP toxicity [ , ]. Yet another intricate study observed that nano-hydroxyapatite (nano-HAP) particles, particularly those with a diameter of around 20 nm (np20), exhibited superior effects on human osteoblast-like MG-63 cells compared to larger nano-HAP (np80) and micro-sized HAP (m-HAP). The np20 particles demonstrated the most effective enhancement of cell proliferation and suppression of cell apoptosis. These findings shed light on the usage of well-controlled, smaller nano-HAP particles as promising candidates for future biomedical applications in bone tissue engineering [ ]. Considering how hydrodynamic size reflects the particle behaviour in biological systems, impacting factors like circulation time in the bloodstream, cellular uptake, and clearance from the body, a study separately reported the core size and hydrodynamic size of a redox-responsive NPs. The particles demonstrated a hydrodynamic size of ~ 80 nm, verified by dynamic light scattering analysis, and a core diameter of ~ 37-38 nm, as shown by transmission electron microscopy [ ].
The size of nanomaterials plays a crucial role in antigen retention and immune cells activation [ ]. At physiological temperature (37 °C), thermally responsive polymers loaded with antigens can self-assemble into NPs with tunable sizes [ ]. By optimising NPs size, antigen-presenting cells (APCs) in lymph nodes efficiently internalise them, leading to enhanced vaccine immunogenicity. Nanomaterials smaller than 5 nm rapidly diffuse into the bloodstream, resulting in entry to systemic circulation [ ]. In contrast, NPs within the 20–100 nm range demonstrate efficient drainage into lymph nodes, where they are effectively captured by resident APCs, facilitating immune activation [ ]. NPs smaller than 30 nm exhibit reduced opsonin binding due to spatial limitations, potentially limiting complement activation. Guo and colleagues showed that engineered sub-30 nm RNA polygons with 10-20 nm SEQ extensions significantly enhanced immunomodulatory activity compared to 7.1 nm counterparts (p < 0.05) [ ]. This finding is important for the rational design of RNA-based immunotherapeutics, balancing immune activation with reduced systemic clearance or adverse inflammatory responses. Different immune cells exhibit size-dependent preferences for NP uptake. Macrophages, due to their high phagocytic activity, efficiently internalise particles ranging from 2 to 3 μm in diameter. In contrast, other immune cells preferentially uptake nanoscale particles rather than microparticles, highlighting the importance of size optimisation in nanomaterial design for immune modulation [ ]. These studies indicate that the size of NPs affects their immune response and distribution in the body. Smaller NPs (< 5 nm) circulate in the bloodstream, while those in the 20-100 nm range efficiently reach lymph nodes for immune activation.

## Shape
Depending on their physicochemical properties and synthesis conditions, nanocompounds adopt diverse morphologies, ranging from circular to oval and, in some cases, irregular shapes [ , ]. In an interesting exploration, bioresponsive NPs with a disulphide-cross-linked hyperbranched polyethyleneimine core and hyaluronic acid shell was designed for ROS sensitive drug release to synergistically inhibit tumour growth and metastasis by targeting pyruvate metabolism. Morphologically, NPs maintain a constant size and uniform dispersion under physiological conditions. However, in the presence of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and glutathione (GSH), mimicking the TME, significant size increases, irregular shapes, blurred edges, and eventual disintegration with scattered fragments and aggregation were observed due to the cleavage of the ROS- and reduction-sensitive cross-linkers [ ].
Studies reflects a crucial aspect of engineered micelle, with varying shapes and sizes based on the mode and sites of action [ , ]. These NPs can essentially form various shapes, including spherical, cylindrical (or worm-like), vesicular (planar bilayers), and inverted micelles, depending on the packing parameter influenced by the hydrophilic head group area and the hydrophobic tail’s length and volume [ – ]. Notably, some studies explore NPs that can dynamically alter their shape in response to specific physiological conditions. For instance, dual-responsive systems have been designed to shift from spherical NPs to nanofibers upon enzymatic cleavage, enhancing tumour penetration and therapeutic efficacy through shape transformation and charge reversal mechanisms [ ]. There are photo or light-responsive NPs as well, which are crystalline and cubic in shape. One crucial factor of such gold azobenzene NPs is their ability to shift from base-centred cubic to face-centred cubic based on the photoisomerisation property [ ].

## Physicochemical properties of bioresponsive NPs
Alongside size, varying shapes, and materials used, physicochemical properties of the elements or compounds involved in the making of these bioresponsive NPs make them diverse and different from each other and from other conventional types of NPs. For instance, a redox-responsive NP, SA-imprinted RNase A@BS-NP, was designed with a redox-sensitive disulphide (S–S) bond within its silica framework, enabling controlled degradation under TME conditions. Along with that, this NP showed stability and structural integrity in PBS buffer (pH 7.4) but underwent structural disintegration in the presence of GSH, leading to the release of encapsulated RNase A. Since its core was imprinted with SA, it allowed selective targeting of tumour cells, while its high encapsulation efficiency (~ 64%) ensured effective protein delivery. Additionally, it also exhibited excellent stability in physiological conditions but degraded efficiently under redox conditions, confirming its biodegradability and potential for in vivo clearance. It was reported that released RNase A retained ~ 79% of its enzymatic activity, emphasising the preservation of protein functionality during encapsulation and release [ ]. In yet another examination, ultrasound-responsive theranostic NPs, referred as CDNPs, were designed by harnessing the duality in functionality by combining air-trapping for ultrasound imaging, controlled drug release, and efficient plaque targeting, which gave them significant advantages over traditional NPs. These CDNPs possessed unique physicochemical properties that made them highly effective as ultrasound-responsive carriers. These NPs incorporated air in their core during synthesis, which enhanced their echogenicity, making them suitable for ultrasound imaging. Additionally, the biodegradable PBCA matrix provided a platform for controlled, sustained release of CD, which helped to dissolve cholesterol crystals and reduce plaque size over time [ ].
A similarly unique thematic analysis reported that acetal-derivatised dextran NPs (Ac-dex NPs) encapsulated within alginate microgels exhibit unique physicochemical properties that enable rapid, glucose-triggered insulin release and sustained delivery. The acetal modification of dextran creates pH-sensitive bonds, which degrade under acidic conditions, releasing the encapsulated insulin, glucose oxidase (GOx), and catalase. When glucose levels rise, GOx converts glucose to gluconic acid, lowering the local pH and triggering the breakdown of the NPs, thus ensuring a controlled release of insulin [ ]. In a recent study, temperature-responsive microneedles incorporated with minoxidil NP were developed. The NPs benefited from the properties of hyaluronic acid, a biocompatible and hydrophilic polymer, ensuring that the microneedles dissolved upon contact with the skin, releasing the minoxidil in a controlled manner. Additionally, the integration of a temperature-responsive adhesion ring provided a switchable, reversible adhesive mechanism, ensuring robust attachment to the skin and prolonged retention of the microneedles in place, particularly in challenging areas like the scalp. This thermally controlled adhesion system allowed for secure application and easy removal, further enhancing the practical usability of the microneedles [ ].
Interestingly, an enzyme-responsive NP was also been developed by the non-covalent assembly of SCD and protamine, both of which are biocompatible. The SCD is a cyclic polysaccharide that is water-soluble and capable of encapsulating molecules within its hydrophobic cavity, making it ideal for constructing nanostructured functional materials [ ]. Protamine, a cationic natural protein, interacts with the polyanionic SCD to create stable NPs. These NPs specifically disassemble in the presence of the enzyme trypsin, which cleaves the peptide bonds in protamine, leading to NP disruption and controlled release of the encapsulated drug. This enzyme-triggered disassembly offers targeted drug delivery at sites where trypsin is overexpressed, as in certain pathological conditions [ ]. Another compelling study has reported light-sensitive NPs formed by coating gold NPs with azobenzene-modified deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) (azoDNA). The azobenzene molecules within the DNA enabled the NPs to undergo reversible assembly and disassembly in response to light. When exposed to UV light, the azobenzene switched from a trans- to cis-state, destabilising DNA bonds and causing the particles to dissociate. Under visible light, the reverse process occurred, allowing the NPs to reassemble. This mechanism allows for precise control over the NP crystal formation and disassembly through light activation [ ]. To add further to the innovation, a dual-responsive NP with diagnosis and monitoring abilities in UC was reported. It was pH-responsive due to its enteric coating (Eudragit S100 and PLGA), which dissolved in the alkaline environment of the colon, ensuring site-specific release. Additionally, its ROS-responsive nature comes from the boronate ester bond linking the chromophore and the drug, which breaks down in the presence of high levels of H2O2, common in inflamed tissues. This dual responsiveness allows for controlled drug release and enhanced imaging capabilities in the inflamed colon [ ].

## Chemical strategies and mechanisms of bioresponsive NPs
The stimuli utilised for developing these advanced NPs are categorised as either endogenous or exogenous. Endogenous stimuli include variations in enzymatic activity, hypoxia, ROS, GSH, pH fluctuations, and temperature shifts, whereas exogenous stimuli encompass magnetic fields, light exposure, thermal changes, and ultrasound waves [ , , ]. Figure illustrates the type of linkers and polymers responsible for sensitising biological signals. The responsiveness of these NPs is achieved through diverse chemical mechanisms and reactions, enabling controlled drug release at target sites while minimising systemic toxicity. For instance, enzyme-responsive nanocarriers exploit the overexpression of specific enzymes in pathological tissues, such as tumours, where enzymes like phospholipases, phosphatases, and oxidoreductases are upregulated. Hydrolases, including caspases, cathepsins, β-galactosidase, hyaluronidases, mannosidase, phospholipases (A2 and C), phosphatases, proteases, and oxidoreductases are known to be overexpressed in pathological conditions such as atherosclerosis, cancer, UC, and pulmonary disorders. These enzymes catalyse the cleavage of specific substrate linkages, facilitating the targeted release of therapeutic agents [ – ]. Moreover, genes encoding reductase enzymes, such as azoreductase, nitroreductase, and quinone dehydrogenase, are typically upregulated in hypoxic tissues. These enzymes mediate the reduction of functional groups, including azo bonds, nitro groups, and quinones. This biochemical feature has been extensively leveraged to design hypoxia-sensitive nanocarriers, offering therapeutic potential for the treatment of diabetes, hypoxic tumours, and ischemic stroke [ – ].
 Building upon the aforementioned mechanisms, redox reactions play a crucial role in facilitating drug release from nanocarriers through two primary pathways. The first mechanism involves oxidation reactions that enhance the hydrophilicity of nanocarrier components, including selenoethers, thioethers, telluroethers, and ferrocenes, thereby promoting drug release [ – ]. The second mechanism relies on bond cleavage triggered by either ROS or GSH. Recent investigations have explored the cleavage properties of diselenides and ditellurides, demonstrating their potential in controlled drug delivery [ , – , , ]. The reductive sensitivity of these chalcogen-based linkages follows a specific hierarchy: disulphide > diselenide > thioether > selenoether, while their oxidative sensitivity exhibits the inverse trend. Apart from these chalcogen-containing groups, ROS can also induce the cleavage of other functional moieties, such as thioketals, arylboronic esters, and proline bonds [ , , ]. These redox-responsive systems offer precise control over drug release, contributing to the development of advanced nanomedicines for targeted therapeutic applications.
Expanding on the previously discussed redox-responsive mechanisms, pH-sensitive drug release systems also play a pivotal role in targeted nanomedicine. There are four primary mechanisms by which pathological pH variations trigger drug release from nanosystems: covalent bond cleavage, coordination bond disruption, inorganic material degradation, and protonation-induced physicochemical changes [ , – ]. Hydrolysis reactions are significantly accelerated under acidic conditions, making the low pH environment characteristic of inflammatory lesions and tumour tissues a valuable stimulus for controlled drug release. Acidic pH facilitates the hydrolytic cleavage of various chemical bonds, including acetal/ketal, ester, hexahydrobenzoic amide, hydrazone, imine, maleic amide, oxime, Schiff base, and β-thiopropionate linkages, thereby enabling site-specific drug release [ , – ]. Additionally, low pH disrupts certain metal–ligand coordination bonds due to competition between protons and metal ions. This principle is commonly exploited in metallo organic framework-based NPs, where a reduction in coordination number destabilises the structure, leading to the release of encapsulated therapeutics [ – ]. Similarly, the acid-induced degradation of inorganic compounds, such as calcium phosphate and zinc oxide, enhances drug release by breaking down structural gatekeepers. Another notable mechanism involves the decomposition of ammonium bicarbonate under acidic conditions, which generates carbon dioxide pressure, causing disruption of polymeric nanocarriers and subsequent payload release [ – ]. Moreover, protonation at acidic pH induces alterations in surface charge, increasing zeta potential and modifying hydrophilicity. These changes contribute to nanocarrier destabilisation and facilitate drug release. DDSs utilising protonation as a triggering mechanism typically incorporate functional groups with pKa values in the range of 4.5–7.4. Examples of such functional groups include carboxylates, amines, imidazole, pyridine, poly(L-lysine), poly(L-histidine), and polysaccharide derivatives, all of which enhance the responsiveness of nanocarriers to pH fluctuations in pathological environments [ – ].
In addition to internal stimuli, external stimuli are extensively utilised to achieve efficient and controlled drug release. One such exogenous stimulus is the application of a magnetic field, which serves as a non-invasive and remotely controllable trigger. Magnetic sensitivity enables the precise guidance of nanocarriers to specific target sites through static magnetic fields and facilitates localised hyperthermia using alternating magnetic fields. Static fields, applied externally or through surgically implanted magnets, promote the accumulation of DDSs at the desired location. In contrast, alternating magnetic fields induce oscillatory motion, and when applied at high frequencies, they generate heat and vibrations that destabilise nanocarriers, thereby enhancing payload release [ – ]. The efficacy of magnetic NPs (MNPs) in biomedical applications is highly dependent on their size (typically 10–50 nm) and geometric configuration [ ].
Another extensively explored exogenous stimulus for drug release activation is photoirradiation, which represents one of the most dynamic areas of research in nanomedicine. A key advantage of photo-responsive nanocarriers is their rapid drug-release kinetics. Unlike other stimuli-responsive systems, such as pH- or redox-sensitive nanocarriers, which typically exhibit response times on the scale of hours, photo-triggered systems can release their payload within seconds to minutes [ , ]. The development of photo-sensitive nanocarriers primarily relies on three major strategies: (i) photo-induced chemical modifications, including covalent bond cleavage and isomerisation, (ii) photodynamic effects, and (iii) photothermal effects [ ]. Several functional moieties have been identified as photo-responsive, including o-alkylated aryl ketones, coumarin derivatives, o-nitrobenzyl derivatives, and pyrene derivatives, all of which have been extensively investigated for their potential in light-triggered drug delivery applications [ – ].
Thermo-responsive polymers are among the most extensively studied strategies for imparting temperature sensitivity to DDSs. These systems primarily utilise polymers with lower critical solution temperatures (LCST) or upper critical solution temperatures (UCST) slightly above physiological temperature. Notable examples include LCST polymers such as poly(N-isopropylacrylamide), poly(N-vinyl caprolactam), and Pluronics, which have been widely investigated [ – , , ]. Beyond synthetic polymers, thermo-responsive biopolymers and peptides have also been developed, offering superior biocompatibility and biodegradability [ , , ].
Another exogenous stimulus employed in bioresponsive DDSs is ultrasound, which has long been utilised in biomedical imaging and recently showed promise as therapeutic strategy [ – ]. High-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU, 0.8–3.5 MHz) serves as a powerful tool for this purpose [ ]. The two primary mechanisms underlying ultrasound-induced tissue damage include the conversion of mechanical energy into heat and inertial cavitation [ ]. As pressure waves travel through biological tissues, they induce compression and rarefaction, leading to cavitation due to medium evaporation during rarefaction is followed by rapid collapse of microbubbles during compression. This effect is further intensified by ultrasound-induced hyperthermia, enhancing therapeutic efficacy [ , ].
These stimuli-responsive NPs, activated by endogenous and exogenous triggers, have demonstrated efficacy in treating various diseases both in vitro and in vivo, including infections, arthritis, bone disorders, cardiovascular diseases, cancer, diabetes, and gastrointestinal conditions. Additionally, they show potential in treating kidney, liver, and lung diseases, as well as promoting wound healing. Their ability to enhance targeted drug delivery and minimise side effects makes them promising candidates for immunotherapy. The following section discusses the advantages and limitations of different stimuli-responsive NPs in immunotherapeutic applications.

## Bioresponsive nanomaterials in immunomodulation
Resistance to immunotherapy remains a major obstacle in pharmacology, highlighting the need for innovative approaches to improve therapeutic outcomes [ , ]. ICD, encompassing processes such as necroptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis, facilitates the release of tumour-associated antigens and bioactive molecules, which can amplify host immune responses and help overcome immunotherapy resistance [ ]. NPs, known for their biocompatibility and immunomodulatory capabilities, are emerging as effective carriers for delivering ICD-inducing agents and immune-stimulatory adjuvants. These NPs enhance the infiltration of immune cells into tumours, thereby boosting the effectiveness of immunotherapy [ ]. This section reviews the role of bioresponsive NPs in modulating immune cells to address chronic inflammatory diseases. ## Targeting dendritic cells (DCs)/T-cells axis
DCs are specialised APCs with a unique capacity to capture and process antigens from damaged or infected peripheral tissues, including the bloodstream, and present them to resting lymphocytes, particularly naïve T-cells, in draining lymph nodes [ , ]. Serving as key mediators between innate and adaptive immunity, DCs can either activate or suppress immune responses [ , ]. Present across virtually all tissues, including blood, DCs of various phenotypes act as sentinel cells and provide both soluble and surface-bound signals that guide T-cell differentiation. The controlled regulation of T-cell activation and differentiation by DCs ensures a balanced immune response [ – ]. To achieve biologically meaningful activation of T-cells, DCs must undergo maturation and exhibit high expression levels of cell-surface major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules, along with other costimulatory molecules [ , ]. Therefore, targeting the DCs/T-cells axis represents a promising immunotherapeutic approach for managing various inflammatory diseases [ ].
NPs responsive to biological stimuli are recognised for their ability to selectively target and promote anti-inflammatory immune responses and/or induce ICD in damaged or dysfunctional target cells mediated through the DCs/T-cells axis (Fig. ). For example, cathepsin B-sensitive polymeric nano-proteolysis targeting chimaeras (nano-PROTACs) were designed to proteolytically degrade indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), an enzyme overexpressed in tumour cells that depletes tryptophan by converting it to kynurenine [ ]. This pathway contributes to DC dysfunction and suppression of T-cell responses. The nano-PROTACs with a hydrodynamic size of 28 nm, when administered intravenously in 200 μL phosphate buffer saline (PBS) (200 μg/mL) to 4T1 tumour-bearing mice effectively degraded IDO and promoted the maturation of DCs, as evidenced by increased levels of CD11c⁺CD80⁺CD86⁺ DCs [ ]. The maturation of DCs subsequently enhanced the infiltration of cytotoxic T-cells, including CD4⁺ T-cells, CD8⁺ T-cells, and CD8⁺Granzyme B⁺ T-cells. Elevated levels of high mobility group box 1 protein (HMGB1), tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and interleukin (IL)−6 further confirmed the induction of ICD, resulting in reduced primary tumour growth and inhibition of distant tumour formation in breast cancer mouse models [ ]. In another study, GSH/ROS responsive folic acid polymers loaded with doxorubicin (DOX), DOX@FPSD NPs, were developed and administered via tail vein injection (5 μg/kg DOX) to breast cancer-bearing mice in combination with decitabine (60 μg/kg) [ ]. This treatment effectively induced pyroptosis in breast tumour cells, significantly reducing tumour growth and metastasis. Immunofluorescence analysis of tumour sections revealed enhanced activation of antitumour CD3+CD4+CD8+ T-cells, highlighting the immunotherapeutic potential of this combination strategy [ ]. Additionally, a redox-responsive nanoassembly (R-mPDV/PDV/DOX/siL) encapsulating DOX and siRNA targeting lactate dehydrogenase demonstrated significant antitumour efficacy by suppressing breast tumour growth at a siRNA dosage of 2 mg/kg. This system reduced the infiltration of tumour-promoting Forkhead boc protein p3+ (FOXP3+) T regulatory cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [ ]. Conversely, it enhanced DC maturation and promoted the infiltration and activation of cytotoxic T-cells in breast tumour-bearing mice, emphasising its potential as an effective immunomodulatory therapy [ ]. Ma et al. developed GSH-responsive DOX-based mannose nanogels (DM NGs) to enhance chemo-immunotherapy. The DM NGs, with a diameter of 86.08 ± 2.87 nm, were administered via tail vein injection at a DOX-equivalent dose of 5 mg/kg, inducing ICD-mediated antitumour immunity in 4T1 xenograft mice. The induction of ICD was evidenced by increased levels of calreticulin, the release of ATP, and HMGB1 [ ]. Besides, the significant upregulation of CD80⁺CD86⁺ DCs and enhanced expression of CD103 on DCs confirmed their maturation following DM NG treatment. Of note, DM NGs reduced CD8⁺ T-cell exhaustion, effectively modulating the TME and improving antitumour immune responses [ ]. Similarly, Chang and colleagues demonstrated that pH-responsive biodegradable NPs, HCP + CpG@PCL‐Hyd‐polyethylene glycol (PEG)‐CD80 Ab NPs, administered intravenously at a dose of 500 µg/100 µL, specifically targeted breast tumour sites and modulated the tumour immune microenvironment [ ]. This treatment suppressed regulatory CD3+CD4+CD25+FOXP3+ T-cells while activating antitumour CD3+CD8+ T-cells, CD3+CD4+ T-cells, and CD8+CD44+CD122+ memory T-cells. These effects collectively delayed tumour progression and significantly prolonged the survival of the treated animals [ ]. Another pH-sensitive magnetic nanopolymer, Fe3O4-SiO2-PLGA-PDA-PTX-siRNA NPs, has been developed as an immunotherapeutic agent [ ]. This nanocarrier, delivering siRNA targeting PD-L1 at a paclitaxel-equivalent dose of 8 mg/kg, enhanced DC maturation and promoted the infiltration of cytotoxic immune cells, including CD3+ T-cells, CD4+ T-cells, and CD8+ T-cells resulted in the suppression of breast tumour growth and metastasis [ ]. Moreover, Bai and colleagues developed multifunctional pH-responsive nanocarriers, termed MARS, and demonstrated their ability to activate a cascade-amplified systemic immune response in a mouse model of breast cancer [ ]. Administration of these nanocarriers via intravenous injection (HTC: 112.5 mg/kg, GOx: 470 μg/kg, R848: 2 mg/kg) every 2 days for a total of four doses, in combination with radiotherapy, resulted in decreased infiltration of CD4+CD25+FOXP3+ regulatory T-cells (Tregs) and increased infiltration of CD3+CD8+CD44+CD62L− cytotoxic T-cells, CD3+CD4+ helper T-cells, and CD3+CD8+ cytotoxic T-cells. This immune modulation promoted ICD, leading to suppression of tumour growth, metastasis, and recurrence [ ]. A tungsten-based pH-sensitive radiosensitiser NP (PWAI) was developed and loaded with 5-Azacytidine, a DNA methyltransferase inhibitor, and ITF-2357, a histone deacetylase inhibitor, to induce a robust antitumour immune response [ ]. This study showed that PWAI promoted dendritic cell maturation, enhanced cytotoxic T-cell infiltration, and reduced the polarisation of protumour macrophages. These immune-modulating effects effectively suppressed tumour growth and improved survival of mice bearing 4T1 breast cancer cells [ ]. Another pH-responsive nanoparticle system co-loaded with curcumin and doxorubicin (DCMALN) demonstrated promising therapeutic potential in a murine model of breast cancer bone metastasis by enhancing the infiltration of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T lymphocytes [ ]. Recently, Deng and colleagues developed a pH-sensitive Ca & Mn dual-ion hybrid nanostimulator (CMS) that functions as an immunoadjuvant by alleviating intratumour hypoxia and activating the stimulator of interferon gene (STING) pathway via Mn2+ [ ]. Intratumoural administration of CMS (20 mg/kg) reprogrammed tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs) to an antitumour phenotype, activated dendritic cells for antigen presentation, and enhanced the infiltration of tumour-specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes, including CD3+CD8+, CD3+CD4+, CD3+CD8+CD44+CD62L−, and CD3+CD8+CD44+CD62L+ T-cells, in breast cancer mouse models. This reduced tumour growth, induced tumour cell apoptosis, and inhibited its progression at distant sites [ ]. Yin and colleagues developed a tumour-associated antigen (TAA)-boosted nanovaccine using breast cancer cell (4T1) membrane-coated Prussian blue NPs co-immobilised with carbon monoxide (4T1-PB-CO NPs). These NPs, when combined with anti-IL-10 therapy, acted as exogenous TAAs to activate CD4 + T-cell responses and promote M1-phenotype polarisation of TAMs [ ]. Upon near-infrared light irradiation, 4T1-PB-CO NPs (administered at 50 μg PB/kg) released carbon monoxide, inducing ICD in tumour cells and generating endogenous TAAs to activate CD8+ T-cell responses. Additionally, ICD led to the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), enhancing DC maturation and amplifying the antitumour T-cell response [ ]. The combination with anti-IL-10 therapy (dose of 2 mg anti-IL-10/kg and 10 mg PB/kg) further counteracted the immunosuppressive TME, effectively suppressing primary tumours and producing an abscopal effect to inhibit distant tumours in a mouse model of breast cancer [ ]. Other bioresponsive NPs, such as CUR/miR155@DssD-Hb NPs, MiR@PCPmP NPs, DACss, and ABE/Cur@SAL/PBNs, have also demonstrated efficacy by promoting dendritic cell maturation and activating cytotoxic T-cells, leading to significant reductions in breast tumour growth and progression [ , , , ]. Additionally, ROS-responsive A1.8Se3O0.5/siPD-L1 cationic nanogels (10 nmol/20 g equivalent siRNA dose) induced autophagy and tumour cell death, leading to tumour regression by promoting the infiltration of antitumour CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-cells in colon cancer in vivo [ ]. Furthermore, treatment of spleen-derived DCs with these nanogels enhanced DC maturation, highlighting their potential in immunotherapy [ ]. Chen and colleagues recently developed photosensitive polydopamine (PDA)-coated iron NPs, referred to as Fe3O4@PDA-PEG-cRGD-AA@Gox, and showed that their intravenous administration (2 mg/mL) alongside an αPD-L1 immune checkpoint inhibitor and laser irradiation significantly suppressed tumour growth and induced ICD in a mouse model of colon cancer [ ]. Mechanistic investigations indicated that these NPs promoted the release of tumour-specific antigens, which facilitated DC maturation in lymph nodes and increased the infiltration of CD8+ T-cells into the TME [ ]. Another formulation of PDA-conjugated iron NPs, Fe3O4@PDA@CaCO3-ICG@CM, coated with a mouse lymphoma cell membrane, demonstrated significant antitumour effects in colon cancer models [ ]. These NPs, responsive to both acidic pH and laser irradiation, were administered intravenously (200 μL) and effectively reduced tumour growth and progression by promoting the maturation of DCs and activating CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells in vivo [ ]. A novel blood–brain barrier (BBB)-crossing nanovaccine was developed using cancer cell membrane-camouflaged poly(N-vinylcaprolactam) (PVCL) nanogels (NGs) loaded with MnO2 and DOX. Intravenous administration ([Mn] = 3.4 mg/kg in 200 μL PBS) effectively activated the STING pathway, promoted dendritic cell maturation, and enhanced cytotoxic T-cell infiltration and activation. This approach significantly reduced tumour growth and recurrence in orthotopic glioma mouse models [ ]. In another study, a novel pH- and hyaluronidase-responsive smart nanoprobe, FeIIITA@HA, was designed and administered intravenously at a dose of 50 µmol Fe3⁺ per kilogramme of body weight. This nanoprobe significantly enhanced CD8+ and CD4+ T-cell infiltration, leading to a marked reduction in the growth of superficial head and neck squamous cell tumours in vivo (182).
 Of note, another study demonstrated that H₂O₂-responsive Ato/cabo@PEG-TK-PLGA NPs, administered transdermally, successfully targeted ROS-overproducing melanoma cells in vivo, resulting in a significant reduction in tumour growth and lung metastasis [ ]. These NPs also promoted the infiltration of antitumour immune cells, including CD3⁺CD4⁺ T-cells and CD3⁺CD8⁺ T-cells and induced maturation and differentiation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) at the melanoma tumour site [ ]. Additionally, acidic pH-responsive nanocarrier, PP-SS-OVA/CpG NPs (intravenous injection, 20 µg/mouse), in combination with anti-PD-1 therapy, significantly enhanced the efficiency of MHC-I antigen presentation on APCs [ ]. This treatment also promoted DC maturation and increased the infiltration of cytotoxic CD3+CD8+ T-cells and CD3+CD4+ T-cells. As a result, it effectively reduced subcutaneous melanoma growth and inhibited lung metastasis in vivo, demonstrating its potential as a robust immunotherapeutic strategy [ ]. Another study demonstrated that pH-responsive ovalbumin-loaded GMP/Eu complex NPs (containing 0.1 μM of CpG or 20 μg of OVA) effectively activated tumour suppressing immune lineages, including CD8 + T-cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and macrophages polarised toward an antitumour phenotype [ ]. This immune activation resulted in a significant reduction of melanoma growth and improved survival in B16-F10 tumour-bearing C57BL/6j mice [ ]. Other acidic bioresponsive NPs, such as pRNVs/HPPH/IND, PEG BR647-NPs, PBAF, CUR/miR155@DssD-Hb NPs, DPQ-DOX NPs, MnO2@BSA@DOX NPs, NCSNPs, and NRP+I nanogels, were also shown to reduce melanoma growth in vivo by promoting the infiltration of cytotoxic CD8+ T-cells into the TME [ , , , , , , , ].
A novel pH-sensitive nanocarrier, siRNA-CaP@PD1-NVs, has been developed to enhance the immune response against tumours. This nanocarrier (tail-vein injection 4 times every 3 days with a dose of 687.5 μg per time) not only improves the cytotoxic activity of immune cells by disrupting the PD1/PDL1 axis but also suppresses tumour immune evasion through Polybromo1/PDL1 gene silencing [ ]. These combined effects significantly enhance the immunosuppressive TME. As a result, the nanocarrier facilitates DC maturation, boosts the infiltration and activity of CD8+ T-cells, and establishes long-lasting immune memory. This approach effectively inhibits liver tumour growth, eliminates existing tumours, and prevents recurrence and metastasis in in vivo models [ ]. In another study, ultrasound-sensitive biomimetic nanometal frameworks, termed AMR-MOF@AuPt, were developed. Intravenous administration of these NPs (10 mg/kg of MOF) five times every 2 days produced substantial ROS, effectively eliminating liver cancer cells [ ]. This treatment also enhanced dendritic cell activation and tumour antigen cross-presentation, triggering an amplified T-cell immune response that induced necrosis and apoptosis in tumour cells within liver cancer mouse models [ ]. Additionally, an aptamer-based pH- and photosensitive nanoconstruct, Apt/PPII/IR780-NPs (administered intravenously with IR780 at 2.5 mg/kg), effectively suppressed liver tumour growth and progression. This treatment induced the activation of CD11c+CD80+CD86+ dendritic cells and enhanced the infiltration of CD4+CD3+ T-cells in vivo [ ]. Another study developed a magnetic metal–organic framework (MOF) NP coated with homologous tumour cell membranes (Hm@TSA/As-MOF) to enhance the antitumour immune response. These nanocarriers specifically targeted acidic tumour sites, promoting the infiltration of cytotoxic T-cells and thereby reducing tumour growth in orthotopic liver cancer mouse models [ ]. Another HOCl-sensitive prodrug, DHU-CBA2, grafted with methylene blue and a COX inhibitor, demonstrated significant therapeutic efficacy by reducing lung tumour growth and associated cachexia [ ]. Additionally, it inhibited spinal metastasis through the activation of antitumour T-cell lineages, particularly CD8 + Granzyme B + T-cells, highlighting its potential as an effective immunomodulatory agent in cancer therapy [ ]. Acidic pH-responsive Mg/Al LDH-5-OP-RU/E7 mRNA nanovaccines (60 µg in 50 µL PBS per mouse), administered via intranasal immunisation, effectively inhibited the occurrence of metastatic lung nodules by activating antigen-presenting DCs and mucosa-associated invariant T-cells [ ]. This treatment also reduced the number of infiltrating neutrophils and inhibited the differentiation of macrophages into a tumour suppressive M1 phenotype, while decreasing the levels of immunosuppressive cytokines [ ].
Another nanocarrier system utilising exosomes coated with CP05 peptide, ROS responsive thioketal, and loaded with chemotherapeutic drug DOX (Exo@RB/DOX), in combination with laser irradiation, demonstrated significant antitumour activity in mice bearing A20 lymphoma cells [ ]. In this study, administration of Exo@RB/DOX (2 mg/mL, 200 µL, via tail vein injection) was shown to enhance the expression of T-cell activation markers, including IL-6, IFN-γ, IL-2, and TNF-α [ ]. Concurrently, it suppressed the expression of T-cell inactivation markers, such as TGF-β and IL-10, highlighting its potential to stimulate an effective antitumour immune response [ ]. Notably, Yuba and colleagues developed bioactive polysaccharide-based pH-sensitive liposomes, namely MGlu-Curd-C10-modified and MGlu-Man-C10-modified liposomes [ ]. These liposomes (100 μg subcutaneous injection) effectively promoted DC activation in vitro, suppressed lymphoma growth, and significantly improved survival in vivo, highlighting their potential as an immunotherapeutic strategy [ ]. Moreover, in another study, GSH and pH-responsive metal-phenolic network NPs (MPNs@OVA + CHA NPs) were subcutaneously injected into the inguinal region (50 μg OVA per mouse). The treatment enhanced the expression of MHC-I and other maturation markers on DCs from inguinal lymph nodes [ ]. When combined with anti-PD-1 therapy, these NPs effectively reduced regulatory T-cells while activating cytotoxic T-cells, resulting in suppression of lymphoma tumour growth and prolonged survival in vivo [ ]. In another study, a mannose receptor-recognising liposomal DNA vaccine (DNA concentration of 4 µg/ml) was shown to target DCs and enhance the expression of maturation markers such as CD80, CD86, and CD40 [ ]. In another study, a nanoliposome-based pH-responsive DDS carrying a DNA vaccine was shown to enhance the activation of APCs and immune cells in draining lymph nodes. The study demonstrated the system’s strong capacity to elicit both cellular and humoral immune responses [ ]. Interestingly, Xu et al. developed a nanovaccine incorporating the male contraceptive peptide FSHR. They observed highly effective endocytosis of the engineered nanovaccine in DCs, which stimulated the production of inflammatory cytokines and activated T-cells through the modulation of DCs [ ]. The results from in vivo trials demonstrated that the nanovaccine exhibited greater efficacy than the conventional CFA-based vaccine, suggesting that nanovehicles could serve as efficient and safe platforms for the development of male contraceptive nanovaccines [ ].
In conclusion, these studies highlight the potential of smart NPs to efficiently induce ICD by activating DCs and cytotoxic T-cells while simultaneously suppressing regulatory T-cells in various inflammatory conditions, including cancer. By effectively inducing DAMPs, these NPs trigger immune response cascades that facilitate the recognition and selective elimination of abnormal cells. Furthermore, these NPs exhibit a preferential sensitisation of inflammatory tissues over normal tissues and activate DCs in draining lymph nodes, demonstrating enhanced therapeutic efficacy compared to free drugs. Given their promising performance, future tailored research is essential to advance these NPs toward clinical application and commercialisation.

## Targeting eosinophils
Eosinophils are granulocytes characterised by a multilobed nucleus and cytoplasmic granules that readily stain with eosin, accounting for 1–5% of circulating leukocytes [ , ]. These cells are evolutionarily conserved among vertebrates and have traditionally been recognised for their critical role in innate immune responses, particularly in defence against parasitic infections [ , ]. Advances in research have broadened our understanding of eosinophil functions, highlighting their involvement in regulating adaptive immune responses as well as maintaining tissue and metabolic homeostasis. Eosinophils play a dual role in pathological conditions, acting as both mediators of tissue damage and drivers of repair, remodelling, and fibrosis [ , , ]. Under normal physiological conditions, the eosinophil count in human blood typically ranges from 0 to 500 cells per microliter. However, this number can increase significantly, by up to 20 times, during certain disease states [ ]. Hence, therapeutic strategies targeting eosinophils have demonstrated promising results in the treatment of inflammatory diseases [ , ]. The following section provides an overview of bioresponsive nanomaterials designed to target eosinophils (Fig. ).
 Reducing the infiltration of eosinophils has emerged as a promising strategy for treating allergic airway inflammation [ ]. The Janus kinase 2 (JAK-2)/signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) pathway is critical in the myelopoiesis of eosinophils [ ]. Sun and colleagues developed ROS-responsive NPs encapsulating fedratinib (FDTN), a JAK-2/STAT pathway inhibitor, to target eosinophils and improve allergic asthma outcomes. In this study, intratracheal administration of NPs-FDTN to ovalbumin-induced asthma in BALB/c mice resulted in enhanced localisation of the NPs to airway sites, attributed to increased ROS production, particularly by eosinophils [ ]. Treatment with NPs-FDTN at a dosage of 7.5 mg/mL significantly reduced mucus production, airway inflammation, and immunoglobulin E levels compared to mice treated with FDTN alone [ ]. Besides, the number of infiltrating eosinophils, CD4+ T-cells, and neutrophils, as well as the expression levels of cytokines such as IL-4, IL-5, and IFN-γ, were significantly reduced in the NPs-FDTN group compared to the FDTN group. Additionally, treatment of eosinophils isolated from asthma patients with FDTN resulted in decreased cell viability, increased caspase-3 activity, and induction of apoptosis through the inhibition of the JAK-2/STAT pathway [ ]. These findings demonstrated that ROS-responsive delivery of a JAK-2/STAT pathway inhibitor via nanomaterials represents a promising therapeutic strategy for managing eosinophilic asthma.
Moreover, gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) was identified as an overexpressed biomarker in inflammatory lung tissues, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target [ , ]. Chen et al. developed GGT-responsive dendrimer-dexamethasone conjugates (GSHDDCs) with 20 nm particle size for aerosol inhalation therapy of allergic asthma. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed a two-fold overexpression of GGT in lung tissues of allergic asthma mice compared to healthy controls, primarily localised to inflammatory cells like eosinophils, macrophages, mast cells, and neutrophils [ ]. Treatment with GSHDDC (0.05 mg/kg equivalent to dexamethasone) significantly reduced airway inflammation, comparable to dexamethasone (3 mg/kg) [ ]. This nanomaterial therapy effectively decreased the infiltration of eosinophils and other leukocytes, suppressed goblet-cell proliferation and mucus production, and lowered inflammatory markers including IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, IL-25, IL-33, Mucin 5AC (MUC5AC), and thymic stromal lymphoprotein (TSLP) in lung tissues of ovalbumin-induced allergic asthma C57BL/6 mice [ ]. Further, Yoo and team synthesised biodegradable H2O2 responsive hydroxybenzyl alcohol (HBA)-incorporated polyoxalate (HPOX) NPs as a potential therapeutic drug candidate for the management of airway inflammatory diseases [ ]. HPOX NPs exhibit both antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties by neutralising H2O2, mitigating intracellular oxidative stress, and downregulating the expression of pro-inflammatory factors, including inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), and IL-1β in activated macrophages. Notably, intratracheal administration of HPOX NPs (50 and 100 μg) to ovalbumin-induced asthmatic rodent models significantly reduced the recruitment of eosinophils and other inflammatory cells, as well as the expression of pro-inflammatory mediators, including in IL-4 and iNOS [ ]. Furthermore, 7,12-dimethylbenz[a]anthracene (DMBA), a polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon, is a known environmental pollutant associated with tumourigenesis, hypertension, and aging [ ]. Sericin, a negatively charged and pH-responsive molecule, was conjugated with silver NPs (AgNPs) and evaluated for its therapeutic potential against DMBA-induced toxicity. Treatment with sericin-conjugated AgNPs (200 mg/kg) demonstrated a significant reduction in DMBA-induced toxicity and immunoglobulin levels, while increasing haemoglobin concentration, platelet counts, and white blood cell (WBC) counts [ ]. Also, these NPs effectively suppressed DMBA-induced elevations in neutrophils, eosinophils, and monocytes [ ]. These findings suggest that targeted intervention using nanoengineered materials such as sericin-conjugated AgNPs could provide a favourable approach for mitigating the adverse effects of pollutants like DMBA.
In conclusion, these findings highlight that selective targeting of eosinophils through modulation of pathological signaling pathways represents a more effective therapeutic approach compared to conventional drugs. This strategy demonstrates significant potential in alleviating inflammation and serves as a promising avenue for the treatment of chronic ailments.

## Targeting macrophages to effectively tune their polarisation
Macrophages are characterised by their remarkable diversity and plasticity [ ]. Classically activated macrophages (M1) play a key role in pro-inflammatory responses and host defence against infections, whereas alternatively activated macrophages (M2) are primarily involved in anti-inflammatory processes and tissue remodelling [ , ]. These two subtypes represent opposite ends of the macrophage activation spectrum. The phenotypic transformation of macrophages is crucial in regulating the onset, progression, and resolution of inflammatory diseases [ ]. To address the dynamic role of macrophages in inflammation, smart NPs are also engineered to modulate macrophage polarisation, shifting the balance between pro-inflammatory (M1) and anti-inflammatory (M2) states (Fig. ). For example, phosphatidylserine-targeting peptide-coated HA-based nanomaterials (HA@PCFT NPs), designed to be ROS-sensitive and selectively target damaged tissues, demonstrated enhanced selectivity toward macrophage foam cells, highlighting their potential efficacy in treating atherosclerotic plaques [ ]. In another study, prodigiosin-loaded mannan-coated lysosomal β-mannosidase-responsive NPs (PG@M-MNPs) demonstrated enhanced cytotoxicity against breast and liver cancer cells in vitro. Mechanistic studies further revealed that these NPs specifically target macrophages overexpressing the mannose receptor CD206, inhibiting their pro-inflammatory M2 polarisation [ ]. MSN-NH2-celecoxib-IP pH-responsive NPs demonstrated the ability to reduce nitric oxide levels and enhance macrophage cell viability, thereby alleviating inflammatory conditions in vitro. These findings suggest their potential application in treating macrophage-associated inflammatory diseases [ ]. In another study, platinum prodrug-conjugated BLZ−945SCNs/Pt NPs (~ 10 nm) were synthesised to specifically target TAMs. In breast and colon cancer mouse models, treatment with these NPs (2.5 mg/kg) inhibited the infiltration of tumour-promoting macrophages [ ]. Additionally, this study demonstrated that these NPs reduced the infiltration of regulatory T-cells while enhancing the infiltration of cytotoxic CD8 + T-cells and CD4 + T-cells, resulting in significant tumour growth reduction [ ]. In addition, a dual-responsive nanoplatform sensitive to GSH and pH, encapsulating DOX and resiquimod (R848) with glucose-modified poly(2-methacryloyloxyethyl phosphorylcholine)-b-poly(N-(3-aminopropyl)-methacrylamine (GPNP), was shown to effectively reprogram M2 macrophages into M1 macrophages. This nanoplatform exposed the core CS(DOX)-PBA under specific conditions, enabling targeted cytotoxicity against MCF-7/ADR cells [ ]. Torrieri et al. have developed a dextran-based nanosystem for the treatment of myocardial infarction. The functionalised nanostructure incorporates two distinct peptides: atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP), which targets cardiac cells, and linTT1, which targets macrophages associated with atherosclerotic plaques [ ]. These NPs exhibit a higher association-to-uptake ratio with M2-like macrophages, potentially utilising the “hitch-hike” effect on these cells to enhance the targeting of infarcted heart tissue. This dual-targeting approach improves the efficacy of the ANP peptide in reaching the affected area [ ]. Additionally, folic acid and methotrexate-loaded pluronic F127-based nanocarriers, named PCAC-FA, effectively reduced the toxicity of free methotrexate while targeting inflamed macrophages and demonstrated significant anti-rheumatoid arthritis activity in vitro [ ]. Ma et al. synthesised an anti-inflammatory TPP@PMM nanoplatform with serial ROS responsiveness and two-photon AIE bioimaging for diagnostic and therapeutic applications. Treatment with TPP@PMM on activated macrophages showed significant attenuation of the ROS signal, which correlated with an alleviated inflammatory response. Additionally, TPP@PMM treatment on activated macrophages resulted in a reduction of inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β, and myeloperoxidase (MPO) and in vivo studies confirmed the biosafety of TPP@PMM [ ]. Notably, ROS-responsive MacTNP demonstrated selective targeting of activated macrophages over resting macrophages and fibroblasts in vitro, effectively reducing inflammation. These findings highlight the significant potential of MacTNP for the selective targeting of inflammatory macrophages and its application in photodynamic therapy for atherosclerosis, with minimal adverse effects [ ].
 Studies in animal models have also demonstrated the efficacy of bioresponsive nanomaterials in targeting macrophages and modulating immune activities. For instance, an inflammatory microenvironment-responsive hydrogel incorporating selenium NPs (OD-PP@SeNPs) has been developed with ROS scavenging and pH-regulating capabilities [ ]. This hydrogel protects cells from oxidative stress and promotes M2 macrophage polarisation, thereby reducing inflammatory cytokine production through the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB and MAPK pathways [ ]. These properties enable the hydrogel to exert anti-inflammatory effects, reshape the inflammatory microenvironment, and effectively treat inflammatory disorders such as S. aureus-infected wounds and rheumatoid arthritis in rat models [ ]. Tian and colleagues designed matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)−2-sensitive HA-AuNR/M-M2pep NPs (64.6 nm) capable of selectively depleting TAMs and inducing ICD. Intravenous administration of HA-AuNR/M-M2pep NPs at a dose of 12 mg/kg of M2pep significantly enhanced DC maturation and promoted the intratumoural recruitment of CD8⁺ T-cells, thereby suppressing melanoma growth and progression in vivo [ ]. Additionally, Li and colleagues developed pH-responsive polymeric NPs (M-PNP@R@C NPs) designed to polarise macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype for tumour cell eradication [ ]. In melanoma-bearing mice, treatment with M-PNP@R@C NPs (intravenously injected at a dose of R848, 10 μg/mouse; cGAMP, 10 μg/mouse) significantly enhanced M1 macrophage polarisation and activated cytotoxic T-cells, resulting in a substantial reduction in tumour size and metastasis [ ]. Yu et al. developed an Evans Blue (EB)-based in vivo self-assembled nanocarrier system, mUNO-EB-ICG-Fc@Alb NPs, referred to as MA NPs, for the targeted imaging and clearance of M2 TAMs to enhance anticancer immunotherapy using a PD-1 inhibitor. In vivo results demonstrated that MA NPs (100 μL, 2 mg/mL)-mediated chemodynamic therapy effectively alleviated the immunosuppressive TME, promoted intratumoural T lymphocyte infiltration, and significantly enhanced the antitumour efficacy of PD-1 inhibitors in breast cancer mice models [ ]. Further, Zhao and colleagues developed mannosylated albumin NPs for the co-encapsulation of disulfiram/copper complex (DSF/Cu) and regorafenib (Rego) to target drug-resistant colon tumours. This study observed that drug-resistant colon cancer cells and pro-tumour M2 macrophages exhibit high expression of two transporters: secreted protein acidic and rich in cysteine and the mannose receptor (MR) [ ]. These NPs utilised this dual targeting mechanism, contributing to their anticancer effects by enhancing apoptosis, increasing intracellular ROS levels, inhibiting angiogenesis, and re-educating tumour-associated macrophages, significantly improving treatment outcomes in animal models with drug-resistant tumours [ ]. Another study developed ROS-responsive H2O2-eliminating NPs, referred to as OCD NPs, which were shown to reduce the expression of TNF-α and IL-6 in PMA-stimulated macrophages and peritoneal neutrophils. These findings indicated that OCD NPs exhibit significant antioxidative and anti-inflammatory properties in inflammatory cells. Further analysis demonstrated that these NPs effectively mitigated inflammation and suppressed tumourigenesis in mouse models of colon cancer [ ]. Another study demonstrated that pH-responsive lipid-coated calcium zoledronate NPs (CaZol@pMNPs) effectively induced apoptotic cell death in TAMs, leading to significant reductions in sarcoma growth and metastasis [ ].
Macrophages are integral to the initial inflammatory response triggered by cardiovascular injury, where they contribute to both tissue damage and the subsequent healing process [ ]. Their dual role involves releasing pro-inflammatory mediators that exacerbate injury while also promoting repair mechanisms essential for recovery [ ]. Therefore, precise targeting of macrophages is crucial to efficiently treat CVDs. Smart NPs are instrumental in targeting inflammatory macrophages to mitigate cardiac injury. An investigation conducted by Yu and colleagues demonstrated that a pH-sensitive nanodelivery system, modified with β-cyclodextrin and hyaluronic acid for CD44 targeting, effectively inhibited inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, and ROS, thereby reducing cell death in oxidised LDL-treated macrophages. In ApoE–/– mice fed a high-fat diet, treatment with these NPs resulted in decreased proliferation of aortic plaques, reduced inflammation, and less intimal thickening [ ]. Mechanistically, the NPs targeted macrophage foam cells and suppressed the expression of TC, HMGCR, ABCA1, LXRα, SREBP2, IL-6, MCP-1, COX-2, VEGF, IL-1β, and TNF-α, while upregulating ACAT2 and PPAR. This comprehensive modulation of lipid metabolism and inflammatory pathways effectively prevented the progression of atherosclerosis [ ]. Moreover, Li and team developed cyclodextrin-derived pH-responsive and integrin-targeted NPs, termed “AAM NP,” encapsulating an antisense oligonucleotide targeting microRNA-33 (anti-miR33) for the precise treatment of atherosclerosis [ ]. Their preclinical investigations indicated the effectiveness of these NPs as an alternative approach for targeted atherosclerosis therapy. The anti-miR33-based NPs substantially enhanced reverse cholesterol transport and influenced adaptive immunity via the modulation of macrophage polarisation and regulatory T-cell differentiation [ ]. Another study fabricated TPCD as a versatile NP for scavenging ROS to address atherosclerosis. TPCD NPs functioned by eliminating excess intracellular ROS and preventing foam cell formation in macrophages and vascular smooth muscle cells. The primary therapeutic benefits of TPCD NPs included their ability to mitigate systemic and local oxidative stress, reduce inflammation, and inhibit the infiltration of inflammatory cells in atherosclerotic plaques [ ]. Another study demonstrated that TPCD NPs exhibit significant antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. TPCD-derived nanotherapy protected macrophages from apoptosis induced by oxidative stress through cellular internalisation and efficient ROS scavenging [ ]. In various murine models of inflammatory diseases, including paw oedema, peritonitis, and acute lung injury, TPCD NPs notably alleviated oxidative stress and inhibited inflammatory responses, as indicated by reduced levels of ALT, AST, TNF‐α, IL‐1β, H2O2, creatinine, and urea in affected tissues or organs, positioning TPCD as a promising therapy for multiple inflammation-related conditions [ ]. Gao et al. developed MM-AT-NPs, a biomimetic DDS for atherosclerosis, using macrophage membrane-coated ROS-responsive NPs (MMNPs). This study demonstrated that MM-AT-NPs respond to ROS and mitigate inflammation generated by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) as well as foam cell formation from macrophages stimulated by oxidised low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) in vitro, suggesting their therapeutic potential for atherosclerosis [ ]. This study also found that MM-AT-NPs reduced plaque area, monocyte levels, and MMP-9 expression in aortic arch plaques while inhibiting endothelial proliferation. MM-AT-NP therapy reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines in aortic tissues, such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, indicating potential for the management of inflammatory disorders [ ]. In addition, hemin-encapsulated HA-based NPs, Hemin/HA-LP, which target infarct macrophages, were shown to suppress TNF-α and induce the expression of haeme oxygenase 1 (HO-1), TGF-β, Arginase 1, and the M2/M1 macrophage ratio in in vitro studies [ ]. Intraperitoneal infusion of Hemin/HA-LP (10 mg lipid/kg) significantly reduced infarct size, scar tissue, apical infarcts, and dilatation, while improving angiogenesis in a Balb/C mouse model of myocardial infarction, demonstrating their therapeutic superiority [ ].
Moreover, Zhang et al. developed macrophage-targeting and ROS-responsive polyplexes, termed “Se-PEI,” to facilitate the systemic delivery of TNF-α siRNA (siTNF-α) for alleviating hepatic inflammation in mice with acute liver failure (ALF). Se-PEI was selectively degraded by ROS produced by inflammatory macrophages, facilitating intracellular siRNA release, enhancing gene knockdown efficiency, and concurrently mitigating the cytotoxicity typically associated with high molecular weight carriers [ ]. Notably, Man-COOH/Se-PEI/siTNF-α polyplexes achieved approximately 80% suppression of TNF-α expression in inflamed hepatic tissues at a dosage of 500 μg siRNA per kg, significantly reducing serum TNF-α levels and eliciting potent anti-inflammatory effects in acute liver failure (ALF) models [ ]. In addition, silymarin-loaded ketalised maltodextrin (s-KMD) NPs responsive to acidic pH was designed for the treatment of various inflammatory diseases, including ALF. These s-KMD NPs displayed antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities on macrophages stimulated with ROS and LPS [ ]. These NPs (5 mg/kg, intravenously) substantially reduced hepatic damage by diminishing the release of the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α in mice models of ALF. These data suggest that KMD NPs have considerable potential as theranostic agents for various inflammatory diseases [ ]. Another study showed that acid-degradable PKAA/anti-TNF-α siRNA nanocomplexes effectively inhibited TNF-α secretion in LPS-treated macrophages and protected liver tissues from further damage in acetaminophen-induced ALF mouse models [ ]. These findings demonstrated that the targeted delivery of TNF-α siRNA to liver macrophages using PKAA/anti-TNF-α siRNA nanocomplexes can safeguard the liver from hepatotoxic insults [ ].
Nandi et al. synthesised caspase-1-responsive YVAD probe-encapsulated NPs loaded with MCC-950 (YVAD-MCC NPs), an inflammasome inhibitor, and demonstrated that these NPs markedly reduce the formation of the NOD-, LRR-, and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome complex in LPS-stimulated macrophages when treated at 10 µM [ ]. Moreover, treatment with these NPs significantly reduced inflammation and improved colitis symptoms in dextran sulphate sodium (DSS)-induced UC mice [ ]. Another study demonstrated that administering Cyclosporine A-encapsulated pH/ROS dual-responsive nanoformulations (equivalent to 2 mg/kg of CsA) via tail vein for three consecutive days effectively targeted and activated macrophages leading to reduced inflammation and the disease severity index in mice with UC [ ]. Additionally, Gou et al. synthesised curcumin (CUR)-loaded NPs from natural silk fibroin and surface-functionalised them with chondroitin sulphate (CS) for the treatment of UC. The CS component specifically targeted macrophages by attaching to the surface glycoprotein CD44 leading to enhanced anti-inflammatory activity. These NPs suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-12) and enhanced the production of the anti-inflammatory cytokine (IL-10) in treated cells. The in vivo studies also confirmed the potential anti-inflammatory activity of these NPs for the management of UC [ ]. Another curcumin loaded poly(diselenide-oxalate-curcumin) NPs reduced inflammatory macrophages in vitro and suppressed the symptoms of IBD in DSS-induced colitis mice [ ]. Another ROS-responsive thioketal NPs, TKNs, were shown to reduce inflammatory cytokine production and enhance the viability of LPS-stimulated macrophages. The in vivo study results suggested the activity of TKNs loaded with siRNA against the proinflammatory cytokine TNF-α, diminished TNF-α mRNA levels in the colon, and protected mice from UC [ ]. Another macrophage-specific NP system, TPP-PPM/siRNA NPs, loaded with TNF-α siRNA, effectively suppressed inflammatory responses in LPS-stimulated macrophages in vitro and mitigated inflammation in a DSS-induced colitis model in vivo [ ]. Laroui and colleagues demonstrated that Fab'-bearing PLA-PEG NPs serve as powerful and efficient nanoscale vehicles for the delivery of siRNAs into colonic macrophages, targeting inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [ ]. These NPs exhibited selective distribution to macrophages, improved endocytosis kinetics, and demonstrated an anti-inflammatory effect via a reduction in myeloperoxidase activity and IκBα accumulation [ ].
In another study, the siRNA/HMG/OR micelle ternary complex (318.48 ± 14.17 nm), responsive to receptors for advanced glycation end products (RAGE) and Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), which are selectively overexpressed on alveolar macrophages, was used to deliver Chil3 and Chil4 siRNA [ ]. Treatment with Chil3 and Chil4 siRNA/HMG/OR micelle complexes reduced bronchoalveolar lavage fluid cell counts, including eosinophils, macrophages, and lymphocytes, and alleviated peribronchial and perivascular inflammation in asthmatic mice. Additionally, siRNA treatment downregulated asthma-related cytokines (IL-4, IL-13, CCL11), reduced mucus production, and diminished airway remodelling, ultimately reduced airway obstruction [ ]. In another study, ROS-responsive TNF-α siRNA-encapsulated Man-COOH/P-G@Zn/siTNF-α NPs (500 μg siTNF-α/kg) demonstrated enhanced targeting of macrophages and effectively suppressed lung tissue damage in LPS-induced acute lung injury in vivo models [ ]. Together, these studies highlight the potential of bioresponsive NPs to selectively target immune cells, thereby offering improved therapeutic outcomes in conditions such as asthma and acute lung injury.
Targeted delivery of therapeutic agents to macrophages in kidney diseases using smart NPs has shown encouraging outcomes in preclinical studies. For example, nanopolyplexes composed of hyaluronic acid and cationic chitosan NPs, loaded with the drug SS-31 (HA/SS NPs), were found to mitigate renal injury and enhance renal function. This was achieved by reducing the infiltration of pro-inflammatory macrophages in mouse models of acute kidney injury (AKI) [ ]. Similarly, Zhao and team synthesised self-assembling KLDD peptide nanofiber hydrogel loaded with Mito-2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-N-oxyl (TEMPO) to mitigate AKI. These NPs targeted macrophages and reduced their infiltration at the inflammatory site, thereby alleviating renal tubular damage [ ]. Another investigation demonstrated that a triptolide-glucosamine conjugate (TPG) effectively suppressed immunocyte adhesion, chemoattractant protein expression, and immune cell infiltration involving neutrophils, macrophages, and lymphocytes. These NP properties contributed to reducing tissue damage in a rat model of ischemia/reperfusion-induced renal injury [ ]. Further, ROS-responsive curcumin and dexamethasone-loaded NPs (CPDC NPs) effectively targeted macrophages at inflammatory sites, suppressing ROS, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, MCP-1, NO, and iNOS in LPS-stimulated macrophages [ ]. In an endotoxin-induced uveitis rabbit model, these NPs (50 μL containing 0.52 mg/mL curcumin and 0.5 mg/mL dexamethasone) significantly reduced inflammatory responses and inflammatory cell infiltration [ ].
In conclusion, bioresponsive NPs have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in addressing the inflammatory phenotype of macrophages by polarising them toward an anti-inflammatory state. These NPs reduce inflammatory markers either by activating anti-inflammatory macrophages or by inhibiting the infiltration and activation of pro-inflammatory macrophages. Moreover, they can be tailored to specifically modulate macrophage polarisation, enabling precise mitigation of inflammation and targeted elimination of abnormal cancer cells.

## Targeting neutrophils
Neutrophils, the most abundant immune cells in the bloodstream, serve as the first responders to acute inflammation [ ]. Under normal physiological conditions, these cells support tissue repair and resolve inflammation by phagocytosing necrotic cells to prevent further immune cell recruitment, releasing growth-promoting mediators, facilitating angiogenesis, and producing resolvins and protectins [ ]. Their critical involvement in chronic inflammation is also well recognised. Neutrophils are persistently recruited to sites of chronic inflammation, where these neutrophils exacerbate the condition by releasing serine proteases, forming neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), and activating other immune cells [ ]. This review will focus on bioresponsive NPs that effectively regulate neutrophil function to enhance anti-inflammatory responses.
Xu and team demonstrated that Ce6-luminol-PEG (CLP) conjugate NPs (25 mg/kg) effectively targeted neutrophils at the tumour site. ROS and MPO produced at the tumour site enhanced the generation of singlet oxygen from the NPs, resulting in tumour regression and apoptosis in A549 lung xenograft models [ ]. Another similar study conducted by Guo et al. demonstrated that luminous MPO-sensitive NPs functionalised with cyclodextrin effectively targeted neutrophils in both superficial and deep tissues across various mouse models of inflammatory diseases, such as acute lung injury, colitis, peritonitis, and paw oedema, and highlighted the potential of this nanoplatform as an MPO-activated nanocarrier, enabling site-specific delivery of therapeutic agents for combination therapy [ ]. In another study, multi-stage trypsin- and neutrophil elastase (NE)-sensitive NPs-in-microgel systems were developed to enhance targeted drug delivery to injured or inflamed lungs [ ]. These NPs-in-microgel demonstrated a low clearance rate by alveolar macrophages and neutrophils in the lungs. The presence of extracellular NE served as a trigger to target neutrophils for controlled drug release [ ]. Moreover, Dai and colleagues developed a pH-responsive DDS using hollow mesoporous silica NPs (HMSN) loaded with DOX, designated as HMSN–PA–HA@DOX. This system effectively reduced liver tumour growth while mitigating DOX-induced toxicity [ ]. Histological analysis further revealed that HMSN–PA–HA NPs protected the heart from DOX-induced damage and significantly reduced neutrophil infiltration in liver cancer mice models, highlighting their potential for enhancing therapeutic efficacy while minimising adverse effects [ ].
While the targeting capacity of smart NPs for neutrophils and other immune cells is less explored compared to their applications in DCs, T-cells, and macrophages, the existing findings are encouraging. These studies suggest significant potential for using NPs to address inflammation-related disorders. Further research is necessary to fully understand and optimise their role in targeting neutrophils and other immune cells for therapeutic purposes.

## Clinical efficacy of bioresponsive nanomaterials in eliciting immune responses
Recent clinical investigations have increasingly highlighted the therapeutic potential of bioengineered nanomaterials in modulating immune responses and addressing a broad spectrum of human diseases. For example, a Phase I/II clinical study evaluated AST-008–102, a nanospherical nucleic acid-based Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) agonist, in patients with solid tumours. The treatment resulted in notable tumour regression, accompanied by enhanced intratumoural infiltration of cytotoxic immune cells, indicating effective immunostimulatory activity (NCT03684785)[ ]. Another example is the bioengineered nanovaccine CHP-NY-ESO-1 (IMF-001), which consists of cholesteryl pullulan (CHP)-based NPs encapsulating the NY-ESO-1 tumour antigen and has demonstrated the ability to effectively activate both CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells in preclinical studies [ ]. In a clinical trial involving patients with oesophageal cancer, robust immune responses were observed in 7 out of 12 patients following administration of a 200 μg dose, and improved survival outcomes in these patients (NCT01003808) [ ]. On average, patients exhibited progression-free survival for 10 weeks, with a median duration of 8.5 weeks and a range of 6 to 18 weeks (NCT01003808) [ ]. Besides, the Matrix-M-adjuvanted quadrivalent NP influenza vaccine (qNIV) demonstrated good tolerability and elicited both qualitatively and quantitatively superior humoral and cellular immune responses in older adults (n = 2654) compared to inactivated IIV4 vaccine against influenza virus (NCT04120194) [ ]. Another randomised and double-blinded phase I clinical trial demonstrated that gold NP (GNP)-formulated synthetic peptide dengue vaccine (PepGNP-Dengue) enhanced CD8+ T-cells and dengue dextramer + memory cell subsets, activated CD137+CD69+CD8+ T-cells, and differentiated effector memory and central memory CD8+ T-cells against dengue virus when administered at low (GNP with gold content of 14.8 μg) but not in high doses (GNP with gold content of 44.4 μg) in healthy individuals (n = 26) (NCT04935801) [ ]. The study also revealed that a minimum of 10% of participants in the low-dose group and 60% in the high-dose group exhibited at least one positive CD8+ T-cell co-marker (CD107a+CD25+ or CD137+CD69+) at any time point (NCT04935801) [ ].
Liposome-based bioresponsive NPs have undergone extensive clinical evaluation for the treatment of various malignancies, including breast, ovarian, prostate, and hematologic cancers. These nanocarriers offer an effective platform for the delivery of tumour-associated antigens and immunostimulatory agents, facilitating targeted immune activation. One notable example is a liposomal formulation incorporating the HER2 antigen with the AS15 adjuvant, which was evaluated in metastatic breast cancer patients (n = 12) overexpressing HER2 and resistant to trastuzumab. This therapeutic regimen induced robust anti-HER2 antibody production and a strong immunogenic response, achieving an overall survival (OS) rate of 92% at 300 days (NCT00952692) [ ]. Another liposomal formulation, DPX-0907 (also known as DepoVax), presents human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-A2-restricted peptides derived from tumour-associated antigens and was assessed in patients with breast, ovarian, and prostate cancers (NCT01095848) [ ]. Immune responses were observed in all breast cancer patients (3/3), the majority of ovarian cancer patients (5/6), and a subset of prostate cancer patients (3/9), resulting in an overall immunogenicity rate of 61%. Among immune responders, 73% exhibited antigen-specific T-cell responses, with 83% demonstrating responses at two or more post-vaccination time points. Furthermore, 64% of these patients maintained persistent immune responses. Cytokine analysis revealed the induction of multifunctional T-cell memory capable of producing interferon gamma (IFN-γ), TNF-α, and IL-2, indicating a sustained and polyfunctional immune profile (NCT01095848) [ ].
DPX-Survivac represents a next-generation T-cell immunotherapy based on the DPX platform. It is engineered to elicit potent and durable cytotoxic T-cell responses against survivin, a tumour-associated antigen broadly expressed in various solid and hematologic malignancies. This vaccine incorporates HLA class I-restricted survivin peptides and is currently under evaluation in multiple phase I and II clinical trials (NCT01416038, NCT03349450, NCT02323230, NCT03029403, NCT02785250, NCT04920617, NCT03836352, NCT03332576, NCT05243524) [ – ]. Completed trials have demonstrated that DPX-Survivac (administered as a 0.5 mL injection) induces survivin-specific T-cell responses in ≥ 80% of treated patients, as confirmed by IFN-γ ELISPOT analysis of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). Tumour biopsies obtained pre- and post-treatment confirmed the presence of survivin-specific T cells within the TME. In another single-arm phase II trial involving patients with recurrent ovarian cancer (n = 22) (NCT01416038, NCT03349450, NCT02323230, NCT03029403, NCT02785250, NCT04920617, NCT03836352, NCT03332576, NCT05243524) (345–355).
In another single-arm phase II trial involving patients with recurrent ovarian cancer (n = 22) [ ], the vaccine was administered subcutaneously in two priming doses of 0.25 mL on days 7 and 28, followed by up to six booster injections of 0.1 mL every 8 weeks [ ]. Concurrently, patients self-administered low-dose oral cyclophosphamide (50 mg twice daily) on a 7-day-on/7-day-off cycle beginning on day 0 [ ]. Survivin-specific CD8 + T-cell responses were detected in 87% (14/16) of evaluable participants via MHC-tetramer assay. Among 19 evaluable patients, four achieved partial responses (PRs), yielding an objective response rate (ORR) of 21.1%. A reduction in total tumour burden was observed in 10 patients (52.6%), with a disease control rate of 63%, including 4 PRs (21%), 8 cases of stable disease (42%), and 7 instances of progressive disease (37%) [ ].
In another phase II study, patients with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) received two initial subcutaneous injections of DPX-Survivac (0.5 mL) administered 3 weeks apart, followed by up to six 0.1-mL booster doses every 8 weeks [ ]. This regimen was combined with low-dose oral cyclophosphamide (50 mg twice daily; 7 days on/7 days off) and intravenous pembrolizumab (200 mg every 3 weeks) for up to 1 year or until disease progression [ ]. Tumour profiling identified 13 patients with CD20+/PD-L1-positive tumours and 10 with CD20 +/PD-L1-negative tumours. Among the CD20+/PD-L1-positive cohort, the ORR was 46% (6/13), with a disease control rate of 77% (10/13) [ ]. Progression-free survival (PFS) and OS in this subgroup were 7.1 months and 17.4 months, respectively. In the overall intent-to-treat population (n = 25), the ORR was 28% (7/25), with median PFS and OS of 4.2 and 10.1 months, respectively. Notably, 6 of the 7 clinical responses occurred in patients with CD20+/PD-L1-positive tumours [ ]. Collectively, these findings highlight the clinical potential of liposome-based platforms such as DPX-Survivac to generate robust and durable antigen-specific immune responses, with promising implications for the management of both solid tumours and hematologic malignancies.
Lipid-based NP platforms have emerged as versatile and potent vehicles for the delivery of RNA and protein antigens in immunotherapy. These systems have shown significant promise across a range of malignancies and infectious diseases. In the Lipo-MERIT trial, an RNA lipoplex-based vaccine was designed to induce antigen-specific CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ T-cell responses in patients with advanced melanoma. This formulation elicited a high rate of vaccine-induced immunity, demonstrating its immunogenic potential in a difficult-to-treat cancer population (NCT02410733) [ ]. In a separate phase I clinical trial, the intratumoural administration of iTu mRNA-2752, a lipid NP formulation encoding mRNAs for human OX40L (a T-cell co-stimulator), IL-23, and IL-36γ, was evaluated both as a monotherapy and in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors (NCT03739931) [ , ]. This approach resulted in tumour shrinkage and upregulation of PD-L1 expression, as well as increased secretion of immunomodulatory cytokines including TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-6, IL-22, IL-23, and IL-36γ. Among 17 evaluable patients with advanced solid tumours or lymphomas, one patient achieved a partial response (PR) per iRECIST criteria, six exhibited stable disease (SD), and ten showed progressive disease (PD) (NCT03739931) [ ].
A personalised liposomal mRNA-4157 vaccine, administered in combination with adjuvant immunotherapy, demonstrated enhanced clinical efficacy in patients with high-risk resectable melanoma in an ongoing clinical trial (NCT03897881) [ ]. The liposomal vaccine Stimuvax (also known as L-BLP25 or tecemotide), which targets mucin 1 (MUC1), has undergone multiple clinical evaluations. In a phase II study involving 171 patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), those treated with L-BLP25 (n = 88) experienced a median OS extension of 4.4 months compared to patients receiving best supportive care (BSC) [ ]. Similarly, in a randomised phase IIb trial, L-BLP25 was administered as weekly subcutaneous injections (930 µg) for 8 weeks, followed by maintenance doses every 6 weeks (NCT00157209) [ ]. The treatment group showed a median OS of 30.6 months compared to 13.3 months in the BSC group. Notably, the 3-year survival rate in patients with stage IIIB locoregional disease was 49% with L-BLP25, versus 27% with BSC alone (NCT00157209) [ ]. In a large-scale, double-blind phase III trial, tecemotide was evaluated in stage III lung cancer patients. Although the OS benefit did not reach statistical significance in the entire cohort, a predefined subgroup analysis revealed a significant OS advantage in patients who had received concurrent chemoradiotherapy prior to vaccination (NCT00409188) [ , ]. The median OS was 25.6 months in the L-BLP25 group compared to 22.3 months in the placebo group. Among patients who had previously undergone concurrent chemoradiotherapy, those receiving tecemotide (538 of 829; 65%) had a median OS of 30.8 months, whereas those in the placebo group (268 of 410; 65%) had a median OS of 20.6 months [ ]. Further analyses suggested that patients with high levels of MUC1 and antinuclear antibodies may derive greater survival benefits from tecemotide therapy (NCT00409188) (362, 363).
Lipid-NP platforms have also demonstrated remarkable efficacy in vaccine development against infectious diseases. The lipid-NP-based mRNA vaccines for SARS-CoV-2, evaluated in a phase I clinical trial (NCT05477186) [ ], elicited robust immune responses not only against the ancestral strain (BA.1) but also against Omicron subvariants and the Delta variant. These promising results have led to subsequent phase II trials utilising the same nanotechnology (NCT05960097) [ ].
In parallel, the SpFN/ALFQ vaccine, a SARS-CoV-2 WA-1 recombinant spike-ferritin NP co-formulated with Army Liposome Formulation containing QS-21 (ALFQ), demonstrated potent antigen-specific CD4⁺ T-cell responses and sustained neutralising antibody titres against a broad array of SARS-CoV-2 variants and related sarbecoviruses (NCT04784767) [ ].
Beyond oncology and viral infections, lipid-based formulations have shown promise in antifungal therapies. The cochleated amphotericin B formulation (cAMB; MAT2203), a lipid-nanocrystal system for targeted oral delivery, was evaluated in a phase I/II study for the treatment of azole-resistant chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis (NCT02629419) [ ]. Clinical efficacy was observed within 2 weeks, with patients receiving 200–400 mg of cAMB twice daily demonstrating therapeutic response (NCT02629419) [ ]. Lastly, a DepoVax-based vaccine (DPX-RSV) containing a recombinant RSV antigen derived from the ectodomain of the small hydrophobic protein was assessed in healthy individuals (NCT02472548) [ – ]. The vaccine induced antigen-specific antibody titres that were 10–100 times higher than those in the placebo group, with durable immunogenicity observed over an extended period (NCT02472548)[ – ].
Recently, the R21 vaccine, combining a circumsporozoite protein fragment with hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) to create virus-like particles, was created against Malaria. Phase I clinical trials on R21 vaccines have shown excellent immunogenicity against Malaria in healthy adults (NCT02572388, NCT02925403, NCT03970993) [ ]. Phase II trial also showed that this vaccine is well-tolerated with good immunogenicity in healthy volunteers (NCT05252845) [ ]. Phase III trials showed that R21, formulated with Matrix-M (Serum Institute of India, Pvt. Ltd.; Novavax, Inc.), was safe for children aged 5–36 months and showed efficacy against clinical malaria by approximately 75% with seasonal administration and 68% with standard administration over the subsequent year (NCT04704830) [ ].
Other drug nanocarrier bioresponsive materials including Atorvastatin in NPs Gel for periodontitis (NCT05583643), gold-silver-cuprous oxide (Au–Ag-Cu2O) composite nano gel for drug-resistant microbial keratitis (NCT05268718), calcium hydroxide with silver NPs for root canal retreatment (NCT04213716), chitosan nano-hydroxyapatite for the treatment of periodontal defect (NCT06373757), cyclosporine ophthalmic nano-emulsion for dry eye syndrome (NCT02461719), CYT004-MelQβG10, virus-like particle-based vaccine against malignant melanoma (NCT00651703), diclofenac nano emulsion cream for osteoarthritis (NCT00484120), a space maintainer composed of an antimicrobial fibre-reinforced composite resin modified with silver NPs for dental caries (NCT05902975), hyaluronic acid-based nanogel as a non-invasive treatment modality for periocular rejuvenation (NCT05742399), plasmid DNA-based liposomal formulation ALF43 vaccine for HIV infections (NCT04826094), ALFQ for Campylobacter infection (NCT05500417), K12@Lip@GSH for oral mucositis (NCT06446180), Lipovaxin-MM for melanoma (NCT01052142), mRNA-loaded DOTAP for melanoma (NCT05264974), nano calcium hydroxide hydrogel for endodontic disease and pulp disease (NCT06196515), nanofiber hydrogels for spinal cord injury (NCT05967325), autologous tumour vaccine containing IL-2 adjuvant for follicular lymphoma (NCT02194751), nano-ozone-based eye drops for keratitis (NCT05689996), PLGA NPs in smart hydrogel for antibiotic-resistant endodontic infections (NCT05442736), PRECIOUS-01, a NP-based NY-ESO-1 adjuvant containing vaccine for advanced solid tumours (NCT04751786), nanomaterial platinum with sol–gel technology functionalised titania (NPt-Ca) for brain tumours and brain stem glioma (NCT03250520), RNA-lipid-based vaccine for adult glioblastoma, and high-grade glioma (NCT04573140) have been under clinical trials at different phases; however, no outcomes have been published yet.
Collectively, these clinical studies indicate that nanomaterials with targeted signaling capabilities possess significant potential for selectively localising to disease sites, stimulating immune responses, and contributing to disease prevention, treatment, or reduced severity. These nanodrugs offer flexibility, as they can be employed as standalone therapies or as immunoadjuvants in conjunction with existing treatments. Notably, liposomal-based immunovaccine targeted therapies, exemplified by R21/Matrix-M1 molecules, have demonstrated efficacy and garnered recognition from the World Health Organization (WHO). These findings emphasise the promising future of bioresponsive nanomaterials that modulate immune responses in the development of novel preventive and therapeutic strategies for various diseases.

## Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of bioresponsive nanomaterials
Pharmacokinetic evaluations offer insights into the half-life (t1/2) of NPs, their stability within the bloodstream, drug release kinetics, and the interactions with proteins or cells that influence drug distribution and biological activity [ ]. For instance, succinobucol-loaded pH-responsive wormlike micelles (PWMs) showed increased drug release rates of 73.9% at pH 4.7 and 43% at pH 5.5, and specifically accumulated in the lungs, targeting breast cancer lung metastases [ ]. Similarly, acidic pH 6.5 and NIR irradiation co-stimulated drug release from selenium particle@porous silica-folic acid-copper sulphide/DOX (Se@SiO2-FA-CuS/DOX) nanocomposites, effectively inhibited breast tumour growth without causing any significant toxicity or adverse effects on major organs [ ]. Additionally, PDA NPs coated with cucurbitacin B (CuB)-loaded 4T1 breast cancer cell membrane (PDA@MB) released 44.64% of CuB at pH 5.0 within 24 h, which increased up to 64.57% under NIR laser irradiation [ ]. Furthermore, in mice models treated with these NPs, no adverse effects on kidney or liver function were observed, and the main organs of the treated mice maintained normal physiological morphology [ ]. Interestingly, a pH-responsive LHRH-conjugated hydroxyethyl starch-DOX (HES-DOX/LHRH) prodrug demonstrated enhanced drug release at acidic conditions, with 40.1% and 71.2% of DOX released at pH 6.8 and 5.5, respectively, and up to approximately 42.1% and 64.5% for HES-DOX [ ]. Plasma DOX concentrations for the HES-DOX conjugates remained higher post-injection and declined gradually in vivo, with the Cmax values for HES-DOX and HES-DOX/LHRH measured at 5.10, and 5.33 μg/mL, respectively. The area under the concentration–time curve (AUC0-t) for HES-DOX and HES-DOX/LHRH were 21.21 and 19.71 μg/(mL.h), which was 4.3 and 4.0 times higher than free DOX•HCl, indicating prolonged blood circulation and lower clearance rates of the HES-DOX conjugate groups. Ex vivo fluorescence imaging revealed that HES-DOX conjugates induced DOX accumulation in the liver and kidneys at 24 h post-injection compared to free DOX•HCl, with significantly higher tumour cell uptake for HES-DOX/LHRH than for free DOX•HCl or HES-DOX alone [ ].
In another study, a pH-responsive, bone-targeting theranostic system was designed using zoledronic acid (ZA)-anchored, gadolinium(III) (Gd)-doped mesoporous silica-coated upconversion NPs (UCNP-MSs), loaded with plumbagin (PL) and poly(acrylic acid) (PAA) (PLGd-UCNP@ZA-PAA, PUCZP) [ ]. These NPs exhibited enhanced drug release at osteoclast acidic conditions (pH 5.86 and 4.01). The t1/2 of Gd-UCNP@MS-ZA-PAA (UCZP) and PUCZP in blood were 147.24 and 128.37 min, respectively, indicating rapid blood clearance due to strong bone-binding affinity. After 24 h, the NPs were no longer detectable in the blood but were found in bones with breast cancer metastasis, confirming their targeting ability [ ]. Xu et al. developed a multistage NP platform composed of a TME pH-responsive PEGylated polymer and a tumour-targeting, cell-penetrating peptide-amphiphile (TCPA), which disassembled at pH 6.8, exposing the siRNA-TCPA complexes [ ]. These TCPA-NPs, protected by a PEG outer layer, exhibited prolonged blood circulation with a t1/2 ~ 4.38 h, and achieved significantly higher tumour accumulation, with tenfold greater siRNA delivery compared to naked siRNA [ ]. Likewise, the siRNA and amphiphilic cationic mitoxantrone (MTO)-based prodrug (SA-MTO) complex-loaded NPs, encapsulated in the TME pH-responsive polymer methoxyl-poly(ethylene glycol)-b-poly(2-(pentamethyleneimino)ethyl methacrylate) (MeoPEG-b-PPMEMA), exhibiting prolonged blood circulation (t1/2 ~ 1.02 h), demonstrated seven times greater tumour accumulation compared to naked siRNA or free MTO in vivo [ ]. Additionally, when 3D tumour spheroids were treated with the siRNA and SA-MTO-loaded NPs at pH 6.8, the TME pH-triggered disassociation of NPs revealed small siRNA–prodrug complexes with enhanced tissue penetration capacity [ ].
A self-assembled nanodrug system that enabled MMP−2-responsive degradation and co-delivery of hydrophobic antitumour agents, camptothecin (CPT) and retinoic acid (RA), was developed for lung cancer treatment and imaging [ ]. The nanodrugs exhibited higher bloodstream retention compared to free CP or RA after 12 h at a dose of 7.73 mg/kg. Additionally, prolonged retention at the tumour site resulted in enhanced drug accumulation in tumour tissues [ ]. Dai et al. engineered an acidic TME-activated charge-reversal nanosystem (PPDC@β-Lap) with self-amplified ROS-responsive drug release, which demonstrated extended blood circulation time and higher accumulation in the tumour tissues [ ]. This system significantly enhanced drug accumulation in tumour tissues, delivering nearly five times more CPT, indicating effective tumour targeting [ ]. Likewise, neurotensin (NT)-modified CPTD NPs (NT-CPTD NPs) demonstrated significantly improved systemic circulation and tumour targeting compared to free CPT [ ]. While the blood concentration of free CPT declined rapidly, CPTD NPs and NT-CPTD NPs remained detectable 48 h post-administration, with their area under the plasma-concentration curve being 5.7- and 7.6-fold higher, respectively. The NPs had a mean residence time of 5.1 times longer than free CPT, and their clearance was much slower, promoting greater drug accumulation in tumour tissues. NT-CPTD NPs outperformed CPTD NPs in tumour targeting, showing a threefold higher accumulation in tumours between 8 and 24 h post-administration while reducing accumulation in major organs except the liver, where slightly higher accumulation occurred without causing significant toxicity [ ].
Further, protein or enzyme-sensitive nanoplatforms like MMP-9-mediated release of chemotherapy drugs from mesoporous silica NPs, selectively induced apoptosis in lung tumours of KRAS mutant mice without causing toxicity to healthy tissues and non-tumour regions [ ]. Another study reported that GSH triggered degradation of cancer cell membrane-coated mesoporous copper/manganese silicate nanospheres (mCMSNs) within the TME, releasing Fenton-like Cu+ and Mn2+ ions [ ]. Mildly acidic conditions (pH = 6.5) further enhanced ion release and depleted GSH, promoting increased ROS production and boosting antitumour effects, without causing histopathological abnormalities in major organs. The blood retention of mCMSNs was 20.1% ID/g, significantly higher than the 2.3% ID/g observed for bare CMSNs 24 h post-administration. The circulation t1/2 of mCMSNs was 7.07 h, compared to 3.21 h for bare CMSNs, attributed to the protective cancer cell membrane coating. This coating also enhanced tumour accumulation by 2.85 times over CMSNs [ ]. In another study, SD rats were administered an intravenous dose of either GSH-responsive DM NGs or free DOX (5 mg/kg). Blood samples were collected at multiple time points (0.33, 0.5, 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 12, and 24 h) for pharmacokinetic analysis [ ]. The t₁/₂ of DM NGs was calculated to be 1.40 ± 0.52 h, which was significantly longer than that of free DOX (0.31 ± 0.04 h) [ ]. Additionally, the area under the curve (AUC) for DM NGs was found to be 10 times higher than that of free DOX, indicating superior retention. The prolonged circulation of DM NGs was attributed to their nanostructure, which resulted in a slower clearance rate and extended systemic exposure compared to free DOX [ ]. Another study investigated the pharmacokinetics of TAAs-boosted TME-responsive 4T1 cell membrane-cloaked Prussian blue NPs (4T1-PB-CO NPs). The findings demonstrated that 4T1-PB-CO NPs exhibited a significantly prolonged circulation half-life (t₁/₂ = 13 h) compared to PB-CO NPs (t₁/₂ = 2.3 h) [ ]. This extended circulation time was likely attributed to the 4T1 cell membrane coating, which enhanced serum stability and facilitated evasion from rapid clearance by the reticuloendothelial system (RES). Additionally, 4T1-PB-CO NPs exhibited improved solubility, reduced aggregation in circulation, and minimised pulmonary accumulation relative to PB-CO NPs [ ]. In vivo fluorescence imaging using labelled NPs revealed that tumours in mice administered 4T1-PB-CO NPs exhibited stronger and more sustained fluorescence intensity than those injected with PB-CO NPs [ ]. Additionally, fluorescence quantification of excised tumour tissues indicated that the fluorescence intensity in 4T1-PB-CO NP-treated tumours was 1.9-fold higher than that in PB-CO NP-treated tissues [ ]. Collectively, these findings suggest that cell membrane-coated NPs exhibit superior biocompatibility and enhanced tumour-targeting capabilities.
The pH-sensitive rapamycin (RAPA)-loaded theranostic system (RAPA/Gd3 + @NPs) exhibited a rapid release of over 80% of RAPA at pH 6.0 within 4 h, enabling selective accumulation in ischemic tissues under stroke conditions while minimising side effects to normal organs in rat models of transient middle cerebral artery occlusion [ ]. In addition, Zhang and colleagues developed pH/ROS dual-responsive RAPA/AOCD NPs that demonstrated accelerated RAPA release at pH levels of 5 or 6, with 1 mM H2O2 significantly enhancing the release rate [ ]. The NPs exhibited a good safety profile, showing no abnormal changes in liver and kidney biomarkers. Flow cytometry analysis of T and B cells in the spleen indicated no significant alterations after 3 months of RAPA/AOCD NP administration at doses of 1 or 3 mg/kg, suggesting no substantial adverse effects on the adaptive immune system [ ]. The TPP@PMM nanomicelles with a t1/2 of 6.97 h, demonstrated effective ROS responsiveness under 1 mM H2O2, achieving up to 89% of prednisolone delivery after 48 h [ ]. The declining fluorescence intensity in the liver and kidneys indicated systemic metabolism of the NPs. Moreover, intravenous administration of TPP@PMM (100 mg/kg) caused no notable injuries compared to saline, confirming its excellent in vivo biosafety for theranostic applications [ ].
Interestingly, a pH-responsive anti-miR33 nanotherapy (AAM NP) demonstrated rapid release of anti-miR33 at pH 5, with > 96% release achieved within 60 h [ ]. Ex vivo fluorescence imaging revealed that both AAM NP and its pH-responsive cRGDfK-targeting counterpart (RAAM NP) were almost completely cleared from the bloodstream within 24 h with the t1/2 of AAM NP and RAAM NP being comparable, at ~ 4.55 ± 0.20 and 4.51 ± 0.38 h, respectively. Moreover, at 12 h post-injection, both NPs showed enhanced accumulation in the aortas of apolipoprotein E-deficient (ApoE−/−) mice, with cRGDfK-coated NPs exhibiting 1.5 times higher fluorescent intensity in atherosclerotic plaques. RAAM NPs demonstrated particularly high fluorescence in the aortic root and arch and showed significant accumulation in CD68+ macrophages and CD31+ endothelial cells [ ]. Similarly, ROS-scavenging TPCD NP, administered intravenously, accumulated in atherosclerotic lesions by targeting dysfunctional endothelium and inflammatory cells, with clearance from the blood within 24 h and no severe side effects [ ]. TPCD NP also colocalised with immune cells, including CD68+ macrophages, Ly6G+ neutrophils, and Ly6C+ monocytes, in the aortic and brachiocephalic arterial regions [ ]. These findings highlight the effective inhibition of atherosclerosis by these novel nanomaterials.
Another study demonstrated that tissue plasminogen activator (t-PA)-installed, nitroxide radical-containing, self-assembled NPs (t-PA@iRNPs) released t-PA under acidic conditions (pH 6.2), and extended the t1/2 of t-PA from 8.2 to 71.2 min [ ]. While naked t-PA was almost completely cleared within 1 h, 24% of t-PA activity remained at 2 h post-administration of t-PA@iRNPs. These findings suggest that t-PA confinement within the NP core enabled prolonged circulation and effective release of t-PA in the penumbra region [ ]. Lastly, a bioengineered nanocarrier SHp-RBC-NP for targeted stroke treatment was designed for the controlled delivery of the neuroprotective agent NR2B9C in response to elevated intracellular ROS levels in ischemic brain tissue [ ]. The SHp-RBC-NPs demonstrated improved BBB penetration and selective targeting of damaged neurons. Both RBC-NP/NR2B9C and SHp-RBC-NP/NR2B9C exhibited prolonged circulation (over 48 h), with SHp-RBC-NP/NR2B9C showing significantly higher AUC and t1/2 compared to NP/NR2B9C without membrane coverage [ ]. In another investigation, the pharmacokinetic properties of GSH and pH dual responsive NPs, GPNP, were evaluated in comparison to CS(DOX + R848)-PBA. BALB/c mice received an intravenous injection of either CS(DOX + R848)-PBA (100 μg, 100 μL) or GPNP (100 μg, 100 μL). Blood samples were collected via the orbital route at various time intervals (0, 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h) [ ]. The observed elimination half-life of GPNP (t1/2 = 6.24 h) was notably extended relative to that of CS(DOX + R848)-PBA (t1/2 = 4.11 h) [ ]. The extended blood retention and prolonged circulation half-life of GPNP provided a significant advantage by facilitating more effective accumulation at the tumour site [ ].
NPs can be delivered through multiple routes, such as intranasal, intraperitoneal, intravenous, oral, pulmonary, subcutaneous, transdermal, or direct administration at the target site, where they encounter biological barriers influencing drug delivery efficiency [ , ]. Traditional pharmacokinetic analysis consists of two distinct phases: the α-phase, or distribution phase, which evaluates drug release, followed by the β-phase, during which drug clearance occurs once equilibrium is established in plasma and tissues [ ]. Therefore, regulating the biodistribution and elimination of NPs is essential for optimising their pharmacokinetic properties and therapeutic effectiveness. To achieve this, NPs should be engineered to bypass key biological barriers, including scavenging cells such as liver sinusoidal endothelial cells and macrophages within the RES [ , ]. Extensive research has demonstrated that various factors influence the circulation time and organ clearance of NPs, including their size, shape, surface charge, aspect ratio, functional groups, and the structural characteristics of the target tissue [ , , ]. These factors affect the circulation of NPs in the bloodstream, their ability to exit blood vessels, and their clearance pathways within different organs. For instance, NPs smaller than 5 nm are primarily eliminated through renal clearance, whereas those ranging from 10 to 20 nm are efficiently removed via rapid liver uptake [ ]. Particles within the 20–200 nm range are typically sequestered by the RES, while those exceeding 200 nm tend to accumulate in the splenic sinusoids [ , ]. In general, NPs sized between 20 and 200 nm exhibit prolonged circulation in the bloodstream [ ]. The kidneys and liver are the primary organs responsible for NP clearance. Small, spherical NPs with a hydrodynamic diameter of less than 5.5 nm are typically eliminated through urine following opsonisation [ , ]. NPs ranging from 6 to 8 nm can also undergo renal filtration if their surface charge facilitates interaction with the negatively charged glomerular capillary wall. For NPs that do not undergo renal clearance, hepatobiliary excretion serves as the primary elimination pathway [ , ]. Those evading phagocytosis by Kupffer cells are metabolised by hepatocytes, which have been shown to preferentially uptake positively charged NPs over negatively charged ones [ , ]. Following hepatic processing, intact NPs or their degradation products are excreted into bile. While renal clearance occurs rapidly, hepatic clearance can take anywhere from several hours to months for complete elimination [ , ]. Additionally, the shape and composition of NPs play a crucial role in determining their pharmacokinetic behaviour. For instance, cylindrical NPs with a high aspect ratio exhibit faster internalisation kinetics compared to those with a lower aspect ratio [ , ]. Another key factor influencing NPs fate is opsonisation, where protein coating facilitates rapid recognition and subsequent phagocytosis by the RES. To counteract this, surface modifications with agents such as PEG are commonly employed to form a hydrophilic protective layer, which sterically hinders opsonin binding and reduces opsonisation [ – ].
Instability in formulations and coatings often leads to premature burst release or rapid clearance of NPs. To achieve optimal therapeutic effects, nanocarriers should be designed to release their payload only after accumulating at the target site [ , ]. Stimuli-responsive nanocarriers serve as effective strategies to prevent early drug release [ ]. Among the most successful examples are antibody–drug conjugates with cleavable linkers, which enable site-specific drug delivery [ , ]. Additionally, modifying nanocarriers with “don’t eat me” signals, such as CD47, can reduce recognition by the RES, thereby extending circulation time [ ]. Moreover, interactions between NPs and the biophysical environment at the target site can further influence their clearance profiles. These interactions may induce changes in NPs properties, including agglomeration, dissolution, phase transitions, protein adsorption, surface reactivity, and degradation [ , ].
The physicochemical characteristics of NPs undergo continuous changes in vivo, influenced by spatial and temporal factors, making their metabolic processes intricate and difficult to anticipate [ ]. Although the distinct properties of NPs enhance their biomedical applications, their diverse chemical makeup may also lead to unintended exposure and potential health concerns [ ]. The interactions of NPs within the body are closely linked to the tissue microenvironment, where factors such as partition coefficients, cellular uptake, and drug release rates depend on the structural and dynamic properties of tissues [ ]. These complexities pose challenges in designing efficient nanosystems. To address this, physiologically based pharmacokinetic models combined with pharmacodynamics are essential for assessing systemic distribution, accumulation at target sites, drug release patterns, clearance mechanisms, and toxicity [ , ]. As nanoplatforms progress from preclinical research to clinical application, a thorough understanding of biological interactions influencing immunogenicity and metabolism is vital to ensuring the development of safer and more effective NPs for improved therapeutic efficacy [ ].
The physicochemical characterisation of bioresponsive NPs is crucial for understanding their behaviour in both in vitro and in vivo environments [ ]. While most studies focus primarily on in vitro characterisation, only a limited number extend their analyses to in vivo conditions. However, these characterisations in animal models may not accurately reflect their behaviour in humans due to differences in biocorona formation, aggregation, and degradation dynamics [ ]. Clinical investigations further shed light on the pharmacokinetics of bioresponsive nanomaterials [ , – ]. For instance, the oral amphotericin B (AMB) formulation called cochleated AMB (cAMB) exhibited consistent pharmacokinetics, with AUC₀-₄₈ maintaining a median of 2200 ng·h/mL across dosing cohorts [ ]. The median t1/2 was observed to be 48 h, with Cmax averaging 59.2 ng/mL. Notably, plasma concentrations remained stable, with 74.8 ng/mL at 24 h and 91.1 ng/mL at 72 h, reflecting sustained drug exposure [ ]. Furthermore, a later study reported that after a single 100 mg dose, cAMB achieved a Cmax of 28.4 ng/mL at 16 h, rising to 71.92 ng/mL following 14 days of continuous 100 mg twice daily dosing [ ]. Median AMB exposure (AUC0–24) increased more than 2.5-fold from 507 to 1357.05 ng‧h/mL upon continuous dosing. Elimination showed a median clearance at 201.67 L/h and t1/2 of 17.15 h. The proportion of the drug eliminated through urine and faeces was exceptionally low (< 1.25%) [ ]. In another phase I clinical trial, CYT-6091, a nanomedicine composed of recombinant human TNF-α and thiolated PEG bound to the surface of gold particles, demonstrated a consistent terminal t½, irrespective of dose, suggesting first-order elimination kinetics [ ]. AUC and Cmax values, however, increased with dose in an approximately monotonic manner [ ]. Additionally, BMS-986263, a lipid NP designed to deliver small interfering RNA targeting heat shock protein 47 (HSP47), showed dose-proportional increases in plasma concentrations between 45 and 90 mg doses [ ]. Plasma levels rose during the infusion, peaking at the end, and rapidly declined post-infusion with no significant accumulation observed during weekly dosing [ ].
Bioresponsive nanomedicine has emerged as a promising approach for disease treatment by enabling precisely triggered therapeutic actions. They offer enhanced tumour targeting and reduced off-target effects possibly via the enhanced permeability and retention effect [ , ]. These nanomaterials exhibit exceptional stability and safety profiles while responding rapidly to biological signals [ , , , , , , ]. This allows for efficient, site-specific drug delivery, particularly for macromolecular therapeutics such as proteins, DNA, and siRNA, offering significantly improved therapeutic outcomes compared to non-responsive systems [ , , , ]. The unique advantage of bioresponsive nanomaterials lies in their ability to maintain high stability under normal physiological conditions while facilitating drug release in diseased environments, effectively addressing the challenge of balancing long circulation times with targeted drug release [ , , , , , , , , ]. As a result, bioresponsive nanomedicines represent a more intelligent and precise therapeutic strategy compared to conventional treatments.

## Safety and tolerability of bioresponsive nanomaterials
Ensuring clinical safety is essential, as many drugs considered safe in preclinical animal and cellular models have been found unsafe during human clinical trials. This section, therefore, addresses the safety and tolerability concerns associated with immunotherapeutic nanomaterials. CYT-6091, a construct comprising colloidal gold, recombinant human tumour necrosis factor (rhTNF), and thiolated PEG, demonstrated enhanced immunogenicity and tumour targeting capabilities (NCT00356980) [ ]. Doses ranging from 50 to 600 μg/m2 were well tolerated, and no maximum tolerated dose (MTD) was identified, exceeding the previously established MTD for native rhTNF by a factor of three (NCT00356980) [ ].
A first-in-human phase I trial of AST-008–102 on healthy volunteers showed good tolerance, no dose-limited toxicities, and no serious side effects for the four dose levels 5, 10, 12.5, and 18.8 µg/kg tested (NCT03086278) [ ]. The administration of AST-008-102 successfully elicited Th1-mediated immune responses. Further studies on patients with solid tumours showed excellent safety and tolerability with durable response at the maximum dose of 32 mg administered at the intratumoural site (NCT03684785) [ ]. In a phase I dose-escalating trial, the IMF-001 nanovaccine was administered 100 and 200 μg doses to oesophageal cancer patients (n = 25). The study showed that a 200 μg dosage is more effective in eliciting immune responses (NCT01003808) [ ]. The study reported grade 3 lymphopenia (n = 10) and other grade 3 and 4 adverse events including swallowing disturbance (n = 8), diarrhoea (n = 3), and fever (n = 2). The study mentioned that these side effects are unlikely due to the vaccination and recommended a 200 μg dose of IMF-001 for higher phase trials (NCT01003808) [ ]. Peptide-based synthetic dengue vaccine PepGNP-Dengue also showed favourable safety and tolerability among healthy volunteers (NCT04935801) [ ]. Engineered bioresponsive nanomaterial carriers have also been utilised for the development of effective immunogenic vaccines against SARS-CoV-2 variants and have shown acceptable safety profile and tolerability among healthy volunteers (NCT05477186, NCT04784767) [ , ]. Another phase I clinical trial examined the safety and tolerability of cAMB formulation administered at daily doses of 1.0 g, 1.5 g, or 2.0 g, divided into 4 to 6 doses, among HIV-positive individuals recovering from cryptococcosis (n = 9). At 1.0 g dose, mild adverse events were observed in two subjects. At 1.5 g, adverse effects were linked to gastroenteritis (n = 7). At 2.0 g, five subjects experienced more frequent adverse side effects, including one grade 3 event. This study also demonstrated that 89% (8/9) of participants met full tolerability criteria at a dose of 2.0 g/day, which was generally safe and well-tolerated without nephrotoxicity in healthy HIV-positive Ugandans (NCT04031833) [ ]. Also, the phase II trial conducted by Desai and colleagues reported good safety and tolerability of cAMB in patients with azole-resistant chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis (NCT02629419) [ ]. Another clinical trial evaluating the safety and effectiveness of oral cAMB for vulvovaginal candidiasis reported excellent safety and tolerability at a dosage of 400 mg/day, with no serious adverse effects observed (NCT02971007). These studies observed no indications of renal, hepatic, or hematologic toxicity during its course (NCT02971007, NCT02629419, NCT04031833) [ , ]. Several clinical trials have demonstrated the safety and tolerability of the R21/Matrix-M1 nano vaccine resulting in its approval in Ghana and Nigeria and subsequently WHO later recommended its use for populations 9 years after the R21 antigen’s patent (NCT04704830, NCT02572388, NCT02925403) [ ]. Other currently ongoing clinical trials on R21/Matrix-M1 vaccine include NCT06080243 on healthy women with child bearing capacity, NCT05385510 in African children with HIV, NCT06320535 multi-prime vaccination in healthy adults, NCT03896724 in 5–17-month-old children, NCT03580824 in 5-month to 45-year-old individuals, NCT02905019 in healthy adults, NCT06578572 conducted for seasonal mass vaccination (phase III), NCT06068530 for mass vaccination in Bangladesh, NCT05155579 in 6-week to 36-month-old children, NCT06507605 in 18–50-year-old adults, NCT05357560 in 5-month-old children to 45-year-old adults, NCT03947190 in 18–45-year-old adults, and NCT06461026 in 1–12-month-old children. In addition, another therapeutic liposome-based tumour vaccine, DPX-0907, presenting seven HLA-A2-restricted peptides originating from TAAs achieved primary end-point safety in phase I trials for treating breast, ovarian, and prostate cancer patients [ , ]. Several clinical trials have also shown the acceptable safety profile and manageable adverse side events for DPX-based survivin targeting DPX-Survivac vaccine against lymphomas and solid peritoneal tumours (NCT01416038, NCT03349450, NCT02323230, NCT03029403, NCT02785250, NCT04920617, NCT03836352, NCT03332576, NCT05243524) [ – ]. In another clinical trial, the DPX-based vaccine, DPX-RSV antigen against RSV infection showed an acceptable safety profile (NCT02472548) [ – ]. Moreover, a phase II clinical trial on BMS-986263, a retinoid-conjugated lipid NP (LNP) containing HSP47 siRNA, that specifically targets retinol-binding protein expressed on hepatic stellate cells, showed that the drug is generally safe and well-tolerated when treated for 12 weeks at 45 and 90 mg doses in patients with advanced hepatic fibrosis (NCT03420768) [ ]. Further, Lipovaxin-MM, a bioengineered liposome coated with tumour antigens, such as the melanocyte differentiation antigens gp100, melanA/MART-1, and tyrosinase derived from plasma membrane vesicles, was reported to be well-tolerated and free of clinically significant toxicity in metastatic cutaneous melanoma patients [ ]. This study observed that among 94 adverse events reported in 10 subjects, 43 adverse effects in six subjects were deemed possibly or probably vaccine-related. Most of these were grade 1 or 2 (95%), with two grade 3 events (anaemia and lethargy) [ ]. The liposomal mRNA-2752 drug was shown to be well tolerated and safe to treat patients with relapsed/refractory solid tumour malignancies or lymphoma when treated at 0.25 to 8 mg once every 2 weeks. The recommended dose for expansion in the study is 8 mg of mRNA-2752 (NCT03739931) [ ]. Furthermore, a phase I trial showed that autologous non-genetically modified multi-clonal T-cell product loaded with an IL15-Fc nanogel namely, RPTR-147, exhibits an acceptable safety profile and well-tolerated in patients with solid tumours and lymphoma (NCT03815682) [ ]. Another liposomal immunotherapy vaccine, Stimuvax, also has not shown any significant safety concerns in patients with advanced lung cancer diseases [ , ]. Several studies have reported severe adverse events linked to nano-immunotherapeutics. For example, a phase II clinical trial assessing the efficacy of the BLP25 liposome vaccine in combination with bevacizumab and chemotherapy for lung cancer patients identified serious complications, including grade 4–5 haemorrhage, oesophagitis, fistula formation, thrombocytopaenia, encephalitis infection, and hepatic failure (NCT00828009). These results highlight the importance of carefully selecting both the nanomaterial and the therapeutic agents used in combination. Ensuring compatibility between the nanocarrier and the drug is crucial to minimise toxicity and enhance therapeutic efficacy. Additionally, optimising drug combinations can help mitigate adverse effects while maximising the immune response. Standardising dosage regimens is equally essential to achieve consistent and safe clinical outcomes.
In the recent global vaccination effort against SARS-CoV-2, over a billion individuals have received mRNA lipid-based NPs, including Spikevax (Moderna) and Comirnaty (Pfizer-BioNTech). These vaccines utilise lipid NPs coated with linear methoxy-PEG to enhance stability and delivery efficiency [ ]. Initial data on the widespread exposure to PEG through these vaccines indicate a strong safety profile, with only rare instances of anaphylaxis. Reports suggest an occurrence of approximately 2.8 to 4.7 anaphylactic reactions per million vaccinated individuals, highlighting the overall low risk associated with PEG-containing mRNA vaccines [ , ]. Additionally, the FDA Briefing Document for Moderna’s clinical trials indicated that 0.03% of vaccinated individuals experienced myocardial infarction. Likewise, data from the Pfizer-BioNTech COVID-19 vaccine trials reported occurrences of cardiac arrest, ventricular arrhythmia, and acute myocardial infarction in 0.02% of recipients [ ]. The risk of myocarditis following mRNA-1273 vaccination is notably higher in younger males, particularly after the second dose. A study reported approximately 1.316 additional cases per 10,000 in males aged 12–19 within a week post-vaccination, while another found 1.88 additional cases per 10,000 in boys aged 16–24 within 4 weeks. As of May 28, 2021, the Pfizer-BioNTech Series Drug Analysis Print documented 2342 cases of cardiac-related adverse events linked to their COVID-19 vaccine. These included 1098 instances of palpitations, 466 cases of tachycardia, 108 reports of atrial fibrillation, and 94 occurrences of myocardial infarction. Additionally, 62 cases of cardiac arrest, 63 of cardiac flutter, 32 of sinus tachycardia, and 46 of angina pectoris were recorded. Other reported events comprised 24 cases of cardiac failure, 38 of arrhythmia, and 16 of acute myocardial infarction [ ].
Collectively, these findings suggest that bioresponsive immunogenic nanomaterials demonstrate favourable safety profiles and are generally well-tolerated by patients. Liposomal-based neoantigens and vaccines, developed for cancer and infectious diseases, have demonstrated effective immunogenicity and have not raised significant safety concerns in clinical settings. Comparative clinical trials have demonstrated the superiority of nanoformulations over free-drug formulations. Notably, most of the bioresponsive nanodrugs have not induced systemic immune-related adverse events, a potentially life-threatening concern associated with immune therapies. Consequently, further clinical trials are warranted to advance the development of various nanomaterials and bring them to clinical application. However, few studies have reported severe grade 4 and 5 adverse events, highlighting significant safety concerns. These findings emphasise the necessity of thorough risk assessment before clinical translation. Ensuring patient safety requires rigorous evaluation of nanotherapeutics to mitigate potential risks. Moreover, clinical evidence indicates that PEGylation is generally safe, and mRNA lipid-based NPs have been widely used for vaccination with an overall favourable safety profile. Initial observations support their efficacy; however, long-term studies have identified potential life-threatening adverse events, particularly in young adults. These complications can contribute to significant morbidity, raising concerns about delayed effects. As a result, rigorous assessment of preliminary safety data is essential before the widespread application of specific nanomaterials. A thorough evaluation will help mitigate risks and ensure the safe development of nanotechnology-based vaccines.

## Current limitations and future perspectives
Compared to traditional DDSs, stimuli-responsive nanomaterials offer notable advantages, particularly in their capacity to respond dynamically to physiological triggers [ , ]. However, several challenges remain in leveraging these materials for clinical applications. A key issue lies in the chemistry, manufacturing, and control (CMC) processes. Large-scale production is hindered by uncontrollable stability and significant batch-to-batch variability, coupled with high manufacturing costs [ , ]. Thus, achieving high-quality control and scalable fabrication methods for these nanomaterials is imperative. In addition, biobased materials, such as cell membranes, must be rigorously purified to eliminate cell-based and pathogen-related contaminants. Denatured proteins should also be removed to avoid unintended immune responses against endogenous antigens. Therefore, reliable and mature preparation techniques are essential to meet production requirements, along with the establishment of robust quality control standards during CMC development [ ].
The systematic development of bioresponsive NPs for targeted therapy process begins with identifying disease-specific stimuli, followed by drug encapsulation or conjugation to enhance therapeutic precision. Physicochemical characterisation assesses stability, size, charge, and surface properties. ECM and cellular mimicry optimise biomimetic interactions. In vitro studies evaluate drug release, uptake, and therapeutic effects, while in vivo testing ensures biocompatibility and efficacy in preclinical models. Transitioning to human biocompatibility assessments, the process advances through evaluation, large-scale production, and long-term monitoring, ensuring clinical feasibility and therapeutic success. Figure  provides a detailed illustration of the sequential processes involved, from the selection of disease-specific stimuli and NP formulations to their characterisation, in vitro and in vivo evaluation, and clinical translation.
 A comprehensive understanding of the interactions between nanocarrier systems and biological components in vivo is equally critical. The complexity of human physiological systems can lead to non-specific distributions of stimuli-responsive carriers [ ]. Despite substantial progress in the development of enzyme-responsive DDSs, several challenges persist, hindering their clinical implementation. A key challenge lies in designing amphiphiles that can self-assemble into vesicles while maintaining enzyme degradability. Achieving the right balance between structural stability and enzymatic responsiveness is crucial for effective drug delivery [ , ]. Optimising amphiphilic composition and molecular architecture is essential to ensure vesicle formation and controlled degradation [ , ]. Moreover, enzyme expression varies across tumour types and stages, with some also elevated in other diseases. Thus, DDSs must be tailored to tumour subtype and patient conditions [ ]. Additionally, the liver, rich in hydrolases, captures foreign materials, leading to premature drug release and toxicity. Optimising enzyme-responsive DDSs with protective layers can reduce liver accumulation and enhance specificity [ ]. Similarly, extensive research on pH- and redox-responsive NPs for drug delivery has yet to overcome critical barriers to clinical translation. Tumour pH varies by type, stage, and location, decreasing toward the hypoxic core, where drug release is hindered by interstitial pressure and physical barriers [ , ]. Similarly, redox-responsive DDSs risk off-target effects, as elevated ROS and GSH levels are also present in hepatocytes and inflammatory sites [ , ]. Besides, endosomal acidity impairs GSH-induced disulphide cleavage, necessitating efficient endosomal escape [ , , ]. Moreover, inflammation sites exhibit acidity, necessitating careful NP design to optimise efficacy and minimise toxicity. Thus, rational design integrating multi-responsiveness is essential for optimising therapeutic efficacy and minimising unintended toxicity [ ]. Also, normal cells or non-tumour tissues may exhibit low pH, elevated GSH concentrations, or elevated expression of certain enzymes, potentially triggering non-specific responses or insufficient sensitivity of the carrier system [ ]. Besides, individual biological variability from patient to patient, such as differences in tumour cell pH and blood metabolite levels, can contribute to heterogeneous therapeutic outcomes. This heterogeneity highlights the necessity of personalised approaches to optimise the efficacy of stimuli-responsive DDSs. By addressing these challenges, bio-based nanomaterials can be better adapted to meet clinical demands and deliver consistent, high-quality therapeutic outcomes [ ].
External stimuli-responsive NPs face several inherent limitations, with the primary drawback being their reliance on external devices for activation, which reduces their convenience [ ]. Additionally, treatments such as those requiring light or strong magnetic fields are often restricted to clinical settings due to practical constraints [ ]. While magneto-responsive DDSs offer a remote, non-invasive therapeutic approach, several challenges hinder their clinical translation. MNPs exhibit toxicity at high doses, and prolonged exposure to high-frequency magnetic fields may induce peripheral nerve damage and cardiac complications [ , ]. Additionally, MNPs are less effective for treating metastatic or disseminated tumours. Further, achieving precise targeting for deep-seated tumours requires complex and costly setups [ ]. Despite these challenges, magneto-responsive NPs remain advantageous for localised, well-defined tumours situated in anatomically challenging or high-risk surgical locations, where conventional interventions may be impractical [ ]. The safety and economic feasibility of advanced light sources necessitate further investigation and optimisation. Although NIR light can penetrate tissues up to 10 cm beneath the skin, biological components along its path significantly absorb NIR, potentially reducing its efficacy [ , ]. Additionally, certain DDSs require prolonged, high-intensity irradiation for activation, raising concerns about potential adverse effects and safety risks [ ].
A major challenge in developing thermosensitive DDSs is the precise prediction and regulation of material and tissue temperatures. Ensuring that drug release does not occur at physiological temperature is crucial to preventing premature payload release [ ]. Achieving homogeneous heating in tumours or target tissues remains challenging due to variations in vascularisation and heat dissipation, leading to inconsistent drug release and reduced therapeutic efficacy [ , ]. Notably, tumour temperature is not always elevated compared to surrounding tissues and can, in some cases, be lower. Therefore, precise control over (i) heating timing for optimal drug release and (ii) heat intensity and duration to balance therapeutic efficacy, minimise hyperthermia-induced side effects, and prevent drug resistance is essential [ ]. A key limitation of ultrasound-responsive DDSs is the low sonosensitivity of small nanocarriers (< 250 nm), which favours stability but weakens ultrasound response [ , ]. Sonosensitive materials improve reactivity but reduce circulation half-life. Ultrasound struggles near air-filled regions, and HIFU, though effective for deep tissues, requires MRI guidance, increasing system complexity and cost [ ].
Materials exhibiting stimuli-responsive behaviour, photo-induced chemical reactivity, or photosensitising properties often have complex chemical compositions, necessitating thorough assessment of their biocompatibility, biodegradability, and the safety of their degradation products [ ]. Similarly, concerns regarding the biocompatibility and biodegradability of certain photothermal materials must be addressed. Additionally, the safety and economic feasibility of advanced nanomaterials and light and magnetic sources require further investigation and optimisation to ensure their suitability for clinical applications [ ].
The design of bioresponsive materials for biomedical applications should prioritise translational feasibility over the incorporation of complex structural modifications or novel methodologies. Many “overdesigned” systems, while scientifically innovative, often involve intricate mechanisms of action that limit their exploration beyond preclinical studies [ ]. To advance this field, further preclinical investigations are necessary to elucidate critical yet underexplored aspects of nanocarriers, including their accumulation, biodistribution, processing, and elimination within living organisms [ ]. Nonetheless, the successful clinical translation of such nanodrugs relies heavily on cost-effectiveness, ensuring that their development and commercialisation remain financially viable [ ].
As the demand for NPs continues to rise in medical, pharmaceutical, and nutritional applications, ensuring high reproducibility, uniformity, and precise property control is essential. Consequently, scaling up production processes becomes inevitable [ ]. NPs are predominantly produced using batch processes, leading to inconsistencies in particle size and product quality across different batches. Traditional laboratory synthesis methods often suffer from poor reproducibility and significant batch-to-batch variations, making their industrial application challenging [ , ]. Achieving efficient scalability necessitates a comprehensive understanding of reaction kinetics and transport phenomena rather than relying on a trial-and-error approach. Additionally, optimising NP synthesis and manufacturing remains a complex and time-intensive task, requiring precise control over process parameters to ensure consistency and reliability in large-scale production [ , ]. The FDA has mandated pharmaceutical manufacturers to transition from batch to continuous processing by 2026. Consequently, chemical flow processes are gradually becoming the norm in pharmaceutical manufacturing. This shift is expected to set a new standard for large-scale NPs production [ ]. Establishing standardised protocols for the synthesis, characterisation, and assessment of safety and risk of developing potential adverse side events attributed to nanocarriers is essential to obtain the approval from regulatory agencies and for the subsequent clinical implementation [ ].
Conclusively, several critical steps must be integrated cohesively to construct bioresponsive systems effectively. These include selecting an appropriate modality, identifying the target biological cue, and choosing suitable responsive building blocks. Two fundamental criteria should guide this process. First, is response efficacy (basic performance), which encompasses high selectivity, sensitivity, and precise timing of response actions. Second, is translation potential (clinical performance), which involves desirable stability, excellent biocompatibility, and scalability for mass production. With their remarkable versatility and wide-ranging applications, bio-based material-derived stimuli-responsive systems are poised to play an increasingly vital role in biomedical science. These systems hold immense potential to address existing therapeutic challenges and unlock new opportunities for advancing healthcare, ultimately benefiting humanity.

## Discussion
Over the past decade, significant advancements in chemistry, material science, nanotechnology, pharmaceutics, and molecular biology have driven extensive exploration of bioresponsive smart nanomaterials [ , , , , , ]. A deeper understanding of disease pathophysiology has facilitated the design of nanomaterials capable of site-specific drug delivery and minimising adverse effects [ ].
Various nanomaterials have been engineered to target the acidic microenvironment (low pH) and elevated ROS/GSH levels at inflammatory sites. Additionally, NPs that selectively recognise overexpressed proteins such as MMP-2, RAGE, NE, and mannose receptors have been developed to treat a range of diseases [ , , , ]. Nanomaterials responsive to external stimuli, including mechanical forces, light, and magnetic fields, have also been designed [ – ]. More recently, multi-stimuli-responsive NPs have emerged, aiming to enhance target site specificity [ , , – ]. Immunomodulatory therapeutics have revolutionised the treatment of various disorders, and nanomaterials are increasingly utilised to modulate the immune system, promoting anti-inflammatory responses. A vast body of research on these NPs and their immune-targeting capabilities has been critically reviewed in this study. Collectively, these findings highlight the ability of bioresponsive nanomaterials to selectively and specifically target disease sites by distinguishing pathological stimuli from normal physiological signals. Smart nanomaterials that enhance DC maturation and subsequent T-cell activation have demonstrated efficacy in reducing primary and metastatic tumour growth across various tumour models, including brain, breast, colon, liver, lung, melanoma, and pancreas [ , , , , , , – , , , – , – , , , , , , – , , ]. Similarly, macrophage-targeting NPs developed for cardiovascular diseases and cancer have shown remarkable in vitro and in vivo efficacy [ , , , , , , , , , – , , , – , – , – ]. Additionally, eosinophil-targeting NPs, designed to treat airway inflammation and allergic asthma, have effectively mitigated eosinophil-related complications and alleviated symptoms [ , , , ]. However, bioresponsive NPs targeting other immune cells remain relatively scarce.
A patient’s immune response to immunotherapy is influenced by factors such as HLA type, gut microbiota composition, tumour mutational burden, and T-cell infiltration [ ]. Notably, anti-CTLA-4 therapy benefits only ~ 20% of melanoma patients [ ]. In lung cancer, phase III trials revealed that anti-PD-1 agents improved survival in patients with > 50% PD-L1 expression (KEYNOTE-024) but not in those with > 5% (CheckMate-026) (NCT02142738, NCT02041533). Immunotherapies also exhibit distinct toxicity profiles, with inflammation-related adverse effects like colitis and hepatitis being more prevalent than conventional chemotherapy toxicities. Enhancing response rates while minimising side effects remains a critical challenge in cancer immunotherapy development. As a result, the development of immunotherapeutic nanomedicines necessitates distinct preclinical and clinical evaluation criteria compared to conventional therapies. Despite substantial progress and a growing number of studies demonstrating the immunomodulatory potential of these NPs in vitro and in vivo, only a limited number of technologies have advanced to clinical trials, with even fewer achieving commercial availability. Numerous trials have confirmed the efficacy and safety of the R21/Matrix-M1 NP-based vaccine in eliciting a robust immunogenic response against Plasmodium falciparum, the causative agent of malaria. This success has led to the vaccine’s commercial approval in Nigeria and Ghana and subsequent recommendation by the WHO [ ]. Currently, phase III and phase IV clinical trials are ongoing in malaria-prone regions, focusing on children and women of childbearing age (NCT06578572, NCT06068530, NCT04704830) [ ]. These promising outcomes highlight the potential of immunogenic and target-specific NPs for the prevention and prophylaxis of other diseases. Site-specific NPs such as AST-008-101, AST-008-102 and CHP-NY-ESO-1 have completed initial clinical trials and demonstrated their ability to elicit robust immune responses (NCT03086278, NCT03684785, NCT01003808) [ , , ]. These trials highlighted their efficacy in treating solid tumours, emphasising their therapeutic potential. Consequently, these NPs warrant progression to subsequent phases of clinical evaluation. Further, liposomal-based nanotherapies, such as DepoVax, have gained significant recognition and are currently undergoing multiple clinical trials for the treatment of various infectious and non-infectious diseases (NCT02472548, NCT01095848) [ , – ]. These nanoengineered materials, including liposomal-based vaccines, have demonstrated an acceptable safety profile with manageable adverse events. These findings highlight the potential of nanomaterials to advance clinical applications and provide optimism that inflammatory diseases can be effectively addressed through nano-based therapeutic approaches.
Further, clinical studies confirm that PEGylation is generally safe; nevertheless, anti-PEG antibodies have been detected in patients over time. This raises safety concerns, as they may reduce therapeutic efficacy and increase the risk of hypersensitivity reactions [ , ]. This immune response could potentially compromise the long-term safety and effectiveness of PEGylated nanomedicines. The mRNA lipid-based NPs have been extensively utilised in vaccination with a favourable safety profile. The FDA has approved these nanovaccines for the SARS-CoV-2 virus [ , ]. While early findings demonstrate their effectiveness, long-term research has revealed rare but serious adverse events, particularly in young adults, which may lead to significant morbidity. These potential delayed effects highlight the need for comprehensive safety evaluations before broad implementation [ , ]. Therefore, conducting rigorous assessments of preliminary safety data is crucial to minimise risks and ensure the advancement of nanotechnology-based vaccines, ultimately supporting their safe and effective integration into medical applications.
Numerous nanomaterials have exhibited exceptional immune-eliciting capabilities, contributing to disease prevention and therapeutic efficacy in vivo. However, their complex synthesis procedures and associated costs have posed significant barriers to their widespread commercial implementation in clinical settings. Consequently, while bioresponsive nanomaterials show immense promise for preventing and treating a range of diseases through immunomodulation with minimal adverse effects, future research must focus on overcoming these challenges. Addressing these limitations is essential to facilitate the translation of these innovations from the laboratory to clinical practice.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, bioresponsive nanomaterials including pH-sensitive, redox-responsive, ligand-targeted, and multi-stimuli-adaptive platforms offer a paradigm shift in immunomodulatory therapeutics by enabling precise, site-specific intervention across diverse pathological conditions such as infections, malignancies, autoimmune diseases, and chronic inflammatory disorders. Despite their immense potential, clinical translation remains constrained by technical challenges in synthesis, scalability, batch reproducibility, and cost-effectiveness. Therefore, advancing this field requires concerted efforts in optimising fabrication technologies, establishing standardised production protocols, and conducting comprehensive preclinical and clinical evaluations. Addressing these critical factors is essential to fully realise the clinical promise of bioresponsive nanomaterials and integrate them effectively into immunotherapeutic strategies.
