## Abstract
Metastasis remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality. The concept of the pre-metastatic niche (PMN) has provided a new framework for understanding how tumors establish favorable conditions in distant organs before metastatic colonization. This review delineates the cellular and molecular hallmarks of PMN, including immune suppression, vascular/lymphatic remodeling, metabolic reprogramming, and stromal reorganization, and traces their spatiotemporal evolution from initiation to colonization. It further examines the origins of metastatic lesions, with a focus on circulating tumor cells (CTCs) and stromal stem-like cells, highlights the pivotal role of extracellular vesicles (EVs) in mediating intercellular communication, metabolic reprogramming, and therapeutic applications. Deciphering the immune and stromal determinants of PMN formation offers key mechanistic insight into organ-specific metastasis. Consequently, this review explores translational strategies targeting the PMN, such as biomarker development, spatiotemporal profiling aided by artificial intelligence (AI), and immune, metabolic, or EV-based interventions. Deciphering PMN biology is therefore poised to open new avenues for the early interception and treatment of metastasis.
Keywords: Pre-metastatic niche, Immune remodeling, Extracellular vesicles, Metabolic reprogramming, Spatiotemporal evolution

## Introduction
Metastasis, which accounts for nearly 90% of cancer-related deaths, remains largely refractory to current therapies such as surgery, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. This underscores a critical unmet clinical need in the early interception of metastatic spread.
However, effective strategies to predict and intercept metastasis remain elusive, largely due to the profound heterogeneity of tumors. This heterogeneity arises from variations in primary and metastatic organ sites, as well as individual differences in factors such as disease progression. In theory, tumor cells can reach all organs of the body through the blood circulation, however, successful metastasis and colonization occur only in specific organs, a phenomenon known as organ tropism. This organotropism occurs because metastatic success depends not only on the tumor cells themselves but also on the prior formation of a receptive microenvironment in distant organs, known as the pre-metastatic niche (PMN) [ ]. Advances in high-resolution technologies, including spatially resolved multi-omics and single-cell sequencing, are increasingly revealing the complexity of PMN formation, unveiling underlying patterns and profound heterogeneity [ – ]. The PMN is defined explicitly as a supportive microenvironment in a distant organ that is remodeled by factors from the primary tumor to facilitate future metastatic colonization. Key hallmarks of the PMN, which evolve in a defined spatiotemporal manner, have been gradually characterized by scientists, including immunosuppression, inflammatory signaling, angiogenesis, lymphangiogenesis, organotropism, and metabolic reprogramming [ , , ]. Increasing evidence suggests that these features operate in a coordinated hierarchy during niche establishment and maturation.
Clinical evidence indicates that while metastasis is common, the preferred sites vary significantly across cancer types [ , ]. This organotropism may be driven by the intrinsic properties of disseminated tumor cells and by the activation or aberrant differentiation of resident stem cells within the target organ. Cells shed from the primary tumor enter the circulation as circulating tumor cells (CTCs), traveling via the bloodstream or lymphatic system to distant organs. In addition to that, successful colonization and outgrowth require a receptive microenvironment. CTCs must survive fluid shear stress and immune surveillance in the circulation [ , ]. Simultaneously, CTCs must undergo metabolic adaptations to maintain viability and fitness, enabling them to reach and seed the PMN [ ]. Therefore, resident stromal stem-like cells within the PMN can be activated by tumor-derived signals. These cells may then undergo malignant transformation, driven by epigenetic modifications, metabolic adaptation, and other mechanisms, acquiring tumorigenic properties [ , ]. This interplay between disseminating “seeds” (CTCs) and receptive “soil” (the PMN) echoes Paget’s classic 1889 hypothesis, which this review aims to update with contemporary mechanistic insights.
The rapid development of spatial omics and single-cell sequencing technologies has ushered in a new paradigm for deciphering the spatial heterogeneity and dynamic evolution of the PMN. These tools allow three-dimensional dissection of PMN architecture, cellular interactions and epigenetic landscapes, thereby clarifying metastatic mechanisms and revealing novel diagnostic and therapeutic opportunities. Intercellular communication is central to both PMN formation and CTCs colonization, driving key processes including inflammatory signaling, immunosuppression, and epigenetic reprogramming [ ]. Tumor-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as key systemic mediators that orchestrating immune suppression, vascular permeability, and stromal remodeling in distant organs. By shuttling proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids, EVs reprogram the distant microenvironment to form a PMN and help dictate organotropic metastasis [ , ]. Recent studies identify a wide array of cellular, molecular, and systemic factors involved in PMN formation. Building on this foundation, increasing attention has shifted toward identifying shared principles that link PMN biology to biomarker-guided risk stratification and interception. In this context, immune regulation, CTCs dynamics, EVs signaling, and data-driven profiling approaches are emerging as interconnected components of a coherent translational framework. However, many studies primarily focused on individual pathways or cell types, leaving the functional coordination of PMN components across space and time less clearly defined.
In this review, we propose a hierarchical and layered framework of PMN formation that organizes diverse mechanisms into permissive, instructive, and selective processes. By integrating insights from spatial omics, metabolic regulation, neuro-immune interactions, and translational studies, we aim to move beyond component-based descriptions toward a systems-level understanding of PMN biology. This perspective highlights PMNs as dynamic and evolving ecosystems, providing a conceptual basis for identifying context-specific vulnerabilities amenable to therapeutic intervention.

## Hallmarks and heterogeneity of the pre-metastatic niche

## Inflammatory signaling
Substantial evidence demonstrates that crosstalk among diverse cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME) triggers systemic changes via the peripheral circulation, shaping a local inflammatory milieu [ ]. PMN formation also involves immune dysregulation characterized by impaired effector responses. As highlighted by numerous studies, cytokines and chemokines are pivotal in this process [ ].
During early tumor progression, primary tumor cells secrete a spectrum of cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, as well as chemokines including CCL2 and CXCL1. These factors recruit inflammatory cells like neutrophils and macrophages to distant organs. The infiltrating cells then amplify the inflammatory response by secreting additional cytokines and chemokines, promoting angiogenesis, lymphangiogenesis, and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling. This cascade effectively reprograms distant organs, ultimately creating a tumor-permissive microenvironment [ ].

## Direct pro-metastatic signaling axes in PMN formation

## STAT3-LCN2 axis
Accumulation of pro-tumor neutrophils in the PMN activates STAT3, which induces lipocalin-2 (LCN2). LCN2 accelerates tumor cell invasion and migration via epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), significantly elevating metastatic risk [ , ].

## Notch1-VCAM1 signaling
In melanoma and tumor-bearing mouse models, activation of the Notch1 receptor (N1ICD) sustainably induces chemokine expression and vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM1). This facilitates neutrophil infiltration, tumor-endothelial adhesion, and lung colonization, thereby directly supporting metastasis [ ].

## COX-2-PGE2 pathway
Ren’s team used single-cell RNA sequencing and immunofluorescence to demonstrate that COX-2-expressing adventitial fibroblasts remodel the lung immune microenvironment. In response to tumor-associated inflammation and IL-1β, these fibroblasts secrete PGE2, which impairs dendritic cell (DC) function and suppresses monocytes, consequently promoting breast cancer lung metastasis. Deleting Ptgs2 (encoding COX-2) in fibroblasts reverses myeloid cell immunosuppression, enhances immune activation, and improves the efficacy of DC therapy and PD-1 blockade; dual inhibition of PGE2 receptors yields similar effects [ ].

## IL-1β-SAA3 cascade
In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), macrophage-derived IL-1β induces alveolar epithelial SAA3 expression via NF-κB, while also promoting autocrine MMP9 expression in epithelial cells. SAA3 then recruits myeloid cells to the lung (without altering myeloid MMP9 levels) to form PMN, facilitating HCC lung metastasis [ ].

## IL-6-STAT3-CCL5 signaling
In triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), IL-6 mediates STAT3 phosphorylation in lymphatic ECs (LECs). Phosphorylated STAT3 (pSTAT3) induces HIF-1α/VEGF (to promote angiogenesis) and forms a pSTAT3-pc-Jun-pATF-2 complex (to induce CCL5 in LECs), enabling LECs to support the formation of lymphatic metastatic foci [ ].

## Organ-specific inflammation and therapeutic modulation of PMN
Early work in breast cancer highlighted that metastatic success depends not only on tumor-intrinsic traits but also on the permissiveness of the PMN. Interactions between ovarian endocrine hormones and paracrine TGF-β signaling were proposed to condition the bone niche, thereby influencing immune and stromal responses that determine whether disseminated tumor cells survive or perish. Notably, clinical studies of adjuvant bisphosphonates suggested that therapeutic modulation of this endocrine-paracrine microenvironment restricted niche permissiveness, resulting in reduced metastasis and improved survival in hormonally defined patient subsets [ ]. In breast cancer, mice deficient in type I IFN signaling (Ifnar1−/−) exhibit increased lung metastases, driven by elevated serum G-CSF and enhanced CXCR2 expression on neutrophils, which leads to massive pulmonary neutrophil accumulation [ ]. This highlights the organ-specific role of inflammatory signaling in PMN formation.
Several drugs exert anti-metastatic effects by targeting inflammatory signaling pathways. Notable examples include agents that disrupt PMN-related chemokine/receptor axes, such as the anti-CCR4 monoclonal antibody mogamulizumab and the CXCR4 inhibitor plerixafor, both of which have demonstrated efficacy in inhibiting metastasis [ – ]. A related study showed that herbal decoctions can modulate gut microbiota in gastric cancer mouse models. This modulation restrains the overproduction of inflammatory factors such as IL-6, IL-10, and TGF-β in serum and lung tissue, suppresses the pro-inflammatory PMN established by myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and thereby reverses gastric cancer metastasis [ ]. However, some tumor-targeted drugs may inadvertently promote PMN formation. For instance, sunitinib (a receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitor) induces a senescence-like phenotype in endothelial cells (ECs) in breast cancer. This leads to increased chemokine secretion and VCAM1 expression, facilitating tumor cell migration across ECs and myeloid cell recruitment to the PMN, ultimately increasing distant metastasis [ ].
These inflammatory cues act not merely in a local context but as systemic rheostats that tune the immune landscape across multiple organs, shaping organ-specific pre-metastatic responses. Further research is needed to precisely modulate these pathways to block PMN development.

## Immune remodeling
Immune cell infiltration and functional remodeling are defining features of both the TME and the PMN, and their heterogeneity across metastatic organs directly determines PMN fate and tumor dissemination [ ]. The immune component of the PMN is not homogeneous but dynamically adapts to the physiological microenvironment of each organ [ ]. The balance between immune activation and suppression defines whether an organ becomes a fertile “soil” for metastatic “seeds”. Single-cell sequencing has revealed drastic differences in immune cell abundance, phenotype, and functional state between organs, variations that are key to organotropism [ , ] (Fig. ).
 

## Innate immune dysfunction

## Myeloid cells

Neutrophils

N2-type neutrophils (a pro-tumor subtype) accumulate in the lung PMN of breast cancer, where they promote neutrophil migration via Ki-67-high lymphatic ECs, thereby reinforcing PMN formation. In the lung PMN, CD11b+Ly6G+Ly6C+ neutrophils secrete Bv8, MMP-9, and S100A8/A9, which enhance cancer cell extravasation and proliferation [ ]. Beyond these protumorigenic functions, neutrophils also actively remodel the immune landscape of PMN through the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). In ovarian cancer, NETs induce CXCL13 expression in the pre-metastatic omentum, promoting recruitment of innate-like B cells that produce IL-10, expand Tregs, and establish an immunosuppressive microenvironment permissive for metastatic colonization. These findings underscore NETs as key mediators linking neutrophil activation to immune suppression during PMN formation [ ].

Myeloid-Derived Suppressor Cells (MDSCs)

MDSCs accumulate in the PMN and inhibit anti-tumor T cells via arginase-1 (ARG1) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. In prostate cancer liver metastases, tumor-associated macrophage (TAMs) recruit splenic MDSCs via CXCL1-CXCR2 signaling, further enhancing metastatic potential [ ]. Researchers at the West China School of Pharmacy, Sichuan University, have developed a sponge-like, neutrophil membrane-coated nanosystem (NM/PPcDG/D). This system can inhibit the recruitment and function of MDSCs, enhance anti-tumor immunity, and suppress lung metastasis of breast cancer by preventing PMN formation. Specifically, they first synthesized PPcDG/D nanoparticles (NPs) through cross-linked self-assembly of a redox-responsive polymer (PPDG) with a doxorubicin (DOX)-loaded nanocore. Subsequently, these NPs were coated with activated neutrophil membranes to construct the biomimetic, sponge-like NM/PPcDG/D. This nanosystem not only exhibits strong natural tropism for postoperative inflammatory sites, thereby alleviating MDSC-mediated immunosuppression, but also inhibits PMN formation by reducing MDSC recruitment, lowering lung vascular permeability, and suppressing the implantation of CTCs, ultimately preventing pulmonary metastasis [ ].

Tumor-Associated Macrophages (TAMs)

In the PMN, TAMs polarize to M2-like phenotypes characterized by poor antigen-presenting capacity and high secretion of cytokines such as IL-10, IL-4, and IL-13. In prostate cancer liver metastases, CD163+ M2-like macrophages are enriched, creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment [ ]. Lung PMN TAMs undergo glycolytic metabolic reprogramming, gaining an immunosuppressive phenotype to promote tumorigenesis [ ].

Natural Killer Cells (NK)

In breast cancer and melanoma models, primary tumor hypoxia recruits CD11b+Ly6CmedLy6G+ immune suppressor cells to lung PMN, which compromise NK cell cytotoxicity by suppressing IFN-γ production, thereby enabling CTC survival [ ]. The recruitment of natural killer T cells (NKT), an essential regulatory step in PMN formation, is tightly modulated by dysregulated bile acid profiles. Specifically, the primary bile acid α-Muricholic acid enhances this recruitment, while secondary bile acids like glycoursodeoxycholic acid and taurohyodeoxycholic acid suppress it. Notably, long-term capsaicin intake increases cancer metastasis risk by reshaping gut microbiota, disrupting the gut barrier to facilitate bacterial translocation to the liver, and altering bile acid metabolism, ultimately impairing NKT cell recruitment and fostering a pro-metastatic hepatic PMN [ ].

## Adaptive immune regulation

## Regulatory T Cells (Tregs)
In mice pre-injected with Lewis lung carcinoma cell exosomes (LLC-exo), the count of CD4+FoxP3+ Tregs in the lungs was markedly elevated. Following co-culture with LLC-exo, lung fibroblasts showed enhanced secretion of CCL1. This chemokine activates its specific receptor CCR8 to induce Treg differentiation, ultimately facilitating the establishment of an immunologically tolerant PMN. Additionally, either inhibiting LLC-exo release via GW4869 (an inhibitor of neutral sphingomyelinase N-SMase) or blocking the CCL1-CCR8 axis with AZ084 (a potent, selective, allosteric and oral active CCR8 allosteric antagonist) effectively suppressed Treg differentiation and lung metastasis of tumors [ ]. GM-CSF secreted by breast cancer cells sustains AHR activity in lung macrophages via activating the STAT5 signaling pathway. AHR then upregulates PD-L1 expression and induces Treg differentiation. This GM-CSF-STAT5-AHR-PD-L1 cascade-mediated cellular crosstalk is a key mechanism underlying the formation of the immunosuppressive lung PMN in breast cancer, with high AHR expression closely associated with increased Treg infiltration and poor prognosis in patients [ ]. In distant organs (draining lymph nodes, liver, lung, bone marrow) of multiple human cancers, Treg expansion is associated with poor prognosis [ – ].

## Regulatory B Cells (Bregs)
Bregs mediate TGF-β-dependent crosstalk with MDSCs and stromal cells, promoting the conversion of CD4+ T cell into Tregs, thereby further reinforcing immunosuppression. Additionally, B cells exhibit heterogeneous infiltration across cancers, with their recruitment regulated by the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis [ ].
A seminal study published in Cell leveraged high-resolution single-cell analysis to systematically map the immune microenvironment of brain metastases across multiple cancer types, including melanoma, breast, lung, and ovarian cancers. A pivotal discovery was the marked heterogeneity in the immune composition of the PMN, which varied significantly with the cancer type of origin. For example, breast cancer brain metastases were dominated by macrophages, lung cancer metastases showed a pronounced enrichment of T cells, whereas melanoma and ovarian cancer metastases displayed relatively higher levels of B cells. These findings provide direct evidence that the immunological “soil” of the PMN is fundamentally shaped by the identity of the metastatic “seed” (Table ).
 Collectively, immune remodeling in the PMN is tailored to the intrinsic tissue microenvironment of the target organ. The identification of organ-specific immunosuppressive circuits provides a rationale for precision immunomodulation to block metastatic seeding. Targeting organ-specific immune cell subtypes such as N2 neutrophils in lung PMN and M2 macrophages in liver PMN may enable selective disruption of PMN formation [ ]. Importantly, these immune alterations function primarily at the permissive and instructive layers of PMN formation, lowering immune surveillance thresholds rather than deterministically dictating metastatic outgrowth.

## Angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis
In addition to regulating and recruiting immune cells, inflammatory factors can also affect angiogenesis or lymphangiogenesis in the tumor environment, thereby contributing to the defining features of the PMN [ ]. This specialized microenvironment, formed through interactions among the vasculature, stromal cells, and immune cells, has been progressively conceptualized as the perivascular niche. Several studies have revealed that the pre-metastatic perivascular niche exhibits dynamic plasticity and harbors potential targetable vulnerabilities [ ].
Angiogenesis requires ECs to proliferate and activate on the basis of existing capillaries and is regulated by angiogenesis-related factors [ , ]. In recent years, research has identified a class of contractile cells called “pericytes” around the ECs of capillaries and veins throughout the body, which constitute a key component of the TME. Pericytes have multiple functions, including maintaining the integrity of the blood-brain barrier and controlling capillary blood flow, and are central players of vascular structural stability [ ]. For instance, they regulate capillary contraction and maintain normal microcirculation in local tissues and organs [ ]. Pericytes also actively participate in the transport of interacting immune cells to blood vessels, thereby affecting the immune response [ ]. They engage in multiple interactions with various TME components, including promoting cancer cell growth [ ] and drug resistance [ ] via paracrine activity, and inducing M2 macrophage polarization [ ]. Conversely, changes in the TME can affect the number, phenotype, and molecular markers of pericytes. Studies have reported that KLF4, secreted by primary tumors, is a key inducer of phenotypic transitions in perivascular cells including pericytes. Increased KLF4 expression in these phenotypically altered perivascular cells leads to their dedifferentiation, enhances their proliferation, migration, and extracellular matrix synthesis, and initiates PMN formation by promoting fibronectin production [ ]. Additionally, KLF4 can synergize with KLF2 to regulate the expression of VEGFR2, ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin5 in tumor ECs, thereby promoting vascular permeability and angiogenesis [ ]. Pathways such as PI3K/AKT, HIF-1α, and VEGF also regulate angiogenesis and neurogenesis in various tumor environments [ – ]. However, studies have also identified VEGF-independent pathways. For instance, BICC1 has been shown to promote angiogenesis and tumor growth in pancreatic cancer independently of VEGF [ ]. In sum, pericyte detachment from ECs within the TME facilitates the invasion of primary tumor cells into the circulation, as well as the enzymatic degradation of local capillary basement membranes and ECs proliferation with sprouting, thereby promoting tumor angiogenesis and metastasis [ ].
We have also noted studies conducting quantitative analysis of lymphatic vessels in tumor metastases. In melanoma, the expansion and increased density of lymphatic vessels in lung tissues promote melanoma metastasis to the lungs. Not only did one study perform quantitative analysis of lymphatic vessels in lung metastases from 266 melanoma patients, but it also revealed via mouse models that VEGF-C is a key regulatory gene inducing increased lymphatic vessel density in lung metastases, suggesting that targeted inhibition of key genes driving lymphangiogenesis in the PMN may hold potential clinical significance for adjuvant therapy in patients with metastatic cancer [ ].
Notably, beyond angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis, tumor metastasis may acquire blood supply by co-opting normal tissue blood vessels [ , ]. A single-cell sequencing study has shown that tumor ECs involved in blood vessel co-option are highly similar to healthy ECs at the transcriptomic level. Furthermore, researchers have identified two pericyte subtypes: one associated with angiogenesis, and the other exhibiting a quiescent state in tumors that co-opt blood vessels, resembling healthy lung pericytes. Specific cancer cell subtypes and macrophage subtypes may also promote blood vessel co-option through matrix remodeling and immune regulation [ ].
Recent studies have revealed that tumor cells themselves exhibit multiple neurodevelopmental characteristics, such as forming ultralong neurite-like membrane protrusions, which are referred to as tumor microtubes, and the establishment of such a tumor neural network may occur in parallel with tumor angiogenesis [ , ]. These findings indicate that the PMN may involve synergistic interactions among various cell types to jointly establish a de novo vascular system, which serves as a critical safeguard for the subsequent progression of tumors in metastatic foci following colonization [ ].
Together, vascular remodeling, lymphangiogenesis, and neurovascular mimicry integrate as structural determinants of PMN heterogeneity and metastatic potential.

## Metabolic reprogramming
Metabolic alterations within the PMN extend beyond energetic adaptation and function as instructive signals that actively shape immune and stromal behavior. Tumor-derived metabolites, including lactate, kynurenine, and lipid species, exert potent immunomodulatory effects by suppressing effector T-cell activity while promoting MDSC expansion and fibroblast activation. Concurrently, metabolic stress reprograms ECs, enhancing vascular permeability and facilitating immune cell trafficking. Collectively, these metabolic-driven cues operate predominantly at the instructive layer of PMN formation, mechanistically linking systemic tumor metabolism to localized immune suppression, stromal remodeling, and niche conditioning [ ].
Consistent with this conceptual framework, metabolic reprogramming within the PMN is not confined to tumor cells. Stromal and immune compartments undergo reversible metabolic adaptations that establish a nutrient-enriched and immunosuppressive microenvironment, thereby enhancing tolerance to oxidative stress for facilitating organ-specific metastatic tropism [ – ]. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), hepatic PMN formation exemplifies this process, whereas coordinated metabolic rewiring of myeloid cells and altered nutrient utilization generate a permissive liver niche for metastatic colonization [ , ]. Importantly, the intrinsic metabolic landscape of target organs further biases metastatic seeding, underscoring the contribution of organ-specific nutrient availability to PMN heterogeneity.

## Lung PMN
In the lung PMN, dietary and tissue-derived metabolites play a prominent role in shaping PMN metabolism. High-fat diet-induced mice exhibit palmitate accumulation activates, which activates NF-κB signaling via p65 acetylation, thereby promoting breast cancer cell growth in the lung [ ]. Additionally, elevated aspartate levels in pulmonary interstitial fluid enhances CTC survival and colonization, reinforcing lung-specific PMN permissiveness [ ].

## Liver PMN
Interestingly, the gut microbiota regulates bile acid (BA) metabolism to reshape the hepatic PMN and promote colorectal cancer liver metastasis (CRLM). Reduced gut bacterial bile salt hydrolase (BSH) activity in CRLM leads to elevated circulating conjugated primary BAs (CPBAs), a metabolic phenotype more pronounced in males, particularly taurocholic acid (TCA), which induces TIMP1 expression and activates the CXCL5/CXCR2 axis to recruit neutrophils and promote liver colonization. Conversely, unconjugated primary/secondary BAs antagonize these effects, highlighting a metabolite-dependent regulatory circuit [ ]. In parallel, upregulation of fatty acid-binding protein 7 (FABP7) in liver macrophages promotes lipid droplet accumulation and lipid transfer to CD8⁺ T cells and tumor cells via exosomes, resulting in T-cell dysfunction and enhanced metastatic outgrowth; Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of FABP7 was shown to reduce liver metastasis in preclinical models [ ].

## Breast PMN
In breast tissue, the expression of ANGPTL4 and KLF4 is significantly upregulated in metastatic specimens. ANGPTL4 plays a pivotal role in adipocyte-driven glycolysis and tumor metastasis. Specifically, interleukin-6 (IL-6) enriched in cancer-associated adipocytes and free fatty acid (FFAs) released by adipocytes activate the STAT3 and PPARα signaling pathways in TNBC cells, thereby amplifying ANGPTL4-mediated glycolytic metabolism and metastatic potential. Furthermore, ANGPTL4 interacts with the transcription factor KLF4 to enhance its transcriptional activity, which in turn potentiates glycolysis and tumor metastasis. Conversely, KLF4 knockdown attenuates the migratory capacity and glycolytic flux of TNBC cells [ ].

## Peritoneal PMN
In gastric cancer, tumor-derived LAMC1 promotes peritoneal PMN formation by inducing preadipocyte maturation and FFA release. Palmitic acid (a major FFA) mediated STAT3 activation suppresses miR-193a-3p (a negative regulator of LAMC1), establishing a positive feedback loop that sustains metabolic reprogramming and peritoneal colonization. Elevated serum LAMC1 correlates with increased risk of peritoneal metastasis, underscoring its potential as a biomarker [ ].
Within the context of organ-specific metabolic reprogramming that regulates PMN formation, mitochondria act as central metabolic switches. For example, mitochondrial RNA methylation enhances mRNA translation to promote tumor metastasis, and its targeted inhibition can suppress translation and prevent metastatic progression [ ]. Another key mechanism involves the MTA1-ATP5A axis, wherein metastasis-associated antigen 1 (MTA1) interacts with ATP synthase subunit α (ATP5A) to modulate oxidative phosphorylation and promote liver metastasis in colorectal cancer (CRC). This interaction can be blocked by sirolimus, resulting in suppressed metastasis in both xenograft models and clinical settings [ ]. Furthermore, emerging studies indicate that distinct mitochondrial subpopulations differentially regulate tumor bioenergetics through lipid and glucose metabolism, a regulatory pattern that may also operate within the PMN [ , ].
At the same time, metabolic reprogramming can induce “trained immunity” in myeloid cells. For example, whole β-glucan particles (WGP) can train macrophages via the metabolite sphingosine-1-phosphate, enhancing their responsiveness to tumor-derived factors. Adoptive transfer of WGP-trained bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) can inhibit metastasis in multiple mouse models, while blocking sphingosine-1-phosphate synthesis or mitochondrial fission abrogates this effect. Moreover, WGP can induce trained immunity in human monocytes, exerting anti-tumor activity [ ]. Metabolic regulation of immunosuppression in metastatic PDAC also highlights mechanisms such as IDO1-mediated kynurenine production and amino acid dysregulation that promote suppressive immune phenotypes and facilitate metastatic outgrowth [ ]. These findings highlight metabolic regulation as a reversible immunological axis that can reshape PMN evolution [ ].
Therefore, the establishment of the PMN can be regarded as a system-wide metabolic network. Within this network, crosstalk among tumor, immune, and stromal compartments not only dictates the energy flow and redox balance essential for metastatic colonization but also is critical for forging a metabolically permissive microenvironment.

## Neural regulation of the PMN
Emerging evidence indicates that neural inputs add a regulatory layer to PMN formation by coupling systemic physiological states with local niche remodeling. Primary tumors can promote neural remodeling by secreting neurotrophic factors such as NGF (Nerve growth factor) and BDNF (Brain-derived neurotrophic factor), leading to tumor-associated axonogenesis and enhanced neural connectivity in distant organs under specific experimental and clinical conditions.
Stress-induced sympathetic signaling, primarily mediated by norepinephrine-β-adrenergic pathways, was shown to suppress NK cell cytotoxicity, enhance MDSC function, and increase vascular permeability, collectively biasing distant tissues toward a permissive pre-metastatic state. Conversely, parasympathetic acetylcholine signaling has been implicated in neutrophil extracellular trap formation, further facilitating metastatic seeding in select organs [ – ]. Psychological stress was shown to activate the sympathetic nervous system, thereby releasing norepinephrine. In tumor cells, norepinephrine signaling through β2-adrenergic receptors (ADRB2) was associated with reduced expression of the RNA m⁶A demethylase ALKBH5, thereby altering epitranscriptomic regulation; Emerging evidence suggests that such neuroendocrine-driven epigenetic changes in tumor cells may, in turn, influence TME interactions, potentially contributing to neural remodeling and niche permissiveness in a context-dependent manner [ ].
Importantly, neural regulation appears to function predominantly as a contextual modulator rather than an autonomous driver of PMN formation. By shaping immune tone, vascular stability, and stromal dynamics, neural cues amplifed or constrained ongoing inflammatory and metabolic programs already engaged within the niche [ ]. This neuro-immune-vascular crosstalk provides a mechanistic link between systemic stress states and organ-specific PMN heterogeneity, highlighting neural signaling as a potential, yet indirect, therapeutic target; together, these observations raise the possibility that neural signaling interfaces with epigenetic regulatory mechanisms during PMN evolution, although the extent and directionality of this coupling likely vary across tumor types and organ contexts [ , ].

## Epigenetic regulation and PMN heterogeneity
Epigenetic reprogramming has emerged as a central mechanism underpinning the persistence and heterogeneity of the PMN. Transient systemic cues can be epigenetically encoded into long-lasting molecular programs that sustain metastatic competence. For example, short-term exposure to dietary palmitic acid induced a durable pro-metastatic memory in tumor cells through stable deposition of H3K4 trimethylation mediated by the Set1A/COMPASS complex; Spatial transcriptomic analyses further revealed that this epigenetically encoded memory is enriched in neural and glial-associated programs, linking chromatin remodeling to tumor-associated innervation and sustained metastatic potential [ ].
In parallel, tumor-derived EVs act as potent conveyors of epigenetic information, reprogramming PMN-associated stromal, endothelial, and immune cells. EV-mediated transfer of regulatory RNAs and metabolic enzymes can remodel chromatin accessibility and histone modifications, thereby embedding a molecular memory of the primary tumor within distant tissues. Representative examples include EV-induced histone acetylation programs, which sustain chemokine expression in fibroblasts, and EV-associated non-coding RNAs, that induce persistent endothelial permeability or stromal activation; these mechanisms collectively reinforce immunosuppressive and pro-metastatic niche states [ , ]. In PDAC, an exosome-enriched tRNA-derived fragment, tRF-GluCTC-0005, modulated WDR1-YAP signaling in hepatic stellate cells, promoting stable recruitment of immunosuppressive myeloid cells and liver-specific metastatic permissiveness [ ]. In ovarian cancer, exosomal miR-141 reprogramed fibroblasts by modulation of YAP1 signaling, leading to persistent GROα production and formation of a tumor-promoting stromal niche that facilitates metastatic colonization [ ].
Importantly, spatially resolved epigenomic analyses indicate that these epigenetic programs are not uniformly distributed, but are instead anchored to distinct microanatomical niches, including perivascular, perineural, and stromal compartments [ , ]. Such spatially constrained epigenetic states provide a mechanistic basis for organ-specific PMN features and help explain why niche permissiveness may persist even after removal of the primary tumor. While the reversibility of epigenetic modifications offers therapeutic opportunities, selectively resetting pathological niche programs without disrupting physiological tissue homeostasis remains a significant challenge.

## Temporal regulation of metastasis
Time adds a fourth dimension to PMN heterogeneity: both the initiation of metastasis and the rhythmic release of CTCs exhibit temporal specificity that profoundly influences metastatic efficiency. This temporal specificity further displays striking heterogeneity across cancer types and is tightly linked to clinical outcomes. Clinical and preclinical evidence collectively highlight two core aspects of this regulation, offering critical insights for early intervention [ ] (Fig. ).
 

## Early initiation of metastatic dissemination
A wealth of clinical data confirms that the onset of metastasis varies significantly among different cancer types. For instance, breast cancer often presents with axillary lymph node metastasis at the time of diagnosis. In contrast, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is characterized by a shorter median time to metastasis and correspondingly reduced overall survival. These differences reflect the inherent biological heterogeneity of tumors and their microenvironments [ – ]. A comprehensive multi-omics analysis performed on 548 samples from 326 patients (including primary tumors, matched normal tissues, peritoneal metastases, and adjacent normal peritoneal tissues) found evidence of PMN modulation even in early-stage gastric cancer patients without clinical peritoneal metastasis. Niche remodeling of the peritoneum during transcoelomic metastasis was further validated in a humanized mouse model [ ].
The long-held notion that metastasis requires a large primary tumor has been challenged by accumulating clinical evidence. Even small tumors can initiate metastasis; for instance, breast cancers < 1 cm in diameter can metastasize, and invasive ductal carcinomas < 2 cm exhibit metastasis rates of 11.3%-33.0%. Genomic analyses have further uncovered the early divergence of metastatic lineages. Researchers found that, in nearly 90% of the 40 cancer patients studied, the cells responsible for the first metastasis already acquired distinct genetic alterations by the time of the initial diagnosis; strikingly, in some cases, this occurred years before the primary tumor was detected [ , ]. This indicates that metastatic dissemination can begin at an unexpectedly early stage, long before the primary lesion is clinically apparent.
Furthermore, mathematical modeling reveals a non-linear relationship between primary tumor size and metastatic risk. This relationship is modulated by tumor-intrinsic factors (including CTC shedding rate and metastatic potential), microenvironmental cues (such as angiogenesis and immune suppression), and host-related variables (including genetic background and systemic inflammation) [ , ]. Together, these findings underscore the complexity of metastatic timing and challenge the simplification of metastasis as a late-stage event [ ].
These data collectively indicate that tumor metastasis follows predictable temporal patterns. Consequently, developing diagnostic tools to assess metastatic risk during this early window, coupled with targeted interventions, holds significant potential for preventing metastasis before it becomes clinically overt.

## Circadian control of CTC dynamics
Beyond the timing of initiation, tumor progression including cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis, is tightly controlled by circadian rhythms, the body’s intrinsic 24-hour biological clock [ ]. Disruption of circadian rhythms (e.g., due to shift work or sleep deprivation) strengthens crosstalk between cancer stem cells (CSCs) and the TME, promoting distant metastasis. This link has spurred interest in “chronotherapy”, an approach that aligns treatment timing with circadian rhythms to optimize efficacy and minimize toxicity.
Preclinical and clinical studies have demonstrated the potential of chronotherapy: administering chemotherapy or immunotherapy during optimal circadian windows reduces treatment-related adverse effects while enhancing anti-tumor efficacy [ , ]. A striking finding in breast cancer patients and mouse models revealed the rhythmic nature of CTC release, showing that most spontaneous CTC intravasation events occur during the rest (sleep) phase. Crucially, CTCs generated during the rest phase exhibit high metastatic potential, whereas those released during the active phase lack metastasis-initiating capacity. Single-cell RNA sequencing of these CTCs confirmed significant upregulation of mitotic genes during the rest phase, which endows them with enhanced metastatic competence [ ].
Key circadian hormones, including melatonin, testosterone, and glucocorticoids, regulate the dynamic release of CTCs, while insulin directly promotes tumor cell proliferation in a time-dependent manner [ ]. Thus, the spontaneous generation of metastasis-prone CTCs is not continuous but peaks during the rest phase. This discovery provides a rationale for time-controlled strategies, for example, scheduling CTC detection during the rest phase to improve early diagnosis of metastasis.
These findings establish time as an integral regulator of PMN dynamics, opening avenues for chronotherapy and for time-based early detection of metastasis. In future clinical practice, documenting temporal information should be prioritized, such as the timing of metastatic lesion detection relative to primary tumor diagnosis. Integrating temporal data into patient stratification could transform metastasis management from reactive treatment to proactive prevention. Additionally, retrospective analysis of clinical data using machine learning and artificial intelligence (AI) could elucidate temporal patterns in metastasis across cancer types, enabling more personalized monitoring and intervention. In summary, spatiotemporal profiling of the PMN offers a new paradigm for intercepting metastasis before colonization becomes irreversible.

## New perspectives from spatial omics
Spatial omics technologies have expanded our understanding of the TME by revealing its metabolic, immune, and stromal reprogramming in three dimensions. Extending this paradigm to the PMN provides a spatially resolved view of how systemic signals remodel distant organs prior to metastatic colonization [ , ] (Fig. ).
 

## Spatial omics deciphers tissue/cell-specific PMN biology
To fully understand how PMNs form and function, their features must be examined through the lens of different tissues and cell types, as PMN biology is inherently shaped by the unique spatial context of target organs and by interactions among diverse cell populations. Spatial omics technologies enable this, shedding light on organ structure, immune-stromal crosstalk, tumor subclonal evolution, and metabolic heterogeneity within the PMN.

## Organ-specific spatial architecture as the physical matrix of PMN
PMN formation relies on the pre-existing spatial architecture of target organs, which determines where and how systemic tumor signals are integrated. spatially resolved multi-omics has identified microanatomical hotspots in metastasis-prone organs that serve as receptive “soil” for disseminated tumor cells [ ].
In the liver, small cell clusters adjacent to hepatic veins are key pre-metastatic hubs. These regions are enriched with activated stromal and immune suppressor cells, which collectively remodel the microenvironment to render it receptive to tumor cell colonization [ , , ]. These perivascular hubs highlight how hepatic microarchitecture pre-patterns susceptibility to PMN formation. In the intestine, crypts are critical sites for PMN priming. Crypt-resident cells respond to primary tumor-derived signals, triggering ECM remodeling and myeloid cell recruitment, thereby laying the groundwork for future metastasis [ , ]. Such epithelial-stromal interactions suggest that the intestinal crypt microenvironment acts as a sentinel site for early PMN priming. In the brain, cerebral vascular branch points are unique loci for PMN formation. When CTCs reach these sites, they activate the plasma coagulation cascade, leading to intravascular clot formation. This clotting process is essential for the permanent arrest and colonization of tumor cells in cerebral microvessels, which explains why thrombin and von Willebrand factor (vWF) inhibitors are promising candidates for preventing brain metastasis [ , ]. These results define the cerebral vascular junction as a spatial bottleneck for metastatic seeding.
Advanced spatial imaging systems, such as DeepMACT, further our understanding through systematic mapping of metastatic features including size, shape, and spatial distribution, as well as the targeting efficacy of therapeutic antibodies across entire organisms [ ]. Collectively, organ-specific spatial architectures represent not passive backdrops but active structural determinants of PMN formation.

## Immune-stromal crosstalk as the functional regulator of the PMN
Beyond structural predisposition, PMN establishment critically depends on spatially organized immune-stromal interactions that modulate inflammation, immune suppression, and matrix remodeling. Spatial omics has uncovered how these proximity- defined interactions fuel immunosuppression and tissue remodeling within the PMN.
Stromal fibroblasts, particularly cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), are activated cells within the tumor stroma. They secrete growth factors, chemokines, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), and ECM components, which are broadly implicated in regulating tumor growth, angiogenesis, and metastasis. Supports their role to form a PMN in the lungs [ ]. In HCC, a striking finding by multi-omics analysis revealed that metastatic subclones without Wnt mutations have a strong selective advantage in colonizing PMN. This advantage stems from their ability to recruit activated fibroblasts to the niche; these fibroblasts, in turn, secrete pro-metastatic factors that further enhance the microenvironment’s permissiveness to tumor cells [ ]. The enzyme-activated assembled peptide FR17 can inhibit stromal fibroblast activation, suppress pro-metastatic host stromal remodeling, reverse vascular instability and angiogenesis, and thereby inhibit PMN formation [ ].
M2-like macrophages are characterized by immunosuppressive and tissue-repairing functions. Within the TME, they promote tumor growth, angiogenesis, and metastasis by secreting cytokines and inhibiting anti-tumor immune cells [ ]. In CRC liver metastases, spatial transcriptomics has identified MRC1+CCL18+ M2-like macrophages as central mediators of immune suppression. These macrophages undergo significant spatial reprogramming in the metastatic microenvironment, developing enhanced metabolic activity that reinforces immunosuppression. Notably, neoadjuvant chemotherapy can reverse this suppressive state in responders, restoring an anti-tumor immune balance, while non-responders experience a worsening of immune suppression [ ]. Colon cancer can polarize M1 macrophages to an M2 phenotype via HSP90B1, thereby initiating PMN formation in the liver [ ]. In breast cancer and melanoma, M2 macrophages, through their highly expressed CYP4A enzyme, activate the STAT3 pathway, thereby leading to secretion of pro-metastatic factors, including TGF-β, VEGF, and SDF-1. These factors act in concert to promote PMN formation in distant organs (notably the lungs) by recruiting VEGFR1+ bone marrow cells and upregulating fibronectin expression, thereby contributing to a permissive environment for metastasis [ ].
Vascular ECs play a pivotal role in regulating metastasis. They mediate organ-specific crosstalk with parenchymal cells through metabolic, immunological, and physicochemical mechanisms, and exists inter-organ endothelial heterogeneity [ ]. In breast cancer, they secrete factors that inhibit PHGDH expression in adjacent tumor cells, driving them to cluster near vessels. This spatial localization enhances metastatic potential, a finding consistent with the correlation between vascular accessibility in the PMN and tumor cell invasiveness [ ]. A recent single-cell atlas study revealed that vascular ECs in brain metastases (BrM) create a physical gateway for tumor cell extravasation and colonization by markedly enhancing vascular permeability. The study delineated key differences: IDH-mutant low-grade gliomas resemble non-tumor brain vasculature; both IDH-wildtype glioblastoma (IDH-WT-GBM) and BrM exhibit significant vascular abnormalities, which are more pronounced in BrM. BrM vasculature also shows more extensive immune cell interactions, whereas IDH-WT-GBM is less infiltrated, and both exhibit blood-brain barrier dysfunction, with BrM displaying more prominent vascular leakage and type IV collagen deposition. At the molecular pathway level, both IDH-WT-GBM and BrM commonly upregulate pathways related to ECM organization and angiogenesis, whereas they downregulate others, such as TNF-α signaling. BrM specifically enriches immune-related and vascular leakage pathways, whereas IDH-WT-GBM upregulates pathways involved in cell adhesion/junctions and downregulates inflammatory cytokine signaling. Furthermore, ECs subtype distribution differs such that cells in IDH-mutant gliomas and non-tumor tissue align along the arteriovenous axis, IDH-WT-GBM is predominantly capillary, and BrM exhibits a higher proportion of angiogenic subtypes plus a unique “arterial type 2” subtype [ ].
In summary, these examples highlight that immune-stromal crosstalk is not random but spatially orchestrated, with cell proximity dictating functional outcomes within the PMN.

## Tumor subclonal evolution as the evolutionary engine of PMN colonization
Metastatic competence is not uniformly distributed across tumor cells but is confined to genetically and phenotypically distinct subclones. Recent spatial multi-omics studies have revealed pronounced intratumoral heterogeneity among these subclones, delineating their evolutionary lineages and spatial distribution. These analyses provide a mechanistic explanation for why only specific subpopulations are capable of colonizing the PMN and ultimately seeding overt metastases.
Phylogenetic analyses indicate that most metastases share a recent common ancestor with the primary tumor, supporting a predominantly monoclonal origin. However, in advanced disease stages, the presence of mixed subclones across multiple metastases sites reflects continued evolutionary diversification after dissemination [ ]. Importantly, not all subclones within the primary tumor possess metastatic competence. In breast cancer, specific subclonal mutations remain confined to in situ carcinoma and lack invasive or metastatic potential, effectively excluding these lineages from participation in PMN colonization [ , ]. These findings suggest that dissemination competence is an intrinsic property of selected subclones rather than a universal feature of tumor cells.
Beyond dissemination, accumulating evidence indicates that genetically defined subclones can actively condition distant organs to establish a permissive PMN. In gastric cancer, whole-exome sequencing identified a distinct metastatic subclone harboring the ARHGEF12 E620K mutation that preferentially gives rise to ovarian metastasis. This mutation enhances invasive capacity, selectively promoting the release of ITGA6-high exosomes, which are efficiently internalized by ovarian fibroblasts to reprogram them into CAFs. This stromal reprogramming initiated early PMN formation and creates a supportive microenvironment for subsequent metastatic seeding [ ]. This example illustrates how specific genetic alterations endow tumor subclones with niche-conditioning capacity that drives organ-specific metastatic tropism.
Similar principles have been observed in other malignancies. In PDAC, genomic profiling reveals multiple heterogeneous subclones within primary tumors, with metastatic competence frequently associated with mesenchymal transcriptional states. However, integrative spatial and multi-omics analyses indicate that dissemination alone is insufficient; successful metastatic outgrowth additionally requires the ability of these subclones to remodel distant organs into permissive PMN environments [ ]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, a large-scale multi-omics study of 182 patients encompassing 257 primary and 176 metastatic regions demonstrated that Wnt-deficient subclones are uniquely capable of colonizing the PMN. Their selective advantage depends on the recruitment and activation of fibroblasts to establish a supportive stromal niche, underscoring the role of niche-imposed selection in shaping metastatic success [ ].
Spatial context further constrains subclonal fitness during PMN colonization. Spatial transcriptomic analyses reveal that metastatic subclones preferentially localize to perivascular regions, where access to nutrients and relative immune privilege enhance survival during early colonization [ ]. These observations indicate that PMN colonization reflects a two-step evolutionary process: pre-existing subclonal traits confer dissemination and niche-conditioning capacity, while spatially structured microenvironmental pressures within the PMN selectively favor subclones with optimal adaptive fitness.
Crucially, this adaptive process entails fitness trade-offs. Subclones optimized for immune evasion, stromal interaction, and stress tolerance often exhibit reduced proliferative capacity, favoring persistence or dormancy within the PMN. Conversely, highly proliferative subclones may lack sufficient adaptability to withstand immune surveillance or metabolic constraints at distant sites, limiting their contribution to successful colonization. These trade-offs help explain why only a minority of disseminated tumor cells progress to overt metastases despite widespread dissemination [ – ].
Collectively, these studies demonstrate that spatial subclonal evolution functions as the evolutionary engine of PMN colonization, integrating intrinsic genetic programs with niche-driven selection to determine metastatic efficiency. Dissecting these evolutionary trajectories may enable therapeutic strategies to intercept the most aggressive subclones before PMN establishment.

## Metabolic reprogramming as an adaptive energy strategy in PMN formation
Metabolic reprogramming is a key feature of the PMN. However, spatial omics has revealed that these changes are not uniform but are spatially restricted and coordinated between tumor and niche cells.
In breast cancer models, a fascinating pattern emerges that tumor cells with low PHGDH (a key serine metabolism enzyme) cluster near vessels and are more metastatic. Yet, upon reaching metastatic sites, their growth requires re-expression of PHGDH. This metabolic flexibility is enabled by vascular ECs that provide amino acids, reducing the tumor cells’ reliance on PHGDH for serine synthesis during metastasis [ ]. Spatial metabolomic technologies, such as mass spectrometry imaging, are uncovering unique metabolic profiles in PMN across organs. For instance, liver PMN exhibit enhanced fatty acid oxidation, whereas lung PMN rely more on glycolysis. These organ-specific adaptations reveal both an adaptive advantage for disseminated cells and a potential therapeutic vulnerability that can be exploited to disrupt PMN formation [ ].
These findings underscore that metabolism within the PMN is spatially regulated, with the niche’s architecture dictating tumor cell metabolic adaptations for survival and proliferation.

## AI as the emerging key to decoding PMN spatial complexity
While spatially resolved multi-omics has revolutionized our understanding of the PMN, the precise interpretation of these complex data poses a new challenge. Integrating these technologies with AI promises to unlock even greater discovery potential. Such integration will enable the recognition of hidden spatial patterns, transforming PMN research from descriptive mapping into a predictive, quantitative discipline and ultimately yielding more profound insights into metastatic risk from complex spatial data.
Researchers from the Institute of Zoology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, recently developed PLATO, a novel spatial proteomics technology. By combining microfluidics, AI, and micro-mass spectrometry, PLATO maps thousands of proteins across entire tissue sections at 25-µm resolution [ ]. Applied to PMN research, PLATO could identify tissue-specific protein variations and rare, niche-driving cell populations, providing a powerful tool for target discovery. Another method integrates single-cell spatial transcriptomic data with high-resolution histology to predict spatial gene expression at ultra-high resolution [ ]. This approach can identify cell-type-specific regulators in PMN without requiring costly single-cell sequencing, thereby making spatial analysis more accessible.
In the future, AI-enhanced spatially resolved multi-omics could enable pathologists to predict molecular expression patterns directly from routine histology. This would allow low-cost, tissue-specific diagnosis of early PMN formation, transforming metastasis prevention from empirical observation to precision medicine. However, most AI-based models in PMN research remain exploratory and require prospective validation, standardized input data, and regulatory consideration before clinical implementation. Despite their transformative potential, spatial omics approaches face important limitations in PMN research. Current platforms are constrained by resolution, limited temporal sampling, and challenges in integrating multi-modal datasets. Moreover, most spatial studies remain correlative, and causal inference requires complementary perturbation-based validation. These limitations underscore the need to interpret spatial patterns as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of functional hierarchy.

## Dual cellular drivers of PMN colonization and malignant progression
The formation of overt metastases relies on two core cellular processes: the distant colonization of CTCs and the malignant transformation of resident stromal stem-like cells within the PMN. CTCs are shed from primary tumors into the circulation and serve as key mediators of metastatic spread. While most CTCs undergo apoptosis or clearance, a small subset survives, extravasates, and seeds secondary lesions, contributing substantially to patient mortality [ ]. Resident stem cells with self-renewal and multilineage differentiation capacity act as a cellular reservoir for tissue maintenance and repair. Under appropriate conditions, these cells can either self-renew or commit to specialized lineages including blood, neural and muscle cells, thereby sustaining tissue and organ integrity [ – ]. In cancer, CTCs and resident stem-like stromal cells interact dynamically within the evolving PMN to facilitate niche maturation and support metastatic progression [ , ]. Recent advances in single-cell multi-omics and spatial analytics are providing new insights into the phenotypic and functional features of these cellular drivers.

## Distant colonization of circulating tumor cells
CTCs serve as the vanguard of metastasis, disseminating from the primary tumor to colonize distant PMNs. Their colonization efficiency depends on both quantitative dynamics (including CTC burden and temporal fluctuations) and qualitative traits (such as invasive, metabolic, and epigenetic adaptability), collectively determining whether a disseminated cell can establish a viable metastatic colony.

## Prognostic significance of CTC abundance and dynamic changes
While it is well recognized that tumor cells detach from the primary tumor to enter the circulation, an often-overlooked point is that CTC abundance may be a prerequisite for successful colonization of distant organs. A critical question is whether a sufficient number of CTCs is required to reach conducive distant sites and initiate colonization.
Evidence supporting the prognostic significance of CTC burden comes from multiple cancers. In colorectal cancer, mesenchymal CTCs (mCTCs) are significantly associated with distant metastasis, a correlation potentially explained by the higher COX-2 expression in mCTCs [ ]. In breast cancer, a meta-analysis of 6,825 patients confirmed that CTC count is a robust prognostic indicator for both early-stage [PFS: HR = 2.86; OS: HR = 2.78] and metastatic disease [PFS: HR = 1.78; OS: HR = 2.33] [ ]. Furthermore, in hepatocellular carcinoma patients undergoing liver transplantation, the dynamic change from pre-operative CTC-negative to post-operative CTC-positive status conferred a significantly higher recurrence risk (49.2%) compared to the reverse scenario (22.1%) [ ]. Together, these studies underscore that sufficient CTC abundance is necessary for successful distant colonization and that dynamic changes in CTC counts are essential for monitoring tumor progression and treatment efficacy.
Technological advances are continually improving the sensitivity of CTC detection. For instance, a high-throughput static microfluidic device with 38,400 chambers has been developed to isolate and enumerate metabolically active CTCs in peripheral blood at single-cell resolution [ ]. This technology enables more accurate quantification of functional CTCs, further enhancing the clinical utility of CTC-based prognostic assessment. Beyond enumeration, dissecting the molecular and metabolic heterogeneity of CTCs provides deeper insight into their metastatic potential.

## CTC characterization as the foundation for metastatic risk assessment
CTCs must possess a strong invasive capacity to penetrate the vascular endothelium, escape the circulation, and enter the distant organ interstitium. This capacity encompasses inherent migratory ability, metabolic regulation, and cytoskeletal support [ , ].

## Functional adaptations supporting CTC Survival and invasion
Groundbreaking studies have revealed that intracellular bacteria can persistently colonize specific tumor cells, playing unique pathophysiological roles in supporting metastasis [ ]. Research led by Professor Shang Cai from West Lake University revealed that low levels of bacteria are present in normal breast cells; however, during breast cancer development, these bacteria expand dramatically. These bacteria remodel the tumor cell cytoskeleton via RhoA-ROCK signaling, helping cells withstand fluid shear stress and damage during vascular/intrastitial penetration, thereby supporting distant metastasis via systemic circulation [ ].
Surface molecules on CTCs, including adhesion molecules and proteases, also play critical roles in metastasis. For example, Adhesion molecules facilitate CTC interactions with vascular endothelial and distant organ parenchymal cells, allowing arrest at specific sites, adaptation to the new microenvironment, and initiation of metastatic lesions [ , ]. High CD47 expression on the surface of CTCs indicates enhanced migratory and invasive capacities, enabling them to evade phagocytosis by immune cells such as T cells, NK cells, and macrophages. This renders CD47 a promising biomarker for the diagnosis and prognosis of cancer patients. Furthermore, combining CD47 targeting with other tumor surface biomarkers, such as Epithelial Cell Adhesion Molecule (EpCAM) and CD44, facilitates isolation of CTCs from patients’ peripheral blood [ , ].

## Metabolic heterogeneity of CTCs
Metabolic heterogeneity in CTCs, defined as intercellular variability in metabolic pathway activity and metabolite composition, critically determines metastatic potential. A seminal single-cell metabolic fingerprinting study classified CTCs into C1 and C2 subgroups based on glutamate, lactate, aspartate, and malate levels. The C2 phenotype serves as a high-risk functional biomarker, strongly associated with metastatic risk in colorectal cancer (AUC = 0.927) and enriched for pro-metastatic pathways like amino acid and glutathione metabolism and the Warburg effect. This metabolic heterogeneity underlies invasive heterogeneity: C2-type CTCs exhibit greater migratory, proliferative, and distal-colonization capacities in vitro and in vivo. Thus, metabolic reprogramming directly dictates a CTC’s potential to invade and form metastases, providing a key mechanistic explanation for why only a minority of CTCs drive metastasis [ ]. This study demonstrates that metabolic heterogeneity in CTCs is a critical determinant of their invasive capacity, and indicating that the CTC metabolic profile may serve as a functional biomarker for real-time metastasis risk stratification.

## CTC clusters as high-metastatic-potential entities
Interestingly, CTCs are not always solitary; they often cluster with other cells, particularly immune cells, to form CTC clusters [ ]. Although CTC clusters are rare compared to single CTCs, they exhibit significantly stronger survival and invasive capabilities with metastatic potential increased 20- to 50-fold [ ]. Individual tumor cells are more vulnerable to immune elimination, whereas clustering provides protective and nutritive support, enhancing survival. Therefore, understanding the patterns of CTC and cluster emergence remains a challenge for metastasis monitoring and intervention. Decoding the epigenetic and mechanical basis of clustering will be crucial for developing strategies to disrupt these multicellular metastatic units.

## Clinical implications and advances in CTC analysis
Invasive CTCs act as the metastatic “vanguard,” colonizing the PMN to form metastases while retaining primary tumor characteristics. Clinically, CTC detection can indicate tumor presence earlier than imaging, thereby supporting early screening. It aids not only in early lesion identification but also in predicting metastatic risk, enabling timely intervention.
Technological advances are refining CTC analysis. Currently, a single-cell sequencing workflow to interrogate a few tumor cells (SWIFT-seq) has been established and successfully applied to analyze paired bone marrow (BM) and CTC samples, enabling the development of a CTC classifier for inferring cytogenetic abnormalities, measuring tumor proliferative index, and capturing clonal dynamics. A circulatory dynamics model incorporating tumor burden, proliferation, cytogenetics, and a circulatory capacity signature has also been established to explain CTC burden, advancing blood-based myeloma diagnostics and illuminating dissemination mechanisms [ ]. Furthermore, CTC molecular subtyping provides a comprehensive tumor status, and subtype analysis based on therapeutic targets can predict drug efficacy and guide treatment. CTC clustering is linked to specific DNA hypomethylation patterns that promote stemness and metastasis. Compared to single CTCs, CTC clusters exhibit specific hypomethylation of DNA associated with stem cell- and proliferation-related transcription factors, including OCT4, NANOG, SOX2, and SIN3A—sharing similarities with embryonic stem cell biology. These findings suggest that cluster-targeting compounds could inhibit cancer spread [ ]. A recent study established a non-invasive pipeline for identifying patient-specific neoantigens via high-throughput isolation and exome sequencing of CTCs from blood. This method identified numerous high-affinity T-cell epitopes from advanced cancer patients, providing novel targets for metastasis intervention. Compared with tissue biopsies, this strategy more comprehensively captures tumor heterogeneity. It is minimally invasive and repeatable, thereby laying a technical foundation for the rapid development of personalized immunotherapies such as peptide or mRNA vaccines [ ].
These findings underscore that CTCs are not passive passengers but metabolically and mechanically adapted entities capable of active colonization. Moving forward, the clinical implementation of dynamic, sequential monitoring of CTC counts and phenotypes is warranted, as such data may provide sensitive indicators for predicting metastasis.

## Malignant transformation of stromal stem-like cells
Parallel to CTC colonization, the activation and malignant transformation of stromal stem-like cells constitute the second essential axis of PMN evolution [ ]. These cells respond to tumor-derived cues and engage in reciprocal interactions with CTCs, collectively shaping a microenvironment permissive for metastatic outgrowth [ , ].

## Activation of endogenous stem cells in the PMN
New technical approaches have not only advanced our understanding of CTCs but also aided in identifying stromal stem-like cells. For example, a study using CTC markers discovered parenchymal cells with stem-like properties in the lung PMN of breast cancer metastasis [ ]. Indeed, while CTC seeding is a prerequisite for PMN formation, subsequent colonization and specialization rely heavily on stem cell-like properties, including self-renewal and differentiation capacities [ ].

## Evidence and mechanistic basis for stem-like cells within PMN
Accumulating evidence confirms the presence of stem-like cells in both the TME and PMN. The existence of stem-like populations within PMN blurs the boundary between stromal remodeling and tumor initiation, suggesting that malignant transformation can originate from non-tumor cells under persistent paracrine stimulation. Early studies showed that bone marrow-derived hematopoietic progenitor cells expressing VEGFR1 accumulate in tumor-specific PMN, forming cell clusters before CTC arrival. Blocking VEGFR1 function with antibodies or depleting VEGFR1+ cells from wild-type mouse bone marrow eliminated the formation of these pre-metastatic clusters and prevented tumor metastasis [ ]. Notably, the process undergone by tumorigenic cancer cells is also analogous to the self-renewal and differentiation of normal stem cells, suggesting that stemness maintenance is a core feature of both tumor cells and stromal cells in the PMN.

## Molecular regulatory mechanisms of stem cell activation
The activation and malignant transformation of stromal stem-like cells are tightly regulated by classic developmental signaling pathways and phenotypic changes.
Numerous tumor-related classic pathways also regulate normal stem cell development. For example, Notch, Sonic hedgehog, and Wnt signaling pathways are all involved in regulating the self-renewal of hematopoietic stem cells and other tissue stem cells. Wnt proteins are intercellular signaling molecules that regulate the development of various organisms; their dysregulated expression triggers tumorigenesis and metastasis [ , ]. Notch and Hedgehog pathways further reinforce the pro-metastatic PMN by promoting EMT, maintaining cancer stem cell properties, and inducing angiogenesis [ , ].
Cell transformation drives key phenotypic alterations, including altered surface receptor expression, remodeled cytoskeletal function, abnormal secretion of growth factors and cytokines, increased production of proteolytic enzymes, and changes in glycosyl transferase and glycosidase libraries. These combined changes reshape the way transformed cells communicate with the microenvironment and alter how surrounding normal cells recognize them. They not only endow transformed cells with the ability to break through tissue barriers (freeing them from the constraints of defined tissue compartments) but also enable them to dominate the host tissue microenvironment and exploit its resources for their own survival, growth, and dissemination [ ].

## Bidirectional crosstalk with cancer stem cells
The origin and activation mechanisms of stem-like cells within the PMN remain incompletely understood. A prevailing theory posits that specific genetic and transcriptional alterations in resident pluripotent adult stem cells can predispose them to undergo malignant transformation into CSCs [ – ]. These alterations dysregulate self-renewal and apoptotic resistance, thereby laying the groundwork for malignant transformation.
The bidirectional crosstalk between stromal stem-like cells and CSCs is mediated by key pathways that establish a self-reinforcing, pro-metastatic feedback loop. These mechanisms include cytokine/chemokine signaling, extracellular vesicle (EV) transmission, and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling [ ]. For example, stromal stem cells secrete collagen I and fibronectin, which form a rigid ECM that activates YAP/TAZ signaling in CSCs, promoting their metastatic potential [ ]. Similarly, the secreted protein Sema3C has been identified as a pivotal regulator in HCC. Sema3C acts in an autocrine manner to maintain stemness in HCC cells and in a paracrine manner to activate hepatic stellate cells, driving their transformation into CAFs and subsequent ECM remodeling. In turn, these CAFs secrete TGF-β1, which further stimulates Sema3C expression in tumor cells, creating a potent positive feedback loop that fuels the malignant transformation of stem-like cells within the TME and PMN [ ]. Meanwhile, the variable differentiation of these stem-like cells contributes to the heterogeneity of the PMN.
Recent research in gastric cancer provides a detailed blueprint of how such a CSC-supportive niche is constructed through multicellular crosstalk. Integrated spatial and single-cell sequencing of human gastric tissues revealed that CSCs engage with a specific consortium of stromal and immune cells. They interact with immunosuppressive CXCL13+ T cells and CCL18+ M2 macrophages to evade immune surveillance, while simultaneously forming a critical alliance with inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs) to promote tumorigenesis and maintain stemness, a niche configuration strongly linked to poor prognosis. At the molecular heart of this interaction, iCAF-derived amphiregulin (AREG) was identified as a master regulator. AREG promotes CSC properties by upregulating the stemness transcription factor SOX9 and concurrently drives chemotherapy resistance via the AREG-ERBB2 axis. This work exemplifies the co-option of specific stromal partners by CSCs to build a resilient, therapy-resistant microenvironment [ ].
Notably, selectivity exists in these cellular interactions. Invasive micropapillary carcinoma (IMPC), which occurs in multiple organs (including breast, lung, and bladder), is characterized by high lymphovascular invasion and lymph node metastasis. Shi et al. (2022) found that numerous lymph node metastases in IMPC often originate from a single primary tumor subclone, a pattern associated with lymph node metastasis and poor survival [ ]. This suggests that within the CSC-stromal cell interaction framework, a “clonal selection” model may operate, wherein only specific mutated subclones gain the ability to cooperate with stromal stem-like cells.
Together, these reciprocal signaling axes illustrate that PMN colonization is a co-evolutionary process between tumor and stroma, with each reinforcing the other’s stemness and survival. Given their central role in orchestrating PMN maturation, stromal stem-like cells are emerging as critical therapeutic gatekeepers of metastasis. Consequently, strategies aimed at disrupting their activation, differentiation, or stemness hold significant preclinical promise for impeding metastatic progression.

## The critical role of extracellular vesicles in PMN formation
The systemic coordination of PMN formation across distant organs requires robust mediators of intercellular communication. Among these, EVs are established key mediators of intercellular communication. By releasing their cargo upon interaction with recipient cells under specific conditions, EVs transmit signals that induce physiological changes in target cells. While early research focused on elucidating the biogenesis of distinct EV subpopulations [ – ], current investigations center on their pathological roles across various diseases, including cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and cardiovascular diseases. The functions of EVs are primarily dictated by their cellular origin and cargo composition, enabling them to regulate diverse biological processes such as immune responses, antigen presentation, cell migration, differentiation, and tumor invasion [ ]. Notably, recent studies reveal that EVs contribute significantly to PMN formation through mechanisms like mediating metabolic reprogramming and fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment [ , , , ] (Fig. ).
 

## EVs as intercellular communication mediators disrupt PMN homeostasis
EVs mediate intercellular communication through three primary mechanisms [ ], which collectively disrupt the homeostasis of distant organ microenvironments to prime PMN formation. These include the binding of EVs membrane proteins to recipient cell surface proteins to activate intracellular signaling pathways; the cleavage of EVs membrane proteins by ECM proteases, with the resulting fragments acting as ligands for target cell receptors to initiate downstream signaling; and the direct fusion of EVs membranes with recipient cell membranes, resulting in the non-selective release of EVs cargo such as proteins, mRNAs, and microRNAs.
These communication modes enable EVs to remodel distant microenvironments, thereby facilitate metastasis. Research confirms that EV membrane proteins are key mediators in activating recipient cell signaling. For instance, HSP60 present on the surface of breast cancer-derived EVs binds to Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) on lung fibroblasts, activating the NF-κB pathway to upregulate CCL2 expression. This signaling cascade orchestrates the recruitment of immunosuppressive cells, representing a critical regulatory step in assembling the pulmonary PMN. Supporting this mechanism, clinical evidence shows that circulating EV levels and surface HSP60 correlate positively with disease progression and lung metastasis in breast cancer patients, underscoring the translational relevance of this mechanism [ ]. In CRC models, primary tumors secrete EVs enriched with integrin β-like 1 (ITGBL1) into the circulation. These EVs activate resident fibroblasts in distant organs. These activated fibroblasts secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and IL-8, to induce PMN formation and promote metastatic growth. Mechanistically, ITGBL1-enriched EVs from primary CRC stimulate TNFAIP3-mediated NF-κB signaling in fibroblasts, promoting the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines that support metastatic progression [ ]. Notably, EVs also shuttle nucleic acids to modulate immune suppression within the PMN. Senescent cells release mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) into the cytoplasm and extracellular space. Upon uptake by MDSCs in the PMN, this extracellular mtDNA activates the cGAS-STING-NF-κB pathway. Furthermore, STING-PERK signaling amplifies NF-κB activation, thereby enhancing PMN-MDSC-mediated immunosuppression. The finding that blocking mtDNA release improves chemotherapy efficacy highlights the critical role of EV-packaged nucleic acids in disrupting PMN immune homeostasis [ ].
Collectively, these mechanisms indicate that EVs are not merely passive byproducts but function as active contributors to PMN formation, bridging systemic inflammation and local niche remodeling.

## EVs as cargo carriers provide essential materials for PMN formation
As mentioned above, EVs package a diverse array of cargo that directly supplies the molecular and structural materials needed to shape the PMN. Recent studies have further elucidated the direct role of EVs in promoting PMN formation, which involves not only enhancing EMT induction but also the delivery of functional mitochondria that provide essential bioenergetic support for this process.
PDAC relies on TME components to promote migration from the primary site, a process often involving EMT. Pancreatic stellate cells and matrix stiffness are major drivers of PDAC invasiveness. Notably, even before pancreatic cancer cell dissemination begins, soluble factors and EVs secreted by primary tumors, including pre-malignant lesions, help form a supportive niche in the liver. They collectively prime the hepatic PMN by providing vascular docking sites for CTCs, enhancing vascular permeability, remodeling the ECM, and recruiting immunosuppressive inflammatory cells [ ].
Tumor cell growth and expansion require substantial energy, with mitochondria serving as the primary energy source. Recent studies reveal that tumor cells can exchange or acquire exogenous mitochondria via tunneling nanotubes (TNTs) [ , ]. Mitochondria can also be transferred between cells via migrasomes to repair damaged units, thereby ensuring tumor cell energy supply [ ]. Importantly, mitochondria or their components can be encapsulated within larger EVs, and interactions between EVs and mitochondria facilitate intercellular mitochondrial transfer [ – ]. Intriguingly, highly metastatic cells can transfer mitochondria to less metastatic or stromal cells, a process that may represent a previously underappreciated mechanism contributing to metastatic support by metabolically influencing stromal and immune cells within the PMN to sustain long-term metastatic support [ ].
Notably, metabolic cues within the PMN are not strictly confined to local cell-cell interactions. Emerging evidence indicates that metabolic information can be systemically disseminated via EVs, which transports lipids, metabolic enzymes, and regulatory RNAs to coordinate immune and stromal reprogramming across distant organs, further reinforcing PMN conditioning [ ].
EVs represent one of several long-range communication mechanisms contributing to PMN conditioning. Although current evidence supports a predominantly modulatory rather than deterministic role, these findings redefine EVs as bioenergetic and structural couriers that facilitate stromal activation and enhance metastatic competence within organ-specific PMNs. Consequently, targeting EVs biogenesis, cargo packaging, or EV-mediated mitochondrial uptake represents a conceptually attractive strategy, although its therapeutic feasibility and safety remain to be rigorously evaluated. Future studies that integrate genetic perturbation, lineage tracing, and organ-specific delivery will be essential to establish causal roles of EVs in PMN formation [ ].

## Limitations and translational challenges of EV-Targeted PMN interventions
Despite growing evidence supporting the involvement of EVs in PMN formation, several critical limitations and unresolved challenges must be acknowledged before EVs-centered concepts may be translated into clinical strategies.
A major challenge arises from the pronounced heterogeneity of EV populations. Current studies frequently define EVs operationally, with isolation approaches such as differential ultracentrifugation, precipitation-based methods, or size-exclusion chromatography yielding vesicles that differ substantially in size, surface markers, and cargo composition [ , ]. As a result, EV-associated functions reported in PMN studies may reflect composite effects of mixed vesicle populations rather than discrete, biologically defined entities. This methodological variability complicates cross-study comparisons, limits reproducibility, and hampers the identification of PMN-specific EV signatures that may be reliably exploited for therapeutic or diagnostic purposes.
Equally important is the difficulty of discriminating the functions of pathological, tumor-educated EVs from their physiological counterparts. EV-mediated signaling constitutes a fundamental mechanism for maintaining tissue homeostasis, immune surveillance, and metabolic coordination under normal conditions [ , ]. Many pathways implicated in PMN conditioning, including NF-κB activation, metabolic reprogramming, and immune cell recruitment, are not inherently tumor-specific. Consequently, systemic interference with EVs biogenesis, release, or uptake carries the risk of disrupting essential physiological communication networks, raising concerns regarding off-target effects, immune dysregulation, and long-term toxicity.
From a translational perspective, additional barriers further temper therapeutic enthusiasm [ ]. Strategies aimed at inhibiting EV production or secretion often target broadly expressed regulators such as neutral sphingomyelinase-2 or Rab family GTPases, which lack tumor or organ specificity. Moreover, although EVs attracted interest as drug-delivery vehicles, challenges related to large-scale manufacturing, cargo-loading efficiency, batch-to-batch consistency, biodistribution control, and regulatory standardization remain largely unresolved [ , ]. These issues collectively constrain the feasibility and safety of EV-based interventions targeting PMN formation.
Taken together, although EVs clearly contribute to PMN conditioning, their role should be viewed as predominantly modulatory rather than deterministic. Future progress will require improved EV classification frameworks, standardized isolation and characterization pipelines, and organ or cell type restricted targeting strategies. Such advances will be essential for defining when and where EVs modulation can be therapeutically beneficial without compromising systemic homeostasis, thereby enabling a more realistic assessment of EVs-based approaches in metastasis prevention.

## Therapeutic and clinical implications
The expanding understanding of the PMN paves the way for clinical strategies aimed at the early interception of metastasis, bridging fundamental discovery and bedside application. Although mechanistic insights are increasing, direct clinical targeting of PMN biology remains challenging, and most proposed strategies are not yet ready for routine clinical application. The overarching goal of this research is to translate mechanistic insights into clinical practices to enable early intervention and improve patient outcomes.

## Developing biomarkers for early PMN detection
Early detection of PMN formation is a prerequisite for effective metastasis prevention, relying on specific biomarkers and advanced monitoring technologies.
As outlined earlier, quantifying EVs combined with multi-omic profiling of their cargo offers a promising approach for assessing the extent and risk of PMN formation. Furthermore, a noteworthy study employed a custom-built single-cell mass spectrometry platform to analyze target metabolites within individual CTCs, a strategy with broad application potential in PMN evaluation. By applying a machine learning pipeline that integrated non-negative matrix factorization and logistic regression to the acquired metabolic fingerprints, CTCs were stratified into two distinct subgroups, designated C1 and C2. The abundance of C2-subgroup CTCs demonstrated a strong correlation with metastatic propensity in experimental models. This work underscores the potential of integrating single-cell metabolomics with advanced computational analysis for precise CTC stratification, offering a powerful strategy to refine metastasis risk assessment [ ]. In gastric cancer peritoneal metastasis, mutations in genes such as ELF3, CDH1, and PIGR, along with stromal infiltration and M2 macrophage enrichment within the TME, have been identified as core biomarkers for predicting metastatic risk. The study also revealed downregulated expression of CLDN18.2 and FGFR2b in peritoneal metastases. Critically, integrated multi-omics data and murine models confirmed that peritoneal PMNs are established even in early-stage gastric cancer, driven by specific TME reprogramming. This work not only delineates key molecular pathways underlying transcoelomic spread but also provides novel targets including ELF3/CDH1 genomic alterations and an M2 macrophage-centered microenvironmental signature for the early detection of PMN, offering new indicators for identifying metastatic risk at the preclinical stage [ ]. Alternatively, measuring the abundance and composition of peripheral immune cell subsets represents another valuable potential biomarker for assessing PMN formation risk [ , ]. Similarly, circulating metabolites and cytokines, particularly those that function as immune cell chemokines or inflammatory mediators, show promise as biomarkers for assessment [ ].
As underlying mechanisms are further elucidated, several tumor-derived factors have emerged as critical determinants of PMN risk [ ]. For example, studies indicate that secreted phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1) plays a key role in promoting metastasis in HCC. In multiple mouse models, SPP1 facilitates HCC lung colonization by remodeling the pulmonary PMN, and plasma SPP1 levels correlate closely with lung metastasis in HCC patients. Mechanistically, SPP1 binds to CD44 on lung alveolar epithelial cells, inducing CXCL1 production. This chemokine subsequently recruits and enriches neutrophils in the lungs. These SPP1-activated neutrophils form neutrophil extracellular trap (NET)-dominant PMNs that capture disseminated tumor cells and promote metastatic colonization. Early intervention targeting the SPP1-mediated PMN through combined blockade of the CXCL1-CXCR2 axis and NET formation effectively suppresses HCC lung metastasis. These findings suggest that molecules including SPP1, CD44 and CXCL1, along with the abundance of activated neutrophils, may serve as effective targets for comprehensively evaluating PMN formation risk [ ].
Collectively, these integrative approaches transform PMN monitoring from static observation to dynamic prediction, enabling truly pre-emptive cancer care. Once reliable biomarkers facilitate early detection of PMN, the next step is to design precision therapeutic interventions that address the heterogeneity of organ-specific PMNs.

## Precision, heterogeneity-adapted therapies
Targeting the PMN requires consideration of organ- and tumor-specific heterogeneity, necessitating evidence-based and promising strategies that integrate existing drugs, molecular insights, and emerging therapies. For instance, EVs possess dual therapeutic relevance that serve as targets for inhibiting PMN formation and as nanocarriers for drug delivery. Given that immunosuppression and metabolic reprogramming are hallmarks and key drivers of the PMN, reversing these alterations in cancer patients represents a promising therapeutic avenue.

## Targeting EVs to inhibit PMN formation
EVs exhibit organotropic targeting properties such as integrin α6β4 for lung and αvβ5 for liver, enabling tissue-specific inhibition of PMN formation with the potential to minimize systemic side effects [ ]. Combining EV-targeted therapies with conventional chemotherapy or immunotherapy may synergistically block metastasis. It should be noted that EV heterogeneity in size, cargo, and surface markers complicates the development of universal targets. Furthermore, potential off-target effects on physiological EV functions, including immune regulation, must be addressed.
Recent studies illustrate that selectively neutralizing or engineering EVs can effectively intercept the metastatic cascade at its earliest stage. Osteosarcoma-derived small EVs (OS-sEVs) induce the formation of CAF, which are characterized by elevated pro-inflammatory factor expression and enhanced migratory and contractile abilities. These fibroblasts play a crucial role in regulating the lung PMN. Consequently, blocking communication between these vesicles and lung fibroblasts has emerged as a rational strategy to hinder osteosarcoma lung metastasis. Supporting this approach, a previously established saponin-mediated cargo elimination strategy can reduce the pro-tumorigenic capacity of OS-sEVs while preserving their intrinsic targeting ability. When applied to OS-sEVs, the resulting cargo-eliminated vesicles (ce-sEVs) exhibited minimal pro-tumorigenic and fibroblast-activation potential but retained their ability to target lung fibroblasts. Functionally, these engineered vesicles inhibited the uptake of native OS-sEVs by lung fibroblasts in a concentration-dependent manner, prevented fibroblast transformation into CAF, and thereby suppressed PMN formation and lung metastasis. Collectively, this work proposes using ce-sEVs to competitively inhibit native vesicle function as a promising strategy to prevent fibroblast activation, intra-pulmonary niche formation, and ultimately osteosarcoma lung metastasis [ ].
This competitive interception strategy exemplifies a broader therapeutic concept: disrupting EV-mediated intercellular communication at any stage of the EV life cycle may effectively suppress PMN formation. Other targeting strategies, including inhibiting EV biogenesis, blocking their release, or preventing their uptake, are being actively investigated for their efficacy in suppressing PMN formation and metastasis, with the aim of achieving clinical application.

## EVs as nanoscale drug carriers
EVs possess inherent biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and natural targeting ability, making them ideal carriers for delivering small molecules, siRNAs, or immunotherapeutic to the PMN.
A study demonstrates an inhalable EV-based strategy to inhibits pulmonary metastasis. Engineered M1 macrophages-derived EVs, overexpressing CXCR4 and loaded with the PD-1/PD-L inhibitor BMS202, were delivered via aerosol to postoperative melanoma mice. These EVs accumulated in the lungs, where surface CXCR4 scavenged CXCL12, thereby limiting monocytic MDSCs recruitment. Simultaneously, the EVs repolarized TAMs toward an M1-like phenotype, alleviating immunosuppression. The BMS202 released induced PD-L1 dimerization on CTCs, blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 interaction to enhance T-cell-mediated clearance. This multipronged approach effectively disrupted PMN formation and suppressed metastatic progression [ ]. It is also worth noting that spontaneous mitochondrial transfer is often limited by insufficient quantity or impaired quality, restricting the efficacy of mitochondrial transplantation therapy. To address this, a team has developed a strategy for preparing “Super EV Mito” vesicles. They use genetically engineered mesenchymal stem cells to stimulate mitochondrial biogenesis and the secretion of mitochondria-containing EVs via activation of intracellular calcium-related pathways, thereby increasing the quantity and quality of packaged mitochondria to overcome the limitations of traditional approaches [ ]. In addition, mesenchymal stem cell-derived EVs may be homed to inflammatory microenvironments, enhancing drug accumulation in PMNs [ ].
However, differences between experimental EVs dosing and physiological exposure should be carefully considered when interpreting these findings. Beyond this methodological consideration, substantial challenges remain for its potential in clinical application, including low cargo loading efficiency, difficulties in large-scale production, and the lack of standardized isolation and purification methods. Furthermore, the limited stability of EVs in systemic circulation and their susceptibility to clearance by the reticuloendothelial system represents additional barriers that must be overcome to advance their therapeutic application.

## Targeting metabolism and immunity to intervene in PMN formation
Accumulating research has shed light on the distinct metabolic dependencies of various immune cell subsets within tumors, suggesting that integrating metabolic interventions with current immunotherapies, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors, could offer a promising strategy to boost treatment response and counter resistance [ , ]. A pivotal study demonstrates that inducing trained immunity with whole beta-glucan particles can reprogram myeloid cells, thereby inhibiting tumor metastasis and prolonging survival in preclinical models. Mechanistically, this effect is mediated through a specific metabolic pathway involving sphingosine-1-phosphate and mitochondrial fission. The research identifies this sphingolipid-mitochondrial axis as a critical targetable mechanism, revealing that metabolic reprogramming of innate immune cells represents a promising strategy to prevent or halt tumor dissemination [ ].
However, several attempts to target PMN-associated pathways, such as broad anti-inflammatory or myeloid-targeting strategies, yielded limited or inconsistent clinical benefit, which highlight challenges in patient stratification, timing of intervention, and distinguishing systemic effects from organ-specific niche vulnerabilities [ , – ] (Table ).
 

## Integrating AI and large-scale data to support PMN research
As a diagnostic tool, AI shows significant value in both tumor diagnosis and outcome prediction [ – ]. As understanding of the PMN deepens, the complexity of its biology demands interdisciplinary integration that bridges bioinformatics, clinical large-scale data analysis, and AI modeling to achieve predictive and personalized metastasis management. A growing body of research demonstrates the value of AI in metastasis research, particularly its integration with multi-omics to address the high-dimensional challenge posed by PMN biomarkers. This indicates that cross-disciplinary tools are essential for overcoming bottlenecks in PMN clinical translation [ – ].
One study utilized AI to analyze HER2 expression levels and TME characteristics in patients with HER2-amplified metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) from the phase II TRIUMPH trial. An AI algorithm was employed to quantify the percentage of tumor cells exhibiting the highest HER2 staining intensity and to measure stromal immune cell densities. These digitally derived features were then correlated with clinical outcomes in patients receiving trastuzumab plus pertuzumab therapy. The results demonstrated that patients with a high percentage of AI-identified HER2 3+ tumor cells experienced significantly improved survival. Notably, this survival benefit was substantially enhanced when combined with a favorable TME characterized by high immune cell density. This study validates the utility of AI for predicting therapeutic response by establishing an objective, quantitative, and integrative biomarker platform. This platform transcends conventional pathological assessment to more accurately identify those patients who are most likely to benefit from targeted therapies [ ].
Another investigation leveraged natural language processing to automatically extract and analyze metastatic patterns from the autopsy records of 2,622 metastatic breast cancer patients within a nationwide pathology database. The AI model demonstrated high performance, achieving an accuracy of 0.90 and a recall of 0.94 in identifying metastasis sites, thereby surpassing the performance of manual extraction. Subsequent analysis uncovered distinct, subtype-specific organotropism. For example, lobular carcinoma exhibited a frequent pattern of dissemination to bone and liver, whereas grade III tumors showed a heightened propensity for bone metastasis. This study underscores the significant utility of AI in metastasis research, as it facilitates the efficient, large-scale analysis of complex clinical data to reveal patterns of cancer spread that are both biologically coherent and clinically informative [ ].
Based on data and tissue images from 456 patients in the CHAARTED trial, a recent study developed and validated a multimodal AI (MMAI) biomarker for metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC). The AI model analyzed digital histopathology alongside and clinical data to generate a prognostic risk score, which strongly predicted overall survival, disease progression, and progression to castration resistance. Importantly, the MMAI biomarker retained its prognostic value across different clinical subgroups, independent of traditional factors such as disease volume or treatment type. This demonstrates the significant application value of AI in metastasis prediction, as it can extract crucial prognostic insights directly from tissue images to enable more precise risk stratification, thereby providing a valuable framework for the image-based analysis of the PMN [ ].
Importantly, the above studies largely demonstrate the retrospective, correlative, or prognostic utility of AI approaches in metastasis-related analyses, rather than validated, real-time clinical decision-support tools specifically designed for PMN diagnosis or intervention.
Despite considerable progress, research on the formation mechanisms and targeted interventions of PMNs remains in its early stages, with substantial work ahead. Looking forward, AI holds promise for advancing PMN research through several exploratory and hypothesis-generating applications. For instance, AI enables the early monitoring of PMN maturation by analyzing longitudinal multi-omics data, facilitating timely intervention before clinically evident metastasis. It further supports personalized prevention by tailoring screening strategies such as testing for EV-associated integrin αvβ5 in high-risk patients, to align with organ-specific metastatic tendencies, for example, the propensity for liver versus lung colonization. Additionally, AI aids in predicting organotropism and metastasis risk by mining retrospective clinical big data, including tumor type, biomarker levels, and imaging characteristics. Through the integration of multi-omics and spatial imaging data, including spatial transcriptomics, EV cargo profiles, and clinical outcomes, AI can help identify organ-specific therapeutic targets such as N2 neutrophils in the lung PMN. Finally, via digital pathology, AI can analyze routine histology images to infer underlying molecular signatures, such as PD-L1 expression or distinct metabolic phenotypes, offering a cost-effective screening tool even in resource-limited settings.
Critical challenges remain before these exploratory AI models may be used clinically. Most current AI models are developed and evaluated using curated datasets under controlled conditions, limiting their immediate generalizability to routine clinical workflows. In particular, the lack of prospective, multicenter validation, limited model interpretability, and evolving regulatory frameworks for AI-based clinical decision support systems currently restrict their deployment in routine oncology practice.
These considerations suggest that PMN interception is more likely to arise from biomarker-guided, context-specific strategies than from broad, pathway-agnostic interventions. Nevertheless, as biomarker-guided and spatially informed trial designs mature, PMN-targeted strategies may become increasingly tractable in selected clinical contexts.

## Conclusion and perspective
Since Stephen Paget’s seminal “seed and soil” hypothesis in 1889, the understanding of tumor metastasis has evolved from a simplistic cell-organ interaction model to the recognition of the PMN as a dynamic, spatiotemporally regulated ecosystem. This review synthesizes current evidence to demonstrate that PMN formation is initiated early in tumor evolution and is orchestrated through coordinated crosstalk among tumor cells, immune and stromal compartments, and systemic factors. Inflammatory signaling, immune remodeling, vascular and lymphatic alterations, metabolic reprogramming, and temporal regulation collectively prime distant organs for future colonization, often long before overt metastases become clinically detectable.
Rather than representing a uniform or static entity, the PMN emerges as a dynamic, hierarchical ecosystem shaped by permissive environmental changes, instructive tumor-host signaling, and selective pressures that govern metastatic success. Across immune regulation, CTC dynamics, EV-mediated communication, metabolic reprogramming, and data-driven profiling, converging evidence indicates that PMN formation follows coordinated and context-dependent principles rather than isolated molecular events. Framing PMN biology through this system-level lens helps reconcile organotropism, temporal heterogeneity, and variable therapeutic vulnerability, and provides a conceptual foundation for biomarker-guided interception strategies aimed at early metastatic control. The multi-layered organization of PMN formation discussed above is summarized schematically in Fig.  .
 A major conceptual advance highlighted in this review is the incorporation of temporal and spatial dimensions into PMN biology. Early dissemination from small primary tumors, circadian regulation of CTC release, and the emergence of spatially restricted niche hubs such as perivascular regions in the liver or vascular branch points in the brain, underscore that metastatic susceptibility is pre-patterned rather than stochastic. In parallel, EVs emerge as central coordinators of niche assembly, integrating systemic signaling with local stromal and immune reprogramming, while stromal stem-like cells and bone marrow-derived progenitors act as amplifiers of niche plasticity.
Despite these advances, several challenges continue to limit clinical translation. First, the lack of specific and non-invasive biomarkers hampers early detection of PMN. Discriminating pre-metastatic alterations from physiological inflammation will likely require integrated biomarker panels that capture niche states rather than individual pathways, combining EV cargo signatures, CTC metabolic fingerprints, and organ-specific immune profiles. Second, pronounced PMN heterogeneity across organs, tumor types, and individuals necessitates precision strategies that target context-dependent niche features, such as neutrophil polarization in lung PMNs [ ] or M2 macrophages in liver PMNs [ ], while avoiding therapy-induced niche activation [ ]. Third, conventional experimental approaches remain insufficient to resolve the spatiotemporal evolution of PMNs, highlighting the need for interdisciplinary integration of spatial omics, advanced imaging, and computational modeling.
Viewed through this lens, PMN formation may be conceptualized as a dynamic, multi-layered process encompassing permissive, instructive, and selective phases. Permissive layers establish a receptive tissue landscape through vascular, metabolic, and stromal remodeling. Instructive layers transmit systemic tumor-derived cues that actively reprogram local immune and stromal compartments. Selective layers then impose evolutionary pressures that favor the survival and outgrowth of tumor cell subclones adapted to specific organ niches. This integrative framework reconciles diverse mechanistic observations across cancer types and reframes PMN not as static preconditions, but as evolving ecosystems shaped by systemic signals, local reprogramming, and tumor cell adaptation.
Looking ahead, progress in PMN research should focus on addressing a focused set of testable priorities rather than further expansion of descriptive complexity. First, robust, minimally invasive biomarkers that reflect niche states, rather than single pathways, must be prospectively validated, integrating EV cargo profiles, CTC metabolic features, and organ-specific immune signatures. Second, causal relationships between PMN components and metastatic competence need to be established using spatially resolved perturbation strategies that can dissect permissive, instructive, and selective layers in vivo. Third, therapeutic efforts can prioritize context-specific vulnerabilities within defined PMN subtypes, while explicitly accounting for the risk of therapy-induced niche activation. Advances in AI-assisted spatial multi-omics and clinical data integration will be essential to operationalize these goals, enabling patient stratification and early risk prediction. By shifting emphasis from treating established metastases to intercepting niche formation at defined stages, PMN biology may ultimately support a transition from reactive metastatic control to proactive, precision prevention.
