## Abstract
We previously proposed the classification of lung adenocarcinoma into two groups: the bronchial epithelial phenotype (BE phenotype) with high‐level expressions of bronchial epithelial markers and actionable genetic abnormalities of tyrosine kinase receptors and the non‐BE phenotype with low‐level expressions of bronchial Bronchial epithelial (BE) epithelial markers and no actionable genetic abnormalities of tyrosine kinase receptors. Here, we performed a comprehensive analysis of tumor morphologies in 3D cultures and xenografts across a panel of lung cancer cell lines. First, we demonstrated that 40 lung cancer cell lines (23 BE and 17 non‐BE) can be classified into three groups based on morphologies in 3D cultures on Matrigel: round (n = 31), stellate (n = 5), and grape‐like (n = 4). The latter two morphologies were significantly frequent in the non‐BE phenotype (1/23 BE, 8/17 non‐BE, p = 0.0014), and the stellate morphology was only found in the non‐BE phenotype. SMARCA4 mutations were significantly frequent in stellate‐shaped cells (4/4 stellate, 4/34 non‐stellate, p = 0.0001). Next, from the 40 cell lines, we successfully established 28 xenograft tumors (18 BE and 10 non‐BE) in NOD/SCID mice and classified histological patterns of the xenograft tumors into three groups: solid (n = 20), small nests in desmoplasia (n = 4), and acinar/papillary (n = 4). The latter two patterns were characteristically found in the BE phenotype. The non‐BE phenotype exhibited a solid pattern with significantly less content of alpha‐SMA‐positive fibroblasts (p = 0.0004) and collagen (p = 0.0006) than the BE phenotype. Thus, the morphology of the tumors in 3D cultures and xenografts, including stroma genesis, reflects the intrinsic properties of the cancer cell lines. Furthermore, this study serves as an excellent resource for lung adenocarcinoma cell lines, with clinically relevant information on molecular and morphological characteristics and drug sensitivity.
Keywords: 3D culture, epithelial mesenchymal transition, lung adenocarcinoma cell lines, SMARCA4, xenograft tumor

Here, we focused on cell line morphology in 3D cultures and xenograft tumors. In the bronchial epithelial phenotype, most of the cell lines displayed a round morphology in 3D culture, and some of them showed acinar or papillary patterns and desmoplastic reactions in xenograft tumors. In contrast, the non‐bronchial epithelial phenotype cell lines frequently displayed stellate or grape‐like morphologies in 3D culture and showed solid patterns with no desmoplastic reactions in xenograft tumors. This study serves as an excellent resource for lung adenocarcinoma cell lines with clinically relevant information on molecular and morphological characteristics.

## INTRODUCTION
Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer death in many developed countries, including the United States and Japan, , and adenocarcinoma is the most common histological subtype of primary lung cancer.
Recently, many studies have reported that primary lung adenocarcinomas can be classified according to their histology and expression of differentiation markers, which correlate with background genetic abnormalities: a non‐mucinous lepidic pattern with TTF‐1 expressions correlates with EGFR mutations, , mucinous cribriform pattern correlates with ALK/RET fusions, , , solid pattern without lepidic growth correlates with SMARCA4 mutations, and mucinous morphology with HNF4alpha expressions correlates with KRAS mutations and TTF‐1 mutations. , , 

Experimental models based on a panel of cell lines are a rich source of information about the mechanisms of growth inhibition and tumor cell death by anticancer drugs. However, it is difficult to determine which cell line is clinically compatible with which primary tumor. Particularly, lung adenocarcinoma has heterogeneity, and the selection of its model cell lines requires caution. Previously, we focused on the relationship between driver mutations and differentiation phenotypes in lung adenocarcinoma and classified a panel of non‐squamous non‐small‐cell lung cancer cell lines (mainly lung adenocarcinoma cell lines) into two groups: the bronchial epithelial (BE) phenotype with high‐level expressions of bronchial epithelial markers (TTF1, CK7, and CDH1), high expressions of cancer invasion‐ and proliferation‐related genes (e.g., TGFB1, LAMC2, MMP1, MET, HER3, and COX2) and frequent actionable genetic abnormalities of tyrosine kinase receptors (EGFR, MET, HER2, and RET) and the non‐bronchial epithelial (non‐BE) phenotype with triple‐negative expression for TTF‐1, CK7, and CDH1, high expressions of EMT markers (ZEB1, FGFR1, and VIM), and no actionable genetic abnormalities of tyrosine kinase receptors. Based on the differences in background genetic abnormalities between the BE and non‐BE phenotypes, it is assumed that their carcinogenic mechanisms are different. Overexpression of so‐called EMT‐related molecules, such as TGF‐β, LAMC2, and MMP1, is observed in the process of malignant progression of the BE phenotype, but this does not mean that the BE phenotype is transformed into a non‐BE phenotype. This classification of BE and non‐BE phenotypes was also applicable to primary lung adenocarcinomas. The gene expression data of 442 primary lung adenocarcinoma cases were classified into three groups: BE, non‐BE, and a subtype of BE with high expression of epithelial markers but low expression of cancer invasion‐ and proliferation‐related genes (e.g., TGFB1, LAMC2, MMP1, MET, HER3, amd COX2), showing well‐differentiated histology and favorable prognosis. Primary lung adenocarcinoma cases in the subtype of BE group are presumably difficult to establish cancer cell lines for and may transform into the BE group of lung adenocarcinomas during the process of malignant progression.
Walter et al. also reported the classification of non‐small cell lung cancer cell lines into epithelial‐like and mesenchymal‐like subsets based on patterns of methylation, which was consistent with our results.
Existing lung cancer cell line databases provide information on the driver mutations and gene expression, but unfortunately, they do not provide information on cell line morphology. In addition, integrated classification of lung cancer cell lines based on morphology and genomic data has not been reported.
Here, we focused on cell line morphology in 3D cultures and xenograft tumors. Recently, 3D culture has attracted attention because it can culture cancer cells in a state closer to in vivo than 2D culture. Based on a panel of breast cancer cell lines, Kenny et al. reported that the morphology of breast cancer cell lines on 3D cultures correlated with transcriptional profiles. Xenograft tumors are also good tools to evaluate the histological growth pattern of cancer cells and the extent of desmoplastic reaction. Desmoplastic reaction, histologically observed as active fibroblast proliferation and the deposition of dense collagen around the cancer cell nests, is involved in tumor progression, such as invasion, metastasis, and angiogenesis. , , , 

Here, we provide an integrated classification of lung adenocarcinoma cell lines based on driver mutations, differentiation phenotype, and morphology in 3D cultures and xenograft tumors. This work can serve as a useful resource for cell line experiments.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1.

## Cell lines and medium
We used 40 non‐squamous non‐small‐cell lung cancer cell lines, 35 adenocarcinoma cell lines (H23, H292, H358, H441, H522, H650, H1395, H1648, H1650, H1651, H1703, H1781, H1793, H1838, H1975, H1993, H2009, H2087, H2228, H2405, HCC827, HCC4006, PC9, Calu‐3, A549, ABC1, PC3, VMRC‐LCD, RELF‐LC‐Ad1, RELF‐LC‐Ad2, RELF‐LC–MS, HLC‐1, LC‐2/ad, RERF‐LC‐KJ, and L27), four large‐cell carcinoma cell lines (Lu65, H460, H661, and H1299), and one adenosquamous cell line (H596). Detailed information on the 40 cell lines is available in our previous reports. , , , , , PC9 was formerly known as PC14 (for more information, see https://www.culturecollections.org.uk/products/celllines/generalcell/detail.jsp?refId=90071810&collection=ecacc_gc).

2.2.

## Mutational analysis of the 40 cell lines
Gene mutations in 40 cell lines were based on our previous reports and data from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (https://portals.broadinstitute.org/ccle/). , , , 

2.3.

## Classification of non‐small cell lung cancer cell lines into bronchial epithelial and non‐bronchial epithelial phenotypes
In our previous study, we classified 40 non‐small‐cell lung cancer cell lines into two groups: the BE phenotype (n = 22) and the non‐BE phenotype (n = 18). However, according to the latest gene mutation data from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (https://portals.broadinstitute.org/ccle/), driver mutations of some cell lines, which had been unknown at the time, were identified. In particular, H2228 had been classified as a non‐BE phenotype in the previous study but was reclassified as a BE phenotype in the present study because it was found to have ALK fusion, so 23 cell lines (H292, H358, H441, H596, H1648, H1650, H1781, H1838, H1975, H1993, H2009, H2087, H2228, HCC827, HCC4006, PC9, Calu‐3, PC3, RELF‐LC‐Ad1, RELF‐LC‐Ad2, LC‐2/ad, RERF‐LC‐KJ, and L27) were BE phenotype and 17 cell lines (H23, H522, H650, H1395, H1651, H1703, H1793, H2405, A549, ABC1, VMRC‐LCD, RELF‐LC–MS, HLC‐1, Lu65, H460, H661, and H1299) were non‐BE phenotype in this study.

2.4.

## Gene expression profile and single nucleotide polymorphism array analysis
A comprehensive gene expression analysis was performed using an oligonucleotide microarray (GeneChip Human Genome U133A, Affymetrix) as described previously. , , Single nucleotide polymorphism array (Affymetrix human mapping 50 K XbaI array) analysis was performed using the Genome Imbalance Map algorithm as described previously. 

2.5.

## Western blot analysis
Cells were lysed in lysis buffer consisting of 20 mmol/L Tris–HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mmol/L NaCl, 50 mmol/L NaF, and 1 mmol/L Na3VO4 with a cocktail of proteinase inhibitors. After sonication, lysates were boiled at 98°C for 5 min and cleared by centrifugation. Protein concentrations were measured using the DC Protein Assay Kit (Bio‐Rad). For western blotting, equal amounts of protein samples were size‐separated on 8% polyacrylamide gels and electroblotted onto nitrocellulose membranes. Nonspecific binding was blocked by immersing the membranes for 20 min in 5% skim milk in TBS at room temperature. The membranes were washed with TBS buffer containing 0.1% Tween 20, incubated at room temperature for 1 h with primary Abs, washed, and then reacted with peroxidase‐conjugated secondary Abs. The antigen was detected using ECL western blotting detection reagents (Amersham) following the manufacturer's instructions. The sources of the Abs used in the present study are summarized in Table  .

2.6.

## 
3D culture
We added 1.5 mL/well of Matrigel (BD Biosciences) twofold diluted with medium (RPMI) onto six‐well plates and left them in a CO2 incubator for more than 30 min to gel them. Next, we gently layered 2 mL/well of medium containing cancer cells (2–8 × 105/mL) on the gel and analyzed it after 4 or 5 days.

2.7.

## Classification of cell line colony morphologies on 3D culture
Referring to the classification of breast cancer cell line colony morphologies on 3D culture in the previous report with a slight modification, two pathologists (DM and TN) classified the 40 cell lines into three groups: round (n = 31), grape‐like (n = 4), and stellate (n = 5). To verify the classification, we used a fivefold cross‐validation test with image analysis based on a two‐stage convolutional neural network (CNN) as described previously. 

2.8.

## Xenograft tumors
We established xenograft tumors of the lung cancer cell lines by injecting cell suspensions (5 × 106) into the flanks of 6‐week‐old female severe combined immunodeficient mice NOD C.B‐17‐Prkdc scid/J (NOD/SCID). Mice were killed after 6 weeks or when weight loss of 20% or more occurred or the maximum tumor size was larger than 10 mm, to prevent suffering. Mice were deeply anesthetized by inhalation of 4% isoflurane and euthanized by manual cervical dislocation. Subcutaneous tumors were harvested, fixed with 10% formalin, and embedded in paraffin blocks. Tumor histology was examined after H&E or Azan stains.

2.9.

## Evaluation of growth patterns of cancer cells in xenograft tumors
Two pathologists (DM and TN) evaluated growth patterns of the xenograft tumors (n = 28) and classified them into three groups based on the predominant pattern: solid pattern (n = 20), small nests in desmoplasia pattern (n = 4), and acinar or papillary pattern (n = 4).

2.10.

## Evaluation of stromal reaction (fibroblasts proliferation and stromal deposition of dense collagen) in xenograft tumors
We performed alpha‐SMA immunostaining and Masson's trichrome stain to evaluate stromal active fibroblasts and collagen fibers in xenograft tumors, respectively. For the methods of immunostaining and evaluation of alpha‐SMA, we followed our previous report. We selected three representative areas of each xenograft tumor, acquired using a 20× objective lens, and measured the ratio of the positive areas in each, using the BZ‐X800 Analyzer software (KEYENCE).

2.11.

## Immunostaining of xenograft tumors
We performed immunostaining of E‐cadherin, Vimentin, ZEB1, TTF‐1, SMARCA4, and SMARCA2 in xenograft tumors. For the methods of their immunostaining and evaluation, we followed our previous reports. , , , 

2.12.

## Evaluation of invasion patterns at the margins of xenograft tumors
The predominant invasion patterns at the tumor border were classified into two groups: the infiltrating growth pattern and the expansile growth pattern (shown in Figure  ). In the infiltrating growth pattern, cancer forms irregular foci or elongates irregularly, with surrounding fibroblasts. In the expansile growth pattern, cancer forms a well‐defined borderline mass and grows in a way that compresses the stroma.

2.13.

## Determination of Ki‐67 index in xenograft tumors
Immunostaining for Ki‐67 was performed. The immunostaining method followed our previous report. The average Ki‐67 positivity (%) was calculated from the number of Ki‐67 positive and Ki‐67 negative cells counted using BZ‐X800 Analyzer software (Keyence) from six independent rectangular regions (400× field of view) of each xenograft tumor. Figure  shows Ki‐67 staining images of H1650 (cell line with the highest Ki‐67 positivity in the BE phenotype), HCC4006 (cell line with the lowest Ki‐67 positivity in the BE phenotype), H460 (cell line with the highest Ki‐67 positivity in the non‐BE phenotype), and H1651 (cell line with the lowest Ki‐67 positivity in the BE phenotype).

2.14.

## Measurement of xenograft tumor size
We measured the size of xenograft tumors in mice killed 6 weeks after injection of the cancer cell lines. Notably, two BE phenotype cell lines (H1975 and H1993) and two non‐BE phenotype cell lines (VMRC‐LCD and H2405) produced tumors that were 10 mm in size without waiting 6 weeks, so these four cell lines were excluded from this measurement.

2.15.

## Inhibitors
We screened the growth inhibitory effects of 285 SCADS (Screening Committee of Anticancer Drugs) Inhibitors using Kit I ver2.2, Kit II initial version, and Kit III ver1.2. , First, the inhibitors were tested at 200 nM in nine cancer cell lines (H358, H441, H23, H460, A549, H552, H1299, H1703, and H2405) selected from our panel of 40 cell lines, based mainly on the non‐BE phenotype and KRAS mutation. From this one‐shot screening, 16 compounds that exhibited more than 75% growth inhibition in at least one cell line were selected. The selected inhibitors were further tested for their growth inhibitory effect across the panel of 40 cell lines.

2.16.

## Assessment of cancer cell sensitivity to inhibitors
Cell viability was measured using the Cell Counting Kit (CCK) − 8 assay (Dojindo, Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cells (4 ∼ 8 × 103 cells) were plated in the wells of 96‐well microtiter plates. After 24 h, each inhibitor was added to respective wells at a concentration of 200 nM. The cells were then incubated for another 3 days at 37°C. The absorbance of each well at 450 nm was measured with a reference at 630 nm using a BIO‐RAD model 680XR microplate reader (Bio‐Rad). The percentage of cell viability was calculated using the following formula: %cell viability = (mean absorbance in test wells)/(mean absorbance in control well) × 100. The experiment was performed twice, and the average values were calculated.

2.17.

## Statistical analysis
The Mann–Whitney U‐test was used to evaluate the correlation of the BE and non‐BE phenotype classifications with the ratios of stromal active fibroblasts and collagen fibers in xenograft tumors. The χ2‐test was used to evaluate the correlation of colony morphologies in 3D cultures with the BE and non‐BE phenotypes, genetic mutations, and gene expression levels. The results were considered significant if the p‐value was less than 0.05. All statistical calculations were performed using the StatView computer program (Abacus Concepts).

## RESULTS
3.1.

## Classification of non‐small cell lung cancer cell lines into bronchial epithelial and non‐bronchial epithelial phenotypes
Figure  shows the determinations of BE and non‐BE phenotypes, colony morphologies in 3D culture, predominant growth patterns in xenograft tumors, the gene level expressions (CDH1, VIM, ZEB1, TTF‐1, SMARCA4, and SMARCA2), and the presence or absence of genetic abnormalities (EGFR, MET, HER2, ALK, RET, KRAS, BRAF, and SMARCA4) for the 40 cell lines. Figure  shows the immunohistochemical expression patterns of E‐cadherin, Vimentin, ZEB1, TTF‐1, SMARCA4, and SMARCA2 in xenograft tumors of representative cell lines of BE and non‐BE phenotypes (BE: PC3, L27, and H441; non‐BE: A549, H650, and HLC1), consistent with the respective gene expression levels shown in Figure  .
 Figure  shows the protein level expressions of E‐cadherin, Vimentin, SMARCA4, SMARCA2, and beta‐catenin of the 40 cell lines by western blotting. Figures  and revealed that the non‐BE phenotype frequently exhibited loss of E‐cadherin, SMARCA4, and SMARCA2 and high expression of Vimentin and ZEB1 at the mRNA levels and protein levels.
 We also examined the gene expression of SNAIL and SLUG, EMT‐induced transcription factors, shown in Figure  . The SNAIL gene tended to be highly expressed in the non‐BE phenotype, whereas the SLUG gene was highly expressed in the BE phenotype (Figure  ), as shown in our previous report. Unfortunately, we could not find antibodies against SNAIL and SLUG that can be used for immunohistochemistry and western blotting.

3.2.

## Classification of 3D morphologies of lung carcinoma cell lines into three groups: round, grape‐like, and stellate‐shaped
We, specifically two pathologists (DM and TN), classified the 40 cell lines into three groups: round (n = 31), grape‐like (n = 4), and stellate‐shaped (n = 5), depending on their 3D morphologies (shown in Figure  ). Figure  shows images of representative cell line colonies with round morphology (HCC827, HCC4006, and H358), grape‐like morphology (H650, H2405, and Lu65), and stellate morphology (H1703, H23, and H522) in 3D culture. The images of colonies of all 40 cell lines in 3D culture are reposited at Figshare (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.20358663). Next, to verify the classification, we performed a CNN‐based image analysis. We prepared a total of 722 images (200× field of view) as learning data, containing 311 images of representative cell line colonies with round morphology (HCC827), 211 images with grape‐like morphology (H650), and 200 images with stellate‐shaped morphology (H1703). We also prepared a total of 230 images of the 40 cell lines as test data, with five to six images per cell line. We performed a fivefold cross‐validation test and confirmed that the match rate with our classification in each test exceeded 90%. Finally, using the architecture, which averages the methods of the five learning sets, we were able to achieve 96.09% accuracy on the test subsets (results shown in Table  ).
 
3.3.

## 
Non‐bronchial epithelial phenotype correlated with non‐round morphology (grape‐like or stellate‐shaped)
The BE phenotype typically exhibited the round morphology, except for PC9. In contrast, non‐round morphologies were significantly frequent in the non‐BE phenotype (one of the 23 in BE, eight of the 17 in non‐BE, p = 0.0014), and stellate‐shaped morphology was only found in the non‐BE phenotype (0 of the 23 BE, five of the 17 in non‐BE, p = 0.0054) (shown in Figure  ). Non‐round morphology was significantly correlated with lower level expressions of CDH1 (p = 0.0002, Mann–Whitney U‐test) and SMARCA2 (p = 0.0464, Mann–Whitney U‐test), and higher level expressions of VIM (p = 0.0037, Mann–Whitney U test) (shown in Figure  ). Figure  also shows that actionable genetic abnormalities, such as mutations and amplifications of EGFR, MET, and HER2, fusions of RET and ALK, and mutations of BRAF, were significantly frequent in round morphology cell lines (15 of the 31 round cell lines, one of the nine non‐round cell lines, p = 0.0305), whereas SMARCA4 mutations were significantly frequent in non‐round morphology cell lines (four of the 30 round cell lines, five of the eight non‐round cell lines, p = 0.0037), especially in stellate‐shaped morphology cell lines (four of the four stellate‐shaped cell lines and four of the 34 non‐stellate‐shaped cell lines, p = 0.0001). KRAS mutations were found in both round and non‐round morphology cell lines (four of the nine non‐round cell lines, seven of the 31 round cell lines, p = 0.1959) (shown in Figure  ).

3.4.

## 
Non‐bronchial epithelial phenotype correlated with solid pattern with less content of desmoplasia in xenograft tumors
Next, from the 40 cell lines, we successfully established 28 xenograft tumors (23 of the 31 round, four of the four grape‐like, and one of the five stellate‐shaped cell lines) in NOD/SCID mice. The establishment of xenograft tumors was more difficult for the non‐BE phenotype (10 of the 17, 59%) than for the BE phenotype (18 of the 23, 78%). Of the 28 cell lines, 20 exhibited solid patterns (shown in Figure  ), four exhibited small nests in desmoplasia patterns (shown in Figure  ), and four exhibited acinar or papillary patterns (shown in Figure  ). The images of HE stains of the xenograft tumors of the 28 cell lines are reposited at Figshare (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.20358663).
 Those exhibiting small nests in desmoplasia or acinar/papillary patterns all belonged to the BE phenotype. H1975 showed a solid pattern with spindle cell morphology (shown in Figure  ). H596 showed small nests in a desmoplasia pattern with squamoid morphology (shown in Figure  ). H2405 predominantly showed a solid pattern but locally showed acinar or papillary morphology. (These images are also available in Figshare: https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.20358663).
The area ratios of active fibroblasts (observed by alpha‐SMA staining) and dense collagen deposition (by Masson trichrome staining) in tumors were significantly higher in the BE phenotype than in the non‐BE phenotype (p = 0.0004, p = 0.0006, respectively, Mann–Whitney U test) (shown in Figure  ). Figure  shows the images of alpha‐SMA staining (Figure  ) and Masson trichrome staining (Figure  ) of the xenograft tumors of representative BE phenotype (H1648, H292, and H2009) and non‐BE phenotype cell lines (ABC1, HLC1, and H460). Thus, the BE phenotype induces a desmoplastic reaction, whereas the non‐BE phenotype proliferates solidly without stromal reaction.
 The images of alpha‐SMA stains and Masson trichrome stains of the xenograft tumors of the 28 cell lines are reposited at Figshare (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.20358663).
We also examined the histological pattern in the invasive front (infiltrating growth pattern vs expansile growth pattern) of 28 xenograft tumors (Table  ). The histological pattern at the invasive front also differs, with the non‐BE phenotype frequently showing an expansile growth pattern without involving stroma, whereas the BE phenotype frequently shows an infiltrating growth pattern and involving stroma (p = 0.0131) (Table  ; Figure  ). In particular, all cell lines that exhibited grape‐like or stellate morphology showed an expansile growth pattern.
Next, we examined the Ki‐67 index and maximum diameter of the xenograft tumors (Table  ). The Ki‐67 index was significantly higher in the non‐BE phenotype than in the BE phenotype (p = 0.0493) (Figures  ). However, tumor size did not differ significantly between BE phenotype and non‐BE phenotype (p = 0.8542) (Figure  ).
Despite the high proliferative potential of the non‐BE phenotype, xenograft tumors for the non‐BE phenotype are more difficult to establish and do not grow larger than those for the BE phenotype. This may be related to the non‐BE phenotype's inability to utilize stromal cells.

3.5.

## Drug sensitivities of 40 cell lines
We previously demonstrated that the non‐BE phenotype is more sensitive to cisplatin and paclitaxel than the BE phenotype. Next, we examined the susceptibility of the 40 cell lines to the following 16 inhibitors: Iressa (EGFR inhibitor), PHA665752 (MET inhibitor), Actinomycin D, Camptothecin, Trichostatin A (HDAC inhibitor), 17‐AAG (Hsp 90 inhibitor), Cucurbitacin I (Jak2 inhibitor), MG‐132 (proteasome inhibitor), Bafilomycin A1 (V‐ATPase inhibitor), Ouabain, Leptomycin B, Chetomin (HIF‐1alpha inhibitor), Cdk1/2 inhibitor III, Cdk2/3 inhibitor, SB218078 (Chk inhibitor), and PDGF receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitor IV. Table  shows cell viabilities (%) of 40 cell lines treated with each drug at a concentration of 200 nM.
We compared the drug sensitivity of the BE phenotype showing round morphology (n = 22) with that of the non‐BE phenotype showing round morphology (n = 9), non‐BE phenotype showing stellate morphology (n = 5), and non‐BE phenotype showing grape‐like morphology (n = 3). The results with significant differences are shown in Figure  . The non‐BE phenotype showing stellate morphology was significantly resistant to Iressa but sensitive to Ouabain, CDK1/2 inhibitor III, Chetomin, Actinomycin D, and MIG‐132. The non‐BE phenotype showing grape‐like morphology was resistant to Chetomin, Actinomycin D, and MIG‐132 but sensitive to Ouabain and CDK1/2 inhibitor III. Moreover, Ouabain was effective against the non‐BE phenotype with any morphology.

## DISCUSSION
It has been stated that adenocarcinoma cell lines are poorly differentiated, with no glandular structure of the stromal response, which is different from the cancers encountered in clinical practice, and their validity as an experimental cancer model is questionable. Our results do not support this opinion. Lung adenocarcinoma cell lines inherited the characteristics of the primary lung adenocarcinoma and can be classified into two characteristic groups: BE and non‐BE phenotypes. , In the BE phenotype, most of the cell lines displayed a round morphology in 3D culture, and some of them showed acinar or papillary patterns and desmoplastic reactions in xenograft tumors. In contrast, the non‐BE phenotype cell lines frequently displayed stellate or grape‐like morphologies in 3D culture and showed solid patterns with no desmoplastic reactions in xenograft tumors. In general, tumor morphology in the human body consists of tumor cell architecture (papillary, glandular, and solid) and stroma. The present data show that, for each cell line, the characteristic architecture and stroma, which are also found in clinical tumors, are reconstituted in the xenograft tumors. Moreover, in co‐injection of human fibroblasts or PDX in mice, human fibroblasts eventually disappear and are replaced by those of mouse origin. , The present study shows that stromal formation in xenograft tumors is also dependent on the intrinsic property of the cancer cells.
Kenny et al. reported that breast cancer cell lines can be classified into four groups based on 3D colony morphologies: round, mass, grape‐like, and stellate. Stellate and grape‐like morphologies reflected poor cell–cell interactions with low‐level expressions of E‐cadherin. In our study, all the BE phenotype cell lines except PC9 showed a round morphology, while the non‐BE phenotype frequently showed grape‐like or stellate morphologies, which was consistent with Kenny et al. The BE phenotype showed high‐level expressions of cell adhesion molecules, like integrins and cadherins, in our previous study. Exceptionally, the PC9 cell line exhibited grape‐like morphology even though it was a BE phenotype. PC9 has an inactive mutation of the CTNNA1 gene, a cell adhesion molecule, which may cause a loss of adhesiveness. , , 

It is also interesting that cells harboring inactivating mutations of SMARCA4 exhibited stellate morphology, which was consistent with our previous study, where the loss of SMARCA4 and SMARCA2 was frequently found in the mesenchymal‐like phenotype in primary lung adenocarcinomas. 

Myofibroblasts (alpha‐SMA positive fibroblasts) play important roles to promote the growth, invasion, and metastasis of cancer cells. , , , Pathologists commonly consider the emergence of myofibroblasts or desmoplasia as evidence of invasion. However, invasion is not always accompanied by stromal myofibroblast proliferation in primary lung cancers. Some invasive cancers showed no sign of myofibroblast proliferation in the stroma. In this study, the BE phenotype frequently showed a prominent desmoplastic reaction with the proliferation of myofibroblasts and dense collagen deposition. Our previous study revealed that the BE phenotype showed high‐level expressions of growth factors (TGFB and TGFA), laminins (LAMA1, A3, C2, and B3), and proteinases (MMP1, CTSS, PLAU, and PLAT), and these genes are involved in the cancer‐stromal interaction. Thus, the BE phenotype cell lines can grow, invade, and metastasize by cleverly utilizing stromal cells, including myofibroblasts. In contrast, the non‐BE phenotype cell lines, with low‐level expressions of cancer‐stromal interaction‐associated genes, are less dependent on stromal cells and show a medullary pattern with less desmoplasia. The non‐BE phenotype often exhibits an expansile growth pattern with no stromal involvement in the invasive front. These characteristics of the non‐BE phenotype are presumably similar to those of large‐cell carcinomas and pleomorphic carcinomas, which generally have relatively clear boundaries with their surroundings. Now, we are proceeding with interactome analysis between cancer (human) and stroma (mouse) in xenograft tumors, which we would like to present in the next paper.
In this report, we also examined the drug sensitivities of the 40 cell lines. Among the non‐BE phenotype, sensitivity to therapeutics varied depending on the morphology under 3D culture. For example, the non‐BE phenotype with stellate morphology was responsive to Chetomin, Actinomycin D, and MG‐132, whereas the non‐BE phenotype with grape‐like morphology was resistant. Furthermore, Ouabain and CDK 1/2 inhibitor III were effective against the non‐BE phenotype with grape‐like morphology, and in particular, Ouabain was effective against all non‐BE phenotypes. Ouabain is a Na+/K + ‐ATPase inhibitor that has recently attracted attention as an anti‐tumor agent, which suppresses cancer metastasis by enhancing cell detachment and the dissociation of circulating tumor cell clusters into single cells. , Ouabain has also been reported to exhibit anti‐tumor effects through inhibiting EMT, which is consistent with our results.
Existing cell line databases provide information on driver mutations and gene expression data, but none include morphological information. In this study, we performed an integrated classification of lung adenocarcinoma cell lines based on driver mutations, differentiation phenotype, and morphology in 3D cultures and xenograft tumors. This data will be an important resource for studies regarding lung adenocarcinoma cell lines because it will help determine which cell lines correspond to which primary lung adenocarcinomas.
In conclusion, the colony morphologies of cancer cell lines in 3D cultures and cancer morphologies and cancer‐stromal reactions in xenograft tumors reflected the phenotypic difference of BE and non‐BE cell lines, classified based on the presence or absence of actionable mutations of tyrosine kinase receptors and expression patterns of cancer‐associated genes.
