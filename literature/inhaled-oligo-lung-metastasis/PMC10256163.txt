## INTRODUCTION
The epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)–rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (RAS)–extracellular signal–regulated protein kinase (ERK) pathway controls many cellular processes, including proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, cell cycle arrest, and metabolism ( – ). Classical components of the EGFR-RAS-ERK pathway include EGFR, growth factor receptor-bound protein 2 (GRB2), Src homology 2 domain–containing protein (SHC), Son of sevenless (SOS), RAS, the RAF kinases, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) kinase 1/2 (MEK1/2), and ERK1/2. EGFR is a transmembrane receptor activated by growth factors, including EGF and transforming growth factor–α. Two adaptor proteins, GRB2 and SHC, bind to activated EGFR and link EGFR to the guanine nucleotide–releasing factor SOS. SOS catalyzes the conversion of guanosine diphosphate–bound RAS (inactive state) to guanosine triphosphate (GTP)–bound RAS (active state), triggering the activation of a cascade consisting of three pivotal kinases, namely, RAF, MEK1/2, and ERK1/2. Activated ERK1/2 phosphorylates nuclear proteins, especially transcription factors that control gene transcription and subsequent cellular processes. Many proteins have been reported to interact with one to three classical components, especially one classical component, of the EGFR-RAS-ERK pathway ( – ). For instance, argonaute 2 interacts with KRAS, a member of the RAS family, to control KRAS-driven cellular transformation. β-Arrestin acts as an adaptor that binds RAF, MEK, and ERK, leading to ERK activation. However, the integral assembly of EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling complexes from the upstream component EGFR to the downstream component ERK is largely unknown.
The EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling pathway plays an important role in cancer development and progression and is dysregulated in cancer. Overexpression and/or mutations of components of the EGFR-RAS-ERK pathway, such as EGFR, RAS, and RAF, are often observed in human cancers, such as lung, colon, and breast cancers, and are associated with aggressive phenotype and poor prognosis in patients with cancer ( – ). Thus, EGFR, GRB2, RAS, RAF, MEK1/2, and ERK1/2 have become targets for anticancer therapeutics ( – ). For instance, EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors were developed to target tumors overexpressing EGFR or tumors with EGFR mutations. Inhibitors targeting RAF, MEK1/2, and ERK1/2 are now in varying stages of preclinical and clinical evaluation. Despite initial good responses to these inhibitors, patients with dysregulated EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling inevitably become resistant. On the other hand, these inhibitors can cause side effects. Therefore, it is of great significance to elucidate how components of the EGFR pathway assemble and to identify alternative targets for cancer therapy.
Hematopoietic pre-B cell leukemia transcription factor (PBX)–interacting protein (HPIP/PBXIP1) is involved in embryogenesis and oncogenesis ( ). HPIP overexpression has been shown in many cancers, such as breast cancer, lung cancer, and colorectal cancer, and predicts poor prognosis in patients with cancer ( – ). HPIP promotes cancer cell proliferation, migration, invasion, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and metastasis ( , ). HPIP activates phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT) and MAPK/ERK by forming a complex with estrogen receptor, the p85 subunit of PI3K and the sarcoma gene encoding a kinase (Src), and promotes estrogen-responsive gene expression ( , ). To further investigate mechanisms underlying the role of HPIP in tumorigenesis, we used co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) combined with mass spectrometry to identify its interaction partners and found that HPIP interacts with all classical components of the EGFR-RAS-ERK pathway. We demonstrated that HPIP is essential for formation of EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling complexes, activation of the EGFR-RAS-ERK pathway and EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling–mediated promotion of aerobic glycolysis, as well as cancer cell proliferation in vitro and tumor growth in vivo.

## RESULTS

## HPIP interacts with all classical components of the EGFR-RAS-ERK pathway
To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying the role of HPIP in tumorigenesis, we carried out co-IP and mass spectrometry to identify its interacting proteins. Besides the previously reported HPIP-interacting proteins (TANK-binding kinase 1, Src, and Calpain) ( , , ), we identified EGFR, KRAS, and ERK2, classical components of the EGFR-RAS-ERK pathway, as potential HPIP interaction partners ( and table S1). Co-IP of exogenous and endogenous proteins confirmed these interactions ( ). HPIP also interacted with neuroblastoma RAS (NRAS) and Harvey RAS (HRAS), other two members of the RAS family (fig. S1A). HPIP associated with all other calssical components of EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling, including SHC, GRB2, SOS1, RAF1, and MEK1/2, but not the EGFR-RAS-ERK downstream targets cellular myelocytomatosis oncoprotein (c-Myc) and cellular FBJ murine osteosarcoma viral oncoprotein homolog (c-Fos) ( ). Purified glutathione S-transferase (GST)–tagged HPIP, but not GST, interacted with in vitro–transcribed and in vitro–translated EGFR, SHC, GRB2, SOS1, KRAS, RAF1, MEK1/2, and ERK1/2 products, but not c-myc and c-Fos products ( ), suggesting that the interaction of HPIP with these components of EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling is direct because the in vitro–transcribed and in vitro–translated products should not contain components of EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling. Given that HPIP interacts with all classical components of the EGFR-RAS-ERK pathway, we chose some components as representatives for further study. Purified GST-HPIP, but not GST, interacted with purified His-tagged KRAS, MEK1, and ERK1, further emphasizing the direct interaction of HPIP with components of EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling (fig. S1B). GST-HPIP (221 to 271), GST-HPIP (272 to 327), and GST-HPIP (328 to 401), but not GST or other GST-tagged HPIP deletion mutants, interacted with KRAS, with the strongest interaction between HPIP (272 to 327) and KRAS. GST-HPIP (402 to 442) and GST-HPIP (443 to 561) interacted with MEK1 with the strongest interaction of HPIP (402 to 442) with MEK1. GST-HPIP (443 to 561) interacted with ERK1 (fig. S1B). Moreover, HPIP colocalized with EGFR, SHC, GRB2, SOS1, KRAS, RAF1, MEK1/2, and ERK1/2 in the membrane and/or cytoplasm of A549 lung cancer cells ( ). We confirmed the specificity of the antibodies by immunoblot or immunofluorescence with control small interfering RNA (siRNA)– or target siRNA–transfected cells (fig. S2).
 

## HPIP forms multiple complexes with classical components of the EGFR-RAS-ERK pathway and enhances interaction between the classical components
As HPIP associates with all classical components of the EGFR-RAS-ERK pathway, we tested how HPIP forms complexes with components of the EGFR-RAS-ERK pathway using fast protein liquid chromatography (FPLC) and a two-step immunoprecipitation assay (Re-IP). FPLC showed that at least two complexes formed with HPIP: one complex comprising HPIP, EGFR, SHC, GRB2, SOS1, RAS, RAF1, and MEK1/2 (close to 2000 kDa); and another complex comprising HPIP, SHC, RAS, RAF1, MEK1/2, and ERK1/2 (around 440 kDa) ( ). Consistent with the results of the first complex, co-IP revealed that EGFR interacted with RAF1, MEK1, and HPIP, but not ERK1 (fig. S3A). Re-IP experiments revealed that EGFR and HPIP formed a complex with MEK1, but not ERK1 (fig. S3B). In addition, in agreement with the results of the second complex, ERK1 interacted with SHC, KRAS, and HPIP, but not GRB2 (fig. S3C). As expected, ERK1 and HPIP formed a complex with KRAS, but not GRB2 (fig. S3D). Furthermore, Re-IP experiments showed that HPIP and the adjacent components of EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling cascade formed complexes, including HPIP, EGFR, and SHC; HPIP, EGFR, and GRB2; HPIP, SHC, and GRB2; HPIP, GRB2, and SOS1; HPIP, SOS1, and KRAS; HPIP, KRAS, and RAF1; HPIP, RAF1, and MEK1; and HPIP, MEK1, and ERK1 ( ). Intriguingly, HPIP overexpression increased the interaction between the adjacent components.
 Activation of a cascade consisting of the kinases RAF1, MEK1/2, and ERK1/2 is regulated by their phosphorylation and interaction with adjacent downstream component. For instance, for RAF-MEK-ERK kinase cascade, the RAF-MEK interaction facilitates MEK phosphorylation and activation, and the MEK-ERK interaction facilitates ERK phosphorylation and activation. RAF1 can be activated by its phosphorylation on threonine-491 (T491) and serine-494 (S494) ( ). Activated RAF1 recruits MEK1 and phosphorylates it on S217 and S221, resulting in MEK1 activation. Given that HPIP increases interaction between adjacent components of the RAF-MEK-ERK kinase cascade, we investigated the effect of HPIP on the interaction of the phosphorylated RAF1 with MEK1 and the interaction of the phosphorylated MEK1 with ERK1. Replacement of T491 and S494 with alanine (A) reduced the RAF1-MEK1 interaction ( and fig. S3E). Similarly, substitution of S217 and S221 with A decreased the MEK1-ERK1 interaction. HPIP failed to affect the RAF1 (T491A/S494A)–MEK1 or MEK1 (S217A/S221A)–ERK1 interaction, although HPIP increased the RAF1-MEK1 or MEK1-ERK1 interaction ( and fig. S3E). These results suggested that HPIP promotes interaction between adjacent components of RAF-MEK-ERK kinase cascade in a phosphorylation-dependent manner.

## HPIP KO or chemical inhibition of HPIP expression reduces interaction between the components of the EGFR-RAS-ERK pathway
As HPIP interacts with all classical components of EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling, we investigated the effect of HPIP knockout (KO) on interaction between the components of the EGFR-RAS-ERK pathway. Endogenous IP with antibodies against individual component of the EGFR-RAS-ERK pathway indicated that HPIP KO greatly decreased the interaction both between adjacent components (e.g., EGFR and SHC) and between nonadjacent components (e.g., EGFR and MEK1/2) in A549 lung cancer cells and ZR75-1 breast cancer cells ( and fig. S4). We chose A549 and ZR75-1 cells for this study because these cell lines have been used in previous research on EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling, and the transfection efficiency of these cells is relatively high. Consistent with the abovementioned results, endogenous EGFR, GRB2, and SOS1 did not associate with endogenous ERK1/2. Similar results were observed when HPIP expression was inhibited by the small chemical molecule TXX-1-10 ( ) ( ).

## HPIP is essential for activation of EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling
EGFR can be activated by the ligand EGF. EGF-stimulated EGFR phosphorylation activates RAS by inducing its binding to GTP via GRB2/SHC-SOS. GTP-bound RAS triggers the phosphorylation cascade, leading to ERK activation. We investigated whether HPIP activates the EGFR-RAS-ERK pathway. We chose three independent HPIP KO clones to determine the effect of HPIP on regulation of the EGFR-RAS-ERK pathway. All three independent HPIP KO clones had similar behaviors in terms of regulation of the EGFR-RAS-ERK pathway ( and fig. S5A). HPIP KO reduced phosphorylation of EGFR, RAF1, MEK1/2, and ERK1/2; GTP-bound RAS level; and expression of downstream targets of the EGFR-RAS-ERK pathway, including oncogene c-Myc, activator protein-1 family member c-Fos, and hypoxia-inducible factor–1α (HIF-1α), and abolished the ability of EGF to promote these effects. HPIP reexpression in the HPIP KO cells reversed these effects ( and fig. S5, A and B). Similar results were observed in HPIP knockdown (KD) Calu-3 lung cancer cells (fig. S5C). Considering previously reported role for HPIP in PI3K/AKT signaling, we also tested the effect of HPIP KO on AKT phosphorylation. HPIP KO also abolished the ability of EGF to activate AKT ( and fig. S5, A and B).
 In addition, HPIP KO in A549 and ZR75-1 cells abrogated the ability of EGFR, GRB2, KRAS, or RAF1 overexpression to stimulate their downstream targets, including GTP-bound RAS level, RAF1, MEK1/2, and ERK1/2 phosphorylation, and c-Myc, HIF-1α, and c-Fos expression ( and fig. S5D). Although A549 cells harbor KRAS (G12S) mutation and ZR75-1 cells have wild-type (WT) KRAS, KRAS overexpression in these cell lines had similar effects. Moreover, similar results were observed when A549 and ZR75-1 cells were treated with TXX-1-10 ( and fig. S5E). The effects of TXX-1-10 could be reversed by HPIP overexpression (fig. S5F). In addition, unlike WT HPIP, HPIP (△221 to 401) mutant that does not contain KRAS-interacting regions and HPIP (△402 to 561) mutant that does not contain MEK1/ERK1-interacting regions failed to enhance GTP-bound RAS level, RAF1, MEK1/2, and ERK1/2 phosphorylation and c-Myc, HIF-1α, and c-Fos expression (fig. S5G), suggesting that interaction between HPIP and components of RAS-ERK pathway is critical for activation of the pathway.
The amino acid at position 12 of KRAS is a hotspot for oncogenic mutations (e.g., G12A, G12C, G12D, and G12S), resulting in cancer development ( ). Activation of RAS (GTP-bound RAS) is catalyzed by the guanylnucleotide exchange factor SOS, and active RAS then interacts with RAF1. Our observations that HPIP overexpression enhanced SOS1-KRAS and KRAS-RAF1 interactions and that HPIP KO reduced these interactions suggest that HPIP is critical for increased KRAS GTPase activity. Thus, we investigated the effect of HPIP on the activities of these KRAS mutations. As expected, compared with wild-type KRAS, KRAS (G12A), KRAS (G12C), KRAS (G12D), and KRAS (G12S) increased KRAS activity, as determined by GTP-bound RAS ( and fig. S5H). HPIP KO in A549 and ZR75-1 cells decreased the activities of most KRAS mutants tested by at least one order of magnitude based on the ratio of the band intensity of GTP-bound RAS to that of input ( and fig. S5H).

## HPIP is critical for EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling–mediated promotion of aerobic glycolysis
Aerobic glycolysis or the Warburg effect, a hallmark of cancer, facilitates cancer cell growth with elevated glucose uptake and production of lactate and adenosine triphosphate (ATP). As the EGFR-Ras-ERK pathway has been shown to promote cancer cell glycolysis by stimulating its downstream targets, such as HIF-1α and c-Myc, two transcription factors critical for inducing glycolytic gene expression and glycolysis ( , ), we tested the effect of HPIP on EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling–mediated aerobic glycolysis. As expected, HPIP KO decreased glucose uptake, lactate production, and ATP level in A549 and ZR75-1 cells ( and fig. S6A). These effects were reversed by HPIP reexpression in the HPIP KO cells. HPIP KO showed decreased extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), a reflection of overall glycolytic flux, and increased oxygen consumption rate (OCR), an indicator of mitochondrial oxidative respiration ( and fig. S6B). Again, HPIP reexpression in the HPIP KO cells rescued these effects. HPIP KO abolished the ability of EGF to increase expression of the glycolytic genes hexokinase 2 (HK2) and lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), glucose uptake, lactate production, and ATP level ( , and fig. S6, C and D). HPIP KO also abrogated the ability of KRAS and MEK1 overexpression to enhance HK2 and LDHA expression, glucose uptake, lactate production, and ATP level ( , and fig. S6, E and F). Similar results were obtained with inhibition of HPIP expression by TXX-1-10 ( , and fig. S6, G and H). Together, these data indicate that HPIP is necessary for EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling–mediated aerobic glycolysis in cancer cells.
 

## HPIP is required for EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling–mediated promotion of cancer cell proliferation and growth in vitro and in vivo
It is well established that EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling promotes cancer cell proliferation in vitro and tumor growth in vivo. HPIP was also shown to enhance cancer cell proliferation and growth ( ). As HPIP activates EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling, we tested the effect of HPIP on EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling–mediated cancer cell proliferation and growth. HPIP KO or KD inhibited proliferation of cultured A549, ZR75-1, and Calu-3 cancer cells ( and fig. S7A). This effect was reversed by HPIP reexpression in HPIP KO or KD cells. HPIP KO or KD abolished the ability of EGF to stimulate cancer cell proliferation. HPIP KO or KD also abrogated the ability of KRAS and MEK1 overexpression to increase cancer cell proliferation ( and fig. S7B). Similar results were observed with inhibition of HPIP expression by TXX-1-10 ( and fig. S7C). These data suggested that HPIP is required for EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling–mediated cancer cell proliferation.
 Next, we examined the effect of HPIP on EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling–mediated tumor growth in nude mice. As expected, overexpression of KRAS and MEK1 promoted lung and breast tumor growth, accompanied by increased ERK1/2 phosphorylation and HK2 and LDHA expression ( and fig. S7D). HPIP KO abolished the ability of KRAS and MEK1 overexpression to promote these effects. Similar results were seen with inhibition of HPIP expression by TXX-1-10 ( and fig. S7E). Overall, these data suggest that HPIP is required for EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling–mediated cancer cell proliferation and tumor growth.

## Genetic ablation of HPIP blocks KRASG12D-driven lung tumorigenesis
Lung cancer is frequently driven by activating KRAS mutations such as KRASG12D. Knock-in of KRASG12D in the respiratory epithelium is sufficient to develop mouse lung adenocarcinomas ( – ). As HPIP plays a key role in promoting RAS-ERK signaling, we examined whether HPIP is important for KRASG12D-driven lung cancer development in mice. To this end, we crossed HPIPfl/fl mice with KRASG12D/+ mice and obtained KRASG12D/+;HPIP+/+, KRASG12D/+;HPIPfl/+, and KRASG12D/+;HPIPfl/fl mice. We delivered Cre recombinase–expressing adenovirus (Adeno-Cre) through nasal inhalation into these mice when they were 8 weeks old. Ten weeks after administration of Adeno-Cre, we evaluated the development of visible lung tumor lesions. All KRASG12D/+;HPIP+/+ mice (n = 10) demonstrated lung tumor development, 4 of the 11 KRASG12D/+;HPIPfl/+ mice developed lung tumors, and none of KRASG12D/+;HPIPfl/fl mice (n = 9) had visible lung tumors ( ). Twenty weeks after administration of Adeno-Cre, all KRASG12D/+;HPIP+/+ (n = 11) mice harbored lung tumors, and 7 of the 12 KRASG12D/+;HPIPfl/+ and 3 of the 8 KRASG12D/+;HPIPfl/fl mice harbored lung tumors. Hematoxylin staining of the whole lung tissue sections confirmed the existence of lung tumor lesions ( ). As compared with the KRASG12D/+;HPIP+/+ group, the KRASG12D/+;HPIPfl/+ and KRASG12D/+;HPIPfl/fl groups had small neoplastic regions in terms of tumor number and tumor burden ( ). As expected, lung tumor tissues of the KRASG12D/+;HPIP+/+ group had higher levels of RAS-GTP, ERK1/2 phosphorylation, and HIF1α than the KRASG12D/+;HPIPfl/+ and KRASG12D/+;HPIPfl/fl groups ( ). These data indicated that genetic ablation of HPIP in mice impairs KRAS signaling and inhibits KRASG12D/+-driven lung tumorigenesis.
 

## HPIP expression correlates with components of EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling and survival in patients with lung cancer
It is well acknowledged that activation of EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling predicts poor prognosis in patients with cancer. HPIP was reported to be overexpressed in lung cancer tissues although it remains unclear whether HPIP predicts clinical outcomes in patients with lung cancer ( ). In 40 lung cancer tissues that we analyzed, HPIP expression was positively correlated with GTP-bound RAS; phosphorylation of RAF1, MEK1/2, and ERK1/2; and HIF-1α expression ( ), suggesting that HPIP activates EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling in patients with lung cancer. Datasets from The Cancer Genome Atlas indicated that increased HPIP expression was associated with overall survival in patients with lung cancer and there was a trend that increased HPIP expression was associated with shorter disease-free survival in patients with lung cancer ( ). Because of the small sample size of patients with mutations in EGFR and/or KRAS, we could not perform stratification analysis.
 

## DISCUSSION
Molecular targeted therapy, which interferes with specific molecules, such as growth factor receptors, signal transducers, and enzymes, to inhibit tumor growth and metastasis, makes precise individualized therapy possible ( ). Molecular targeted therapy has been successful in the treatment of many types of cancers, including breast, lung, and colorectal cancers. Many small-molecule inhibitors or antibodies targeting components of signaling pathways that are dysregulated during carcinogenesis have been or are being developed. Compared to conventional chemotherapy that not only kills cancer cells but also destroys normal cells, the advantage of molecular targeted therapy is its capability to deliver drug with higher specificity and less toxicity. Although molecular targeted therapy has achieved great success, it also has limitations. Drug resistance and side effects are still unavoidable in molecular targeted therapy similar to chemotherapy ( ). Thus, it is important to identify other key molecules that may become ideal targets for cancer drug development.
EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling is one of the most important pathways implicated in tumorigenesis and progression and dysregulated in a myriad of cancers, including lung cancer and breast cancer. Overexpression or mutations of components of EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling have been identified in many cancer types. Thus, chemical inhibitors or antibodies targeting components of EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling, such as EGFR, GRB2, SHC, RAS, RAF, MEK, and ERK, have been widely investigated, and some drugs have been applied in clinic. Like other targeted therapies, EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling component–targeted therapies also have problems of drug resistance, poor efficacy, and side effects. By coinhibiting two or more targets in single or complementary pathways, treatment with combination of different drugs, such as molecular targeted therapy agents and chemotherapeutic drugs, may have synergistic anticancer effects as compared to single drug treatment. For instance, simultaneous inhibition of RAF and EGFR enhances antitumor effects in pancreatic cancer ( ). Patients with RAFV600E-mutant colorectal cancer have poor prognosis with conventional therapy versus patients with colorectal cancer harboring WT RAF ( ). Compared with conventional therapy or a RAF inhibitor alone, combination of RAF inhibitor with anti-EGFR antibody improves clinical outcomes for patients with RAFV600E-mutant colorectal cancer ( ). In this study, using different protein-protein interaction methods, we showed that HPIP physically interacts with all classical components of EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling, including EGFR, SHC, GRB2, SOS1, RAS, RAF, MEK, and ERK ( ). HPIP forms at least two complexes: one comprising HPIP, EGFR, SHC, GRB2, SOS1, RAS, RAF, and MEK; and another comprising HPIP, SHC, RAS, RAF, MEK, and ERK. To the best of our knowledge, HPIP is the first protein interacting with all classical components of EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling. HPIP overexpression enhances interaction between adjacent components of EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling, whereas HPIP KO or chemical inhibition of HPIP expression decreases interaction between components of EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling. HPIP is required for activation of EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling and EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling–mediated aerobic glycolysis and cancer cell proliferation and growth in vitro and in vivo, because HPIP KO or chemical inhibition of HPIP expression abolishes these effects. It is conceivable that cancer treatment via HPIP inhibition could inhibit all known therapeutic targets of EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling, probably producing good therapeutic effect. Thus, HPIP may become an ideal target for treatment of cancer with dysregulated EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling. On the other hand, intrinsic and acquired resistance to drugs targeting the EGFR-RAS-ERK pathway has been reported in many cancers. For instance, intrinsic and acquired resistance to MEK1/2 inhibitors exists in lung cancer, colorectal cancer, and melanoma ( , ). Reactivation of MEK1/2 following loss of negative-feedback loops may contribute to intrinsic resistance to MEK1/2 inhibitors. The acquired resistance to MEK1/2 inhibitors may be mediated by reactivation of ERK1/2 through selective BRAF amplification or BRAF splice variants. It would be interesting to investigate whether HPIP is involved in these processes.
RAS proteins, including KRAS, HRAS, and NRAS, are important regulators of cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and survival ( , ). Approximately 19% of patients with cancer harbor RAS mutations. Oncogenic mutant RAS proteins show more potent biological activity than WT RAS, and cancer cells harboring mutant RAS reveal more aggressive phenotypes. Accordingly, patients with cancer with mutant RAS have a poorer prognosis compared with those without RAS mutation. Thus, chemical drugs targeting RAS are being developed. Because of lack of drug-binding pockets on the surface of RAS proteins, direct targeting of RAS seemed impossible. However, Lumakras (sotorasib, AMG510) has been recently approved for the treatment of patients with non–small cell lung cancer harboring KRASG12C mutation ( ). Despite effective treatment of KRASG12C-mutant cancers, majority of RAS-mutant cancers are still difficult to be treated. We showed that HPIP interacts with KRAS, HRAS, and NRAS. Intriguingly, HPIP KO in cultured cancer cells not only reduced WT KRAS activity but also decreased the activities of most KRAS hotspot mutants tested by at least one order of magnitude. Consistent with these results, genetic ablation of HPIP in mice markedly inhibited KRAS signaling and decreased KRASG12D/+-driven lung cancer incidence rate. HPIP was reported to be overexpressed in more than 15 different types of cancers, including breast cancer, lung cancer, liver cancer, gastric cancer, and colorectal cancer ( ). Patients with high HPIP expression have shorter survival time than those with low HPIP expression. HPIP promotes cancer cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and metastasis. Combined with these previous reports, our data indicate that HPIP is a key promoter of tumorigenesis and also indicate a causal role for HPIP in cancer development, further suggesting that HPIP may become a very promising target for cancer therapy.
HPIP gene encodes a 731–amino acid protein with no substantial homology to known proteins. HPIP was shown to facilitate physical interaction with various signaling proteins, such as Src kinase, the p85 subunit of PI3K, and casein kinase 1α ( , ). Using KRAS, MEK1, and ERK1 proteins as models, we showed that different regions or overlapping regions of HPIP directly interact with these proteins. Two or three consecutive regions of HPIP associating with MEK1 and KRAS, respectively, may allow an efficient recruitment of HPIP to the EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling complex, thus facilitating signaling activation. It requires to be investigated which regions of HPIP directly interact with other components of EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling besides KRAS, MEK1, and ERK1. Targeting interaction between HPIP and components of EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling, especially overlapping regions of HPIP involved in interaction with components of EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling, may also hold promise in the treatment of cancer with dysregulated EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling.
It has been reported that HPIP interacts with the p85 subunit of PI3K and the Src kinase to activate both AKT and ERK ( , ). In this study, we showed that HPIP KO abolished the ability of EGF to activate AKT. EGF can stimulate EGFR, leading to RAS activation ( ). Activated RAS interacts with the p110α subunit of PI3K to stimulate AKT ( ). Because HPIP modulates SOS1-KRAS interaction and subsequent KRAS activation, it is possible that HPIP regulates AKT activation through activated KRAS in response to EGF except for the previous mechanism. Whether HPIP activates AKT through KRAS remains to be investigated. On the other hand, HPIP acts as a scaffolding protein running through the EGFR-RAS-ERK pathway. Whether HPIP acts as an adaptor protein of the PI3K-AKT pathway needs to be examined.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Plasmids, lentiviral vectors, and reagents
The eukaryotic expression plasmids encoding FLAG-, MYC-, or hemaglutinin (HA)-tagged proteins or untagged proteins were made by cloning polymerase chain reaction (PCR)–amplified fragments into pcDNA3 (Invitrogen) with or without respective tag. Prokaryotic vectors encoding GST- or His-fusion proteins were generated in pGEX-KG (Amersham Pharmacia Biotech) and pET28a (Novagen), respectively. Short hairpin RNA (shRNA)–resistant HPIP expression vector was constructed by recombinant PCR. The cDNA target sequence of HPIP shRNA is AGCAGCTTGGATCAGGGAG. Lentiviral shRNA vectors were generated by inserting corresponding fragments into pSIH-H1-Puro (System Biosciences). Lentiviral vectors for gene overexpression were generated by cloning PCR-amplified gene fragments into pCDH (System Biosciences). The cDNA target sequences of siRNAs for EGFR, HPIP, SHC, GRB2, SOS1, RAS, KRAS, RAF1, MEK1/2, and ERK1/2 were listed in table S2.
Anti-EGFR (51071-2-AP; RRID: AB_2096449), anti-GRB2 (66880-1-Ig; RRID: AB_2882212), anti-GRB2 (10254-2-AP; RRID: AB_2263577), anti-SOS1 (55041-1-AP; RRID: AB_10804659), anti-SHC (12496-1-AP; RRID: AB_2187889), anti-RAF1 (26863-1-AP; RRID: AB_2880660), anti-KRAS (12063-1-AP; RRID: AB_878040), anti-MEK1/2 (11049-1-AP; RRID: AB_2140649), anti–HIF-1α (20960-1-AP; RRID: AB_10732601), and anti-HPIP/PBXIP1 (12102-1-AP; RRID: AB_2160484) were purchased from Proteintech. Anti-FLAG (A8592; RRID: AB_439702), anti-Flag M2 agarose (A2220; RRID: AB_10063035), anti–c-Myc gel (E6654; RRID: AB_10093201), and anti-c-Myc-peroxidase (A5598; RRID: AB_439682) were from Sigma-Aldrich. Anti-HPIP/PBXIP1 (H00057326-B01P; RRID: AB_1578462) was from Abnova.

## Cell lines, transfection, and infection
Human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells (CRL-3216), human breast cancer ZR75-1 cells (CRL-1500), and human lung cancer A549 and Calu-3 cells (CCL-185 and HTB-55) were purchased from American Type Culture Collection and have previously been tested for mycoplasma contamination. Cells were routinely cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 25 mM glucose (Invitrogen) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (HyClone) at 37°C. Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen) and Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen) were used for transfections of plasmids and siRNAs, respectively. For transfection of plasmids only, cells were collected 24 hours after transfection for further study. For siRNA transfection, cells were harvested at 48 hours after transfection for further study. Lentiviruses were produced by cotransfecting HEK293T cells with recombinant lentiviral vectors and pPACK Packaging Plasmid Mix (System Biosciences) using Megatran reagent (Origene) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Viral supernatants were harvested 48 hours after transfection, and the titer was examined. Target cells were then infected with lentiviral constructs with polybrene (8 μg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich). For establishment of stable cell lines, A549, Calu-3, and ZR75-1 cells were selected in puromycin of 2, 2, and 1 μg/ml, respectively; and A549, Calu-3, and ZR75-1 cells were selected in G418 of 600, 600, and 800 μg/ml, respectively.
HPIP KO cancer cells were made by clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)–CRISPR-associated protein 9 system. CRISPRs were designed using a CRISPR design web tool (http://www.e-crisp.org/E-CRISP/). The single-guide RNA (sgRNA) sequence targeted by HPIP is AGACCTGCAGGAGACCACCG. The sgRNA was cloned into the lentiCRISPRv2 vector (Addgene, no. 52961). The recombinant lentiviruses were produced by cotransfecting HEK293T cells with the lentiviral vector using Megatran reagent (Origene). The KO stable cell lines were confirmed by PCR amplification of genomic sequences, DNA sequencing, and immunoblot.

## Co-immunoprecipitation
Cells were washed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and lysed in lysis buffer (50 mM tris at pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP-40, 1 mM dithiothreitol, and protease inhibitors). Protein extracts were immunoprecipitated with anti-Flag or anti-Myc agarose beads (Sigma-Aldrich) or indicated antibodies for 4 hours at 4°C according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After washing beads three times with the lysis buffer, the immunoprecipitates were eluted in SDS sample buffer, resolved by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE), and analyzed by immunoblot with indicated antibodies.

## GST pull-down assay
GST- or His-fusion proteins were expressed and purified according to the manufacturers’ protocols (Amersham Pharmacia and Qiagen). Expression of His- and GST-fusion proteins in Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) was induced by 0.1 mM isopropyl β-d-thiogalactoside for 20 hours at 20°C. His- and GST-fusion proteins were purified with nickel beads (Qiagen) and glutathione Sepharose 4B beads (Amersham Pharmacia), respectively. The expression vector for EGFR, SHC, GRB2, SOS1, KRAS, RAF1, MEK1, MEK2, ERK1, ERK2, c-Myc, or c-Fos was used for in vitro translation using the TNT Quick Coupled Transcription/Translation System (Promega). GST-fusion proteins bound to glutathione Sepharose 4B beads were incubated with purified His-fusion proteins or in vitro translated products for 4 hours under rotation at 4°C in 500 μl of binding buffer. The beads were precipitated, washed four times with binding buffer, eluted by boiling in SDS sample buffer, and analyzed by immunoblot analysis.

## Immunoblot
Standard immunoblot was performed. If there is a big difference in molecular weights (> 40 kDa) between two proteins, then we cut the same membrane into two pieces for immunoblot with respective antibodies. Otherwise, the same volume of samples was used for examination of different proteins, including the loading control β-actin. Immunoblot was performed independently at least three times.

## Mass spectrometry
FLAG-tagged HPIP complex was obtained by immunoprecipitation with anti-Flag from 108 ZR75-1 cells stably expressing FLAG-HPIP according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Sigma-Aldrich). Cells were lysed in IP buffer (20 mM tris at pH 8.0, 0.25 M NaCl, 0.5% NP-40, and 5 mM EDTA) and centrifuged to get supernatants. The supernatants were immunoprecipitated with anti-Flag agarose beads for 4 hours at 4°C. The beads were washed four times with IP buffer, and the FLAG-tagged HPIP complex was eluted with Flag peptide. The protein complex was separated by SDS-PAGE, followed by Coomassie brilliant blue staining. Differential bands were excised and subjected to mass spectrometry analysis. In-solution and in-gel digestion were performed as previously described ( ). Briefly, gel bands were minced and destained, and proteins were reduced and alkylated. Trypsin digestion was performed, and digested peptides were isolated, vacuum-dried, and reconstituted. The treated samples were analyzed by nanoLC-MS/MS (nanoACQUITY UPLC and SYNAPT G2 HD mass spectrometer, Waters). Tandem mass spectrometry data were obtained with data-dependent analysis mode and analyzed using PLGS 2.4 software (Waters), and the resulting peak list was searched against the National Center for Biotechnology Information database with the MASCOT search engine.

## Immunofluorescence analysis
A549 cells were seeded in six-well dishes with glass coverslips containing regular DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Cells on coverslips were transfected, washed with PBS, and stained with anti-HPIP and antibodies against the indicated component of the EGFR-RAS-ERK pathway for 1 hour. The nuclei were stained with 4′,6′-diamidino-2-phenylindole. Images were captured by a Radiance 2100 confocal microscope (Bio-Rad). The colocalization of the two signals is indicated by the presence of yellow color resulting from red/green merging. Colocalization analysis was performed using ImageJ software, and colocalization ratio = the pixel area of colocalization puncta/the pixel area of the indicated protein puncta.

## Fast protein liquid chromatography
FPLC was performed as described previously ( ). Briefly, A549 cells (2 × 108) were homogenized and dialyzed against buffer D [20 mM Hepes (pH 8), 10% glycerol, 0.1 mM EDTA, and 300 mM NaCl]. Approximately 1 ml of volume of cell lysate was separated by a FPLC system with a Superose 6 10/300GL column (GE Healthcare Life Sciences).

## Glucose uptake, lactate, and ATP assays
The Glucose Uptake Colorimetric Assay Kit, Lactate Assay Kit II, and ATP Colorimetric Assay Kit were used to assess glucose uptake and levels of lactate and ATP according to the manufacturer’s protocols (BioVision). Data were normalized to cell number unless specified.
For glucose uptake assay, 10,000 cells were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated in DMEM containing 10% FBS for 10 hours, at which time the cell number for each group was very similar. Cells were washed three times with PBS and then starved for glucose by incubating with 100 μl of Krebs-Ringer-Phosphate-Hepes buffer containing 2% bovine serum albumin for 40 min. Ten microliters of 10 mM 2-deoxy-d-glucose (2-DG) was added to the medium for 20 min. Cells were lysed with extraction buffer, frozen/thawed, heated, and neutralized with neutralization buffer. After centrifugation at 1.2 × 104 rpm for 5 min, the supernatant was used for measurement of glucose uptake at 412 nm in a microplate reader.
For lactate level assay, 100,000 cells were seeded into 12-well plates and incubated in DMEM containing 10% FBS for 10 hours. To examine lactate secretion, the media were replaced with DMEM without FBS. After incubation for 1 hour, the supernatant was harvested for assessment of lactate production at 450 nm in a microplate reader.
For ATP level assay, 5 × 105 cells were collected and extracted with the assay buffer. The cells were centrifuged, and the supernatant was examined at 570 nm in a microplate reader.

## Extracellular acidification and OCR assays
The ECAR and cellular OCR were assessed using the Seahorse XFe 96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The Seahorse XF Glycolysis Stress Test Kit and Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (Agilent Technologies, USA) were used to measure ECAR and OCR levels, respectively. Briefly, 10,000 cells per well were seeded into Seahorse XFe 96 cell culture microplates for 10 hours, at which the time cell number for each group was very similar. For ECAR, glucose, the oxidative phosphorylation inhibitor oligomycin, and the glycolytic inhibitor 2-DG were sequentially injected into each well at indicated time points. For OCR, oligomycin, the reversible inhibitor of oxidative phosphorylation carbonyl cyanide p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (FCCP), and the mitochondrial complex I inhibitor rotenone and the mitochondrial complex III inhibitor antimycin A (Rote/AA) were sequentially injected. Data were analyzed by Seahorse XFe 96 Wave software and normalized to cell numbers.

## Cell proliferation
Three thousand cells were seeded per well in 96-well plates in regular cell growth medium. Cell proliferation was analyzed by the CCK-8 Kit (Dojindo Laboratories) per the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 10 μl of CCK-8 solution was added to cultured cells in each well and incubated at 37°C for 1 hour. The absorbance at 450 nm was assessed using a microplate reader.

## RAS activity assay
RAS-GTP levels were determined using the Active Ras Pull-Down and Detection Kit (Thermo Scientific). Briefly, 80 μg of GST-RAF1 RBD (RAS-binding domain) or GST was added to the spin cup containing the glutathione resin. Five hundred microliters of cell lysates containing 500 μg of total proteins or tissue homogenates containing 1 mg of total proteins were incubated with GST-RAF1 RBD or GST bound to the glutathione resin at 4°C for 1 hour with gentle rocking. The resin was precipitated, washed, and eluted by boiling in SDS sample buffer, followed by immunoblot analysis.

## Animal experiments
Animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care Committee of Beijing Institute of Biotechnology. Mice were randomly allocated to different groups. HPIP-floxed KO C57BL/6 mice that have loxP site (HPIPfl/+) were generated by Cyagen Biosciences Inc. and have been described previously ( ). Male and female HPIPfl/+ mice were then mated to obtain HPIP homozygote mice (HPIPfl/fl). Loxp-stop-loxp-KRASG12D (KRASG12D/+) C57BL/6 mice have been described previously ( ). HPIPfl/fl mice were crossed with KRASG12D/+ mice, and KRASG12D/+;HPIP+/+, KRASG12D/+;HPIP+/−, and KRASG12D/+;HPIP−/− mice were obtained. To induce lung tumors, 8-week-old mice were treated with adenoviral virus encoding Cre-recombinase (Ad-Cre) through nasal inhalation. Ten million plaque-forming units of Ad-Cre were administered per mouse. Ten or 20 weeks after virus administration, mice were euthanized for lung dissection.
For nude mice experiments, 1 × 107 ZR75-1 cells carrying different constructs were subcutaneously inoculated into the second mammary fat pad on the right side of 6-week-old female BALB/c nude mice, and 1 × 107 A549 cells carrying different constructs were subcutaneously inoculated in the right flank of 6-week-old nude mice. For treatment with TXX-1-10, mice were intraperitoneally injected with TXX-1-10 (30 mg/kg) with an equivalent volume of saline injected in control mice. The length and width of observable tumor were measured at indicated times using calipers. Tumor volume was calculated by the formula: volume = (longest diameter × shortest diameter2)/2. Mice were euthanized at the indicated time. Excised tumors were frozen in liquid nitrogen for further study.

## Human lung cancer samples
Forty primary lung cancer tissues were collected from Chinese People's Liberation Army (PLA) General Hospital, with the informed consent of patients and with the approval of the Institutional Review Committees of Chinese PLA General Hospital. Similar experiments performed previously were used to estimate sample size. None of patients with lung cancer had received any chemotherapy or radiation therapy prior to surgery. The age range of 40 patients (32 males and 8 females) was 38 to 78 (mean age, 61.8 years).

## Statistical analysis
Trial experiments or similar experiments done previously were used to assess sample size with adequate statistical power. Disease-free survival and overall survival curves were generated by the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between survival curves were determined using the log-rank test. Data were analyzed with two-tailed student’s t test for two comparisons or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test for multiple comparisons. The correlation of HPIP expression with components of EGFR-RAS-ERK signaling was determined using Pearson’s correlation test. All statistical tests were two-sided. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
