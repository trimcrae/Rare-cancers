## Abstract
Microgravity can exacerbate radiation-induced DNA damage response, suggesting that microgravity may increase the risk of tumor initiation and development. However, the specific mechanism is still unclear. This study used X-rays, protons, and carbon ions to simulate space radiation, and three-dimensional clinostats or hind limb unloading to simulate microgravity. It was found that simulated space radiation and/or microgravity promoted malignant transformation and tumor development of lung epithelial cells BEAS-2B, and the two factors showed a synergistic effect. The mechanism involves simulated space radiation and/or microgravity leads to changes in intracellular calcium ion concentration, affecting cellular signaling pathways, inducing the interaction between CAMK2G and ARRB1, and promoting ARRB1 nuclear translocation. ARRB1 nuclear translocation enhances CA9 transcriptional activity following simulated space radiation and/or microgravity exposure. In short, changes in intracellular calcium concentration play a crucial role in ARRB1 nuclear translocation and subsequent malignant transformation.

## Introduction
Spaceflight exposes astronauts to unique environmental stressors, notably space radiation and microgravity, which can lead to cancer risk. Unlike terrestrial radiation, space radiation includes galactic cosmic rays (GCR), solar particle events (SPE), and the Earth’s radiation belts . GCRs, composed of high-energy protons and heavy ions (e.g., iron, oxygen), exhibit high linear energy transfer (LET), inducing complex DNA damage and persistent oxidative stress that promote genomic instability and tumorigenesis – . SPEs, while lower in LET, deliver high-energy protons capable of overwhelming cellular repair mechanisms, increasing mutation rates and carcinogenic potential , . Despite partial shielding in low Earth orbit, cumulative radiation exposure remains a concern for long-duration missions .
Microgravity further compounds cancer risk by altering cell behavior, including gene expression, cytoskeletal dynamics, and intercellular signaling , . It disrupts apoptosis, DNA repair, and cell cycle regulation, contributing to abnormal proliferation and resistance to cell death – . These mechanical changes impair immune surveillance and enhance the metastatic potential of transformed cells – . Microgravity also reshapes the tumor microenvironment, exacerbating malignancy progression , .
ARRB1 is a multifunctional scaffold protein involved in agonist-mediated desensitization of G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), linking receptors to clathrin-mediated endocytosis and directing G protein signaling. It plays a crucial role in regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, autophagy, and apoptosis, integrating oncogenic signaling pathways such as MAPK/ERK and PI3K/AKT and modulating gene expression through nuclear translocation – . Previous studies have linked ARRB1 to cancer progression, yet its role under spaceflight conditions remains unclear.
In this study, lung epithelial cells BEAS-2B were exposed to selected components of the space radiation environment (X-rays, protons, and carbon ions) and simulated microgravity environments. Results showed that simulated spaceflight conditions promoted epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), malignant transformation, and tumor formation, with carbon ions exerting the most significant effects. ARRB1 nuclear translocation emerged as a critical event in this process, and its knockout suppressed transformation. Mechanistic analyses revealed that simulated space radiation and microgravity disrupted calcium homeostasis, activating CAMK2G and inducing ARRB1 Ser412 phosphorylation, facilitating nuclear translocation. Additionally, CA9 upregulation contributed to malignancy, and its inhibition mitigated transformation. These findings highlight novel mechanisms by which spaceflight conditions drive tumorigenesis and provide insights for astronaut health risk assessment and countermeasure development.

## Results

## Simulated space radiation combined with simulated microgravity promotes cell malignant transformation and tumorigenesis
To assess the combined effects of simulated space radiation and microgravity on malignant transformation, human lung epithelial BEAS-2B cells were exposed to X-rays, protons, or carbon ions, with or without simulated microgravity. Proliferation, migration, invasion, and anchorage-independent growth were significantly increased in all treated groups, with the combined treatments exhibiting a stronger effect than single treatments (Fig. ). These findings indicate that simulated space radiation and microgravity promote cell malignant transformation in a synergistic manner.
 To further evaluate the impact of simulated space radiation and/or microgravity on tumorigenesis, subcutaneous tumor formation assays using BEAS-2B cells stably passaged for 50 generations revealed increased tumor incidence, volume, and weight in the combined treatment groups compared to radiation or microgravity alone (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). IHC staining showed elevated Ki67 (a marker of malignant cell proliferation), CK7 (a marker for lung adenocarcinoma), and Vim (EMT-related marker), and decreased E-cad (EMT-related marker) expression, with the carbon ion and microgravity group showing the strongest effect, supporting enhanced malignancy (Fig. ). These results collectively demonstrate that simulated space radiation and microgravity promote cell malignant transformation and tumorigenesis in a synergistic manner.

## Simulated space radiation combined with microgravity promotes EMT progression
EMT plays a crucial role as a molecular switch in tumor progression. During this process, the loss of epithelial cell polarity and intercellular adhesion, mediated by the downregulation of adhesion molecules such as E-cad, enables cells to acquire mesenchymal-like migratory properties. When EMT-related signaling pathways are activated, N-cad and FN1 are upregulated, facilitating the breakdown of basement membrane constraints.
To examine EMT progression, key EMT markers were analyzed. The study found upregulation of N-cad, FN1, and Vim, along with downregulation of E-cad in BEAS-2B cells after proton irradiation and/or simulated microgravity (Supplementary Fig. ). Mouse lung tissues subjected to similar treatments confirmed these findings (Supplementary Fig. ). qPCR results supported protein data, showing synergistic effects on mRNA expression (Supplementary Fig. ). Masson’s trichrome staining indicated increased collagen deposition in combined groups (Supplementary Fig. ). These results suggest that simulated space radiation combined with microgravity promotes EMT progression in a synergistic manner.

## Simulated space radiation and/or microgravity promote ARRB1 nuclear translocation
ARRB1 is predominantly localized in the cytoplasm under normal conditions, where it participates in GPCR signaling. Previous studies suggest that ARRB1 overexpression, functional dysregulation, or nuclear translocation may contribute to malignant transformation and are closely associated with various cancers. Thus, we hypothesize that ARRB1 may play a role in simulated space radiation- and/or microgravity-induced malignant transformation.
Immunofluorescence and subcellular fractionation showed that simulated space radiation and/or microgravity promoted ARRB1 translocation to the nucleus, with combined treatments exerting the strongest effect (Fig. ). Total ARRB1 protein and mRNA levels remained unchanged (Fig. ). It was speculated that, due to the high content of ARRB1 in the cytoplasm and the low amount of nuclear translocation protein, the impact on total protein changes is not significant. Animal lung tissues using IHC confirmed ARRB1 nuclear localization under combined treatments (Fig. ), suggesting its role in transformation.
 

## ARRB1 nuclear translocation promotes simulated space radiation- and/or microgravity-induced malignant transformation and tumorigenesis
To further explore the impact of ARRB1 localization on malignant transformation, stable BEAS-2B cell lines with nuclear-overexpressing ARRB1 (NLS), NLS-mutated ARRB1 (Q394L), and knockout (KO) were established and confirmed by immunofluorescence and qPCR (Supplementary Fig. ). Functional assays revealed that ARRB1 nuclear localization enhanced proliferation, migration, and colony formation (Fig. ).
Stable nuclear-overexpressing BEAS-2B cells and their controls were subjected to carbon ion irradiation and/or simulated microgravity treatment and serially passaged for 50 generations. Compared to controls, ARRB1 nuclear translocation enhanced proliferation, migration, invasion, and colony formation (Supplementary Fig. ). Subcutaneous tumor formation assays further demonstrated that ARRB1 nuclear translocation promoted tumor incidence and increased tumor volume and weight (Supplementary Fig. ). These findings indicate that ARRB1 nuclear translocation facilitates simulated space radiation- and microgravity-induced tumorigenesis.

## ARRB1 knockout inhibits simulated space radiation- and/or microgravity-induced malignant transformation and tumorigenesis
Further studies were conducted on cells with ARRB1 knockout and their negative control groups. After 50 stable passages, ARRB1 knockout cells showed reduced proliferation, migration, invasion, and transformation after carbon ion and/or simulated microgravity exposure compared to controls (Fig. ). These results indicate that ARRB1 knockout inhibits simulated space radiation- and/or microgravity-induced malignant transformation.
 Subcutaneous tumorigenesis assays showed that ARRB1 knockout significantly reduced tumor formation compared to controls, with lower tumor volume and weight (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ), indicating ARRB1’s role in promoting tumorigenesis under spaceflight-like conditions.
To validate these findings in vivo, lung-specific ARRB1 knockout mice (Arrb1flox/flox; Sftpc-CreERT2+/-) and control mice (Arrb1flox/flox; Sftpc-CreERT2-/-) were exposed to X-rays or carbon ions with or without tail-suspension simulated microgravity, along with urethane to induce lung cancer. Histological analysis revealed that simulated space radiation and/or microgravity enhanced lung tumor formation, while ARRB1 knockout suppressed this effect. Notably, carbon ion exposure induced more tumors than X-rays (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ).

## Simulated space radiation and microgravity promote intracellular Ca²⁺ Concentration Increase, inducing ARRB1 nuclear translocation
Ca²⁺ concentration plays a critical role in intracellular signal transduction. To explore the effects of radiation and/or simulated microgravity on cellular Ca²⁺ signaling, Fluo-4 assays revealed that simulated space radiation and/or microgravity increased intracellular Ca²⁺, with sustained elevation under combined conditions (Fig. and Supplementary Fig. ). These results indicate that simulated space radiation and/or microgravity can influence intracellular Ca²⁺ concentrations, possibly affecting intracellular signaling and influencing ARRB1 nuclear translocation.
 Next, we examined whether changes in Ca²⁺ concentration would affect ARRB1 nuclear translocation. By adding different concentrations of CaCl2 to the base medium to simulate increased intracellular Ca²⁺ concentration, and performing IF and protein nuclear-cytoplasm separation experiments after a 2-hour treatment, we observed ARRB1’s behavior. Exogenous CaCl₂ treatment induced dose- and time-dependent ARRB1 nuclear translocation (Fig. ), while Ca²⁺ chelation via BAPTA-AM suppressed this process after combined simulated space radiation and microgravity exposure (Fig. ). These results identify Ca²⁺ signaling as a key mediator of ARRB1 nuclear translocation and its downstream oncogenic effects.

## Ca²⁺ concentration changes affect ARRB1 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of phosphorylated ARRB1, promoting interaction with CAMK2G
Previous studies suggest that ARRB1 phosphorylation modulates its role in signaling and protein interactions, but its impact on nuclear translocation remains unclear – . Given that Ser412 phosphorylation affects ARRB1 binding and signal transduction , , the study investigated whether this modification influences ARRB1 nuclear localization.
Immunofluorescence showed a dose-dependent increase in p-ARRB1 (Ser412) nuclear translocation with increasing CaCl₂ concentrations (0, 1, 3, 5 mM) (Fig. ). Time-course experiments with 5 mM CaCl₂ indicated a progressive increase in nuclear p-ARRB1 up to 48 h (Fig. ). Additionally, proton irradiation and/or simulated microgravity significantly enhanced p-ARRB1 (Ser412) expression and nuclear translocation, confirmed by both immunofluorescence and nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation (Fig. ).
 Research has shown that pThr287 is a key step in CAMKII activation, enabling it to acquire Ca²⁺/CaM-independent activity, which enhances its ability to regulate other proteins – . To assess calcium signaling involvement, the study examined expression levels of CaM, CAMKII, and pThr287-CAMKII. While CaM levels remained unchanged, CAMKII and its phosphorylation at Thr287 increased following irradiation and simulated microgravity treatment (Fig. ). Among CAMKII isoforms in lung tissue, CAMK2G, but not CAMK2D, was upregulated (Fig. ). Co-localization and nuclear translocation of CAMK2G and ARRB1 were observed post-treatment (Fig. ).
We hypothesized that CAMK2G activation following simulated space radiation and/or microgravity promotes ARRB1 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation. Structural models of CAMK2G (PDB ID: 2UX0) and ARRB1 (PDB ID: 8AS4) were analyzed via molecular docking using pyDockWEB. The top ten models, selected based on binding energy (Supplementary Table ), showed stable interactions, with multiple potential binding sites across ARRB1 domains (Fig. ). Co-immunoprecipitation in BEAS-2B cells co-transfected with HA-CAMK2G and Flag-ARRB1 confirmed enhanced CAMK2G-ARRB1 interaction after simulated space radiation and microgravity exposure (Fig. ).

## ARRB1 promotes the expression of carbonic anhydrase (CA9), affecting cellular malignant transformation
Based on the aforementioned background and the findings from previous sections, it is hypothesized that simulated space radiation and/or microgravity treatment cause ARRB1 to translocate into the nucleus, where it functions as a transcription factor to regulate gene expression and promote cellular malignant transformation. Transcriptomic analysis of BEAS-2B NLS cell lines identified 286 differentially expressed mRNAs, affecting pathways involved in cytoskeletal regulation, DNA repair, immune function, and metabolism (Fig. ). Cross-referencing with lung cancer datasets revealed 20 candidate genes (Supplementary Table ), with CA9, neuronal differentiation and growth factor 1 (NDEG1), and retinoic acid receptor gamma (RARG) associated with poor prognosis (Fig. ). qPCR confirmed CA9 expression was significantly upregulated in NLS and Q394L cells and downregulated in ARRB1 KO cells, suggesting direct transcriptional regulation by ARRB1 (Fig. ).
 In BEAS-2B cells, CA9 mRNA and protein expression increased following proton irradiation and/or simulated microgravity, with a synergistic effect observed when combined (Fig. ). In vivo, lung tissues from treated mice also showed elevated CA9 expression, especially under combined conditions (Fig. , ). Promoter analysis identified three potential ARRB1 binding sites using hTFtarget database within the CA9 promoter (Supplementary Tables and ). Luciferase assays confirmed that nuclear ARRB1 enhances CA9 transcription, while ARRB1 knockout suppresses it (Fig. ). ChIP-qPCR validated ARRB1 binding to these sites, enhanced under proton radiation and simulated microgravity treatment (Fig. ), suggesting that the combined effects of simulated space radiation and microgravity may further exacerbate this transcriptional regulatory mechanism.

## Inhibition of CA9 reduces malignant transformation induced by simulated space radiation and microgravity
Previous studies have shown that high expression of CA9 can promote cellular malignant transformation, and inhibition of CA9 can effectively reduce malignant transformation in cells – . This study further explores whether inhibiting CA9 can reduce tumor formation induced by simulated space radiation and microgravity, thereby confirming the role of CA9 in the tumorigenesis process associated with simulated space radiation and microgravity. To assess CA9’s role in malignancy, BEAS-2B cells were transfected with siRNAs targeting CA9. siCA9-3 showed the most sustained knockdown (Fig. ). 50th-passage cells treated with carbon ions and simulated microgravity and then treated with siCA9-3 or CA9 inhibitor U104 exhibited significantly reduced tumor volume and weight upon subcutaneous inoculation (Fig. ). IHC revealed reduced proliferation and invasiveness in tumor tissues (Fig. ), confirming that CA9 contributes to malignant transformation under simulated space radiation and microgravity.
 

## Discussion
The interaction between space radiation and microgravity is complex. A study found that combining simulated microgravity with ionizing radiation increased cell death, DNA damage, and ROS production in human bronchial epithelial cells, showing an additive effect . However, other research suggests antagonistic effects on the nervous system, such as γ-ray irradiation normalizing 5-HT metabolite levels in the striatum and reducing 5-HT metabolism in the prefrontal cortex and amygdala , . γ-ray irradiation also counteracts stress responses from simulated microgravity, impairing immune function. While early studies suggested a synergistic effect in lower organisms, these findings were inconsistent . Numerous studies have indicated that both space radiation and microgravity can induce DNA damage and affect DNA repair mechanisms, potentially leading to carcinogenic effects. Both microgravity and space radiation induce DNA damage, raising the risk of carcinogenesis . When space radiation and simulated microgravity are combined, neuronal network integrity and cell viability exhibit synergistic changes , and mutation rates increases in human lymphocytes . Experimental data from NASA’s Twin Study showed significant changes in gene expression related to DNA damage and immune function . We hypothesize that such persistent alterations could potentially contribute to the established increase in cancer susceptibility observed, underscoring the need for further mechanistic investigation.
ARRB1 regulates tumor progression through cell proliferation, invasion, migration, and metabolic reprogramming. In this study, simulated space radiation and/or microgravity did not affect ARRB1 levels but increased its nuclear translocation. In the nucleus, ARRB1 interacts with transcription factors, enhancing oncogene expression (c-Fos, c-Jun) and promoting AP-1-dependent gene transcription, cell proliferation, and apoptosis resistance , . ARRB1’s nuclear translocation also affects the tumor microenvironment by stabilizing HIF-1α, increasing glucose uptake, and enhancing glycolysis, giving tumor cells a growth advantage under hypoxic conditions . Using stable cell lines overexpressing nuclear-localized ARRB1 and ARRB1 knockout models, this study showed that simulated space radiation and/or microgravity promoted malignant transformation and tumorigenesis via ARRB1 nuclear translocation, while ARRB1 knockout inhibited these effects.
Ca²⁺ is crucial for various cellular processes, with its gradient enabling signaling in movement, gene transcription, and muscle contraction . Mechanosensitive ion channels convert mechanical forces into Ca²⁺ signals, regulated by membrane or ER proteins, influencing physiological and pathological processes – . CaM activates CaMKII by binding Ca²⁺, triggering autophosphorylation that enhances kinase activity and regulates cellular functions , , . Phosphorylated CaMKII can also associate with membrane-associated protein complexes, influencing localized signal transduction events . In cardiomyocytes exposed to simulated microgravity, increased CaMKII and histone deacetylase 4 phosphorylation indicated responses to altered mechanical conditions . Similarly, in this study, lung epithelial cells were exposed to simulated space radiation and/or microgravity, and changes in calcium homeostasis were observed. While CaM levels remained stable, CAMKII and CAMK2G levels increased, promoting ARRB1 phosphorylation and its nuclear translocation.
CA9 is a transmembrane carbonic anhydrase that regulates pH balance and adapts to hypoxia, playing a key role in the tumor microenvironment – . It is normally expressed at low levels in healthy tissues but increases significantly under hypoxic conditions, especially in tumors , . High CA9 expression is linked to tumor progression, invasion, and drug resistance in various cancers, including lung, breast, colorectal, and renal cell carcinomas, with poor prognosis in NSCLC , , – . Given its role in tumorigenesis, CA9 inhibitors like the small-molecule SLC-0111 (U104) and the monoclonal antibody Girentuximab have shown promise in preclinical and clinical studies, respectively, for treating solid tumors – . This study found that ARRB1 regulates CA9 expression through transcriptional initiation. Under simulated space radiation and microgravity, ARRB1 nuclear translocation was upregulated, increasing CA9 expression, which may enhance cell survival and proliferation under stress.
This study aimed to explore the role of ARRB1 in the cellular response to simulated space radiation and microgravity; however, several limitations must be considered when interpreting the results. First, the use of nude mice for subcutaneous xenograft tumor experiments introduces certain limitations. While nude mice are commonly used in cancer research, they lack a functional immune system, which can alter the tumor microenvironment and the interactions with the immune response. As a result, the tumorigenesis process in nude mice may not fully replicate that in immunocompetent hosts. Additionally, the subcutaneous tumor model does not accurately mimic the natural tumor microenvironment, which is critical for studying tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis, particularly in response to space radiation and microgravity. These factors need to be considered when applying the findings to human cancer biology and space radiation risk assessment.
Another limitation lies in the use of a limited radiation exposure model based on X-rays, protons, and carbon ions. While these exposures are useful for investigating specific aspects of space radiation, they do not fully replicate the complexity of cosmic rays, including their charge and kinetic energy distribution. Moreover, the dose rate used does not accurately reflect the radiation environment encountered during space missions. Specifically, the use of a single, high dose of 0.5 Gy for carbon ions exceeds the career exposure limits for astronauts and corresponds to an effective dose well beyond the recommended radiation protection thresholds. While this dose was chosen to ensure a statistically significant effect for studying ARRB1-mediated mechanisms in vitro, it should not be directly extrapolated to human cancer risk assessments for space missions. Additionally, the high dose used in this study prevents an exploration of non-targeted effects (NTEs), which are crucial for understanding space radiation risks. Future studies employing a dose-response design with lower doses, simulating fluences of less than one particle per cell, are needed to more accurately model the effects of space radiation on cellular transformation and NTEs.
Furthermore, the use of the BEAS-2B immortalized human bronchial epithelial cell line, while valuable for studying early stages of cellular transformation, introduces its own limitations. BEAS-2B cells lack the complexity of the native lung tissue microenvironment, particularly the interactions with immune and stromal cells. As a result, caution is needed when extrapolating these in vitro findings to human lung carcinogenesis in vivo. Previous study demonstrated dose-dependent EMT changes in BEAS-2B cells exposed to γ-rays, with significant alterations observed at 1 Gy (14 mGy/h) but not at lower doses (0.1 Gy, 1.4 mGy/h) . Research on heavy ions has mainly focused on other cell types. For instance, carbon ion irradiation at 0.25–0.5 Gy was shown to suppress E-cadherin expression in B16F10 cells, suggesting EMT promotion even at relatively low doses . Similarly, a relevant study demonstrated that heavy ions (silicon/iron) can induce TGFβ-dependent EMT in epithelial cells (EPC2-hTERT and Mv1Lu) even at low doses (0.1 Gy) . These evidences collectively indicates that the induction of EMT and subsequent transformation is influenced by multiple factors, including radiation quality, dose parameters, and specific cellular context. Our work, therefore, contributes new insights. To establish a more comprehensive understanding, future studies should systematically investigate those variables.

## Methods

## Cell culture
Normal human bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS-2B; Baidi Biotech Ltd) were cultured in DMEM (VivaCell, China) with 10% serum (ExCell, China), penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 μg/mL) at 37 °C in 5% CO₂.

## Simulated irradiation treatment
Cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 10⁵ cells in T25 culture flasks. X-ray irradiation (X-RAD SmART, 225 kV, 0.1 Gy/min), proton irradiation (CYCIAE-100 cyclotron, 100 MeV/u, 0.08 Gy/min) and carbon ion irradiation (156 MeV/u, 0.8 Gy/min, LET: 50 keV/μm) delivered 0.5 Gy. Mice received whole-body single-dose irradiation.

## Simulated microgravity
Cells were exposed to simulated microgravity using a 3D clinostat (SM-31, a random rotating speed of 0-10 rpm for ~0.001 g) for 48 h or 5 days. Mice were subjected to hindlimb unloading at a 30° tilt for 21 days. Each group consisted of 10 mice. The combined treatment group was irradiated first and then subjected to microgravity simulation.

## Establishment of malignant transformed cell lines
Cells were assigned to eight groups: Ctrl, X (X-ray), P (proton), C (carbon), SM (simulated microgravity), XSM, PSM, and CSM (single-dose irradiation followed by simulated microgravity). Cells were passaged to the 50th generation post-treatment.

## Cell proliferation
Cells were counted and seeded at 2 × 10⁴ cells/well in 12-well plates. Proliferation was assessed every 3 days by cell counting.

## Migration and invasion assays
Migration was assessed via scratch assay in 1% FBS medium, and images were captured at 0 h, 24 h, and 48 h post-scratch using an inverted microscope. Invasion used Matrigel-coated Transwell chambers (8 μm pores). 5 × 10⁴ serum-starved cells were seeded. After 24 h, invaded cells were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and analyzed using ImageJ.

## Soft agar colony formation
Both the 0.7% top agar containing 10⁴ cells and the 1.2% base agar were mixed with 2× culture medium before layering. Colonies (>50 cells) were counted after 3 weeks of incubation.

## Subcutaneous tumorigenesis
Cells were mixed with Matrigel and subcutaneously injected (0.2 mL containing 1 × 10⁷ cells) into BALB/c background, 6–8 weeks old, and male nude mice (10 sites/group). Tumors were harvested at 1 cm, fixed or snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for analysis.

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
Tissue sections were stained with antibodies against ARRB1 (Abcam, ab32099), Ki67 (AiFang, AF20068), a marker for lung adenocarcinoma, Cytokeratin 7 (CK7, AiFang, AF20042), invasion and metastasis markers E-cadherin (E-cad, AiFang, AF20137) and Vimentin (Vim, AiFang, AF20105), and CA9 (Santa Cruz, sc-365900). DAB staining was visualized using an inverted microscope and quantified using ImageJ with IHC-profiler plugin.

## Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Vazyme, China) and dissolved in DEPC-treated water. Reverse transcription and qPCR were performed, with GAPDH as the internal reference. Gene expression was analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Tables and .

## Protein analysis
Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were prepared (Beyotime, China). Western blot was performed using standard protocols. Antibodies against ARRB1 (Abcam, ab32099), N-cadherin (N-cad, CST, 14215S), E-cad (CST, 3195S), Fibronectin 1 (FN1, Proteintech, 15613-1-AP), Vim (CST, 5741S), GAPDH (Biodragon, B3029), H3 (Biodragon, B1055), CaM (Santa Cruz, sc-137079), CAMKII (Santa Cruz, sc-5306), CAMK2D (Santa Cruz, sc-100362), CAMK2G (Santa Cruz, sc-517278), p-CAMKII (Biodragon, BD-PP0781), p-ARRB1 (Selleck, F2121) and CA9 (Santa Cruz, sc-365900) were used. The uncropped images of all blots were shown in Supplementary Fig. .

## Masson staining
Masson’s trichrome staining was performed using a commercial Masson staining kit (Solarbio, China), and collagen area was quantified using ImageJ, calculating the collagen volume fraction.

## Immunofluorescence
Cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h. Primary antibodies (4°C/overnight) and fluorescent secondaries (1 h/dark) were applied, followed by DAPI staining and confocal imaging (Olympus, FV1200). Confocal images were analyzed with ImageJ.

## Lentivirus infection
Lentivirus was produced in 293T cells by co-transfecting lentiviral vectors with psPAX2 and pMD2.G using lipofectamine™ 3000 transfection reagent (Thermofisher, USA) and used to infect target cells. Post-selection with puromycin (2 μg/mL).

## Genetically engineered mice
Arrb1flox/flox mice and lung tissue-specific Cre mice Sftpc-CreERT2+/− were crossed. Genotyping was performed via PCR and agarose gel electrophoresis. The primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Table . Tamoxifen (20 mg/mL in corn oil, 75 mg/kg/day, i.p.) was administered for 7 days to induce lung-specific knockout.

## Urethane induction
Post-tamoxifen, mice received radiation (X-rays or carbon-ion irradiation) and/or hindlimb suspension, followed by urethane treatment (0.8 g/kg i.p., every 5 days for 1 month). Lungs were collected after an additional month for histological analysis. Each group consisted of 5 mice.

## Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining
H&E staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Solarbio, China). Tissue sections were mounted with neutral resin, dried completely, and examined under a light microscope for morphological assessment and image acquisition.

## Intracellular calcium ions (Ca²⁺) detection and modulation
Five micrometers fluo-4 AM (Beyotime, China) staining assessed intracellular Ca²⁺ levels via confocal microscopy. Cells were treated with CaCl₂ (1–5 mM) or BAPTA-AM (2 μM) to modulate Ca²⁺ levels.

## Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
Cell lysates were incubated with antibodies and magnetic beads (Selleck, USA) overnight at 4 °C. Eluted proteins were analyzed by Western blot. The antibodies against the following proteins were used: HA (Abclonal, AE008; Proteintech, 51064-2-AP), Flag (Abclonal, AE005; Proteintech, 20543-1-AP), ARRB1 (Abcam, ab32099), CAMK2G (Santa Cruz, sc-517278), and IgG (Biodragon, BF01001).

## Transcriptome analysis
RNA-seq was performed by GENEWIZ. Differential gene expression was assessed via DESeq2 and limma (|FC| > 2.0, p < 0.05). GO and KEGG enrichment was conducted using clusterProfiler.

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-PCR
ChIP was conducted (Beyotime, China) with ARRB1 (Abcam, ab32099) antibody or IgG control (Biodragon, BF01001). Immunoprecipitated DNA was purified and analyzed by PCR.

## Luciferase assay
Cells were transfected with luciferase reporters (pGL3-NC or pGL3-CA9 obtained from RiboBio, China). Luciferase activity was measured using a commercial kit (Beyotime, China).

## Animal drug treatment
Tumor-bearing mice received U104 (0.4 mg/mouse/day, i.p.), saline, siCA9 (1 nmol/injection every 3 days, intratumoral), or siNC. Tumor volume and weight were recorded after 21 days. Each group consisted of 5 mice.

## Animal euthanasia
All mice were euthanized at the study endpoint or earlier if they met humane endpoints (e.g., severe disease, injury, or inability to eat/drink). Euthanasia was carried out using controlled CO₂ inhalation. Mice were first rendered unconscious in a pre-filled chamber with a flow rate maintained at 30–70% chamber volume per minute, in accordance with AVMA guidelines. Death was verified by cessation of vital signs and confirmed by cervical dislocation.

## Statistical analysis
All experiments were repeated at least three times. GraphPad Prism 8.0 was used for statistical analysis. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Comparisons between two groups were performed using t-tests. Significance: *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001. A synergistic effect was assessed using the Bliss Independence model and represented by “&“.
