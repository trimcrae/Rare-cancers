## Background
Interferon regulatory factor 1 (IRF1) plays a crucial role in the type I interferon (IFN) response. However, its functional role and underlying mechanisms in gastric cancer (GC) remain unclear. This study aims to investigate the biological significance of IRF1 in GC progression and its potential as a therapeutic target.

## Methods
IRF1 expression was analyzed using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and GTEx databases, validated by immunohistochemistry (IHC) in 366 GC patients. Functional experiments, including CCK-8, Transwell migration and invasion assays, and apoptosis analysis, were conducted in GC cell lines with IRF1 overexpression or knockdown. A subcutaneous xenograft model was established to evaluate the in vivo effects of IRF1 on tumor growth. Co-immunoprecipitation and western blotting were performed to explore the molecular interactions between IRF1 and Myxovirus resistance 2 (MX2), as well as its regulation of the PI3K signaling pathway.

## Results
IRF1 expression was significantly higher in gastric cancer tissues than in adjacent normal tissues. Higher IRF1 levels were also associated with improved patient survival. Overexpression of IRF1 inhibited GC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion while promoting apoptosis, whereas IRF1 knockdown had the opposite effects. Mechanistically, IRF1 suppressed PI3K/p-AKT signaling while enhancing p-ERK1/2 activation. Moreover, IRF1 directly interacted with MX2, a protein involved in epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and this interaction was essential for suppressing MX2-mediated oncogenic activity. In vivo experiments confirmed that IRF1 overexpression significantly reduced tumor growth and metastasis.

## Conclusions
IRF1 functions as a tumor suppressor in GC by modulating the PI3K signaling pathway and interacting with MX2 to inhibit EMT. These findings highlight IRF1 as a potential therapeutic target for GC treatment.

## Background
Cancer remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide, treatment outcomes for many solid tumors remain unsatisfactory. The shift toward precision oncology has improved outcomes in select cancers, but challenges such as tumor heterogeneity, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance persist [ ]. Gastric cancer (GC) ranks as the fifth most common malignancy and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with over 1 million new cases and 769,000 deaths annually [ ]. Over 70% of patients are diagnosed at advanced stages due to nonspecific early symptoms, leading to poor prognosis (5-year survival < 30%) [ , ]. Current therapeutic strategies, including surgery, platinum-based chemotherapy, and targeted therapies (e.g., trastuzumab for HER2 + GC), have shown limited efficacy. This is largely due to tumor heterogeneity, immunosuppressive microenvironments, and the development of acquired resistance [ ]. Additionally, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) like anti-PD-1/PD-L1 agents demonstrate variable responses, with objective response rates (ORR) below 20% in unselected populations [ ]. These challenges underscore the urgent need to identify novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets to improve GC management.
Interferons (IFNs) are a class of cytokines capable of inhibiting viral replication [ ]. They also play roles in cell proliferation, apoptosis, inflammation, and adaptive immunity [ ]. Moreover, interferon signaling serves as a crucial foundation for tumor development and progression, being upregulated in various cancers [ ]. IFNs induce the expression of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) and the transcription factor family (IRFs) by activating the JAK-STAT signaling pathway [ , ], thereby promoting innate immunity and inhibiting malignant tumor growth. However, the specific key molecules involved remain to be further explored. Type I interferons, notably IFN-α/β, exhibit dual anti-tumor roles by activating dendritic cells, enhancing CD8 + T cell cytotoxicity, and directly inducing tumor apoptosis [ ]. In gastric cancer, there have been some reports on the combination of interferon drugs with various chemotherapy agents [ – ]. However, clinical trials reveal significant limitations: systemic toxicity (e.g., myelosuppression, fatigue) and transient efficacy, which may counteract therapeutic benefits. These challenges necessitate identifying key modulators within IFN signaling to refine therapeutic strategies.
IRF1, a member of the IRF family, is a nuclear-localized transcription factor encoded by the IRF1 gene on chromosome 5q31.1. IRF1 can regulate the expression of ISGs by directly binding to the interferon-stimulated response element (IRE) in response to IFN-I and IFN-II [ ]. Structurally, IRF1 harbors a conserved N-terminal DNA-binding domain (DBD; residues 1–113) containing a “tryptophan cluster” motif critical for binding interferon-stimulated response elements (ISREs) in target gene promoters, and a C-terminal regulatory domain mediating protein interactions and post-translational modifications [ ]. As a tumor suppressor, IRF1 plays a direct antagonistic role in tumor cell growth [ – ]. In tumor immunity, IRF1 not only directly influences NK cell maturation and activity but also plays a crucial role in IL-12 production by macrophages, Th1 development, and CD8 + T cell maturation [ ]. Additionally, it is involved in the differentiation and maturation of dendritic cells and the inhibition of regulatory T (Treg) cell development [ , ]. Moreover, IRF1 affects antitumor immunity by upregulating PD-L1 expression in tumor cells [ ]. Mechanistically, IRF1 intersects with the JAK/STAT, PI3K/AKT, and NF-κB pathways [ ]. In GC-related studies, silencing IRF1 has been reported to indirectly induce epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), thereby enhancing the invasion and metastasis of GC cells [ ]. There are also reports suggesting that IRF1 influences drug resistance in GC cells [ ], though these studies provide only partial insights. Critically, while IRF1’s role in tumor progression is recognized, its interplay with co-regulators and downstream effectors in GC pathogenesis remains unexplored, creating a gap that this study aims to address.
In this study, we investigated the role of IRF1 in GC and its potential interaction with the Myxovirus resistance 2 (MX2) protein. We analyzed IRF1 expression in GC tissues and its correlation with clinical outcomes. Functional studies were conducted to evaluate the effects of IRF1 on GC cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and apoptosis. Mechanistically, we explored the involvement of IRF1 in the PI3K/AKT pathway and its interaction with MX2, a protein implicated in viral defense and tumorigenesis. Current biomarkers and therapeutic targets in gastric cancer often lack predictive power or durability, highlighting the need for alternative strategies such as modulating interferon regulatory pathways. Our findings provide novel insights into the tumor-suppressive function of IRF1 in GC and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target.

## Methods

## Download and preprocessing of TCGA and GEO gastric cancer patient expression data
Using the TCGAbiolinks package (version 2.25.2), RNA sequencing quantification data for gastric adenocarcinoma (STAD) were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). This dataset includes gene expression profiles from 375 gastric cancer patient tumor tissues and 32 adjacent normal tissues. Gene expression values were converted from FPKM to TPM and subsequently log2-transformed. Additionally, corresponding clinical data for gastric cancer patients were downloaded, including age, sex, TNM staging, and survival information.
Using the GEOquery package, expression data from six independent gastric cancer patient microarray datasets were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), including GSE15459, GSE29272, GSE34942, GSE57303, GSE62254, and GSE84437. The annotation platforms for these datasets are as follows: GSE15459, GSE34942, GSE57303, and GSE62254: Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array (GPL570). GSE29272: Affymetrix Human Genome U133A Array (GPL96). GSE84437: Illumina HumanHT-12 V3.0 expression beadchip (GPL6947). Clinical and prognostic information for gastric cancer patients from these datasets was also extracted for further analysis.

## Collection of gastric cancer patient specimens and their clinicopathological characteristics
A retrospective collection of 420 gastric adenocarcinoma patients who underwent radical gastrectomy at The First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University between January 2014 and December 2016 was conducted. Gastric cancer tissue specimens and paired adjacent normal tissues (at least 10 cm from the tumor margin) were collected, fixed in formalin, and embedded in paraffin. The histopathological diagnosis of each sample was performed according to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification and the AJCC/UICC TNM staging system. Inclusion Criteria: (1) Histologically confirmed gastric adenocarcinoma, with planned elective therapeutic surgery. (2) Age > 18 years. (3) Complete postoperative follow-up data available. All included patients had no other malignancies and had not received preoperative radiotherapy or chemotherapy. For each patient, corresponding clinicopathological characteristics were recorded, including sex, age, tumor location, tumor size, depth of invasion, lymph node metastasis, distant metastasis, TNM stage, and recurrence status. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Ethics Approval No. 2021-R092), and written informed consent was obtained from all study participants.

## Preparation of tissue microarrays for gastric cancer patient specimens
Tissue microarrays (TMAs) are microarray-based histological tools for analyzing multiple tissue samples simultaneously. The gastric cancer TMAs used in this study were prepared under the guidance and assistance of professional pathologists. Among the 420 gastric cancer tissues and 41 adjacent normal tissues obtained from The First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University, hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining was performed to confirm histopathological types and determine the sampling sites for the microarray. Based on the selected sites, 1-mm diameter core tissue samples were extracted using fine needles and arranged systematically into the microarray. The assembled tissues were then re-embedded and sectioned. After TMA construction, HE staining was repeated, and the tissue selection was re-evaluated by pathologists to ensure accuracy.

## Expression analysis of key gene IRF1 in tumor tissues
RNA sequencing data from 33 different tumor types and their corresponding normal adjacent tissues were downloaded from the TCGA and GTEx databases. The mRNA expression levels of IRF1 across various cancer types were extracted to evaluate the differential expression of IRF1 between tumor and adjacent normal tissues. For gastric cancer patients, ROC curves were plotted based on IRF1 mRNA expression levels and prognostic information to determine the optimal cutoff value. Patients were classified into high-IRF1 and low-IRF1 expression groups, and Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to assess the impact of IRF1 expression on the survival outcomes of gastric cancer patients.

## Immunohistochemical detection of IRF1 expression in gastric cancer and adjacent tissues
Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis was performed on the gastric cancer tissue microarrays. The tissue microarrays were deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval. After blocking with goat serum, the sections were incubated with rabbit polyclonal IRF1 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:200 dilution) at room temperature for 2 h. The sections were then incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (MaiXin Bio, Fuzhou, China) at 37 °C for 30 min. The color reaction was developed using the DAB kit (Zhongshan Golden Bridge Bio, Beijing, China) for 5 min following the manufacturer’s protocol. The sections were subsequently counterstained with hematoxylin, differentiated in hydrochloric acid alcohol, dehydrated through an ethanol gradient, and mounted with neutral resin for microscopic analysis. IHC staining results were acquired using a tissue section digital scanner or imaging system, and quantitative analysis was performed using Image-Pro Plus software. After excluding specimens with > 50% tissue loss or missing clinical information, 366 gastric cancer tissues and 39 adjacent normal tissues were included in the final analysis.

## Cell culture and transfection
Gastric cancer cell lines AGS, BGC-823, and HGC-27 were cultured in RPMI-1640 and DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. The culture medium was replaced every 48 h, and cells were passaged when they reached 80%-90% confluence. All experiments were conducted when cells were in the logarithmic growth phase. For transfection, cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into six-well plates. When cell confluence reached 60%-80%, transfection was performed. 500 µL of OPTI-MEM medium was used to dilute 5 µL of siRNA or 2 µg of specific plasmid DNA and 4 µL of Lipofectamine 2000, followed by incubation at room temperature for 5 min. The siRNA and Lipofectamine 2000 mixture was then combined and incubated for another 20 min at room temperature to allow for complete encapsulation of the siRNA and plasmid DNA. The transfection mixture was then added to the cells along with 1 mL of OPTI-MEM medium. After 6 h of transfection, the medium was replaced with 2 mL of DMEM containing 10% FBS.

## Construction and extraction of IRF1 and MX2 overexpression plasmids
The IRF1 gene coding sequence (CDS) was retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), selecting the longest transcript. The BamHI and EcoRI restriction enzyme sites were used for cloning, with the following primer sequences: Forward primer (FP): CGCGGATCCGCCACCATGCCCATCACTCGGAT, Reverse primer (RP): CCGGAATTCTTATCAAGCGTAGTCTGGGACGTCGTATGGGTACTACGGTGCACAGGGAATG. For MX2 overexpression plasmid, BamHI and XhoI restriction sites were used, with the following primers: Forward primer (FP): CGCGGATCCGCCACCATGTCTAAGGCCCACAAG, FLAG-tag Reverse primer (RP): CCGCTCGAGTTATCACTTGTCATCGTCGTCCTTGTAATCGTGGATCTCTTTGCTG, HA-tag Reverse primer (RP): CCGCTCGAGTTATCAAGCGTAGTCTGGGACGTCGTATGGGTAGTGGATCTCTTTGCTG. Using Vazyme 2× Phanta Evo HS Master Mix high-fidelity DNA polymerase, the cDNA of AGS gastric cancer cells was used as a template. The PCR reaction system was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, wild-type pcDNA3.1(+) plasmid and HA-/FLAG-tagged pcDNA3.1(+)-IRF1/MX2 recombinant plasmids were successfully constructed, and their plasmid concentrations were measured.

## CCK-8 assay for cell proliferation
Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). Cells were seeded at a density of 5,000 cells per well in a 96-well plate, with five replicate wells per experimental group. After 24 h of incubation, once the cells adhered to the plate, transfection was performed following the previously described protocol. The pcDNA3.1(+) control group and the pcDNA3.1(+)-IRF1 experimental group were transfected, and the culture medium was replaced 6 h post-transfection. At 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h post-transfection, the culture medium was discarded, and fresh medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by incubation for 3 h in a 37 °C incubator. The optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a multifunctional microplate reader. A cell proliferation curve was plotted based on the OD values.

## Transwell cell migration and invasion assay
Gastric cancer cells were transfected with the corresponding overexpression plasmids or siRNA and incubated for 24 h. Cells were then digested and resuspended in serum-free medium, adjusting the cell concentration to 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. For the migration assay, 100 µL of the prepared cell suspension was added to the upper chamber of the Transwell insert, while 500 µL of complete medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. The Transwell system was incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for an appropriate duration. The upper chamber was removed. Cells on the lower side of the membrane were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and excess cells on the upper side were gently wiped off using a cotton swab. The inserts were air-dried at room temperature. Cell migration was observed under a microscope, with five random fields (10× magnification) per chamber imaged and analyzed using Image-Pro Plus software to quantify the number of migrated cells. For the invasion assay, Matrigel was pre-coated in the Transwell inserts. Matrigel was diluted at a 1:19 ratio in pre-cooled culture medium, and 200 µL of the diluted Matrigel was added to each upper chamber, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h until solidified. The remaining liquid was carefully removed, and the same experimental procedures as the migration assay were performed.

## Apoptosis assay
Gastric cancer cells were seeded in six-well plates at an appropriate density. Once the cells were fully adhered, Dox was used to induce stable expression of IRF1 protein. After 48 h of induction, cells were digested with trypsin and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min to collect the cell pellet. The supernatant was discarded, and cells were resuspended in pre-cooled PBS, followed by another centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 min. The washing step was repeated twice. Cells were then resuspended in 1× binding buffer, adjusting the cell concentration to 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. 100 µL of the cell suspension (1 × 10⁵ cells) was transferred to a new 1.5 mL EP tube. The cells were stained with FITC Annexin V and PI according to the manufacturer’s instructions of the FITC-Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD, San Diego, USA). Apoptosis was analyzed using a BD FACS Calibur™ Flow Cytometer, and data were processed with ModFit Software.

## Western blot (WB) analysis
After the required cell treatments, total protein was extracted from cells using RIPA lysis buffer containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (1:100 dilution). Lysis was performed on ice for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, mixed with protein loading buffer, and boiled for denaturation. Proteins were separated via SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis using separation gels of appropriate concentrations and a 5% stacking gel. Electrophoresis was performed at 80 V initially and then increased to 120 V until the dye front reached the bottom of the gel. Proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes using a semi-dry transfer system, assembled in the order sponge–filter paper–gel–membrane, with 80 V for an optimized time based on molecular weight. The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against: IRF1, Caspase-3, Caspase-9, Flag-tag, HA-tag, E-cadherin, N-cadherin, Snail, STAT1/pSTAT1, STAT3/pSTAT3, PI3K (Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA, USA), MX2, GAPDH, AKT/p-AKT, ERK1/2 (Santa Cruz, CA, United States). Primary antibodies were diluted 1:1000 in TBST buffer. The membrane was then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) at room temperature for 1.5 h. After washing with TBST, the signals were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence detection system.

## Immunofluorescence (IF) assay
Gastric cancer cells were seeded in six-well plates containing glass coverslips at a density of 5000 cells per well. After 8 h, the cells were transfected and incubated for 48 h. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h, followed by PBS washing. To permeabilize the cells, they were treated with 0.1% Triton X-100 at 37 °C for 10 min and washed again with PBS. Blocking was performed using 5% goat serum for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in 1% serum-PBS, including: HA (1:250), IRF1 (1:100), MX2 (1:50). After overnight incubation, cells were washed and incubated with secondary antibodies (1:1000 dilution in 1% serum-PBS) at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark. The secondary antibodies used included: Cy3-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG, FITC-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Invitrogen). The nuclei were stained with DAPI for 3 min, followed by PBS washing. The slides were mounted using anti-fade mounting medium and imaged using a Nikon C1–i fluorescence microscope (Japan).

## Construction of inducible IRF1 stable gastric cancer cell lines
To establish a stable inducible IRF1-expressing gastric cancer cell line, the pENTR3C-IRF1 plasmid was first constructed using the pENTR3C vector. Gateway LR recombination was performed to generate the pInducer20-IRF1 plasmid. For recombination, 150 ng of pENTR3C-IRF1 plasmid and 150 ng of pInducer20 vector plasmid were added to a 200 µL EP tube, diluted to 8 µL with DEPC-treated water, and then supplemented with 2 µL of LR Clonase™ II enzyme. The mixture was incubated in a PCR instrument at 25 °C for 1 h. Afterward, Proteinase K solution was added, and the reaction continued at 37 °C for 10 min. Following the reaction, the plasmid was transformed into competent bacteria and stored as glycerol stocks. The pInducer20-IRF1 plasmid was then transfected into AGS and BGC-823 cells, followed by selection with an appropriate concentration of G418 (Merck Sigma, Germany). Cells were maintained under G418 selection, with intermittent medium changes, until all control cells were dead. Surviving experimental cells were passaged and further expanded under the same G418 selection conditions. The stable cell lines were cryopreserved and used for subsequent functional studies and in vivo subcutaneous tumor transplantation experiments.

## Construction of the BGC-823-IRF1-LUC stable gastric cancer cell line
First, Lv-pInducer20-IRF1 lentivirus packaging was performed following the instructions of the Lenti-X™ HTX Packaging System. The pInducer20-IRF1 plasmid was mixed with the Lenti-X™ HTX Packaging reagent and added to HEK-293T cells for culture. After 48 h of infection, the viral supernatant was collected, aliquoted, and stored at -80 °C. Subsequently, the BGC-823-IRF1-LUC stable gastric cancer cell line was established. BGC-823 cells were seeded in a 24-well plate at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well. The experimental group was treated with 500 µL of viral supernatant along with polybrene at a final concentration of 4 µg/mL. The control group received 500 µL of DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum and the same concentration of polybrene. Cells were incubated in a 37 °C incubator. Selection with an appropriate concentration of G418 was performed until all control group cells died. The surviving experimental group cells were passaged into a 6-well plate and cultured with the same concentration of G418 for further expansion and cryopreservation. The selected cells were seeded in a 24-well plate, incubated for one day, and then infected with 50 µL of LV-LUC-PURO virus. After 6 h of infection, the medium was replaced. After 48 h, selection with puromycin (1:500, 1 mg/mL) was initiated. Cells were cultured until all control group cells died, and the experimental group cells were expanded, cryopreserved, and used for subsequent animal experiments.

## Construction of the subcutaneous xenograft tumor model in nude mice
Eight healthy BALB/c nude mice with similar growth status were randomly divided into two groups, with four mice in each group: the BGC-IRF1-Dox(+) experimental group and the BGC-IRF1-Dox(-) control group. The stable BGC-823-IRF1 cell line was subcutaneously injected into the right dorsal region of the nude mice at a concentration of 2 × 10⁷ cells/mL, with 0.1 mL injected per mouse. Starting from the seventh day after injection, tumor size was measured. The experimental group was fed with 1 mg/mL Dox (Merck-Sigma, Germany) in drinking water. Tumor size was measured every other day using calipers, with tumor volume (V, mm³) calculated as V = a × b² / 2, where “a” represents the tumor length and “b” represents the width. On the 30th day, tumors were excised and recorded. At the end of the experiment, the animals were sacrificed, and tumor tissues were collected for further analysis.

## Construction of nude mouse tail vein lung metastasis model and in vivo imaging
A total of 10 female BALB/c-nu nude mice (4 weeks old, with similar body weights, good health status, and well-developed tail veins) were selected for injection with the stable BGC-823-IRF1 cell line. Each mouse was slowly injected with 100 µL of a total of 1 × 10⁶ cells through the tail vein. In vivo imaging was performed 4 h post-injection. Before anesthesia, fluorescent substrate D-luciferin (15 mg/mL) was injected at a dose of 100 µL per 10 g body weight into the left or right side of the midline of the animal’s abdomen. Two minutes after injection, the mice were placed in a small animal anesthesia machine for isoflurane inhalation anesthesia for 3 min and then imaged using an in vivo imaging system. Based on photon counts from high to low, the 10 mice were randomly divided into two groups: the BGC-IRF1-Dox(+) experimental group and the BGC-IRF1-Dox(-) control group, with 5 mice per group. Doxycycline (Dox) was added to the drinking water of the experimental group at a final concentration of 1 mg/mL to induce IRF1 protein expression, and the drinking water was changed every 2 days to prevent Dox degradation. Starting 2 weeks after the initial injection, in vivo imaging was performed once per week to monitor tumor metastasis. When the bioluminescence signal in the lungs increased to 10⁹ p/s, all mice were euthanized under isoflurane anesthesia. The lungs were dissected, photographed, recorded, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for preservation.

## Construction of IRF1 fragment deletion mutant plasmid IRF1-Δ1
The IRF1 protein domains were predicted using the PDBe-KB (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/) prediction tool, and a recombinant plasmid was constructed with a deletion of amino acids 1–34 of IRF1. The IRF1-Δ1 primer design involved deleting amino acids 1 to 114 and starting from the following codon: IRF1-Δ1-114-FP: CGCGGATCCGCCACCATGCTCACCAAGAACCAG, IRF1-Δ1-114-RP: GCTGGAGGCATCATGGCAGGGGCTGCTCAG. The plasmid was amplified, sequenced, and extracted for further use.

## Co-immunoprecipitation
When the treated cells reached 90% confluence, they were lysed using NP40 lysis buffer and centrifuged at 4 °C to collect the supernatant. The supernatant was incubated overnight at 4 °C with BeyoMag™ Anti-HA Magnetic Beads pre-washed with TBST. After magnetic separation and discarding the flow-through, the beads were vortex-washed with TBST. Proteins were eluted by incubating the beads in acidic elution buffer (0.1 M Glycine-HCl) at room temperature for 5 min, followed by immediate pH neutralization using neutralization buffer (0.5 M Tris-HCl). To improve efficiency, repeated elution was performed. The final eluates were temporarily stored at 4 °C or long-term stored at -20 °C/-80 °C.

## Construction of MX2 knockout gastric cancer cell line
The recombinant plasmid Cas9-sgRNA-Puromycin was designed and constructed by Shanghai Sangon Biotech. The sgRNA (small guide RNA) sequences (5’-3’) were as follows: sgRNA-MX2-1: GGCGCACCTTCTGCTCGTACTGG, sgRNA-MX2-2: GGCACTGTGCCGAATGGCGGTGG. Exponentially growing cells were seeded into a 6-well plate at 5 × 10⁴ cells per well. When cell confluence reached approximately 80%, plasmid transfection was initiated. The transfection mixture was prepared as follows: Solution A: 100 µL OPTI-MEM + 2 µg plasmid, Solution B: 100 µL OPTI-MEM + 4 µL Lipofectamine 2000. Solution A was mixed with Solution B, incubated, and then added to the cells. After 48 h of transfection, puromycin was added at a final concentration of 1 µg/mL to each well for selection. The selection process continued for 2 weeks, after which protein extraction and WB analysis were performed to validate knockout efficiency. The gastric cancer cells with the highest knockout efficiency were selected and subjected to single-clone isolation using the limiting dilution method. After expansion, protein extraction was performed again to validate the knockout efficiency. Single clones with significant knockout efficiency were cultured for 2–3 passages before cryopreservation.

## Construction of MX2 GTPase activity-deficient mutant plasmid MX2-K131A-FLAG
Previous studies have shown that MX2 GTPase activity depends on lysine (K) at position 131. Mutation of this lysine to alanine (A) significantly reduces GTPase activity. Primer design for K131A mutation: The AAG codon encoding lysine (K) at position 131 was mutated to GCG encoding alanine (A), with 15 bases flanking each side: K131A-FP: CAGAGCTCGGGCGCGAGCTCTGTGCTGG, K131A-RP: CCAGCACAGAGCTCGCGCCCGAGCTCTG. Using the pcDNA3.1-MX2-FLAG plasmid as a template, two fragments were amplified separately via PCR: MX2 forward primer + K131A reverse primer, K131A forward primer + MX2 reverse primer. The two PCR products were gel-purified and mixed. This mixture was then used as a template for another round of PCR using the MX2 forward and reverse primers. The final product underwent restriction enzyme digestion, ligation, bacterial transformation, and single-clone selection. The sequence was verified by DNA sequencing, and validated clones were subjected to plasmid extraction.

## Construction of MX2 deletion mutant plasmids MX2-ΔN, MX2-Δ1, MX2-Δ2, MX2-Δ3
Using the PDBe-KB (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/) prediction tool, the structural domains of MX2 protein were analyzed. Recombinant plasmids were constructed with deletions of the following amino acid segments: MX2-ΔN: Deletion of amino acids 1–25, MX2-Δ1: Deletion of amino acids 115–387, MX2-Δ2: Deletion of amino acids 388–594, MX2-Δ3: Deletion of amino acids 619–709. Primer design for MX2-ΔN, Δ1, Δ2, and Δ3 (each primer flanks the deleted region by 15 bases): MX2-ΔN-FP: CGGGATCCGCCACCATGAATTCCTTCCAGC, MX2-ΔN-RP: CCGCTCGAGTTATCACTTGTCATCGTCGTCCTTGTAATCGTGGATCTCTTTGCTG, MX2-Δ115-387-FP: GGTGTGGAGCAGGACTTGTTAGAAGGACAA, MX2-Δ115-387-RP: TTGTCCTTCTAACAAGTCCTGCTCCACACC, MX2-Δ388-594-FP: CAAAAATCGCTCCCGGAGATTTTTAACCCT, MX2-Δ388-594-RP: AGGGTTAAAAATCTCCGGGAGCGATTTTTG, MX2-Δ619-709-FP: CCCAGTAATGAGTCTTCCAGCAAAGAGATC, MX2-Δ619-709-RP: GATCTCTTTGCTGGAAGACTCATTACTGGG. The deletion constructs were confirmed through plasmid amplification, sequencing, and extraction.

## Statistical analysis
All data processing and analysis were performed using R software (version: 4.1.2). For the comparison of continuous variables between two groups, an independent Student’s t-test was used to assess the statistical significance of normally distributed variables, while the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to analyze differences in non-normally distributed variables. For comparisons between categorical variables, the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test was employed to compare statistical differences between the two groups. In addition, the survival and survminer packages in R were used to perform survival analysis, with Kaplan-Meier survival curves used for visualization and the log-rank test used to assess differences in survival between groups. All statistical P-values were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

## Results

## IRF1 is a critical regulatory component in the type I interferon response in gastric cancer cells
We analyzed the core gene IRF1 in the type I interferon response to observe its effect on the biological characteristics of gastric cancer. First, we incubated gastric cancer cells with recombinant human IFN-α2b protein and examined the expression changes of type I interferon pathway-related proteins. We observed an increase in the expression of pSTAT1, STAT1, IRF1, and ISG15 proteins in gastric cancer cells, with a particularly notable increase in IRF1 protein expression (Fig.  A). On the other hand, we investigated whether overexpression or inhibition of IRF1 protein affects the expression of type I IFN response-related upstream and downstream proteins. We constructed the pcDNA3.1(+) -IRF1-HA plasmid and transfected gastric cancer cells to achieve IRF1 overexpression. Meanwhile, we silenced IRF1 expression using siRNA. The efficiency of the overexpression plasmid (Fig.  B) and siRNA-mediated knockdown (Fig.  C) was validated via WB analysis. We found that overexpression of IRF1 promoted the expression of downstream proteins ISG15 and IFI6. Notably, IRF1 expression also exerted positive feedback on upstream pSTAT1, leading to an amplification loop that further enhances IRF1 expression (Fig.  D).

 

## Expression characteristics of IRF1 in gastric cancer tissues
We analyzed the mutation status and expression levels of IRF1 in tumor and adjacent normal tissues from the TCGA database. Our results revealed that IRF1 exhibits a low frequency of variations, including SNPs and CNVs, across different tumor types, with an overall mutation rate below 5% (Fig. A and B). Based on the combined analysis of TCGA and GTEx databases (https://www.gtexportal.org/), IRF1 expression was found to be elevated in multiple tumor tissues (Fig. C). Specifically, in the TCGA-STAD dataset, IRF1 expression was significantly upregulated in gastric cancer tissues compared to normal tissues (P < 0.001; Fig.  A). Paired analysis further confirmed that IRF1 expression in tumor tissues was significantly higher than that in adjacent normal tissues (P = 0.002; Fig.  B). Moreover, survival analysis indicated that gastric cancer patients with high IRF1 expression had a better prognosis compared to those with low IRF1 expression (Log-rank P = 0.030, Fig.  C).
To validate these findings, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis, which also demonstrated significantly higher IRF1 expression in gastric cancer tissues than in adjacent normal tissues (P = 0.021; Fig.  D, E). Paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues showed a similar trend (P < 0.001; Fig.  F). Based on the optimal cutoff value of IRF1 expression in gastric cancer tumor tissues, we classified 366 gastric cancer patients into low IRF1 expression and high IRF1 expression groups. The clinical and pathological characteristics of these groups are summarized in Table  . Survival analysis further confirmed that high IRF1 expression was associated with a better clinical outcome (Log-rank P = 0.010, Fig.  G).

 

 

## IRF1 inhibits gastric cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion while promoting apoptosis
To further investigate the role of IRF1 in gastric cancer, we transfected pcDNA3.1(+)–IRF1-HA overexpression plasmids into gastric cancer cells. Through CCK8 assays, we found that after transfecting AGS and BGC-823 cells with the IRF1 overexpression plasmid, IRF1 overexpression significantly inhibited gastric cancer cell proliferation (Fig.  A, B). Similarly, using Transwell assays, we observed that 48 h after transfecting gastric cancer cells with the IRF1 overexpression plasmid, the migration and invasion abilities of IRF1-overexpressing cells were significantly reduced compared to the control group (Fig.  C, D), with statistically significant differences. These results suggest that IRF1 may play a protective role in gastric cancer. At the same time, we used siRNA gene silencing technology to suppress IRF1 expression in gastric cancer cells. After 72 h of transfection with siRNA-IRF1, the number of experimental group cells was significantly higher than that of the control group (Fig.  E, F). Similarly, in the Transwell migration and invasion assays, IRF1 knockdown significantly promoted the migration and invasion of gastric cancer cells, with consistent results in AGS and BGC-823 cells (Fig.  G, H).
In the flow cytometry apoptosis experiments, we also found that overexpression of IRF1 could promote apoptosis in gastric cancer cells. In AGS-IRF1 cells, after 48 h of Dox-induced IRF1 expression, apoptotic cells significantly increased, showing statistical significance (P < 0.01, Fig.  I). Moreover, as IRF1 expression was induced, the protein levels of Cleaved-Caspase 3 and Cleaved-Caspase 9 significantly increased (Fig.  J), confirming that IRF1 promotes gastric cancer cell apoptosis. On the other hand, after overexpressing IRF1 in AGS-IRF1 stable cell lines, immunofluorescence localization experiments showed that both the IRF1-specific antibody and HA-tag antibody stained the nucleus, confirming that IRF1 is expressed in the nucleus (Fig.  K). 

## In vivo experiments confirm the inhibitory role of IRF1 in gastric cancer
To further validate the biological function of IRF1 in gastric cancer, we conducted in vivo animal studies. First, we established Tet-on inducible stable gastric cancer cell lines overexpressing IRF1, AGS-IRF1-HA (AGS-IRF1) and BGC-823-IRF1-HA (BGC-IRF1), and confirmed IRF1 protein expression and cell function through validation experiments (Fig. ). We then used the inducible BGC-823-IRF1-HA stable cell line to construct a subcutaneous xenograft tumor model in nude mice. We found that the tumor size in the Dox-treated group (1 mg/ml) was significantly smaller than that in the control group (Fig.  A, B), with statistical significance (P < 0.01). Histological analysis of tumor sections via HE staining (Fig.  C) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) (Fig.  D) confirmed IRF1 protein expression in the experimental group, validating the reliability of the model.
Additionally, we constructed a stable BGC-IRF1-LUC cell line expressing luciferase using lentiviral transduction, allowing for the establishment of a nude mouse lung metastasis model with inducible IRF1 expression. Weekly in vivo imaging revealed a significant decrease in fluorescence intensity in lung tissue during the first week, followed by a gradual increase starting from the second week (Fig.  E). Over time, the fluorescence intensity in the experimental group was significantly lower than in the control group, although due to the limited number of mice and tumor heterogeneity, the fluorescence data did not reach statistical significance (Fig.  F). Furthermore, numerous white, miliary metastatic nodules were observed in both groups, but the number of metastatic nodules was markedly lower in the experimental group (Fig.  G). HE staining was used to examine lung metastases, and IHC confirmed IRF1 protein expression in the Dox-treated (Dox+) group (Fig.  H). Through both in vivo and in vitro experiments, we demonstrated the protective role of IRF1 in gastric cancer. 

## IRF1 regulates cell function via the PI3K pathway through its core sequence
To explore the protective mechanism of IRF1 in gastric cancer, experiments revealed that IRF1 overexpression inhibits PI3K and p-AKT protein expression while promoting p-ERK1/2 protein expression (Fig.  D). These findings suggest that IRF1 may influence gastric cancer cell proliferation and apoptosis through the PI3K pathway. As a member of the IRF family, IRF1 consists of 325 amino acids and contains a unique “tryptophan cluster” DNA-binding domain located at amino acids 1–113 (Fig.  A). We successfully constructed a core fragment-deletion plasmid, pcDNA3.1(+)-IRF1Δ1, and validated its protein expression using WB (Fig.  B). Experimental results demonstrated that overexpression of this truncated IRF1 protein did not affect apoptosis-related proteins such as Caspase 3, Caspase 9, and c-Myc (Fig.  C). Additionally, compared to the pathway protein changes observed with full-length IRF1 overexpression, the truncated IRF1Δ1 failed to exert an effect on PI3K pathway-related proteins. These findings further confirm that IRF1 likely exerts its functional role in gastric cancer through the PI3K pathway

## Interaction between IRF1 and MX2 in gastric cancer cells
To identify potential interacting proteins of IRF1, we performed immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and proteomic analysis. Screening for IRF1-associated proteins revealed that MX2 interacts with IRF1, which was confirmed by both forward and reverse Co-IP validation. We then constructed a pcDNA3.1(+)-MX2-Flag overexpression plasmid and co-transfected pcDNA3.1(+)-IRF1-HA and pcDNA3.1(+)-MX2-Flag into AGS and BGC gastric cancer cells (Fig. S ). Using HA and Flag magnetic beads, we enriched the protein complexes and performed Western blot analysis, confirming the presence of IRF1-HA and MX2-Flag proteins in the Co-IP reaction (Fig.  E). This validated the interaction between IRF1 and MX2. Furthermore, immunofluorescence co-localization analysis showed that MX2 is expressed in both the cytoplasm and nucleus, while IRF1 is mainly localized in the nucleus. Notably, the two proteins exhibited co-localization (Fig.  F), supporting their interaction in gastric cancer cells.

 

## Exploration of the function and mechanism of the IRF1-MX2 protein complex in gastric cancer cells
We identified and validated the interaction between IRF1 and MX2 and further constructed MX2 truncation mutants for in-depth study. According to predictions from PDBe-KB (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/) and NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/structure), MX2 primarily consists of three functional domains. Based on their positions within the 715 amino acid sequence, we divided MX2 into three segments, with segment 1 (residues 116–387) containing the GTPase-binding site and nuclear localization signals (Fig.  A). Upon constructing MX2 deletion mutants and conducting co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays, we found that the MX2-FlagΔ1 mutant (segment 1 deletion) lost its interaction with IRF1 (Fig.  B), suggesting that MX2 may bind to IRF1 via segment 1 to exert its function.
Furthermore, we explored MX2 function by generating MX2 mutant plasmids and validated their expression via WB (Fig.  C, D). The ΔN and K131A mutations were associated with nuclear localization. Through migration and invasion assays, we found that MX2 overexpression promoted gastric cancer cell migration and invasion (Fig.  E, F), suppressed E-cadherin expression, and upregulated N-cadherin and Snail expression (Fig.  I), thereby promoting EMT. We also examined the subcellular localization of MX2 mutants and found that MX2-ΔN and MX2-K131A mutants were exclusively localized in the cytoplasm, failing to translocate into the nucleus and subsequently losing their pro-migratory and pro-invasive effects (Fig.  G, H). Interestingly, MX2 exhibited oncogenic properties opposite to those of IRF1. Given that IRF1 is primarily localized in the nucleus, we hypothesize that IRF1 overexpression may inhibit cell proliferation via the PI3K/AKT pathway while also sequestering MX2 in a nuclear complex, thereby silencing MX2’s oncogenic effects (Fig.  ).

 

 

When IRF1 is overexpressed, it inhibits AKT phosphorylation via the PI3K pathway, thereby suppressing gastric cancer cell migration. Additionally, overexpressed IRF1 binds to MX2 in the nucleus to form a protein complex, which inhibits the EMT process in gastric cancer cells. Conversely, when IRF1 expression is low, PI3K-AKT phosphorylation promotes gastric cancer progression, and free nuclear MX2 influences Snail to enhance the EMT process in gastric cancer cells.

## Discussion
Gastric cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide, necessitating the identification of reliable prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Currently, clinical prognostic markers such as HER2 status, microsatellite instability (MSI), and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) status have been integrated into GC management, guiding both prognosis and treatment strategies [ , ]. Additionally, emerging molecular markers, including PD-L1 expression, tumor-infiltrating immune cells, and immune-related gene signatures, have been proposed to refine patient stratification [ ]. However, there remains a critical need to identify novel biomarkers that can provide more accurate prognostic information and potential therapeutic avenues.
In this study, we identified IRF1 as a potential prognostic biomarker in GC. IRF1 is a transcription factor that plays a crucial role in immune regulation, apoptosis, and tumor suppression. In various cancers, IRF1 has been reported to exert both tumor-suppressive and oncogenic effects depending on the tumor type and microenvironment [ – ]. In gastric cancer, emerging evidence suggests that IRF1 is involved in immune responses and tumor progression, but its precise function remains controversial. Some studies indicate that IRF1 acts as a tumor suppressor by promoting apoptosis and enhancing anti-tumor immunity, while others suggest that it may contribute to inflammation-associated tumorigenesis [ ].
Our analysis demonstrated that IRF1 expression is significantly elevated in tumor tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues, with high IRF1 expression correlating positively with improved patient survival. Our use of TCGA datasets follows established analytical frameworks applied in prior cancer studies [ ], while acknowledging the technical and biological biases inherent to bulk transcriptomic data [ ]. Functional assays revealed that IRF1 inhibits GC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion while promoting apoptosis. Additionally, animal experiments confirmed these findings, showing that IRF1 overexpression leads to reduced tumor growth and metastatic potential in vivo. Furthermore, we constructed an IRF1Δ deletion overexpression plasmid lacking the DBD domain to explore the cellular pathways involved in IRF1’s tumor-suppressive role in gastric cancer. Our study revealed that IRF1 affects gastric cancer cell proliferation and migration through the PI3K-AKT-ERK1/2 pathway. However, when the DBD domain was deleted, IRF1 lost its influence on the PI3K pathway. Similarly, IRF1 overexpression activated Caspase-3/9 to promote gastric cancer cell apoptosis, whereas overexpression of IRF1 lacking the DBD domain had no effect on Caspase-3/9 protein expression. This demonstrates that the tumor-suppressive function of IRF1 in gastric cancer is closely related to the DBD region.
Our findings reveal a cell-intrinsic tumor-suppressive mechanism of IRF1, that may inform the design of future therapies targeting both cancer cells and the tumor microenvironment. This aligns with the conceptual framework proposed in recent literature comparing direct targeting of malignant cells versus modulation of stromal and immune components [ ]. A similar strategy was employed in recent VGSC studies, where pan-cancer analyses identified SCN3B as a glioma biomarker, followed by functional validation of its role in tumor cell motility [ – ]. This supports the value of integrating bioinformatics, clinical data, and experimental models, as done in our study.
On the other hand, we explored the role of IRF1 in modulating GC progression and discovered its interaction with MX2. MX2 is an interferon-stimulated GTPase with nuclear localization properties that plays a crucial role in antiviral defense and cellular signaling [ , ]. Recent studies suggest that MX2 may have diverse functions in cancer, with its role varying across different tumor types [ , ], however, its specific role in gastric cancer remains unclear. In gastric cancer, we found that MX2 overexpression promotes gastric cancer cell migration and invasion while enhancing N-cadherin and Snail expression, thereby facilitating the EMT. The MX2 protein consists of an N-terminal GTPase (G) domain, a bundle signaling element (BSE) composed of three antiparallel helices, and a stalk domain formed by four antiparallel helices. The N-terminal GTPase domain binds and hydrolyzes GTP, while the BSE domain connects and transmits signals between the GTPase and stalk domains.
Based on MX2’s protein structure, we constructed truncated mutant overexpression plasmids, along with additional site-directed mutant overexpression plasmids. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments showed that deletion of the N-terminal GTPase domain abolished the interaction between IRF1 and MX2, indicating that the G domain serves as the interaction site. Furthermore, we generated nuclear localization signal (NLS) truncation mutants and K131A point mutants, with the latter causing GTP-binding defects and preventing nuclear localization. Our experimental results suggest that MX2’s biological function in gastric cancer is closely linked to its nuclear localization. Loss of nuclear localization due to NLS truncation or K131A mutation also resulted in the loss of MX2’s ability to promote gastric cancer cell migration and invasion. Thus, considering the co-localization of IRF1 and MX2 observed in immunofluorescence assays, along with IRF1’s nuclear localization, we propose a dual regulatory mechanism by which IRF1 modulates GC progression. On one hand, IRF1 overexpression inhibits GC metastasis by suppressing the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. On the other hand, IRF1 forms a nuclear complex with MX2, potentially suppressing MX2-driven epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Conversely, in conditions of IRF1 downregulation, enhanced PI3K/AKT phosphorylation promotes GC progression, while free nuclear MX2 may interact with Snail to facilitate EMT and metastatic processes. However, these findings likely represent only a fraction of IRF1’s regulatory functions, highlighting the need for further investigation into its molecular mechanisms.
While our study provides novel insights into the role of IRF1 in GC, several questions remain to be addressed. Further research is required to elucidate the precise molecular interactions between IRF1 and MX2 and their downstream signaling pathways. From a clinical perspective, larger patient cohorts are needed to confirm the prognostic relevance of IRF1. Translating these findings into therapeutic applications will also require safety and efficacy assessments in human models. Future investigations should explore whether targeting the IRF1–MX2 axis offers a novel therapeutic strategy for gastric cancer, and assess the potential of small-molecule IRF1 activators or their combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors to enhance treatment efficacy.

## Conclusions
Our findings suggest that IRF1 serves as a tumor suppressor in GC by inhibiting cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, while promoting apoptosis. Moreover, its interaction with MX2 and regulation of PI3K/AKT signaling highlight its multifaceted role in GC progression. Given its prognostic significance, IRF1 holds promise as a biomarker for GC risk stratification. Furthermore, our findings open new avenues for potential therapeutic strategies, particularly through the combination of interferon-based therapies with targeted treatments to enhance the anti-tumor effects of IRF1. Personalized treatment approaches incorporating IRF1 modulation may improve patient outcomes and provide a novel avenue for GC management.
