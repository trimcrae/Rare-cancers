## Abstract
The abnormal expression of PHAX was observed in esophageal cancer, however, its specific function and mechanism remain to be further elucidated. We demonstrated that PHAX, LIN28B, and PBX3 were upregulated in esophageal cancer, while TET2 was downregulated. Elevated PHAX correlated with adverse outcomes among esophageal cancer patients. PHAX or PBX3 knockdown not only inhibited esophageal cancer cell proliferation, and promoted apoptosis and autophagy in vitro, but it also repressed tumor growth and lung metastasis in mice. Mechanically, PHAX stabilized PBX3 mRNA through interacting with LIN28B. PBX3 directly bound to the TET2 promoter region and inhibited its expression. In conclusion, PHAX directly bound to LIN28B and enhanced LIN28B‐mediated stabilization of PBX3 mRNA, leading to upregulation of PBX3. PBX3 then transcriptionally repressed TET2 expression to promote esophageal cancer cell proliferation, and suppress apoptosis and autophagy. Targeting this signaling cascade could represent a promising therapeutic strategy for esophageal cancer.
Keywords: autophagy, esophageal cancer, LIN28B, PBX3, PHAX, TET2

PHAX was upregulated in esophageal cancer and promoted cancer cell proliferation, while inhibiting apoptosis and autophagy. PHAX contributed to tumor growth and lung metastasis through suppressing autophagy in vivo. PHAX enhanced LIN28B‐mediated PBX3 mRNA stability by directly binding to LIN28B. PHAX promoted esophageal cancer cell proliferation and repressed apoptosis and autophagy by regulating the LIN28B/PBX3 axis. PBX3 facilitated esophageal cancer cell proliferation and inhibited apoptosis and autophagy through binding to the TET2 promoter region.

## INTRODUCTION
Esophageal cancer is an aggressive malignancy, and although multimodal therapies have improved outcomes, the overall prognosis remains poor. Autophagy is a cellular process that involves the degradation and recycling of damaged or unnecessary components. There are reports suggesting connection between autophagy and the development of esophageal cancer. For instance, Salvia chinensia Benth. suppressed esophageal cancer by promoting autophagy. Dehydrocostus lactone effectively repressed esophageal cancer cell proliferation by inducing apoptosis and autophagy. Therefore, the exploration of autophagy offers a promising avenue for more effective treatments against esophageal cancer.
Phosphorylated adapter RNA export protein (PHAX) is a nuclear export adapter that facilitates the nuclear export of certain RNA molecules. A previous study has implicated PHAX in activating the binding activity of the mRNA cap complex and maintaining RNA stability. Moreover, PHAX was reported to be highly expressed in metastatic clear cell renal cell carcinoma. Interestingly, data from UALCAN highlighted the notable overexpression of PHAX in esophageal cancer tissues. Furthermore, the upregulation of PHAX was associated with advanced tumor stage, lymph node metastasis, and poor overall survival. These findings underscore the importance of investigating the dysregulated expression of PHAX in driving esophageal cancer progression.
To explore the role of PHAX in preserving mRNA stability, a bioinformatics analysis using BioGRID revealed a potential interaction between PHAX and LIN28B. LIN28B is a conserved RNA‐binding protein that exhibits high expression in embryonic tissues but is silenced within healthy adult cells. Intriguingly, LIN28B was aberrantly reactivated in diverse human cancers, such as breast cancer, colorectal cancer, and esophageal cancer, and its increased expression correlated with clinical tumor stage and poor prognosis. In addition, as an RNA‐binding protein, LIN28B can stabilize the mRNA of downstream genes, such as binding and posttranscriptionally regulating CLDN1 mRNA. Therefore, we hypothesized that PHAX might interact with LIN28B protein, thereby affecting the stability of downstream mRNAs and regulating esophageal cancer development.
PBX3, a pre‐B‐cell leukemia homeobox (PBX) transcription factor, is implicated as an oncogenic driver. For instance, PBX3 overexpression promoted cell growth and metastasis in laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma. Knockdown of PBX3 was suggested to repress proliferation, migration, lactate production, and glucose consumption in breast cancer. Furthermore, the high expression of PBX3 was observed in esophageal cancer. Interestingly, the ENCORI database predicted a potential binding site between PBX3 mRNA and LIN28B. As LIN28B could bind and stabilize its target mRNAs, it is plausible that LIN28B enhanced the stability of PBX3 mRNA, therefore potentially promoting esophageal cancer progression.
Accumulating evidence indicated that PBX3 acted as a transcription factor. Here, the JASPAR database revealed potential PBX3 binding sites within the ten‐eleven translocation 2 (TET2) promoter region. TET2 aberrant expression has been frequently identified in various malignancies. For instance, TET2 enhanced antitumor immunity and vascular remodeling in liver cancer. A previous study indicated the downregulation of TET2 in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma. Notably, knockdown of TET2 was suggested to inhibit autophagy. He et al. suggested that TET2 suppressed mTORC1 signaling to promote autophagy. However, the link between the PBX3/TET2 axis and esophageal cancer progression remains unexplored.
Based on all the above evidence, we formulated a hypothesis that PHAX contributed to the LIN28B‐mediated enhancement of PBX3 mRNA stability, resulted in the upregulation of PBX3, therefore repressing transcriptional expression of TET2, ultimately promoting cell proliferation while inhibiting apoptosis and the autophagy process in esophageal cancer.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1.

## Clinical samples
Thirty‐two pairs of esophageal tumor tissues and adjacent normal esophageal samples were obtained from esophageal cancer individuals who had not received prior radiotherapy or chemotherapy at Xiangya Hospital, Central South University. All specimens were placed in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for further experiments. Ethical permission was granted by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (202307–31) and informed consent was obtained from all patients. The clinicopathologic characteristics of esophageal cancer patients are shown in Table  .
 
2.2.

## Cell culture and treatment
Esophageal cancer cell lines (KYSE‐410, TE‐10, TE‐11, Eca‐109) and normal human esophageal epithelial cell line (Het‐1A) were purchased from ATCC and cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS (Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Invitrogen) at 37°C and supplied with 5% CO2.

2.3.

## Cell transfection
To modulate gene expression, shRNA oligonucleotides targeting PHAX, LIN28B, PBX3, and TET2 (sh‐PHAX, sh‐LIN28B, sh‐PBX3, and sh‐TET2, respectively) were inserted into pGLVH1 vector, purchased from GenePharma. For ectopic overexpression of PHAX, PBX3, and LIN28B, their full length coding sequences were amplified and subcloned into pcDNA3.1 (Invitrogen) vector. KYSE‐410 and Eca‐109 cells were transfected with the respective plasmid constructs using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The sequences of shRNAs of PHAX, LIN28B, PBX3, and TET2, and information regarding PHAX, LIN28B, and PBX3 overexpression vectors are shown in Data  .
When packaged lentivirus particles by 293T cells for further experiments, the expression vectors and the helper plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G (Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA) were co‐transfected into 293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000. Lentiviral particles in the supernatant were harvested at 48 h after transfection. For viral infection, target cells were cultured in 6‐well plates and infected with the harvested lentiviral particles, using polybrene (8 μg/mL; Beyotime) to enhance infection efficiency. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium, and cells were further cultured for 48–72 h before selection with puromycin (2 μg/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for stable cell line generation.

2.4.

## Animal experiments
All procedures involving mice were authorized by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (202307–16). Male 4‐week‐old BALB/c nude mice, weighing between 16 and 20 g, were acquired from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. These mice were randomly assigned to four experimental groups including control, short hairpin RNA for negative control (sh‐NC), sh‐PHAX, and sh‐PBX3 (n = 6 mice in each group). Subsequently, mice received s.c. injection of either KYSE‐410 or Eca‐109 cells (1 × 106 cells in 200 μL PBS/mouse) into their dorsal flank, which stably infected with sh‐NC, sh‐PHAX, or sh‐PBX3 lentiviral vector. Tumor dimensions were measured using calipers to calculate the volumes, based on the standard formula: 0.5 × length (L) × width (W)2. Following an experimental period of 25 days, mice were killed by CO2 inhalation followed by cervical dislocation to ensure death. Tumors were surgically removed for further analyses.
To establish the metastatic tumor model, KYSE‐410 or Eca‐109 cells (5 × 105 cells in 150 μL PBS/mouse) stably expressing sh‐NC/sh‐PHAX lentiviral vector were administered through tail vein injection. After 4 weeks, the lungs of mice in different groups were harvested and the tissue sections were stained with an H&E kit (ab245880; Abcam). Metastatic nodules in lung tissues were counted under a microscope.
More methods are in Data  , and the original data of western blot analyses are shown in Data  .

## RESULTS
3.1.

## 
PHAX highly expressed in esophageal cancer and related to poor prognosis of esophageal cancer patients
The UALCAN database revealed the expression of PHAX in various cancers, in which upregulated PHAX was also found in esophageal cancer (ESCA) tissues (Figure  ). Its expression was significantly upregulated when assessed across different variables including sample types, individual cancer stages, and nodal metastasis status (Figure  ). Moreover, upregulated PHAX was associated with poor overall survival among esophageal cancer patients (Figure  ). In line with the bioinformatics findings, our clinical study validated the overexpression of PHAX mRNA in cancer specimens (Figure  ). Notably, PHAX mRNA level was further augmented in primary esophageal cancer tissues from patients with metastatic disease compared to those without detectable metastases (Figure  ). Furthermore, the protein level of PHAX was also increased in esophageal cancer tissues (Figure  ). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis indicated that esophageal cancer patients with high PHAX level had shorter overall survival (Figure  ). As shown in Table  , the high PHAX expression level was associated with TNM stage and lymph node metastasis in our clinical samples. Consistent with the clinical observations, PHAX mRNA (Figure  ) and protein (Figure  ) levels in esophageal cancer cell lines were upregulated. Among these cancer cell lines, KYSE‐410 and Eca‐109 showed the highest PHAX expression and were thus selected for subsequent functional studies. Collectively, our multidimensional analyses substantiated the aberrant overexpression of PHAX in esophageal cancer. The robust association of PHAX upregulation with advanced tumor stage, nodal dissemination, and dismal prognosis underscored its potential oncogenic role in esophageal cancer.
 
3.2.

## 
PHAX knockdown repressed proliferation of esophageal cancer cells, and promoted their apoptosis and autophagy
We then undertook RNA interference‐mediated knockdown of PHAX in KYSE‐410 and Eca‐109 cells. First, efficient silencing of PHAX was validated, shown by mRNA (Figure  ) and protein (Figure  ) levels. Subsequently, knockdown of PHAX repressed the proliferative ability of esophageal cancer cells, as evidenced by reduced cell viability (Figure  ) and decreased ability to form colonies (Figure  ). Concomitantly, PHAX knockdown triggered a pronounced increase in apoptotic cell death (Figure  ). Given the intricate interplay between apoptosis and autophagy, we next investigated the impact of PHAX on autophagy. Intriguingly, PHAX silencing promoted the conversion of cytosolic LC3 I to its lipidated form LC3 II and increased the expression of Beclin‐1 and ATG5, accompanied by a decrease in the level of p62 (Figure  ). These molecular signatures, coupled with the augmented formation of LC3 puncta (Figure  ), collectively indicated that PHAX depletion potentiated autophagy. Additionally, PHAX knockdown in KYSE‐410 and Eca‐109 cells showed a marked increase in the colocalization of autophagosome marker LC3 and lysosomal marker LAMP2 (Figure  ), indicating enhanced autophagosome–lysosome fusion events. These findings unveiled a pivotal role for PHAX knockdown in suppressing esophageal cancer cell proliferation, and sustaining apoptosis and autophagy concomitantly.
 
3.3.

## 
PHAX knockdown repressed tumor growth and lung metastasis in nude mice
We extended our validation to mouse experiments, and KYSE‐410 and Eca‐109 cells stably expressing sh‐PHAX or sh‐NC were subcutaneously implanted into nude mice. Remarkably, PHAX silencing potently reduced tumor volume and weight (Figure  ). PHAX knockdown inhibited proliferation marker Ki‐67 levels in tumor tissues, indicating its antiproliferative effect in vivo (Figure  ). Furthermore, the expression of PHAX in tumor tissues was inhibited after transfecting with sh‐PHAX (Figure  ). Consistent with our cellular observations, PHAX‐silenced tumors exhibited the increased LC3 II/LC3 I ratio, upregulation of Beclin‐1 and ATG5, and decreased p62 level (Figure  ), indicative of an enhanced autophagic process. PHAX knockdown also potently suppressed the metastatic dissemination of esophageal cancer cells to the lungs, which led to a striking reduction in the number of metastatic nodules (Figure  ). In summary, PHAX silence suppressed tumor growth and lung metastasis, while inducing autophagy in nude mice.
 
3.4.

## 
PHAX promoted LIN28B‐mediated PBX3 mRNA stability by directly binding to LIN28B

We further used the BioGRID database to explore potential PHAX interaction partners and identified LIN28B as a putative candidate. Intriguingly, the ENCORI database predicted LIN28B binding sites within PBX3 mRNA. Consistently, the upregulation of LIN28B and PBX3 in esophageal cancer tissues (Figure  ) and cells (Figure  ) was observed. Furthermore, a positive correlation was evident among PHAX, LIN28B, and PBX3 expression in the clinical specimens (Figure  ). Subsequently, bioinformatics findings showed that PBX3 was overexpressed in esophageal cancer tumor samples (Figure  ), and significantly reduced the overall survival rate of patients (Figure  ). To functionally validate the interaction between LIN28B and PBX3 mRNA, we modulated LIN28B levels in esophageal cancer cells through transfecting with LIN28B overexpression or silence vector (Figure  ). Remarkably, LIN28B overexpression enhanced the stability of PBX3 mRNA, while its knockdown exerted the opposite effect (Figure  ). Interestingly, LIN28B overexpression did not change the luciferase activities of either PBX3‐WT or PBX3‐mutant (MUT) vector (Figure  ), indicating that LIN28B stabilized PBX3 mRNA without affecting its transcription. Furthermore, LIN28B Ab‐bound complex resulted in a significant enrichment of PBX3 mRNA (Figure  ). The existence of LIN28B protein within the PBX3 sense group of RNA pull‐down samples was also observed (Figure  ). Consistently, PBX3 mRNA (Figure  ) and protein (Figure  ) levels were increased after overexpressing LIN28B, while decreased by LIN28B knockdown. Having established the link between LIN28B and PBX3 mRNA, we next investigated whether PHAX modulated this axis. As indicated in Figure  , LIN28B was readily detected in PHAX immunoprecipitates, demonstrating the interaction of these two proteins. Subsequently, knockdown of PHAX was observed to reduce the enrichment of PBX3 mRNA by LIN28B, while overexpression of PHAX led to opposite effect (Figure  ). Additionally, overexpression of PHAX stabilized PBX3 mRNA, while LIN28B knockdown blocked this effect (Figure  ). In conclusion, PHAX promoted LIN28B‐mediated stabilization of PBX3 mRNA by directly binding to LIN28B, thereby increasing PBX3 expression in esophageal cancer cells.
 
3.5.

## 
PHAX regulated esophageal cancer cell proliferative, apoptotic, and autophagic capacities by directly binding to LIN28B

We then undertook rescue experiments wherein KYSE‐410 and Eca‐109 cells were cotransfected with PHAX overexpression and sh‐LIN28B vectors. PHAX overexpression did not impact LIN28B expression but substantially upregulated PBX3 (Figure  ). Knockdown of LIN28B did not affect PHAX expression but reversed the promoting effect of PHAX overexpression on PBX3 expression (Figure  ). Moreover, PHAX overexpression increased the enrichment of PBX3 mRNA in LIN28B‐precipitated complexes, but this effect was abolished by LIN28B knockdown (Figure  ). Functionally, PHAX overexpression facilitated the cell viability (Figure  ) and proliferation (Figure  ) of cancer cells and suppressed their apoptosis (Figure  ), as expected, LIN28B knockdown reversed these effects (Figure  ). Moreover, PHAX overexpression potently inhibited autophagy, as evidenced by the decreased LC3 II/LC3 I ratio, elevated p62 level (Figure  ), and impaired autophagosome–lysosome fusion (Figure  ). However, these inhibitory effects were reversed by silencing LIN28B (Figure  ). Taken together, PHAX exerted multifaceted roles in esophageal cancer cell proliferation and autophagy by directly interacting with LIN28B.
 
3.6.

## 
LIN28B regulated esophageal cancer cell proliferative, apoptotic, and autophagic capacities by mediating the stability of PBX3 mRNA

The expression levels of LIN28B and PBX3 were decreased after knocking down LIN28B, while PBX3 overexpression had no effects on LIN28B and restored PBX3 expression (Figure  ). Functionally, LIN28B knockdown‐induced cell viability (Figure  ) and proliferation (Figure  ) reduction and apoptosis promotion (Figure  ) were abolished by overexpressing PBX3. Notably, knockdown of LIN28B promoted autophagy in esophageal cancer cells, manifested by increasing LC3 II/LC3 I ratio, decreasing p62 expression level (Figure  ), and enhancing autophagosome–lysosome fusion (Figure  ), while overexpression of PBX3 abolished this promoting effect. These findings unequivocally established PBX3 as a crucial downstream effector of LIN28B in modulating the proliferative, apoptotic, and autophagic responses in esophageal cancer cells.

3.7.

## 
PBX3 knockdown inhibited tumor growth and promoted autophagy in vivo
To further validate the role of PBX3 in esophageal cancer progression, we established a xenograft model using KYSE‐410 and Eca‐109 cells with PBX3 knockdown. As shown in Figure  , PBX3 knockdown significantly reduced tumor size, volume, and weight compared to control groups. Immunohistochemistry analysis revealed the decreased Ki‐67 expression in tumors of the sh‐PBX3 group, indicating reduced proliferation (Figure  ). Moreover, we observed increased LC3 II/LC3 I ratio, as well as Beclin‐1 and ATG5 levels accompanied by decreased p62 level in the sh‐PBX3 group, consistent with the enhanced autophagy in tumors (Figure  ). These results confirmed that PBX3 knockdown suppressed tumor growth and promoted autophagy in vivo, similar to the effects observed with PHAX knockdown.
 
3.8.

## 
PBX3 regulated esophageal cancer cell proliferative, apoptotic, and autophagic capacities by binding to TET2 promoter
To unravel the downstream target of PBX3, we used the JASPAR database and identified that TET2 acted as a potential transcriptional target of PBX3. The potential binding motif of transcription factor PBX3 and the specific promoter region sequence of TET2 bound with PBX3 are shown in Figure  respectively. Next, dual‐luciferase reporter and ChIP assays substantiated the direct interaction between PBX3 and the TET2 promoter region. Specifically, knockdown of PBX3 increased TET2‐WT luciferase activities, but did not affect TET2‐MUT (Figure  ). The promoter region of TET2 was enriched by PBX3 protein (Figure  ). Moreover, PBX3 overexpression was observed to reduce TET2 level, while transfection with sh‐PBX3 led to opposite results (Figure  ). Additionally, the upregulation of TET2 caused by silencing PBX3 was reversed by knocking down TET2 (Figure  ). Remarkably, TET2 silence abrogated the antiproliferative (Figure  ) and proapoptotic (Figure  ) effects triggered by PBX3 knockdown. Additionally, PBX3 knockdown promoted the LC3 II/LC3 I ratio and repressed p62 levels; TET2 knockdown abolished these changes as expected (Figure  ). In summary, PBX3 inhibition suppressed esophageal cancer cell proliferation and promoted their apoptosis and autophagy by binding to TET2 promoter.

## DISCUSSION
It is imperative for researchers to gain a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying esophageal cancer and devise more efficient diagnostic and prognostic approaches. This study unveiled a novel axis that plays a pivotal role in esophageal cancer pathogenesis. PHAX directly bound to LIN28B, increasing its interaction with PBX3 mRNA and thereby promoting PBX3 mRNA stabilization and upregulation. The elevated PBX3 then transcriptionally repressed TET2, ultimately potentiating esophageal cancer cell proliferation, while concomitantly suppressing apoptosis and autophagic processes.
Emerging evidence suggests that PHAX also plays a significant role in cancer development. PHAX was not only overexpressed in clear cell renal cell carcinoma but was also essential for the efficacy of the antiangiogenic drug sunitinib. Surprisingly, we first observed significant upregulation of PHAX in esophageal cancer, particularly in metastatic cases. This upregulation was robustly associated with poor prognosis among esophageal cancer patients, underlining its clinical relevance. Further investigation into the biological functions of PHAX revealed its multifunctional roles. Knockdown of PHAX not only inhibited esophageal cancer cell proliferation and promoted apoptosis and autophagy in vitro, but also repressed tumor growth and facilitated autophagy in nude mice. Recently, the roles of autophagy in the pathogenesis of esophageal cancer were discussed. PSMD2 activated the mTOR pathway by upregulating ASS1 to inhibit autophagy, thereby promoting esophageal cancer cell growth. Meng et al. indicated that ciRS‐7 accelerated esophageal cancer progression through repressing autophagy by sponging microRNA‐1299. Our findings highlighted the relation between PHAX and autophagy in esophageal cancer for the first time. Interestingly, our in vivo studies also revealed that PHAX knockdown inhibited lung metastasis of esophageal cancer. However, it is important to note that the mechanisms underlying this antimetastatic effect may differ from those governing tumor growth and autophagy. We believed that the specific downstream mechanisms by which PHAX influenced lung metastasis and whether these were related to autophagy required further investigation.
Mechanistically, we discovered that PHAX increased PBX3 mRNA stability through its interaction with LIN28B. LIN28B was proven to be frequently overexpressed in cancers and correlated with unfavorable prognosis. The upregulation of LIN28B was observed in esophageal cancer, which was consistent with our results. Notably, high expression of LIN28B correlated with lymph node metastasis and poor prognosis in esophageal cancer. Previous reports emphasized the pivotal role of aberrant LIN28A/B and let‐7 regulatory loops in oncogenesis. Intriguingly, LIN28B was observed to suppress autophagy in glioblastoma cells. Our findings similarly supported the inhibitory impact of high LIN28B on autophagy in esophageal cancer cells. Furthermore, LIN28B was proven to regulate mRNA stability. , As our findings indicated, the enhanced PBX3 mRNA stability in cells overexpressing PHAX was suppressed by LIN28B knockdown. PBX3 often plays a crucial role in both development and persistence of malignant characteristics. Elevated PBX3 expression was associated with reduced overall survival in cancers, such as colorectal cancer, lung cancer, and hepatocellular carcinoma. In the study of esophageal cancer, PBX3 was reported to be upregulated and PBX3 overexpression promoted esophageal cancer proliferation and invasion. , We also proved that PBX3 was upregulated in esophageal cancer tissues and its high level correlated with poor overall survival of patients, and not only increased the viability and proliferation of esophageal cancer cells but also inhibited autophagy. PBX3 knockdown inhibited tumor growth and promoted autophagy in vivo as well. The relationship between PHAX, LIN28B, and PBX3 mRNA has not been previously reported, our study established for the first time a triadic association among these molecules, uncovering a novel axis in the molecular landscape of esophageal cancer pathogenesis. Interestingly, LIN28B was reported to stabilize several other mRNAs, including CDX2, c‐MYC, and HIF‐1α, all of which were highly expressed in esophageal cancer. , , Consequently, investigating the regulatory effects of LIN28B on other potential target genes could offer valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the occurrence and progression of esophageal cancer. This will be a focus of our future research.
Considering that PBX3 plays important roles in cancer progression by regulating downstream gene transcription, we discovered and validated the interaction between PBX3 and the TET2 promoter region, and PBX3 transcriptionally suppressed TET2 expression. TET2 was considered as a tumor suppressor in various cancers. , Notably, recent research has increasingly delved into the involvement of TET2 in cancer cell autophagy. For instance, He et al. indicated that TET2 inhibited cancer cell growth and promoted autophagy by suppressing mTORC1 signaling. Herein, the downregulation of TET2 was observed in esophageal cancer, which is consistent with previous studies. , Importantly, TET2 knockdown reversed the reduction in esophageal cancer cell proliferation and the promotion of apoptosis and autophagy induced by silencing PBX3. We therefore concluded that PHAX aggravated esophageal cancer by facilitating cancer cell proliferation and repressing apoptosis and autophagy by regulating the LIN28B/PBX3/TET2 axis. However, more in vivo data are needed to support this conclusion, which will be our main research direction in the future. Meanwhile, the reasons for upregulated PHAX in esophageal cancer require future study. Furthermore, TET2 has functions extending beyond autophagy, such as regulation of immune response and inflammation, glycolysis, DNA repair, angiogenesis, and drug sensitivity, suggesting the possible roles of the PHAX/LIN28B/PBX3/TET2 axis in multiple mechanisms except for autophagy.
Notably, autophagy plays neutral, tumor suppressive, or promotive roles in different stages of cancer. Specifically, in the early stages of cancer, autophagy functions as a tumor suppressor by removing damaged organelles and proteins, thus preventing genomic instability and tumor initiation. Conversely, during advanced stages of cancer, autophagy supports tumor survival by helping cancer cells cope with metabolic stress and maintaining cellular homeostasis. , In the context of esophageal cancer, autophagy similarly exhibits a dual nature. Several studies have reported this paradoxical role. For instance, Sun et al. indicated that induction of autophagy promoted cancer progression and cisplatin resistance. CLDN1 promoted the proliferation and metastasis of esophageal cancer by triggering autophagy both in vitro and in vivo. It is important to note that the current study has not definitively established whether this double‐edged sword effect of autophagy in esophageal cancer is related to the stages, but we speculate that the PHAX/LIN28B/PBX3/TET2 axis exerts its role on autophagy in early stages of esophageal cancer. This presents an intriguing avenue for future research.
In conclusion, PHAX increased PBX3 expression through enhancing its mRNA stability by interacting with LIN28B, then transcriptionally inhibited TET2 expression, thereby facilitating proliferative ability of cancer cells and suppressing the apoptotic and autophagic abilities in esophageal cancer. Our study identified a compelling association between PHAX and autophagy, shedding light on its pivotal role in esophageal cancer progression and outcomes.
