The poor prognosis associated with ESCC is due primarily to late-stage diagnosis, rapid disease progression, and the development of resistance to standard therapies such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy [ , ]. These challenges require the development of novel therapeutic strategies targeting the molecular drivers of ESCC.
Epigenetic regulation has emerged as a critical factor in the pathogenesis of ESCC, with histone modifications playing a significant role [ – ]. Among the key regulators, JMJD2A (KDM4A), a lysine-specific demethylase, has garnered attention for its role in oncogenesis [ – ]. JMJD2A demethylates histone H3 on lysine 9 (H3K9me3), a mark associated with gene repression, thereby promoting the transcription of genes involved in tumorigenesis [ ]. Elevated levels of JMJD2A have been observed in various cancers, including ESCC, where its expression is correlated with poor clinical outcomes [ ]. To strengthen the rationale for focusing on JMJD2A in ESCC, I first interrogated public patient cohorts. Across TCGA-ESCA (squamous subset) and independent GEO ESCC datasets (GSE53625 and GSE23400), high JMJD2A/KDM4A expression was associated with poorer survival and more aggressive clinicopathologic features, and showed a transcriptomic pattern consistent with ferroptosis suppression based on curated ferroptosis regulators (FerrDb). Mechanistic studies have revealed that JMJD2A contributes to ESCC progression by inhibiting ferroptosis, a form of regulated cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation [ ]. Ferroptosis is a tumor-suppressive mechanism, and its inhibition is associated with increased tumor survival and therapeutic resistance [ – ]. In previous research, JMJD2A was shown to interact with SMARCA4, a chromatin remodeler, to repress the expression of genes critical for ferroptosis, such as ACSL4 and TfR1, while promoting the expression of antiferroptotic proteins such as GPX4. Targeting JMJD2A to restore ferroptosis represents a promising therapeutic approach in ESCC.
In addition to its role in ferroptosis, JMJD2A is a potent driver of epithelial‒mesenchymal transition (EMT), a process by which epithelial cells acquire mesenchymal characteristics, leading to enhanced migratory and invasive abilities. EMT is pivotal in cancer metastasis, with JMJD2A regulating this process by modulating key transcription factors, including ZEB1, which represses E-cadherin and promotes mesenchymal markers such as N-cadherin and vimentin [ – ]. Tumors employ various mechanisms to evade immune surveillance, including upregulation of immune checkpoint proteins such as PD-L1 and secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines, including TGF-β1 and CCL2, which promote macrophage polarization toward the tumor-promoting M2 phenotype [ – ]. M2 macrophages, or tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), support tumor growth, angiogenesis, and metastasis while suppressing antitumor immune responses [ – ]. Consistently, TISIDB/ssGSEA analysis of TCGA-ESCC suggested that tumors with higher KDM4A expression exhibit reduced cytotoxic immune signatures, including CD8 + T cells and NK cells, findings consistent with recent ESCC cohort studies linking JMJD2A to immune evasion and immunotherapy resistance [ ].
Given the multifaceted role of JMJD2A in ESCC progression, ranging from ferroptosis inhibition and EMT induction to immune modulation, targeting this enzyme represents a promising therapeutic strategy [ , ]. RNA interference (RNAi), particularly small interfering RNA (siRNA), provides a precise method for silencing oncogenes such as JMJD2A. However, the clinical application of siRNAs is hindered by challenges related to delivery, stability, and off-target effects. To overcome these obstacles, ultrasound-targeted microbubble destruction (UTMD) has emerged as a novel technique to enhance siRNA delivery directly to tumor tissues [ ]. Recent mechanistic work has implicated JMJD2A in ESCC immune evasion and ferroptosis resistance [ ]. UTMD has been selected because it enables image-guided, spatiotemporally controlled, and repeatable local delivery of nucleic acids while minimizing systemic exposure. The present study aims to translate this target into a therapeutically actionable strategy by developing and validating UTMD-mediated delivery of si-JMJD2A and determining whether localized JMJD2A silencing can induce ferroptosis, restrain EMT-driven metastasis, and improve responsiveness to PD-L1 blockade.
UTMD uses microbubbles as carriers for therapeutic agents, which are then targeted and destroyed by focused ultrasound, thereby releasing their cargo at the desired site. This method significantly improves siRNA delivery efficiency, reducing systemic exposure and off-target effects. In this study, I synthesized and characterized siRNA-loaded UTMD systems targeting JMJD2A (si-JMJD2A) to evaluate their therapeutic potential in ESCC. The integration of siRNA technology with UTMD represents a promising approach for overcoming the limitations of conventional treatments and advancing personalized cancer therapy.

## Methods and materials

## Synthesis and characterization of si-JMJD2A-loaded UTMD
Synthesis of Microbubbles: UTMD loaded with si-JMJD2A was prepared according to established protocols, with modifications to optimize siRNA loading efficiency. The microbubbles were formulated with a lipid mixture containing 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DSPC, Avanti Polar Lipids, USA), 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[methoxy(polyethylene glycol)−2000] (DSPE-PEG2000, Avanti Polar Lipids, USA), and cholesterol (Sigma‒Aldrich, USA) at a molar ratio of 9:1:2. Lipids were dissolved in chloroform, and the solvent was evaporated under a stream of nitrogen to form a lipid film, which was subsequently hydrated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4, Gibco, USA) containing si-JMJD2A (Dharmacon, USA). The mixture was sonicated under controlled conditions (Branson Sonifier, USA) to produce microbubbles encapsulating the siRNA.
Characterization of Microbubbles: The size distribution and zeta potential of the synthesized microbubbles were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and electrophoretic light scattering, respectively, using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, UK). The size distribution profiles were analyzed for empty UTMD, si-empty-loaded UTMD, and si-JMJD2A-loaded UTMD, yielding mean particle sizes of 267.06 ± 19.37 nm, 304 ± 29.52 nm, and 311.74 ± 26.27 nm, respectively (Fig.  B-C). Zeta potential measurements revealed surface charges of 3.15 ± 1.16 mV for empty UTMD, 3.07 ± 2.24 mV for si-empty-loaded UTMD, and 3.38 ± 1.93 mV for si-JMJD2A-loaded UTMD (Fig.  D). Stability studies were performed by storing microbubbles at 4 °C for 7 days, and their size and zeta potential were monitored to ensure their stability.

 

siRNA loading efficiency: The encapsulation efficiency of si-JMJD2A within UTMD was quantified via a Quant-iT™ RiboGreen® RNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, USA) after the microbubbles were lysed with ethanol. The loading efficiency was calculated by comparing the amount of siRNA encapsulated within the microbubbles to the initial amount added during synthesis.

## Cell culture and siRNA transfection
Cell Lines and Culture Conditions: The ESCC cell lines K450 and K150 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA) and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Sigma‒Aldrich, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, USA) under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). The cells were regularly tested for mycoplasma contamination via the MycoAlert™ Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza, Switzerland). The cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates and incubated overnight. The following day, si-JMJD2A-loaded UTMD or si-empty UTMD was added to the cells at a final siRNA concentration of 100 nM. Ultrasound irradiation was performed using a therapeutic ultrasound system (Sonidel SP100, Sonidel Ltd., Ireland) at 1 MHz, 2 W/cm², and 50% duty cycle for 3 min. The transfection efficiency and gene silencing effects were evaluated at 24, 48, and 72 h posttransfection via quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‒PCR) and Western blotting. Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) was used as a positive control for siRNA transfection according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To validate that the observed phenotypes were specifically attributable to JMJD2A knockdown, an siRNA-resistant JMJD2A expression construct (JMJD2Ares) was generated by introducing silent nucleotide substitutions within the si-JMJD2A target region without altering the encoded amino-acid sequence. K450 and K150 cells were first transfected with JMJD2Ares plasmid or empty vector, and 24 h later, the same cells were treated with UTMD-delivered si-JMJD2A (100 nM) or si-NC under the ultrasound conditions described above. Successful JMJD2Ares re-expression under si-JMJD2A treatment was confirmed only at the mRNA level using qRT–PCR. Rescue effects were then assessed using the same downstream assays described in this study for ferroptosis-related phenotypes and EMT/metastatic traits. The silent-mutation design and the siRNA target region used for resistance are provided in the new Supplementary Table (Table ).

## qRT‒PCR
Total RNA was extracted from treated cells via the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany). cDNA synthesis was performed via a high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Applied Biosystems, USA). qRT‒PCR was carried out on a StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) via TaqMan® Gene Expression Assays for JMJD2A (Assay ID: Hs00364305_m1; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). GAPDH was used as an internal control (Assay ID: Hs02786624_g1; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Relative gene expression was calculated via the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method.

## WB
Protein lysates were prepared using RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche, Switzerland). The protein concentration was determined via a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were resolved on SDS‒PAGE gels, transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA), and probed with primary antibodies against JMJD2A (1:1000, Abcam, UK), GPX4 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), ACSL4 (1:1000, Abcam, UK), TfR1 (1:1000, Abcam, UK), and β-actin (1:2000, Sigma‒Aldrich, USA). The blots were visualized via an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (GE Healthcare, USA), and densitometric analysis was performed via ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

## Cell viability
Following siRNA transfection, ferroptosis was induced by treating cells with 100 µM erastin (Sigma‒Aldrich, USA) for 24 h. Cell viability was assessed via the CCK-8 assay (Dojindo Laboratories, Japan). Lipid peroxidation, a hallmark of ferroptosis, was measured via the BODIPY™ 581/591 C11 probe (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), followed by flow cytometry analysis (BD FACSCanto II, BD Biosciences, USA).

## Colony formation assay
Transfected cells were seeded at a density of 500 cells per well in 6-well plates and cultured for 14 days. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.5% crystal violet, and counted under a light microscope (Olympus, Japan).

## Senescence-associated β-galactosidase staining
The cells were stained via a Senescence β-Galactosidase Staining Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The cells were fixed, stained overnight at 37 °C, and observed under a phase-contrast microscope (Nikon, Japan).

## DNA damage and apoptosis assays
DNA damage was assessed by immunofluorescence staining for γ-H2AX via a primary antibody (1:500, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) and a secondary antibody conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 (1:1000, Invitrogen, USA). Images were acquired using a confocal laser-scanning microscope (Leica TCS SP8, Leica Microsystems, Germany). Apoptotic cells were detected using the TUNEL assay (In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit, Roche, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## In vivo tumor growth and metastasis studies
All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Kangda College of Nanjing Medical University and conducted in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Six-week-old female BALB/c nude mice and C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratories (USA) and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and ad libitum access to food and water. For the subcutaneous tumor models, 1 × 106 K450 or K150 cells were injected subcutaneously into the right flank of BALB/c nude mice. The tumor volume was measured every 3 days using calipers and calculated using the formula: volume = (L × W²)/2, where L is the length, and W is the width of the tumor. Once the tumors reached an average size of 100 mm³, the mice were randomly assigned to receive tail vein injections of si-JMJD2A-loaded UTMD (200 µL, containing 50 µg of siRNA) or si-Empty-loaded UTMD, followed by ultrasound irradiation (Sonidel SP100, Sonidel Ltd., Ireland) as described previously. Treatments were administered on days 7, 14, and 21 post-tumor inoculation. Tumor growth was monitored for 35 days, and the mice were euthanized for tissue collection. For the metastasis studies, 1 × 108 K450 or K150 cells were injected into the tail vein of BALB/c nude mice. The mice were treated with si-JMJD2A-loaded UTMD or control siRNA as described above on days 7, 14, 21, and 28 postinjection. On day 35, the mice were euthanized, and the lungs were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with H&E. The number of metastatic nodules was counted under a dissecting microscope (Leica, Germany). The committee’s limits for mouse tumor studies were: subcutaneous tumors not to exceed 1.5 cm in any single diameter or ≤ 1,500–2,000 mm³ in volume (V = L × W²/2), no ulceration/necrosis, and total tumor mass ≤ 10% of body weight. For experimental metastasis and intracardiac-injection models, predefined humane endpoints included labored respiration, paresis/paralysis, severe lethargy, inability to ambulate/feed/drink, or ≥ 20% body-weight loss. Animals meeting any endpoint were euthanized immediately. Animals were monitored at least once daily, and tumor size was measured three times weekly. No animal exceeded the IACUC-approved maximal tumor size/burden limits or other humane endpoints before scheduled euthanasia; no tumors ulcerated, and no animal lost ≥ 20% body weight. All procedures (subcutaneous, intravenous, and intracardiac cell injections; ultrasound/UTMD treatments) were performed under inhalational anesthesia using isoflurane (3–4% for induction, 1.5–2% for maintenance in oxygen at ~ 1 L·min⁻¹) delivered by nose cone. Eyes were protected with ophthalmic lubricant, and body temperature was maintained on a warming pad. When indicated, pre-emptive analgesia was provided (buprenorphine SR 1.0 mg·kg⁻¹ s.c. before intracardiac injection and for any procedure expected to cause discomfort). At experimental endpoints, mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation using a gradual fill rate of 20–30% chamber volume per minute until respiratory arrest, followed by a secondary physical method (bilateral thoracotomy or cervical dislocation) to ensure death, in accordance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020), after which tissues were collected.

## Histopathology and immunohistochemistry
Tumor and lung tissues were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm, and stained with H&E for histopathological evaluation. For immunohistochemical analysis, the sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval via citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide, and the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against JMJD2A (1:500; Abcam, UK) and Ki67 (1:1000; Abcam, UK). Detection was performed via a DAB substrate kit (Vector Laboratories, USA), and the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. The slides were scanned via a digital slide scanner (Hamamatsu, Japan), and quantitative analysis was performed via ImageJ.

## Statistical analysis
The data are expressed as the means ± SDs unless otherwise indicated, for comparisons between two groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way ANOVA was applied; when two independent variables were involved, two-way ANOVA was used, followed by Tukey’s post hoc multiple-comparison test, as appropriate. Survival analysis was performed via the Kaplan‒Meier method, and differences were assessed via the log-rank test. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, USA). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered significant. Unless otherwise stated, figure legends refer to this section for the statistical tests used.

## Results

## Synthesis and characterization of si-JMJD2A-loaded UTMD
In accordance with previously established protocols, I successfully encapsulated si-JMJD2A within a UTMD system, as depicted in Fig.  A. Subsequent characterization revealed that the mean particle sizes of the synthesized empty UTMD, si-empty-loaded UTMD, and si-JMJD2A-loaded UTMD were 267.06 ± 19.37 nm, 304 ± 29.52 nm, and 311.74 ± 26.27 nm, respectively (Fig.  B-C). Zeta potential measurements revealed surface charges of 3.15 ± 1.16 mV, 3.07 ± 2.24 mV, and 3.38 ± 1.93 mV for empty UTMD, si-Empty-loaded UTMD, and si-JMJD2A-loaded UTMD, respectively (Fig.  D). Furthermore, following the application of ultrasound irradiation to ESCC cells (K450 and K150), I observed the intracellular presence of FAM-labeled siRNA, confirming the successful delivery of the siRNA into the cells (Fig.  E). Comparative analysis of gene silencing efficiency demonstrated that, compared with Lipofectamine 2000, UTMD-mediated delivery of JMJD2A siRNA resulted in significantly enhanced knockdown efficiency (Fig.  F-G).

## UTMD-Loaded si-JMJD2A induces ferroptosis and inhibits ESCC cell proliferation in vitro
In the preliminary study, I analyzed the TCGA-ESCA and GEO datasets, revealing a significant association between JMJD2A expression and poor prognosis in ESCA patients (Fig. A-C). Additionally, I observed that patients with high JMJD2A expression frequently exhibited more advanced clinical stages and lower differentiation grades (Fig. D). Consistent with the expectations, JMJD2A expression positively correlated with the KI67 positivity rate in ESCA patients (Fig. E). Using edgeR for differential expression analysis, I identified 669 upregulated and 326 downregulated genes. Ferroptosis-related genes were collected from FerrDb (https://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb), and verified human-expressed genes were extracted and categorized as markers, drivers, and suppressors. The intersection of these genes suggested that the low-KDM4A group may be more prone to ferroptosis (Fig. F-G). To further elucidate this mechanism, I assessed the expression of ferroptosis-related markers following si-JMJD2A treatment in K450 and K150 cells. Notably, si-JMJD2A treatment significantly downregulated GPX4 expression, a key antiferroptotic protein, while markedly upregulating ACSL4 and TfR1, indicative of ferroptosis activation (Fig.  A-B). Functional assays revealed that si-JMJD2A treatment significantly inhibited the proliferation and clonogenic potential of K450 and K150 cells, as evidenced by CCK-8 and colony formation assays (Fig.  C-D). Furthermore, β-galactosidase staining demonstrated that si-JMJD2A treatment markedly increased cellular senescence (Fig.  E). Additionally, I observed a significant increase in DNA damage, as indicated by the elevated γ-H2AX fluorescence intensity in si-JMJD2A-treated cells (Fig.  F), and an increase in the number of apoptotic cells, as determined by TUNEL staining (Fig.  G). Collectively, these findings suggest that UTMD-mediated knockdown of JMJD2A robustly activates ferroptosis and inhibits the in vitro growth of ESCC cells. Because siRNA-based depletion may introduce off-target effects, I performed a genetic rescue experiment by re-expressing a JMJD2Ares in the context of UTMD-mediated si-JMJD2A treatment. qRT–PCR confirmed that JMJD2Ares restored JMJD2A mRNA levels despite si-JMJD2A exposure compared with empty-vector controls. Importantly, JMJD2Ares significantly attenuated the si-JMJD2A–associated phenotypes, partially reversing ferroptosis-related changes and restoring EMT- and metastasis-associated characteristics relative to si-JMJD2A alone. These rescue data support that the principal effects observed following UTMD-si-JMJD2A treatment are specifically attributable to JMJD2A loss.

 

## UTMD-loaded si-JMJD2A suppresses the EMT and metastatic potential of ESCC cells
Subsequent investigations focused on the impact of si-JMJD2A on the EMT and metastatic potential of ESCC cells. Treatment with si-JMJD2A led to significant upregulation of epithelial markers, such as E-cadherin (CDH1) and ZO-1, and concurrent downregulation of mesenchymal markers, including N-cadherin (N-cad), Snail1 (Snai1), and vimentin (VIM1), in both K450 and K150 cells (Fig. A-F). Given the critical role of ZEB1 in the initiation of EMT, where it represses E-cadherin and promotes Snail1 expression, I analyzed ZEB1 nuclear translocation [ – ]. The results revealed a substantial reduction in ZEB1 nuclear localization in si-JMJD2A-treated cells, confirming the inhibitory effect on EMT (Fig. G). Functional assays further demonstrated that si-JMJD2A treatment significantly impaired the migratory and invasive capacities of K450 and K150 cells, as evidenced by wound healing and Transwell assays, respectively (Fig. H-J).

 

## Ferroptosis antagonists inhibited UTMD-Loaded si-JMJD2A function
To investigate whether UTMD-loaded si-JMJD2A affects ESCC cell growth and metastatic characteristics through the modulation of ferroptosis, I treated cells with the ferroptosis-specific inhibitor Fer-1 (Fig.  A). After Fer-1 treatment, I observed a significant increase in the growth activity of K450 and K150 cells, accompanied by a notable reduction in cellular senescence and apoptosis (Fig.  B–D). Furthermore, Fer-1 treatment led to a marked upregulation of SNAI1 and VIM expression, while the expression of CDH1 and ZO-1 was significantly reduced (Fig.  E). This was also associated with a substantial enhancement in the migratory and invasive capabilities of K450 and K150 cells (Fig.  F–G).

 

## UTMD-loaded si-JMJD2A modulates immune evasion and M2 macrophage polarization in ESCC cells
On the basis of the previous findings that JMJD2A plays a pivotal role in immune evasion in ESCC, I explored the effects of si-JMJD2A on immune surveillance markers [33]. Based on the expression levels of KDM4A, the data were divided into three groups (> Q1, IQR, < Q3). Immune gene sets curated from TISIDB were collected, and ssGSEA was applied to calculate enrichment scores. ANOVA was then used for multiple group comparisons, revealing significant differences in immune cell populations, including CD8 + T cells and NK cells. Subsequently, a Pearson correlation analysis was performed for the immune cell types showing significant differences (Fig S2A, B). Posttreatment analysis revealed a significant reduction in the expression of immune checkpoint proteins, including PD-L1, HHLA2, galectin-3 (Gal-3), and galectin-9 (Gal-9) (Fig.  A). Moreover, ELISAs of conditioned media from si-JMJD2A-treated cells revealed a notable decrease in the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines, such as CCL2, TGF-β1, Gal-3, and Gal-9 (Fig.  B). To assess the impact on macrophage polarization, K450 and K150 cells treated with si-JMJD2A were cocultured with PMA-differentiated THP-1 macrophages (Fig.  C). The results indicated a significant attenuation of M2 macrophage polarization, as evidenced by the reduced expression of M2-associated cytokines (CCL10, CCL24, and CCL26) and increased expression of M1-associated cytokines (CCL3, CCL11, and CCL13) (Fig.  D-G). Additionally, si-JMJD2A treatment significantly reduced macrophage chemotaxis toward ESCC cells (Fig.  H).

 

## In vivo efficacy of UTMD-loaded si-JMJD2A in tumor-bearing mice
To evaluate the in vivo efficacy of UTMD-loaded si-JMJD2A, I first treated C57BL/6 mice via tail vein injection, followed by ultrasound irradiation for one week (Fig.  A). Histological analysis of lung, liver, brain, and myocardial tissues collected 28 days posttreatment revealed no significant organ damage (Fig.  B-E). To assess antitumor efficacy, I established an orthotopic ESCC model in C57BL/6 mice via intracardiac injection of mEERL cells, followed by si-JMJD2A treatment and ultrasound irradiation on day 3 (Fig.  F). K‒M survival analysis revealed a significant increase in survival in the si-JMJD2A-treated group compared with the control group (Fig.  G).

 

## UTMD-loaded si-JMJD2A inhibits tumor growth and metastasis in mice
In a subcutaneous tumor model, K450 and K150 cells were implanted into BALB/c nude mice, followed by si-JMJD2A treatment and ultrasound irradiation on days 7, 14, and 21 postimplantation (Fig.  A). Tumor growth was significantly inhibited in si-JMJD2A-treated mice, as evidenced by reduced tumor volume and decreased immunohistochemical staining for JMJD2A and Ki67 (Fig.  B-E). To investigate the effects on metastasis, K450 and K150 cells were intravenously injected into BALB/c nude mice, and si-JMJD2A was administered on days 7, 14, 21, and 28. Lung tissue collected on day 35 revealed a significant reduction in the number of metastatic nodules in si-JMJD2A-treated mice (Fig.  F-G). These findings collectively indicate that UTMD-mediated delivery of si-JMJD2A effectively suppresses tumor growth and metastasis in vivo.

 

## UTMD-loaded si-JMJD2A combined with Anti-PD-L1 enhances the immune evasion resistance of esophageal cancer cells
To investigate the combined effects of UTMD-loaded si-JMJD2A and anti-PD-L1 on immune evasion in esophageal cancer, I injected mEERL mouse esophageal cancer cells intravenously into C57BL/6 mice, followed by systemic administration of o-siRNA via tail vein injection. Subsequently, the mice underwent ultrasound irradiation for one week, in conjunction with anti-PD-L1 treatment (Fig.  A). I first observed a significant reduction in the number of metastatic nodules formed by mEERL cells in the lung tissue of the mice, with the lowest number of metastatic nodules found in the mice treated with both o-siRNA and anti-PD-L1 (Fig.  B, C).
 Consistent with previous findings, I observed that o-siRNA treatment reduced M2 macrophage infiltration. To further assess this, I performed immunofluorescence and flow cytometry (FACS) analyses to detect CD206 + cells in mouse lung tissue. I observed that o-siRNA significantly reduced the number of M2 macrophages in the metastatic lesions formed by mEERL cells, but the addition of anti-PD-L1 did not significantly affect the M2 macrophage count (Fig.  D, E). Additionally, I found a significant increase in the number of PFN + or GZMB+ CD8 T cells with active cytotoxicity in the metastatic nodules, indicating that the combination of o-siRNA and anti-PD-L1 effectively restored CD8 T cell function and inhibited their exhaustion (Fig.  F, G). Finally, I observed that mice treated with both o-siRNA and anti-PD-L1 exhibited the highest survival rate at 60 weeks (Fig.  H).

## Discussion
The present study demonstrated that si-JMJD2A-loaded UTMD systems effectively target JMJD2A in ESCC, leading to significant antitumor effects both in vitro and in vivo. By integrating RNAi technology with UTMD, enhanced gene silencing efficiency, ferroptosis activation, inhibition of EMT, and modulation of the tumor immune microenvironment were achieved. These findings suggest that UTMD-mediated delivery of siRNA represents a promising therapeutic approach for ESCC, addressing the challenges of conventional treatments and offering new avenues for targeted cancer therapy. Notably, whereas primarily research delineates JMJD2A-centered regulatory mechanisms in ESCC [ ], this work focuses on therapeutic implementation, demonstrating an ultrasound-guided UTMD delivery platform and its in vivo antitumor efficacy, including synergy with PD-L1 blockade.
One key finding of this study is the robust induction of ferroptosis following JMJD2A knockdown in ESCC cells. Ferroptosis, a form of regulated cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, has been increasingly recognized as a crucial mechanism in cancer suppression [ ]. Previous studies have demonstrated the therapeutic potential of inducing ferroptosis in various cancers, including hepatocellular carcinoma and glioblastoma, where targeting ferroptosis-related pathways reduces tumor growth and increases sensitivity to existing treatments [ , , , ]. Similarly, the results showed that si-JMJD2A treatment led to significant downregulation of GPX4, a key antiferroptotic protein, and upregulation of ACSL4 and TfR1, markers of ferroptosis activation. These findings are consistent with research by Yang et al.., which highlighted the importance of GPX4 in maintaining the cellular redox balance and preventing ferroptosis in cancer cells [ ].
Inhibition of EMT is another significant outcome observed following si-JMJD2A treatment. EMT is a well-established mechanism by which epithelial cells acquire mesenchymal properties, thereby enhancing their migratory and invasive capacities, key characteristics of metastatic cancer cells. This process is closely linked to cancer metastasis, and previous research has shown that the inhibition of EMT can reduce the metastatic potential of various cancers, including breast cancer and colorectal cancer [ – ]. This study revealed that si-JMJD2A treatment led to marked upregulation of epithelial markers, such as E-cadherin, and concurrent downregulation of mesenchymal markers, including N-cadherin, Snail1, and vimentin. This reversal of EMT was further supported by a reduction in the nuclear localization of ZEB1, a transcription factor that suppresses E-cadherin and promotes EMT. These findings align with the work of Ahn S et al.. and Wang F et al.., who reported that targeting JMJD2A in prostate cancer cells inhibited EMT and reduced metastatic potential, further supporting the role of JMJD2A as a driver of EMT across multiple cancer types [ , ].
Modulation of the tumor immune microenvironment is another significant aspect of JMJD2A’s role in ESCC. Tumors often exploit immune evasion mechanisms to evade destruction by the host immune system, a major challenge in cancer therapy [ , ]. JMJD2A has been implicated in the regulation of immune checkpoint proteins and cytokines that promote immune suppression and tumor immune escape. This study revealed that si-JMJD2A treatment significantly reduces the expression of immune checkpoint proteins such as PD-L1, HHLA2, galectin-3, and galectin-9, which are associated with immune evasion in ESCC. Additionally, conditioned media from si-JMJD2A-treated cells showed reduced levels of immunosuppressive cytokines, including CCL2 and TGF-β1, which promote M2 macrophage polarization. This finding is supported by recent studies, including one by Zhang et al.., which demonstrated that targeting JMJD2A in ovarian cancer cells reduced PD-L1 expression and enhanced the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors [ ]. The attenuation of M2 polarization, as evidenced by reduced expression of M2-associated cytokines and increased expression of M1-associated cytokines, suggests that JMJD2A plays a pivotal role in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment [ , ]. This aligns with the growing body of literature highlighting the importance of targeting the tumor immune microenvironment to enhance the efficacy of immunotherapies. However, the primary in vivo tumor-growth and metastasis experiments evaluating UTMD-si-JMJD2A monotherapy were conducted in BALB/c nude mice (Fig. ), which lack functional T cells, thereby limiting the extent to which immune-mediated mechanisms can be inferred from those monotherapy efficacy data. In contrast, immune-associated endpoints were assessed in an immunocompetent C57BL/6 setting only in the context of anti–PD-L1 combination therapy (Fig. ); thus, the in vivo immune-remodeling contribution of UTMD-si-JMJD2A alone remains incompletely defined.
While this study provides strong evidence for the therapeutic potential of si-JMJD2A-loaded UTMD in ESCC, several limitations should be acknowledged. The long-term effects of JMJD2A knockdown on tumor recurrence and resistance were not fully explored in this study. Future studies should investigate the durability of the antitumor response and whether combining JMJD2A-targeted treatments with other modalities, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors, can further enhance efficacy. Additionally, the potential off-target effects of si-JMJD2A and the broader implications of JMJD2A inhibition for normal tissue homeostasis require careful evaluation in preclinical and clinical settings. Other researchers, such as Wang et al. [ ]., have noted similar challenges in siRNA delivery, emphasizing the need for thorough preclinical evaluation to minimize potential risks. Importantly, this study lacks direct validation in human ESCC specimens. Accordingly, JMJD2A protein abundance and its relationships with key markers highlighted here—including the ferroptosis regulator GPX4, the immune checkpoint PD-L1, and the densities of CD8 + T cells and CD163 + macrophages remain to be established in patient tissues. Future studies using well-annotated ESCC cohorts (e.g., tissue microarrays and/or spatial profiling) will be needed to confirm these clinicopathologic associations and to define the clinical predictive value of JMJD2A-targeted ferroptosis induction. Accordingly, although the in vitro assays (checkpoint/cytokine modulation and macrophage polarization/chemotaxis) and the C57BL/6 combination-therapy model support an immune component, definitive validation of immune microenvironment remodeling by UTMD-si-JMJD2A monotherapy will require immunocompetent ESCC models. Future studies should therefore repeat key in vivo monotherapy experiments in syngeneic immunocompetent systems and comprehensively profile tumor-infiltrating immune populations, including CD8 + T cells (activation/exhaustion states) and macrophage subsets, using flow cytometry and/or multiplex immunostaining.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, si-JMJD2A-loaded UTMD is a highly effective strategy for targeting JMJD2A in ESCC, activating ferroptosis, inhibiting EMT, and modulating the tumor immune microenvironment. The integration of RNAi technology with UTMD offers a novel therapeutic approach that addresses the limitations of conventional treatments and provides a promising avenue for personalized cancer therapy. These findings are consistent with and build upon the literature, highlighting the potential of siRNA-based therapies in oncology. Further preclinical and clinical studies are warranted to validate these findings and explore the full therapeutic potential of si-JMJD2A-loaded UTMD in ESCC and other malignancies.
