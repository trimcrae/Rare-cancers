## Significance
Cancer cells change the way they use nutrients to support rapid growth and proliferation. Our study shows that the oncoprotein SE translocation (SET) partners with the metabolic enzyme SHMT2 to enhance serine-derived one-carbon metabolism, a process that supplies essential building blocks for cell biosynthesis and antioxidant defense. Inhibiting cellular SHMT enzymes (both SHMT1 and SHMT2) slowed tumor growth induced by SET overexpression. In human lung tumors, high levels of both SET and SHMT2 are linked to worse outcomes. These findings reveal that SET-mediated one-carbon metabolic reprogramming contributes to tumorigenesis.
Keywords: SET, SHMT2, one-carbon metabolism, enzymatic activity, tumor

## Abstract
Cancer cells frequently reprogram one-carbon metabolic pathways to fulfill their vigorous demands of biosynthesis and antioxidant defense for survival and proliferation. Dysfunction of oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes is critically involved in this process, but the precise mechanisms by which cancer cells actively trigger one-carbon metabolic alterations remain incompletely elucidated. Here, by using untargeted metabolomic analysis, we identify the oncoprotein SE translocation (SET) as a key regulator of one-carbon metabolism in cancer cells. SET physically interacts with mitochondrial SHMT2 and facilitates SHMT2 enzymatic activity. Loss of SET profoundly suppresses serine-derived one-carbon metabolic flux, whereas reexpression of ectopic SET leads to the opposite effect. Notably, although the presence of SHMT2 is critical for SET-mediated one-carbon metabolic alterations, the depletion of SHMT2 alone is insufficient to antagonize SET-induced tumor growth, probably due to functional compensation by its cytosolic isozyme SHMT1 upon SHMT2 knockdown. Instead, pharmacological targeting of cellular SHMT (including both SHMT1 and SHMT2) activity results in dramatic suppression of SET-induced tumor growth. Moreover, by using a Kras/Lkb1 mutation-driven lung tumor mouse model, we demonstrate that the loss of SET compromises both tumor formation and intratumoral SHMT2 enzymatic activity. Clinically, the overexpression of SET and SHMT2 is observed in lung tumors, both of which correlate with poor prognosis. Our study reveals a SET–SHMT2 axis in regulating serine-derived one-carbon metabolism and uncovers one-carbon metabolic reprogramming as a mechanism for SET-driven tumorigenesis.

One-carbon metabolism, primarily driven by the folate cycle in both the mitochondria and the cytosol, provides one-carbon units for the biosynthesis of a number of essential molecules, which play pivotal roles in supporting cell survival, proliferation, and intracellular homeostasis ( – ). As a nonessential amino acid (NEAA), serine represents one of the major sources of one-carbon units in the folate cycle for subsequent metabolism ( ). Serine-derived one-carbon units are required for the de novo synthesis of purines, which serve as the building blocks of nucleic acid macromolecules ( ). In addition, the one-carbon unit catabolized from serine contributes to the generation of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH), which fuels the reductive equivalent for biosynthesis and antioxidant defense ( ). By coupling with the methionine cycle, serine-derived one-carbon units also participate in the regeneration of methionine from homocysteine, as well as the production of the active methyl donor S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), which is required for methyltransferase reactions involving nucleic acid and protein substrates ( ). Moreover, serine could be a precursor of other amino acids, including glycine and cysteine, both of which contribute to the synthesis of glutathione, an endogenous oligopeptide critical for antagonizing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and maintaining the cellular redox balance ( ). One-carbon metabolic activity is frequently elevated among different tumor types, and alterations in serine metabolism in cancer cells may profoundly contribute to tumor progression ( , , , ).
The generation of serine-derived one-carbon units is initiated by serine hydroxymethyltransferase (SHMT), which catalyzes the conversion of serine and tetrahydrofolate (THF) to glycine and 5,10-methyleneTHF (5,10-meTHF, CH2-THF) ( , ). 5,10-meTHF can be further converted into other types of THF-loaded one-carbon units, such as 5,10-methenylTHF (5,10-me+THF, CH-THF), 10-formylTHF (CHO-THF), and 5-methylTHF (CH3-THF), by a serial of biochemical reactions ( , ). In mammalian cells, SHMT consists of two isozymes including cytosolic SHMT1 and mitochondrial SHMT2 ( , ). Since the majority of cellular one-carbon units are derived from mitochondrial serine catabolism ( – ), SHMT2, in contrast to SHMT1, is considered particularly important for rapidly proliferating cancer cells that demand high amounts of one-carbon units for biosynthesis and antioxidant defense ( – , , , ). Indeed, the amplification and overexpression of SHMT2 have been identified in different types of cancer, and these changes are frequently associated with tumor progression across tumor types ( ). In addition, the enzymatic activity of SHMT2 can be increased by dynamic posttranslational modifications (PTMs), such as acetylation/deacetylation and succinylation/desuccinylation, in cancer cells ( , ). Depletion of SHMT2 in several cancer cells has been reported to compromise tumor growth in xenograft models ( , ), although the effect might vary depending on context ( ). Targeting SHMT2-mediated serine and one-carbon metabolic pathways has emerged as a potential tumor intervention strategy ( – ).
SE translocation (SET), also known as template-activating factor I (TAF-I) or phosphatase 2A inhibitor (I2PP2A), was originally identified from an oncogenic SET-CAN fusion gene discovered in acute undifferentiated leukemia (AUL) ( ). SET consists of an N-terminal dimerization domain (DD), a middle earmuff domain (ED), and a C-terminal acidic domain (AD) ( ). These domains are critical for mediating the interactions of SET with other protein partners, such as histones ( ), transcription factors (e.g., p53, FOXO1, and ERα) ( – ), and enzymes (e.g., phosphatase PP2A) ( ), providing SET with diverse molecular functions. As a multifaceted protein, SET participates in the regulation of a spectrum of various biological processes, including gene transcription, chromosome segregation, DNA repair, the cell cycle, cell movement, and programmed cell death ( , ). Loss of SET results in embryonic lethality in a mouse model ( ), demonstrating that SET is essential for early development and cellular homeostasis under physiological conditions. The overexpression of SET is frequently observed among different types of tumors ( – ) and is correlated with tumor progression, distal metastasis, and a reduced survival rate ( , ). Thus, targeting abnormal SET expression or SET-mediated oncogenic pathways has been developed as a promising strategy for cancer intervention ( , , ). Currently, little is known about whether SET directly modulates cell metabolism and, more importantly, whether aberrant SET contributes to tumorigenesis through metabolic reprogramming of cancer cells.
In this study, we find that the aberrant expression of SET promotes serine-derived one-carbon metabolism. SET can localize in mitochondria, where it physically binds to SHMT2 and facilitates its enzymatic activity. The expression of SET stimulates serine-derived one-carbon metabolic flux, which largely relies on the presence of SHMT2. Pharmacological inhibition of SHMT activity suppresses SET-induced tumor growth. Using a spontaneous lung tumor mouse model, we demonstrate that the loss of SET inhibits tumor formation and compromises SHMT2 enzymatic activity in tumors driven by oncogenic Kras/Lkb1 mutations. Our study reveals a mechanism by which the oncoprotein SET participates in tumorigenesis by modulating one-carbon metabolism.

## Results

## The Oncoprotein SET Contributes to Metabolic Reprogramming in Cancer Cells.
To explore the potential roles of SET in modulating cancer cell metabolism, we generated H1299 lung cancer cell line-derived control stable cells (EV) or stable cells that constitutively express Flag-HA-tagged SET (SET-OE) and performed untargeted metabolomics via liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) ( ). Overall, 148 differentially abundant metabolite features were quantitatively recognized from a total of 15,550 raw features between EV cells and SET-OE cells ( and Dataset S1). Further analyses of these differentially abundant metabolite features revealed 51 metabolites with clear annotations, among which 31 metabolites were upregulated and 20 metabolites were downregulated upon SET overexpression ( and Dataset S1). Among these differentially abundant metabolites, we noticed that 10-formyl-tetrahydrofolate (10-formylTHF, SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B), a critical intermediate of the folate cycle and one-carbon metabolism, exhibited a dramatic increase after SET overexpression ( ). To validate this finding, we performed targeted metabolite analysis of cellular 10-formylTHF by using a standard compound as a reference. Since the cellular THF species can be diversely polyglutamated, thereby potentially interfering with their measurement by MS, we thus detected the cellular 10-formylTHF after stripping the polyglutamate tails ( ). As shown in , SET overexpression indeed increased the cellular levels of 10-formylTHF. This observation prompted us to investigate whether aberrant SET triggers one-carbon metabolic alterations in tumors. To this end, we evaluated the relationship between SET expression and one-carbon metabolism. Analysis of the TCGA lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) dataset revealed that SET expression was positively correlated with one-carbon metabolic activity ( ). In fact, among the 33 types of tumors collected from the TCGA datasets, 27 exhibited a positive correlation between SET expression and one-carbon metabolic activity ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B), strongly implying that SET-mediated one-carbon metabolic reprogramming could be a general phenomenon in tumors.
 By providing one-carbon units, 10-formylTHF participates in multiple biochemical reactions that are critically involved in determining cell proliferation and survival ( ). For example, 10-formylTHF is directly involved in two catalytic reactions for the de novo synthesis of purines ( ). In addition, 10-formylTHF promotes the production of NADPH, a key H+ supplier that is essential for the majority of biosynthetic processes ( , ). Moreover, by increasing NADPH levels, 10-formylTHF promotes the synthesis and regeneration of reduced glutathione (GSH), which, in turn, antagonizes cellular ROS and consequently maintains the cellular redox balance and cell survival ( , ). We found that cellular glycinamide ribonucleotide (GAR) and 5-aminoimidazole-4-carboxyamide ribonucleoside (AICAR), two key intermediates during purine synthesis, were elevated upon SET overexpression ( ). In addition, SET overexpression increased the NADPH/NADP+ and GSH/GSSH ratios ( ), accompanied by reduced cellular ROS levels ( ). By using the CRISPR technique, we generated an H1299 SET-KO cell line (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B). Loss of SET in H1299 cells had no effect on cell viability (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). Compared with parental cells, SET-KO cells exhibited a decrease in the cellular GAR, AICAR, NADPH/NADP+ ratio and GSH/GSSG ratio, and an increase in the cellular ROS level (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 B–F). To further validate this regulation by SET, we employed siRNA-mediated knockdown of SET in H1975, another lung cancer cell line (SI Appendix, Fig. S4G). Similarly, the viability of H1975 cells remained unaffected (SI Appendix, Fig. S4H). Following SET depletion, decreases in the cellular GAR, AICAR, NADPH/NADP+ ratio and GSH/GSSG ratio, along with increases in the ROS level, were clearly observed (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 I–M). These results suggest that SET participates in modulating one-carbon metabolism, thereby contributing to the regulation of cellular biosynthesis and redox homeostasis for cell survival and proliferation.

## SET Physically Interacts with the One-Carbon Metabolic Enzyme SHMT2.
In mammalian cells, the production and conversion of 10-formylTHF occur concurrently in both the cytoplasm and the mitochondria through serine metabolism and the folate cycle, which are controlled by a series of enzyme-catalyzed biochemical reactions ( ). Since protein–protein interactions are critical for SET-mediated biological functions, we sought to evaluate whether SET physically associates with the enzymes involved in this process, thereby affecting one-carbon metabolism. To this end, we performed tandem affinity purification coupled with mass spectrometry (TAP-MS) to screen the binding partners of SET in H1299 or U2OS cells that express ectopic Flag-HA-tagged SET (SET-OE) ( ). MS analyses revealed that SHMT2 specifically existed in SET-containing protein complex purified from both H1299 and U2OS cells ( and Dataset S2). The MS findings were validated by coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays in HEK293T cells, where the interaction between ectopic SET and SHMT2 was readily detected ( ). In addition, we successfully demonstrated the interaction between endogenous SET and SHMT2 in H1299 cells ( ), indicating that these proteins interact under physiological conditions in lung tumor cells. Moreover, SET can exist in the mitochondria where it colocalizes with SHMT2 ( ), implying a potentially functional interplay between SET and SHMT2 for mitochondrial one-carbon metabolic regulation.
 We next characterized the details of the interaction between SET and SHMT2. In vitro pull-down assays using purified proteins indicated that SET directly interacts with SHMT2 ( ). To quantitatively analyze the strength of the SET–SHMT2 interaction, a biolayer interferometry (BLI) assay was conducted to monitor the dynamics of SET–SHMT2 complex association and dissociation ( ). The results demonstrated a strong interaction between SET and SHMT2, with a dissociation equilibrium constant (Kd) of approximately 2 × 10−9 M ( ). Next, we investigated the exact regions between SET and SHMT2 for their interaction. We mapped that AD was not only required but also sufficient to bind with SHMT2 ( ). A BLI assay also confirmed that the loss of AD almost completely abrogated the binding affinity of SET to SHMT2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B). In contrast, a very long region of SHMT2, ranging from 22 to 504 amino acids, was responsible for its association with SET ( ). Together, our data reveal that the oncoprotein SET physically interacts with the one-carbon metabolic enzyme SHMT2 in mitochondria.

## SET Facilitates the Enzymatic Activity of SHMT2.
We next investigated whether SET potentially regulates SHMT2 enzymatic activity. Since SET cannot interact with MTHFD2 in vivo or in vitro (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B), we speculated that SET is unlikely to directly modulate MTHFD2 activity. Thus, we designed a SHMT-MTHFD2 coupled enzymatic reaction to evaluate SHMT activity ( ): In the first step, SHMT catalyzes serine and THF to produce glycine and 5,10-meTHF; in the second step, 5,10-meTHF is further catalyzed to 10-formylTHF by MTHFD2, accompanied by the conversion of NAD+ to NADH that is detectable via a colorimetric method (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C). This enzymatic reaction was sensitive enough and suitable for measuring both SHMT1 and SHMT2 enzymatic activities (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 D and E).
By using this strategy, we found that the enzymatic activity of SHMT2 purified from Escherichia coli was gradually increased in the presence of increasing amounts of purified SET under in vitro conditions ( ). Notably, the interaction between SET and SHMT2 was essential for the SET-mediated enhancement of SHMT2 enzymatic activity, since the presence of SET-ΔAD, a truncated form of SET that lost the ability to bind SHMT2, failed to increase SHMT2 enzymatic activity ( ). To corroborate SET-mediated alterations in SHMT2 enzymatic activity under physiological conditions, we next measured cellular SHMT2 activity. To this end, the cellular SHMT2 protein complex was purified with a SHMT2-specific antibody, which subsequently underwent enzymatic activity analysis by using coupled enzymatic reactions (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C). As shown in , the loss of SET in H1299 cells reduced cellular SHMT2 activity, while the expression of SHMT2 remained unchanged before and after SET knockout. A similar result of reduced cellular SHMT2 activity was also observed in H1975 cells upon SET knockdown by siRNA (SI Appendix, Fig. S6F). We also evaluated SHMT1 activity. As expected, there was no detectable interaction between endogenous SHMT1 and SET in H1299 or H1975 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B). The enzymatic activity of SHMT1 was not regulated by SET either in vitro or in vivo (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 C and D).
 To validate the SET-mediated regulation of SHMT2 enzymatic activity in vivo, we conducted tracing assays at different time points to determine the conversion rate of 13C-labeling serine to glycine (m+2). Consistent with the reduced SHMT2 activity upon loss of SET, the production of serine-derived glycine was markedly slowed down in SET-KO cells ( ). More importantly, reexpression of ectopic SET in H1299 SET-KO cells (re-SET-OE) rescued cellular SHMT2 activity and accelerated serine-to-glycine conversion ( ), confirming the “on-target” regulatory effect of SET on SHMT2 enzymatic activity.
To further explore the mechanism by which the interaction between SET and SHMT2 promotes SHMT2 activity, we conducted a molecular docking assay. The predicted structural features of the interaction between SET (isoform 1) and SHMT2 revealed that the AD of SET contacted multiple regions of SHMT2 by employing a “C-like” half-surrounded shape ( ), which is consistent with our experimental findings that AD is required for binding with SHMT2 ( ). In addition, the amino acid residues Lys189, Lys193, and Arg194, located within the SET ED, may contact several key amino acid residues of the SHMT2 catalytic pocket through hydrophobic bonds or salt bridges ( ). This structural feature prompted us to speculate that SET modulates SHMT2 enzymatic activity by optimizing the conformation of the SHMT2 catalytic pocket. To evaluate this possibility, we generated a SET mutant lacking amino acids 174-181 (SET-Δ174-181, equal to SET-Δ187-194 in isoform 1). Compared with the full-length SET, the SET-Δ174-181 mutant maintained a similar binding affinity for SHMT2 ( ). However, unlike the intact SET protein that increased SHMT2 activity, the SET-Δ174-181 mutant almost completely lost its ability to enhance SHMT2 enzymatic activity ( ), indicating that the contacts between SET amino acids 174-181 and the SHMT2 catalytic pocket critically contribute to SET-mediated regulation of SHMT2 activity. Thus, our data indicate that SET facilitates SHMT2 enzymatic activity and reveal that the AD and ED of SET coordinate to achieve this regulation.

## Loss of SET Dampens Serine-Derived One-Carbon Metabolism.
Given that SET promotes SHMT2 enzymatic activity, we investigated whether SET has an impact on one-carbon metabolism. To this end, we performed metabolic flux analysis (MFA) to trace 13C-labeling serine-derived metabolites by MS. Serine catabolism catalyzed by SHMT2 yields glycine and one-carbon intermediates, which are precursors for the synthesis of purine nucleotides, including inosine monophosphate (IMP), adenosine monophosphate (AMP), and guanosine monophosphate (GMP) ( ). As expected, the loss of SET profoundly inhibited the flux from serine to glycine, as evidenced by the approximately 50% reduction in the abundance of m+2 glycine upon SET knockout ( ). In addition, the fraction of m+2 glycine within the cellular glycine pool decreased in response to the loss of SET ( ). Similarly, the levels of 13C-labeled IMP (m+1-4), AMP (m+1-4), and GMP (m+1-4) were also reduced in SET-KO cells ( ). Notably, the compromised flux from serine to completely 13C-labeled IMP (m+4), AMP (m+4), and GMP (m+4) clearly reflected the blockade of the de novo synthesis of purines ( ).
 Serine-derived glycine and cysteine contribute to glutathione synthesis ( ), which plays a critical role in maintaining cellular redox homeostasis. We noticed that SET knockout primarily led to a decrease in m+2 glutathione ( ), suggesting that the reduced flux from serine to glutathione was due to compromised production of glycine (m+2) from serine. In addition, serine-derived glycine and one-carbon units can also be utilized for SAM synthesis ( ). SET knockout resulted in reduced 13C flux from serine to SAM ( ). Notably, this reduction occurred mainly in the SAM (m+4) but not the SAM (m+1) form ( ), suggesting that loss of SET primarily inhibited de novo synthesis of SAM but not SAM production from remethylation of homocysteine. Notably, the loss of SET reduced the cellular pool of glycine (SI Appendix, Fig. S8A). Although SET knockout tended to decrease the cellular levels of IMP, AMP, GMP, glutathione, or SAM, the changes in these metabolites were not statistically significant under our experimental conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 B–F). Taken together, our data reveal that SET negatively regulates serine-derived one-carbon metabolism in lung tumor cells.

## SHMT2 Participates in SET-Mediated One-Carbon Metabolic Regulation.
We next explored whether SHMT2 is involved in SET-mediated regulation of one-carbon metabolism. In H1299 SET-KO cells, we rescued SET expression through an adenovirus-mediated strategy (Ad-SET) combined with or without SHMT2 depletion by siRNA ( ), and a serine metabolic tracing assay was conducted under these conditions. Knockdown of SHMT2 and/or reexpression of SET had no effect on cell viability (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A). As expected, the production of glycine (m+2) derived from 13C-labeling serine was severely inhibited upon SHMT2 knockdown ( ), indicating that the maintenance of SHMT2 plays a predominant role in controlling serine catabolism in tumor cells. The reexpression of SET increased the flux from serine to glycine, and more importantly, this regulatory effect was markedly abrogated in the absence of SHMT2 ( ). These results provide direct evidence that SHMT2 contributes to SET-mediated serine catabolism.
 Similarly, the 13C flux from serine to IMP, AMP, and GMP was enhanced by the reexpression of SET, which was largely dependent on the presence of SHMT2 ( ). Interestingly, compared with IMP (m+4), the upregulation of AMP (m+4) and GMP (m+4) induced by SET was more striking ( vs. ), suggesting that SET may also actively promote the transition from IMP to AMP or GMP, in parallel with SET-mediated SHMT2 regulation. We also measured the 13C flux of glutathione and SAM derived from serine catabolism. Similarly, SET-induced upregulation of labeled glutathione and SAM was profoundly compromised in tumor cells depleted of SHMT2 ( ).
We also evaluated whether SHMT1 is functionally involved in SET-mediated one-carbon metabolic regulation. To this end, we performed metabolic flux analysis by using 2,3,3-2H3-labeled serine as a tracer ( ). Cytosolic 5,10-methyleneTHF provides one-carbon units for thymidine synthesis: The production of 5,10-methyleneTHF catalyzed by SHMT1 generates m+2 deoxythymidine trisphosphate (dTTP), whereas the transfer of mitochondrial one-carbon units, produced by SHMT2-involved reactions, to the cytosol yields m+1 dTTP (SI Appendix, Fig. S9B). As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S9 C and D, SET reexpression in H1299 SET-KO cells increased m+1, but not m+2, labeling dTTP, suggesting that SET primarily enhances SHMT2 enzymatic activity. As expected, knockdown of SHMT2 resulted in a decrease in m+1 labeling dTTP but an increase in m+2 labeling dTTP (SI Appendix, Fig. S9C), indicating that serine catabolism is shunted into SHMT1-mediated flux upon SHMT2 depletion. In addition, knockdown of SHMT2 markedly abrogated SET reexpression-induced increase in m+1 labeling dTTP (SI Appendix, Fig. S9D), further suggesting that SET controls cellular serine metabolism primarily through the modulation of SHMT2 enzymatic activity. Collectively, our data indicate that SHMT2 is critically involved in the SET-mediated regulation of serine-derived one-carbon metabolism.

## Targeting SHMT Enzymatic Activity Suppresses SET-Dependent Tumor Growth.
SET overexpression is observed in different types of tumor cells and potentially contributes to tumor growth ( , ). Interestingly, reexpression of full-length SET, but not the SET-ΔAD mutant that disables the binding of SHMT2 and triggers SHMT2 enzymatic activity ( and ), in H1299 SET-KO cells accelerated xenograft tumor growth (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A–D). This observation suggests that the interaction between SET and SHMT2 might contribute to SET-mediated tumorigenesis. To evaluate the role of SET-mediated one-carbon metabolism through the regulation of SHMT2 activity in tumor development, we generated a xenograft tumor growth model. The stable knockdown of SHMT2 in EV or SET-OE cells was confirmed by western blotting (SI Appendix, Fig. S11A). As expected, SET-OE profoundly accelerated tumor growth (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 B and C). However, depletion of SHMT2 had no effect on SET-induced tumor growth (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 B and C). Interestingly, we found that the knockdown of SHMT2 resulted in increased expression of SHMT1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 D and E), an SHMT isozyme that is expressed in the cytoplasm to catalyze serine catabolism and one-carbon unit production ( ). Thus, we speculated that the functional compensation by SHMT1 upon SHMT2 depletion likely masks the regulatory effect of SHMT2 on SET-mediated tumor growth. In addition, this observation also suggested that targeting cellular SHMT activity rather than SHMT2 expression alone could be considered for the treatment of SET-OE tumors.
To this end, we tested SHIN2, a potent folate-competitive and cell-permeable human SHMT1/2 inhibitor ( , ). As expected, treatment with SHIN2 for 24 h led to a reduction in the enzymatic activity of both SHMT1 and SHMT2 in H1299 cells ( ), accompanied by a profound decrease in serine-to-glycine conversion, as detected by a 13C-labeling serine tracing assay ( ). Notably, the cellular expression of SHMT1 and SHMT2 was not affected by SHIN2 ( ). No cell viability was compromised upon SHIN2 treatment ( ). Similar results were also observed in SHIN2-treated H1975 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S12 A–D). We next investigated whether blockade of SHMT activity by SHIN2 affects SET-mediated tumor growth ( ). SHIN2 treatment alone slightly inhibited tumor growth in control (EV) cells ( ). More importantly, SET overexpression-induced tumor growth was profoundly suppressed in the presence of SHIN2, as evidenced by reduced tumor volume and tumor weight ( ). Together, our data reveal that targeting SHMT enzymatic activity suppresses SET-dependent tumor growth.
 

## Loss of SET Suppresses Lung Tumor Growth and Intratumoral SHMT2 Activity.
To assess SET-mediated tumor growth and SHMT2 activity under more physiological conditions, we generated genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs): Kras+/LSL-G12D, Lkb1Fl/Fl, Rosa26-e(CAG-LSL-Luc-EGFP) and SetFl/Fl, Kras+/LSL-G12D, Lkb1Fl/Fl, Rosa26-e(CAG-LSL-Luc-EGFP) (hereafter referred to as KLLE and KLLE-SetFl/Fl, respectively). Spontaneous lung tumor formation can be induced by inhalation of adenovirus-based Cre recombinase (Ad-Cre) and visualized by bioluminescence through an in vivo imaging system (IVIS) ( ). Approximately 8 wk after Ad-Cre inhalation, the bioluminescence signals monitored by IVIS were highly enriched within the chest, suggesting the development of primary lung tumors ( ). In addition, the isolated lung tissues displayed more striking bioluminescence signals ( ), which corresponded to the local sporadic tumors observed macroscopically on the lung surface ( ). Notably, in contrast to their KLLE counterparts, KLLE-SetFl/Fl mice exhibited reduced and slowed primary lung tumor formation caused by Kras-G12D expression and Lkb1 loss of function ( ), suggesting that loss of SET suppresses genetic lesion-induced lung tumor growth. Consistent with this notion, immunohistochemistry (IHC) revealed much less Ki-67 staining in the SET-KO tumor sections ( ), indicating the slow proliferation of tumor cells in the absence of SET in vivo. Moreover, we detected intratumoral SHMT2 enzymatic activity. Compared with adjacent lung tissues, which exhibited similar SHMT2 activity between the KLLE and KLLE-SetFl/Fl groups, the loss of Set in tumors resulted in a dramatic reduction in SHMT2 activity ( ). In addition, we conducted an in vivo tracing assay by injecting 13C-labeling serine into the tail vein of KLLE and KLLE-SetFl/Fl mice with lung tumor burdens, and both the primary lung tumors and the adjacent lung tissues were subjected to MFA 2 h postinjection ( ). Overall, the primary lung tumors exhibited higher levels of 13C-labeling serine-derived glycine (m+2) than adjacent lung tissues did ( ). In addition, the loss of SET in primary lung tumors profoundly inhibited glycine (m+2) production from 13C-labeling serine ( ), which is consistent with the findings that the loss of SET suppresses SHMT2 enzymatic activity in lung tumors ( ). These observations also support the findings of a recent study showing that Kras/Lkb1 mutation-derived lung tumors are associated with serine-glycine-one-carbon (SGOC) metabolic alterations ( ) and suggest that SET-mediated regulation of SHMT2 enzymatic activity may be involved in this adaptive metabolic change. Collectively, our data demonstrate that loss of SET suppresses lung tumor growth and reduces intratumoral SHMT2 enzymatic activity.
 

## Aberrant SET and SHMT2 Correlate with Poor Prognosis in Lung Cancer Patients.
To further evaluate the clinical relevance of SET and SHMT2 in lung cancer, we monitored their expression in LUAD cohorts from the GEO dataset (GSE30219) ( ). We found that the expression of both SET and SHMT2 was upregulated in tumors compared with normal lung tissues ( ). In addition, there was a strong positive correlation between the expression of intratumoral SET and that of SHMT2 ( ), suggesting the functional cooperation of SET and SHMT2 in tumor initiation or progression. Furthermore, we also evaluated the protein expression of SET and SHMT2 in a lung tissue array containing paired lung tumors and their adjacent normal counterparts. Similar to the observations in the GEO dataset, the expression of both SET and SHMT2 was markedly elevated in lung tumors ( ), and their expression was positively correlated ( ). More importantly, high expression of both SET and SHMT2 was correlated with shorter overall survival (OS) in lung cancer patients ( ), indicating that the aberrant expression of SET and SHMT2 frequently observed in lung tumors leads to a poor prognosis in lung cancer patients. Together, all these clinical data provide a notion that SET, SHMT2, and their interplay are critical to support the metabolic demands of lung cancer.
 

## Discussion
Metabolic reprogramming represents one of the characteristic features of cancer cells ( , ). In this study, we identified the oncoprotein SET as a key regulator of serine-derived one-carbon metabolism. SET specifically interacts with mitochondrial SHMT2 but not its cytosolic isozyme SHMT1, which in turn triggers serine-derived metabolic flux toward purine nucleotides, glutathione, and SAM ( ). These metabolic alterations were functionally consistent with the observations of elevated purine synthesis, NADPH-to-NADP+ ratios and GSH-to-GSSG ratios, and reduced cellular ROS levels induced by SET overexpression ( ). Previous studies show that the reduced 10-formylTHF is typically associated with the accumulation of GAR and AICAR ( , ). Given that SET overexpression enhances overall serine-derived one-carbon metabolism, the observation that simultaneous elevation of cellular 10-formylTHF, GAR, and AICAR induced by SET likely reflects an accelerated turnover of these intermediates rather than a simple blockade of serine-derived one-carbon pathway for purine synthesis. Moreover, intervention in serine-derived one-carbon metabolism markedly suppressed SET-dependent tumor growth ( ), supporting the critical role of the aberrant SET–one-carbon metabolism regulatory axis during tumorigenesis.
In addition to the PTMs that have been well recognized as key steps in regulating SHMT2 molecular functions ( , , ), our current work provides alternative mechanistic insight into the modulation of SHMT2 enzymatic activity through protein–protein interactions. Notably, although the AD of SET was required to bind with SHMT2, the presence of AD alone was not sufficient to stimulate SHMT2 enzymatic activity. Instead, we identified a small region ranging from 174 to 181 aa within the ED of SET, which is essential for SET-mediated SHMT2 hyperactivity ( ). This region formed a loop-like structure that was spatially close to the SHMT2 catalytic pocket and was predicted to directly contact Glu55, Glu77, and Ser144 ( ). A series of amino acid residues in the SHMT catalytic pocket have been identified to be responsible for binding substrates (serine/THF) and cofactors (PLP) or stabilizing reaction intermediates ( , ). For example, the conserved active site Ser144 of human SHMT2 may contribute to stabilizing PLP and assisting in catalysis ( ). Although the exact mechanism of SET-mediated SHMT2 hyperactivity remains unclear, the characteristics of the physical interaction between SET and SHMT2 prompted us to speculate that SET is likely to induce conformational changes within the catalytic pocket of SHMT2, which in turn facilitates SHMT2-mediated catalysis by optimizing substrate/cofactor binding or stabilization within the SHMT2 catalytic pocket. Together, our data reveal a “two-step” mechanism by which SET regulates SHMT2 enzymatic activity: First, the AD of SET provides a platform for the physical binding of SHMT2 with high affinity; second, the physical interaction between SET and SHMT2 further promotes local contact between the loop-like structure of SET (174-181 aa) and the catalytic pocket of SHMT2, ultimately leading to increased SHMT2 enzymatic activity.
Although SHMT2 plays a critical role in initiating serine catabolism and one-carbon unit production ( , ), the importance of SHMT2 in supporting tumor growth is still under debate. Studies using hepatocellular carcinoma and neuroblastoma models have shown that SHMT2 depletion leads to a robust tumor suppressive effect ( , ), whereas SHMT2 knockdown in a colon cancer model has no effect on tumor growth ( ). Similar to the latter results, our current work, by using a lung tumor xenograft model, revealed that the loss of SHMT2 did not compromise tumor growth triggered by SET-OE (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 A–C). These split phenotypes of tumor growth most likely reflect distinct adaptive capabilities among different cancer cell types in response to mitochondrial serine metabolic stress. Indeed, we detected increased expression of SHMT1 in lung cancer cells upon SHMT2 knockdown (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 D and E), which was consistent with the observation that depletion of SHMT2 increased SHMT1-mediated dTTP production (SI Appendix, Fig. S9C). These findings suggest that the compensation of serine and one-carbon metabolism occurs, to some extent, through increased cytosolic SHMT1-mediated catalysis ( , ). This metabolic adaptation is speculated to be critical for sustaining tumor growth, since simultaneous blockade of SHMT1 and SHMT2 enzymatic activity by the small-molecule SHIN2 dramatically suppressed tumor growth driven by SET overexpression ( ). Thus, we speculate that, on the one hand, the hyperactivity of SHMT2 triggered by the aberrant expression of the oncoprotein SET in cancer cells is critical to serine-derived one-carbon metabolism, which facilitates tumor growth; on the other hand, targeting SHMT2 alone might frequently elicit cancer cell metabolic adaptation that consequently sustains tumor growth through the compensation of impaired SHMT2 activity.
Given that both SET and SHMT2 are upregulated in lung tumors and are correlated with a poor prognosis ( ), simultaneous targeting of SET and SHMT2 can be expected to benefit cancer therapy. One strategy for this purpose could be to develop small-molecule compounds or blocking peptides to disrupt the SET–SHMT2 interaction, which could combine with SHMT inhibitors, such as SHIN2, for tumor intervention. In addition, since SET may activate distinct oncogenic pathways and hijack multiple tumor suppressors ( , ), targeting the aberrant expression of endogenous SET could be another way to synergize with SHMT inhibitors and likely result in additional therapeutic benefits. For example, the tumor suppressor p53 serves as a master regulator of SET, where p53 transcriptionally represses the expression of SET ( ). Thus, the activation of the p53 pathway by compounds (e.g., Nutlin3 and APG115) ( ) might synergize with SHIN2, although the therapeutic effect of such combination treatment could be partially mediated through the inhibition of the p53–SET–SHMT2–one-carbon metabolism axis. Taken together, our work elucidates that the oncoprotein SET promotes one-carbon metabolism by increasing SHMT2 enzymatic activity and uncovers that one-carbon metabolic reprogramming is critically involved in aberrant SET-induced tumorigenesis.

## Materials and Methods

## Cell Culture.
H1299, HEK293T, and U2OS cell lines were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, C11995500BT) supplemented with 10% (vol/vol) FBS (Gibco, 10099141). The H1975 cell line was cultured in RPMI-1640 (Cell Resource Center of IBMS-CAMS) supplemented with 10% FBS. All cell lines were originally obtained from the ATCC and were proven to be negative for Mycoplasma contamination.

## BLI.
The BLI was performed at 25 °C on an Octet® RED96e System (ForteBio) with BC100 buffer. Purified His-SET-FL or His-SET-ΔAD was biotinylated using the Biotinylation Kit (Genemore, G-MM-IGT) and then loaded for 3 min onto streptavidin biosensors preequilibrated in running buffer for 2 min. The biosensors were incubated with various concentrations of GST or GST-SHMT2 for 10 min, followed by dissociation for 10 min. The dynamic parameters of the protein–protein interactions were determined using Octet Data Analysis HT software (ForteBio).

## SHMT Enzymatic Activity Assay.
For the in vitro assay of SET-mediated alteration of SHMT enzymatic activity, the purified SHMTs, MTHFD2, and SET were incubated in partial reaction buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.3, 100 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM PLP, and 1 mg/mL BSA) for 15 min at 25 °C, followed by the addition of THF (2 mM), serine (0.5 mM), and NAD+ (2 mM) for further incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. The enzymatic activity of SHMTs was determined by measuring NADH using an NADH/NAD+ Kit (Beyotime, S0175). For detection of cellular SHMT enzymatic activity, the cells were lysed with BC100 buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.3, 100 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 0.2 mM EDTA, and 0.1% Triton X-100) containing 1× protease inhibitor, and the SHMT-containing protein complex was purified by immunoprecipitation (IP) with an anti-SHMT antibody conjugated with Protein A/G beads. Equal amounts of bead-conjugated SHMT were subjected to enzymatic activity assays in complete reaction buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.3, 100 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM PLP, 2 mM THF, 0.5 mM serine, 2 mM NAD+, and 1 mg/mL BSA) following the procedure described above for the in vitro assay.

## Metabolomics and Metabolic Flux.
For the untargeted metabolomics assay, the metabolites from approximately 2 × 107 cells were extracted with 0.8 mL of 80% (v/v) precooled methanol at −80 °C for at least 2 h. The lysates were subsequently centrifuged at 15,000× g for 15 min at 4 °C. The metabolite-containing supernatant was transferred to a new 1.5 mL EP tube, spun to dry, and then reconstituted with 100 μL of 2% acetonitrile. Metabolomic analyses were conducted using a Waters ACQUITY H-class LC system coupled with an LTQ-Orbitrap mass spectrometer velos (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Metabolites were separated with an 18 min gradient on a Waters HSS C18 column (3.0 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. Mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid in H2O, and mobile phase B was acetonitrile. The gradient was set as follows: 0 to 1 min, 2% solvent B; 1 to 3 min, 2 to 55% solvent B; 3 to 8 min, 55 to 100% solvent B; 8 to 13 min, 100% solvent B; 13 to 13.1 min, 100 to 2% solvent B; 13.1 to 18 min, 2% solvent B. The column temperature was set at 45 °C. Full MS acquisition scanned from 100 to 1,000 m/z at a resolution of 60 K. Automatic gain control (AGC) target was 1 × 106 and maximum injection time (IT) was 100 ms. Subsequently, differential metabolite identification was performed by UPLC targeted-MS/MS analyses of QC sample. It acquired at a resolution of 15 K with AGC target of 5 × 105, maximum IT of 50 ms, and isolation window of 3 m/z. Collision energy was optimized as 40, 60, 80 for each target with higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) fragmentation. The raw spectra were further imported to Progenesis QI for metabolite annotation. In the present study, two databases were used for MS/MS matching: 1) Metlin MS/MS library (Waters, version 1.0.6499.51447, commercial, composed of authentic standards spectra obtained by orbitrap and TOF mass spectrometry) 2) Fragment library constructed using theoretical fragments calculated by a theoretical fragmentation algorithm: the “MetFrag” algorithm ( ). Confirmation of differential compounds was performed by the parameter of score value, calculated using mass error, isotope similarity, and fragmentation similarity. According to the score results of the reference standards, the threshold of score value was set at 35.0. The compound identifications are more reliable with higher score values obtained. For the targeted metabolite analysis, standard solutions were diluted to concentrations of 0.1, 1, 10, and 100 μM to establish calibration curves for the relative quantification of metabolite concentrations in the samples. Analyses were conducted using the Waters Acquity UPLC system coupled to a SCIEX QTRAP 6500 mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX). For the metabolic flux assay of the cultured cells, approximately 2 × 106 cells precultured in serine-free DMEM for 24 h were replenished with 0.4 mM L-serine-13C3 (Sigma, 604887) or L-serine-D3 (also known as 2,3,3-2H3-L-serine, MCE, HY-N0650S8) and cultured for the indicated period of time. The 13C- or 2H-labeled metabolites were extracted as described above. For the in vivo metabolic flux assay, the mice were injected through the tail vein with L-serine-13C3 (250 mg/kg) dissolved in 0.9% NaCl. Two hours after injection, the mice were killed. Equal amounts of lung tumors and adjacent lung tissues were excised, immediately flash-frozen, and pulverized for metabolite extraction. The samples were analyzed by using a TSQ Quantiva MS (Thermo).

## GEMMs of Lung Tumors.
4 × 107 PFU of Ad-Cre diluted in 10 mM CaCl2-containing DMEM was delivered into the lung tissues of KLLE or KLLE-SetFl/Fl mice (~6 wk old, male or female) through intranasal inhalation. Approximately 10 wk after Ad-Cre infection, lung tumor formation was monitored by using a Xenogen IVIS (PE) in the presence of 150 mg/kg D-luciferin (PerkinElmer, 122799; i.p.). The maintenance and experimental procedures for the mice were approved by the IACUC of Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences & Peking Union Medical College under the protocol (ACUC-A01-2023-048).

## Bioinformatic Analysis of One-Carbon Metabolic Activity.
The transcriptomic count matrix of LUAD was obtained from the TCGA database, and the count values were converted to log2 (TPM+1). The gene set for the “One carbon pool by folate” pathway (KO00670) was retrieved from the KEGG database. The ssGSEA method was used to quantify the pathway activity for each sample. The R packages of ggplot2 and ggpubr were used for data visualization. Pearson’s correlation was calculated to determine the relationship between the expression of SET and the activity of the “One carbon pool by folate” pathway.

## Statistical Analysis.
The data were presented as the mean ± SD. Differences were determined by using the two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t test, paired-samples t test, or two-way ANOVA with the Bonferroni post hoc test. The adjusted P value, if applicable, was determined by using the Benjamini–Hochberg (B-H) method. All the statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
