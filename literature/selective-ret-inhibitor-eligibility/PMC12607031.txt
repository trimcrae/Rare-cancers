## Background
Thyroid carcinomas (TCs) can harbor a variety of oncogenic drivers, but their prevalence according to the European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO) Scale for Clinical Actionability of Molecular Targets (ESCAT) is unknown. Timing and modalities of molecular testing are not standardized, and access to targeted therapies is heterogeneous across institutions and countries. We analyzed the clinical and molecular features of radioiodine-refractory (RAIR) TC and anaplastic TC (ATC) patients evaluated for actionable molecular targets (AMTs) at our referral center, with the aim of proposing a therapeutically relevant algorithm.

## Methods
Patients with RAIR TC and ATC who were candidates for systemic therapy underwent single-gene analyses (BRAF, RET, and NTRK) and/or DNA/RNA next-generation sequencing (NGS) on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue. Actionability was assessed using ESCAT and discussed at the molecular tumor board (MTB). Eligible patients could receive novel therapies (NTs; i.e. targeted therapies ± immunotherapy) via basket clinical trials, managed access programs, or off-label use. Objective responses and the duration of NT treatments were recorded.

## Results
From 2019 to 2023, 133 consecutive patients were included in the study. Overall, 270 molecular tests were carried out, with 58.8% evaluating gene mutations and 41.1% assessing gene rearrangements. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue archived for ≥5 years yielded a high rate of nonevaluable NGS results (43.2% versus 9.7%; P = 0.0001). Overall, 66.9% of the study population harbored at least one AMT (21% ESCAT I, 45.9% II-IIIA), and 43.6% received matched therapies (71.4% ESCAT I, 34.4% II-IIIA). Based on the a priori probability of detecting specific oncogenic drivers across TC histotypes, a histology-driven algorithm was designed to optimize AMT identification and expand NT access.

## Conclusions
Advanced TCs harbor a high prevalence of ESCAT I-III AMTs. Patients eligible for clinical trials with experimental drugs should undergo comprehensive genomic profiling and evaluation by an MTB to assess therapeutic actionability. A new biopsy is recommended for formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded specimens older than 5 years to increase the likelihood of obtaining evaluable results.

## Introduction
Follicular-derived thyroid carcinomas (TCs) encompass a spectrum of histotypes, including differentiated and poorly differentiated TC (DTC/PDTC) with papillary TC (PTC)/follicular TC (FTC)/oncocytic (OCA) differentiation, and anaplastic TC (ATC). Specimens with mixed differentiation patterns can be found, as some cases can lose differentiation across time.
Although most differentiated cases can be cured by surgery and radioiodine (RAI) therapy, up to one-third become radioiodine-refractory (RAIR). This condition has an incidence of 4-5/1 000 000/year and a poor prognosis—less than half of patients are alive at 5 years. The European Medical Agency (EMA) approved multikinase inhibitors (MKIs) lenvatinib and cabozantinib in first line (1L) and second line (2L), respectively, based on two phase III multicentric randomized clinical trials demonstrating their efficacy in comparison with placebo. , Of note, certain clinical conditions represent relative contraindications for antiangiogenic drugs (e.g. trachea/bronchus infiltration by tumor mass, active bleeding, severe cardiovascular comorbidities). , ATC is the most aggressive histotype, accounting for 2% of all TCs, with a median overall survival (mOS) around 6 months in unresectable cases from historical data. During the early stages, ATC can be treated with surgery followed by chemoradiation. However, ∼40% of patients are diagnosed with unresectable disease, often presenting with symptoms such as impaired swallowing and/or respiratory difficulties caused by rapidly enlarging neck masses.
Over the past decade, molecular profiling has enabled the use of targeted therapies (TTs) in advanced TC. Mutually exclusive oncogenic drivers such as BRAF mutation (present in ∼40% of papillary DTCs and ATCs), RET fusions (10%-20% of DTCs), and NTRK fusions (2-4% of all TC) are therapeutically actionable. The Thyroid Cancer European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO) Clinical Practice Guidelines (2022) recommend conducting molecular tests before the start of 1L therapy. A prompt assessment of BRAF gene status at ATC diagnosis is recommended to guide 1L treatment with dabrafenib-trametinib, , based on a phase II ROAR study. Testing for NTRK and RET fusions is also advised, supported by the pooled analyses of larotrectinib/entrectinib efficacy , and the phase II LIBRETTO-001 study with selpercatinib, , respectively. The ESMO Precision Medicine Working Group (2024) further advocates next-generation sequencing (NGS) in advanced TC, particularly for ESMO Scale for Clinical Actionability of Molecular Targets (ESCAT) level I alterations. However, the magnitude of clinical actionability of TC alterations beyond ESCAT I in the real world has not been described in the literature so far.
TC histologic phenotypes and underlying genotypes are strongly correlated. Different histotypes can harbor multiple potentially actionable molecular targets (AMTs) and high tumor mutational burden (TMB), especially in PDTC and ATC. While single-agent immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) such as spartalizumab and pembrolizumab have shown profound and durable responses in a small subset of TCs, predictive biomarkers such as programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression and TMB require further validation across histologies. , Combination strategies of TTs, MKIs, and ICIs are under clinical investigation. , 
Despite recent advances, access to TC molecular testing, novel therapies (NTs), and basket trials remains uneven across Europe. Compared with patients affected by other rare cancers, TC patients are underrepresented in basket trials. At our institution, patients with advanced TC are studied for AMT, and selected NGS results are discussed in the institutional molecular tumor board (MTB).
This study analyzes a large real-world cohort of patients with advanced follicular-derived TCs referred to our center. We describe their clinical and molecular features, frequencies and types of AMTs, ESCAT classification, accessibility to NTs (via healthcare system, clinical trials, compassionate use programs, or off-label), and treatment outcomes. Based on these findings and the probability of finding certain molecular alterations in different TC histotypes, we propose a histology-driven algorithm to optimize cost-effective molecular testing strategies for advanced TC.

## Methods
This retrospective observational study was conducted at Fondazione IRCCS Istituto Nazionale Tumori (INT), Milan, Italy, in accordance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol was approved by the institutional Ethics Committee (INT 152/23).
We included consecutive patients with unresectable RAIR DTC/PDTC, or ATC who received at least one molecular test at INT between January 2019 and December 2023. Patients diagnosed or treated externally were eligible only if molecular testing was carried out at our institution. Data were retrieved from institutional electronic medical records, with a cut-off date of 30 April 2024.
Clinical data included the age at diagnosis, tumor–node–metastasis (TNM) stage (American Joint Committee on Cancer 8th Edition), type of locoregional treatments (surgery, RAI, or external beam radiation therapy), cumulative RAI dose before RAIR onset, time (months) between diagnosis and referral to our Head and Neck Medical Oncology Unit, number and type of systemic therapies received before NTs, metastatic sites at NT start, treatment responses, and grade ≥3 adverse events [Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE)].
The histopathological classification followed internal review when available. Tumors were grouped as DTC (including PTC, FTC, OCA subtypes), PDTC, ATC, or mixed forms. For the analyses, mixed histologies were classified by the most aggressive component (ATC > PDTC > DTC). PD-L1 combined positive score (CPS) was evaluated as an exploratory biomarker ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105856).
Molecular testing included BRAFV600 PCR, DNA NGS (NGS 50-gene hotspot panel; Ion AmpliSeq Cancer Hotspot Panel v2, Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA), here referred to as DNA-50), or a 500-gene comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP; 500-gene CGP; Oncomine Comprehensive Assay Plus, Thermo Fisher Scientific, available since 2022), as previously described. Gene rearrangements were investigated by rearranged during transfection (RET) FISH, NTRK1/2/3 immunohistochemistry (IHC), and RNA-based NGS (50-gene panel; , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105856). Testing site (primary, nodal, and metastatic lesion) and the time interval between specimen collection and molecular testing were recorded. Detected AMTs were categorized by type, pathogenicity, and actionability score using ESCAT levels (five-tier system). , 
Single-gene test results were managed by the treating oncologist and not referred to the MTB. Cases with ESCAT >1 molecular alterations identified by NGS were discussed within the institutional MTB. Treatment access pathways included basket clinical trials, managed access programs, approved use under Law 648/1996 (e.g. dabrafenib and trametinib for BRAF-mutant ATC), and off-label via nominal request to the Italian Medicines Agency [Agenzia Italiana del Farmaco (AIFA)] 5% Fund (Law 326/2003). For patients with AMTs, we documented the type and accessibility of NT, as well as the reasons for the lack of novel treatment initiation.
Radiological responses were internally reviewed and categorized per RECIST version 1.1. Best response was used to determine overall response rate (ORR), and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the exact Clopper–Pearson method. Descriptive statistics were carried out. The median follow-up was calculated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method. Duration of treatment and mOS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method.
A literature review was carried out at data cut-off on PubMed to verify the current ESCAT classification of all identified AMTs, with a focus on less frequent alterations.

## Results
The study enrolled 155 patients referred to our Medical Oncology Unit between 2019 and December 2023, 133 of whom underwent molecular assessment ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105856).

## Study population—features, histologies, previous treatments
The study population was well-balanced (50.4% female), with a median age at diagnosis of 62.4 years, independent of sex. TNM stage at diagnosis was available for 87.2% of cases, showing an equal distribution between early/locally advanced and unresectable/metastatic (43.6%). All patients with unavailable TNM stage (12.8% cases) had undergone total thyroidectomy. The majority of patients were diagnosed with DTC (54.1%), followed by ATC (20.3%), mixed histologies (16%), and PDTC (9.8%; ). Total thyroidectomy was carried out in 78.9% of cases (86.5% of DTC/PDTC and 13% TC with anaplastic component), and 83.3% received RAI treatment, with a median cumulative dose of 300 milliCurie (mCi) before RAIR onset. Overall, 50.4% of patients received at least one molecular assessment before starting systemic therapy, 41.3% after 1L, and 8.3% after 2L ( ). 

## Molecular assessments—timing, type, and evaluability
The median time from TC diagnosis to presentation at our Medical Oncology Unit was 0.7 months for ATC and 57 months for DTC/PDTC. The median time from relapsed/metastatic ATC or RAIR TC diagnosis to first molecular assessment was 0.7 months for ATC and 38.4 months for DTC/PDTC. Molecular testing was carried out mostly on primary tumor specimens (60.9%), followed by lymph nodes (21.8%) and metastases (17.3%). In the first molecular assessment, BRAF V600 single-gene PCR was carried out in 42.1% of cases, and NGS DNA in 57.9%. In 85.7% of cases, at least one type of NGS was conducted, mostly DNA-50 (54.3%), associated with NGS RNA in 27.2%. CGP was carried out in 20 cases (17.5%), associated with NGS RNA in 14%. Overall, 270 molecular tests were carried out: 58.8% to evaluate gene mutations (65.2% DNA-50 and 34.8% PCR BRAF) and 41.1% to evaluate gene rearrangements (52.6% NGS RNA, 27.2% IHC NTRK, and 20.2% FISH RET). The NGS tests delivered unevaluable results in 12.4% of NGS DNA and 16.9% of NGS RNA samples. In our cohort, a longer interval from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue inclusion to NGS testing significantly increased the rate of nonevaluable tests, which was higher in specimens aged ≥5 years compared with those aged <5 years (P < 0.0001; , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105856).

## Pathogenic molecular alterations and tumor mutational burden
Overall, BRAF V600E was the most frequent gene alteration identified, by either PCR or NGS, present in 54% of PTC, 45.9% of ATC, 25% of PDTC, and absent in nonpapillary DTC. Three samples with uninformative NGS were subsequently analyzed with PCR and found to be positive for BRAF mutation. BRAF non-V600E mutations were identified in 4.3% of NGS DNA cases, predominantly in aggressive histologies (1 ATC, 2 PDTC, and 1 PTC; , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105856).
RAS gene mutations were found in 22.8% of evaluable NGS DNA, with RAS Q61X being the most common (80.9%). Specifically, RAS mutations were detected in 25% of PDTC, 22.7% of nonpapillary DTC, and 16.2% of ATC. Beyond the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, other mutations were observed in TP53 (20.6%, including one p.Y220C), in mammalian target of rapamycin pathway genes (PIK3CA 10.9%, PTEN 8.7%), and in DNA damage repair genes (ATM 6.5%). CGP revealed three pathogenic or likely pathogenic mutations in the DICER1 gene. In one male patient, diagnosed at 48 years with TC after a prior diagnosis of clear cell carcinoma of the kidney, a DICER1 D1709N mutation in exon 24 with a VAF of 60% prompted the MTB to recommend genetic counseling for DICER1 tumor predisposition syndrome. A TERT promoter mutation was identified in only one case by CGP.
RET rearrangements were detected in 11.6% of cases tested with either FISH or RNA-based NGS, and in 15.4% of the BRAF-wt subgroup, all of which were PTC. Two of five cases positive for RET fusion by FISH had unevaluable NGS RNA. An ETV6-NTRK fusion was identified in 2 out of 67 (2.9%) cases tested for NTRK fusions by either IHC NTRK or NGS RNA, and in 3.8% of the BRAF-wt subgroup (one PTC and one PDTC). All NTRK fusions were detected by NGS RNA.
In 11 cases, a biopsy of a metastatic site was carried out after progression on systemic therapy, revealing transformation to ATC in five cases (45.4%; two PTC with baseline BRAF mutation, three PDTC with baseline mutations in BRAF, PTEN, and NRAS) and TMB-high (TMB-H) in three cases (23.3%; two ATC and one OCA). All the pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants detected by NGS tests are reported in . 
TMB was available for samples characterized by CGP (20 patients, 15%), revealing four cases with TMB-H: one FTC, one OCA, and two ATC (one ex PDTC and one de novo), all identified at rebiopsy after 1L treatment with lenvatinib. PD-L1 CPS was assessed as an exploratory predictive biomarker of response to ICI in 23 of 133 cases and was positive (score ≥1) in 91.3% ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105856).

## Actionability of molecular alterations
Overall, 66.9% of the population (89/133) presented with at least one AMT (ESCAT I-IIIA), and 12.8% (17/133) had two or more AMTs, identified either by single-gene testing or NGS. ESCAT level I alterations (NTRK fusions, RET fusions, BRAF mutation in ATC, or TMB-H) were detected in 21% (28/133), while ESCAT level II-IIIA alterations (mutations in BRAF in DTC/PDTC, BRAF non-V600E, RAS Q61X, TP53 Y220C, PIK3CA, ATM, FGFR3, PTEN, and MTAP loss) were identified in 45.9% (61/133). Matching TT was administered to 43.6% of patients with AMTs, of whom 73.2% achieved an objective response.
BRAF V600E mutation was identified in 54% of PTC, 25% of PDTC, and 45.9% of ATC. In addition, four atypical BRAF mutations were detected by CGP, two of which co-occurred with BRAF V600E. Compared with BRAF-wt, patients with RAIR BRAF-mutant TC had a with higher frequency of cutaneous melanoma in their personal history (6/50 versus 0/79, P = 0.002), and had received a significantly lower median cumulative dose of RAI before the onset of RAIR: 200 mCi (n = 25, IQR 300 mCi) versus 373.5 mCi (n = 38, IQR 313.5 mCi; P = 0.041). Overall, 18 of 33 (54.5%) patients with BRAF V600E-mutant RAIR TCs received dabrafenib and trametinib, eight after at least one line of standard MKI [four in third line (3L)] and ten as 1L due to contraindications to antiangiogenic drugs (e.g. arrhythmias, encasement of neck vessels in the tumor mass, tracheal infiltration by tumor mass, concomitant anticoagulant medications increasing bleeding risk). ORR in the 1L subgroup was 90%, and in the ≥2L subgroup 62.5%. One patient with DTC harboring a Class II BRAF mutation (p.T599dup) received dabrafenib–trametinib in 3L, achieving stable disease. In the BRAF-mutant ATC population, 15 of 17 patients received dabrafenib–trametinib (n = 13 in 1L), with an ORR of 73.3%. Two cases that progressed on BRAF–MEK inhibitors received pembrolizumab in combination with TT, achieving partial response and stable disease, respectively. At data cut-off, the median duration of treatment was 7.82 months (95% CI 1.94-26.48 months) and mOS was 6.97 months (95% CI 2.66-28.52 months). In the PD-L1 CPS-positive population, 8 of 21 patients (4 BRAF-wt ATC, 2 PDTC, and 2 DTC) without available standard therapy options received ICI, and 6 of 8 achieved disease control.
All actioned AMTs in the study population are detailed in . Putative drugs for targets that could not be actioned, along with the reasons for exclusion, are also reported. Overall, this table provides preliminary data indicating that matched TTs in TCs can achieve objective responses with manageable toxicities, although efficacy and PFS for each treatment should be evaluated in drug-specific prospective clinical trials. 

## Discussion
This is the largest real-world study evaluating the actionability of molecular targets in advanced follicular-derived TCs using the ESCAT framework. Conducted at a high-volume referral center with expertise in a multidisciplinary approach to rare cancers, the OrienTHYring study underscores the value of the MTB in maximizing precision oncology access for eligible patients. Referral timing to our institution varied significantly by histotype, with ATCs referred within 1 month and non-ATCs after a median of 4 years, reflecting differing clinical urgencies. Among 133 patients, 66.9% harbored at least one AMT, including 21% with ESCAT I and 45.9% with ESCAT II-IIIA alterations. Of these, 43.6% received matched therapy (71.4% of ESCAT I and 34.4% of ESCAT II-IIIA). BRAF V600E mutations predominated in ATC (ESCAT I) and PTC (ESCAT II) and were mutually exclusive with NTRK and RET fusions. RAS mutations, particularly Q61X, were observed across histotypes and have been investigated in early-phase clinical trials. , In a previous study conducted at another referral center, 13 TC cases of unspecified histotypes were genotyped, and 11 received matched TT with RET, BRAF/MEK, or epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitors. 
While the prevalence of different TC histotypes and the rates of ESCAT I oncogenic driver mutations were comparable to those reported in the literature, the frequency of ESCAT >I scores had not been previously described. ESCAT I alterations typically do not require MTB discussion, whereas cases with ESCAT >I alterations often rely on MTB input to access investigational or off-label therapies. This study included first-level tests (e.g. PCR, IHC, FISH, small-panel NGS) and CGP, with several alterations (e.g. atypical BRAF mutations, MTAP loss, TMB) detectable only by the latter. This supports a stepwise molecular testing strategy, using targeted methods for high-probability ESCAT I alterations and reserving CGP for broader characterization in selected cases. This approach avoids the redundancy of conducting CGP upfront in all cases of advanced TC. The timing and tissue source of CGP are crucial, as advanced RAIR TC can acquire additional molecular alterations and lose differentiation over time or across lines of therapy. Moreover, the transformation from DTC to ATC significantly impacts therapeutic choices. Rebiopsy after disease progression can reveal on-target, actionable acquired resistances in NTRK and RET fusions, , as well as novel targets in patients eligible for clinical trials. Specimen storage duration can also affect tissue quality and the evaluability of molecular assessments, particularly for NGS.
Based on the OrienTHYring findings, the prevalence of certain oncogenic drivers in specific histologies, and the ability to detect AMTs in a cost-effective manner, we propose a histology-driven algorithm for sequential molecular testing, which is currently employed at our institution ( ). For ATCs, immediate BRAF testing (via RT-PCR or IHC) should be prioritized to guide 1L treatment decisions. Notably, the concept of rapid BRAF testing using RT-PCR or IHC was also proposed in a previous study focused on NTRK testing and applied to all TC histotypes. However, this approach is not applied to FTC and OCA, as the likelihood of detecting a BRAF mutation in these histologies is extremely low, and NGS would be more informative. Rebiopsy is recommended in cases of nonevaluable NGS results, after progression on standard therapies, and/or in the event of rapid disease progression with suspected ATC transformation. For specimens stored for >5 years and lacking evaluable NGS RNA results, if rebiopsy is not feasible, an alternative single-gene testing approach may be considered. ESMO guidelines caution that false-negative IHC results are mainly reported for NTRK3 fusions, which are the most frequent in TCs. Therefore, NTRK fusions should preferably be assessed using NGS RNA testing. The algorithm aligns with the workflow for MTB evaluation recently proposed by the ESMO Precision Oncology Working Group, and provides additional details on initial molecular assessments, taking into account the different TC histotypes. Notably, current international TC guidelines do not outline specific workflows for test selection in molecular assessment, nor do they distinguish between hotspot NGS and CGP. , , This sequential approach provides a pragmatic workflow to optimize cost-effective testing while maximizing access to TTs. 
A recent study reported that patients with rare cancers benefit most from CGP compared with those with common cancers. Despite the high prevalence of AMTs, participation of TC patients in basket trials remains low. Improving access to precision treatments requires early identification of ESCAT I alterations—ideally before initiation of systemic therapy—and timely referral to centers with CGP and MTB facilities, particularly in cases of absent upfront actionable alterations and/or inability to receive standard systemic therapy. The recently presented multibasket, randomized, phase II ROME study supports the clinical benefit of a matched TTs approach in terms of ORR and survival. 
The limitations of this study are its monocentric and retrospective design. However, the use of cases from a single referral center allowed for homogeneous handling of clinical data, molecular testing, and MTB discussions. Prospective studies in non-ATC cases would require long follow-up due to the indolent nature of many TCs. Furthermore, the in-depth collection of clinical data allowed us to observe features typical of BRAF-mutant compared with BRAF wild-type TCs, such as the high rate of personal history of malignant melanoma. An association between melanoma and TC has been previously described, but not specifically in relation to BRAF-mutant TC. Both histologies share a high frequency of BRAF mutations and sensitivity to BRAF/MEK inhibitors. This finding could be further investigated in large multicenter studies to explore whether BRAF-mutant TC patients may benefit from regular dermatologic surveillance. Another clinical feature significantly more prevalent in BRAF-mutant TCs compared with wild-type cases was the earlier onset of RAI resistance, typically after 200 mCi. This aligns with the known mechanisms of MAPK-driven downregulation of the sodium iodide symporter. Strategies to restore RAI sensitivity using targeted agents are under investigation, with several ongoing trials exploring optimal treatment duration and dosing. 
Clinical trials investigating the combination of ICIs and TTs are ongoing, , and studies with pan-RAF and RAS inhibitors are paving the way to target RAS Q61X mutations. , Since June 2022, dabrafenib and trametinib have been FDA-approved with tissue-agnostic indications for BRAF-mutant solid tumors, and National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines support their use in DTC/PDTC when MKIs are contraindicated. EMA has not approved BRAF/MEK inhibitors for patients with BRAF-mutant non-ATCs, and compassionate use programs are currently unavailable, highlighting an unmet clinical need along with an ethical concern regarding the principle of health equity. Despite the rapid rise of precision oncology over the last decade and the limited availability of standard therapies, patients with advanced TC in Europe still face challenges in accessing innovative treatments, particularly those with ATC. Furthermore, data on the burden of late toxicities and health-related quality of life during MKI treatments remain limited. Additional studies are needed to compare quality of life outcomes across MKI, TT, and ICI therapies. Structured compassionate use programs provide a valuable opportunity for patients with rare cancers who have limited licensed treatment options and/or lack access to basket studies to receive potentially active therapies based on a strong biological rationale, while also collecting real-world data on drug activity and safety. Nevertheless, prospective clinical trials with innovative drugs for patients with advanced TC are urgently needed, requiring close collaboration among endocrinologists, nuclear medicine physicians, and oncologists to strengthen multidisciplinary research within an international network of institutions focused on precision therapies for these rare and challenging cancers.
