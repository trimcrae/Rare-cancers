## Abstract

## Background/Aim
This study aimed to explore changes in cardiovascular performance among patients with thyroid cancer following thyroidectomy, radioiodine therapy (RAIT) and thyroid-stimulating hormone suppression therapy (TST), with or without treatment with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs).

## Patients and Methods
We enrolled 32 patients who underwent thyroidectomy and subsequent RAIT (except for one patient who underwent partial thyroidectomy only) and TST, with or without TKI therapy. We assessed myocardial perfusion using quantitative myocardial perfusion imaging (qMPI), myocardial blood flow (MBF), and coronary flow reserve (CFR) via dynamic single photon-emission computed tomography/computer tomography. We also analyzed data from laboratory tests or heart-related circulation markers, such as N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) to compare patient results, including correlations with different TKIs, RAIT, and TST.

## Results
qMPI indicated that the TKI-treated group (n=19) exhibited reduced MBF, CFR, and coronary flow capacity compared to the non-TKI-treated group. The right coronary artery (RCA) territory was notably affected by TKI treatment, particularly during the stress phase: RCA-stress blood flow: 2.4±0.6 vs. 1.9±0.7 ml/min/g; RCA-CFR: 2.3±0.8 vs. 2.93±0.9 for TKI-treated vs. non TKI-treated groups, respectively. This effect was more pronounced in the subgroup undergoing sequential anti-vascular endothelial growth factor TKI treatment (e.g. sorafenib and lenvatinib) (n=4) compared to non-TKI-treated controls. Additionally, TKI-treated patients showed a higher mean NT-ProBNP level (203±322.2 pg/ml) than non-TKI-treated patients (39.1±36 pg/ml). Abnormal MBF/CFR was also associated with higher cumulative RAIT dose and lower thyrotropin level.

## Conclusion
TKI therapy, especially anti-vascular endothelial growth factor TKI, can negatively affect myocardial perfusion and lead to coronary microcirculation dysfunction, reducing MBF/CFR during the stress phase. The link between cumulative RAIT dose, lower thyroid-stimulating hormone level, and abnormal MBF/CFR highlights the need for further research.

Keywords: Thyroid cancer, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, radioiodine therapy, thyroid-stimulating hormone suppression, myocardial perfusion, coronary flow reserve, myocardial blood flow, SPECT/CT, NT-proBNP, anti-VEGF therapy, coronary microcirculation

## Introduction
Thyroidectomy followed by radioiodine therapy (RAIT) is the primary treatment for managing differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) with an intermediate to high risk of recurrence or persistent disease ( ). Patients who have undergone thyroidectomy will experience a period of hypothyroidism until levothyroxine supplementation is administered. This situation also occurs in some patients with DTC being prepared for RAIT or whole-body scans ( ). After initial treatment, patients with DTC often undergo thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH)-suppression therapy through excessive thyroxine intake. Maintaining the TSH level below 0.1 mIU/l is advantageous for inhibiting tumor growth, although it may lead to a subclinical hyperthyroidism state, which carries a certain risk of cardiovascular events. The advantages and disadvantages of TSH suppression in DTC have been thoroughly reviewed by Biondi and Cooper ( ). Several cardiovascular risk factors have been identified in patients receiving excessive levothyroxine therapy as a compromise to control tumor growth ( ). Indeed, the risk of cardiovascular events increases in patients with DTC, and a lower TSH level is associated with increased cardiovascular mortality ( , ), with atrial fibrillation being the most commonly presented symptom. However, Hong et al. were unable to demonstrate a significant difference in cardiac performance, structure, and cardiac-related biomarkers [e.g., plasma N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NTproBNP)] between patients with DTC under TSH-suppressive therapy and the control group ( ). Previous studies have indicated that subclinical or overt hypothyroidism can lead to left ventricular dysfunction and impaired vascular relaxation, as insufficient serum thyroid hormone result in bradycardia, endothelial dysfunction, increased intima-media thickness, diastolic dysfunction, increased vascular resistance, and pericardial effusion ( - ). Inoue et al. found significant associations of subclinical hypothyroidism and high-normal TSH levels with cardiovascular death, particularly notable in females or those aged over 60 years ( ). Another concern for patients with DTC regarding the potential risk of cardiovascular mortality or morbidity is the use of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). For patients with RAI-refractory DTC, TKIs including sorafenib and lenvatinib, have been proven to enhance progression-free survival through the DECISION and SELECT trials ( - ); however, treatment-related adverse effects, including cardiovascular events, have been reported. Lenvatinib-induced hypertension is among the most common treatment-related adverse effect ( ). Consequently, anti-hypertensive medication or adjusting the TKI dose is considered to mitigate this issue ( ). While most literature agrees on the increased cardiovascular risks for patients with DTC in hypothyroidism, subclinical hyperthyroidism, or those taking anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) TKIs, the precise mechanisms in various settings may differ and remain to be clarified. This study aimed to investigate the potential cardiovascular risks for patients with advanced thyroid cancer after their initial therapy (including thyroidectomy, with or without RAIT) and TKI therapy.

## Patients and Methods
This study was conducted from January 2022 to December 2022 and aimed to monitor patients diagnosed with advanced thyroid cancer who had undergone surgical with or without RAIT, or were receiving TKIs, including lenvatinib, sorafenib, dabrafenib plus trametinib, and pralsetinib. A cohort of 32 patients was selected for this prospective study. Exclusion criteria included patients with medullary thyroid carcinoma, pre-existing cardiovascular disease, hypotension (systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg), pre-existing hepatic or renal impairment, allergy to dipyridamole, active asthma, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.
Comprehensive assessments were performed to measure thyroid function and various cardiovascular risk factors, such as lipid profiles, blood pressure, and diabetes status. The study aimed to correlate these parameters with thyroid function and clinical management features to explore changes in myocardial perfusion and overall cardiovascular health. Additionally, we specifically investigated the incidence of coronary microvascular disease (CMVD) in this patient population under different therapeutic regimens.
Laboratory blood tests. Participants were asked to fast overnight. Approximately 10 ml of blood was collected from participants through venipuncture. The blood tests covered thyroid function tests (free T4, TSH, T3), thyroglobulin, and blood biochemistry parameters (aspartate aminotransferase, alanine transaminase, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, cholesterol, triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein, blood glucose), in addition to the cardiac biomarker NT-proBNP. NT-proBNP was selected as a cardiac biomarker due to its potential in identifying and assessing cardiotoxicity related to cancer treatment. It is indicative of cardiac ventricular filling pressures. Previous research has shown that elevated levels of this biomarker are associated with an increased risk of cardiotoxicity ( ) and a decline in ventricular function ( ).
Dynamic single photon-emission computed tomography/ computer tomography (SPECT/CT) protocol. All participants were instructed to refrain from eating for 4 h and to avoid caffeinated beverages and cigarettes for more than 24 to 48 h before undergoing the dynamic SPECT/CT study. The measurement of myocardial blood flow (MBF) was conducted both under resting conditions and under stress, induced by dipyridamole. The rest/stress dynamic SPECT/CT scans were carried out using a dedicated Siemens Symbia-S SPECT system. Before dynamic image acquisition, 1 mCi of 99mtechnetium sestamibi (MIBI) was injected intravenously for visualization of heart contour and adjustment of patient positioning. For the resting condition, a sequence of rapid gantry rotations with a 180˚ arc was configured to collect dynamic raw data before administering 13 mCi intravenous injection of MIBI, followed by a low-dose chest CT for attenuation correction. After waiting for 1 h, dipyridamole was administered intravenously at a rate of 0.56 mg/kg over 4 min; 3 min later, a similar sequence of rapid gantry rotations with a 180˚ arc was configured to gather dynamic raw data before intravenous injection of 30 mCi of MIBI. An antidote was administered to mitigate any adverse effects caused by dipyridamole, such as headaches or gastrointestinal discomfort.
Image analysis and MBF/coronary flow reserve (CFR) calculation. All dynamic SPECT images were reconstructed using 3-dimensional ordered subset expectation maximization, incorporating physical corrections and noise filtration via the matched projection method. Time-activity curves for both the blood pool and myocardium were generated by delineating regions of interest on two consecutive mid-ventricular short-axis image planes for the blood pool and four for myocardial activity. For myocardial time-activity curves (TACs), sectorial regions were automatically positioned at the endocardial border of the short-axis image planes. These dynamic datasets were analyzed using radial activity profiles and the TACs were fitted to estimate MBF values using a three-compartment model as described by Hutchins et al. CFR were calculated as the ratio of hyperemic MBF (post-dipyridamole) to resting MBF.
We adopted the methodology outlined by Hsu et al., which determined that average MBF at rest for healthy individuals was 0.57±0.14 ml/g/min, increasing to 2.69±0.57 ml/g/min under stress conditions ( , ). Our protocol employed qMPI, a technique noted for its relatively higher sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy in detecting multi-vessel coronary artery disease compared to traditional perfusion imaging ( , ). This approach significantly enhanced our ability to detect and quantify Coronary CMVD.
Statistical analysis. Results are presented as the mean± standard deviation or frequencies (%). The statistical significance of the results from dynamic SPECT/CT was evaluated using Student’s t-test, and included stress MBF, rest MBF, CFR and coronary flow capacity (CFC). A value of p<0.05 was deemed to indicate statistical significance. All statistical analyses were conducted using the SPSS software package (SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 17.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

## Results
Overall population. We prospectively enrolled 35 patients from January 2022 and December 2022 who visited our Nuclear Medicine Department for thyroid cancer management. Three patients were excluded: one patient with pathology revised to be pre-cancerous neoplasm and two other patients without 72-h caffeine abstinence before Dynamic SPECT/CT. Thus 32 patients were evaluated in this study. The demographic and clinical characteristics are noted in .
 Classification of thyroid cancer and related treatment. Our study included patients with various types of thyroid cancer: 25 with papillary thyroid cancer (78.1%), five with follicular thyroid cancer (15.6%), and two (6.2%) with anaplastic thyroid cancer. Of these, 29 patients (90.6%) underwent total or near-total thyroidectomy, while 1 (9.4%) underwent partial thyroidectomy. Central neck lymph node dissection was performed on 11 patients (84.6%), and two patients received left neck lymph node dissection. RAIT was administered to 30 patients (93.8%). The two patients with anaplastic thyroid cancer were deemed unsuitable for RAIT. Regarding medication, 19 patients were treated with TKIs, including 10 with sorafenib (400-800 mg daily), thirteen with lenvatinib (10-20 mg daily), 5 with combined dabrafenib and trametinib therapy, and 1 with pralsetinib (200-400 mg daily). The dosage regimens were adjusted based on treatment-related adverse effects and the anticipated response. Notably, in the lenvatinib-treated subgroup, four patients had previously been treated with sorafenib before switching to lenvatinib as a salvage treatment. All this information is summarized in . There were no significant differences in lipid profile, liver functional tests and renal functional tests between TKI-treated patients with thyroid cancer and non-TKI-treated patients.
 MBF/CFR of patients with thyroid cancer with vs. without TKI treatment. In our study, we compared MBF and CFR between 19 TKI-treated patients and 13 non-TKI-treated patients, as measured by quantitative myocardial perfusion scans. Both groups exhibited CFR values within the normal range (>2.1), in line with previously established cut-off values ( ). However, the TKI-treated patients displayed comparatively lower stress-phased MBF and CFR in the territories of the left ventricle (LV), left anterior descending artery, right coronary artery (RCA), and left circumflex artery (see ). Notably, the TKI-treated group had significantly lower measurements than the non-TKI-treated group: the former had an RCA-stress blood flow of 1.9±0.7 ml/min/g and an RCA-CFR of 2.3±0.8, compared to the latter's RCA-stress blood flow of 2.4±0.6 ml/min/g and an RCA-CFR of 2.93±0.9. Conversely, there was no significant difference in MBF during the rest phase between the two groups.
 CFC of patients with thyroid cancer with vs. without TKI therapy. CFC is used to assess the severity of coronary artery disease by integrating MBF during stress and rest phases, as well as CFR, through cardiac positron-emission tomography/CT or dynamic SPECT/CT scans ( , ). In our study, using quantitative myocardial perfusion scans, we measured the differences in CFC between TKI-treated (n=19) and non-TKI-treated (n=13) patients with thyroid cancer (as detailed in ). The CFC in the non-TKI-treated group showed a greater area with definitely normal LV function (47.2±36% vs. 23.1±31.7%) and normal LV function (42.1±29.3% vs. 39.2±26%), but less area with mild and moderate abnormal LV function. This indicates a potential for microvascular dysfunction of the LV in TKI-treated thyroid cancer patients.
 MBF/CFR of patients with thyroid cancer treated with two anti-VEGF TKIs vs. those without TKI treatment. Both sorafenib and lenvatinib are multi-kinase inhibitors that primarily target the VEGF receptor to achieve tumor control ( , ), while also presenting a risk of cardiovascular toxicity ( ). Consequently, we specifically compared the MBF and CFR in patients with thyroid cancer who were treated with both lenvatinib and sorafenib to those in the non-TKI-treated group. According to the data presented in , the group treated with both anti-VEGF TKIs (n=4) exhibited generally lower CFR values, even falling below the normal cutoff value of 2.3. It is also important to note the significantly lower stress-phase MBF in the RCA and left circumflex artery territories for this group (p<0.05), whereas the difference in rest-phase MBF between the TKI-treated and non-TKI-treated groups was minimal.
 CFC of patients with thyroid cancer treated with two anti-VEGF TKIs vs. those without TKI treatment. The analysis of CFC results revealed that patients treated with two consecutive anti-VEGF TKIs (n=4) exhibited a significantly lesser area of definitely normal LV function compared to those not treated with TKIs (47.4±34.6% vs. 5.6±6.5%, p<0.01). Conversely, the area of mildly abnormal and moderately abnormal LV function was greater in this group (as detailed in ).
 NT-ProBNP in patients with thyroid cancer with and without TKI treatment. NT-ProBNP is a crucial biomarker for cardiac diseases and offers a straightforward diagnostic method for congestive heart failure ( , , , ). In our comparison of NT-ProBNP levels between TKI-treated and non-TKI-treated patients with thyroid cancer, it was observed that the former group exhibited a relatively higher NT-ProBNP level (203±322.2 pg/ml) compared to those not undergoing TKI treatment (39.1±36 pg/ml, p<0.05).
Cumulative RAI dose and abnormal stress-MBF/CFR in patients with thyroid cancer. Previous studies have established that the normal cut-off value for LV-stress MBF is 1.8 ml/min/g, and that for LV-CFR is 2.3 ( ). Accordingly, we categorized our patient cohort based on these established cut-off values: 12 patients with abnormal LV-stress MBF (<1.8 ml/min/g) vs. 20 patients with normal LV-stress MBF; and 8 patients with abnormal LV-CFR (<2.3) vs. 24 patients with normal LV-CFR. We then compared the cumulative RAI dose administered to these patients between those with normal and abnormal LV-stress MBF or LV-CFR. The cumulative RAI doses were observed to be somewhat higher in patients with abnormal LV-stress MBF compared to those with normal LV-stress MBF (488±521 vs. 254±194 mCi, p=0.078). Similarly, cumulative RAI activities were higher in the group with abnormal LV-CFR compared to the group with normal LV-CFR (513±634 vs. 285±206 mCi, p=0.349). However, the differences did not reach statistical significance in either analysis.
TSH suppression and abnormal stress MBF/CFR in patients with thyroid cancer. To investigate the association between TSH suppression and abnormal stress MBF/CFR, we analyzed the serum TSH levels of patients with normal versus abnormal stress MBF/CFR. Three patients, who exhibited significant fluctuations in serum TSH levels during the study, were excluded from this analysis. The remaining dataset included 12 patients with abnormal LV-stress MBF (<1.8 ml/min/g) and 17 patients with normal LV-stress MBF; 8 patients with abnormal LV-CFR (<2.3) and 21 patients with normal LV-CFR. We compared the serum TSH levels between patients with normal and abnormal LV-stress MBF and/or LV-CFR. The comparisons showed little difference in serum TSH level between the groups ( ).
 

## Discussion
Our study highlights the potential cardiovascular risks associated with TKI therapy in patients with advanced thyroid cancer. Quantitative myocardial perfusion analysis revealed significant alterations in MBF and CFR within the RCA territory during the stress phase for TKI-treated versus non-TKI-treated patients with thyroid cancer. Specifically, RCA-stress blood flow and RCA-CFR were significantly lower in the TKI-treated group (1.9±0.7 ml/min/g and 2.3±0.8, respectively) compared to the non-TKI-treated group (2.4±0.6 ml/min/g and 2.93±0.9, respectively). We also observed a trend toward reduced CFC in TKI-treated patients with DTC, characterized by an increased area of abnormal CFC and a reduced area of normal CFC. This trend was particularly notable in the subgroup of patients who had been treated with both sorafenib and lenvatinib, two consecutive anti-VEGF TKIs. Following an extensive review of the existing literature, only a limited number of studies have explicitly examined the potential impact of TKI on coronary artery blood flow ( ). Our current investigation substantiates this hypothesis, demonstrating a significant influence of TKI on the reduction of coronary artery perfusion. We report a case of a female patient showing significant differences in MBF, CFR and CFC while on and off the medication lenvatinib. The comparative examinations highlight that the substantial variation in her coronary circulation correlated with the administration and cessation of this drug ( ).
 Additionally, elevated levels of serum NT-pro BNP, an early marker of heart damage, were observed in TKI-treated patients with thyroid cancer. Our findings also suggest a potential association between higher cumulative doses of RAIT and abnormal myocardial coronary perfusion and flow reserve. To the best of our knowledge, our current study is the first to utilize qMPI for measuring various coronary circulation parameters. Traditional myocardial perfusion imaging coupled with stress testing primarily aims to identify myocardial ischemia in the LV for diagnosing ischemic heart disease linked to different coronary arteries. Pharmacological stress induced by dipyridamole, a nonselective competitive inhibitor of type 2 adenosine receptors, is commonly employed to achieve coronary dilation ( ). However, the effectiveness of traditional myocardial perfusion imaging is constrained in scenarios such as balanced ischemia, post-myocardial infarction, and LV cardiomyopathy, among others. In contrast, qMPI, conducted through positron-emission tomography or dynamic SPECT/CT, facilitates the measurement of several quantitative myocardial perfusion parameters ( , ), including MBF, CFR, and CFC. This approach not only aids in diagnosing coronary artery disease but also in assessing patient outcomes and cardiac performance ( ). CFR, the ratio of stress-induced blood flow during coronary vasodilation to resting blood flow, indicates coronary vasodilator capacity and clinical outcomes. CFC is used to determine the severity of coronary disease by integrating stress MBF, rest MBF, and CFR ( ). Additionally, another advantage of qMPI is its ability to detect CMVD. Characteristics of CMVD include mildly and diffusely reduced CFC, borderline low-flow, and abnormal CFR on qMPI ( , ). Such patterns may be considered as evidence of endothelial dysfunction within the coronary vasculature, which is linked to an increased risk of cardiac diseases. Furthermore, qMPI serves as a crucial diagnostic tool for distinguishing CMVD from suspected ischemic diseases. This is exemplified by a prospective study that employed SPECT/CT to examine myocardial perfusion and investigate the correlation between the free T4/T3 ratio in patients with thyroid dysfunction who did not have overt coronary artery obstruction. Significant reductions in stress MBF/CFR within the CMVD group were documented ( ). In our study, we observed reduced MBF/CFR in patients with thyroid cancer treated with TKI, indicating that CMVD represents a key adverse cardiovascular effect induced by TKI therapy. Anti-VEGF TKIs for advanced thyroid cancer, including sorafenib and lenvatinib, have been approved for clinical use since 2015 and 2016, respectively. These TKIs, known for their multiple molecular targets, are categorized as being anti-VEGF due to their significant anti-angiogenic potency. However, VEGF blockade is associated with reduced production of nitric oxide and prostaglandin-I2, alongside vascular endothelial cell dysfunction ( ). It is considered systemic administration of anti-VEGF signaling drugs may cause systemic complications, such as hypertension, atherosclerosis or thrombosis ( , ) and increase risk of cardiotoxicity in patients with cancer ( ). Contrary to the anti-angiogenic tumorostatic potency of the anti-VEGF TKIs, sorafenib ( ) and lenvatinib ( ), several other TKIs, such as combined dabrafenib and trametinib, along with pralsetinib, achieve tumor control primarily through the blockade of the mitogen-activated protein kinase pathway ( - ). In our study, cases that were treated with two sequential anti-VEGF TKIs exhibited significantly reduced MBF, CFR, and CFC. This finding suggests that the clinical use of anti-VEGF TKIs for patients with thyroid cancer should be carefully monitored with respect to their cardiovascular performance, particularly in terms of myocardial perfusion.
NT-proBNP, a peptide consisting of 108 amino acids secreted by the cardiac ventricles, can be measured in serum. It is used clinically to monitor coronary disease and congestive heart failure. An early study demonstrated the impact of thyroid dysfunction on NT-proBNP levels ( ). A subsequent study even claimed its important role as a cardiac thyroid hormone ( ). The most recent study in particular points out its importance in indicating cardiovascular risk in patients with thyroid cancer ( ).
In our study, we observed a significant difference in serum NT-proBNP levels between patients with thyroid cancer undergoing TKI therapy and those not receiving such treatment. Consequently, further investigation is warranted to explore the clinical utility of measuring this biomarker in patients when TKI therapy is employed for thyroid cancer control. The literature indicates a scarcity of published studies discussing the myocardial perfusion of the LV or related cardiac parameters in patients with thyroid cancer treated with various TKIs. Our prospective study successfully utilized qMPI to evaluate important coronary vascular parameters via dynamic SPECT/CT. Furthermore, it has highlighted differences in serum NT-proBNP levels among patients with thyroid cancer under different treatment regimens.
Study limitations. Firstly, only a small sample size of patients with advanced thyroid cancer was enrolled for cardiovascular evaluation, despite them receiving the latest targeted therapy. Secondly, performing another dynamic SPECT/CT scan for comparison on each patient when they discontinue or change their TKI therapy was not feasible. Thirdly, regarding the impact of accumulated RAI dose or TSH level on MBF or CFR, interference from the patient's current use of TKI therapy may have been a confounding factor.

## Conclusion
We successfully utilized qMPI to evaluate important coronary vascular parameters via dynamic SPECT/CT among patients with thyroid cancer under TKI therapy. This study highlighted differences in serum NT-proBNP levels, finding that anti-VEGF TKIs in particular can negatively affect myocardial perfusion and lead to coronary microcirculation dysfunction. The link between cumulative RAIT dose, lower TSH level, and abnormal MBF/CFR highlights the need for further research.
