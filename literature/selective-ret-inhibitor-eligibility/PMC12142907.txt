## Abstract

## Background
Dose-expansion cohort (DEC) has been increasingly used as the pivotal trial to demonstrate preliminary efficacy and safety of target anticancer drugs. We aimed to investigate the reporting quality in the published articles of DECs.

## Methods
We retrospectively identified the published articles of DEC pivotal trials of the FDA-approved targeted anticancer drugs between Jan 1st, 2012 and March 31st, 2023. All published articles were evaluated by using the CONSORT Dose-finding Extension (CONSORT-DEFINE) guidance.

## Results
Forty-one articles on DEC trials were identified, of which 32 (78.0%) were published in journals with a high impact factor (≥ 50). The median proportion of adequately reported items in the evaluated 41 articles was 83.7% (with an interquartile range of 79.1% to 88.4%). Inadequacies were observed in several areas of DEC trial reporting. Specifically, planned cohort size (item 3a8), dose modification and discontinuation (item 5b), sample size calculation (item 7a), interim analysis (item 7b), adjusted analyses (item 12b) for efficacy endpoints, data monitoring (item 26), safety review committee (item 26a), and the role of funders (item 25) were mentioned in only 4 (9.8%), 17 (41.5%), 30 (73.2%), 14 (34.1%), 30 (73.2%), 6 (14.6%), 4 (9.8%), and 12 (29.3%) articles, respectively. Additionally, inconsistencies were noted in the definition of DEC across different articles, revealing irregular naming methods being used.

## Conclusions
Despite being published in high-impact journals, DEC pivotal trials were inadequately reported. There is still a need to improve the completeness and transparency in reporting DEC trials to facilitate external review and evaluation of the DEC evidence.

## Background
In the past decade, the landscape of pivotal clinical trials for anticancer drug approvals has changed dramatically [ , ]. Nearly 60% of pivotal trials supporting the FDA approval of targeted anticancer drugs were early-phase clinical trials (EPCTs) [ ]. EPCT is defined as the trials before phase 3 and mainly consists phase1/2 clinical trials such as dose-escalating, dose-expansion cohort (DEC) [ , ]. DECs archived fast growth due to their smaller sample size and easier conducting than that of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) for the FDA accelerated approval (AA) [ ]. A variety of small molecular kinase inhibitors especially for those targeting cancer driver genes (CDGs) such as osimertinib [ ], lorlatinib [ ], and pralsetinib [ ] adopted DEC evidence in new drug application (NDA) to the FDA. Moreover, several blockbuster antibody drugs such as pembrolizumab [ ] and durvalumab [ ] have been initially approved for marketing on the basis of DEC evidences. To date, DEC prevails in exploring primary efficacy and safety outcomes for targeted anticancer drugs [ – ].
The structure of DECs exhibits significant discrepancies compared to RCTs [ ]. Based on the results from the previous dose-escalating study, the expansion cohort further recruits more eligible participants and builds one or more additional disease-specific cohorts with objectives of efficacy assessments [ ]. For example, after the dose escalation to find the optimal treatment dose (also called recommended phase 2 dose [RP2D]), the expansion cohort converges on this RP2D dose level and further recruits qualified patients to build one or several indication-matched cohorts to explore efficacy [ , ]. The features of DEC structure typically are “open-label,” “nonrandomized,” “multiple cohorts in different indications or conditions,” and “each cohort without parallel control group” [ , – ]. The existing standards in the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2010 Statement for reporting parallel group randomized trials could not be appropriately applied to DEC trials [ ]. Therefore, the CONSORT Dose-finding Extension (CONSORT-DEFINE) guidance was developed by trial methodologists in 2023 for the explicit purpose of improving clinical trial reporting [ ]. The CONSORT-DEFINE guidance provides a valuable framework for reporting early phase trials including DECs, which can help ensure that all relevant information is included and that the structure is clear and consistent [ ].
With the rapid development of precision oncology, more and more molecular targets were identified and the related drugs were clinically developed and approved on the basis of DEC evidences [ ]. Most of these DEC evidences were published in high-impact journals such as The Lancet and New England Journal of Medicine (NEJM). The transparent and adequate reporting of DEC is a very important window for other researchers or investigators to know the trial and facilitate the systematic review and critical appraisal of the DEC evidences [ , ].
The absence of specific reporting guidelines in the past for DEC trials may lead to inconsistencies and insufficient reporting quality. This study was conducted to retrospectively evaluate the reporting quality of all the published articles that reported DEC trials of the targeted anticancer drugs approved by the FDA. We systematically checked and evaluated these articles using the checklist in the CONSORT-DEFINE guidance [ ]. Given that DEC trials were conducted and published before specific reporting guidelines were available, identifying the reporting gaps that existed prior to the development of CONSORT-DEFINE could raise awareness of the need for standardized reporting and encourage the adoption of the CONSORT-DEFINE guidance 2023 in future DEC trials to improve transparency and reproducibility.

## Methods

## Study design
This study was a retrospective cross-sectional study that evaluated the reporting quality of DEC trials supporting the indications for targeted anticancer drugs approved by the FDA. Importantly, the reporting guidelines used in this study were developed after these DEC trials had already been conducted and published.

## Search strategy
We identified all the targeted anticancer drugs’ indications approved by the FDA between Jan 1st, 2012 and March 31st, 2023. Before 2012, no indication was approved on the basis of dose-expansion cohort. Information of the original and supplemental application approvals were manually searched from the Drugs@FDA database (https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cder/daf/). We then reviewed the full prescribing information (PI) and approval documents (FDA medical reviews and the cancer drug labels) to determine whether each drug was a targeted anticancer therapy and to extract indications that were approved based on DEC trials. Both the indications of monotherapy and combination therapy were included. We excluded the nontherapeutic indications such as diagnostic and contrast drug indications. We also cross-verified pivotal clinical trials supporting each identified indication using ClinicalTrials.gov, based on the National Clinical Trial (NCT) number provided in the FDA approval documents. We selected the pivotal trials of dose-expansion cohorts that supported the approvals of the indications. Peer-reviewed publications linked to the NCT number of these pivotal trials were identified.

## Data extraction

## Basic information
The obtained data of basic information of the published articles included year of publication, journal name, journal impact factor, tumor site, product type (small molecular kinase inhibitor, antibody, enzyme inhibitor, or antibody–drug conjugate), treatment type (monotherapy, combination), type of submission (initial, supplemental), trial type (dose-expansion cohort) and the phase of trials (phase 1, phase 2, or phase 1–2).

## Reporting characteristics
Based on the CONSORT-DEFINE guidance, reporting characteristics of all the included published articles were extracted by using the Excel spreadsheet. Each item was assessed as “yes” or “no.” For composite items, they were assessed as “yes,” “partially,” or “no.” The items that not applicable to DEC were not evaluated. The data were extracted independently by two investigators (Y.H. and J.Z.). Discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

## Outcomes
The primary outcome was (1) the proportion of items adequately reported in the each evaluated published article, that is, the number of adequately reported items divided by the total number of applicable items for each article, and (2) the proportion of articles that had reported each item adequately, that is, the number of adequately reported articles divided by the total number of articles for each item.

## Statistical analysis
Descriptive statistics were used to examine the basic information of published articles and reporting characteristics. These characteristics were summarized using frequencies and percentages, or medians and interquartile ranges.

## Results

## Identification of 41 published articles in reporting dose-expansion cohorts as pivotal trials
A total of 392 oncology indications of targeted anticancer drugs were identified between Jan 1st, 2012 and March 31st, 2023, including 195 new drug applications and 197 biologics license applications (Fig.  ). Of these 392 indications, 47 indications were approved on the basis of dose-expansion cohort and 46 (97.9%) indications had corresponding published articles. Finally, a total of 41 articles reporting dose-expansion cohorts were included in this study (Appendix Table 1).
 

## Basic characteristics of 41 published articles in reporting dose-expansion cohort as pivotal trials
The dose-expansion cohorts as pivotal trials to support the FDA-approved indications of targeted anticancer drugs have shown a marked increase in publication from 2011 to March 31st, 2023. Of 41 articles, 22 (53.7%) were published from 2020 to 2022. More than 90% of articles were published in the high impact factor journals such as New England Journal of Medicine (NEJM), The Lancet and The Lancet series journals (The Lancet Oncology, The Lancet Haematology), JAMA Oncology, and Journal of Clinical Oncology. The median impact factor of included journals was high (54.4, interquartile range: 50.7 to 176.1). Most articles focused on hematological malignancies (15/41, 36.6%) and non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (10/41, 24.4%). More than half of the trials (21/41, 51.2%) investigated drugs of small molecular kinase inhibitors. The most (36/41, 87.8%) were trials of monotherapy. Twenty-eight (68.3%) trials supported the initial application of the agent (Table  ).
 

## The reporting characteristics of 41 published dose-expansion cohort
All the 41 articles had more than 75% of the items adequately reported, with a median proportion of 83.7% (IQR 79.1% to 88.4%). The percentage of articles with more than 95% of items reported adequately was 4.9% (n = 2). The percentage of articles with less than 80% of items reported adequately was 26.8% (n = 11).
Table lists the selected items with the proportion of articles that had reported adequately lower than 80%. The reporting characteristics of 41 published DEC articles based on CONSORT-DEFINE guidance were summarized and presented in Appendix Table  . Totally, the items respectively in section of trial design, interventions, sample size, statistical methods, results, and trial monitoring were inadequately reported in all 41 articles. In the trial design section, the planned cohort size (item 3a8) was reported in only 4 (9.8%) articles. Regarding the intervention section, only 17 (41.5%) articles reported dose modification and discontinuation (item 5b). Concerning the sample size section, sample size calculation (item 7a) and interim analysis (item 7b) were reported in 30 (73.2%) and 14 (34.1%) articles, respectively. In the statistical methods section, adaptive design features (item 12a2) were reported in only 26 (63.4%) articles. Moreover, only 30 (73.2%) articles partially reported additional analyses (item 12b) for adjusted analyses. In the outcomes and estimation section, interim results (item 17c) were reported in only 14 (34.1%) articles. Within the protocol, funding and data monitoring sections, access to the full trial protocol (item 24), the role of funders (item 25), data monitoring committee (item 26), and safety review committee (item 26a) were reported in only 28 (68.3%), 12 (29.3%), 6 (14.6%), and 4 (9.8%) articles, respectively.

 

## The diversity of naming methods in the titles and abstracts of the 41 published articles
The consistency in defining trial types through the naming in titles and abstracts was lacking across the 41 articles. Fifteen (36.6%) articles did not specify the trial phase details in their titles. Among the remaining 26 articles, we identified 12 distinct naming conventions for DEC. Similarly, in the abstract sections of these 41 articles, we observed 18 different naming conventions for DEC. The terms “phase 1” (or “phase I”), “phase 2” (or “phase II”), “phase 1–2” (or “phase 1/2”), and “phase 1b” (or “phase Ib”) were inconsistently used to describe the trial types in both titles and abstracts (Appendix Table 3).

## Discussion
To our knowledge, this is the first investigation on the reporting quality in published articles that reported DEC trials. These DEC trials such as ASCEND-1 [ ], ARROW [ , ], CHECKMATE-040 [ , ], and KEYNOTE-012 [ ] provided clinical evidences supporting the FDA approvals of those well-known drugs. The design, conduction, and analyses of these DEC trials were published in high-impact journals such as NEJM and The Lancet. However, it is noticed that in the past, the reporting quality for DEC was neglected due to the absence of a CONSORT specifically tailored to DEC trials, likely contributing to variability in reporting quality. The new published CONSORT-DEFINE [ ] highlights several missing items in previously published DEC articles, particularly in the statistics and data monitoring sections. Since CONSORT-DEFINE was only recently introduced, earlier articles exhibited incomplete reporting, impacting the completeness and transparency of these studies and potentially hindering reviewers’ full understanding of the findings.
In this study, the reporting of statistics in 41 articles was inadequate, with neglect of some key items in the reporting. The lack of specific guidelines for DEC statistics may explain this inadequacy [ ]. Incomplete statistical reporting may introduce potential risks of bias. For example, unclear sample size calculations and unreported data monitoring could lead to selective reporting or overestimation of treatment effects. Unlike traditional RCTs focusing on overall survival (OS), DEC trials often use adaptive designs to assess objective response rate (ORR). The FDA-recommended Simon two-stage design uses interim analysis to adjust sample size or stop the trial based on response rates [ ]. The interim analysis would be indispensable for these trials. Based on CONSORT-DEFINE checklist [ ] such as items 7a and 7b, we found several items inadequately reported in such statistical description. Key statistical aspects were often missing: 90% lacked planned cohort size, 25% omitted sample size calculation, 60% did not report interim analysis, and 80% failed to mention data monitoring. The interim analysis and planned cohort size are very useful in adaptive design of oncological trials [ – ]. When the results from interim analyses demonstrated that both efficacy and safety endpoints met predefined standards, there was no need to continue the later stages of the trial. In DEC trials, many have adopted designs such as Simon’s two-stage design in ARROW (NCT03037385) trial and Fleming’s two-stage design in LIBRETTO-001 (NCT03157128) trial [ , ]. These trials utilized interim analyses and planned cohort size techniques to accurately determine the sample size, thus saving time and clinical resource. The key feature of multi-stage trial designs is the incorporation of stopping rules that are prespecified criteria that determine whether a clinical trial should be continued, modified, or terminated at an interim point, based on accumulating data. Transparent reporting of these items is essential for future researchers to understand DEC trial designs and statistical assumptions. Without clear documentation of statistical methods and data monitoring, future researchers may struggle to replicate the findings, potentially undermining the reliability of DEC trials.
Concerning DEC typically using a non-randomized design, the central review and adjudication of efficacy and safety outcomes holds particular significance [ – ]. The FDA has recommended independent endpoint adjudication committee to ensure accurate evaluation in efficacy and safety outcomes [ , , – ]. This safeguards data integrity and enhances result reliability. For DECs supporting FDA approvals, incomplete reporting can obscure efficacy and safety assessments, potentially delaying regulatory decisions or leading to post-approval safety concerns. Actually, adjudicators such as blinded central review committee (BCRC) were adopted in many of DEC pivotal trials and were transparently reported in published articles. However, 27% (11/41) of articles still lacked endpoint adjudication, making outcome verification unclear. Due to the seriousness and life-threatening nature of cancer, patients with cancer might encounter numerous safety events even in the absence of medication intake. It is challenged to evaluate safety events linked specifically to the tested drug, particularly when identifying adverse drug reactions (ADRs) and establishing causality relationships for DECs without randomized control group. The establishment of a SAC can help to ensure more precise assessment of safety events in cancer patients [ ]. Unfortunately, this study found that nearly three-quarters (30 out of 41) of the articles did not offer information regarding the SAC and safety outcome adjudication. Over the past decade, half of the anticancer drugs withdrawn, such as umbralisib, were delisted by the FDA due to safety concern [ ]. SAC or similar safety assessment entities are recommended to improve the quality in safety assessment [ , ]. Reporting on endpoint adjudication should be transparent. Even in cases where there is not an assessment committee or similar entities for adjudication, it is essential to explain the methods employed to ensure the reliability of endpoint assessment, particularly in non-randomized circumstances.
In intervention domain, the information regarding dose modification and discontinuation should be mentioned in the articles. As toxicity events are common for anticancer drugs, the patients who are intolerant to full dosages should be managed with programmed reduced dosages during the trial [ ]. We found that over half of the articles did not mention dose modification. This omission may mislead readers into assuming a fixed dosage for all patients. For the item in oversight of DECs, it was observed that over two-thirds of the DEC articles did not mention role of funders. With the increasing use of adaptive designs in DEC trials, FDA experts have emphasized the critical role of sponsor oversight in areas such as selecting qualified personnel, establishing an Independent Data Monitoring Committee (iDMC), and reporting adverse events [ , ]. The FDA emphasizes transparent reporting of sponsor responsibilities throughout the trial [ ]. Therefore, the role of the sponsor and funding sources should be clearly disclosed in DEC reports. In item 24 for description of DEC protocols, DECs often includes disease-specific or dosing cohorts, using basket, umbrella, or platform designs within a master protocol [ ]. However, few articles disclosed assess to the full trial protocol, limiting transparency.
Notably, this study identified inconsistency in defining the trial phase and type through DEC names in titles and abstracts of published articles. Unlike the clear and uniform indication of trial type in the title or abstract as outlined in “CONSORT-DEFINE” with item 1a and 1b, currently published DEC articles lack standardized naming conventions to describe trial phases and types [ ]. Across 41 DEC articles analyzed, a variety of different naming methods were used to define trial phases in both titles and abstracts, leading to difficulties in understanding of trial phases and readers’ review efficiency. The diverse naming of DEC trials also hinders research communication and citation, making standardization essential. DEC phase is classified differently across studies—some as phase 1, others as phase 1/2, and some as phase 2. Structurally, DEC and dose escalation share the same master protocol, leading some studies to label DEC as phase 1. However, since DEC aims to provide preliminary evidence of efficacy, aligning with phase 2 objectives, many studies classify it as phase 2. A significant number also label it as phase 1/2 [ , ]. We recommend using “phase 1/2” as the appropriate label, as it reflects the close connection to dose escalation while also emphasizing the trial’s focus on evaluating efficacy and safety, consistent with phase 2 objectives. Adopting a unified naming method in the title or abstract to indicate trial phases and types could significantly enhance the study’s indexing, facilitating easier identification by subsequent researchers. This could improve accessibility and discoverability, offering convenience and efficiency for future summaries and systematic reviews.
In summary, “the whole of medicine depends on the transparent reporting of clinical trials” [ ]. Transparent reporting of clinical trials sustains the efficient communication of evidence-based medicine. CONSORT-DEFINE guidance established reporting standards for early-phase clinical trials (EPCTs) such as DEC, covering dozens of items across multiple domains, including trial design, conducting, and statistical analysis. With such a standard, clinical researchers, physicians, sponsors, and regulatory agencies can form a technical consensus, use the checklist to identify potential weaknesses in the trial, and ultimately conduct a standardized evaluation and reporting of trial quality [ ]. Our study assessed the quality of DEC reporting before CONSORT-DEFINE published in October 2023. Future research may need to assess DEC reporting after the publication and to determine the impact of the guidance on reporting practices. Since CONSORT-DEFINE was not specifically developed for oncological DEC trials, but rather serves as a guideline for EPCTs across various disease areas, our study illustrated the reporting characteristics and common issues of oncological DEC trials through the lens of CONSORT-DEFINE. Moreover, we also sought to explore how the CONSORT-DEFINE can be more effectively applied in the context of oncological DEC studies. We hope our findings will offer useful reference and experience for future research in this field. We have summarized the typical weaknesses in DEC reporting in Appendix Table 4 to facilitate better discussion and communication with researchers. Finally, we also note that the CONSORT 2025 update expanded the interpretation of certain fundamental items for all trials, and these changes also help us to deepen the understanding of trial reporting [ ].

## Limitations
This study has two limitations. First, this cross-sectional study focused on the reporting in the DEC trials of the FDA-approved target anticancer drugs. This study did not include other resource of published DECs. There are two reasons for this. Firstly, DEC is typically employed in the development of target anticancer drugs, while cytotoxic anticancer drugs or other kind drugs scarcely use DEC trials [ ]. Secondly, DEC structures are diversity among different drugs. For instance, some drug DEC studies may integrate pharmacodynamics design [ ]. Thus, we only investigated the case of targeted anticancer drugs.
Second, the systematic search was conducted up to March 31st, 2023. This study is the retrospective application of the CONSORT-DEFINE checklist to trials that were published before the guidance became available. Since these earlier trials were conducted without reference to CONSORT-DEFINE, certain checklist items were inherently less likely to be reported. Therefore, some of the reporting deficiencies identified in this study may reflect historical differences in reporting standards rather than intentional omissions. This should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. Future research could continue monitoring the FDA website to track newly approved indications based on DEC trials. Additionally, comparative analyses before and after CONSORT-DEFINE helped to exhibit the impact of the guideline on quality improvement in DEC reporting.

## Conclusion
DEC pivotal trials of targeted anticancer drugs were inadequately reported before the CONSORT-DEFINE publication. We call for relevant investigators and researchers to pay more attention to DEC reporting to align with CONSORT-DEFINE guidance, aiming for a more transparent, reliable, and accurate presentation of DECs. To improve reporting quality is crucial not only for ensuring clear communication but also for minimizing bias, enhancing reproducibility, and strengthening evaluation of trial evidence.
