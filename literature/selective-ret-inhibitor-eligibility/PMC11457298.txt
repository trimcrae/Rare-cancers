## Abstract

## PURPOSE
Tissue-based next-generation sequencing (NGS) analysis is highly recommended for patients with advanced/metastatic non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). We investigated a specific patient population with NSCLC that required tissue-based NGS analysis.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS
We enrolled 500 patients with advanced/metastatic (1) epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations or anaplastic large-cell lymphoma kinase (ALK) rearrangement–positive NSCLC who had failed at minimum one line of tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) therapy, (2) EGFR-/ALK-negative nonsquamous, and (3) non- or light-smoker patients with squamous NSCLC who were treatment-naïve or had failed at maximum two lines of systemic treatment. These patients were divided into five cohorts. Comprehensive tissue-based NGS testing (ACTOnco+) was conducted.

## RESULTS
Cohort 1: EGFR TKI-pretreated EGFR-mutated population (50.0%, n = 250), cohort 2: ALK inhibitor-pretreated ALK-positive population (1.6%, n = 8), cohort 3: treatment-naïve EGFR-/ALK-negative population (28.2%, n = 141), cohort 4: pretreated EGFR-/ALK-negative population (16.8%, n = 84), and cohort 5: squamous cell carcinoma (3.4%, n = 17). In cohort 1, 11.2% (28/250) of the patients had MET amplification, 32.4% (81/250) had been treated with osimertinib, and EGFR C797S was detected in 6.2% (5/81) of these patients. In cohort 2, resistance ALK mutation was detected in 37.5% (3/8) of the patients. In cohorts 3 and 4, targetable genetic alterations, including EGFR mutation (13.3%), ERBB2 mutation (9.3%), MET exon 14 skipping (5.3%), KRAS G12C mutation (4.4%), ROS1 fusion (2.7%), RET fusion (1.8%), and BRAF V600E mutation (1.3%), were detected. In cohort 5, MET exon 14 skipping was detected in 29.4% (5/17) of the patients.

## CONCLUSION
This multicenter registration study investigated tissue-based NGS for a specific patient population with NSCLC in Taiwan.

## INTRODUCTION
Non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains the leading cause of cancer mortality in Taiwan despite the advancements in systemic therapy. Many clinical guidelines have advocated for comprehensive genomic testing for targetable genomic biomarkers, such as epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations (including common mutations and exon 20 insertions), anaplastic large-cell lymphoma kinase (ALK) fusions, ROS1 fusions, RET fusions, NTRK fusions, KRAS G12C mutation, BRAF V600E mutation, ERBB2 mutation, and MET exon 14 skipping variants. Molecular targeted therapies are available for patients harboring the aforementioned genetic alterations. In East Asia, including Taiwan, EGFR mutation incidence is about 55% among patients with newly diagnosed metastatic lung adenocarcinoma; thus, the Taiwan National Health Insurance (NHI) reimburses only EGFR mutation testing with qualitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) methods, such as cobas EGFR Mutation Test v2 and therascreen EGFR RGQ PCR Kit, in qualified laboratories for patients with metastatic nonsquamous NSCLC. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of ALK and ROS1 is reimbursed in EGFR wild-type patients to detect ALK fusion and screen for ROS1 fusion before verification using a ROS1 fluorescence in situ hybridization test. It is recommended that patients who are EGFR/ALK/ROS1 alternation–negative pay out of pocket for comprehensive tissue next-generation sequencing (NGS) to detect other targetable genetic alterations. This sequential manner is time- and tissue-consuming, which may delay treatment. In addition, tissue NGS is recommended for detecting tumor mutation burden (TMB), identifying patients who are suitable for immune check point inhibitor monotherapy. In addition, tissue-based NGS is used to identify the resistance mechanisms of targeted therapy, such as MET amplification, for EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) therapy, EGFR C797S for osimertinib, and ALK mutation for ALK inhibitors. , 

## CONTEXT

Key Objective

In a dearth of reimbursement for comprehensive next-generation sequencing (NGS) in Taiwan, we conducted this registry study to provide tissue-based comprehensive NGS for patients with advanced non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) who were epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) or anaplastic large-cell lymphoma kinase (ALK) targeted therapy–pretreated, treatment-naïve or pretreated EGFR-/ALK-negative, or non- or light-smokers with squamous cell histology.

Knowledge Generated

Known resistance mechanisms, such as EGFR C797S and MET amplification, were detected in a small portion of EGFR targeted therapy pretreated patients. A few patients were diagnosed to have genetic alternations with corresponding molecular targeted therapies. However, only a few of them received these targeted therapies, given a limited reimbursement policy. MET exon 14 skipping was detected in a high percentage of patients with squamous histology who never smoked.

Relevance

Our aim is to provide substantial reasons to persuade our health care system to reimburse tissue-based NGS for patients with recurrent/metastatic NSCLC in Taiwan.

The Taiwan Cooperative Oncology Group (TCOG) of the National Health Research Institutes (NHRI) conducted this large-scale tissue-based NGS study for patients with NSCLC in collaboration with 10 hospitals in Taiwan (TCOG T1521 study, ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04849481). This study aimed to identify patients with targetable genetic alterations and resistance mechanisms to receive recommended therapies or participate in clinical trials.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Patients
We enrolled patients older than 20 years, who were diagnosed with recurrent/metastatic NSCLC. Only non- or light-smokers, which was defined as <10 cigarettes daily, among patients with squamous cell histology were included. For patients with EGFR- or ALK-positive adenocarcinoma, we enrolled patients whose disease progressed after <two lines of systemic treatment, including TKIs. For patients with EGFR-/ALK-negative NSCLC, treatment-naïve or disease progression after <two lines of systemic treatment were allowed. Patients with life expectancy of >3 months provided informed consent for access to the archival tumor tissues and their medical records of cancer diagnosis, smoking status, genetic profiling, tumor PD-L1 status, and previous treatment of metastatic disease. We also prospectively collected the patients' treatment, response to treatment, and survival after participating in this study.

## NGS and Genomics
Tumor samples of one hematoxylin and eosin–stained slide and 10-15 slides of 5-μm thickness for cancer panel–based NGS analysis (including fusion panel) were collected. The samples were sent to the central laboratory of the National Institute of Cancer Research, NHRI.
Testing occurred in a certified precision medicine laboratory, using ACTOnco+ (ACT Genomics, Taiwan), which is a cancer panel of 440 cancer-related genes, 31 fusion genes, 500× coverage, and is >1 MB. For tests, characteristics besides concomitant diagnoses or reference genes for the medication, microsatellite instability, and TMB were included. The copy-number amplification was defined as a copy number ≥6.

## Data Management and Statistical Analysis
The baseline patient characteristics, systemic treatment, treatment response, and genetic profiling were analyzed by descriptive statistics in a different subpopulation. Baseline characteristics were compared between cohorts using Student's t-test for continuous measures and chi-squared test for categorical measures. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare the differences of TMB between groups. The association between genetic mutations and survival was analyzed by using log-rank test and Cox proportional hazard model.
TCOG carried out data management. Data handling and analysis were conducted in compliance with the International Conference on Harmonisation-Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP) and applicable regulatory guidelines. Data recorded in the case report form (CRF) were entered into a validated computer database. Data cleaning will include visual and computer driven procedures to minimize logical inconsistencies, omissions, and errors within the collected CRF data. The authorized personnel answered all queries. A final database with the quality and integrity necessary for a proper final reporting was submitted for statistical analysis, which was accomplished using SAS version 8.2 or higher (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC). The clinical data collected in this trial were stored in the Clinical Trial Information Management System of TCOG for further analysis ≥15 years after this study. The clinical data were granted permission to link to the biobank of the NHRI or each hospital (for their own cases) permanently, 3 years after the patients had signed the informed consent. We provided the deidentified data, including the final report of the NGS OncoPanel and patient's baseline clinical data, to the cooperative companies of this study for genetic/clinical correlation analysis.

## Ethics
The current study's protocol, including the written Informed Consent Form, was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of all 10 participating institutes with reference numbers (202103086RSPB, 2021-07-027BCF, C202105097, C202105097, N202203207, CS2-21110, CMUH110-REC2-092, NE21165B, B11002026, A-ER-110-173, KMUHIRB-F(I)-20210193, and EC1100604-E) and is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov identifier (NCT048049481). The principal investigator is responsible for informing and obtaining IRB approval regarding any amendment to the protocol as per local requirements. The study was performed in accordance with the ethical principles in the Declaration of Helsinki, ICH-GCP, and applicable regulatory requirements.

## RESULTS
From June 2021 to December 2022, 500 patients were enrolled from 10 hospitals in Taiwan (Fig ). Patient characteristics were listed in Table . The median age was 65 years. Fifty percent (n = 250) of these patients were EGFR TKI–pretreated EGFR-mutated (cohort 1), 1.6% (n = 8) were ALK inhibitor–pretreated ALK-positive (cohort 2), 28.2% (n = 141) were treatment-naïve (cohort 3), 16.8% (n = 84) were pretreated EGFR-/ALK-negative (cohort 4), and 3.4% had (n = 17) squamous histology (cohort 5). There were significantly more smokers in cohorts 3 and 4 compared with cohorts 1 and 2 (57.8 v 22.9%; P < .0001). Most of the patients (91.1%) in cohorts 3 and 4 had known tumor PD-L1 status, whereas only 44.0% in cohort 1 had known tumor PD-L1 status. In cohort 1, 99.6% (249/250) of the patients have received at least one EGFR-TKI therapy and 32.4% (81/250) had received osimertinib therapy with 8.6% (7/81) of them having osimertinib as their only EGFR-TKI treatment.
 

## Tissue-Based NGS Analysis
The most common genetic alteration was TP53 (62.4%; Fig ). In cohort 1, MET and ERBB2 amplification were detected in 11.2% (28/250) and 4.0% (10/250) of the patients, respectively (Table ). Of the 81 patients who had been treated with osimertinib, EGFR C797S (6.2%, 5/81) and BRAF V600E (2.5%, 2/81) mutations were detected. We identified seven and 23 patients from these 81 osimertinib-pretreated patients who received osimertinib as the only EGFR-TKI and received first- or second-generation EGFR-TKI followed by osimertinib with documented EGFR T790M mutation, respectively. EGFR C797S was detected in two (28.6%, 2/7) and two (8.7%, 2/23) while MET amplification was detected in one (14.3%, 1/7) and two (4.3%, 2/23) of these patients, respectively. In cohort 2, 87.5% of the patients received alectinib therapy (7/8) and resistance ALK mutation was detected in 37.5% (3/8) of the patients.
 In cohort 4, platinum-based chemotherapy, immunotherapy plus platinum-based chemotherapy, and immunotherapy monotherapy were administered in 73.8 (62/84), 13.1 (11/84), and 2.4% (2/84) of the patients, respectively (Table ; Appendix Table A ). In cohorts 3 and 4, BRAF V600E, ROS1 fusion, RET fusion, KRAS G12C, non-G12C KRAS mutations, MET exon 14 skipping, and ERBB2 mutation were detected in 1.3 (3/225), 2.7 (6/225), 1.8 (4/225), 4.4 (10/225), 17.3 (39/225), 5.3 (12/225), and 9.3% (21/225) of the patients, respectively (Table ). Notably, 13.3% of the patients were diagnosed with EGFR mutations (including common and exon 20 insertion mutations), although not during the initial diagnostic procedure. In cohorts 3 and 4, the TMB was significantly higher than that in cohorts 1 and 2 (P < .0001; Fig ). In cohort 5, MET exon 14 skipping was detected in 29.4% (5/17) of the patients (Table ).
Other concomitant mutations were shown in Figure . There were significantly more STK11 and KEAP1 alternations in cohorts 3 and 4, and more PIK3CA and CCNE1 alternations in cohorts 1 and 2. CDKN2A, RBM10, and TERT alterations were similar in all cohorts. Regarding gene copy-number amplification, EGFR/ERBB2/MET amplification was detected in 15.8%, 2.2%, and 8.0%; and when the gene copy-number amplification is ≥6 and ≥8, the amplification is detected in 9.8%, 2.0%, and 6.4% of the study population, respectively.

## Poststudy Treatment
The median follow-up time was 10.0 months (range, 0.4-24.2) as of the data cutoff date of August 23, 2023. In cohort 3, 47.5%, 15.6%, and 10.6% of the patients received platinum-based chemotherapy, immunotherapy plus platinum-based chemotherapy, or immunotherapy monotherapy, respectively (Table ; Appendix Table A ). In cohort 4, most of the patients received subsequent systemic therapy (Table ). In cohorts 3, 4, and 5, 83.3%, 40.0%, and 47.1% of the patients with ROS1 fusion, EGFR exon 20 insertion, and MET exon 14 skipping, received ROS1 inhibitor, amivantamab, or mobocertinib, or MET inhibitor therapy, respectively (Table ). Only one patient (4.8%) with ERBB2 insertion received trastuzumab-deruxtecan. No patients with KRAS G12C mutation received sotorasib.
 

## DISCUSSION
Tissue-based NGS is a recommended diagnostic procedure for patients with recurrent/metastatic NSCLC. However, given the reimbursement policy of the single-payer National Health Insurance in Taiwan, NGS was not reimbursed. This study provided an opportunity for all patients requiring tissue-based NGS to detect potential targetable genetic alterations and resistance mechanisms to EGFR or ALK inhibitors. Most of the novel targeted therapies are available in Taiwan, such as BRAF V600E, MET, RET, KRAS G12C, and NTRK inhibitors, and the necessity lies in making accurate diagnosis rapidly.
In Taiwan, all patients who were diagnosed to have recurrent/metastatic nonsquamous NSCLC were reimbursed by the National Health Insurance (NHI) for EGFR mutation testing with RT-PCR. Patients who had ALK and ROS1 IHC testing were also reimbursed for diagnostic and screening purposes, respectively. Some hospitals used BRAF V600E IHC to diagnose BRAF alterations. Some of the patients participated in clinical trials to obtain specific genetic alternation or tissue-based NGS reports. The remaining patients paid out of pocket to do tissue-based NGS to detect targetable genetic aberrations. Yang et al demonstrated that testing for single-gene EGFR mutation followed by NGS is a cost-efficient strategy for patients with lung adenocarcinoma in an area with high prevalence rates of EGFR mutation. In this study, EGFR mutations and other targetable genetic alterations (excluding non-G12C KRAS mutations) were detected in 13.3% and 24.9% (56/225) of the patients in cohorts 3 and 4, respectively, which may change the treatment strategies of these patients. We expect that there will be more patients receiving corresponding targeted therapy agents once reimbursed.
In cohort 1, most of the patients received first- or second-generation EGFR TKI as first-line treatment, and only 2.8% received osimertinib as the only EGFR TKI. In the first-line setting, the NHI only reimburses osimertinib in patients with EGFR exon 19 deletion–positive NSCLC accompanied by brain metastasis in Taiwan, although osimertinib is the preferred first-line treatment for patients with EGFR-mutated (exon 21 L858R mutation or exon 19 deletions) advanced NSCLC worldwide. 
MET amplification was detected in 11.2% of the patients in cohort 1, which was similar to previous reports from Korea (14.7%) but slightly higher than the reports from others (6%). , However, among the 81 osimertinib-pretreated patients, EGFR second-site mutation C797S was detected in only 6.2% of the patients, which was lower than other study reports. , In an analysis of lung cancer tissue samples using the Memorial Sloan Kettering-Integrated Mutation Profiling of Actionable Cancer Targets NGS platform, C797S/G was detected after later-line osimertinib treatment (29% of patients) but was not detected after first-line osimertinib treatment. Another study from Korea showed that C797S was detected in 13.8% of second-line osimertinib-pretreated patients. In Taiwan, some physicians transitioned first- or second-generation EGFR-TKI to osimertinib without documenting EGFR T790M mutation because of difficulty in tumor rebiopsy or the patient's preference. Hence, we identified seven patients who received osimertinib as the only EGFR-TKI and 23 patients who received osimertinib with documented EGFR T790M as a later-line therapy in cohort 1. EGFR C797S and MET amplification were detected in some of these patients, and the interpretation should be with caution because of the small sample size.
Many guidelines recommended NGS studies for patients with nonsmoker, light-smoker, or young lung squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). In cohort 5, patients with nonsmoking lung SCC may harbor targetable genetic alternations, such as EGFR mutations and MET exon 14 skipping. Herein, we found a high percentage (29.5%) of the patients harbored MET exon 14 skipping. MET exon 14 skipping can be detected in patients with advanced non–small cell lung carcinoma, including lung SCC. Clinical practice guideline also recommends tissue NGS analysis to detect this genetic alteration in unusual cases of lung SCC, such as young patients, never/former light smokers, or long-time ex-smokers. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate that patients who never smoke with advanced lung SCC had a high incidence of MET exon 14 skipping.
This study has some limitations. First, the distribution of each genetic alteration did not reflect their true incidence as many patients have been diagnosed with other targetable genetic alterations via other diagnostic tools. Second, a sufficient tissue sample was not always available for NGS study, and some patients had a sufficient tissue sample for detecting genetic alterations only but not TMB. Third, at the time of data analysis, the survival data have not yet been mature. Only few patients harboring actionable mutations received corresponding treatments, and only few patients underwent frontline immunochemotherapy or immunotherapy monotherapy on the basis of the recommendations of the clinical practice guidelines. Given the limited reimbursement for the drugs, it is not expected that the survival outcome will be prolonged. This database has been ongoing in its collection of clinical outcome data and is anticipated to offer more comprehensive real-world data in future analyses.
In conclusion, this study provided the opportunity for patients to have comprehensive tissue-based NGS, corresponding with many clinical guideline recommendations. This database collected the patients' clinical data and survival outcome for future studies. Our aim is to provide substantial reasons to persuade our health care system of the importance of tissue-based NGS, thus persuading them to reimburse them for patients with recurrent/metastatic NSCLC.
