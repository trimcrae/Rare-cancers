## CONTEXT

Key Objective

What therapeutic approaches can overcome acquired resistance of medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) to RET inhibitors?

Knowledge Generated

A model of RET-inhibitor–resistant MTC showed both genic and pharmacological sensitivity to MAPK pathway inhibition. In xenograft models, the combination of RET inhibition with downstream MEK inhibition was strongly synergistic.

Relevance

Selective and nonselective RET inhibitors have strong clinical activity in MTC, but acquired resistance occurs in nearly all cases. Combining RET and MEK inhibition for both RET inhibitor–naïve and resistant MTC may prevent or target resistant disease states, respectively.

Activating mutations in the RET receptor tyrosine kinase are found in the majority of MTC and are either acquired sporadically or associated with the inherited cancer syndrome multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 (MEN2). Multitarget tyrosine kinase inhibitors (vandetanib, cabozantinib) and selective RET inhibitors (RETi, eg, selpercatinib, pralsetinib, EP0031) have excellent response rates in RET-mutant MTC. - However, prolonged RETi treatment in patients with unresectable disease can lead to the development of resistance to RETi and, ultimately, poor outcomes. 
Several mechanisms of resistance have been reported in patients with RET-mutant MTC upon receiving treatment with RETi, including the development of secondary mutations at RET p.V804 and p.G810. , Additionally, bypass signaling that sustains RET transduction pathways, like the RAS/MAPK signaling pathway, has been proposed as a mechanism of resistance. Given the current lack of therapeutic options for RETi-resistant disease, overcoming acquired resistance has become an important focus for the field.
This study aimed to identify therapeutic strategies for treating RETi-refractory MTC. We performed a comprehensive genomic and pharmacological analysis of an RETi-resistant MTC cell line, previously generated to identify the molecular mechanism underlying the acquired drug resistance. Our study reveals oncogenic pathway addiction to RET signaling, sustained through the activation of the RAS/MAPK pathway via multiple mechanisms, including the potential genetic loss of NF1. We demonstrate strong synergy between RET and MEK inhibitors, which overcomes resistance to RET inhibition and offers significant clinical potential for treating patients with MTC.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Cell Lines
The parental TT cells and their RETi cross-resistant progenies generated through long-term in vitro exposure to vandetanib were previously described. TT VanR was isolated from the drug-resistant cell population through the process of clonal selection. Cells were maintained in F12K media (ATCC, Manassas, VA, Cat. No. 30-2004) containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C. The TT cell line identity was confirmed via STR profiling.

## CRISPR/Cas9 Gene Knockout
NF1 was knocked out of TT parental cells using two unique sgRNAs inserted into LentiCRISPR v2 (Addgene, Watertown, MA, Cat. No. 52961) vector backbone alongside a nontargeting Cas9 control. CRISPR knockout sgRNA seed sequences were as follows: sgNonTargeting 5′ CAACGTCGCGAACGTCGTAT 3′; sgNF1a 5′ ACCTTAACCATTGCAAACCA 3′; sgNF1b 5′ TGGGCTTGTCGGCAAATCGG 3′. CRISPR plasmids were packaged into lentiviruses using psPAX2 (Addgene, Cat. No. 12260) and pMD2.G (Addgene, Cat. No. 12259) transfected into Lenti-X 293 T cells (Takara, San Jose, CA, Cat. No. 632180). TT parental cells were transduced with lentivirus and selected with puromycin (1 µg/mL) for 3 days until 100% death in a parallel plate of wild-type cells. Effective knockout was validated by Western blot and the cell lines were used in downstream studies.

## Cell Viability Assays
TT cells (10,000) were plated in white-walled, clear-bottom 96-well plate. After 24 h, cells were treated with serial dilutions of vandetanib (Selleckchem, Houston, TX, Cat. No. S1046), selpercatinib (Selleckchem, Cat. No. S8781), or cabozantinib (Selleckchem, Cat. No. S1119) at a final 1% DMSO concentration in triplicate for 96 hours. CellTiter-Glo (Promega, Madison, WI, Cat. No. G9243) was used to produce a luminescent readout representative of cell viability. Drug-treated groups were normalized to DMSO controls and IC50 values were calculated. For statistical analysis, the area under the curve was calculated from dose-response curves with a 95% CI, and the significance was evaluated using an unpaired t-test.

## Western Blots
TT cells were treated with DMSO or vandetanib (0.1, 0.5, 1, 5 µM) for 6 h. Total protein cell lysates were prepared in RIPA buffer (Pierce, Rockford, IL, Cat. No. 89901) with phosphatase and protease inhibitors (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, Cat. No. 78442). Protein lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 18,000 × g for 15 minutes. Protein concentration was determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay kit (Pierce, Cat. No. 23227). Lysates were analyzed by SDS-PAGE Western blotting run on NuPAGE 4%-12% bis-tris gels (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, Cat. No. NP0336BOX) and transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membrane was probed with NF1 (Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, Cat. No. 83620-5-RR), phospho-RET (Y1062) (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, Cat. No. AF5009), total RET (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, Cat. No. sc-365943), phospho-ERK1/2 (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, Cat. No. 4370S), total ERK1/2 (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. No. 9102S), GAPDH (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat. No. sc-47724), and vinculin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat. No. sc-73614) followed by respective HRP anti-mouse or anti-rabbit secondary antibodies. Protein signal was detected using ECL prime (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, Cat. No. RPM2236) or Trident femto (GeneTex, Irvine, CA, Cat. GTX14698) and imaged on a BioRad Chemi-Doc (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA).

## High-Throughput Drug Screen
Drug screening was performed in collaboration with National Center for Advancing Translational Sciences in Rockville, MD. TT Parental and VanR cells were treated with drugs from the Mechanism Interrogation PlatEs (MIPE) 6.0 drug library of 2,803 compounds for 72 hours Cell viability was determined using Cell-Titer Glo (Promega, Cat. No. G9243). Drug-Target Set Enrichment Analysis (DTSEA) was performed using Gene Set Enrichment Analysis and a custom collection of drug-target sets representing any MIPE 6.0 drug target that is covered by at least three small-molecule drugs (n = 308). Drugs with known mechanisms of actions were ranked based on AUC values, and these files were used as an input to identify significantly enriched pathways that are targeted by specific drug classes.

## In Vivo Assays
Animal studies were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Frederick National Laboratory for Cancer Research (FNLCR) and conducted through the Laboratory Animal Sciences Program. FNLCR is accredited by AAALAC International and follows the Public Health Service Policy for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Animal care was provided in accordance with the procedures outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. TT parental or VanR cells were injected subcutaneously into the flank of 4- to 6-week-old athymic nude mice (Charles River Laboratories, Wilmington, MA, strain code 490) in 100 µL of 50% Matrigel at a concentration of 50,000 cells/µL. Once tumors reached a volume of 100 mm3, mice were randomly assigned into four groups treated with vehicle (twice daily), selpercatinib (10 mg/kg, twice daily), trametinib (0.5 mg/kg, once daily), or combination selpercatinib and trametinib in DMSO:Tween20:100% Ethanol:5% glucose water (5:15:15:65) by oral gavage. Mice with parental cells were treated for 28 days. Mice with VanR cells were treated for 28 days, paused for 19 days, and continued treatment for 30 days. Tumor volumes and body mass were measured until mice were removed from the study because body mass differed by 20% or tumor volume exceeded 4,000 mm3. Statistical analysis was performed using an unpaired t-test at day 30 of treatment.
For genomic studies, see Data Supplement Materials and Methods.

## RESULTS

## Genomic Changes Resulting From Chronic Exposure to RET Inhibitors
The TT MTC parental cell line model harbors a common RET mutation p.C634W, which drives ligand-independent signaling via receptor dimerization. An RET inhibitor–resistant TT cell population was previously established by long-term in vitro exposure to vandetanib leading to cross-resistance to RET inhibition (Fig A). From these cells, we have isolated a subline that exhibits relatively high drug resistance (referred to as TT VanR hereafter).
 To identify genomic alterations in TT VanR cells, we performed spectral karyotyping (SKY) and multiplex interphase fluorescence in situ hybridization (miFISH) analyses to study the karyotype, ploidy, and heterogeneity. The major population of TT parental cells displayed a diploid karyotype with two copies of chromosome 10, which harbors the RET gene locus (chr10q11.2; Fig B, Data Supplement, Data file S1). TT VanR cells had a similarly diploid karyotype with two subclonal populations A and B which varied by two or three copies of chromosome 10, respectively (Fig B, Data Supplement, Data file S1). miFISH analysis using RET gene locus probes indicated that one of the two copies of chromosome 10 contained three copies of RET, suggestive of a tandem duplication of the RET gene locus in TT parental cells (Fig C). Subclone B, representing 62% of measured nuclei, demonstrates a newly acquired chromosome 10 in TT VanR cells with this tandem duplication, leading to an increase in total RET alleles from four copies in TT parental cells to seven copies in TT VanR (Fig C, Data Supplement, Figs S1 and S2).
Whole-exome sequencing and Sanger sequencing revealed an acquired secondary mutation in RET (p.Gly810Ser) in the TT VanR cell line (Figs D and E). The p.G810 solvent front mutation in RET has been reported in patients with MTC treated with RET inhibitors and is associated with acquired resistance. , , , No other mutations, copy number alterations, or gene fusions of known or putative significance were detected. To elucidate the relationship of these two RET mutations, long-read RNA sequencing was performed on both parental and VanR cells. We found that 74% of RET transcripts contained the founder RET mutation p.C634W in TT parental cells (Fig F). In TT VanR cells, however, roughly a third of these transcripts contained both the founder p.C634W mutation and a secondary p.G810S mutation in cis (Fig F).
In summary, we identified distinct genomic changes in TT VanR cells following prolonged exposure of TT parental cells to vandetanib (Fig G). Increased mutant RET copy number and acquisition of a known secondary mutation associated with acquired resistance in patients with MTC treated with RET inhibitors highlight a continued dependency on the activated RET signaling pathway.

## Genome-Wide CRISPR Knockout Screening Identifies Genes Responsible for RETi-Resistant MTC With RET Mutations
To identify essential genes in RET-mutant MTC, we conducted a whole-genome CRISPR-knockout screen in TT parental cells (Data Supplement, Fig S3A). As expected, RET was identified as an essential gene as were genes involved in mitochondrial respiration as previously reported (Data Supplement, Fig S3b-e). To identify genes that conferred resistance or sensitivity of TT cells to RET inhibitors, we performed two parallel whole-genome knockout screens in the presence of IC70 doses of vandetanib or selpercatinib (Fig A, Data Supplement, Data File S2). sgRNAs targeting NF1 were the top-ranked differentially enriched sgRNAs in both screens, indicating that knockout of NF1 conferred resistance to both vandetanib and selpercatinib (Figs B, C, D and Data Supplement, Fig. S4a,b). A search of the literature identified NF1 to be recurrently mutated or lost in a subset of tumors from patients with RET-wild-type MTC (Data Supplement, Fig S5). - Although the clinical significance is not clear, it suggests the importance of NF1 inactivation in RETi-resistance. To validate that NF1 loss leads to RET inhibitor resistance, we generated NF1 CRISPR knockout with two different sgRNAs in TT parental cells (Fig E). Loss of NF1 led to two-fold and three-fold decrease in the potency of vandetanib (IC50 467 nM for sgNonTargeting to 899 nM and 873 nM for sgNF1a and sgNF1b, respectively) and selpercatinib (IC50 24 nM for sgNonTargeting to 70 and 73 nM; Figs F and G).
 NF1 is an RAS-GAP that negatively regulates the RAS/MAPK signaling pathway, a major downstream effector of RET signaling. CRISPR-knockout of NF1 in TT cells increased basal ERK1/2 phosphorylation and maintained higher phosphorylation in the presence of vandetanib (Fig H). Overactivation of the RAS/MAPK pathway was also observed in TT VanR cells and sustained signaling during vandetanib treatment as described previously, indicating elevated RAS/MAPK signaling is associated with resistance to RET inhibitors (Fig I). Altogether, these data demonstrate that sustained downstream RAS/MAPK signaling via NF1 deletion is sufficient to reduce the efficacy of RET inhibitors.

## Pharmacological Screening Identifies Synergy of MEK Inhibition With RET Inhibition
To discover drugs that overcome resistance to RET inhibitors, we screened a library of >2,800 mechanistically annotated drugs (MIPE 6.0) against TT parental and VanR cells (Fig A, Data Supplement, Data file S3). Few differences in drug class sensitivities were observed between the parental and VanR cells (Fig B). DTSEA revealed that the top difference between these lines was the relative sensitivity to a broad spectrum of tyrosine kinase inhibitors that target RET (Fig C). However, both parental and VanR cells were highly sensitive to a broad range of MEK inhibitors (Figs D and E). This was especially noteworthy since our CRISPR knockout screen identified NF1 loss and subsequent downstream MAPK pathway activation as a mechanism of RETi resistance. Our drug screening thus suggested that MEK inhibitors may provide a therapeutic strategy to overcome this resistance (Fig F). Therefore, we performed drug synergy assays to validate whether blockade of MEK signaling improved the efficacy of RET inhibitors. Trametinib, an FDA-approved MEK1/2 inhibitor, was synergistic with selpercatinib in both TT Parental and VanR cells (Figs G and H), implying its potential utility in patients with RETi-resistant MTC.
 

## Selpercatinib Plus Trametinib Overcomes Resistance to RET Monotherapy in Preclinical Models
We further evaluated the synergy between RET and MEK dual inhibition in preclinical cell line–derived xenograft (CDX) models using both RET inhibitor–naïve (TT parental) and RETi-resistant (TT VanR) cell lines. Selpercatinib and trametinib were administered at well-tolerated doses (10 mg/kg twice daily and 0.5 mg/kg once daily, respectively) for 28 days after the engrafted tumors reached 100 mm3 (Fig A, Data Supplement, Fig S6a,b). Treatment of TT parental CDX with selpercatinib alone led to a complete response (Fig B, Data Supplement, Fig S6c), consistent with the high initial response rates in patients with RET-mutant MTC to the treatment. , Trametinib monotherapy administered twice daily had a more modest effect of delayed tumor progression (Fig B, Data Supplement, Fig S6c). Treatment with a combination of selpercatinib and trametinib led to a complete response, like that of selpercatinib alone (Fig B, Data Supplement, Fig S6c).
 In contrast to the treatment-naïve CDX model, TT VanR CDX models had a diminished response to both selpercatinib and trametinib monotherapy (Figs C and D). The combination of selpercatinib with trametinib, however, led to complete tumor control during the treatment cycles (Figs C and D). During the 19-day drug holiday at the end of the 28-day treatment cycle, tumors progressed in both selpercatinib monotherapy and combination therapy arms. Interestingly, another cycle of combination therapy rapidly shrunk the tumors to a stable size, demonstrating the RETi-resistant tumors remain sensitive to combination therapy (Figs C and D). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) for Ki67 revealed reduced proliferation in parental and VanR tumors treated with selpercatinib or combination therapy (Fig E). Furthermore, IHC for apoptosis showed a significant increase in the frequency of cleaved caspase 3 positive cells in combination therapy, although this was muted in VanR tumors (Fig F). In summary, these data demonstrate that combination of selpercatinib with trametinib is a potent treatment for both RET inhibitor–naïve and resistant MTC.

## DISCUSSION
In conclusion, thyroidectomy remains the only curative therapeutic option for patients presenting with MTC, but its efficacy is limited to approximately 20% of patients with disseminated disease. , Multikinase and selective inhibitors against RET have become an integral therapy for MTC, offering significant clinical benefit for both nonmetastatic and metastatic disease. - Despite this progress, acquired resistance to targeted therapies is expected and has been clinically reported, creating an urgent need for strategies that overcome or prevent resistance to RETi. , , Although mechanisms of acquired resistance, including both secondary mutations and bypass signaling, have been identified, non-RET actionable targets to overcome clinical resistance are not well-defined. - This study applied genomic and pharmacological screens to RETi-resistant MTC cells to identify clinically relevant targets and develop a combination therapy that overcomes acquired resistance in preclinical models.
We describe distinct genomic changes in TT cells following chronic exposure to RET inhibitors that reveal a continued oncogenic dependence on RET signaling which can be sustained by secondary mutations in RET. Acquired on-target mutations are a common mechanism of resistance to RETi in patients with RET-mutant cancers, including MTC. Solvent front mutations at p.G810 as well as mutations in the hinge (RET p.Y806) have been clinically identified in MTC and proven to confer resistance to targeted inhibitors including selpercatinib in vitro. , , This clinical scenario has led to the development of next-generation RET inhibitors that overcome resistance due to solvent front mutations. Enbezotinib (TPX-0046), a recently reported targeted kinase inhibitor with activity against RET and SRC kinases, may retain activity against RET solvent front mutations although it has been recently discontinued due to adverse events associated with the drug (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04161391). Additional next-generation RET-targeting TKIs vepafestinib (TAS0953/HM06) and BOS172738 are being investigated in early-phase clinical trials (NCT04683250; NCT03780517).
Although newer RET-targeting agents overcome on-target secondary mutations, they do not address mechanisms of resistance that bypass RET signaling. RET is involved in multiple pathways that regulate cell survival and motility, including RAS/MAPK, PI3K/AKT, JAK/STAT, and JNK signaling. , Elevated MAPK signaling downstream of RET has previously been indicated as a potential mechanism of resistance to RETi following disease progression in patients with MTC as well as in preclinical models. , In this study, we revealed a dependency on RAS/MAPK signaling through a whole-genome CRISPR knockout screen that identified NF1 loss as conferring resistance. NF1 knock-out TT cells were resistant to RETi, aligning with the emerging role of somatic inactivating NF1 mutations as putative oncogenic drivers in some MTC tumors. - A study of 18 RET and RAS wild-type MTC cases found two patients (11%) had biallelic loss of NF1. Given that mutant RET signals through the RAS/MAPK pathway and that mutations in RAS or NF1 are also recurrent drivers, there should be renewed interest in co-targeting the RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK signaling axis for MTC. In addition to NF1 loss, we also identified FOXA2 deletion as a potential mechanism of resistance to RETi. Although FOXA2 loss has not been reported in RETi-resistant MTC, there is evidence that FOXA2 downregulation is associated with EMT in MTC. FOXA2 loss may drive resistance to RET inhibitors possibly by activating compensatory (bypass) pathways or by maintaining RET signaling in the presence of RET inhibitors. Future studies will be needed to further understand the role of FOXA2 in MTC biology and acquired resistance to RETi.
Our study also demonstrated that both RETi-naïve and resistant MTC showed synergistic responses to inhibiting both RET and MEK using selpercatinib and trametinib. At present, clinical management of MTC uses targeted therapies as single agents, but the therapeutic potential of combined therapies has been demonstrated in other RET-mutant cancers and has been proposed for MTC. , , In this study, treatment with a combination selpercatinib and trametinib in subcutaneous CDX models led to a complete response in an RETi-naïve model and a significant response in an RETi-resistant model. These preclinical data support the evaluation of combination MEK and RET inhibitor therapy in the setting of progressive disease and possibly earlier in the disease course. Although not MTC, a patient with an RET fusion–driven NSCLC with acquired selpercatinib resistance developed an SKAP2BRAF fusion and achieved a near-complete response to the combination of selpercatinib and trametinib therapy. There were gastrointestinal toxicities of unknown etiology following combination therapy, warranting a measured approach to this combination in future studies.
This study would be strengthened by increasing the number of cell lines and tumor models, although the unavailability of other MTC models with hotspot RET mutations continues to be a limitation for the field. In addition to the model limitations, there may be acquired resistance mechanisms that are independent of MAPK signaling, as observed in our study, and should be considered in other models of acquired resistance to RETi. Likewise, high-throughput drug synergy screening, using RETi as a backbone, might help discover better synergy drug pairs than what was identified in the current study.
In summary, we have identified an oncogenic addiction to RET signaling mediated by unique genomic changes following chronic exposure to RET inhibitors. Resistant cells were marked by sustained RET activation and downstream RAS/MAPK signaling. We demonstrate that combined RET and MEK inhibition overcomes RET inhibitor resistance in preclinical models, offering a potent and clinically relevant combination therapy against MTC.
