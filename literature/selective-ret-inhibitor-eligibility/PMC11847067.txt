## Introduction
Patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC derive meaningful benefits from targeted therapy with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). Nevertheless, resistance to TKIs inevitably develops, usually driven by genomic alterations or non-genomic mechanisms such as phenotypic transformation. Approximately 20% of EGFR-mutant NSCLC cases develop off-target resistance, often involving gene amplifications or activating alterations in the MAPK or PI3K3 pathways. This report describes a complex case of a patient with EGFR-mutant NSCLC who developed sequential CCDC6-RET fusion and BRAF V600E mutation as resistance mechanisms and received personalized treatment.

## Case Report
A 69-year-old woman with no history of smoking, environmental exposure to carcinogens, or significant comorbidities, was diagnosed with advanced lung adenocarcinoma (cT4N3M1c) in 2019. Immunohistochemistry reported TTF1 positivity, P40, ALK, and ROS1 negativity, with 20% PD-L1 expression. Next-generation sequencing conducted on the diagnostic biopsy using an in-house DNA-based panel, revealed an EGFR exon 19 deletion (p.Glu746_Thr751delinsIleAla), leading to the initiation of first-line treatment with osimertinib. The patient achieved a partial response (40% tumor reduction by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors criteria). After 30 months, she developed disease progression in the lymph nodes, liver, lungs, and bones. A liquid biopsy (in-house DNA-based panel) revealed the persistent EGFR mutation with a 19.7% variant allele frequency. Subsequent chemotherapy with carboplatin and pemetrexed, combined with ongoing osimertinib, was ineffective, as indicated by disease progression at the first radiological evaluation and an increased variant allele frequency of 83.4%, suggesting EGFR amplification. Third-line therapy with paclitaxel and bevacizumab, in combination with osimertinib, was initiated. Nevertheless, progression occurred after two months. A biopsy of a progressing liver lesion excluded SCLC transformation, but next-generation sequencing analysis using an in-house DNA-based panel confirmed EGFR amplification and identified a CCDC6-RET fusion. The patient was then treated with cabozantinib, osimertinib, and gemcitabine, but the regimen was poorly tolerated due to grade 3 diarrhea and was discontinued two months after progression. A liquid biopsy using the FoundationOne panel confirmed the presence of EGFR exon 19 deletion (82.1%), EGFR amplification, and CCDC6-RET fusion (6.7%), among other gene amplifications, suggesting an aneuploid genotype ( ). The molecular tumor board recommended a combination of osimertinib 80 mg/day and selpercatinib (RET inhibitor) 80 mg/day. Despite initial adverse effects, including grade 3 neutropenia, grade 2 edema, and grade 2 hypertension, the patient’s condition improved significantly, with the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status improving from 3 to 1, and improved liver function and radiological response assessed at the first evaluation ( ). Combined therapy maintained disease control for 10 months before liver progression occurred. A novel FoundationOne liquid biopsy detected a new BRAF V600E mutation (2.3%), along with the persistent CCDC6-RET fusion (1.4%) and EGFR alterations. The patient’s treatment was adjusted to include osimertinib 80 mg/day, selpercatinib 80 mg/day, and trametinib 0.5 mg/day, a MEK inhibitor. Nevertheless, the patient’s condition deteriorated with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 2, grade 2 lower limb edema, grade 2 diarrhea, and visual impairment, leading to the discontinuation of targeted therapies and the initiation of chemotherapy with carboplatin and pemetrexed. Unfortunately, chemotherapy was poorly tolerated, and the disease progressed within 2 months. The patient transitioned to palliative care and passed away 5 years after the initial diagnosis. Written informed consent for correlative studies was provided. 

## Discussion
In this case report, a patient with advanced EGFR-mutant NSCLC underwent serial liquid and tissue biopsies upon progression on TKI therapy, revealing two genomic events contributing to acquired resistance. In the context of targeted therapy, off-target mechanisms of resistance involve activating mutations and gene amplifications, most frequently in MET and HER2, and oncogenic fusions, leading to the inefficacy of monotherapy with TKIs. Implementing tailored treatments on the basis of resistance mechanisms is a strategy to temporarily overcome resistance. The combination of EGFR and RET inhibitors has shown efficacy in patients with acquired RET fusions after progression on osimertinib. In our case, disease progression occurred after 10 months of treatment with selpercatinib and osimertinib, with the emergence of an acquired BRAF V600E mutation. In the study by Rotow et al. an acquired BRAF V600E mutation was detected by liquid biopsy as a resistance mechanism to combined therapy in one of the six evaluated cases, but the patient did not receive combination therapy with BRAF or MEK inhibitor. In addition, a case of polyclonal RET- and BRAF-mediated resistance to osimertinib has been reported. For acquired BRAF mutations, preclinical and clinical evidence support the rationale for combining EGFR and BRAF/MEK inhibitors. Nonetheless, outcomes with the combined use of EGFR, RET, and BRAF/MEK inhibitors have not yet been reported. Because cytochrome P450 (CYP3A4) is involved in osimertinib metabolism and selpercatinib is a CYP3A4 inhibitor, osimertinib levels could exceed safe limits when co-administered, increasing the risk of concentration-related adverse events. Regarding BRAF/MEK inhibitors, trametinib is safer than dabrafenib when combined with other TKIs as it has no proven in vivo interaction with CYP3A4, whereas dabrafenib is a CYP3A4 inducer. In our case, the addition of low-dose trametinib significantly impacted treatment tolerance, leading to its discontinuation.
The following limitations should be considered when combining targeted agents for complex resistance: (1) Safety: Combinatorial regimens often lack comprehensive safety data, requiring close monitoring; (2) Efficacy: Actionability scales for primary drivers may not apply to resistance pathways; (3) Sequencing: The optimal drug sequence is frequently unclear. In EGFR-mutated lung cancer, drugs like amivantamab or patritumab deruxtecan remain effective after EGFR TKI progression, regardless of resistance type (though these were not available for our patient at the time).

## Conclusion
Co-targeting primary drivers and bypass pathways can be effective, but it may fail in cases of multiple alterations or complex resistance. From a safety and efficacy standpoint, we advocate targeting both the driver and resistance alteration when resistance is driven by a single bypass mechanism, without evidence of polyclonal resistance.
