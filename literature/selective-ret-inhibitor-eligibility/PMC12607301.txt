## Classification of thyroid cancer
Thyroid cancer, classified into three major categories-differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC), anaplastic thyroid cancer (ATC), and medullary thyroid cancer (MTC), represents a spectrum of neoplastic disorders arising from thyroid follicular cells and parafollicular C cells [ , ] ( ). DTC originates from follicular epithelial cells which is the most common type of thyroid cancer, encompassing papillary and follicular histological variants, and constitutes the predominant form, accounting for approximately 90% of cases. At present, the rising trend in the incidence rate of thyroid cancer is mainly due to the rising level of DTC diagnosis, especially thyroid papillary carcinoma (PTC) [ ], which accounts for about 85% of thyroid cancer. It primarily affects women, typically presenting between the ages of 30 and 50 [ , ]. The primary treatment for DTC is surgical resection, often followed by radioactive iodine therapy to ablate residual thyroid tissue. Prognosis is generally favorable, especially for papillary thyroid cancer, with a low recurrence rate and high long-term survival rates achievable through multimodal management strategies. Among them, PTC is mostly caused by mutations in proto-oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes. Most PTC can be treated by surgery and 131I, but still about 10% of patients die from poorly differentiated and advanced tumors [ ]. Follicular thyroid cancer (FTC) is also a kind of tumor with low malignancy. Although its prevalence rate is not as high as that of PTC, its incidence rate is also increasing in iodine deficient regions of the world and in some populations of thyroid tumor susceptibility syndromes [ ].
 In contrast, ATC, although rare, manifests as the most malignant subtype, representing 1–2% of thyroid cancers. ATC also originates from follicular epithelial cells. Its incidence has not increased significantly, but it is the most malignant thyroid cancer. It is often accompanied by cervical lymph node masses and is highly invasive and lethal [ ]. Predominantly afflicting older individuals with a median age exceeding 60 years, ATC poses significant therapeutic challenges. Treatment modalities include a combination of surgery, radiation therapy, and occasionally chemotherapy. Unfortunately, the prognosis for ATC remains poor, with limited recovery prospects due to its aggressive nature and propensity for rapid metastasis.
MTC originates from neuroendocrine parafollicular cells or C cells, which account for about 2–4% of malignant thyroid cancer. It can occur sporadically or as part of hereditary syndromes. Surgical intervention constitutes the primary treatment, involving thyroidectomy and, when indicated, lymph node dissection. Targeted therapies, such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors, may be considered for advanced cases. Prognosis for MTC varies with the stage at diagnosis, with early-stage cases exhibiting better outcomes.
In addition, the Bethesda System for Reporting Thyroid Cytopathology (TBSRTC) provides a standardized framework for fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) diagnosis of thyroid nodules, serving as a crucial basis for malignancy risk assessment and clinical management. The system comprises six diagnostic categories, among which Category II (Benign) and Category III (Atypia of Undetermined Significance/Follicular Lesion of Undetermined Significance, AUS/FLUS) are particularly critical in clinical practice, as they represent the most diagnostically and therapeutically challenging groups and are also the most prone to incidental malignancies [ ].
Bethesda Category II nodules are generally considered benign, with a low risk of malignancy. Most studies report an incidence ranging from 0% to 3% [ ]. However, in cases with highly suspicious ultrasonographic features or high-risk clinical factors, the malignancy rate can increase to 12.7% [ ], and even reach 24% in pediatric populations [ ]. Another study reported that approximately 1.53% (8/522) of Bethesda II nodules harbored incidental DTC, suggesting that vigilance should still be maintained even within the “benign” category [ ].
In contrast, Bethesda Category III (AUS/FLUS) represents the most diagnostically indeterminate group, with malignancy rates reported at approximately 10–30% in adults [ ] and up to 52.6% in pediatric patients [ ]. The malignancy risk also varies significantly among subtypes, with the AUS-nuclear atypia subtype showing a markedly higher rate than the AUS-other subtype [ ]. A large cohort study demonstrated an overall malignancy rate of 15–40% among Bethesda III–IV nodules. Among patients who underwent surgery, incidental malignancies were detected in 18.42% (35/190) of multinodular goiter (MNG) cases and 20.13% (31/154) of single-nodule goiter (SNG) cases, with the follicular variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma being the most common type [ ].
Overall, Bethesda II nodules exhibit a low malignancy risk (0–3%), whereas Bethesda III nodules show a substantially higher risk (10–30%, occasionally up to 50%). Incidental malignancies are predominantly DTC, while ATC and MTC are rare [ , , ]. Therefore, the evaluation of Bethesda II and III nodules should integrate ultrasonographic characteristics, clinical risk factors, and molecular analyses (such as BRAFV600E mutation testing) through a multidisciplinary approach to improve diagnostic precision and optimize clinical management [ ].

## Drug resistance in thyroid cancer
Several tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) are emerging as new therapies for DTC, MTC, and ATC, offering the potential to stabilize these diseases. Lenvatinib and sorafenib have been approved for radioactive iodine (RAI) refractory DTC, while cabozantinib and vandetanib have been approved for MTC. These TKIs extend the median progression-free survival but do not increase overall survival. Patients with TC treated with TKIs may experience severe side effects and develop resistance [ ]. Further research is needed to identify potentially effective targeted therapies for aggressive TC based on its molecular characteristics. The RET gene encodes a transmembrane receptor with an intracellular TK domain. Approximately 40% of sporadic adult PTC patients exhibit RET/PTC rearrangements [ ]. A phase II trial conducted in 2020 evaluated the impact of sunitinib malate on patients with TC (DTC and MTC) with RAI-refractory or unsuitable for surgery. Current data indicate that sunitinib malate can inhibit tumor cell growth either by blocking certain enzymes necessary for cell growth or by inhibiting tumor angiogenesis [ ]. This study supports the efficacy of sunitinib as both a first-line and second-line treatment for advanced DTC patients. Additional prospective studies are needed to assess the efficacy of sunitinib in RAI-refractory TC. Finally, a patient with ATC, who had contraindications to systemic chemotherapy, was treated with sunitinib and showed a good clinical response [ ].
Targeted monoclonal antibodies that block the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway have demonstrated encouraging antitumor activity in various cancer types. Immunotherapy, exemplified by immune checkpoint inhibitors such as pembrolizumab and nivolumab, has shown promise in advanced thyroid cancers [ , ]. Research suggests that tumor mutational burden (TMB) might be a useful biomarker, representing the total number of somatic mutations per coding area of a tumor genome and indicating all non-synonymous coding mutations in the tumor exome. A high tTMB status identifies a subgroup of patients who may exhibit strong and durable tumor responses to pembrolizumab monotherapy [ ]. Additionally, an ATC patient who was BRAF-negative and treated with pembrolizumab following chemoradiotherapy during IVC showed tumor regression accompanied by the expansion of T-, B-, and NK-cell populations in peripheral blood [ ]. Nivolumab, initially used for treating malignant melanoma, has since been approved for other cancers. Recently, significant regression and complete radiological and clinical remission have been reported in anaplastic thyroid carcinoma treated with a combination of vemurafenib and nivolumab [ ]. While immunotherapy represents a paradigm shift in cancer treatment, responses can be variable, prompting ongoing research to identify predictive biomarkers and optimize patient selection.
In the realm of medullary thyroid cancer (MTC), specific drugs targeting the RET proto-oncogene, such as vandetanib, have demonstrated efficacy in delaying disease progression [ ]. These agents interfere with the aberrant signaling pathways associated with MTC, showcasing the importance of targeted therapies tailored to the genetic underpinnings of specific thyroid cancer subtypes. Vandetanib is a quinazoline drug that inhibits VEGFR-2, EGFR, RET, and the Src kinase family, among others. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved vandetanib in April 2011 for the treatment of advanced (metastatic) MTC in adults who are not candidates for surgery [ ]. Unfortunately, acquired resistance to vandetanib has been observed in MTC. Research indicates that the overexpression of Yes-associated protein (YAP) leads to resistance to vandetanib in medullary thyroid cancer cells (TT cells), while knocking down YAP resensitizes vandetanib-resistant TT cell line to the drug [ ].
Overall, the evolving landscape of thyroid cancer therapeutics underscores the significance of a multimodal approach. Combining targeted therapies, radioiodine, and immunotherapy holds promise for optimizing treatment outcomes, while ongoing research endeavors seek to unravel the complexities of drug resistance and refine therapeutic strategies in the quest for improved patient outcomes.
Within the realm of differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC), encompassing papillary and follicular variants, the occurrence of drug resistance has been a focal point of investigation. Notably, studies addressing the efficacy of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), a mainstay in the management of advanced DTC, reveal variable rates of resistance, ranging between 20% and 40% [ , ]. This variability is often attributed to the intricate interplay of genetic mutations, such as BRAF and RAS alterations, and the duration of treatment. Noteworthy distinctions in sensitivity to TKIs have been observed between papillary and follicular histological subtypes, with papillary thyroid cancer generally exhibiting a higher responsiveness.
Anaplastic thyroid cancer (ATC), distinguished by its aggressive phenotype, poses formidable challenges in terms of drug resistance. Studies investigating conventional chemotherapy report resistance rates ranging from 40% to 60% [ , ], contributing to the complexities of achieving sustained treatment responses [ ]. Furthermore, targeted therapies, initially promising, encounter limitations due to the rapid evolution of resistance mechanisms. The molecular heterogeneity intrinsic to ATC, coupled with mutations such as TP53 alterations, underscores the formidable barrier of drug resistance in this aggressive thyroid cancer subtype [ ].
In the context of Medullary thyroid cancer (MTC), research has shed light on the nuanced landscape of drug resistance. Conventional chemotherapy resistance rates are reported within the range of 30% to 50% [ ]. Targeted therapies, notably tyrosine kinase inhibitors, have demonstrated efficacy, but the persistent emergence of resistance remains a significant concern. The prevalence of drug resistance in MTC is intricately linked to the genetic makeup, with mutations in the RET proto-oncogene playing a pivotal role in shaping therapeutic challenges. Tailoring treatment strategies to individual genetic profiles becomes imperative to effectively address and potentially overcome drug resistance in MTC [ ].
In summary, the intricate dynamics of drug resistance in thyroid cancer subtypes, particularly prominent in DTC, demand a comprehensive understanding. These insights are instrumental in refining treatment approaches and steering research efforts toward more efficacious and personalized therapeutic interventions.

## Tolerance issues of radioactive iodine 131I
RAI therapy is another cornerstone in the management of thyroid cancer, particularly for DTC. It relies on the selective uptake of radioactive iodine by thyroid cells, facilitating the destruction of malignant tissue. However, resistance to RAI can occur, limiting its effectiveness, especially in aggressive subtypes [ ].
RAI is the first-line treatment for patients with unresectable or metastatic DTC, thyroid cancer, typically responsive to RAI with a favorable prognosis, presents a notable challenge in cases where resistance to 131I treatment emerges. This resistance, particularly prevalent in metastatic DTC, poses a substantial threat to patient outcomes. Approximately half of patients with metastatic DTC and 5% to 15% of those without metastasis exhibit resistance to RAI, contributing to poorer prognoses and lower survival rates [ ]. Furthermore, this resistance extends to other aggressive forms of thyroid cancer, including poorly differentiated thyroid cancer (PDTC) and undifferentiated carcinoma, leading to suboptimal responses to RAI. Notably, the incidence of resistance rises significantly in cases of distant metastasis, affecting approximately two-thirds of patients [ ]. Even in instances where RAI treatment is administered following total thyroidectomy, certain subtypes like poorly differentiated and undifferentiated carcinomas exhibit limited responsiveness. The 5-year survival rate for patients with RAI refractory (RR) thyroid cancer hovers around 60% to 70%, with a stark drop to a 10% 10-year survival rate [ , ]. However, the main post-thyroidectomy treatment remains RAI therapy, primarily for two reasons. Firstly, it serves to eliminate any normal thyroid residues, ensuring undetectable serum thyroglobulin (TG) levels. Secondly, RAI functions as adjuvant therapy, reducing the risk of recurrence and addressing persistent or recurrent diseases [ ]. This comprehensive approach underscores the intricate balance clinicians must strike between optimizing treatment outcomes and addressing the challenges posed by RAI resistance in certain subsets of thyroid cancer. The nuanced understanding of these factors is crucial for tailoring effective therapeutic strategies and improving long-term prognoses in patients with thyroid cancer.
The sodium/iodide symporter (NIS) is a crucial glycoprotein responsible for iodine uptake in the thyroid gland, but its function is also critical in other tissues like the salivary glands, gastric mucosa, and rectal mucosa. In the thyroid, NIS regulates iodine absorption and accumulation, which is vital for proper thyroid function and development, as well as preventing thyroid diseases [ ]. However, in thyroid cancer, the affinity for RAI is often reduced, linked to both transcriptional and post-transcriptional changes in NIS expression [ ]. Transcriptional regulation of NIS is primarily controlled by the TSH/TSHR/cAMP/PKA pathway, and several thyroid-specific transcription factors such as TTF1, TTF2, and PAX8 are involved in promoting NIS transport to the plasma membrane [ ]. The downregulation of NIS expression in poorly differentiated and anaplastic thyroid carcinoma is partly due to alterations in these pathways. Post-translational modifications and autophagy also play critical roles in regulating NIS activity. ROS, produced by NOX4 in the tumor microenvironment (TME), can affect both transcriptional and post-transcriptional mechanisms of NIS regulation [ ]. Specifically, ROS can activate the TGFβ/SMAD signaling pathway, which influences NIS expression and contributes to radioiodine resistance. Additionally, autophagy can mediate the degradation of NIS through lysosomal pathways, further complicating the regulation of iodine uptake. These intricate interactions highlight the complexity of NIS regulation in thyroid function and underscore potential therapeutic strategies aimed at overcoming radioiodine resistance by targeting the ROS-NIS-autophagy axis.

## The mechanism of action of reactive oxygen species in thyroid cancer
5.1.

## Carcinogenic effect of DNA damage caused by ROS
DNA damage refers to the physical or chemical changes in DNA caused by exogenous or endogenous factors. The effects of ROS on DNA replication are usually reflected on the oxidative bases produced by ROS as well as deoxy-ribonucleoside triphosphate (dNTP) and polymerase activities that both effected by ROS, and these effects are largely mediated by kinase ATR (ATM- and Rad3-related) [ , ]. ROS mainly leads to sulfhydryl oxidation of key cysteine residues and reversible post-translational modification of many amino acids [ ]. ROS can also lead to DNA oxidation, resulting in some mutations, such as DNA deletion, insertion, translocation, double strand break and more [ ]. Oxidative stress (OS) can affect ROS production and DNA damage response (DDR) by activating ATM and γH2AX [ ]. ROS can be used as a signal of cancer due to its increased levels. ROS will cause the loss of tumor suppressor gene function, promote glucose metabolism, angiogenesis and tumor growth, and lead to the mutation of oncogene [ , ].
However, in tumors, DNA damage caused by ROS can also inhibit the growth of tumors. For example, DNA-dependent protein kinase catalytic subunit (DNA-PKcs) is an abnormally high expression in rapidly proliferating tumor tissues, while low or no expression in slowly growing cells. The accumulation of ROS can inhibit DNA-PKcs, thereby inhibiting tumor proliferation. Similarly, in tumor cells, the cycle arrest recovery genes PLK1 and Cdc25 protein phosphatase family are highly expressed, and the accumulation of ROS can act on PLK1 and Cdc25 protein, so that the tumor cell cycle is blocked and the cells cannot proliferate rapidly [ , ]. In addition, increased ROS can mediate p53 to activate pro-oxidative genes, resulting in tumor death [ ].
Oxidative stress is a key factor to regulate the generation of ROS in cells. The abnormal accumulation of reactive oxygen species will do harm to intracellular components and cause tumors, cardiovascular diseases, neurodegeneration and other diseases [ ]. DNA damage caused by ROS is closely related to thyroid function. Normal iodine metabolism of the thyroid requires the participation of oxidants [ ], It will also continuously produce H2O2, which also plays a key role in the production of hormones [ ]. The thyroid gland is a target of oxidative stress, in which TPO/DUOX can play a localization role, to increase ROS caused by OS and increase apoptosis caused by DNA damage [ ]. However, it is still especially vital for thyroid gland to prevent excessive oxidation, NFE2 related transcription factor 2 (Nrf2) in the thyroid gland has been shown to regulate antioxidant responses. Nrf2 can protect the body from the damage of OS induced by iodide overload, including the oxidative damage of DNA, protein and lipids. Nrf2 coordinates thyroid hormone synthesis with antioxidation mechanism to homeostasis [ ]. Some researchers have shown that the activity levels of catalase, GPX and DUOX2 are low in breast cells with prophase hypothyroidism. And compared with normal breast cells, the expression of DUOX2 decreased in hypothyroid breast cells. Stimulated by higher concentrations of thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH), the expression of NADPH oxidase NOX2/NOX4/DUOX2 increased, the production of intracellular and mitochondrial ROS also increased, and the amount of focal points of 53BP1 increased, which is one of the indicators of DNA damage [ ]. In addition, G3842a is a nonsense mutation of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide hydride (NADH) dehydrogenase subunit 1 gene (ND1), and is also a risk factor for thyroid cancer gene mutation. G3842a mutation also promotes the tumorigenicity of thyroid cancer by increasing ROS production and ERK1/2 phosphorylation [ ].

## Effect of ROS on thyroid cancer
On the one hand, Cancer cells may exhibit high levels of O2•− and H2O2, which are associated with malignant transformation and cancer progression. Elevated ROS production can lead to mitochondrial membrane potential depolarization and impairment of the mitochondrial respiratory chain, thereby increasing the generation of O2•−. This phenomenon is referred to as the malignant cycle of ROS propagation in cancer [ ]. The abnormal formation of ROS leads to carcinogenesis, which is mainly reflected on DNA damage and mutation. It will also directly or indirectly affect the migration and proliferation of thyroid cancer cells. ROS play crucial roles in the initiation, progression, and treatment of various cancers; however, their functional differences and underlying mechanisms in DTC, ATC, and MTC remain insufficiently understood. As intracellular signaling molecules, ROS at physiological levels regulate cellular metabolism, proliferation, and apoptosis through redox signaling pathways [ ]. However, excessive ROS accumulation can lead to oxidative stress, resulting in DNA damage, protein oxidation, and lipid peroxidation, thereby promoting tumor initiation and progression [ , ]. In thyroid cancer, the regulatory mechanisms of ROS may vary across pathological subtypes, providing new perspectives for understanding their biological roles in different thyroid cancer variants. In DTC, ROS levels are generally low, which correlates with its relatively favorable prognosis and slower progression. The role of ROS in DTC may primarily involve the regulation of signaling pathways associated with cell proliferation and apoptosis, such as the PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways [ ]. Studies have shown that ROS promote DTC cell proliferation and survival by activating these pathways while simultaneously suppressing apoptosis [ ]. Furthermore, ROS can regulate the expression of thyroid-specific genes, such as NIS and TG, thereby influencing the iodine uptake capacity of DTC cells and affecting the efficacy of radioiodine therapy [ ]. Nevertheless, the specific mechanisms of ROS in DTC remain to be fully elucidated, particularly regarding their interactions with components of the tumor microenvironment, such as immune cells and angiogenesis. In contrast, ATC, a highly aggressive subtype of thyroid carcinoma, exhibits markedly elevated ROS levels, which may contribute to its rapid progression and poor prognosis [ , , ]. In ATC, ROS not only induce genomic instability and mutation accumulation through oxidative stress but also activate transcription factors such as NF-κB and HIF-1α, thereby promoting tumor cell invasion and metastasis [ ]. In addition, ROS can modulate immune cells within the tumor microenvironment (e.g., tumor-associated macrophages) and angiogenic factors, further accelerating ATC progression [ ]. Notably, the elevated ROS levels in ATC may provide therapeutic opportunities, such as inhibiting ROS production or enhancing antioxidant defense systems to suppress tumor growth [ ]. MTC, a neuroendocrine tumor derived from thyroid C cells, displays distinct ROS regulatory mechanisms compared with DTC and ATC. The ROS levels in MTC are typically intermediate between those of DTC and ATC, which may correspond to its moderate aggressiveness and prognosis [ ]. In MTC, ROS influence tumor cell proliferation and survival by modulating the expression and activity of the RET proto-oncogene [ ]. Moreover, ROS regulate calcium signaling pathways and the expression of neuroendocrine markers, such as calcitonin and chromogranin A (CgA), thereby affecting MTC cellular behavior [ ]. Nonetheless, the detailed mechanisms of ROS action in MTC remain poorly defined, particularly regarding their interactions with the tumor microenvironment and immune evasion. Mechanistically, the functional divergence of ROS among thyroid cancer subtypes may be attributed to differences in their sources and regulatory networks. In DTC, ROS are primarily generated by the mitochondrial electron transport chain and NADPH oxidases, whereas in ATC and MTC, ROS production appears more complex, involving mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammatory responses, and immune cells within the tumor microenvironment [ ]. Furthermore, the ROS-associated signaling networks differ among subtypes—for instance, in DTC, ROS mainly act through PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways, whereas in ATC and MTC, ROS exert their effects via NF-κB, HIF-1α, and RET pathways [ ]. In summary, the functional differences and underlying mechanisms of ROS in DTC, ATC, and MTC are of significant research interest. Future studies should further elucidate the specific roles of ROS across different thyroid cancer subtypes and develop ROS-targeted therapeutic strategies to improve the diagnosis and treatment of thyroid cancer.
In summary, the functional differences and underlying mechanisms of ROS in DTC, ATC, and MTC are of significant research interest. Further elucidation of these mechanisms may provide novel therapeutic targets. For example, forkhead box R2 (FOXR2), a new member of the FOX family, belongs to a proto-oncogene and is highly expressed in TC tissue and is involved in a series of biological processes, such as regulation of basic embryonic developmental processes, promotion of cell differentiation and involvement in metabolic homeostasis. And the process of down-regulating FOXR2 inhibiting migration and invasion of thyroid cancer cells is achieved through decreasing the intracellular level of ROS [ ]. Additionally, ROS, particularly H2O2, act as second messengers in frequently overactivated signaling cascades in cancer, mediating cell growth, proliferation, and survival. Low doses of H2O2 and O2 can directly regulate pro-proliferative processes. For example, mitochondrial ROS have been shown to modulate MAPK pathway activity and are essential for KRAS-induced anchorage-independent growth and tumorigenesis [ ].
On the other hand, excessive ROS may lead to apoptosis and the failure of the intracellular antioxidant system, thereby alleviating thyroid cancer. Studies have shown that the increase of ROS will lead to the damage of cell membranes, which will increase the apoptosis of thyroid cancer cells [ ]. Similarly, in various thyroid cancer cells (such as 8505 C/TCP-1/KTC-1), it was found that the activity of apoptosis related protein caspase3/7 decreased, while melatonin could increase the activity of caspase3/7 by increasing ROS, thereby promoting cancer cell apoptosis [ ]. In research on the treatment of thyroid cancer, it was found that the drug produced cytotoxicity and induced the production of ROS. Abnormal activation of PI3K/Akt signals promotes tumor formation in thyroid follicular cells, while inhibition of Akt activation can promote ROS production, regulate DNA damage, and promote tumor death [ ]. The study by Yiqun Hu et al. indicates that alantolactone primarily mediates an increase in ROS, which lowers the Bcl-2/BAX ratio, downregulates the mitochondrial membrane potential, and increases the release of cytochrome C, leading to caspase 9/3 cleavage. This results in G2/M phase cell cycle arrest in ATC cells. Furthermore, alantolactone induces both apoptosis and pyroptosis in ATC cells, along with the cleavage of poly ADP-ribose polymerase (PARP) and Gasdermin E (GSDME), significantly increasing the release of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and IL-1β. In addition, Alantolactone also induces the translocation of immunogenic cell death markers, such as calreticulin, to the cell membrane [ ].
In light of ROS’s pivotal role in tumor biology, several clinical trials are exploring targeted therapies modulating ROS-associated pathways in thyroid cancer. Selpercatinib (LOXO-292), a selective RET inhibitor, demonstrated promising efficacy and safety in the phase I/II LIBRETTO-001 trial (NCT03157128), involving RET-mutant medullary thyroid cancer (MTC; n = 324) and RET fusion-positive thyroid cancer (TC; n = 66) across diverse subtypes [ ]. Remarkably, a 73-year-old male with locally advanced papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) harboring focal ATC responded durably to LOXO-292 with a partial response lasting over 19 months [ ]. Similarly, PLX8394, a BRAFV600E- and BRAF fusion-targeted inhibitor, is under evaluation in a phase II trial (NCT05503797) for its potential in tumor reduction and surgical feasibility in BRAF-mutant thyroid cancers. Multi-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as cabozantinib, which targets VEGFR, MET, and RET, are also being assessed in phase III and pediatric trials, underscoring the therapeutic relevance of ROS-regulated pathways such as RET, BRAF, and VEGFR signaling [ ].
Beyond apoptosis, ROS can also inhibit tumor cell proliferation. For example, blocking cystathionine β-synthase (CBS) increases ROS levels and suppresses tumor growth by inhibiting the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway [ ]. Overexpression of NKX2.5, a transcription factor elevated in most PTC patients, correlates with low recurrence rates and is linked to increased ROS production alongside downregulation of thyroid differentiation markers [ ]. Moreover, vitamin C inhibits ROS-mediated EGF/EGFR-MAPK/ERK signaling and promotes Akt ubiquitination and degradation, effectively eliminating BRAF wild-type thyroid cancer cells. In BRAF-mutant cells, vitamin C exerts antitumor effects by inducing ROS-dependent proteasomal degradation of Akt [ ].
Due to their altered metabolism and genetic background, thyroid cancer cells often exhibit elevated ROS levels compared to normal cells. To survive, they upregulate antioxidant defenses, making them more sensitive to oxidative imbalance. This adaptation creates a “ROS threshold”—a therapeutic window wherein further elevation of ROS induces irreversible oxidative stress, damaging DNA, proteins, and lipids, leading to cell death via apoptosis or necrosis [ , ]. Conversely, reducing ROS below critical levels may suppress tumor proliferation [ ]. Therefore, precise modulation of ROS represents a promising strategy in thyroid cancer therapy, especially in treatment-resistant and aggressive subtypes.

## The mechanism of autophagy in thyroid cancer
6.1.

## Autophagy
Autophagy is a tightly regulated lysosomal degradation and recycling pathway that is ubiquitous in eukaryotic cells. It maintains intracellular homeostasis by degrading and recycling dysfunctional proteins and organelles, thus playing a critical role in cellular quality control and adaptation to stressors [ ]. Autophagy is commonly activated under conditions such as nutrient deprivation, mitochondrial damage, and hypoxia, and it is deeply involved in diverse biological processes, including cell survival, death, differentiation, and development [ ]. In the immune system, autophagy is essential for the development and survival of lymphocytes, particularly for the establishment of CD8+ T cell memory. Disruption of autophagy, whether locally or systemically, may impair immune function and be detrimental to cancer patients. In the context of cancer, autophagy plays a complex and dual role. During the early stages of tumorigenesis, it can act as a tumor suppressor by removing damaged cellular components and reducing oxidative stress, thereby preventing malignant transformation. However, in established tumors, autophagy often shifts to a pro-survival role, supporting tumor progression, invasion, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance [ ]. Consequently, whether autophagy benefits or harms tumor outcomes depends on the stage and context of tumor development.

6.2.

## Autophagy and thyroid cancer
Autophagy has emerged as a central research focus in thyroid cancer biology, with increasing evidence highlighting its diverse functions and regulatory mechanisms across different histological subtypes. Differentiated thyroid carcinoma (DTC), anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (ATC), and medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) exhibit distinct autophagy-regulating pathways and functional characteristics. As a highly conserved intracellular degradation process, autophagy not only contributes to cellular homeostasis but also plays a multifaceted role in tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic response [ ]. The following section summarizes the functional distinctions and potential mechanisms of autophagy in DTC, ATC, and MTC.
In DTC, autophagy primarily exerts a protective function. DTC is characterized by a low mutational burden and favorable prognosis, and its autophagic activity is closely associated with cellular differentiation and thyroid hormone synthesis [ ]. Studies have shown that DTC cells utilize autophagy to remove damaged organelles and protein aggregates, thereby maintaining homeostasis and suppressing tumor progression [ ]. Furthermore, autophagy in DTC may enhance sensitivity to chemotherapy and radiotherapy by regulating cell cycle progression and DNA repair pathways [ ]. However, excessive autophagic activation may enable tumor cell survival under nutrient-deprived conditions, potentially contributing to tumor recurrence and metastasis [ ]. Therefore, autophagy in DTC exhibits dual functionality—it can act as a tumor suppressor but may also facilitate adaptive tumor survival under specific stress conditions. By contrast, autophagy in ATC is more complex and predominantly pro-tumorigenic. ATC, a highly aggressive malignancy with substantial genomic instability and a significantly higher mutational load than DTC [ ], exploits autophagy to sustain metabolic flexibility and anti-apoptotic capacity, allowing survival under hostile microenvironmental conditions [ ]. Notably, autophagy promotes ATC cell proliferation and survival by regulating endoplasmic reticulum stress and the unfolded protein response (UPR) [ ]. In addition, autophagy in ATC is closely linked to lipid metabolic reprogramming. For instance, overexpression of stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1) enhances lipid metabolism via autophagic pathways, thereby supporting rapid tumor cell proliferation [ ]. These findings suggest that autophagy primarily plays a tumor-promoting role in ATC, and targeting autophagy-related pathways may offer novel therapeutic opportunities. Autophagy in MTC exhibits unique characteristics. As a neuroendocrine tumor originating from thyroid parafollicular C cells, MTC shows distinct biological behaviors compared with DTC and ATC [ ]. Studies have revealed that autophagic activity in MTC correlates with the expression of cell-surface glycosphingolipids, which may serve as tumor-specific markers involved in autophagy regulation [ ]. Moreover, MTC cells utilize autophagy to maintain metabolic adaptation and resist apoptosis, supporting survival within metastatic lesions [ ]. Unlike ATC, however, autophagic activity in MTC appears to be strongly influenced by microenvironmental factors—such as tumor-infiltrating immune cells—which may modulate autophagy-related signaling pathways and affect disease progression [ ]. Thus, autophagy regulation in MTC is likely more complex and warrants further investigation.
At the molecular level, the functional divergence of autophagy across thyroid cancer subtypes may reflect subtype-specific regulatory signaling. The mTORC1 pathway, a key negative regulator of autophagy, displays distinct activity patterns in DTC and ATC [ ]. In DTC, mTORC1 inhibition may promote autophagy and enhance therapeutic sensitivity, whereas in ATC, mTORC1 hyperactivation may suppress autophagy and facilitate tumor adaptation [ ]. In addition, the miRNA–autophagy axis plays a notable regulatory role in thyroid cancer. For example, miR-146b, miR-221, and miR-222 are highly expressed in DTC, where they may modulate tumor progression by targeting autophagy-related genes [ ]. In ATC, dysregulated miRNA expression may further promote tumor invasion and metastasis through autophagy-associated signaling pathways [ ].
Collectively, these findings indicate that autophagy plays distinct and sometimes opposing roles in thyroid cancer depending on the histological subtype. In DTC, autophagy mainly exerts a protective and tumor-suppressive effect, whereas in ATC and MTC, it shows prominent tumor-promoting properties. Such functional diversity likely results from subtype-specific signaling networks and microenvironmental influences.
In parallel, several studies have demonstrated that manipulating autophagy may influence thyroid cancer progression and therapeutic response. Some researchers have proposed that autophagy induction promotes tumor cell death and improves cancer prognosis. For instance, succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) can suppress autophagy in thyroid cancer cells; PTC cells expressing SDHD-G12S grow faster and inhibit autophagy by downregulating FOXO3a target genes via a PTEN-dependent pathway [ ]. In PTC, the expression of baculoviral inhibitor of apoptosis protein (IAP) repeat containing 7 (BIRC7) was increased in thyroid cancer tissues compared with normal tissues. It inhibits autophagy to promote tumor invasion and metastasis by regulating ATG5 and BCN1 [ ].
PI3K/mTOR and NF-κB are considered autophagy-related pathways. In solid tumors, ROS is often produced by infiltrating myeloid cells which have been confirmed to activate pro-inflammatory transcription via NF-κB pathway. Autophagy antagonizes NF-κB activation by degrading Rela/p65 aggregates in tumor-associated macrophages (TAM) [ ]. Adenosine 5′-monophosphate (AMP)-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is a metabolic sensor that maintains the energy and nutritional balance of cells. AMPK is widely expressed in various tissues of the organ system, such as heart, kidney, liver, brain, and skeletal muscle. Mammalian AMPK is activated by the decrease of cell energy state, and its signal is the increase of AMP/ATP and ADP/ATP ratio [ ]. Many proteins and genes can affect oxidative balance and autophagy by regulating AMPK-mTOR pathway. Upregulation of SIRT3 can increase ferroptosis and autophagy by inhibiting AMPK-mTOR pathway and reducing the level of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4). Autophagy induction can synergize with SIRT3 to enhance ferroptosis [ ].
The activation of AMPK directly or indirectly affects tumor growth by blocking mitogen activated protein kinase (MAPK) and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways. Lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) is also one of the substances highly expressed in PTC, which will promote the migration and invasion of PTC. One of the mechanisms by which LDHA promotes the growth of thyroid cancer cells is to restrain autophagy by inhibiting the activation of AMPK [ ]. A variety of drugs are also targeted at PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway to alleviate thyroid cancer. Prunella vulgaris L. (PV) has been shown to induce autophagy and cause the death of thyroid cancer cell K1. PV induced autophagy and anti-tumor proliferation effects can be reversed by mTOR activators and AMPK inhibitors ( ) [ ]. Apatinib can act on the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway, which, on one hand, leads to apoptosis of PTC cells and exerts therapeutic effects, and on the other hand, induces autophagy through this pathway, resulting in drug resistance. However, hydroxychloroquine can inhibit the occurrence of autophagy, improving apatinib resistance and enhancing the therapeutic effect ( ) [ ].
 BRAF mutation is also an important factor in the occurrence and development of thyroid cancer. The high expression of Ref-1 is closely related to BRAF mutation. The expression of Ref-1 was up-regulated by oxidative stress induced by BRAF activation. The combination of Ref-1 redox inhibitor and BRAF inhibitor leads to autophagy flux overload, MAPK activation is inhibited, and leads to cell aging and cell death in vitro [ ].

## The relationship between reactive oxygen species and autophagy
The correlation between ROS and autophagy has become a major focus in recent years within cell biology and molecular medicine. As crucial intracellular signaling molecules, ROS not only mediate oxidative stress responses but also play key regulatory roles in autophagy. Autophagy, a conserved self-degradative process, maintains cellular homeostasis by eliminating damaged organelles and misfolded proteins. The interaction between ROS and autophagy is intricate and context-dependent—it can be either synergistic or antagonistic depending on ROS levels, cell type, and environmental conditions. At physiological concentrations, ROS function as signaling molecules that activate autophagy. For example, ROS modulate autophagosome formation through oxidative modification of Atg4 and Beclin-1 [ , ]; oxidation of Atg4 enhances its enzymatic activity, promoting autophagosome formation [ ]. In addition, ROS can indirectly induce autophagy by activating AMPK and inhibiting mTOR signaling [ ]. Collectively, these mechanisms indicate that ROS serve as important mediators of autophagy under normal conditions, helping cells adapt to metabolic and oxidative stress.
However, excessive ROS accumulation exerts cytotoxic effects that suppress autophagy or trigger apoptosis. High ROS concentrations induce mitochondrial dysfunction, cytochrome c release, and activation of apoptotic pathways, thereby inhibiting autophagy [ , ]. Moreover, excessive ROS can disrupt autophagosomal membrane integrity and impair lysosomal fusion [ ]. This dual nature highlights the dynamic balance between ROS and autophagy. Their interplay plays crucial roles in various pathophysiological contexts. In tumor cells, ROS-induced autophagy helps remove damaged mitochondria and proteins, enhancing cell survival but potentially promoting chemoresistance [ , ]. In neurodegenerative diseases, ROS-triggered autophagy mitigates misfolded protein accumulation, yet excessive ROS may aggravate neuronal injury [ , , ].
At the molecular level, the regulatory network linking ROS and autophagy involves several key mediators. The transcription factor Nrf2, a central regulator of antioxidant defense, is activated by ROS and governs the expression of autophagy-related genes. Likewise, p62/SQSTM1—a selective autophagy substrate—is degraded in a ROS-dependent manner, underscoring the tight interconnection between redox regulation and autophagy [ , ]. Autophagy itself can modulate ROS levels via pathways such as Nrf2/Keap1, forming a feedback mechanism to preserve redox homeostasis. BECLIN1 (ATG6/BECN1), a critical autophagy-related gene, also participates in redox balance and tumor suppression. Its loss or downregulation results in mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress accumulation, and cancer progression, establishing BECLIN1 as a molecular bridge between autophagy and ROS regulation [ ].
In thyroid cancer, particularly at advanced stages, dysregulated autophagy contributes to tumor dedifferentiation and radioiodine refractoriness (RR), highlighting its dual role in tumor biology—cytoprotective in early stages but tumor-promoting when aberrantly activated. NADPH oxidase (NOX), especially NOX4, plays a central role in ROS generation within thyroid cells. Excessive iodide (I-) reduces sodium/iodide symporter (NIS) mRNA and impairs its membrane localization in a ROS-dependent manner. RAI treatment further upregulates NOX4 expression and ROS levels, whereas NOX4 inhibition restores NIS expression suppressed by iodide overload, indicating that NOX4-derived ROS underlie iodine-induced NIS dysfunction ( ) [ ].
 Following tumor dedifferentiation, conventional RAI therapy often fails due to loss of iodine uptake. Redifferentiation therapies aim to restore RAI sensitivity by reactivating iodine-handling pathways. mTOR and MAPK are major inhibitory regulators of iodine uptake in thyroid cancer; their inhibition enhances iodine accumulation and 131I uptake [ ]. Iodine itself can generate mitochondrial O2-, activate PI3K, and suppress NIS expression, whereas PI3K inhibitors restore NIS levels in rat thyroid cells and human PTC [ ].
The BRAFV600E mutation—the most frequent alteration in thyroid cancer—is closely associated with dedifferentiation and downregulation of NIS. ROS-producing NOX4 is elevated in BRAFV600E-positive PTC, while NOX4 knockdown alleviates NIS inhibition. These tumors are highly sensitive to MEK inhibitors, and small-molecule BRAF or MEK inhibitors can restore RAI incorporation in BRAFV600E transgenic mouse models ( ) [ , ].
Iodination, essential for thyroid hormone synthesis, depends on hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and transcription factors such as PAX8, NKX2.1, and FOXE1. The BRAFV600E mutation upregulates TGF-β1, promoting EMT and repressing NIS through inhibition of TSH-induced FOXE1 expression [ , ]. TGF-β1 also induces NOX4 expression in a BRAFV600E-dependent manner, and NOX4-derived ROS are crucial for TGF-β1–mediated NIS suppression ( ) [ ]. Moreover, loss of the tumor suppressor Rap1GAP increases TGF-β1 and decreases NIS expression in thyroid cancer cells [ ].
Recent studies have also explored gene delivery approaches to restore iodine uptake. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) engineered to express NIS demonstrate enhanced iodine uptake when stimulated by TGF-β1, significantly delaying tumor growth and prolonging survival in 131I-treated mice [ ]. Transcription factor PAX8, a major regulator of NIS and thyroid differentiation, binds to the NIS upstream enhancer (NUE) to induce its expression [ , ].
HMGB1, a non-histone chromatin-binding protein and redox sensor, regulates autophagy-mediated NIS degradation via the AMPK/mTOR pathway. ROS and HMGB1 exhibit a reciprocal regulatory relationship: HMGB1 release enhances ROS production, while ROS promote HMGB1 oxidation [ ]. Under nutrient-deprived conditions, sustained ROS generation drives autophagy activation, HMGB1 translocation, and subsequent NIS degradation, suppressing iodine uptake ( ) [ ]. Additionally, macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF), a pro-inflammatory cytokine elevated in thyroid tumors, induces ROS, activates ERK signaling, and promotes HMGB1 translocation, further contributing to NIS inhibition [ ].
 Collectively, these findings indicate that ROS and autophagy are deeply intertwined in thyroid cancer biology. Their regulatory networks not only influence NIS expression and RAI sensitivity but also determine tumor differentiation status. However, the precise mechanisms underlying ROS–autophagy crosstalk across different thyroid cancer subtypes remain incompletely understood and warrant further investigation [ ].

## Clinical significance and future prospects
ROS and autophagy have emerged as critical players in cancer, influencing its initiation and progression. Elevated ROS levels are often associated with the malignant transformation of thyroid cells, leading to genomic instability and the establishment of a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment. Conversely, autophagy, a cellular recycling process, exhibits a dual role in thyroid cancer—acting both as a tumor-suppressive mechanism and as a survival strategy that enables cancer cells to adapt to stress conditions. The intricate crosstalk between ROS and autophagy underscores their significance in the pathophysiology of thyroid malignancies. Beyond thyroid cancer, ROS and autophagy are deeply involved in the pathogenesis and treatment of various diseases, highlighting their translational potential as therapeutic targets. ROS play a dual role in physiological and pathological processes, serving both as signaling molecules and mediators of cellular damage. The interaction between ROS and autophagy has become a research focus in recent years, especially for developing therapeutic strategies targeting these pathways. Autophagy, a highly conserved self-degradative process, maintains cellular homeostasis by removing damaged organelles and protein aggregates. However, excessive ROS accumulation disrupts this balance, leading to cellular dysfunction or death [ , ]. Therefore, precise modulation of ROS and autophagy represents a promising therapeutic avenue for multiple diseases, including cardiovascular disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, and cancer.
In cardiovascular diseases, excessive ROS production is a major driver of myocardial ischemia–reperfusion (I/R) injury. ROS activate NF-κB signaling and suppress PPARγ expression, aggravating inflammation and cardiomyocyte apoptosis [ ]. Natural compounds such as rosmarinic acid (RosA) have been shown to activate PPARγ while inhibiting NF-κB, thereby reducing myocardial injury [ ]. Moreover, moderate activation of autophagy can clear damaged mitochondria and decrease ROS generation, offering cardioprotection [ ]. Conversely, excessive autophagy may trigger cell death, highlighting the need for fine-tuned regulation. Innovative ROS-responsive nanodelivery systems have been developed to locally release autophagy modulators at lesion sites, achieving targeted therapy [ ].
In neurodegenerative diseases, ROS accumulation impairs mitochondrial function and induces neuronal death, while autophagy activation helps remove damaged mitochondria and alleviate oxidative stress [ ]. However, dysregulated autophagy can also promote neuronal degradation. Novel dual-target strategies have thus been developed to modulate both ROS and autophagy. For instance, the Smart Antioxidant Function Enhancing (SAFE) system employs nanomaterials to deliver siRNA targeting Keap1, thereby activating the Nrf2–ARE pathway and enhancing endogenous antioxidant capacity [ ]. ROS-responsive prodrugs have also shown promise; one example releases an ATF6 activator under oxidative conditions to strengthen the endoplasmic reticulum stress response and protect neurons from ROS-induced injury [ ].
In cancer therapy, targeting ROS and autophagy offers unique opportunities. Within the tumor microenvironment, ROS exhibit a dual role—promoting proliferation and survival at moderate levels while inducing apoptosis at high concentrations [ ]. Similarly, autophagy can support tumor growth or lead to cell death depending on context. Strategies that modulate these pathways have demonstrated potential to improve therapeutic efficacy. For instance, ROS-generating nanoplatforms can induce autophagic cell death [ ], whereas ROS-responsive drug delivery systems release autophagy inhibitors in situ to enhance chemotherapy sensitivity [ ].
Furthermore, in immune regulation and fibrotic diseases, modulation of ROS and autophagy also shows significant potential. NADPH oxidases (NOXs) are major enzymatic sources of ROS, and their activity plays essential roles in immune responses and tissue remodeling [ ]. Inhibiting NOXs can suppress ROS generation, thereby reducing inflammation and fibrosis [ ]. Meanwhile, autophagy activation helps degrade damaged extracellular matrix components and facilitates tissue repair [ ]. Multi-target pharmacological strategies that concurrently regulate NOXs and autophagy-related pathways have thus emerged as promising approaches for treating fibrotic disorders [ ].
When exploring the complex landscape of ROS and autophagy in cancer, future research should focus on unraveling the intricate interplay between these pathways, considering variations across cancer subtypes and disease stages. Advanced molecular and integrative analyses can help identify specific biomarkers and therapeutic targets, promoting precision medicine approaches. Moreover, the clinical applications of ROS and autophagy modulation in thyroid cancer extend to prognostic evaluation and therapeutic monitoring. Biomarkers linked to these pathways may serve as indicators of disease progression or treatment response, aiding clinicians in optimizing therapeutic strategies.
In essence, continued exploration of ROS and autophagy in thyroid cancer not only advances our mechanistic understanding but also holds promise for clinical translation. Converting these insights into therapeutic applications may revolutionize the management of thyroid malignancies through more personalized and effective interventions. Collaborative efforts among researchers, clinicians, and the pharmaceutical community will be essential to bring these bench discoveries to the bedside, ultimately improving patient outcomes and redefining cancer therapy paradigms.
