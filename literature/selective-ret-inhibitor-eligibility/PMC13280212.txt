## ABSTRACT
Expedited pathways in the United States (U.S.), European Union (EU), and Japan (JP) enable earlier access to innovative therapies for rare and serious diseases. In 2025, Japan explicitly introduced revocation provisions to its Conditional Early Approval (CEA). Considering the challenges in a related prior scheme—conditional and time‐limited approval for regenerative medical products—particularly withdrawals prior to full approval, this study examined oncology cases under the U.S. Accelerated Approval (AA) and EU Conditional Marketing Authorization (CMA) pathways to identify operational challenges for revocation under Japan's CEA. We conducted a descriptive comparative analysis of oncology drugs granted AA or CMA between January 2015 and December 2024 using publicly available data. Post‐authorization outcomes and JP approval/development status were summarized. AA encompassed 97 products (138 indications), CMA included 27 products (30 indications), and CEA covered 4 products (4 indications). Approximately half of AA/CMA indications were approved in Japan, while some remained undeveloped. The mean time from AA to full approval was 2.7 years and to withdrawal was 3.6 years. Overall, about 15% of AA indications were withdrawn, primarily due to failed confirmatory trials; 8 products/10 indications underwent Oncologic Drugs Advisory Committee deliberations prior to withdrawal or continuation. With recent policy changes aligning CEA eligibility with AA/CMA, broader implementation of CEA is anticipated. For revocation operations, three priorities emerge: (1) timely confirmatory trials and alternative approaches to reduce uncertainty; (2) transparent public evaluation and decision‐making; and (3) flexible patient‐access systems following withdrawal. Continued discussion is required to support effective use of CEA.
Keywords: drug development, FDA, oncology, patients, public policy, regulatory

## Study Highlights

What is the current knowledge on the topic?
○In oncology, the U.S., EU, and JP have expedited approval pathways that allow patients earlier access to new medicines contingent on subsequent confirmatory (verification) studies. Compared with the U.S. and EU, JP's pathway has been underutilized; however, recent policy revisions have aligned its operation more closely with those of the U.S. and EU and have clearly defined the framework for revocation of approval.

What question did this study address?
○Given concerns regarding withdrawal of approval in analogous frameworks for regenerative medical products, we reviewed withdrawal and revocation cases in oncology from the U.S. and EU between 2015 and 2024. The aim was to assess how such actions could be operationalized under the updated policy framework in JP. We identified potential challenges under JP's system and proposed considerations and measures to address them.

What does this study add to our knowledge?
○In the U.S. and EU, some drugs granted expedited approval are withdrawn when confirmatory trials fail to demonstrate sufficient clinical benefits. To prepare for similar situations in JP, we propose three key elements: (1) reducing uncertainty, (2) incorporating input from external experts, and (3) ensuring continued patient access to treatment after withdrawal.

How might this change clinical pharmacology or translational science?
○Encouraging timely completion of confirmatory trials may enable earlier verification of clinical benefit, reducing unnecessary treatment duration and accelerating transition to full approval. Incorporating transparent external advisory processes may enhance the clarity and acceptability of regulatory decisions. Establishing access programs for patients requiring continued treatment may facilitate feasible implementation of withdrawal.

## Introduction
Japan (JP), the United States (U.S.), and the European Union (EU) have established pathways that allow marketing authorization based on exploratory trial results without waiting for confirmatory trial outcomes for drugs intended to treat rare and serious diseases with high medical need (U.S.: Accelerated Approval, [AA]; EU: Conditional Marketing Authorization, [CMA]; JP: Conditional Early Approval, [CEA]) [ , , ]. Since its introduction in 1992, the U.S. AA pathway has been widely utilized, with more than 300 products (indications) approved to date [ ], whereas CMA has been applied in over 80 cases in the EU since 2006 [ ].
In the U.S. AA pathway, it is explicitly stated that approval may be granted based on surrogate endpoints. When a clinical benefit is considered likely based on an evaluation using surrogate endpoints, a new drug may be approved early. In addition, AA includes provisions for withdrawal if confirmatory studies fail to demonstrate efficacy and safety. Discussions to optimize the use of AA are ongoing; the FDA issued draft guidance addressing challenges in the accelerated approval of oncology drugs in March 2023 and January 2025, respectively [ , ].
In the EU, CMA allows authorization based on less comprehensive clinical data than is normally required, particularly when the public health benefit of immediate availability outweighs the risks associated with limited evidence. Key features include annual renewal, labeling requirements indicating conditional status, and applicability in public health emergencies, such as pandemics [ ].
In JP, CEA was implemented in October 2017 and codified into law in September 2020, clarifying its requirements. It enables approval of medicines with high medical necessity when conducting sufficiently powered clinical trials to verify efficacy and safety. However, unlike the U.S. and EU pathways, CEA does not explicitly require post‐approval confirmatory studies to verify efficacy.
To promote greater use of CEA, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) issued a partial revision notice in October 2024 [ ]. In addition, the 2025 amendment to the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Act introduced explicit provisions for revocation of approval [ ]. Despite these developments, CEA has been used in only five cases (four in oncology) as of March 2025. Stakeholders, including industry, healthcare institutions, and patient groups, have called for broader utilization [ ].
This study aimed to elucidate the operational challenges and measures related to the newly introduced revocation provisions under JP's CEA by drawing on experiences from the U.S. AA and EU CMA pathways. In related framework for regenerative medical products in JP (conditional and time‐limited approval), two of six products were withdrawn without obtaining full approval, highlighting operational challenges such as limited predictability and the need for greater transparency in review processes [ , , ]. In this study, revocation refers to authority‐initiated cancelation, whereas withdrawal refers to sponsor or FDA‐initiated cancelation.

2.

## Materials and Methods
2.1.

## Study Design and Period
We conducted a descriptive, cross‐sectional comparative analysis of oncology drugs granted U.S. AA or EU CMA, and JP CEA (since 2017) between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2024. The U.S. AA, EU CMA, and JP CEA pathways were introduced in 1992, 2006, and 2017, respectively.

2.2.

## Data Sources
U.S.: Drugs@FDA; the FDA database of accelerated approvals; approval, and withdrawal documents, labeling, and publicly available Oncologic Drugs Advisory Committee (ODAC) meeting minutes [ , ].
EU: European Medicines Agency (EMA) databases, including Authorization of Medicines, European Public Assessment Reports, and Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use (CHMP) summaries of opinion.
JP: Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) review reports and approval summaries [ ], and Japan Pharmaceutical Manufacturers Association clinical data package summaries [ ].
Sponsors: Company websites and press releases.

2.3.

## Eligibility Criteria and Unit of Analysis
We included oncology drugs and their individual indications that received AA in the U.S. or CMA in the EU within the study window. Each indication was treated as an independent unit of analysis. We excluded non‐oncology products and indications, pediatric indications, and emergency authorizations related to pandemics.

2.4.

## Data Extraction and Definitions
Eligible oncology indications granted AA or CMA were identified from publicly available FDA and EMA sources, with data extracted as of April 12, 2025. Corresponding regulatory outcomes in JP were obtained from PMDA records. Data extracted included authorization dates, subsequent regulatory outcomes, and dates of withdrawal or revocation. Reasons for withdrawal or revocation were classified based on regulator‐published documents for each product. Advisory committee involvement was identified from publicly available ODAC and EMA Scientific Advisory Group (SAG) records when available. Two authors independently extracted and classified all data; discrepancies were resolved through discussion and rereview of source documents.
Outcome categories were defined according to each regulator's terminology: withdrawal (sponsor or FDA‐initiated cancelation of authorization), revocation (authority‐initiated cancelation of authorization), continued conditional authorization with annual reassessment (EU), and conversion to full (standard) approval (U.S.). Time‐to‐outcome was defined as the interval (years) from the AA or CMA date to the earliest of the following full approval, withdrawal, or revocation. Time to JP approval was calculated separately from the AA or CMA date. JP development status was categorized as approved, in development, discontinued, or not initiated as of March 31, 2025.

3.

## Results
3.1.

## Products and Indications Granted U.S. AA, EU CMA, and JP CEA

Between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2024, oncology drugs granted AA/CMA/CEA included 97 products with 138 indications in the U.S. and 27 products with 30 indications in the EU, compared with only four products (four indications) in JP. The number of AA products in the U.S. was highest each year, while CMA products in the EU increased after 2019. Although CEA has been operational in JP since 2017, its use has remained limited, with no cases since its legislative codification in 2020 (Figure  ).
 
3.2.

## Post‐Authorization Outcomes Following U.S. AA and EU CMA

In the U.S., 32 products (53 indications; 38% of total indications) converted to full approval. The mean interval from AA to full approval was 2.7 years (median 2.3; range 0.6–7.2). In contrast, 16 products (21 indications; 15% of total indications) were voluntarily withdrawn by sponsors, with a mean interval to withdrawal of 3.6 years (median 3.2; range 1.3–7.1). Some indications required extended periods to complete confirmatory trials.
In the EU, three products (three indications; 10% of total indications) were withdrawn by sponsors (two products, two indications) or revoked (one product, one indication), with a mean interval to outcome of 2.9 years (median 2.9; range 2.8–3.1). No withdrawals or revocations were observed in JP following CEA (Figure  ).
 
3.3.

## 
JP Development Status for Indications Granted U.S. AA and EU CMA

Among 138 indications granted U.S. AA, 70 (51%) were approved in JP. Of the remainder, 34 (25%) were in development, 14 (10%) discontinued, and 20 (14%) not initiated. Notably, two withdrawn indications had been approved in JP.
Among the 30 indications granted EU CMA, 15 (50%) were approved in JP, 12 (40%) were in development, 2 (7%) discontinued, and 1 (3%) not initiated (Figure  ). None of the withdrawn EU indications were approved in JP. Overall, 19 products (21 indications) had not been initiated in JP, indicating a persistent drug lag and loss (Table  ).
 
3.4.

## Time Comparison Between JP CEA and U.S. AA

Among the 138 AA indications, 70 (51%) were approved in JP. The mean interval from AA to JP approval was 1.5 years (median 1.4; range −9.0 to 5.7). The mean interval from U.S. full approval to JP approval was −0.7 years (median 0.2; range −9.7 to −3.4). To assess potential time lags in publicly available sources, sensitivity analyses assuming JP approvals occurred 6 and 12 months earlier yielded mean intervals from AA to JP approval of 1.0 and 0.5 years (median 0.9 and 0.4; range −10.0 to 5.2) respectively, and from U.S. full approval to JP approval of −1.2 and −1.7 years (median −0.3 and −0.8; range −10.7 to −2.9).
For seven indications (10%), JP approval preceded U.S. AA (Table  ). The mean interval from U.S. AA to JP approval was −1.8 years (median −0.8; range −1.4 to −0.1), reflecting differences in indication scope including combination regimens and programs such as SAKIGAKE [ ]. Additionally, 15 indications (21%) were approved in JP before conversion from AA to full approval in the U.S. (Figure  ).
 
3.5.

## Time Comparison Between JP CEA and EU CMA

Among 30 CMA indications 15 (50%) were approved in JP. The mean interval from CMA to JP approval was 0.4 years (median 0.2; range −1.1 to 2.5). Five indications (25%) were approved in JP before CMA (Table  ). The mean interval from JP approval to CMA was −0.6 years (median −0.7; range −1.1 to 0; minimum difference 3 days) (Figure  ).

3.6.

## Withdrawn Products and Indications in the U.S.
We analyzed 16 products (21 indications) following AA (Table  ). Of the withdrawn indications, all were voluntary withdrawals by the sponsors except melphalan flufenamide, which was withdrawn by the FDA. Withdrawal reasons included failed confirmatory trials (16 indications), infeasible trials (two indications), safety concerns (two indications), and commercial reasons (one indication). Most withdrawals followed discussions with the FDA or the advisory committee when clinical benefit was not confirmed. The ODAC [ ] evaluates safety and efficacy data from scientific and ethical perspectives and provides recommendations to the FDA through public discussions and voting. Although not binding, these recommendations are typically considered in regulatory decisions. ODAC reviewed eight products (10 indications) to assess continuation of approval. The main points based on public meeting minutes of the ODAC are summarized below:
 For PD‐1/PD‐L1 inhibitors (e.g., pembrolizumab, nivolumab, and atezolizumab), repeated failures of confirmatory trials—including those involving other agents in the same class—prompted the ODAC to evaluate whether approval should be maintained for certain indications [ , ]. Atezolizumab indications for urothelial carcinoma and triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC) were subsequently withdrawn; notably, the TNBC indication was withdrawn despite an ODAC recommendation to maintain approval.
For PI3K inhibitors, concerns regarding toxicity prompted the ODAC to conduct a crosscutting deliberation on a future evaluation approach for this drug class, including agents developed and approved for hematologic malignancies [ ].
Melphalan flufenamide was reviewed by the ODAC based on confirmatory trial results. Potential worsening of overall survival (OS) and severe toxicity were identified, leading to the conclusion that a limited efficacy signal alone does not justify approval or maintenance [ ].
For panobinostat, AA was granted based on subgroup analyses; however, conducting a more robust confirmatory trial was deemed infeasible. The ODAC subsequently evaluated the risk–benefit profile of panobinostat‐based combination therapy and concluded that the observed improvement in progression‐free survival (PFS) did not provide sufficient clinical benefit to outweigh toxicity [ ]. In Japan, the drug was approved on July 3, 2015, following the U.S. AA; however, in line with the subsequent U.S. withdrawal, sales were discontinued in March 2024.

3.7.

## Revoked or Withdrawn Products and Indications in EU

We summarize the reasons for revocation or withdrawal from the EU for three products (three indications) following CMA (Table  ). Of these, two indications were voluntarily withdrawn by the sponsors, and one was revoked by the EMA. The reasons included failure of confirmatory trials (two indications) and commercial reasons (one indication). The EMA utilizes external advisory committees, including SAG [ ] for Oncology, composed of experts who provide scientific and clinical input on medicinal product evaluation. However, proceedings of these groups are generally not publicly accessible; therefore, no public records were identified for deliberations related to revocations or withdrawals by the Oncology SAG.
 Pralsetinib for rearranged during transfection (RET) fusions‐positive non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) was withdrawn in the EU for commercial reasons, whereas in the U.S., the same indication transitioned from AA to full approval. In JP development had not been initiated at the time of this study. This discrepancy likely reflects prior selpercatinib approval, another RET inhibitor, across the U.S., EU, and JP. Regulatory decisions regarding continuation of approval after AA/CMA/CEA may vary across regions due to differences in healthcare systems, availability of alternative therapies, and sponsor/partner strategies. Similarly, for atezolizumab in TNBC, this indication was withdrawn in the U.S. and application was withdrawn in the EU, while approval remains in effect in JP [ ]. In the present study, most products and indications withdrawn after U.S. AA or EU CMA were either discontinued or not initiated in JP.

4.

## Discussion
4.1.

## Why CEA Has Been Underused in JP and Why This May Change
In principle, CEA in JP is not substantially different from AA in the U.S. and CMA in the EU; however, its utilization has been markedly lower. Although AA use in the U.S. declined after 2020―possibly reflecting stricter FDA expectations for confirmed clinical benefit—the gap with JP remains substantial, which may also have limited CEA uptake in JP (Figure  ). Post‐authorization withdrawals or revocations occurred in 15% of AA and 10% of CMA indications, with mean times of 3.6 and 2.9 years, respectively; similar or higher rates may emerge in JP as CEA use expands (Figure  ). Approximately half of AA/CMA indications were approved in JP; however, fewer U.S. AA indications were in development (25% vs. 40%), and more were not initiated (15% vs. 3%) compared with CMA, suggesting greater divergence between JP and the U.S. and persistent drug lag and loss versus the U.S. (Figure  ).
Since the 2006 revision of oncology guidelines in JP, OS has been emphasized, and approvals based only solely on surrogate endpoints have become less common [ ]. Although surrogate endpoints are increasingly used in rare cancers, JP lacks a consolidated framework specifying acceptable surrogates, reflecting a more conservative regulatory stance. The PMDA also requires additional studies when ethnic differences or JP‐specific concerns arise [ ]. In practice, among JP approved indications with U.S. AA (N = 70), 76% included Japan in pivotal multiregional clinical trial, 53% had domestic studies, and 29% had both. For JP approved indications with EU CMA (N = 15), the corresponding proportions were 80%, 53%, and 29%, respectively. These Japanese data constituted the primary evidence base for regulatory approval, and no indication was approved without Japanese patient data. This suggests that both the acceptability of surrogate endpoints and the requirement for Japanese patient data are key drivers of differences in framework utilization across JP, the U.S., and the EU.
Multiple drugs have been approved in JP ahead of U.S. AA or EU CMA. The SAKIGAKE designation, implemented in September 2020, applies to medicines that enable early development and submission in JP ahead of other regions. It offers a shortened review period (6 months vs. 9 months for priority review), although eligibility is limited. In addition, many drugs approved in JP prior to full approval in the US were for rare cancers and were approved before the introduction of CEA, based on exploratory trials that included Japanese patients. Consequently, there was limited incentive to use CEA, given its stringent applicability criteria and lower predictability (Figure  ).
The October 2024 partial revision notice for CEA guidance encourages its use for products approved under schemes analogous to overseas conditional approvals for which confirmatory trials are planned post‐approval, including across different lines of therapy. Although Japanese data remain important, the guidance clarifies that inclusion of Japanese participants is not always required and that overseas confirmatory clinical trials may be acceptable. Furthermore, the 2025 amendment to the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Act introduced explicit provisions for revocation of approval.
As CEA becomes more closely aligned with AA/CMA, its broader utilization may increase access to innovative therapies. Historically, JP's regulatory system has limited approval of lower‐evidence drugs through stringent evaluation of surrogate endpoints and requirements for Japanese patient data. Increased use of CEA may introduce greater uncertainty regarding the clinical benefit of approved drugs in JP. From the clinical development perspective, reducing uncertainty is essential; from the regulatory perspective, a robust and transparent evaluation process is required; and from the patient perspective, access to treatment after revocation or withdrawal remains a key concern. These issues are discussed below from each of these three perspectives.

4.2.

## Reducing Uncertainty
Establishing systems to obtain confirmatory results promptly after AA can minimize prolonged exposure to drugs without verified clinical benefits, reduce legal and ethical risks, and improve predictability in development planning. This is supported by the following cases presented in this study.
As shown in Figure  , confirmatory trials required as conditions for approval often take considerable time, raising concerns about extended use of drugs with uncertain efficacy. In the U.S., delays and non‐completion of confirmatory trials, as well as ethical challenges in conducting such trials when alternative therapies exist, have been reported [ , , ].
As observed with panobinostat, confirmatory trials may be difficult to conduct, leading to delays in withdrawal decisions particularly in rare diseases with small patient populations. Even when global confirmatory trials are feasible, JP participation may be limited because of differences in clinical infrastructure and treatment practices, limiting evaluation in Japanese patients. According to the notification on “Basic Principles on Japanese Clinical Trial Data for Drugs for Rare Diseases, etc., for Which Confirmatory Clinical Trials Have Been Conducted Only Overseas” [ ], clinical trials are not always required; their necessity should be assessed based on scientific validity and feasibility. Alternative approaches, such as post‐marketing surveillance or studies using medical information databases (e.g., MID‐NET) and patient registries may be acceptable if they fulfill data requirements. Accordingly, post‐marketing programs incorporating real‐world data (RWD) for effectiveness and safety evaluations may serve as viable alternatives. RWD has been used in regulatory submissions, suggesting its potential to support approval decisions [ ]. However, compared with the U.S. and EU, RWD utilization in JP remains limited, with challenges including data transparency and interfacility collaboration [ ].

4.3.

## Public Evaluation and Decision‐Making Process
Findings from ODAC deliberations presented in this study suggest that open discussions involving external experts and patients can enhance social acceptability and may substantially influence FDA evaluations and final decisions [ ]. However, limitations remain in the evaluation of single‐arm trials [ ], including low precision of surrogate endpoints [ , , ] and potential concerns related to ethnic differences, both of which complicate data interpretation and subsequent decision‐making. In particular, evaluating rare cancers and therapies with novel mechanisms of action can be challenging.
As shown in Table  , olaratumab for treatment of soft tissue sarcoma initially showed a correlation between increased OS observed in early phase trials and drug exposure, which was considered supportive evidence of efficacy. However, a subsequent large confirmatory trial failed to demonstrate any clinical benefit, ultimately leading to withdrawal in the U.S. and revocation in the EU [ ].
As reported in the case of atezolizumab, there are instances in which products whose AA/CMA have been withdrawn remain approved in other countries [ , ]. For atezolizumab and pembrolizumab, unlike other agents in the PD‐1/PD‐L1 inhibitor class, it has been reported that the incidence of adverse events in Japanese populations is not higher than that in non‐Japanese populations [ ]. Based on cases identified in this study, when confirmatory trial results are discordant or when the safety profile differs in Japanese patients, it is important to discuss in an open forum whether continued approval remains appropriate.

4.4.

## Patient Access After Withdrawal or Revocation
In nearly all cases in which approval was withdrawn, sponsors have offered continued access to the drug via Expanded Access Programs (EAPs) [ ]. In the U.S., EAPs allow exceptional access to investigational or post‐revocation medicines for patients with serious diseases, while in the EU, Compassionate Use provides a similar pathway for continued access [ ]. In addition, the U.S. has the physician‐initiated Individual IND pathway, and the EU has Named Patient Programs both of which provide established mechanisms for individual patient access [ , ]. Compared with systems in the U.S. and EU, JP has physician‐initiated mechanisms, such as expanded access trials and the patient‐requested therapy scheme [ , ]; however, their utilization remains lower. The utilization rate of expanded access trials is approximately 2.5%, and the system has been criticized for its complexity and limited resources. For patient‐requested medical care, the pathway to access is less transparent and, unlike in other countries, it largely relies on the framework of standard clinical trials. Accordingly, recommendations include the introduction of individual‐patient access programs similar to those in the U.S., simplification of procedures, and provision of financial and operational support [ , , ].

4.5.

## Limitations
This study has certain limitations. It was a descriptive analysis based on publicly available information limited to anticancer drugs, including official regulatory documents and company disclosures, and is therefore subject to publication lags and incomplete reporting of nonpublic details. Reasons for withdrawal and revocation were classified based on publicly available descriptions and may have been incompletely documented. Our classifications are based on publicly available information; thus, the timing of disclosure is determined by government agencies and may not capture confidential discussions between regulators and sponsors, particularly regarding trial feasibility in rare diseases and the underlying reasons for withdrawal or revocation. Nevertheless, our sensitivity analysis suggests that the estimate of the drug lag remains consistent. As this was a secondary analysis of publicly available information and did not involve human subjects, ethical review was not required. Prospective evaluations are warranted to assess operational challenges arising during real‐world implementation of JP's CEA.

4.6.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, the present study highlights key operational challenges for CEA in JP based on findings from the U.S. AA and the EU CMA. Recent regulatory revisions are expected to increase CEA utilization by aligning it more closely with international frameworks and clarifying revocation processes. While broader utilization of the system is anticipated, several challenges remain with respect to revocation: (1) reducing uncertainty through the rapid and reliable execution of confirmatory trials and alternative approaches such as RWD; (2) ensuring open and transparent evaluation and decision‐making processes; and (3) establishing flexible patient access systems after withdrawal or revocation. CEA is a groundbreaking framework that enables timely access to innovative medicines. Sustained efforts are required to enhance predictability and social acceptability, thereby supporting practical and trustworthy implementation of the system (Figure  ).
 These findings are consistent with prior studies on AA withdrawals [ , ] and reports on real‐world use following AA withdrawal [ ]. Prior studies in Japan [ ] as well as comparative research on divergent regulatory decisions across countries and regions [ , ] emphasize the need for transparent regulatory frameworks. This study is distinctive in that it systematically summarizes post‐AA/CMA authorization outcomes and identifies the operational challenges associated with CEA revocation from three actionable perspectives.
