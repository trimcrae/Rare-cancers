Updated studies by Wirth et al. have demonstrated that selpercatinib provides durable efficacy and consistent safety in RET-mutant medullary thyroid cancer (MTC) and RET fusion-positive differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) for a longer period of time ( , ). At a median follow-up of 42.4 months, the objective response rate (ORR) for cabozantinib/vandetanib-naïve MTC patients was 82.5%. For treatment-naïve radioiodine-refractory DTC (RR-DTC), the ORR was 95.8% at a median follow-up of 24.9 months ( ) ( ). Such excellent outcomes observed for MTC patients in the study of Wirth et al. ( ) are not surprising, as similar results have been demonstrated in a previous randomized controlled phase 3 trial showing that selpercatinib significantly outperforms a control group, with a hazard ratio for progression-free survival (PFS) of 0.28 (P<0.001) and a 12-month PFS rate of 86.8% vs. 65.7% ( ). Selpercatinib also showed a higher overall response rate (69.4% vs. 38.8%) and a lower rate of treatment discontinuation due to adverse events (4.7% vs. 26.8%) ( ).
 Currently, one of the standard treatments for advanced RR-DTC is lenvatinib, which exhibits a high efficacy ( ). A randomized phase 3 SELECT trial has demonstrated the efficacy of lenvatinib in patients with advanced, progressive RR-DTC, reporting a median PFS of 18.3 months, significantly longer than a median PFS of 3.6 months observed in the placebo group ( ). An updated analysis of the SELECT trial with an extended follow-up further validated these results, showing a median PFS of 19.4 months in the lenvatinib group compared to 3.7 months in the placebo group ( ).
Although lenvatinib has demonstrated a high efficacy, treatment-related toxicities, such as hypertension (67.8%), fatigue (59.0%), diarrhea (59.4%), decreased appetite (50.2%), weight loss (46.4%), and proteinuria (31.0%), were frequently reported in the SELECT trial ( ). In addition, grade ≥3 dose-limiting and troublesome adverse events such as severe hypertension (16–60%) and profound proteinuria (17.2–32%) happened in a real-world study of more Asian patients ( - ). Especially, as a multi-kinase inhibitor with a vascular endothelial growth factor receptor targeting and nonselective nature, lenvatinib carries inherent cardiovascular off-target adverse effects, including an increased risk of vascular complications such as hypertension, cardiovascular events, and stroke, particularly with a prolonged use ( , ).
In contrast, selpercatinib, a selective RET inhibitor, shows a manageable safety profile, with the most common grade ≥3 adverse events being hypertension (15.2%) and increased alanine aminotransferase (ALT) (6.1%) in RET fusion-positive RR-DTC. Grade ≥3 prolonged QT interval occurred in 4.5% of TC patients. Although rare, treatment-related severe adverse events including abdominal pain, hyperbilirubinemia, and vomiting occurred in Wirth et al.’s report ( ). In this regard, selpercatinib offers the potential to overcome limitations of existing therapies in terms of both efficacy and safety in RET fusion-positive DTC.
In a phase 3 study with MTC patients, treatment failure-free survival at 12 months was significantly higher in the selpercatinib group (86.2%) than in the control group (62.1%) ( ). Additionally, adverse events requiring dose reduction and treatment discontinuation were notably less frequent in the selpercatinib group than in the control group (adverse events requiring dose reduction: 38.9% vs. 77.3%; adverse events requiring treatment discontinuation: 4.7% vs. 26.8%) ( ). In addition to QT interval prolongation and elevated ALT levels, ascites, a rare and unfamiliar adverse event, occurred in 1.5% of patients with MTC in the LIBRETTO-001 study ( ). Ascites or chylous effusion has been observed in patients with thyroid cancer or lung cancer following selpercatinib treatment based on our own clinical experience and others ( , ). It might be due to the possibility that RET inhibition could alter lymphatic endothelial cells’ integrity, leading to an impairment of lymphatic endothelial permeability ( ).
On the other hand, Wirth et al. have reported an ORR of 95.8% for treatment-naïve RET fusion-positive RR-DTC patients (n=24) while previously treated patients (n=41) achieved an ORR of 85.4% ( ). These results carefully provide a rationale for using selpercatinib as a first-line treatment in RET fusion-positive DTC ( , ). In addition, selpercatinib has demonstrated efficacy in both RET-positive lung cancer, thyroid cancer, and other solid tumors, suggesting its potential for tissue-agnostic therapy ( ).
While most patients exhibited durable responses to selpercatinib, some cases showed disease progression ( ). Among treatment-naïve RET fusion-positive DTC patients, disease progression was 12.5%, while it was notably higher at 39.0% in previously treated DTC patients. Similarly, in RET-mutant MTC, progression was observed in 23.1% of cabozantinib/vandetanib-naïve patients and 34.9% of previously treated patients, suggesting a higher likelihood of disease progression in pretreated populations ( ). These observations highlight the need for in-depth studies on resistance mechanisms to pave the way for second-generation RET inhibitors or combination therapies to effectively manage resistance, just like in the experience with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) targeting the BCR::ABL1 fusion protein in chronic myeloid leukemia ( - ). Acquired resistance often happens when new mutations occur in the RET gene (e.g., solvent front mutations like G810C/S/R), making selpercatinib less effective ( ). It can also result from cancer cells activating other signaling pathways such as mesenchymal-epithelial transition (MET) factor or Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) amplification to bypass RET inhibition ( ). Primary resistance, on the other hand, may arise due to inherent tumor heterogeneity, where subpopulations of cancer cells harbor alternative driver mutations or signaling pathways, rendering them less dependent on RET activation ( ).
Resistance to TKIs in thyroid cancer is now prompting research into alternative strategies. Approaches such as combining TKIs with immune checkpoint inhibitors and targeting specific resistance mechanisms are under investigation to improve therapeutic outcomes ( ). However, dual immune checkpoint inhibitors have shown limited efficacy in DTC or MTC likely due to their low tumor mutation burden. In contrast, these therapies have demonstrated effectiveness in anaplastic thyroid cancer, which has a higher tumor mutation burden ( ). Therefore, in DTC and MTC, future research should focus on targeting specific mutations and developing second-generation therapies to overcome resistance and improve patient outcomes.
Selpercatinib has demonstrated the potential to serve as a first-line treatment option for RET fusion-positive DTC, offering a high efficacy with a low incidence of adverse effects and providing patients with a novel therapeutic alternative. However, ongoing research is needed to address acquired resistance and manage long-term adverse effects, which have not been fully identified yet. Wonderful results from thyroid cancer will enhance selpercatinib’s clinical application and contribute to the advancement of tissue-agnostic therapies based on RET inhibition.
