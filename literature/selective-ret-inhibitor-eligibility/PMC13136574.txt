By contrast, the incidence of adult thyroid cancer is 14.6 per 100,000 person-years according to the SEER database, representing an incidence approximately 13–15 times higher than that observed in children [ ]. While a recent increase in incidence has been reported, with an annual percent change of 4.0%–9.5% from the 2000s to the mid-2010s, a similar trend has also been observed in the adult population [ ]. Despite its rarity, pediatric thyroid cancer exhibits distinct clinical and molecular characteristics that allow differentiation from adult thyroid cancer [ ]. In children, the likelihood of malignancy when a thyroid nodule is present is notably higher than in adults, ranging from 22%–26% compared with 5%–10% in adults [ ].
With respect to molecular profiles, BRAF V600E mutations, which are commonly observed in adult thyroid cancer (49%–62%) [ ], are less frequently reported in pediatric thyroid cancer (20%–39%) [ ]. Instead, gene fusions involving RET or NTRK are more prevalent, particularly in younger children [ ]. In addition, pediatric patients are more likely to present with advanced disease at diagnosis. Regional lymph node metastases occur in approximately 60%–80% of pediatric thyroid cancers, compared with 20%–50% of thyroid cancers in adults, and distant metastases are reported in 5%–25% of pediatric patients, in comparison with typical rates of 2%–5% in adults [ ]. Another important consideration is the smaller thyroid gland volume in children; therefore, a tumor of the same absolute size may occupy a greater proportion of the thyroid in pediatric patients [ ], potentially increasing the likelihood of capsular invasion or extension into adjacent vital structures. However, despite a typically more aggressive initial presentation, the prognosis of pediatric thyroid cancer is generally more favorable than that of thyroid cancer in adults [ ]. All-stage mortality is less than 1% in children, compared with 1%–2% in adults [ ]. Moreover, in the setting of distant metastasis, mortality rates in children remain between 1%–7%, which is substantially lower than the rates of at least 20% observed in adults [ ]. This improved prognosis may be attributable to several factors, including the predominance of well-differentiated tumor histology, a lower incidence of bone metastases, and a favorable response to radioactive iodine therapy in the pediatric population.
Given these biological and clinical differences, it may not be appropriate to apply adult-based management guidelines to pediatric thyroid cancer. In this context, the Korean Society of Thyroid Radiology published the 2021 Korean Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System (K-TIRADS), which reflects the unique characteristics of the pediatric population. Accordingly, this review aims to present the background and details of the 2021 K-TIRADS from a pediatric-specific perspective, highlight the need for tailored diagnostic and therapeutic approaches, and discuss future refinements of pediatric-specific ultrasound (US) risk-stratification systems.

## APPLICATION OF ADULT-BASED ULTRASOUND RISK-STRATIFICATION SYSTEMS IN CHILDREN BEFORE THE 2021 K-TIRADS
The primary consideration for radiologists when evaluating thyroid nodules is estimating the likelihood of malignancy and determining the necessity for biopsy. Several international societies have proposed US-based stratification systems that utilize sonographic features, including composition, echogenicity, shape, orientation, margins, and echogenic foci, to classify nodules according to their malignancy risk. Based on the assigned category, different size thresholds were proposed to guide decisions regarding the requirements for fine-needle aspiration biopsy, making this stratification a cornerstone of nodule management.
Commonly used US risk-stratification systems include the American College of Radiology TIRADS (ACR TI-RADS) [ ], American Association of Clinical Endocrinology/American College of Endocrinology/Associazione Medici Endocrinologi (AACE/ACE/AME) Medical Guidelines for Clinical Practice for the Diagnosis and Management of Thyroid Nodules-2016 Update [ ], American Thyroid Association (ATA) Management Guidelines for Adult Patients with Thyroid Nodules and Differentiated Thyroid Cancer [ ], European TIRADS (EU-TIRADS) [ ], and Chinese TIRADS (C-TIRADS) [ ]. Although several guidelines include pediatric-specific descriptions, these systems were mainly developed using adult populations, raising concerns about their direct application to children ( ).
In 2021, a meta-analysis evaluated studies that investigated the diagnostic accuracy of the ACR TIRADS and ATA guidelines when directly applied to children [ ]. When category 4 or 5 was defined as a positive index test for the ACR TI-RADS, the pooled sensitivity was 84% (95% CI: 78%–89%) and the pooled specificity was 64% (95% CI: 41%–81%). For the ATA system, considering high- or intermediate-suspicion nodules as a positive index test yielded a pooled sensitivity of 90% (95% CI: 83%–94%) and a specificity of 50% (95% CI: 32%–68%). For ACR-TIRADS, the pooled missed malignancy and unnecessary biopsy rates were 21.7% and 62.7%, respectively, which are relatively high values, indicating that the direct application of adult-based risk stratification systems is questionable.
Kim et al. [ ] evaluated 277 nodules from 221 pediatric patients who underwent percutaneous biopsy and compared the diagnostic performances of the ACR TI-RADS, AACE/ACE/AME, ATA, EU-TIRADS, and 2016 K-TIRADS. In detail, the sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were as follows: ACR TI-RADS: 70% (95% CI: 62%–77%), 78% (95% CI: 70%–84%), and 74% (95% CI: 68%–78%), respectively; ATA: 77% (95% CI: 69%–83%), 42% (95% CI: 34%–51%), and 61% (95% CI: 55%–67%); AACE/ACE/AME: 76% (95% CI: 68%–83%), 51% (95% CI: 42%–59%), and 65% (95% CI: 59%–70%); EU-TIRADS: 76% (95% CI: 68%–83%), 46% (95% CI: 37%–54%), and 62% (95% CI: 56%–68%); 2016 K-TIRADS: 78% (95% CI: 71%–84%), 43% (95% CI: 35%–52%), and 62% (95% CI: 57%–68%).
Using the biopsy recommendation as a positive index test, ACR TI-RADS demonstrated the highest overall diagnostic accuracy (74% vs. 61%–65%) and specificity among the evaluated systems (78% vs. 42%–51%). Similarly, in other studies, the ACR TI-RADS showed higher specificity than the ATA guideline [ ] or EU-TIRADS [ ], except for the study by Tuli et al. [ ], in which the EU-TIRADS showed slightly higher specificity than the ACR TI-RADS (57% vs. 50%). This higher specificity was likely due to the relatively higher biopsy cutoff sizes applied to moderately and mildly suspicious nodules in the ACR TI-RADS, given that biopsy cutoff sizes are a well-established factor influencing diagnostic accuracy in risk-stratification systems [ ]. In all five risk-stratification systems, the biopsy cutoff size for the highest category was 1 cm. However, the biopsy cut-off sizes differ in the next highest category: ACR TI-RADS recommends biopsy for moderately suspicious nodules ≥1.5 cm, whereas the ATA and 2016 K-TIRADS recommend biopsy for intermediate-suspicion nodules ≥1 cm. In addition, ACR TI-RADS recommends biopsy for mildly suspicious nodules ≥2.5 cm, while the ATA guideline and 2016 K-TIRADS recommend biopsy for low-suspicion nodules ≥1.5 cm, and EU-TIRADS recommends biopsy for low-risk nodules ≥2 cm. This indicates that, even in pediatric thyroid nodules, increasing the biopsy size cutoff for categories with a low risk of cancer may help reduce unnecessary biopsy rates, thereby leading to a higher overall accuracy.
In contrast, the risk of malignancy in the highest category was higher in pediatric patients. Kim et al. [ ] reported that the risk of malignancy in the highest category of pediatric thyroid nodules ranged from 85.4% to 94.0% (93.2% for ACR TI-RADS, 93.4% for ATA, 85.4% for AACE/ACE/AME, 86.0% for EU TIRADS, and 94.0% for 2016 K TIRADS). Considering that the corresponding risk in adults ranges from 32.3% to 66.1% [ ], the values in the pediatric population were high. This suggests that lowering the biopsy size cutoff for the highest category could enhance sensitivity and minimize missed malignancies without substantial tradeoffs in specificity or unnecessary biopsy rates.
These findings suggest that a two-track approach, that is, lowering the biopsy cutoff size for the highest-category nodules while raising the biopsy cutoff size for nodules in relatively lower categories, could be effective in pediatric patients. Kim et al. [ ] demonstrated that when all nodules in the highest category were biopsied (regardless of nodule size) rather than just those over the conventional 1-cm threshold, the sensitivity increased by 13%–18%. In addition, when the ACR TI-RADS biopsy criteria were applied to the other guidelines, the specificity improved by 7%–37%. Notably, the overall accuracy was the highest when both strategies were combined, with a 5%–20% improvement compared with the original versions.

## APPLICATION OF THE 2021 K-TIRADS TO CHILDREN
When adult-based US risk stratification systems have been applied to pediatric populations, they have shown suboptimal diagnostic accuracy, suggesting that further modifications are necessary. These modifications can include adjustments of biopsy size cutoff thresholds, incorporation of clinical risk factors, such as a family history of cancer or prior irradiation, and integration of specific US characteristics of the diffuse sclerosing subtype of papillary thyroid carcinoma, which is more prevalent in pediatric papillary thyroid cancers (PTCs) [ ]. In this context, the Korean Society of Thyroid Radiology released an updated 2021 version of the K-TIRADS, which revised the guidelines established in 2016 [ ]. The major changes in K-TIRADS from 2016 to 2021 are summarized in .
The 2021 K-TIRADS introduced several refinements to the US lexicon to improve diagnostic consistency, interobserver agreement, and clinical applicability. In the 2016 K-TIRADS, marked hypoechogenicity was defined as echogenicity lower than that of the anterior neck muscles; in the 2021 K-TIRADS, this category was expanded to include nodules with similar echogenicity relative to the anterior neck muscles, based on evidence demonstrating comparable malignancy risk between these nodules [ ] and the practical difficulty in reliably distinguishing these echogenicity levels [ ]. In the 2016 K-TIRADS, mild hypoechogenicity was defined as hypoechoic relative to the surrounding thyroid parenchyma, whereas in the 2021 K-TIRADS, normal thyroid parenchyma was used as the reference to avoid misclassification in cases of diffuse thyroid disease. Notably, the terminology for calcifications was revised to echogenic foci, acknowledging that echogenic foci may represent inspissated colloids as well as microcalcifications. Additionally, orientation assessment was standardized to the transverse plane, as prior evidence demonstrated no significant difference in the diagnostic accuracy of the K-TIRADS biopsy criteria according to the imaging plane used [ ]. Finally, margin descriptors were simplified by integrating spiculated or microlobulated margins into the category of irregular margins, thereby enhancing the reporting clarity and consistency.
The 2021 K-TIRADS also revised the biopsy size cutoff criteria for pediatric patients. The biopsy size cut-off for high-suspicion nodules in children was reduced from 1.0 cm to 0.5 cm, whereas the cut-off for intermediate-suspicion nodules was increased from 1.0 cm to 1.5 cm (in the absence of specific risk factors), and that for low-suspicion nodules was increased from 1.5 cm to 2.0 cm. In addition, when the patient has certain risk factors, such as [18F]fluorodeoxyglucose avidity on PET scan, familial cancer (e.g., DICER1 syndrome, MEN type 2, Cowden syndrome), worrisome symptoms (e.g., dysphonia), worrisome nodule locations (e.g., attachment to the trachea or the posteromedial capsule along the course of the recurrent laryngeal nerve), or patient-specific factors (e.g., age, comorbidities, and personal preference), the biopsy size cut-off for intermediate-suspicion nodules can be lowered to 1.0 cm.
In children, the 2021 K-TIRADS demonstrated significantly higher sensitivity (90.3% vs. 81.2%; P = 0.004), specificity (47.9% vs. 37.0%; P < 0.001), and accuracy (68.9% vs. 58.4%; P = 0.01) than the 2016 K-TIRADS ( ) [ ]. When the biopsy cut-off sizes for other categories were held constant, the application of a 0.5-cm biopsy cut-off size for high-suspicion nodules in the 2021 version resulted in slightly lower specificity (47.9% vs. 51.6%; P = 0.004) but higher sensitivity (90.3% vs. 77.2%; P < 0.001) and accuracy (68.9% vs. 62.7%; P < 0.001) compared to using a 1.0-cm biopsy cut-off size. Similarly, when a 1.0–1.5-cm biopsy cutoff size was applied to intermediate-suspicion nodules while the biopsy cutoff sizes for other categories were held constant, there was no significant difference in sensitivity (90.3% vs. 91.6%; P = 0.063) or accuracy (68.9% vs. 68.0%; P = 0.436); however, the specificity was slightly higher (47.9% vs. 44.9%; P = 0.018), as described in [ ]. This supports the effectiveness of the two-track approach advocated by the 2021 K-TIRADS, which lowers the biopsy cutoff size for high-suspicion nodules while increasing it for intermediate- to low-suspicion nodules.

## OTHER ATTEMPTS TO MODIFY EXISTING ULTRASOUND RISK-STRATIFICATION SYSTEMS FOR PEDIATRIC APPLICATIONS
Similar to the recent revisions in the K-TIRADS, several other efforts have been made to improve the diagnostic performance of US risk-stratification systems in pediatric populations. Among these, the ACR TI-RADS is the most extensively studied system.
 summarizes the diagnostic performance of the original ACR TI-RADS and various studies that evaluated modified biopsy cutoff size strategies [ ]. These modifications include lowering the biopsy threshold for TR5 nodules to 0.5 cm or performing a biopsy for any TR5 nodule when technically feasible, as well as lowering or conversely increasing the biopsy cutoff for TR3 or TR4 nodules. Srivatsa et al. [ ] modified the ACR TI-RADS biopsy thresholds as follows: TR5: from 1.0 cm to any size if technically feasible; TR4: from 1.5 cm to 1.0 cm; TR3: from 2.5 cm to 1.5 cm. These modifications resulted in an increase in sensitivity from 94.6% to 100%, a decrease in specificity from 71.9% to 55.3%, and a decrease in the overall accuracy from 75.4% to 62.3%. Similarly, Marzukie et al. [ ] reduced the biopsy cutoff sizes for TR3, TR4, and TR5 nodules, leading to an increase in sensitivity from 83.3% to 94.4%, a decrease in specificity from 75.5% to 63.3%, and a decrease in accuracy from 76.7% to 68.1%. Although an increase in sensitivity would promote the early detection of pediatric thyroid cancer and reduce the likelihood of missed malignancy, it comes at the cost of increasing unnecessary biopsy rates. Furthermore, considering that pediatric thyroid cancer tends to present with higher rates of regional lymph node and distant metastases at diagnosis yet does not generally confer poorer survival, it remains unclear whether such an increase in sensitivity is truly beneficial. Thus, further studies focusing on clinically meaningful outcomes, such as patient prognosis, are warranted.
Another approach is to lower the biopsy cutoff size in pediatric patients with known risk factors for thyroid cancer. Although this strategy is partly incorporated into the 2021 K-TIRADS, which recommends reducing the biopsy threshold for K-TIRADS 4 nodules from 1.5 cm to 1.0 cm in patients at risk of thyroid cancer, data supporting similar approaches in other US risk-stratification systems are still limited. In a simulation study by Lee et al. [ ], the biopsy cut-off sizes for K-TIRADS and ACR TI-RADS category 4 nodules were further reduced to 0.5 cm in patients with risk factors. In the study, the authors found that the specificity of the K-TIRADS decreased slightly from 59% to 53%; however, the sensitivity increased from 78% to 85%, leading to an improvement in accuracy (71% to 74%). Similarly, when the biopsy threshold for ACR TI-RADS category 4 nodules with risk factors was lowered from 1.5 cm to 0.5 cm, specificity decreased from 80% to 65%, while sensitivity increased from 60% to 84%. This change resulted in an overall accuracy improvement from 67% to 77% ( ). Therefore, risk factor-adapted modifications may be a viable option for future refinements of other US risk stratification systems.

## CONSIDERATIONS FOR FUTURE REFINEMENTS OF PEDIATRIC-SPECIFIC ULTRASOUND RISK-STRATIFICATION SYSTEMS

## Overdiagnosis: Pediatric-Specific Clinical Considerations
Overdiagnosis is one of the most critical concerns in thyroid cancer. Although the incidence of thyroid cancer has steadily increased over the past two decades, the survival rates have remained largely unchanged [ ]. This trend suggests that the widespread use of ultrasonography has contributed to overdiagnosis and escalating medical costs. However, the actual clinical impact on patient outcomes remains questionable.
Pediatric thyroid cancer exhibits paradoxical clinical characteristics [ ]. They are usually detected as large, rapidly growing tumors, often accompanied by lymph node and distant metastases, and show higher postoperative recurrence rates than thyroid cancers in adults. Nevertheless, the long-term prognosis in children remains excellent, with a lifespan survival rate exceeding 95%. Indeed, although the Fukushima Health Management Survey, which screened approximately 380,000 children after the nuclear accident, revealed an unexpectedly high prevalence of pediatric thyroid cancer (30/100,000) [ ], most of these cancers remained small throughout the follow-up period. Therefore, careful consideration of overdiagnosis and its associated consequences is warranted in pediatric patients.
To date, modifications to TIRADS for application in pediatric populations have focused on improving diagnostic performance in a balanced manner, not only with increased biopsy cut-off sizes for K-TIRADS 4 or lower nodules to reduce unnecessary biopsies, but also with attempts to lower the biopsy cut-off size for nodules in the highest suspicion category and to incorporate clinical risk factors when determining biopsy thresholds. However, clinical considerations of these modifications should be extended beyond diagnostic performance alone. In pediatric patients, it is also important to comprehensively consider the long-term outcomes related to diagnostic and therapeutic interventions, such as growth and quality of life. Indeed, postthyroidectomy thyroid dysfunction in children may have adverse effects on growth, skeletal maturation, and cognitive development [ ]. Therefore, future modifications to the TIRADS should include further discussions beyond diagnostic performance.

## Heterogeneity Within Pediatric Thyroid Cancer
The cutoff age for pediatric and adult thyroid cancer is currently 18 years, as described in the ATA guidelines [ ]. However, several studies have reported that clinical outcomes for pediatric thyroid cancer vary according to the patient’s age. In particular, thyroid cancers in younger children exhibit more aggressive features than those in older pediatric populations [ ]. Sugino et al. [ ] analyzed 288 patients under 21 years of age with differentiated thyroid cancer and found that patients younger than 15 years had poorer distant metastasis-free survival compared to those 15 years or older, along with a higher prevalence of tumors larger than 4 cm (45.3% vs. 25.5%; P < 0.01), lymph node metastasis (39.6% vs. 18.7%; P < 0.005), and distant metastasis (22.6% vs. 3.8%; P < 0.001). Similarly, Lazar et al. [ ] reported that, compared to adolescents, pre-pubertal thyroid cancer patients had a higher prevalence of a family history (50.0% vs. 17.5%; P = 0.037), extrathyroidal extension (80.0% vs. 35.5%; P = 0.012), lymph node metastasis (100% vs. 47.0%; P = 0.009), and distant metastasis (70.0% vs. 23.5%; P = 0.009). Liu et al. [ ] also reported that thyroid cancers in patients ≤10 years old showed a higher prevalence of extrathyroidal extension (80.0% vs. 51.4%; P = 0.012), lymph node metastasis (94.3% vs. 74.3%; P = 0.022), and distant metastasis (31.4% vs. 11.4%; P = 0.041), as well as a higher recurrence rate (80.0% vs. 20.0%; P = 0.040), compared to patients older than 10 years. In this context, further clarification of the cut-off between pediatric and adult thyroid cancer, as well as the adoption of a stratified US approach tailored to younger and older pediatric age groups, may be considered in future TIRADS.

## Toward Incorporation of Molecular Profiles Into Pediatric TIRADS
These differences in presentation, even among pediatric thyroid cancers, may be genetically driven. Indeed, BRAF mutations, the most common genetic alteration in adult thyroid cancer, have been reported in 40% of patients younger than 15 years and in 75% of those aged 15–24 years (P = 0.008) [ ]. Pediatric thyroid cancers are more frequently driven by oncogenic fusions, including RET, NTRK, or ALK [ ]. Tumors associated with these gene fusions often exhibit more invasive behavior, with higher rates of regional and distant metastasis [ ].
The identification of actionable fusions has significant implications for prognosis and targeted therapy selection, as selective inhibitors such as larotrectinib and selpercatinib have shown tumor responses and restoration of radioiodine uptake in fusion-positive pediatric PTCs [ ]. While the pathogenesis underlying tumor behavior in pediatric thyroid cancers is not yet fully understood, accumulating evidence suggests that comprehending their molecular landscape is essential for elucidating disease heterogeneity and, ultimately, for improving clinical management in pediatric thyroid cancer. Consequently, future modifications of K-TIRADS could consider moving beyond US features alone to incorporate molecular profiles. In this approach, US-based risk stratification would guide the indication for molecular testing, while molecular results could be integrated into post-diagnostic risk assessment and treatment decision making.

## Application of Artificial Intelligence
US evaluation of thyroid nodules is inherently limited by operator dependency, leading to substantial interobserver variability in both image acquisition and interpretation [ ]. To mitigate these limitations, various artificial intelligence (AI)–based systems have been developed for thyroid nodule assessment and classification. Notable commercially available systems include AmCAD-UT (AmCad Biomed, Taipei, Taiwan) and S-Detect™ (Samsung Medison Co. Ltd., Seoul, Korea) [ ].
While many studies have shown that experienced physicians typically outperform AI systems [ ], some research indicates comparable or even superior diagnostic performance from AI [ ], particularly when compared to less-experienced radiologists [ ]. AI-based classification has demonstrated significantly higher sensitivity and negative predictive value compared to less-experienced radiologists, suggesting a potential role in reducing false-negative diagnoses among operators with limited experience in thyroid US.
In the pediatric population, a pilot study evaluating AI-based thyroid nodule assessment has been reported. Ha et al. [ ] applied a deep learning model trained on adult thyroid US data to 156 pediatric thyroid nodules (http://cdss.co.kr). The AI model showed robust performance in differentiating malignant from benign nodules, with areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve ranging from 0.913 to 0.929, sensitivities of 78.7% to 89.4%, and specificities of 79.8% to 91.7%. Notably, the AI model achieved a significantly higher area under the curve than radiologist assessments based on either the ACR TI-RADS or K-TIRADS (AI-thyroid vs. ACR TI-RADS, 0.927 vs. 0.831; P = 0.016; vs. K-TIRADS, 0.927 vs. 0.831; P < 0.001). Importantly, while the sensitivity did not differ significantly among methods, the specificity of the AI model was significantly higher than that of K-TIRADS (79.8% vs. 49.5%; P < 0.001).
However, it should be noted that the previously reported study was limited to cohorts with available histopathologic confirmation and was retrospective in nature, relying solely on previously acquired US images, raising concerns about the generalizability of these results to real-world clinical practice. Moreover, despite advances in AI-based image interpretation, operator dependency during image acquisition remains, and real-time AI-assisted classification systems capable of providing immediate information during US exams are still limited. Overall, these findings suggest that AI-assisted diagnostic systems may play a role in pediatric thyroid nodule US evaluation and could be integrated into thyroid imaging workflows to enhance objectivity and efficiency, although substantial and robust evidence is required as a prerequisite.

## Diagnostic Challenges
The diffuse sclerosing subtype of PTC is a rare histologic subtype, accounting for about 0.5% to 0.8% of all PTCs [ ]. However, it tends to occur at a younger age than conventional PTC, resulting in a relatively higher frequency in pediatric populations. Indeed, a population-based study found that the diffuse sclerosing subtype of PTC comprised 3.1% of pediatric PTCs but only 0.5% of adult PTCs [ ]. Compared to classic PTC, this subtype is associated with a higher incidence of lymph node and distant metastases and poorer survival [ ], highlighting the importance of early detection. On US, it typically does not present as a well-defined mass but rather as diffuse thyroid enlargement with scattered punctate echogenic foci ( ), which can easily be misinterpreted as diffuse thyroid parenchymal disease [ ]. In response to this diagnostic challenge, the 2021 K-TIRADS specifically classifies the diffuse sclerosing subtype of PTC, characterized by extensive parenchymal punctate echogenic foci without discrete nodules, as K-TIRADS 4 [ ]. By contrast, the AACE/ACE/AME guidelines acknowledge it as a diagnostic challenge but do not specify how it should be categorized on ultrasonography [ ], and the ATA guidelines likewise make no mention of US evaluation [ ]. Therefore, future pediatric-specific US guidelines may need to establish clear ultrasonographic criteria for detecting the diffuse sclerosing subtype of PTC.
Another diagnostic challenge concerning pediatric thyroid nodules is intrathyroidal ectopic thymus ( ), a common mimicker of pediatric thyroid nodules. Indeed, Chang et al. [ ] reported that intrathyroidal ectopic thymus was observed in 1% to 3% of pediatric patients. Generally, intrathyroidal ectopic thymus has a smooth margin and appears as a hypoechoic solid nodule with linear or punctate echogenic foci, showing absent or minimal vascularity relative to the surrounding thyroid parenchyma [ ]. Notably, internal punctate echogenic foci may mimic calcifications and should be carefully differentiated. In this regard, the differentiation of intrathyroidal ectopic thymus from a true thyroid nodule can be made by comparing its sonographic appearance with that of a normal mediastinal thymus. Additionally, intrathyroidal ectopic thymus is typically detected at a young age, with the mean age ranging from 8.7 months to 6.3 years, and most cases remain stable or decrease in size on follow-up [ ]. In some cases, the margins become less distinct or the echogenicity may change to hyperechoic. Therefore, when a nodule with punctate echogenic foci is detected in children under 4 years of age—an age group in which thyroid cancer is exceedingly rare [ ]—consideration of ectopic thymus as a differential diagnosis, with follow-up without biopsy, is warranted. Accordingly, future pediatric TIRADS may require clarification regarding the consideration of ectopic thymus in young children.

## CONCLUSION
Pediatric thyroid cancer is rare but shows clinical features distinct from those of adult thyroid cancer, indicating that pediatric thyroid nodules require a specific management strategy. Recent modifications of the K-TIRADS in 2021 include pediatric-specific biopsy cut-offs and risk-adapted considerations that have improved diagnostic accuracy compared with the previous version. Therefore, the 2021 K-TIRADS may be an appropriate framework available at present for the management of pediatric thyroid nodules. However, important challenges remain in the pediatric population, including age-related heterogeneity, pediatric-specific diagnostic mimickers, and the need for further validation and integration of molecular- and AI-based approaches. These challenges need to be addressed in future studies.
