## Background
Larotrectinib is a first-in-class, highly selective tropomyosin receptor kinase (TRK) inhibitor approved for tumour-agnostic use in patients with TRK fusion cancer. Data on treatment-naïve adult and paediatric patients or the subset of treatment-naïve paediatric patients who discontinued larotrectinib after surgery or achieving durable clinical benefit are unknown.

## Materials and methods
Patients with treatment-naïve (no prior systemic therapy) metastatic/unresectable TRK fusion cancer from three larotrectinib clinical trials [NCT02122913, NCT02637687 (SCOUT) and NCT02576431 (NAVIGATE)] were included. Responses were assessed by an independent review committee (RECIST v1.1). SCOUT-enrolled patients could electively discontinue larotrectinib after surgical resection of disease, or ongoing non-surgical complete/partial response (≥1 year) or stable disease (≥2 years) in a ‘wait-and-see’ approach.

## Results
As of 20 July 2023, 101 patients were enrolled, with a median age of 37 years (range 0-90 years). There were 14 different tumour types; the most common were non-infantile fibrosarcoma (IFS) soft tissue sarcoma (30%), IFS (18%), salivary gland carcinoma (18%) and thyroid carcinoma (17%). The overall response rate was 77% [95% confidence interval (CI) 68% to 85%]. Median duration of response, progression-free survival and overall survival were 59 months [95% CI 33 months-not estimable (NE)], 61 months (95% CI 33 months-NE) and not reached, respectively. Twenty-five of 42 SCOUT-enrolled patients entered a ‘wait-and-see’ period; at the data cut-off time, the ‘wait-and-see’ period was ongoing in 12 of these patients. Seven of 13 patients who exited the first ‘wait-and-see’ period had progressive disease and resumed larotrectinib; five of these seven patients had a response to re-treatment. Most treatment-related adverse events were grade 1/2.

## Conclusions
Larotrectinib achieved extremely durable responses, extended survival and had a favourable safety profile in treatment-naïve patients with TRK fusion cancers, supporting its use in this population. Elective discontinuation of larotrectinib may be feasible in selected paediatric patients, with response achievable after restarting larotrectinib in cases of recurrent off-therapy disease.

## Introduction
NTRK1, NTRK2 and NTRK3 encode the tropomyosin receptor kinase (TRK) family proteins TRKA, TRKB and TRKC, respectively. TRK proteins are a family of tyrosine kinase receptors that are vital for normal nervous system development and function. 
NTRK fusions lead to the expression of constitutively active TRK fusion proteins, which have been identified as oncogenic drivers in various cancer types in adult and paediatric patients. 
NTRK fusions occur with differing frequencies, from up to ∼90% in some rare cancers [e.g. secretory carcinomas and infantile fibrosarcoma (IFS)] to <0.5% in more common tumour types (e.g. non-small-cell lung cancer and colorectal carcinoma). 
Larotrectinib is a first-in-class, highly selective, TRK inhibitor. In a combined analysis of three phase I/II trials, larotrectinib achieved a robust objective response rate and durable antitumour efficacy, irrespective of patient age or tumour type. This led to the tumour-agnostic approval of larotrectinib for the treatment of adult and paediatric patients with TRK fusion cancer. , With an updated and larger dataset of 153 assessable adult and paediatric patients with TRK fusion cancer, larotrectinib subsequently demonstrated an objective response rate of 79% [95% confidence interval (CI) 72% to 85%], a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 28 months [95% CI 22 months-not estimable (NE)] and a median overall survival (OS) of 44 months (95% CI 37 months-NE). In these patients, larotrectinib was administered to 22% as first-line therapy and to 78% of patients after previous systemic therapies (data cut-off February 2019). 
To date, only one study investigating larotrectinib as first-line therapy, which was conducted in paediatric patients, has been published, while previous reports have combined data from both treatment-naïve and systemic therapy-pretreated patients. , Treatment-naïve outcomes in adult and paediatric patients are important because they may have less genomically complex tumours, increasing their likelihood of achieving more durable disease control with targeted therapy. , Furthermore, isolating treatment-naïve activity can establish benchmarks for comparing other first-line, disease-specific systemic therapy options. The importance of larotrectinib efficacy in these groups leads to its consideration as a first-line treatment option.
We report the first analysis of the efficacy and safety of larotrectinib in adult and paediatric patients with TRK fusion cancer who have not received prior systemic therapy from the dataset of three clinical trials: NCT02122913 (phase I), SCOUT (NCT02637687; phase I/II) and NAVIGATE (NCT02576431; phase II). In addition, we provide the seminal report of larotrectinib discontinuation outcomes following surgical resection of disease or sustained durable clinical benefit [prolonged response or stable disease (SD)].

## Materials and methods

## Study design
Patients were eligible for inclusion in this analysis if they had not received any prior systemic therapy (excluding surgery and radioiodine) in a metastatic or unresectable setting (patients who had received prior systemic therapy in the localized setting were eligible) and had participated in one of three larotrectinib trials: NCT02122913, a phase I trial in adults ≥18 years of age; SCOUT (NCT02637687), a phase I/II trial involving individuals ≤21 years of age; and NAVIGATE (NCT02576431), a phase II ‘basket’ trial in individuals ≥12 years of age. Details of these studies have been published previously. , 
In the phase I trial and NAVIGATE, eligible patients had a locally advanced or metastatic solid tumour, an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0-3 and adequate major organ function. In the SCOUT trial, eligible patients had a locally advanced or metastatic solid tumour, a Karnofsky or Lansky performance score of at least 50 and adequate major organ function. Patients, except those with IFS, had tumour relapse or progression, or were unresponsive to standard therapies. Patients with IFS who would require, in the opinion of the investigator, mutilating surgery or limb amputation to achieve a complete surgical resection were also eligible. NTRK fusions were detected by local molecular testing in Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments-certified or similarly accredited laboratories. , 
Larotrectinib was administered orally at 100 mg twice daily (b.i.d.) to adult patients ≥18 years of age; one patient received 150 mg b.i.d. in the dose-escalation phase. Most paediatric patients <18 years of age received larotrectinib 100 mg/m2 b.i.d. (up to a maximum dose of 100 mg b.i.d.); one patient received other doses during dose escalation (125-150 mg/m2 b.i.d.). Trial participation was continued until disease progression, withdrawal of the patient or unacceptable toxicity. , 
The SCOUT trial permitted patients to stop larotrectinib in the event of on-study surgical resection [i.e. negative surgical margins (R0)] or ongoing non-surgical complete response (CR), partial response (PR) for at least 1 year or SD for at least 2 years (a ‘wait-and-see’ approach). Patients who stopped treatment remained on trial and were actively followed for disease progression as per protocol. Those whose tumours progressed during this period were allowed to resume larotrectinib. Patients who resumed larotrectinib were permitted to have their treatment held again. Patients from SCOUT who stopped larotrectinib in the absence of on-treatment disease progression were identified and included in a ‘wait-and-see’ analysis.
Protocols were approved by an institutional review board or independent ethics committee at each site, and all the protocols complied with the International Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research Involving Human Subjects, Good Clinical Practice guidelines, the Declaration of Helsinki and local laws. All patients or parents provided written informed consent.

## Study endpoints
The primary endpoint was overall response rate (ORR), measured using RECIST version 1.1 based on assessment by an independent review committee (IRC). ORR was defined as the proportion of patients with confirmed CR [including pathological CR (pCR)] and PR out of the total number of patients who received treatment. Secondary endpoints included: time to response; duration of response (DoR), defined as the time from start of the initial response to the date of disease progression or death; PFS, defined as the time from the first dose of larotrectinib treatment to the earliest date of documented disease progression or death; and OS, defined as the time from the first dose to death of any cause.
The time to first ‘wait-and-see’ period; time to pCR, R0 or microscopic residual tumour (R1) surgery with a start of ‘wait-and-see’ period; and time to remission were assessed in a subset of patients from SCOUT. The time to first ‘wait-and-see’ period was defined as the time from the day of first dose to the start of the first off-drug period on study. The time to remission was defined as the time from the day of first dose to the time point all the following three criteria were met: start of first ‘wait-and-see’ period; first best overall response of radiological CR, pCR or R0 or R1 surgery in the same treatment period that the off-drug period also belongs to; and at least 6 months of follow-up without documented progressive disease (PD).
The occurrence of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) and treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs), including treatment discontinuation, was also assessed.

## Study assessments
Tumour assessments were carried out by IRC using computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging and clinical measurement at baseline and every 8 weeks for 1 year, then every 12 weeks thereafter until disease progression. All tumour responses were confirmed at least 4 weeks after the initial response.
In the ‘wait-and-see’ analysis, the absence of on-treatment disease progression, assessment for progression during ‘wait-and-see’ and response to restart of treatment in patients from SCOUT was determined by investigator assessment.
Adverse events (AEs) were graded as per the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for AEs version 4.03 and assessed from the date informed consent was obtained until at least 28 days after the last dose of larotrectinib was administered.

## Statistical analysis
DoR, PFS, OS, time to first ‘wait-and-see’ period, time to first ‘wait-and-see’ period in patients with pCR, or R0 or R1 surgery and time to remission were estimated using Kaplan–Meier analyses. Confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated using the Clopper–Pearson method.

## Results

## Patients
As of 20 July 2023, 101 patients had initiated larotrectinib treatment in the frontline setting and were included from the three pivotal larotrectinib clinical trials: phase I trial (n = 5; 5%), SCOUT (n = 42; 42%) and NAVIGATE (n = 54; 53%). ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105110). Baseline patient characteristics are shown in , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105110. Fifty-one percent of patients were female with a median age of 37 years (range 0-90 years); 59% of patients were adults and 41% were paediatric patients. There were 14 different tumour types, including 30% non-IFS soft tissue sarcoma (STS); 18% each of IFS and salivary gland carcinoma; 17% thyroid carcinoma; 4% each of breast and colon carcinomas; 2% each of cervix carcinoma and congenital mesoblastic nephroma; and 1% each of bone sarcoma, cholangiocarcinoma, external auditory canal cancer, gastric cancer, lipofibromatosis and lung carcinoma. The median time from initial diagnosis to study enrolment was 1 year (range 0-46 years). Seven percent of patients had received prior systemic therapy (excluding radioiodine) in the adjuvant/neoadjuvant setting. At enrolment, 61% of patients had metastatic disease (51 adult and 11 paediatric patients) and 39% of patients had locally advanced disease (9 adult and 30 paediatric patients).
The most common NTRK fusions identified involved NTRK3 (n = 58; 57%), followed by NTRK1 (n = 38; 38%) and NTRK2 (n = 5; 5%). NTRK fusions were identified locally by a variety of testing methods ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105110). There were 23 different fusions identified, the most common being ETV6::NTRK3 ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105110).

## Efficacy outcomes

## ORR
At the data cut-off, 100 treatment-naïve patients were eligible for analysis by IRC. The ORR for all patients was 77% (95% CI 68% to 85%). Forty-seven percent of patients had CR (including 10 pCR), 30% had PR, 11% had SD, 9% had PD and 3% of patients were not assessable ( and A). 
Treatment response in adult and paediatric patients as well as those with locally advanced and metastatic disease at enrolment is shown in and B and C. The ORR was 68% (95% CI 54% to 79%) for adults, 90% (95% CI 77% to 97%) for paediatric patients, 90% (95% CI 76% to 97%) for patients with locally advanced disease and 69% (95% CI 56% to 80%) for patients with metastatic disease at enrolment.
Treatment response for the most common tumour types is shown in , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105110. The ORR for patients with non-IFS STS (n = 30), IFS (n = 18), salivary gland carcinoma (n = 18) and thyroid carcinoma (n = 17) was 77% (95% CI 58% to 90%), 100% (95% CI 81% to 100%), 89% (95% CI 65% to 99%) and 53% (95% CI 28% to 77%), respectively.
Treatment responses by NTRK gene and NTRK fusion are shown in and , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105110. Seventy-eight percent of patients with NTRK1 and 83% of patients with NTRK3 fusions had an ORR. The ORR for patients with an ETV6::NTRK3 fusion was 88% (95% CI 76% to 95%). None of the five patients with NTRK2 fusions [one each with bone sarcoma, cervix cancer, cholangiocarcinoma, gastric cancer and non-IFS STS (perivascular epithelioid cell tumour)] had a best overall response of CR or PR.

## Treatment duration and time to response
The median time to response was 1.8 months for all patients ( A), including adult and paediatric patients ( B) and those with locally advanced or metastatic disease at enrolment ( C). Treatment duration ranged from 0 to 77+ months for all patients, 0 to 74+ months for adults, 1 to 77+ months for paediatric patients, 2 to 77+ months for patients with locally advanced disease and 0 to 74+ months for patients with metastatic disease at enrolment. At data cut-off, 47 (47%) patients were on treatment ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105110). By the data cut-off, 42 (42%) patients experienced disease progression (investigator assessment). Of these, 26 continued or restarted treatment post-progression: four progressed during the ‘wait-and-see’ period and restarted treatment (a second treatment period), while the other 22 continued despite investigator-assessed PD due to continued clinical benefit. Twelve (29%) paediatric patients and 30 (50%) adults experienced disease progression, with 9 (22%) and 17 (28%) continuing treatment post-progression, respectively. 

## DoR
The median DoR for all treatment-naïve patients was 59 months (95% CI 33 months-NE). The median DoR was 59 months (95% CI 59 months-NE), 38 months (95% CI 26 months-NE), not reached (95% CI 27 months-NE) and 45 months (95% CI 26 months-NE) for adults, paediatric patients and patients with locally advanced and metastatic disease at enrolment, respectively ( A and B). The median DoR for patients with non-IFS STS, IFS, salivary gland carcinoma and thyroid carcinoma was not reached, 38 months (95% CI 23 months-NE), 59 months (95% CI 29 months-NE) and not reached (95% CI 4 months-NE), respectively ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105110). 

## PFS
The median PFS for all treatment-naïve patients was 61 months (95% CI 33 months-NE). The median PFS was 61 months (95% CI 15 months-NE), 46 months (95% CI 32 months-NE), not reached (95% CI 32 months-NE) and 46 months (95% CI 23 months-NE) for adults, paediatric patients and patients with locally advanced and metastatic disease at enrolment, respectively ( C and D). By the data cut-off, 9 of the 13 events (1 event was a death without prior PD) in the paediatric subset happened during the ‘wait-and-see’ period; only four (10%) paediatric patients progressed while on therapy. The median PFS for patients with non-IFS STS, IFS, salivary gland carcinoma and thyroid carcinoma was 32 months (95% CI 10 months-NE), 40 months (95% CI 25 months-NE), 61 months (95% CI 31 months-NE) and not reached (95% CI 2 months-NE), respectively ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105110).

## OS
The median OS for all treatment-naïve patients was not reached; the 5-year OS rate was 76% (95% CI 65% to 86%). The 5-year OS rate was 67% (95% CI 53% to 82%), 88% (95% CI 73% to 100%), 88% (95% CI 71% to 100%) and 67% (95% CI 54% to 81%) for adults, paediatric patients and patients with locally advanced and metastatic disease at enrolment, respectively ( E and F). The 5-year OS rate for patients with non-IFS STS, IFS, salivary gland carcinoma and thyroid carcinoma was 68% (95% CI 46% to 89%), 100% (95% CI 100% to 100%), 71% (95% CI 47% to 96%) and 76% (95% CI 55% to 96%), respectively ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105110).

## ‘Wait-and-see’ analysis
Patients from SCOUT were eligible to stop larotrectinib in the absence of on-treatment disease progression (investigator assessment) for inclusion in a ‘wait-and-see’ analysis where they would be actively followed for progression. Specifically, patients could have elected to discontinue larotrectinib following surgical resection of disease or achieving a sustained non-surgical CR/PR (≥1 year) or SD (≥2 years).
A total of 39 paediatric patients who were treatment naïve were eligible to be included in the ‘wait-and-see’ analysis ( A). The median time to first ‘wait-and-see’ period was 20 months (range 3-57 months) at a median follow-up of 38 months. The median time to pCR, R0 or R1 surgery with a start of ‘wait-and-see’ was not reached (range 4-77 months) at a median follow-up of 52 months. The median time to remission was 48 months (range 4-76 months) at a median follow-up of 39 months ( A). 
Twenty-five (64%) of the 39 paediatric patients entered at least one ‘wait-and-see’ period ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105110). There were five different tumour types: 12 (48%) IFS, 9 (36%) non-IFS STS, 2 (8%) congenital mesoblastic nephroma and 1 (4%) each of breast and cervix carcinomas. Twenty-one (84%) patients had locally advanced disease and four (16%) had metastatic disease.
The best response before or at the time of stopping larotrectinib was 12 CR, 4 pCR, 5 PR (1 of which is pending confirmation) and 4 SD. The median time on treatment before the ‘wait-and-see’ period was 14 months (range 3-42 months) in all patients who had started a ‘wait-and-see’ period. At the time of data cut-off, the ‘wait-and-see’ period was ongoing in 12 (48%) patients. The median duration of the first ‘wait-and-see’ period was 35 months (range 0-55+ months).
Of the 13 (52%) patients who exited the first ‘wait-and-see’ period, 7 had PD (investigator assessment) and resumed treatment ( B). Median time from stopping larotrectinib to resumption of treatment was 3 months (range 1-41 months). Of the seven patients who resumed larotrectinib, five patients had a response to re-treatment (three CR, one PR, one PR pending confirmation) and two had SD. The additional six patients exited the ‘wait-and-see’ period and did not resume treatment: four had no subsequent progression after surgery, one had a prolonged CR, and one entered long-term follow-up. All patients who entered at least one ‘wait-and-see’ period were alive at the last report [24 patients were documented as alive at the data cut-off and 1 patient was alive upon leaving the study in 2020 (due to consent withdrawal) without subsequent data reported].

## Safety
TEAEs that occurred in ≥10% of patients are shown in , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105110. Ninety-nine percent of patients experienced a TEAE and 88% experienced a TRAE. The worst grade 1/2 TEAEs occurred in 40% of patients and the worst grade 1/2 TRAEs occurred in 62% of patients. The worst grade 3/4 TEAEs occurred in 57% of patients, with 26% considered related to larotrectinib. There were two grade 5 TEAEs (respiratory failure and sepsis), neither of which were considered related to larotrectinib. Four percent of patients permanently discontinued treatment due to AEs; three events were considered unrelated to treatment (gait disturbance, respiratory distress and sepsis) and one event was considered treatment-related (hypoventilation). Ten (10%) patients (five adult and five paediatric patients) had a bone fracture event (five grade 1 and five grade 2; , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105110). No fractures were considered treatment-related.

## Discussion
This series of trials was the first to analyse the efficacy and safety of larotrectinib in a combined population of adult and paediatric patients with TRK fusion cancer who had not previously received systemic therapy in a metastatic or unresectable setting. As first-line treatment, larotrectinib demonstrated a high response rate (ORR: 77%) with long durability (median DoR: 59 months, or 4 years and 11 months), and extended survival benefit (5-year OS rate: 76%). While the use of larotrectinib in the first-line setting is specified in certain tumour guidelines (e.g. non-small-cell lung cancer), these results suggest that its first-line use could be considered as a valuable option in a wider variety of tumours. Ideally, genomic testing, for example, next-generation sequencing, should include testing for NTRK fusions, and should be carried out in eligible patients with solid tumours as early as possible in the treatment pathway. This would help ensure that patients are offered the most appropriate treatment for their disease as soon as possible.
Larotrectinib demonstrated greater efficacy in paediatric patients than in adults (ORR: 90% versus 68%; 60-month OS rate: 88% versus 67%). In addition, DoR and PFS estimates in paediatric patients may be conservative, given that 9 of the 13 progression events in the paediatric subset occurred off-drug during the ‘wait-and-see’ period, and potentially impacted the overall DoR and PFS results. Larotrectinib also showed greater efficacy in patients with locally advanced versus metastatic disease at enrolment (ORR: 90% versus 69%; 60-month OS rate: 88% versus 67%) in this analysis. With regard to the most common tumour types, larotrectinib demonstrated greatest efficacy in patients with IFS (ORR: 100%; 60-month OS rate: 100%). Given the strong correlation between young age, diagnosis and absence of metastatic disease in patients with IFS, it is difficult to distinguish which of these features were responsible for the better outcomes. These results, along with the previously reported frontline paediatric study of larotrectinib, suggest that patients should be tested to determine their eligibility for first-line larotrectinib therapy, particularly when conservative tumour resection is not feasible or in life-threatening situations. In patients with IFS, larotrectinib has been associated with a reduced likelihood of initiating new systemic treatment or undergoing subsequent surgery compared with standard-of-care chemotherapy, irrespective of the therapy line, according to a retrospective, observational study ; together, these results further reinforce the use of larotrectinib as a first-line treatment for patients with TRK fusion cancer.
Twenty-five of the 39 paediatric patients from SCOUT who were eligible for the ‘wait-and-see’ analysis entered at least one ‘wait-and-see’ period; at the time of data cut-off, the ‘wait-and-see’ period was ongoing in 12 of these patients. Just over half of the paediatric patients who exited the first ‘wait-and-see’ period had disease progression. However, five out of seven of these patients were able to achieve a response when larotrectinib was resumed. All patients who entered at least one ‘wait-and-see’ period were presumed alive at the time of data cut-off. This suggests that elective discontinuation of larotrectinib in the first-line treatment setting may be feasible in selected paediatric patients. However, more information on the optimal treatment duration is required. In addition, it is important to note that this approach has not been studied for cancers in adults who may have a greater likelihood of relapse after stopping treatment.
Larotrectinib was well tolerated and had a favourable safety profile with no new or unexpected safety findings observed. TRAEs were mainly grade 1/2, and only one patient discontinued larotrectinib due to a TRAE (hypoventilation). While no randomized controlled data on larotrectinib exist, these results further reinforce its robust efficacy and favourable safety profile, as observed in earlier pooled analyses of the three single-arm trials, , and in the first-line setting in a single-arm, open-label phase II trial conducted in paediatric patients with IFS or other solid tumours. In addition, larotrectinib showed greater efficacy versus non-TRK inhibitor standard-of-care therapies observed in a real-world study of patients with various TRK fusion cancers.

## Data sharing
Availability of the data underlying this publication will be determined according to Bayer’s commitment to the EFPIA/PhRMA “Principles for responsible clinical trial data sharing”. This pertains to scope, time point and process of data access.
As such, Bayer commits to sharing upon request from qualified scientific and medical researchers patient-level clinical trial data, study-level clinical trial data and protocols from clinical trials in patients for medicines and indications approved in the United States and European Union (EU) as necessary for conducting legitimate research. This applies to data on new medicines and indications that have been approved by the EU and US regulatory agencies on or after 1 January 2014.
Interested researchers can use www.vivli.org to request access to anonymized patient-level data and supporting documents from clinical studies to conduct further research that can help advance medical science or improve patient care. Information on the Bayer criteria for listing studies and other relevant information is provided in the member section of the portal. Data access will be granted to anonymized patient-level data, protocols and clinical study reports after approval by an independent scientific review panel. Bayer is not involved in the decisions made by the independent review panel. Bayer will take all necessary measures to ensure that patient privacy is safeguarded.
