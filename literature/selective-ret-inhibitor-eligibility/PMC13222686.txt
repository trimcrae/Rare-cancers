## Abstract
We herein report a case of successful rechallenge with tepotinib after tepotinib-induced adverse events following first-line treatment failure with pembrolizumab in a patient with non-small cell lung carcinoma harboring mesenchymal-epithelial transition factor exon 14 skipping mutations. Initial tepotinib administration was suspended because of hepatotoxicity and hypersensitivity. However, the re-administration of tepotinib after a prolonged interval from pembrolizumab administration achieved sustained disease control. The patient continued tepotinib treatment for approximately two years after the first administration. Therefore, the treatment sequence should be considered based on comprehensive multigene testing results to achieve better therapeutic benefits. 
Keywords: tepotinib, non-small cell lung carcinoma, immune checkpoint inhibitor, MET exon 14 skipping mutations, immune-related adverse event

## Introduction
Mesenchymal-epithelial transition factor (MET) is a tyrosine kinase receptor expressed on the surface of various epithelial cells. Its ligand is the hepatocyte growth factor/scatter factor, which regulates cell proliferation, survival, and migration ( ). MET exon 14 skipping mutations, gene amplification, and protein overexpression may lead to tumorigenesis by activating MET-mediated signal transduction ( ). MET exon 14 skipping mutations have been identified in approximately 3% of patients with non-small cell lung carcinoma (NSCLC) ( , ). Recently, tepotinib and capmatinib, MET-tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), have been approved for treating NSCLC patients harboring MET exon 14 skipping mutations ( , ). Regarding adverse events, increases in alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and/or aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels were reported in 12.2% of patients in the VISION trial. Treatment interruption was reported in 3.5% of patients ( ). The National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines recommend MET-TKIs as first-line therapy for patients with NSCLC harboring a MET exon 14 skipping mutation ( ). In Japan, a nationwide registry study reported that multigene testing was performed in 47.7% of the patients with advanced or recurrent NSCLC ( ). Given that not all patients undergo multigene testing, some with oncogenic driver mutations may not receive targeted therapy and may instead be treated with chemotherapy, including immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), as the initial therapy.
Administering TKIs following ICI may increase the risk of adverse events ( ). For instance, hepatotoxicity associated with capmatinib after ICI therapy has been reported ( ). Thus, the treatment sequence in clinical practice is essential.
We herein report a case of a successful rechallenge with tepotinib after tepotinib-induced adverse events following first-line treatment failure with pembrolizumab, an anti-programmed death-1 monoclonal antibody, in a patient with NSCLC harboring MET exon 14 skipping mutations.

## Case Report
A 61-year-old Japanese man presented to another hospital with a productive cough. Further investigations led to a diagnosis of lung adenocarcinoma Stage IIIC (cT3N3M0). Genetic tests were negative for epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), and c-ros oncogene 1 (ROS1). Immunohistochemistry using the 22C-3 antibody demonstrated high programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression, with a tumor proportion score of 90%. Subsequently, the patient was referred to our hospital for lung cancer treatment. The patient was an ex-smoker (23 packs/year), drank alcoholic beverages occasionally, had a history of dyslipidemia, and was prescribed oral statins. The Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status score was 0. Pembrolizumab was initiated as the first-line treatment.
After three cycles of pembrolizumab, chest radiography and enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed an enlargement of the primary lesion and increased right pleural effusion ( ). These CT findings met the criteria for progressive disease based on the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) guidelines (version 1.1). The subsequent test for MET exon 14 skipping mutations, conducted using an ArcherMET instrument (Archer DX, Boulder, USA) at another hospital, was positive. Considering the recent administration of ICI, platinum doublet chemotherapy was administered to the patient as second-line treatment. However, the patient preferred tepotinib.
 Therefore, we conducted follow-up observations using chest radiography and CT to extend the interval from the previous pembrolizumab treatment. CT three months after the final administration of pembrolizumab revealed disease progression, including worsening pulmonary lymphangitic carcinomatosis. Therefore, the patient was treated with tepotinib (500 mg daily) as a second-line chemotherapy. presents laboratory data before tepotinib administration. After seven days of treatment, the patient's AST, ALT, and γ-glutamyl transpeptidase (γ-GTP) levels were elevated and classified as grade 1 based on the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0. Therefore, tepotinib was suspended and ursodeoxycholic acid was initiated. Consequently, the levels of these liver enzymes spontaneously decreased.
 Twenty-three days after suspending tepotinib, the AST, ALT, and γ-GTP levels improved to CTCAE Grade 0. The patient was re-administered tepotinib; however, two days later, he developed a 38°C fever, rash, and fatigue. As a result, tepotinib was re-administered. Laboratory tests revealed an elevated eosinophil count (339/μL), and chest radiography revealed tumor shrinkage and decreased right pleural effusion ( ). Therefore, we decided to continue treatment with a tepotinib suspension.
 Approximately three months after the second tepotinib suspension, chest radiography revealed disease progression, including an enlargement of the primary lesion and increased right pleural effusion ( ). The patient developed dry cough. Therefore, we presented the patient with two treatment options: platinum-based combination chemotherapy or the re-administration of tepotinib, which the patient selected.
Tepotinib was re-initiated six months after the last pembrolizumab administration. After re-administration, no elevated liver enzymes were observed while the patient was taking ursodeoxycholic acid. However, CTCAE Grade 1 generalized edema was noted, as was an increased serum creatinine level (grade 1), and the cystatin C level remained within the normal range. The patient also experienced two episodes of Grade 1 pneumonitis during treatment, both of which resolved with temporary drug suspension; re-administration was performed at the same dose.
 details the patient's clinical course. Contrast-enhanced CT showed primary lesion shrinkage, improved lymphangitic carcinomatosis, and reduced lymph node metastases ( ). These CT findings met the criteria for a partial response based on the RECIST guidelines. The patient continued taking tepotinib for approximately two years after the first administration.
 

## Discussion
Pembrolizumab has shown significant efficacy as a first-line treatment for NSCLC patients with PD-L1 expression ≥50% compared to chemotherapy ( ). However, this trial excluded patients with sensitizing EGFR mutations or ALK translocations. Therefore, the effects of pembrolizumab on patients with NSCLC, PD-L1 levels ≥50%, and rare driver mutations, including a MET exon 14 skipping mutation, remain unknown. Furthermore, a cohort study of patients with NSCLC and MET exon 14 skipping mutations reported that patient responses to ICI were not consistently associated with either the PD-L1 expression or tumor mutational burden ( ). Moreover, the objective response rate to ICI therapy is 17% ( ). In the VISION trial, the objective response rate to tepotinib was 57.4% in untreated patients and 45% in previously treated patients ( ). MET-TKIs are recommended as first-line therapy; however, in this case, the MET-exon 14 skipping mutation was not examined at the initial hospital. Hence, pembrolizumab was administered as the first-line treatment.
In patients with NSCLC, the use of molecular inhibitors following or in combination with ICIs is potentially associated with a risk of severe toxicity ( ). Several reports have described adverse events associated with these agents following ICI treatment, including hepatotoxicity with sotorasib (a KRASG12C inhibitor), capmatinib, crizotinib (an ALK/ROS1/MET inhibitor), osimertinib (an EGFR-TKI), hypersensitivity reactions to selpercatinib (a RET kinase inhibitor), and pneumonitis with osimertinib ( , - ). Switching to an alternative TKI may be an option if adverse events occur ( , ). However, in this case, the interval between the cessation of pembrolizumab and the initiation of tepotinib may have influenced the occurrence of adverse events, as extending the interval from the last pembrolizumab administration resulted in a successful rechallenge with tepotinib. The risk of grade 3 or higher hepatotoxicity increases in patients who receive sotorasib within 90 days of ICI treatment ( ). One possible explanation is that nivolumab remains bound to T cells for more than 20 weeks after the final administration ( ).
In this case, the patient continued tepotinib treatment for approximately two years after the initial tepotinib administration. This duration exceeds the median progression-free survival of 11.2 months reported in the long-term follow-up of the VISION trial ( ).
TKIs play a pivotal role in the treatment of NSCLC. Therefore, comprehensive multigene testing before initiating first-line therapy is crucial. Based on these results, an appropriate treatment sequence should be carefully planned to ensure that patients receive the maximum therapeutic benefit.
