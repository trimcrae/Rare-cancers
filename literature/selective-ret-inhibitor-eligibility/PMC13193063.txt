## Abstract
Multiple endocrine neoplasia (MEN) syndromes constitute a heterogeneous group of hereditary disorders predisposing to endocrine tumours of varying malignant potential. Recent advances in molecular genetics and tumour biology have significantly reshaped our understanding of underlying pathophysiology, enabling earlier diagnosis, genotype-specific surveillance and the emergence of targeted therapies. This review provides a contemporary overview of key recent advances and consensus guidelines across MEN syndromes, including MEN1, MEN2, MEN4, and MEN5 syndromes, SDHx syndrome, Carney complex, McCune–Albright syndrome, DICER1 syndrome and von Hippel–Lindau syndrome. These insights reflect a rapidly evolving field with genetic diagnosis at the centre of prognostic assessment, targeted surveillance and individualised management.
Keywords: neuroendocrine tumours, molecular genetics, multiple neuroendocrine neoplasia

## Introduction
Endocrine neoplasms are a heterogeneous group of tumours that occur either sporadically or as a part of a multiple endocrine neoplasia (MEN) syndrome, with a potential for hormone production ( ). Endocrine neoplasms are associated with germline genetic variants to a variable degree, approximating 30% in the case of phaeochromocytoma and paraganglioma (PPGL) ( ). In the MEN setting, causative variants are found on next-generation sequencing gene panel testing in 17.4% of affected individuals and families ( ). Implicated genes include genes associated with MEN syndromes (MEN1, RET, CDKN1B, MAX, PRKAR1A, SDHx), isolated endocrine neoplasia syndromes (AIP, ARMC5, CDC73, CASR, GCM2), neurocutaneous syndromes (NF1, TSC1, TSC2) and those including a significantly increased risk of malignancy (DICER1, APC, TP53, VHL, PTEN) ( ). More recently, particularly in the case of RET, these genetic drivers have presented potential therapeutic targets ( ). Genetic testing and identification of underlying associated disease is critical to prompt diagnosis and institution of patient-centred personalised management tailored to both the gene and, in some cases, the specific variant risk ( ); there is an added complexity from an individual potentially having multiple concurrent hormone-secreting tumours. In this review, we use the term ‘multiple endocrine neoplasia syndromes’ in a broader clinical sense to encompass not only the archetypal MEN1-5 syndromes but also related conditions characterised by predisposition to multiple endocrine tumours, in addition to non-endocrine manifestations (see ); these overlapping phenotypes reflect a shared underlying paradigm of heritable tumour susceptibility and need for coordinated guideline-directed surveillance. This framework has been adopted to provide a clinically relevant synthesis of select recent advancements for practitioners managing patients with endocrine neoplasia. Conditions associated with a predisposition to a single endocrine tumour type (including familial isolated pituitary adenoma syndrome, hyperparathyroidism-jaw tumour syndrome and bilateral macronodular adrenal hyperplasia) have been considered beyond the scope of this review, as their clinical management is typically organ-specific and does not require the same multisystem surveillance framework that underpins MEN1-5 and other MEN syndromes as defined herein. Similarly, while several of these genes (including NF1, TSC1/2, TP53 and PTEN) are associated with endocrine tumours, they are typically part of broader tumour predisposition syndromes in which endocrine manifestations form only a minor component of a wider clinical phenotype; consequently, a detailed discussion of these conditions falls outside the intended scope of this review. PPGL syndromes will be covered given their frequent adrenal (phaeochromocytoma) and multifocal extra-adrenal (paragangliomas) involvement, which complicates the assessment and management of these syndromes.
 Surveillance guidelines across multiple endocrine neoplasia syndromes are inherently heterogeneous, reflecting differences in disease penetrance, tumour spectrum and the strength of available evidence. While well-established, consensus-driven guidelines exist for conditions such as MEN1 and MEN2, there is a distinct lack of international consensus opinion for MEN4 and MEN5, with practice often extrapolated from MEN1 syndrome. As a result, surveillance approaches must often be individualised, balancing established guidelines with emerging evidence and clinical judgement. This variability underpins the differences in detail and strength of recommendations presented in .
 

## Methods
We performed a critical narrative review of English-language, PubMed-indexed publications from the preceding 5 years to November 2025. Searches were conducted using combinations of terms including ‘multiple endocrine neoplasia’, ‘MEN1’, ‘MEN2’, ‘MEN4’, ‘SDHx’, ‘Carney complex’, ‘VHL’ and ‘DICER1’ alongside terms related to ‘genotype-phenotype correlation’, ‘screening’, ‘surveillance’ and ‘management’. Additional relevant articles were identified through reference list screening and targeted searches. Priority was given to recent consensus guidelines, international recommendations and high-impact original research articles. Earlier landmark studies were included where relevant to provide appropriate historical context. Articles were selected based on relevance to the scope of this review.

## MEN1 syndrome
Inactivating variants of the MEN1 gene (chromosome 11q13; encoding menin) produce MEN1 syndrome with a prevalence of 3–20 per 100,000 persons ( , , ). This autosomal dominant, variably expressive condition is principally characterised by primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT; notably a biochemical diagnosis reflective of either parathyroid adenoma/s or multigland hyperplasia), duodenopancreatic NET and pituitary adenoma (also referred to as pituitary neuroendocrine tumour/PitNET) ( ). Affected individuals may develop additional endocrine (e.g. adrenocortical tumour, thymic NET, bronchopulmonary NET, rarely parathyroid carcinoma) and non-endocrine (e.g. angiofibroma, meningioma) tumours ( ). Breast cancer, melanoma, meningiomas and ependymomas have been reported at higher incidence in individuals with MEN1 syndrome as compared with the general population; however, there is insufficient evidence to establish causality or to consider these tumours as components of MEN1 syndrome ( ). Age-related penetrance for any clinical MEN1 manifestation is low under the age of 10 years and increases steadily to reach >95% by 70 years ( ).
With respect to surveillance, an international Delphi consensus statement on MEN1 syndrome management published in 2025 by Brandi et al. offers up-to-date guidance on assessment and management. Recommended tumour surveillance involves clinical assessment, biochemical monitoring of calcium and select hormones and imaging (see ). Notably, screening for chromogranin A, pancreatic polypeptide, glucagon and vasoactive intestinal peptide is no longer recommended in asymptomatic individuals with negative abdominal imaging, nor is insulin and glucose screening in those without symptoms of hypoglycaemia; this guideline shift reflects a goal of identifying clinically relevant disease while minimising cost and false-positive results ( ). Pancreatic NET ≤ 2 cm may undergo active surveillance with repeat imaging every 1–2 years, provided tumour dimensions remain stable (<1 mm growth/year); evidence to date shows no difference in metastatic disease or mortality rates for non-functioning pancreatic NET ≤ 2 cm when comparing surgery with active surveillance ( ). Occurrence of MEN1-associated disease and survival outcomes for MEN1-associated duodenopancreatic NET do not differ between male and female sex; thus, surveillance is not gender-specific ( ).
Regarding management, the criteria for intervention in MEN1-related PHPT are identical to sporadic PHPT, but the intervention differs, with subtotal parathyroidectomy combined with transcervical thymectomy considered standard of care in most MEN1-associated cases ( ). Compared to unilateral clearance or less, this approach is associated with a lower risk of disease persistence and recurrence, but a higher risk of iatrogenic chronic hypoparathyroidism. Nevertheless, unilateral clearance is sometimes considered in particularly young individuals ( ). With regard to pituitary adenomas, management should follow principles as for sporadic cases, particularly as recent data indicate that prolactinomas – the most frequent pituitary adenoma subtype in MEN1 syndrome – respond well to dopamine agonist treatment regardless of MEN1 status ( ). Of all MEN1-associated pituitary microadenomas, microprolactinomas are the most likely to progress to invasive pituitary macroadenoma, though the risk remains low at 7.2% over eight years, supporting long-term infrequent – rather than short-term intensive – surveillance ( ). Up to 80% of individuals with MEN1 syndrome develop duodenopancreatic NET; when small (<2 cm), stable and non-functioning, duodenopancreatic NETs can be radiologically monitored ( ). Somatostatin receptor scintigraphy (e.g. 68Ga-DOTATATE PET) is recommended in both the work-up of suspected gastrinoma and the staging prior to duodenopancreatic surgery to exclude multifocal or distant disease ( ). While surgical intervention is the only possible avenue for cure from duodenopancreatic NET, many are multifocal and up to 40% are metastatic at the time of diagnosis ( ). Previous trials have shown improved progression-free survival of well-differentiated MEN1-associated duodenopancreatic NETs with somatostatin receptor antagonists (lanreotide and octreotide), tyrosine kinase inhibitors (sunitinib) and mTOR inhibitors (everolimus) ( ). Specifically considering MEN1 syndrome-associated pancreatic NET <2 cm, lanreotide has been proven to significantly improve median progression-free survival as compared with active surveillance (median not reached vs 40 months, respectively) ( ).

## Recent advances
The variable expressivity of MEN1 syndrome has been found to relate at least in part to altered methylation. Recent protein studies of the large Tasman-1 pedigree associated with the MEN1 c.446-3 C>G variant in Tasmania, Australia, demonstrated significantly increased promoter hypermethylation of CpG sites of parathyroid tissue from affected relatives compared with sporadic PHPT, and showed that methylation score increased with relative severity of PHPT amongst affected Tasman-1 relatives ( ).
Some studies have shown the benefit of 18F-FDG-PET imaging in risk-stratifying MEN1-associated pancreatic NET, with increased avidity associated with increased Ki-67% (≥5%) and other aggressive features suggestive of increased malignant potential ( ).
Studies into the possible utility of quantitative PCR of circulating NET mRNA have shown the ability to discriminate between MEN1-affected individuals with pancreatic NET and those without associated manifestation; however, there remain no gene expression models or biomarkers able to reliably predict pancreatic NET development, progression or metastasis ( ). There are currently no established genotype-phenotype correlations for MEN1 variants; thus, the intensive screening program is considered for all asymptomatic individuals from the age of 10 years, with initial endocrinology review recommended from the age of 5 years, although the burden of screening should be considered on a case-by-case basis ( ). Individuals with MEN1 syndrome return lower health-related quality-of-life scores, worsened in individuals aware of their pancreatic NET or pituitary adenoma diagnosis, requiring consideration in the context of screening ( ). Health-related quality-of-life scores are improved with referral to a dedicated centre and patient association support groups ( ).
Peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT) involves administration of a β-emitting radionuclide (90Y or 177Lu) attached to a chelator (e.g. DOTA) and an SSTR agonist (e.g. TOC-Tyr3 octreotide), which binds to SSTRs present on the tumour cell membrane, causing cell death ( ). PRRT has demonstrated improved progression-free survival in well-differentiated but locally advanced or metastatic SSTR-positive duodenopancreatic NET in both the MEN1 and sporadic setting ( ). Further theranostic investigation is underway into the possibility of SSTR antagonists, e.g. 177Lu-DOTA-JR11 ( ). MEN1-associated duodenopancreatic NET show upregulation of c-MET (hepatocyte growth factor receptor) and subsequent tumour growth, paving the way for targeted therapies ( ). Trials of c-MET inhibitors, e.g. cabozantinib, in pancreatic NETs have shown partial response in 15% and stable disease in 75%, with a median progression-free survival 21.8 months, although there are yet to be dedicated studies for those involving MEN1 syndrome ( ).

## MEN2 syndrome
Missense activating variants in the RET proto-oncogene (chromosome 10q11.2; encoding the transmembrane receptor tyrosine kinase protein, RET) produce MEN2 syndrome with a prevalence of 1–10/100,000 people ( , ). MEN2 syndrome is an autosomal dominant condition characterised by medullary thyroid cancer (MTC) and phaeochromocytoma, as well as parathyroid tumours in MEN2A but not MEN2B ( ). Across MEN2A and MEN2B, MTC is the most frequent manifestation, with near 100% penetrance ( ). Phaeochromocytoma occurs in 20–50% of patients with MEN2 syndrome, with a propensity for bilateral disease (70%) and younger age at diagnosis (median age: 32 years) ( ). The most common MEN2A variants, which involve codon 634, correlate with the American Thyroid Association’s (ATA) high-risk factor for MTC, with recommendations for total thyroidectomy at or before the age of 5 years ( ). In addition to primary hyperparathyroidism (10–20%), MEN2A syndrome may also be associated with Hirschsprung’s disease (exon 10 variants only) and cutaneous lichen amyloidosis ( ). MEN2B syndrome, typically due to germline RET c.2753T>C (p.Met918Thr) variants, accounts for 5% of MEN2 cases and results in highly aggressive presentations of MTC; other manifestations include ganglioneuromas of the respiratory and gastrointestinal tract, ophthalmologic abnormalities and marfanoid habitus ( , ). RET p.Met918Thr correlates with the highest risk category or ATA HST, wherein total thyroidectomy is recommended within the first year of life ( ). More than 80% of RET M918T-affected individuals will have de novo RET M918T variants, precluding the possibility of early diagnosis through familial genetic testing ( ).
Regarding management, appropriate genotype-based risk stratification and tailored surveillance protocols, dictating in particular recommended age for total thyroidectomy, are associated with a less aggressive MTC including less lymph node involvement, and reduced recurrence rates ( ). Furthermore, a wealth of research has demonstrated clear genotype-phenotype correlations for MTC that have permitted the ATA variant risk stratification system to guide surveillance and thyroidectomy timing ( ). Provided basal calcitonin levels remain within normal limits, total thyroidectomy offers MTC prophylaxis and can spare RET carriers from overtreatment, e.g. central node dissection ( ). Regarding phaeochromocytoma, regular screening from a young age with supine plasma metanephrines, which offers sensitivity of 96–100% and specificity of 87–92%, is essential ( ). Annual parathyroid hormone and serum calcium concentration are recommended for those with MEN2A syndrome ( ).

## Recent advances
In 2022, a set of RET-specific modifications to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) variant classification framework was proposed, allowing significant variant classification changes in 15.7% of original variants within the MEN2 RET proto-oncogene database; 46.2% were changed from variant of uncertain significance to benign or likely benign ( ). A potential parent-of-origin effect has recently begun to emerge within MEN2A, with offspring of affected fathers more likely to have nodal metastases (45 vs 19%), bilateral phaeochromocytoma (24 vs 10%) and PHPT (13 vs 4%) as compared with offspring of affected mothers ( ).
A recent large cohort study of 506,544 unrelated individuals from the United Kingdom Biobank and the Geisinger MyCode cohort in the United States including a total of 246 individuals with incidental pathogenic RET variants, mostly of the moderate-risk category of ATA, found a markedly lower cancer risk than those with clinically ascertained RET variants ( ). The authors found that despite only <2% of the incidental pathogenic RET cases having undergone thyroidectomy, all-cause mortality at the age of 75 years was not statistically different from non-carriers; this has called into question the appropriateness of ubiquitous recommendation for total thyroidectomy in asymptomatic individuals, though screening remains paramount ( ).
Selpercatinib is an ATP-competitive and highly selective inhibitor of RET-altered kinases ( ). The LIBRETTO trials have demonstrated clinical efficacy of selpercatinib in the treatment of advanced MTC, predominantly of sporadic cases but inclusive of individuals with MEN2 syndrome, with an objective response rate of 81.0% and ongoing response at 2 years in 84% in those treatment naïve; where multikinase inhibitors had previously been used, objective response rate was 77.3%, and 65% saw ongoing response at two years ( ). Furthermore, in LIBRETTO-531 involving individuals with advanced treatment-naïve germline or somatic RET-variant MTC, selpercatinib has demonstrated improved progression-free survival at 24 months of 76.4% compared with only 37.2% in those treated with either cabozantinib or vandetanib, associated with a hazard ratio for disease progression or death of 0.28; fewer adverse effects were seen with selpercatinib ( ). In the ARROW trial of locally advanced or metastatic germline or somatic RET-variant thyroid cancers, pralsetinib showed an objective response rate of 71.6% in treatment-naïve individuals, and 52.2% in those previously treated with either cabozantinib or vandetanib; the progression-free survival rate was 79.4% at 18 months ( ).
Radionuclide therapy with somatostatin analogues (e.g. DOTATATE) radiolabelled with lutetium, yttrium or indium has a growing evidence base for treatment of metastatic PPGL, including MEN2 patients, with partial response rates of 5–23% seen in small series with 177Lu-DOTATATE ( ). Pembrolizumab immunotherapy has been trialled, but objective response rates were low at 0–9% ( ). There have been recently published case reports of selpercatinib demonstrating efficacy in RET-variant phaeochromocytoma; a series of six cases showed partial or complete response in four individuals with stable disease in two individuals ( ).

## MEN4 syndrome
Inactivating variants of the CDKN1B gene (chromosome 12p13; encoding cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor p27Kip1, a nuclear tumour suppressor protein) cause the autosomal dominant MEN4 syndrome ( ). The exact prevalence of MEN4 syndrome is unknown, but pathogenic CDKN1B variants are identified in 0.07% of those with clinical MEN1 syndrome ( ). Often considered the MEN1 mimicker, MEN4 syndrome has similar cardinal features of PHPT, pituitary adenoma and duodenopancreatic neuroendocrine tumours ( ). Approximately 25% of MEN4 patients develop a pituitary adenoma, predominantly non-functioning pituitary adenoma followed by somatotrophinomas and corticotrophinomas; in contrast to MEN1 syndrome, prolactinomas are rare ( ). Duodenopancreatic NETs occur in up to 20% of MEN4 patients ( ). The limited cases to date indicate a broad, heterogeneous phenotype, including ectopic Cushing’s syndrome secondary to atypical thymic carcinoid tumour in a patient with a germline pathogenic CDKN1B (c.374_375delCT, p.Ser125*) variant in combination with a somatic pathogenic MEN1 (c.974C>T, p.Pro325Leu) variant ( ). Other manifestations include adrenocortical tumours (4–7%), carcinoid tumours (4–7%), and papillary thyroid carcinoma (8%). Unlike MEN1 syndrome, there are no known cutaneous manifestations ( ). Regarding surveillance, there are no MEN4-specific surveillance guidelines; thus, MEN1 surveillance is typically adopted, though this may not be appropriate given the relatively more indolent course ( ). Experts have alternatively proposed biennial screening for pituitary adenoma from the age of 10 years, PHPT from the age of 15 years and duodenopancreatic NET from the age of 30 years ( ).

## Recent advances
MEN4 syndrome is of very low prevalence, with a recent study of genetic screening for MEN-associated disease identifying class 4 or 5 CDKN1B variants per ACMG guidelines in just 0.07% compared to 6.8% found to have class 4 or 5 MEN1 variants ( ). In a 2023 systematic review, 65 cases of clinically significant CDKN1B variants have been identified, of which over 70% had symptomatic disease ( ). There has been recent identification of genotype-phenotype correlations, with indels (small insertions and deletions) appearing to confer a higher risk of PHPT as compared with missense variants ( ). Variants at codons 94–96, thought to be a critical region for protein structure and function, appear to impart a higher risk of both PHPT and pituitary adenoma, though not of duodenopancreatic NET ( ). There appears to be a founder effect in the pathogenic variant CDKN1B c.320del (p.Gln107Argfs12*), which has been found to be more prevalent within Ashkenazi Jewish populations ( ).
Recent data show emerging differences between the phenotypes of MEN4 and MEN1. In a recent study by Chevalier and colleagues, 33 individuals with CDKN1B variants were compared against 66 matched individuals with MEN1 variants, showing similar rates of both diagnosis and age at diagnosis of pituitary adenoma and adrenal adrenocortical tumour ( ). However, while PHPT occurred at similar rates, mean age at diagnosis was significantly older in those with CDKN1B compared with MEN1 variants at 51 vs 43 years, respectively; none occurred in individuals aged less than 20 years with CDKN1B variants ( ). Furthermore, class 4/5 CDKN1B variants were associated with duodenopancreatic NETs occurring less frequently (30.3 vs 62%) and at an older age (age 55.5 vs 42.6) when compared to those with class 4/5 MEN1 variants ( ). A literature review of 29 cases of CDKN1B variants found that 86% developed PHPT at a median age of 47 years, with most correlating histologically to parathyroid adenomas, followed by hyperplasia and atypical adenoma ( ). The tendency towards later age at clinical manifestation has raised concern among some that patients with MEN4 syndrome may be missed by some genetic testing guidelines ( ).

## MEN5 syndrome
Inactivating variants of the MAX gene (chromosome 14q23.3; encoding MAX, a 160-amino acid transcription factor) produce MEN5 syndrome, designated by Seabrook et al. in 2021, following the association of MAX variants with PPGL since 2011 ( ). Inactivating MAX variants prevent normal activation of the MYC-MAX heterodimer, leading to unregulated MYC function and subsequent cell proliferation, differentiation and angiogenesis ( ); it is thought that the increased MYC activity leads to the development of a more aggressive subtype of neural crest chromaffin cells ( ).
Clinical data to date suggest that MEN5 syndrome is characterised predominantly by a high penetrance of phaeochromocytoma and paraganglioma ( ). A series of 109 patients from 87 families found that 92.7% developed phaeochromocytoma, of which 58.4% were bilateral and 18.8% were metastatic; mean age at diagnosis was 32.8 ± 12.6 years and other tumours often co-existed, including paraganglioma (10.9%), functioning pituitary adenoma (8.3%) or other neuroendocrine tumour (10.9%) ( ). Screening with supine plasma metanephrines is essential, with the catecholamine phenotype generally being mixed noradrenaline and adrenaline secretion ( ). The exact prevalence of MEN5 syndrome is unknown, but it is thought to account for 0.8–1.9% of PPGL ( ).
Both loss of immunohistochemical staining for MAX and loss of heterozygosity have been identified in MAX-related phaeochromocytoma; thus, such tests may be pursued in clinical settings suspicious for a causative MAX variant, e.g. in the case of a germline MAX variant of uncertain significance ( , ). Critically, there are no formal guidelines regarding comprehensive MEN5 surveillance; however, an international consensus statement regarding childhood PPGL management recommended that surveillance for those with a known pathogenic germline MAX variant should include annual clinical assessment from the age of 10 years, and annual plasma metanephrines and 2–3 yearly MRI neck to pelvis from presentation or from the age of 15 years ( ).

## Recent advances
Inactivating variants of the MAX gene have recently been implicated in pituitary tumourigenesis; in a patient with a pathogenic MAX variant, c.97C>T (p.Arg33*), and bilateral asynchronous phaeochromocytoma, a prolactin/growth hormone co-secreting pituitary adenoma demonstrated loss of MAX expression on immunohistochemistry, consistent with tumoural loss of heterozygosity ( ). Pituitary adenomas associated with MAX variants tend to be functioning, typically of PIT1 lineage with evidence of growth hormone and/or prolactin secretion ( , ). Pituitary gigantism has recently been documented to occur, identified in a case with a novel variant MAX c.228delG (p.Asn78Thrfs*92) ( ). MAX-associated lactotroph adenomas tend to respond well to dopamine agonist therapy, whereas MAX-associated somatotrophinoma tends to require multiple lines of therapy ( ).
The broader phenotype of MAX variants has been hypothesised to additionally comprise other neural crest cell-lineage diseases, including ganglioneuromas, neuroblastomas, parathyroid adenomas, chondrosarcoma, myelolipoma and lung adenocarcinoma ( , ). While baseline assessment for neural crest-derived disease has been suggested, the optimal frequency and extent of ongoing surveillance for these specific tumours remain to be defined ( ).

## SDHx-related syndromes
Inactivating variants in the succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) genes (SDHA, SDHB, SDHC, SDHD and SDHAF2; collectively known as SDHx) are associated with the ‘3P (pituitary adenoma, PPGL) association syndrome’; other SDHx-associated tumours are renal cell carcinoma and gastrointestinal stromal tumour ( ). Germline SDHx variants account for 20% of all PPGL cases, which in turn have an annual incidence of 2–8 cases per million people ( , ).
Biallelic inactivation of any SDHx gene results in negative SDHB protein immunohistochemistry, known as SDH deficiency ( ). SDHB immunohistochemistry is recommended for all PPGL and consideration to genetic testing is strongly recommended for all SDH-deficient tumours ( ). Biallelic somatic pathogenic variants are exceptionally rare, with up to 99% of cases with a biallelic loss exhibiting a germline variant ( ).
Germline variants of SDHA (chromosome 5p15.33) account for 30% of all SDH-deficient gastrointestinal stromal tumours ( , ). Variants of SDHAF2 (chromosome 11q12.2) have not been proven to be associated with pituitary adenoma to date and exhibit a parent-of-origin effect with clinical manifestations virtually always only occurring with paternal transmission ( , ). Variants of SDHB (chromosome 1p36.13) are associated with familial paraganglioma type 4 and account for 83% of SDH-deficient renal cell carcinoma ( , ). Variants of SDHC (chromosome 1q23.3) are associated with familial paraganglioma type 3 and typically manifest as unifocal non-secretory paraganglioma with low risk of malignancy and low penetrance ( , ). SDHC promoter hypermethylation causes epigenetic inactivation of SDH and is associated with development of the Carney triad, a sporadic condition comprising gastrointestinal stromal tumour, paragangliomas and pulmonary chondroma ( ). Variants of SDHD (chromosome 11q23) are associated with familial paraganglioma type 1 with a predilection for head and neck paragangliomas and multifocal disease ( , ). SDHD typically exhibits a parent-of-origin effect due to maternal imprinting of a downstream promotor; there are, however, documented cases of paraganglioma from maternally inherited SDHD variants (<5%), first identified in 2008 ( , , ).
PPGL are the most common SDHx variant manifestation, with SDHA, SDHB, SDHC and paternally inherited SDHD penetrance rates of 1.7, 8.3, 22.0 and 43.2%, respectively ( ). Germline pathogenic variants in SDHx account for approximately 50% of all hereditary paragangliomas, increasing to 67% for carotid body paragangliomas specifically, and prompting experts to recommend genetic testing in all patients with carotid body paragangliomas ( ). The risk of pituitary adenoma development in people with SDHx variants appears to be low but may be underestimated given a lack of routine pituitary surveillance ( ). Amongst people with pituitary adenomas, SDHx variants are only found in 0.3–1.8% ( ).
Regarding surveillance, the 2021 international Delphi consensus statement recommended screening for asymptomatic adult carriers to include annual supine plasma metanephrines, baseline 68Ga-DOTATATE PET CT/MRI and MRI of skull base to coccyx every 2–3 years ( , ).

## Recent advances
Recent studies show that SDHB variants are more likely to be associated with distant metastatic disease from primary head and neck paraganglioma ( ). Tumour size ≥ 40 mm is associated with increased risk of progression to metastatic disease (odds ratio: 16.9), but non-proband individuals with surveillance-detected tumours see improved 10-year metastasis-free survival (84 vs 66% in probands), smaller tumours (median: 27 vs 45 mm in probands), and improved 10-year overall survival rates (100 vs 79% in probands), thus highlighting improved clinical outcomes with surveillance ( ).
The deepest SDHx intronic variant to date, SDHC c.20+74A>G, was identified in recent years in two Italian kindreds with suggestion of a founder effect, affected by SDH-deficient paragangliomas, renal cell carcinoma, gastrointestinal stromal tumour and prolactinoma ( ). Deep intronic variants are typically beyond the limits of standard genetic sequencing techniques, but should be considered in individuals with SDH-deficient tumours and negative genetic tests ( ).
Regarding tumour management, observation is generally now the preferred first-line management of SDHD-associated head and neck paraganglioma, given their generally slow growth and multifocal nature; neoadjuvant radiation treatment, typically stereotactic radiosurgery, is the first line of management for those with an evidence of growth or risk of vagal nerve compromise ( ).
While gastrointestinal stromal tumours are typically treated with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI) ( ), those associated with SDHx pathogenic variants often possess primary resistance, with possible exceptions undergoing further clinical research including regorafenib, pazopanib and linsitinib ( ). Research on temozolomide continues, with early trials showing positive impact ( ).
As SDH-deficient paraganglioma stem from the cluster 1 (pseudohypoxia) pathway, phase II trials of TKIs targeting anti-angiogenesis (e.g. sunitinib, cabozantinib) and epigenetic alteration (guadecitabine) have shown some promise ( ). Cyclophosphamide, vincristine and dacarbazine combination chemotherapy is the first line for SDH-deficient metastatic paraganglioma, with some evidence for temozolomide maintenance therapy for SDHB variants ( ). There is an active research on combination of olaparib (poly-ADP-ribose polymerase inhibitor) and temozolomide for metastatic phaeochromocytoma ( ).

## Carney complex
Inactivating variants of the PRKAR1A gene (chromosome 17q22-24; encoding the regulatory 1α subunit of protein kinase A) cause the autosomal dominant Carney complex ( ). Carney complex is a rare condition, with approximately 750 known cases to date ( ). Carney complex describes the constellation of myxomas, lentiginosis and endocrinopathy; endocrine manifestations include Cushing’s syndrome secondary to primary pigmented nodular adrenocortical disease (PPNAD; 45–70%), acromegaly and thyroid tumours ( ). Psammomatous melanotic schwannoma represents a unique manifestation of Carney complex ( ). A study of 140 individuals with Carney complex found that 35.7% had growth hormone excess and 20% had symptomatic acromegaly, with a median age at diagnosis of 25.3 and 26.3 years, respectively ( ); prolactin co-hypersecretion may occur ( ). Cardiac myxomas affect 63.1% of patients with Carney complex at a median age of 39 years and a female preponderance ( ). Studies have shown that up to 100% of patients will require surgical intervention if cardiac myxomas develop with good long-term survival, though over half will recur ( ).
There is near-complete penetrance of Carney complex manifestations by the age of 50 years ( ). Manifestations may appear prior to the age of 3 years, and thus, genetic testing and screening commencement should be considered by the age of 2 years ( ). When diagnosed after puberty, baseline screening investigations should include transthoracic echocardiogram, 24 h urinary free cortisol, serum IGF-1, ultrasound thyroid and ultrasound testes (male patients) ( ). Predictive screening of those with affected first-degree relatives should be considered by the age of 2 years ( ).

## Recent advances
Cumulative data regarding this exceedingly rare condition suggest that large deletions of PRKAR1A lead to a more severe disease phenotype. Assessment for large intragenic or whole gene deletions should, therefore, be considered when individuals otherwise meet diagnostic criteria for Carney complex but have no variant detected by sequencing ( ). Furthermore, gain-of-function variants in PRKACB, which encodes the β-subunit of protein kinase A, lead to a Carney complex phenotype, including PPNAD ( ).
In addition to the known association between Carney complex and benign breast tumours (e.g. breast/nipple myxomas and adenomas, affecting 20–40% of female patients ( )), an association with breast cancer has recently been identified affecting 13.5% of females under the age of 50 years. Women with a PRKAR1A pathogenic variant have an odds ratio of 6.34 of developing breast cancer, and further occurring at a mean of 17 years younger as compared with the general population ( , ). Regular monitoring with ultrasonography and/or MRI has, therefore, been suggested ( ).

## McCune–Albright syndrome
Postzygotic activating variants of GNAS (chromosome 20q13.3; encoding G protein α-subunit) cause McCune–Albright syndrome ( , ). It is a mosaic condition by definition, meaning that only a portion of cells within an individual harbour the pathogenic variant, with a mixture of both wild-type and variant cells present ( ). Germline activating GNAS variants are considered incompatible with life and are thus not transmitted to live offspring ( ). McCune–Albright syndrome has an incidence of 3.6 per 1,000,000 person-years and prevalence of 61.0 per 1,000,000 persons ( ). McCune–Albright syndrome is diagnosed based on the combination of fibrous dysplasia (monostotic or polyostotic) and ≥1 extra skeletal feature, or the presence of ≥2 extra skeletal features ( ); extra skeletal features include myxomas, café-au-lait macules, gonadotrophin-independent precocious puberty, thyroid tumours and pituitary adenoma, typically with growth hormone excess ( ). FGF-23-mediated hypophosphataemia is considered a marker of skeletal fibrous dysplasia severity as opposed to a cardinal feature of McCune–Albright syndrome ( ). While predominantly a clinical diagnosis, genetic testing for somatic GNAS variants is of clinical utility for cases of monostotic fibrous dysplasia, particularly of the skull, in the absence of other associated skeletal or extra skeletal features; given its mosaicism, this is facilitated via biopsy of affected tissue ( ).
Skeletal burden is typically detectable by the age of 15 years, and clinically significant disease, defined as that causing functional disability, by the age of 5 years; bone scintigraphy or MRI whole body should be performed in affected individuals aged >5 years ( ). Regular biochemical assessment should include kidney function, alkaline phosphatase, bicarbonate, serum corrected calcium, phosphate, 25-hydroxy vitamin D and parathyroid hormone ( ). Craniofacial fibrous dysplasia staging involves formal assessment of facial asymmetry and consideration of subspecialist referral with formal visual field assessment and audiogram, as dictated by disease location ( ). Precocious puberty with bone age advancement ≥2 years or frequent menstruation in girls should be managed with letrozole 2.5 mg daily ( ).
Untreated growth hormone excess is associated with enlargement of craniofacial fibrous dysplasia, predisposing to macrocephaly, vision impairment and hearing impairment (odds ratio: 3.1); neonatal hypercortisolism is also associated with increased risk of hearing loss (odds ratio: 11) ( ). Individuals with craniofacial fibrous dysplasia should, thus, undergo otolaryngologic evaluation alongside standard risk reduction management ( ).

## Recent advances
Thus far, there have been no established genotype-phenotype differences or in endocrine manifestations between the two most common GNAS variants, R201H (accounting for 63.9% of cases) and R201C (accounting for 36.1% of cases) ( ). As a mosaic condition, genetic diagnosis can be difficult to ascertain non-invasively; recent studies of blood-circulating cell-free DNA were able to identify R201H or R201C variants in 68.2% of cases, with detection likelihood correlating with skeletal disease severity ( ).
Recent data from the Netherlands have suggested that McCune–Albright syndrome is associated with an increased risk of osteosarcoma, cervical cancer, thyroid cancer, prostate cancer and melanoma ( ). Patients with fibrous dysplasia had a 3.4-fold increased risk of breast cancer, incrementing to a 5.7-fold increased risk if thoracic disease was present, and occurring at a younger age; early breast screening should, thus, be considered ( ). It is hypothesised that the presence of growth hormone excess, polyostotic fibrous dysplasia and radiation exposure may contribute to the increased risk ( ).
Medical treatment for polyostotic fibrous dysplasia remains antiresorptive agents such as zoledronic acid, pamidronate and denosumab, which have been associated with 33% reduction in pain, correlating with reduced bone remodelling as demonstrated by a 41% reduction in bone resorption markers and 22% reduction in bone formation markers within 6–12 months of treatment ( ). Bisphosphonates do not prevent progression of scoliosis; however, management of hyperthyroidism and FGF-23-mediated hypophosphataemia may be beneficial ( ).

## DICER1 syndrome
Inactivating variants of the DICER1 gene (chromosome 14q32.13; encoding cytoplasmic ribonuclease (RNase) III endoribonuclease, critical to microRNA production and regulation of messenger RNA) produce the autosomal dominant DICER1 syndrome ( ). Pathogenic DICER1 variants have an estimated prevalence in the general population of 1 per 10,600 people ( ). DICER1 syndrome is associated with a plethora of rare benign and malignant neoplasms, including pleuropulmonary blastoma, cystic nephroma and various endocrine tumours ( ). Endocrine manifestations include thyroid disease (multinodular goitre and differentiated thyroid cancer), Sertoli–Leydig cell ovarian tumour, pineoblastoma and pituitary blastoma ( ). Pituitary blastoma is considered a pathognomonic feature of DICER1 syndrome and can present with Cushing’s syndrome (58.8%), ophthalmoplegia (41.2%), developmental delay (23.5%) and/or symptoms of raised intracranial pressure (17.6%); hypopituitarism is frequent ( ). DICER1 syndrome penetrance is low, with pituitary blastoma occurring in <1% of cases; other manifestations show penetrance of up to 5–10% ( ).
Noting the absence of published evidence on the yield of tumour surveillance in DICER1 syndrome, a consensus opinion has been published to guide surveillance for pleuropulmonary blastoma, thyroid and female reproductive tract tumours ( ).

## Recent advances
DICER1 syndrome has been associated with a 16- to 24-fold increased risk of developing thyroid cancer, with 75 and 17% of affected men and women, respectively, developing a multinodular goitre by the age of 40 years ( , ). While there is an increased risk of DICER1-associated thyroid cancer and diagnosis at a younger age, DICER1-associated papillary thyroid cancers have recently been shown to exhibit low invasiveness, prompting a call for more conservative management in these neoplasms ( ).
While pituitary blastoma is typically associated with a very young median age at diagnosis of 11 months ( ), more recently cases of young adults with pituitary blastomas have been described including a 27-year-old man with a pituitary blastoma exhibiting two somatic DICER1 pathogenic variants affecting the RNase IIIb domain ( ) and a woman developing pituitary blastoma at the age of 19 years, although DICER1 testing was not available ( ). Recent molecular characterisation of pituitary blastomas has revealed reduced 5p miRNAs, with 441 mRNA transcripts differentially expressed, and activation of the PI3K pathway ( ). The most upregulated mRNA was PRAME, encoding preferentially expressed antigen in melanoma and involved in the retinoic acid receptor (RAR) signalling pathway ( ). The discovery of these altered pathways in pituitary blastomas holds the potential precision-driven therapeutic intervention for these rare tumours. Currently, pituitary blastomas continue to be associated with high mortality, where surgical resection, and to lesser extent radiotherapy and chemotherapy, represent primary treatment options for affected patients ( ).

## von Hippel–Lindau syndrome
Inactivating variants of the VHL gene (chromosome 3p25.5; encoding von Hippel–Lindau disease tumour suppressor) produce the autosomal dominant VHL syndrome, characterised by numerous and multisystemic benign and malignant tumours ( ). Under normal physiology, the VHL protein regulates ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of HIFa subunit; loss-of-function VHL variants result in constitutive activation and stabilisation of the HIF-2a subunit, causing tumourigenesis to ensue as a response to pseudohypoxia ( ). VHL syndrome has an estimated prevalence of 1 in 46,900 people and incidence of 1 in 27,300 live births ( ). Renal cell carcinoma is the most common manifestation of VHL syndrome, occurring in approximately 70% of affected individuals ( ). Pancreatic NETs occur in 9–17% of individuals with VHL disease; these are always non-functional and tend to be asymptomatic albeit with metastatic potential, particularly when larger in size ( ). Smaller pancreatic NETs without worrisome features might undergo active surveillance; however, larger pancreatic NETs (≥3 cm in the pancreas body, ≥2 cm in the pancreas head) or those with concerning growth velocity have been managed with pancreatectomy ( ).

## Recent advances
Adding to the complexity of genetic diagnosis, there has been a recent identification of variants within the first intronic region of the VHL gene, which results in the formation of a cryptic exon known as E1′ ( ). E1′ germline variants account for 1.32% of cases with clinical VHL syndrome but without otherwise detected genetic variant ( ). To our knowledge, the E1′ exon is not currently included in commercially available PPGL target gene panels; hence, Sanger sequencing of this region may be considered in people with a classical phenotype of VHL syndrome but without an identifiable pathogenic VHL variant ( ). Furthermore, pathogenic germline variants in ELOC (chromosome 8; encoding elongin C) have recently been implicated in clinical VHL syndrome with the loss of heterozygosity affecting tumours of a canonically affected family; elongin C forms a complex with the VHL protein to ubiquitinate and degrade hypoxia inducible factors under normal circumstances ( ).
The HIF-2a inhibitor, belzutifan, has been specifically studied in germline VHL-associated tumours, with the phase 2 LITESPARK-004 trial showing an objective response rate (either partial or complete response) of 91% in those with pancreatic NETs with a mean follow-up of 21.8 months ( , ). Of the 61 individuals who then progressed into the extension arm with a mean follow-up of 37.8 months, objective response rate was 84% for all pancreatic lesions and 91% (20/22) for those with pancreatic NET only, including seven with a complete response with a mean linear growth rate of −4.2 mm/year after commencing belzutifan ( ). Impressively 100% of treated individuals experienced reduction from baseline in the total sum of pancreatic NET target lesion diameter and none underwent pancreatic-related surgery ( ). All patients experienced treatment-related side effects, with anaemia (89%) and fatigue (64%) most common ( ). Median time to response was 8.2 months; neither median progression-free survival nor median duration of response was reached in this preliminary study ( ). Thus, belzutifan has revolutionised treatment of VHL-associated tumours, with particularly promising results seen for VHL-associated pancreatic disease.
Given the complex and multiorgan nature of VHL syndrome, a world-first VHL care pathway has recently been published from a Dutch group, emphasising the role of interdisciplinary care in treating MEN syndromes ( ).

## Conclusion and future perspectives
Recent developments in MEN syndromes have markedly advanced our understanding of their clinicopathological landscape, enabling more precise and effective therapeutic strategies. The recent identification of two new syndromes, MEN4 and MEN5, both of which overlap with MEN1 syndrome, underscores the expanding and evolving genetic spectrum of MEN disorders. Ongoing discovery and reclassification of germline variants have strengthened genotype-phenotype correlations, allowing for more individualised surveillance protocols and treatment strategies, e.g. with increasingly refined genotype-guided prophylactic thyroidectomy advice in MEN2. Advances in molecular oncology are exemplified by the success of belzutifan for VHL-associated tumours, particularly pancreatic NETs. These developments underscore the importance of an informed but broad differential diagnosis when evaluating MEN syndromes. Critically, evidence continues to demonstrate that prompt diagnosis and surveillance commencement lead to improved prognosis and clinical outcomes, particularly in SDHB-associated tumours and MEN2-related MTC where there is a clear mortality benefit. Collectively, these advances will continue to refine diagnostic and therapeutic approaches for patients with MEN syndromes, translating to more tailored interventions and hopefully improved outcomes in this diverse and complex group of disorders.
