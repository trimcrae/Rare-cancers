## Summary
Neurodegeneration and cancer, long considered opposing processes, neuronal loss versus uncontrolled proliferation, are increasingly recognized as mechanistically convergent. This review reframes the field by positioning neurodegeneration as an active driver of tumor evolution. Integrating insights from multi-omics, spatial transcriptomics, and neuroimmune–microbiota studies, we show how degenerating neurons disrupt DNA repair, mitochondrial function, immune regulation, and barrier integrity, thereby creating tumor-permissive niches. Particular emphasis is placed on the enteric nervous system, where neuronal loss promotes colorectal and pancreatic cancers through immune suppression and microbial dysbiosis. Shared molecular regulators, including p53, Pin1, STING, and the Biglycan–IL-10–microbiota axis, exhibit dual roles, pro-apoptotic in neurons yet pro-survival in tumors, highlighting context-dependent biology. We propose a mechanistic, target-based therapeutic classification that transcends disease boundaries. Recognizing neurodegeneration as a tumor-promoting process uncovers novel diagnostic and therapeutic opportunities, expanding cancer neuroscience beyond its current paradigm.

## Introduction
Neurodegeneration and cancer, though traditionally viewed as divergent pathologies, with one marked by progressive neuronal loss and the other by uncontrolled cellular proliferation, share an intricate and evolving molecular dialogue that is increasingly recognized across disciplines. Emerging evidence suggests that neurodegenerative processes may not only coexist with tumorigenic environments but also actively contribute to cancer initiation and progression. While previous high-profile reviews in cancer neuroscience have largely emphasized tumor-induced neurogenesis and synaptic integration, here we focus on neurodegeneration itself as a mechanistic driver of tumor progression, particularly within peripheral neural systems such as the enteric nervous system (ENS), a perspective not comprehensively addressed in the existing literature. However, the nature of neural involvement varies across cancer types: while gliomas integrate into synaptic neural circuits within the central nervous system (CNS), , , gastrointestinal tumors, though of epithelial origin, are influenced by peripheral nerves such as the enteric or autonomic nervous systems through immune and paracrine signaling. , , Recent spatial multi-omics studies reveal that MAPK9-positive degenerating sensory neurons infiltrate colorectal tumors and stimulate EGFR-mediated epithelial proliferation, underscoring the need to target neuron–tumor interactions in colorectal cancer (CRC). 
Through diverse molecular, cellular, and spatial mechanisms, neurodegeneration reconfigures the tumor microenvironment (TME). This conceptual shift has emerged from integrated insights into DNA damage response (DDR), cell fate regulation, tumor–neural crosstalk, and neuroimmune interactions. At the core of this interface is the p53 signaling pathway. In neurodegeneration, p53 is hyperactivated in response to oxidative DNA lesions, promoting apoptosis and cellular senescence. Tau protein, commonly aggregated in tauopathies such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), directly interacts with p53 and stabilizes its conformation during the DNA damage response. , Mechanistic studies have shown that Tau loss leads to decreased p53 stability through the direct disruption of this interaction, thereby favoring a shift from apoptosis to senescence in damaged neurons, which may sustain chronic neuroinflammation. , Conversely, in cancer, p53 is frequently inactivated through mutation or degradation by MDM2, enabling genomic instability and tumor proliferation. This context-dependent modulation of p53, pro-apoptotic in neurons and anti-apoptotic in cancer, illustrates a fundamental divergence rooted in shared regulatory nodes.
Recent multiomics and spatial transcriptomics studies further underscore how neurodegeneration, especially within the ENS, promotes CRC progression. Degenerating MAPK9-positive neurons exhibit enhanced physical and signaling interactions with epithelial tumor cells, activating EGFR-dependent mitogenic pathways and remodeling the TME to favor malignant expansion. Similarly, studies in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) reveal that sympathetic axon sprouting exerts protective effects against tumor progression, yet nerve ablation fosters macrophage-mediated immunosuppression, highlighting the dualistic role of neuroimmune networks depending on neural phenotype and anatomical context. Mechanistic insights from genetic and co-culture models have revealed that enteric neurodegeneration alters gut homeostasis and promotes tumorigenesis through secretome-mediated pathways. Specifically, loss of Ndrg4 in enteric neurons upregulates Nidogen-1 and Fibulin-2, extracellular matrix proteins that enhance CRC cell migration and growth. Similarly, Biglycan, a proteoglycan enriched in enteric neurons, modulates IL-10 secretion and microbial composition, further contributing to CRC progression. The integration of ENS neurodegeneration with immune modulation and microbiota dysbiosis, known as the ENS–microbiota–immune axis, constitutes a central and novel thematic focus of this review. These findings highlight a novel neuroepithelial crosstalk wherein neurodegeneration alters immune signaling and microbial ecology in ways that support tumorigenesis.
Beyond structural changes, sensory and autonomic neurons actively modulate antitumor immunity through neuromodulatory signals such as calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) and substance P, which are released in response to tumor-derived cues such as nerve growth factor (NGF). These neuropeptides influence immune cell recruitment, cytokine secretion, and checkpoint molecule expression, ultimately promoting immune suppression within the TME. CGRP–RAMP1 signaling has been shown to drive T cell exhaustion and reduce the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade therapies in models of melanoma and head and neck cancer, while CGRP inhibition enhances cytotoxic T cell infiltration and tumor clearance. Tumors, in turn, can remodel innervation by secreting axonogenic factors, forming new nerve–tumor circuits that further reshape immunity and promote metastatic spread. 
Moreover, comprehensive reviews have consolidated the role of neural inputs in regulating gastrointestinal tumor biology. Neurally derived factors such as neurotransmitters, neurotrophins (e.g., NGF, BDNF), and enteric neuron–derived cytokines modulate tumor cell behavior, cancer stemness, and immune evasion, placing the nervous system as a master regulator of gastrointestinal malignancies. , A recent synthesis of preclinical and clinical evidence further emphasizes that both enteric and central nervous system pathways converge to regulate CRC progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance through neural-immune signaling networks, highlighting novel intervention points within these circuits. Neural remodeling within the TME fosters cancer plasticity, and tumors in turn elicit aberrant neurogenesis and neurotrophic signaling, establishing a bidirectional feedforward loop. Recent findings also indicate that nociceptive neurons express pattern recognition receptors, Toll-like receptors (TLRs), and cGAS–STING components, equipping them to sense immune stressors and microbial signals while simultaneously shaping local immune landscapes. These immuno-neural feedback loops intensify chronic inflammation and immune evasion, further blurring the boundary between degenerative and oncogenic processes. 
At a systemic level, transcriptomic meta-analyses and cross-disease studies demonstrate that neurodegeneration and cancer share overlapping molecular signatures, including DDR, redox imbalance, and proteostasis collapse, but are frequently modulated in opposing directions. , Genes upregulated in AD, Parkinson’s disease (PD), and schizophrenia are often downregulated in lung, prostate, and colorectal cancers, suggesting an inverse comorbidity mediated by dysregulated p53, cyclin D/E/F, Pin1, and PP2A. Such patterns reinforce the concept that while neurons succumb to stress via apoptotic cascades, cancer cells often hijack these same pathways to evade death.
Collectively, these findings challenge the canonical dichotomy between neurodegeneration and cancer and instead support a convergent model in which chronic neuronal stress, immune dysregulation, and altered intercellular communication generate fertile ground for tumor development ( ). By positioning the neuro-immune-tumor axis at the center of this interplay, we highlight how neural activity shapes both oncogenic transformation and immune surveillance. In this review, we examine the bidirectional and context-dependent roles of neurodegenerative processes in cancer, which opens new avenues for therapeutic intervention, biomarker discovery, and integrative disease modeling. 

## Cellular and environmental triggers of neurodegeneration
Neurodegeneration is initiated by a multifactorial convergence of intrinsic cellular dysfunctions and external environmental insults. Chief among these cellular perturbations are oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation, proteostasis failure, and synaptic disruption. These triggers interact dynamically with genetic predispositions and environmental exposures to drive the onset and progression of neurodegenerative disorders. Oxidative stress stands as a central trigger of neurodegeneration. Neurons are particularly susceptible to redox imbalance due to their high oxygen consumption, abundant lipid content, and low antioxidant capacity. Excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) damage DNA, lipids, and proteins, initiating a cascade of neuronal dysfunction. This is consistently reported across several studies, highlighting the accumulation of oxidative damage in AD, PD, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) as a common early event. , , Mitochondrial dysfunction is intricately linked to oxidative stress and plays a critical role in neurodegeneration. Disrupted mitochondrial dynamics, impaired ATP production, and defective mitophagy, such as seen with PINK1-Parkin dysfunction in PD and SOD1 mutations in ALS, lead to energy failure and heightened oxidative damage. , These deficits compromise calcium homeostasis and amplify neuronal vulnerability, especially in energy-demanding regions such as the substantia nigra or hippocampus.
Chronic inflammation and innate immune activation represent both a response to and an initiator of neurodegeneration. Microglial activation and cytokine production, initially thought to be secondary to protein aggregation, are now known to precede and drive pathological changes. Immune signaling pathways triggered by environmental stimuli, infections, or protein aggregates (e.g., amyloid-β, α-synuclein, TDP-43) perpetuate neurotoxic cycles. Recent findings reveal that peripheral neurons themselves can act as sensors and amplifiers of inflammatory signals, expressing TLRs and cytokine receptors such as TNFR1, which allow direct response to systemic or local inflammatory stimuli. The activation of these pathways induces neuropeptide release, sensitization, and sustained immune activation, forming a bidirectional feedforward loop with innate immune cells such as macrophages and microglia. Furthermore, inflammation can be exacerbated by failure in the resolution phase, due to the reduced production of specialized pro-resolving lipid mediators in aging individuals. Protein misfolding and aggregation are hallmark events that not only reflect disease pathology but also actively contribute to its initiation. Aggregates such as Aβ, tau, and α-synuclein are capable of disrupting synaptic integrity and inducing immune responses. The synaptic spread hypothesis posits that misfolded proteins propagate in a prion-like manner, triggering pathology in connected brain regions. Genetic and epigenetic factors further influence susceptibility to neurodegeneration. Mutations in genes such as APOE4, TREM2, PINK1, Parkin, and SOD1 are well-established contributors. Additionally, epigenetic modifications to antioxidant gene promoters (e.g., SOD2) reduce endogenous defenses against oxidative stress and have been implicated in disease onset. , 
Environmental exposures, including aging, diet, toxins, systemic infections, and gut microbiota dysbiosis, serve as extrinsic modulators of neurodegeneration. These factors influence inflammatory tone, oxidative burden, and immune priming, thereby creating a permissive environment for disease development. , , Notably, gut-derived metabolites and peripheral inflammation can activate vagal afferents and dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons, contributing to central sensitization and early neuroimmune dysregulation, even before overt neurodegenerative pathology is apparent. 
Collectively, these interlinked pathophysiological features, oxidative stress, mitochondrial failure, immune activation, proteostasis disruption, and environmental influence, form a cohesive framework driving neurodegeneration from its earliest stages. Additional layers of dysfunction, such as DNA and RNA damage, autophagy impairment, and cytoskeletal instability, further reinforce this network of neurotoxic stress. 
Importantly, these neuron-intrinsic stress signals initiate complex molecular cascades that extend beyond isolated neurodegeneration. They converge on evolutionarily conserved regulators, such as p53, STING, and Pin1, which serve as signaling hubs linking neuronal injury to systemic transcriptional and immune reprogramming. These molecules also function in oncogenic contexts, where they regulate DNA damage responses, cellular senescence, and innate immunity. , , , , , Their dual roles across neurodegeneration and cancer highlight the mechanistic bridge between chronic neuronal damage and tumor-promoting microenvironmental alterations. As such, these shared molecular pathways merit a focused exploration.

## Neurodegenerative signaling pathways that reprogram the tumor microenvironment
Neurodegeneration initiates a cascade of stress-activated signaling pathways, originally intended to preserve neuronal integrity, that can profoundly reshape the surrounding microenvironment. Increasing evidence indicates that these neurodegenerative cascades extend beyond neuronal compartments, exerting paracrine, metabolic, and immunologic effects that actively reprogram the TME and promote malignancy. Key molecular regulators, including p53, Pin1, STING, and cell cycle proteins, operate at the interface between neuronal injury and tumor progression, acting as shared, but contextually divergent, drivers of cell fate decisions , , ( ).
Among these, the tumor suppressor p53 plays a central role. In neurodegeneration, p53 is activated by genotoxic stress such as oxidative DNA damage and drives apoptotic or senescent fates. For instance, in AD, p53 is phosphorylated at Ser15, altering its oligomerization and impairing DNA repair capacity, thereby accelerating neuronal loss. In contrast, in cancer, p53 is often mutated or degraded via MDM2 overexpression, allowing cells to evade apoptosis and sustain uncontrolled proliferation. Thus, p53 activation in neurons leads to neurodegenerative loss, whereas its suppression in tumor cells facilitates immune evasion and genomic instability. Importantly, senescent neurons with dysfunctional p53 can release pro-inflammatory mediators such as IL-6 and CCL2, which are known to attract immunosuppressive cells (e.g., myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC)), thereby reinforcing a tumor-permissive niche.
A second regulator, Pin1, further exemplifies this duality. As a peptidyl-prolyl isomerase, Pin1 regulates post-phosphorylation conformational changes in proteins. In neurons, Pin1 enhances p53-induced apoptosis, contributing to neurotoxicity. In glioblastoma (GBM) and other cancers, however, Pin1 acts oncogenically by stabilizing sumoylation machinery (e.g., Ubc9) and promoting cell cycle progression through deubiquitinase USP34. Thus, neuronal Pin1 signaling may amplify apoptotic signals, while in cancer it rewires cellular stress responses toward survival.
Additional shared regulators include cyclins (D, E, F) and protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A). In neurons, the aberrant reactivation of the cell cycle leads to apoptosis, whereas in cancer, overexpression of cyclins drives proliferation. PP2A, meanwhile, not only functions as a tumor suppressor in epithelial tissues but also modulates tau phosphorylation and aggregation in neurodegeneration, linking synaptic dysfunction to oncogenic signaling. These findings underscore how cell cycle and phosphatase dysregulation in stressed neurons may spill over into surrounding tissues, altering local proliferative and inflammatory cues.
Oxidative stress and chronic inflammation serve as upstream activators of these shared signaling hubs. Both neurodegeneration and cancer exhibit perturbations in redox-sensitive pathways, such as NRF2, and deficiencies in DNA repair enzymes. While persistent oxidative stress leads to neuronal death, cancer cells often co-opt ROS to promote mutagenesis and adaptive survival. , Thus, redox imbalance initiated in degenerating neurons can release lipid peroxidation products and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) into the local environment, where they may fuel chronic inflammation and tumor growth.
The cGAS–STING pathway, a critical sensor of cytosolic DNA, links genomic instability to innate immune activation in both neurodegeneration and cancer. However, its downstream effects are highly tissue- and context-dependent. In the CNS, chronic STING signaling, predominantly in microglia and astrocytes, promotes neuroinflammation and synaptic dysfunction via sustained production of type I interferons (IFN-I) and IL-6. , Mitochondrial DNA leakage, oxidative damage, and defective autophagy all contribute to aberrant STING activation in neurodegenerative diseases such as AD, where it cooperates with p53 inactivation to impair DNA repair and drive neuronal loss. , Conversely, in peripheral tissues and the TME, STING activation plays dual roles. In dendritic cells and macrophages, STING agonism enhances antitumor immunity by boosting antigen presentation and cytotoxic T cell recruitment. , However, in many tumors, STING is epigenetically silenced or functionally repressed to allow immune evasion. , Intriguingly, chronic STING activation in tumor-associated neurons and glia can foster immune tolerance and cancer-promoting inflammation, establishing neuroinflammatory niches that feed into both neurodegeneration and malignancy. , These findings emphasize the need for the tissue-specific modulation of STING activity, especially in the development of targeted agonists for cancer immunotherapy or antagonists for neurodegenerative disease. 
Beyond cytokine release, epigenetic dysregulation offers another shared axis of TME reprogramming. In neurodegeneration, hypermethylation of promoters such as NRF2 or SOD2 reduces antioxidant defenses, whereas in cancer, these same loci are often hypomethylated to promote oxidative stress tolerance. These epigenetic shifts alter not only intrinsic cell behavior but also influence the local immune tone and stromal remodeling in both disease states.
In summary, neurodegeneration activates signaling pathways, including p53, Pin1, STING, and redox regulators, that extend beyond neuronal loss to actively reconfigure the TME. These pathways modulate immune responses, cytokine release, and epithelial–stromal interactions in ways that promote tumor progression. Rather than opposing diseases, neurodegeneration and cancer are linked through shared molecular logic deployed in divergent contexts, revealing therapeutic opportunities to target common pathways while accounting for tissue-specific outcomes. , , , 

## Cellular mechanisms by which degenerating neurons remodel the tumor microenvironment
The TME is a complex and dynamic ecosystem, and recent research has increasingly highlighted the role of the nervous system, particularly degenerating neurons, in modulating cancer development and progression. This concept aligns with the updated classification of the tumor microenvironment, which recognizes the “innervated niche” as a core component alongside hypoxic, immune, metabolic, and acidic niches, collectively shaping tumor behavior and therapeutic response. Far from being passive victims of tumor invasion, neural elements actively engage with tumor cells, reshape the surrounding stroma, and contribute to the evolution of malignancy. In CRC, which originates from epithelial cells and lacks direct neural integration, spatial transcriptomics and single-cell multiomics have demonstrated that neurodegeneration within the ENS promotes tumor growth. A recent study showed that MAPK9-positive sensory neurons, which exhibit signatures of neurodegeneration, infiltrate deep into tumor compartments, correlate with poor prognosis, and stimulate tumor proliferation via epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) signaling. These neurons also enhance tumor–neuron interactions through homotypic and heterotypic spatial networks, increasing cell-cell communication and promoting a more aggressive tumor phenotype. Supporting this, analyses of tumor tissue from patients wih CRC demonstrated that de novo neurogenesis, indicated by increased PGP9.5 expression, is associated with lower disease-free and overall survival. Neurogenesis appears to be stimulated by cancer-derived signals, suggesting a bidirectional crosstalk between tumors and nerves.
Tumor-infiltrating neurons not only remodel their microenvironment through neurotransmitter and cytokine release but may also contribute to perineural invasion, a hallmark of advanced and aggressive cancer. , Mechanistically, perineural invasion and neuroplasticity play key roles in shaping the TME. Tumor cells exploit axonal structures as conduits for invasion, while degenerating neurons release DAMPs, growth factors, and chemokines that attract immune and stromal cells, contributing to angiogenesis and immune evasion. , Complementary evidence indicates that neural activity directly promotes tumor proliferation, metastasis, and immune modulation, reinforcing the concept of the nervous system as an active architect of the TME. CGRP, a neuropeptide upregulated in sensory neurons under inflammatory stress, has been shown to suppress dendritic cell maturation and T cell priming, thereby establishing an immunosuppressive TME. In mouse models of melanoma, inhibition of CGRP signaling reversed T cell exhaustion and improved response to PD-1 blockade, indicating that neural-derived signals can directly affect antitumor immunity. Extending this concept, a recent study describes cancer-induced nerve injury as a novel mechanism by which degenerating neurons directly reprogram the immune microenvironment. Tumor-associated nerve damage activates IFN-I and IL-6–driven inflammatory pathways, which evolve into chronic neuroimmune signals that suppress cytotoxic T cell function and drive resistance to PD-1 therapy. Importantly, this phenotype can be reversed by denervation, genetic deletion of Atf3 in neurons, or dual PD-1/IL-6R blockade, underscoring degenerating neurons as active regulators of immunotherapy outcomes. These findings suggest that degenerating neurons release not only structural cues but also immunomodulatory signals that shape the immune landscape of tumors. , 
Studies show that neuroinflammation, driven by microglial and astrocytic responses in the CNS, is mirrored in the periphery by ENS-driven cytokine release, supporting tumor-promoting inflammation. , This neuroimmune interplay is mediated through shared receptors such as TNFR1, IL-1R, and pattern recognition receptors expressed on both neurons and immune cells, forming positive feedback loops of cytokine production, neural sensitization, and immune dysfunction. Tumor-derived axonogenic signals, including NGF and semaphorins, promote aberrant innervation that sustains neuroinflammatory responses and stromal remodeling. 
Interestingly, data also suggest a context-specific role of neural remodeling in different tumor types. In pancreatic and prostate cancer, neural infiltration correlates with high-grade disease, while in gastrointestinal cancers such as CRC, neuronal degeneration itself may create a permissive microenvironment through the loss of neuroepithelial homeostasis, deregulated neurotransmission, and disrupted intestinal motility. , The degeneration of enteric neurons alters microbiota composition and immune barrier integrity, further destabilizing epithelial architecture. Recent data also indicate that chronic inflammation within neuron-rich niches, such as the gut or pancreas, synergizes with neuronal damage to trigger tissue remodeling programs that favor carcinogenesis. For example, injured neurons can produce IL-6 and VEGF, enhancing MDSC recruitment and angiogenesis. , Adding another layer, epidemiological and molecular studies point to an inverse relationship between neurodegeneration and some cancer types. However, both disorders share overlapping mechanisms, including mitochondrial dysfunction, immune dysregulation, and proteostasis collapse, indicating that the relationship is not linear but deeply interconnected. , 
Taken together, the cumulative evidence from multiomics, pathology, and mechanistic studies supports the notion that degenerating neurons are not mere victims in cancer progression but active participants. By releasing soluble factors, promoting structural invasion pathways, and disrupting tissue homeostasis, they reshape the TME in ways that support proliferation, immune escape, and therapy resistance. This reconfiguration of the tumor–neural–immune axis not only sustains tumorigenesis but also limits the efficacy of immune checkpoint therapies and targeted interventions. , , This integrated perspective demonstrates that neurodegeneration is both a consequence and a driver of tumor evolution, particularly in cancers with high neural involvement.

## Immune dysregulation linking neurodegeneration and cancer
Chronic inflammation and immune imbalance are central features that bridge the pathophysiology of both neurodegenerative diseases and cancer. In neurodegeneration, sustained activation of innate immune pathways, including microglial and astrocytic responses, leads to neuronal injury through the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, nitric oxide, and ROS. This inflammatory signature mirrors tumor-promoting inflammation in the TME, where immune cell infiltration can paradoxically facilitate cancer initiation, immune evasion, and metastasis. A major shared pathway is NF-κB signaling, which is activated in both degenerative neurons and tumor cells. In neurodegeneration, NF-κB promotes chronic microglial activation and the transcription of pro-inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, sustaining neurotoxic inflammation. , In cancer, NF-κB plays a pivotal role in promoting tumor cell proliferation, survival, angiogenesis, and resistance to therapy. 
The NLRP3 inflammasome represents another convergence point. Its activation leads to the maturation and release of IL-1β and IL-18, driving pyroptosis in neurons and glial cells in AD and PD. , In the oncological context, NLRP3 signaling promotes tumor growth by modulating immune cell recruitment and creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment, particularly in gliomas and gastrointestinal cancers. Microglial and macrophage polarization (M1 vs. M2 phenotypes) further highlights the functional overlap. In neurodegeneration, chronically activated M1-like microglia release neurotoxic factors that impair synaptic function and accelerate neurodegeneration. , In tumors, M2-like tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) promote immune tolerance, matrix remodeling, and vascularization, correlating with worse prognosis. , , 
Another point of intersection is T cell exhaustion. In late-stage neurodegenerative diseases, peripheral T cells show signs of clonal expansion and exhaustion, likely due to persistent antigenic stimulation and chronic neuroinflammation. Similarly, in cancer, exhausted CD8+ T cells accumulate in the TME and exhibit poor effector function, limiting anti-tumor immunity and reducing the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade therapies. , Recent findings have shown that neuropeptides such as CGRP and substance P, released from sensory neurons during neuroinflammation, can directly dampen dendritic cell function and T cell activation, further amplifying immune exhaustion. In both gliomas and gastrointestinal cancers, this neurogenic suppression of immunity is associated with the reduced infiltration of cytotoxic lymphocytes and poorer response to immunotherapy. Emerging work also demonstrates that neurotransmitters and neurotrophins, including acetylcholine, dopamine, NGF, and BDNF, orchestrate tumor–immune microenvironment remodeling by modulating immune cell recruitment, cytokine secretion, and checkpoint molecule expression, thereby shaping tumor immune evasion and offering novel neuromodulatory therapeutic targets. 
Beyond the central nervous system, neurodegeneration within the peripheral nervous system (e.g., enteric or sympathetic nerves), has emerged as a potent immune modulator in the TME. Recent findings by Amit et al. demonstrated that tumors such as cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) can induce tumor-associated nerves (TAN) demyelination and axonal degeneration, triggering the upregulation of neurodegeneration-associated proteins (e.g., PINK1, PARK7, LRRK2) and IFN-I signaling. These changes correlate with poor response to anti-PD-1 therapy and the expansion of immunosuppressive M2 macrophages and regulatory T cells in perineural niches. Functional studies using murine models showed that nerve injury prior to immune checkpoint blockade impairs antitumor efficacy, while surgical denervation or pharmacologic IL-6 blockade restores CD8+ T cell infiltration and improves tumor control. , , This immune-evading process has been termed cancer-associated peripheral nerve degeneration (CAPND), and represents a newly recognized barrier to effective immunotherapy. Mechanistically, CAPND reshapes the TME by sustaining chronic inflammation and neuroinflammatory signaling, leading to immune exhaustion and therapy resistance. , Importantly, this mechanism may extend beyond cSCC to other neurotropic malignancies such as pancreatic, prostate, and breast cancer, where perineural invasion and IL-6-driven inflammation are also prevalent and associated with poor response to checkpoint inhibitors. , 
Heat shock proteins (HSPs), particularly Hsp90, are implicated in both disease processes. In neurodegeneration, Hsp90 prevents the clearance of misfolded proteins such as tau and α-synuclein, thereby exacerbating proteinopathy. In cancer, it stabilizes oncogenic proteins and signaling pathways, facilitating tumor cell survival and resistance. Moreover, the chaperone network has been shown to mediate immunoproteasome activity in both neurons and cancer cells, influencing antigen presentation and modulating immune recognition. Aberrant chaperone function thus contributes not only to proteostasis collapse but also to immune evasion. 
At the genomic level, inverse comorbidity patterns have been described. Key tumor suppressors such as TP53 and PTEN, upregulated in AD, are frequently mutated or suppressed in cancers. In contrast, pro-proliferative and anti-apoptotic genes such as PIN1 and BCL2 show opposite regulation, being downregulated in neurodegeneration and upregulated in tumors. Transcriptomic profiling has further identified overlapping inflammatory modules between neurodegenerative and cancerous tissues, including shared interferon-stimulated genes, inflammasome components, and chemokines such as CCL2 and CXCL10. These patterns underscore a conserved yet context-dependent immune landscape shaped by chronic stress signals. 
In summary, a landscape of shared immunological dysfunction links neurodegeneration and cancer. Chronic inflammation, innate immune activation, adaptive immune exhaustion, and molecular chaperone imbalance form a common biological scaffold that supports both neuronal loss and malignant progression. The contribution of neuronal signals to immune programming, such as CGRP- or IL-6–mediated T cell suppression, further reveals that the nervous system is not only a passive target but also an active modulator of immune dysfunction across diseases. The convergence of these mechanisms points to the immune system not merely as a responder but as a driver of both disease trajectories. Therapeutic interventions targeting inflammasome activation, NF-κB signaling, Hsp90 function, or T cell reinvigoration hold promise for treating both cancer and neurodegenerative diseases.

## Tissue-specific neurodegeneration and organ-specific cancers
The intricate relationship between neurodegenerative processes and cancer is increasingly being recognized as tissue-context dependent, where regionally specific neuronal loss correlates with oncogenic predisposition or suppression across diverse organ systems. Molecular and cellular alterations in neurodegeneration often mirror those in carcinogenesis, yet the clinical manifestations diverge due to the distinct biological context of each tissue. The neural dynamics in gliomas differ markedly from those in gastrointestinal tumors. Gliomas arise from glial cells within the CNS and directly integrate into neuronal networks, forming pseudo-synapses that facilitate tumor progression. This allows gliomas to hijack activity-dependent mechanisms for proliferation and invasion. , In GBM, axonal injury has been identified as an initiating driver of progression. Early glioma cells preferentially colonize white matter, where they induce Wallerian degeneration, an active axonal death program mediated by SARM1. This degeneration amplifies neuroinflammation, recruits reactive glia, and fuels tumor proliferation. Genetic inactivation of SARM1 preserved axonal integrity, slowed tumor evolution, and prolonged survival, highlighting neurodegeneration as a targetable vulnerability in GBM. 
In the gastrointestinal system, where tumors originate from epithelial cells rather than neurons, enteric neurodegeneration contributes to tumor development through indirect mechanisms involving immune suppression, altered epithelial–neuronal crosstalk, and microbial dysbiosis. , , The degeneration of enteric neurons disrupts gut homeostasis and influences local immunity and motility, which may contribute to a protumorigenic environment. Histopathological analyses in patients wih CRC reveal marked atrophy of enteric nerve fibers and myenteric plexuses in tumor-invaded regions, suggesting that the structural degeneration of the ENS accompanies biochemical and immune alterations in promoting malignancy. , Notably, PDAC exhibits profound neuroplasticity and perineural invasion, a feature closely linked to neuropathic alterations within the pancreas. PDAC cells exploit neurotransmitters and growth factors released from degenerating nerves to support tumor survival and spread. , This is further supported by transcriptomic analyses revealing elevated expression of neurotrophic factors such as NGF and BDNF in perineural niches, which enhance tumor–nerve crosstalk and fuel local immune suppression. Moreover, pancreatic sensory neurons undergoing stress or injury release IL-6 and chemokines that recruit regulatory myeloid cells, reshaping the immune milieu to favor tumor progression. These findings highlight how localized neurodegeneration contributes to stromal remodeling in a cancer-specific fashion.
The hepatic system also reflects this bidirectional pathology. In models of hepatic encephalopathy and chronic liver disease, neuroinflammation and neural dysfunction often result from systemic metabolic derangements. Conversely, neurodegenerative signaling cascades such as mitochondrial dysfunction and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress in hepatic cells can promote hepatocarcinogenesis. Mutations in common regulators such as PINK1 and PARKIN, implicated in both PD and hepatocellular carcinoma, provide mechanistic insight into shared vulnerability factors. , Recent evidence also shows that hepatic stellate cells, under chronic neural or inflammatory input, activate fibrogenic and tumorigenic pathways via JAK–STAT signaling, suggesting that hepatic neuroimmune crosstalk can convert chronic injury into a cancer-permissive state. 
Moreover, in the breast and prostate tissues, studies show differential susceptibility depending on neuroendocrine input. Neurodegenerative diseases are sometimes inversely associated with these hormone-responsive cancers. For instance, reduced dopaminergic tone and lower systemic insulin-like growth factor signaling in PD have been associated with decreased breast and prostate cancer incidence. Yet, paradoxically, melanoma shows increased co-occurrence with PD, suggesting that neurodegenerative molecular signatures may also predispose to malignancy under certain systemic or genetic contexts. , In particular, MITF, a transcription factor central to both melanocyte biology and dopaminergic neuron maintenance, may represent a shared vulnerability node. Dysregulation of MITF-related pathways could explain the dual susceptibility in PD and melanoma, linking pigmentation, oxidative stress, and neuronal fate commitment to tumorigenesis. 
Immune-privileged organs such as the eye and testes present further complexity. In ocular neurodegeneration, such as in glaucoma, neuroinflammatory cascades can alter the local microenvironment and promote the oncogenic transformation of glial or retinal cells. Similarly, in testicular tissue, neuroendocrine disruption resulting from hypothalamic degeneration can influence hormonal regulation and potentially modify germ cell tumor risk. , Moreover, the blood–testis barrier, such as the blood–brain barrier, exhibits unique immunological and neuronal regulatory mechanisms that, when perturbed, may enable immune infiltration and oncogenic transformation. In both contexts, local neurons and glia may lose homeostatic control over immune surveillance, contributing to tumor permissiveness. , , , 
The lung-brain axis also illustrates organ-specific interplay. Chronic pulmonary neurodegeneration, often triggered by environmental toxins, can feedforward into neuroinflammatory brain circuits, while small cell lung cancer frequently expresses neuroendocrine markers, reflecting partial neuronal lineage commitment. This shared molecular identity highlights the possibility of common progenitor-like reprogramming events in lung and neural cells. Single-cell analyses of small cell lung cancer (SCLC) reveal transcriptional programs enriched for ASCL1 and NEUROD1, two factors also involved in neuronal differentiation and plasticity, suggesting convergent neuroendocrine reprogramming during tumor evolution. Similarly, exposure to chronic inflammatory stimuli in the respiratory tract can induce neuronal remodeling that propagates to central neural circuits, exemplifying bidirectional neural–epithelial communication. Collectively, these observations underscore the importance of regional and systemic neurodegenerative signals in modulating tissue-specific cancer susceptibility. Dissecting these spatially resolved interactions holds translational promise for both diagnostic stratification and the development of neuroprotective-oncopreventive interventions.
The ENS exhibits heightened susceptibility to cancer-promoting neurodegeneration relative to other peripheral nervous system components due to its unique anatomical, immunological, and microbial interface. Embedded within the gut wall, enteric neurons are in direct contact with intestinal epithelial cells, resident immune populations, and the gut microbiota, forming an exceptionally dense neuroepithelial–immune network that is continuously exposed to microbial metabolites, dietary antigens, and inflammatory cytokines. , Unlike other peripheral nerves, the ENS lacks a well-defined blood–nerve barrier, allowing the rapid infiltration of systemic and luminal inflammatory cues, which heightens its vulnerability to immune-mediated neuronal injury. , Moreover, enteric neurons possess limited regenerative capacity and exhibit region-specific transcriptional signatures that predispose them to stress-induced degeneration. , Neuroinflammatory events, amplified by microbial dysbiosis and epithelial barrier dysfunction, can promote the release of DAMPs and neuropeptides such as CGRP, which in turn reprogram the tumor microenvironment toward immune evasion and stromal remodeling. , These features create a permissive microenvironment in which neurodegeneration, immune suppression, and microbial imbalance converge to promote gastrointestinal tumorigenesis, thereby explaining the preferential involvement of the ENS in cancers such as colorectal and pancreatic cancer. , , , 

## Therapeutic and diagnostic implications of the neurodegeneration-cancer axis
The convergence between neurodegeneration and cancer offers unique therapeutic opportunities grounded in shared mechanisms, namely proteostasis dysregulation, immune remodeling, DNA damage responses, and metabolic imbalance. Rather than organizing interventions by compound class, grouping them by mechanistic action provides a more coherent framework for identifying rational combination strategies, biomarker development, and overcoming tissue-specific delivery barriers.
Impaired proteostasis lies at the heart of both neurodegenerative diseases and malignancies. A compelling example is Pin1, a prolyl isomerase that regulates protein conformation. In neurodegeneration, Pin1 downregulation contributes to tau hyperphosphorylation and amyloidogenesis, while in cancer, it is overexpressed and facilitates cell cycle progression via the modulation of p53 and cyclin D1. Pin1 inhibition, therefore, offers dual therapeutic potential, halting both tumor growth and neurotoxic protein aggregation. , However, due to Pin1’s broad cellular roles, precise dosing and tissue-specific delivery systems are essential to maximize efficacy while minimizing off-target effects.
Immune remodeling is another point of mechanistic convergence. In both contexts, macrophages and microglia can adopt immunosuppressive phenotypes. In GBM, tumor-associated microglia support malignancy, whereas in neurodegeneration, these cells contribute to chronic inflammation. Therapeutic reprogramming of these cells using CSF1R inhibitors such as Pexidartinib or Emactuzumab has shown promise in both settings. In peripheral tumors, such as pancreatic and colorectal cancer, stressed neurons secrete IL-6 and CGRP, polarizing macrophages toward immunosuppressive states. Blocking these signals not only enhances response to immunotherapy but also preserves neural integrity. , , Still, region-specific barriers such as the blood–brain barrier or the enteric glial niche complicate drug delivery, necessitating strategies such as nanoparticle conjugation or regional administration.
Targeting the DNA damage response machinery, particularly CHK1 and CHK2 kinases, offers another avenue for dual-purpose therapies. These checkpoint kinases are essential for cell cycle regulation and DNA repair. In tumors, CHK inhibition sensitizes cells to immunotherapy by promoting immunogenic cell death, while in neurons, dysregulated CHK signaling contributes to degeneration and apoptosis. Moreover, CHK1 inhibition reactivates the STING pathway in dendritic cells, reinvigorating T cell priming, a mechanism relevant in contexts of chronic immune exhaustion, such as in AD or PD. Modulating these kinases, however, must be approached cautiously to avoid unwanted neuronal toxicity, highlighting the need for context-dependent dosing regimens.
The cGAS–STING–STAT3 signaling axis represents a central hub linking innate immune sensing, inflammation, and oncogenesis. In tumors, STING activation supports antigen presentation and cytotoxic T cell recruitment, while in neurodegenerative diseases, sustained STING signaling, especially in microglia, drives chronic inflammation and synaptic dysfunction. Accordingly, spatial and temporal control of this pathway is critical: STING agonists show promise in enhancing antitumor immunity, whereas STING inhibition alleviates neuroinflammation in tauopathy models. , Delivery strategies such as intratumoral injection or biomaterial-assisted release systems are under development to maximize therapeutic benefit while avoiding systemic toxicity.
Mitochondrial dysfunction and redox imbalance are also shared hallmarks of neurodegeneration and cancer. Loss of mitophagy and accumulation of ROS promote both neuronal loss and tumor progression. Therapeutic strategies targeting mitochondrial quality control, particularly through the activation of the PINK1–PRKN pathway, are currently being explored for both disease contexts. , However, because enhanced mitochondrial function may also support cancer cell survival, translational efforts must carefully calibrate dose-response relationships and metabolic context to avoid undesired effects.
Tumor–neural crosstalk via neurotrophic and neurotransmitter pathways provides another therapeutic intersection. Gliomas exploit BDNF–TrkB signaling and glutamate transport to support proliferation and invasion, while these same pathways regulate neuroplasticity and synaptic homeostasis in neurodegenerative diseases. Modulating these pathways may promote neuron survival or inhibit tumor progression depending on the disease setting and dosing regimen. Thus, therapeutic targeting requires temporal and tissue-specific precision to avoid exacerbating either disease.
Complementing these mechanistic approaches, artificial intelligence (AI) and systems biology are accelerating drug discovery across the neurodegeneration–cancer interface. Deep learning models have identified small molecules that reverse shared transcriptional signatures, including regulators of mitochondrial function, oxidative stress, and epigenetic programming. These approaches enable multi-target drug development and mechanistically informed combination therapies. However, successful translation requires rigorous pharmacodynamic validation, including assessments of tissue penetration, toxicity, and therapeutic index.
Several of these targets are currently under clinical investigation. For example, STING agonists such as MK-1454 and GSK3745417 are in early-phase trials for advanced solid tumors, including the phase 1 study of MK-1454 (NCT03010176) and the first-in-human phase 1 trial of GSK3745417 ± pembrolizumab (NCT03843359). While STING inhibition in tauopathy models remains preclinical, these agonists underscore the broader translational relevance of the STING axis. CHK1 inhibitors such as Prexasertib (LY2606368) and SRA737 are under active evaluation. Prexasertib has been studied in phase 1 pediatric and adult trials for solid and CNS tumors (e.g., NCT02808650) and in platinum-resistant ovarian cancer, with durable single-agent activity. , CSF1R inhibitors such as Pexidartinib (PLX-3397) and Emactuzumab (RG-7155) have demonstrated clinical efficacy by modulating tumor-associated macrophages. Pexidartinib has been evaluated in multiple phase 1/2 trials (e.g., NCT01004861), including TGCT and advanced solid tumors, and is FDA-approved for tenosynovial giant cell tumor. , Emactuzumab showed strong phase 1 antitumor activity via TAM depletion in diffuse-type TGCT. Both agents are also being explored in neuroinflammatory/neurodegenerative models, given their ability to modulate microglial activation. Pin1 inhibitors remain in preclinical stages, with mounting evidence of activity in cancer models and tauopathy systems, although no clinical trials are yet registered. Together, these ongoing trials highlight the translational potential of therapeutically targeting shared molecular nodes within the neuro–immune–tumor axis.
From a diagnostic perspective, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers commonly used in neurodegeneration, such as neurofilament light chain (NfL), tau, and amyloid-β, are now being investigated as potential indicators of CNS malignancies. Conversely, cancer-derived exosomal miRNAs and metabolomic signatures may serve as early indicators of neurodegenerative risk, particularly in patients receiving neurotoxic therapies. Furthermore, single-cell RNA sequencing has revealed the expression of neurodegeneration-associated genes such as APOE and TREM2 in tumor-infiltrating immune populations, suggesting their potential as shared biomarkers for neuro-oncologic surveillance. , , 
In addition, enteric neurodegeneration has emerged as a driver of colorectal cancer progression through its impact on epithelial barrier integrity and immune remodeling. Transcriptomic profiling has identified neuron-derived extracellular matrix components, such as Nidogen-1, Biglycan, and Fibulin-2, as candidate biomarkers for neuroinflammation-associated tumorigenesis in the gut. , , These findings underscore the potential of developing tissue-specific diagnostics and therapeutic strategies aimed at preserving neuroepithelial homeostasis.
In summary, the neurodegeneration–cancer axis represents a fertile landscape for dual-action therapies and integrative biomarker platforms. Mechanism-oriented therapeutic design, centered on shared vulnerabilities such as Pin1, STING, STAT3, CHK1/2, and PINK1–PRKN, combined with spatially targeted delivery and dose optimization, holds great promise for simultaneously mitigating neurodegenerative decline and controlling tumor progression.

## Conclusion, limitations, and future perspectives
The long-standing dichotomy between neurodegeneration and cancer is being fundamentally revised. Once considered opposing outcomes of cellular fate, these conditions are now understood to share profound mechanistic convergence, including common disruptions in DNA repair, mitochondrial function, proteostasis, and immune regulation. , , These shared vulnerabilities are synthesized in , highlighting overlapping regulatory nodes and therapeutic targets. 
Spatial transcriptomics and multi-omics approaches have shown that neuronal degeneration, particularly within the ENS, can actively remodel the TME. In CRC, MAPK9+ neurons infiltrate tumors and activate EGFR signaling, driving malignant progression. In PDAC, sympathetic innervation exerts protective effects, while denervation promotes immunosuppressive macrophage infiltration and tumor growth. Across models, injured neurons release cytokines such as IL-6 and CCL2, which recruit MDSCs and TAMs, establishing an immunosuppressive milieu that favors tumor progression. These findings suggest that neurodegeneration is not merely a bystander but a co-driver of tumor evolution.
Evidence from gastrointestinal cancers reinforces this paradigm. Loss of Ndrg4 in enteric neurons enhances the secretion of Nidogen-1 and Fibulin-2, which promote CRC cell migration and proliferation. Similarly, biglycan deficiency alters IL-10 production and microbial composition, particularly Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, creating a tumor-permissive environment. , While compelling, the proposed Biglycan–IL-10–microbiota axis currently rests on a single study and requires validation in independent systems.
At the systems biology level, transcriptomic meta-analyses reveal inverse comorbidity: genes upregulated in neurodegenerative diseases are often suppressed in cancer, and vice versa. These trends are evident in cell cycle regulators such as cyclins and PP2A, which exhibit opposing roles in neuroprotection and oncogenesis. Recent genomic architecture analyses further demonstrate that neurodegeneration and cancer follow shared yet divergent molecular trajectories, suggesting that these conditions may represent alternative outcomes of common regulatory networks influenced by cellular context and microenvironmental cues. Moreover, neuron-derived signals, including neurotrophins, neurotransmitters, and inflammatory mediators, modulate angiogenesis, cancer stemness, and immune suppression in a context-dependent manner shaped by neuron subtype, anatomical location, and chronicity of injury. , , 
Nevertheless, this emerging conceptual framework is not without limitations. A significant proportion of mechanistic insights derive from murine or in vitro models, which often fail to capture the cellular heterogeneity, neuroimmune complexity, and spatial resolution of human tissues. , For instance, the dual role of sympathetic innervation, as tumor-promoting in prostate and breast cancers but protective in PDAC, illustrates the context-specificity that is difficult to model outside of human systems. , Furthermore, epidemiological associations suggesting inverse comorbidity between neurodegenerative diseases and cancer are subject to multiple confounding factors, including mortality bias, treatment exposure, underdiagnosis, and differing healthcare utilization patterns. , Many transcriptomic and biomarker studies also suffer from limited sample sizes, lack longitudinal validation, and are underpowered to detect spatial or temporal effects. Reproducibility is further hindered by inconsistencies in analytical pipelines and the absence of standardized neuroimmune phenotyping across cohorts. Addressing these challenges will require investment in spatial transcriptomics, multiplex imaging, and multi-omic profiling of well-annotated human biobanks.
Despite these constraints, the neurodegeneration–cancer axis presents unique translational opportunities. Molecular targets such as p53, Pin1, PP2A, STING–STAT3, and IL-10–MAPK9 offer promising avenues to modulate shared pathological pathways. , , , , For example, STING agonists are under investigation for potentiating antitumor immunity and ameliorating neuroinflammation in Alzheimer’s disease models, while CSF1R inhibitors show dual capacity to reprogram microglia and TAMs. , 
Moreover, the gut–brain–tumor triad reveals a systemic regulatory network where microbiota-derived metabolites, neuronal signals, and epithelial integrity converge. Interventions such as microbiome-targeted therapies, dietary modulation, chronotherapy, and neuroprotective agents may simultaneously affect neurodegenerative and oncologic pathways. , , , For instance, short-chain fatty acids and tryptophan metabolites influence both neuroinflammation and T cell activation, underscoring microbial metabolism as a critical mediator of immune–neural crosstalk. , However, therapeutic efficacy in this space will likely depend on patient-specific factors, including disease stage, tissue-specific immune tone, and microbiota composition. , , 
In conclusion, this review highlights that neurodegeneration is not merely coincidental or inversely correlated with cancer, but an active and dynamic contributor to tumor progression. Degenerating neurons remodel the TME through the release of cytokines, neuropeptides, and extracellular matrix components that suppress antitumor immunity, promote angiogenesis, and enhance tumor cell proliferation and invasion. Neurodegenerative stress also reprograms shared molecular hubs, such as p53, STING, and Pin1, toward oncogenic phenotypes in a tissue- and context-dependent manner. This rewiring of the neuro–immune–tumor axis fosters immune evasion, stromal remodeling, and therapeutic resistance, particularly in cancers with high neural involvement such as colorectal and pancreatic cancer. Recognizing neurodegeneration as a driver of cancer evolution opens new avenues for diagnostic and therapeutic innovation that target the shared vulnerabilities of these traditionally opposing disease states.
