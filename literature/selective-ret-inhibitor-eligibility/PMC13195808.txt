FeldmanRebeccaPhDConceptualizationData curationFormal analysisWriting - original draftWriting - review & editing Comprehensive molecular profiling (CMP), including whole-exome sequencing (WES), whole-transcriptome sequencing (WTS), and tissue-based assays (e.g., immunohistochemistry [IHC], chromogenic in situ hybridization [CISH], fluorescence in situ hybridization [FISH]), enables identification of multiple biomarkers from a single sample to inform targeted therapy selection. Molecular testing reports, delivered near the time of testing, summarize actionable biomarkers and associated therapeutics to guide treatment decisions for oncologists. However, the static nature of these reports limits their clinical relevance as new biomarkers and therapies emerge.
Advances in molecular interrogation—such as larger gene panels and multi-platform approaches—further increase the likelihood of detecting biomarkers that may become actionable in the future. However, keeping pace with the expanding list of targetable biomarkers and their optimal platforms (WES, WTS, IHC) poses a significant challenge for clinicians. As a result, providers may remain unaware of identified biomarkers that were not previously actionable but have since gained therapeutic relevance. These challenges underscore the need for support tools to ensure that clinical decision making keeps pace with newly approved targeted therapies.
Lookback programs are clinical decision support services designed to address this gap by proactively notifying clinicians when a previously profiled patient may be eligible for new targeted therapies based on biomarkers that were detected in original test results but had not yet been approved for predictive efficacy. Leveraging comprehensive upfront molecular testing that can be retrospectively reevaluated in real time for clinically actionable results, lookback programs may enable patients to remain candidates for newly approved biomarker-driven therapies months, or even years, after the initial report. In this study, we describe the methodology, feasibility, and reach of a lookback program for identifying therapeutic candidates. In addition, we describe the biomarkers, platforms, and agents that the lookback program assessed, and summarize its ability to identify a substantial proportion of patients who become candidates for novel treatment options following new FDA approvals.

## Methods
This study involved retrospective analysis of molecular data collected by Caris Life Sciences between 2018 and 2025. The lookback program is conducted as part of the standard operations of a HIPAA-covered entity and is not designed to contribute to generalizable knowledge. As such, it does not meet the regulatory definition of human subjects’ research under 45 CFR 46, and IRB review or approval is not required.

## Comprehensive molecular profiling and reporting
Tumor samples were submitted to Caris Life Sciences for CMP. All samples undergo WES and WTS. In addition, select IHC assays are performed based on tumor type to provide clinically actionable information or as an add-on service if requested by the ordering provider. Tumor enrichment is performed by manual microdissection. Genomic DNA from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue is sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. WES employs a hybrid capture panel targeting 720 clinically relevant genes at high coverage (>500×) and >20 000 additional genes at lower depth (>250×). Variants are detected with >99% confidence and an analytic sensitivity of 5% variant allele frequency, then classified by board-certified molecular geneticists according to American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines. In addition to variants, predicted HLA alleles and genomic signatures like microsatellite instability, tumor mutational burden, genomic loss of heterozygosity, and others are also reported. Copy number variations are calculated from normalized exon coverage using CNVKIT, with both amplifications and homozygous deletions reported. RNA is extracted from FFPE samples. Libraries are prepared by enrichment of targets through hybridization with a custom bait panel and sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform for gene expression analysis. Clinically relevant fusions and/or splice variants are detected from WTS data using STAR-Fusion. A panel of IHC tests are also performed, depending on tumor type, including ALK, AR, ER, PR, HER2, CLDN18.2, FOLR1, MAGE-A4, MET, MMR, PTEN, and various clones of PD-L1.
An automated, web-based clinical decision support tool integrates aforementioned CMP data with the most current clinical evidence and generates updated information for potential therapeutic guidance in real time. Updates are informed by publicly available sources, including FDA approval notifications and NCCN clinical practice guidelines, , with associated primary studies referenced therein. The results are compiled into a structured PDF report and delivered through multiple mechanisms to ordering oncologist, to support evidence-based treatment decisions.

## Evaluation of new biomarker-directed FDA approvals
Conceptual work on the Caris Lookback Program began in 2018 and was recently formalized with procedures described here. At the time of a new biomarker-directed FDA approval, , the clinical decision support tool is promptly updated to reflect the new biomarker–drug indication. A clinical impact assessment is then conducted to evaluate whether the new indication is a candidate for inclusion in the lookback program. The clinical impact assessment includes a scoring system for multiple inclusion criteria, such as technical feasibility, prior listing of biomarker on reports, novel mechanism of action, new indication for a specific disease, me-too indication, unmet clinical need, and rare alteration ( ). Line of treatment is excluded from assessment because treatment-line information is not consistently available in the retrospective molecular profiling dataset and because the purpose of the program is to identify and communicate potentially relevant newly actionable findings, not to establish definitive treatment eligibility. Scores are tabulated for the new biomarker-drug indication and reviewed with the medical affairs team to adjudicate whether to proceed with the program, and if so, the period for retrospective review. The clinical impact survey ( ) was performed retrospectively on all biomarker-directed FDA approvals since the lookback program began in 2018. The clinical impact survey was developed as an internal prioritization framework to support review of newly approved biomarker–drug indications for potential inclusion in the lookback program but was not designed or validated as a standalone measure of clinical benefit. Although the clinical impact survey was intended to standardize consideration of factors such as novelty of the mechanism, disease-specific significance, and unmet clinical need, it does not employ a predefined cutoff score. Rather, it serves as a criteria-based framework to guide discussion regarding the potential clinical significance of new therapeutic approvals.
 Survey results were reviewed by a medical affairs team composed of pathologists, clinical geneticists, and medical oncologists, and decisions regarding lookback implementation were made through this clinical review process. Profiles are typically selected from cases within the past 12 months, though this varies based on disease prognosis and other factors determined by medical affairs. To respect clinicians’ time while maximizing clinical relevance, the lookback program is initiated only for biomarker–drug approvals with substantial potential to impact patient management. The ultimate decision to include a drug in the lookback program is an evidence-based process that includes clinical insights from the medical affairs team but avoids any conflicts of interest by excluding members in discussions with pharmaceutical partners from the decision-making process. This unbiased approach ensures that outreach is reserved for high-impact opportunities, avoiding unnecessary notifications.

## Lookback and outreach notifications
Once an indication is approved for lookback, existing molecular profiles within the appropriate disease-site context are selected from the database and assessed for relevant biomarker status, in which case alterations may have been previously detected but not highlighted in the original report. Data analytics personnel compile a list of profiles with biomarker status matching that of the newly approved indication which is then shared with the field-based molecular scientists, our molecular science liaison (MSL) team. After a lookback has been initiated for a newly approved drug, the local MSL will send a templated email and letter from medical affairs leadership informing the oncologists of any patients who are potential candidates for the new therapy due to identified biomarker(s). Read receipts are tracked to confirm delivery and MSL log outreach attempts and any physician responses. In some instances, the program will prompt requests for regeneration of the PDF report to include the new therapy association. Notifications are limited to updated FDA approvals and relevant biomarker status but do not include systematic follow-up to request information regarding subsequent clinical decisions. Caris Life Sciences remains neutral throughout the look-back process with a commitment to providing information for clinical relevance and therapeutic awareness.

## Results
Since 2018, a total of 87 biomarker-directed FDA approvals were evaluated for the lookback program ( ). Among new FDA-approved drugs requiring biomarker status for therapy selection, 33 (38%) required assessment by WES, 15 (17%) by WTS, 23 (26%) by IHC, and 16 (18%) by a combination of platforms (7% WES/WTS and 11% WES/IHC) ( ). These approvals encompassed 13 unique proteins, mutations in 39 genes, fusions involving 11 genes, and 3 genomic signatures.
 Clinical impact survey scores ranged from 0 to 8. Of the evaluated biomarker–drug indications, 30 (33%) were advanced into the lookback program ( ), with advanced indications having a higher mean survey score (5 vs. 3 for non-advanced). The medical affairs team screened over 483 000 patient profiles that were eligible for retrospective review (screened patients, ) and identified 13 293 matched patients ( ) that were potential candidates for newly approved agents based on relevant biomarker status in their original molecular test results. These cases span ten distinct tumor types (breast, non–small cell lung cancer [NSCLC], prostate, cholangiocarcinoma, uveal melanoma, colorectal, pancreatic, ovarian cancer, glioma, gastrointestinal stromal tumor, [GIST]) and several tumor-agnostic indications ( ). NSCLC accounted for 40% of all lookback-related approvals, comprising 7 355 approvals from 12 distinct biomarker–drug indications, reflecting the predominance of NSCLC in biomarker-directed therapeutic development over the past several years.
 While most pharmaceutical emails have an open rate of between 18% and 20%, analysis of the emails sent by the MSL team for the lookback program demonstrated a 60% open rate. Customized outreach from the MSL team provides the oncologist with the opportunity to ask a subject matter expert directly for additional education around the new approval.

## Discussion
This analysis highlights the feasibility of a lookback program as a systematic, proactive approach to bridging the gap between evolving biomarker–drug approvals and historical patient molecular profiling data. Since 2018, 87 biomarker-directed FDA approvals were reviewed, with nearly one-third advanced for lookback program notification based on higher clinical impact survey scores and multidisciplinary review. Notably, screening more than 483 000 profiles resulted in identification of over 13 000 patients as potentially eligible for newly approved therapies after their original molecular profiling, which underscores the scale at which actionable therapeutic opportunities can emerge over time. Furthermore, there are many examples of newly FDA-approved drugs with biomarker guidance that did not have a designated CDx test at the time of drug approval. Thus, molecular testing platforms that can be retroactively used to identify emerging targets may enable rapid access for previously tested patients to new treatments, without waiting for additional tests.
The predominance of NSCLC among lookback program-advanced approvals (40%) reflects the rapid pace of novel target-selected therapy, driven by molecular characterization of NSCLC driver alterations such as EGFR (including novel subsets: exon 20 insertions, activating mutations, uncommon), ERBB2 (including novel subsets: TKD), ROS1, RET, and KRAS G12C. While tumor-specific approvals remain a major component of lookback program activity, the inclusion of several tumor-agnostic approvals—such as those targeting NTRK, RET, and BRAF V600E—demonstrates the expanding role of molecular biomarkers in driving treatment decisions across histologies. These findings demonstrate the potential of CMP both at initial diagnosis and as a long-term resource to identify candidates for emerging therapies.
The use of CMP in oncology practice has steadily increased over the last 10 years. While some of the technical obstacles to CMP adoption (ie, high costs, bioinformatics, reimbursement) have been gradually alleviated, the body of evidence for clinical and economic value has grown substantially. , Studies have reported improved outcomes with targeted therapies compared to chemotherapy alone, while as more targeted therapies have emerged, implementation of CMP has increased patient eligibility for approved targeted therapies or clinical trials. Although the scope of the current study does not include clinical or economical outcomes associated with lookbacks, it does further demonstrate the potential of CMP methods to retrospectively capture relevant biomarker information for new approvals, thus “future proofing” the original testing platform.
Unlike targeted panels, which are inherently constrained by the need for a predefined awareness of clinically relevant genes during assay design, WES and WTS capture the coding regions of the exome and transcriptome in an unbiased manner. In this study we demonstrate how this broad approach enables dynamic and ongoing assessment of newly actionable biomarkers from retrospective data, including rare events such as NRG1 fusions, H3F3A or HIST1H3B mutations in diffuse midline gliomas, or HLA typing necessary for new therapeutics available to uveal melanoma and synovial sarcoma ( ). The value of this approach is pronounced for very low-prevalence biomarkers, where the pre-test probability of positivity is low and dedicated single-biomarker assays would require screening large numbers of patients at considerable cost. Following the December 4, 2024, FDA approval of zenocutuzumab for NRG1 fusion–positive NSCLC and pancreatic cancer, we reviewed 22 412 NSCLC and 6 797 pancreatic CMP profiles and identified 57 and 14 NRG1 fusion–positive cases, respectively ( ). In this setting, the ability to leverage existing WTS data through the lookback program may provide additional benefit in rapidly progressive diseases such as pancreatic cancer, where expedited notification of eligible patients is critical. Several actionable genes (such as NRG1, NTRK, RET) are known for promiscuous fusion partners, and the discovery of novel fusion events has increased rapidly along with development of new therapies. , However, due to intronic depth and breakpoint complexity, newly discovered fusions are not uniformly detected by DNA assays or targeted RNA panels but are more reliably detected with comprehensive methods such as WTS. , Other rare alterations such as NTRK1/2/3 fusions are technically challenging for standard targeted assays but are more consistently identified through RNA-based sequencing methods. 
While most CMP reporting is static in nature, retrospective analyses of sequencing data enable access to information previously believed to be research grade that subsequently became clinically actionable following regulatory approval. When workflows such as these are fully integrated, the team obtains support when new approvals occur. Proactive identification of eligible patients and subsequent outreach may facilitate translation of new evidence into patient care by ensuring that new therapeutic opportunities are not overlooked or delayed. Coordinated support may help to reduce time and operational burden for oncologists and care teams who may otherwise be required to search for previous test results or order additional testing, while patients may be considered sooner for new agents and spared the discomfort of additional testing and associated costs. , The potential to avoid repeat testing may also spare payors the additional expenses associated with facilitation of additional testing and biopsy procedures or care for complications resulting from procedures or delays.
There are several limitations to this analysis. The clinical impact survey was developed as an operational prioritization tool for this program and has not undergone formal external validation. Although the lookback program currently excludes biopharma and commercial partners from the adjudication process to reduce potential commercial bias, we acknowledge that broader external validation of the framework would improve transparency and generalizability.
Another significant limitation is the lack of clinical outcomes data associated with lookback notifications, which limits conclusions about clinical benefit. By design, the lookback program is inherently limited to notifications of updated biomarker-drug indications to practicing oncologists who submitted the original samples for analysis. However, there is no follow-up to gather information on further therapeutic decisions or resultant clinical outcomes, as the goal is to support the oncologist by efficiently providing potentially useful information regarding updates in FDA guidelines.
The number of patients identified through the lookback program represents potentially eligible patients based on biomarker status and disease context, not confirmed treatment candidates. Furthermore, implementation of provided updated therapeutic options may be declined for a significant number of patients due to various factors. In particular, line of treatment, interval clinical status, prior therapies, comorbidities, and other patient-specific factors were not uniformly available for retrospective review and may substantially reduce the number of patients ultimately appropriate for treatment.
There may also be other barriers to proper implementation of optimal therapeutic guidance with available molecular data. A recent large-scale study of NSCLC patients who underwent CMP reported that 29.2% of patients did not receive appropriate targeted therapies despite receiving molecular test results. Thus, the most apparent benefit of lookback programs may be to ensure that lack of up-to-date information is not the limiting factor in such cases. Another lookback program was reported by a network of oncologists (OneOncology) with access to follow-up information on subsequent therapies. Although this study did not report the percentages of identified candidates from patients screened for eligibility of new therapeutics, they did report that among 194 patients whose primary oncologists were notified, 15% of identified candidates received updated therapies.
Support for wider implementation of lookback programs could be strengthened by the inclusion of associated outcomes data for demonstration of clinical value and economic impacts, including costs of additional procedures, retesting, ineffective therapies, and care for associated adverse events. However, as the goal of the program involves providing unbiased information to the treating oncologist, requiring extensive clinical data from a case report form may interfere with the practicing oncologist’s workflow and potentially limit the impact of the program by reducing participation. Future efforts may involve collaboration with qualified oncology institutions engaged in precision medicine, which may not only generate evidence to support the clinical and economic benefits of lookback programs but also may further refine the methodology through increased participation and feedback.
Finally, while the program currently focuses on U.S. FDA approvals, lookback programs could be expanded to incorporate consensus guideline updates and other regulatory bodies to support global clinical practice. Although providers may request an updated report to include new therapy associations, future directions should include real-time push notifications and integration with electronic health record (EHR) systems to automate and streamline communication, prospective evaluation of patient outcomes following notifications, and assessment of the time from new drug approval to provider alert. Expansion of the program to include non-FDA regulatory approvals, such as new NCCN additions or emerging biomarkers with expansive clinical trial availability, could also further broaden its impact.
In conclusion, the lookback program represents a scalable model for ensuring that eligible patients might benefit from ongoing advances in precision oncology, even years after their initial molecular profiling. With an expanding number of biomarkers used for guiding cancer therapy, the use of both WES and WTS can increase the chances of capturing relevant information for patients and providers in a single test while also capturing many as yet unapproved biomarkers. By coupling comprehensive profiling with continuous evidence surveillance and proactive provider engagement, lookback programs may help to close the gap between therapeutic innovation and clinical application by providing timely information, supporting the ultimate goal of improving patient access to emerging targeted therapies.
