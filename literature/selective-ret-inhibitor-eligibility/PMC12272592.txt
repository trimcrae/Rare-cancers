RET inhibitors are used to treat thyroid cancer with rearranged during transfection (RET) gene alterations; however, the emergence of drug resistance challenges effective treatment. Therefore, we aimed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying resistance to the RET inhibitor, selpercatinib. We used the papillary carcinoma cell lines, TPC-1 and CUTC48, which harbor the CCDC6-RET fusion gene. We established a selpercatinib-resistant subline (TPC-1/SELR) in TPC-1 via long-term exposure to selpercatinib. The growth-inhibitory effects of the drugs were analyzed using the WST assay. Changes in intracellular signal transduction were analyzed using western blotting. The 50 % inhibitory concentration (IC50) of selpercatinib in TPC-1 cells was 3 nM; however, growth inhibition in TPC-1/SELR and CUTC48 cells did not reach 50 % even at concentrations >100 nM. TPC-1/SELR cells showed increased phosphorylation of EGFR, ERK, AKT, and mTOR compared with that in TPC-1 cells. In CUTC48 cells, the phosphorylation of EGFR and ERK did not increase, whereas that of AKT and mTOR did. We investigated the growth-inhibitory effects of everolimus, an mTOR inhibitor, and found that TPC-1, TPC-1/SELR, and CUTC48 cells were highly sensitive to the drug. Our results suggest the involvement of AKT-mTOR pathway activation in selpercatinib resistance in thyroid cancer and that inhibition of the AKT-mTOR pathway may have therapeutic potential to overcome selpercatinib resistance.

## Highlights
•Some selpercatinib-resistant thyroid cancers show enhanced AKT-mTOR pathway activation.
•AKT-mTOR pathway may be involved in selpercatinib resistance of thyroid cancers.
•Selpercatinib-resistant thyroid cancers are sensitive to mTOR inhibitor everolimus.
•Inhibiting the AKT-mTOR pathway may overcome selpercatinib resistance.

## Introduction
Thyroid cancer is one of the most prevalent endocrine malignancies, and its global incidence is increasing [ ]. Thyroid cancer has a high frequency of driver gene mutations, which are candidate therapeutic targets. Recently, precision oncology, in which molecularly targeted drugs are selected based on genetic mutations, has been developed for treating thyroid cancer [ ]. A large proportion of medullary thyroid carcinomas (MTCs) and a subset of papillary thyroid carcinomas (PTCs) are characterized by mutations or fusions in the rearranged during transfection (RET) proto-oncogene. These genetic alterations drive oncogenesis through constitutive activation of RET signaling pathways, making RET an attractive target for therapeutic interventions [ ].
Selpercatinib is a highly selective RET kinase inhibitor that has demonstrated significant clinical efficacy in patients with RET-mutant MTC and RET fusion-positive thyroid cancer. By selectively inhibiting RET kinase activity, selpercatinib effectively blocks downstream signaling pathways essential for tumor cell proliferation and survival [ ]. Clinical trials have reported the efficacy of selpercatinib in patients with advanced RET-altered thyroid cancers, and it has already become the standard of care for those patients [ ]. However, despite the initial promise of selpercatinib, many thyroid cancers eventually develop resistance to the drug, limiting its long-term efficacy. In addition, some thyroid cancers harboring RET alterations demonstrate resistance to selpercatinib at treatment initiation.
To date, mutations in the G810 residue on the solvent-front side of RET have been reported as an on-target resistance mechanism to selective RET inhibitors in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) with RET fusion genes and MTC with RET mutations [ , ]. However, no reports have been made regarding on-target resistance to selective RET inhibitors in PTC. As an off-target resistance mechanism, NRAS, KRAS, or BRAF mutations have been reported in RET fusion gene mutation-positive NSCLC and PTC resistant to selective RET inhibitors [ , ]. This suggests an involvement of the activation of molecules downstream of the signal transduction pathway via RET or the activation of other pathways, such as the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, which plays a crucial role in various cellular functions, in the emergence of resistance to RET inhibitor [ ].
Thus far, little is known about the mechanisms underlying selpercatinib resistance in thyroid cancer. Understanding these mechanisms is critical for improving therapeutic outcomes and devising strategies to overcome resistance [ ]. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the mechanisms that promote selpercatinib resistance in thyroid cancer cells.

## Materials and methods
2.1

## Cell lines and reagents
We used the PTC cell lines, TPC-1 and CUTC48, both of which harbor the CCDC6-RET fusion gene. TPC-1 was provided by Dr. Yamashita during his tenure at Nagasaki University (Nagasaki, Japan) [ ]. CUTC48 was provided by Dr. Schweppe from the University of Colorado Cancer Center (Aurora, CO, USA) [ ]. TPC-1 was cultured in RPMI-1640 (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), and CUTC48 cultured in D-MEM (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), both supplemented with 10 % FBS at 37 °C under 5 % CO2. The selpercatinib-resistant subline (TPC-1/SELR) was established by exposing parental TPC-1 cells to gradually increasing concentrations of selpercatinib over a period of more than six months. Following this selection period, single-cell-derived clones were isolated using the limiting dilution method, in which cells were diluted and seeded at low density to ensure that each well of a 96-well culture plate contained, on average, a single cell. Several selpercatinib-resistant clones were obtained, and we selected one clone that exhibited stable proliferation and could be serially passaged for use in subsequent experiments. TPC-1/SELR was maintained in medium containing 100 nM selpercatinib throughout the study. To analyze the biology of cancer cells upon acquisition of sufficient selpercatinib resistance, selpercatinib was removed 24 h before each assay. Selpercatinib and everolimus were purchased from Selleck Biotech Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan).

2.2

## WST assay for cell proliferation
Growth-inhibitory effects of the drugs were analyzed using a tetrazolium salt-based proliferation assay (WST assay; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) [ ]. Each experiment was performed independently in triplicate.

2.3

## Western blotting
Proteins were isolated from cells, as previously described, and used for western blotting analyses (10 μg/lane) [ ]. Membranes were probed with various antibodies ( ). Protein bands were visualized using ChemiDoc XRS (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Tokyo, Japan), and protein levels corresponding to each band were quantified based on band intensity using ImageJ software (version 1.53 k; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda MD, USA).

2.4

## DNA sequence analyses
Gene sequence analyses of TPC-1, TPC-1/SELR, and CUTC48 cells were performed by DNA Chip Research Inc. (Tokyo, Japan). All analyses were performed using an Agilent analysis system.

2.5

## Statistical analysis
Statistical significance was assessed using unpaired Student's t-tests or one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.2.3 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA), and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results
3.1

## Establishment of a selpercatinib-resistant thyroid cancer cell line
To analyze the mechanism underlying selpercatinib resistance in thyroid cancer cells, we established a selpercatinib-resistant strain using TPC-1 cells. Through stepwise drug selection followed by the limiting dilution method, we established several selpercatinib-resistant strains, one of which (TPC-1/SELR) was used in subsequent experiments. The resistant strain exhibited a significantly reduced growth inhibition by selpercatinib (p < 0.01) and showed approximately 68-fold higher resistance to selpercatinib than the parental TPC-1 cells ( ). 
In contrast, CUTC48 cells showed less than 50 % growth inhibition, even during exposure to 100 nM selpercatinib, and demonstrated 500 times more selpercatinib resistance than TPC-1 cells. Therefore, we considered CUTC48 cells to be de novo resistant to selpercatinib and used them in subsequent experiments as a representative thyroid cancer cell line exhibiting such resistance.
Subbiah et al. reported that the 50 % inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of selpercatinib for cells with RET on-target mutations that exhibited resistance to the drug were 149.8–2744.0 nM [ ]. Moreover, the IC50 values measured for TPC-1/SELR (185.1 nM) and CUTC48 (1375.0 nM) were consistent with the results of previous studies.

3.2

## Expression and phosphorylation of signal transduction-related proteins in TPC-1, TPC-1/SELR, and CUTC48 cells
We analyzed the expression and phosphorylation of signaling molecules in TPC-1, selpercatinib-resistant TPC-1/SELR, and de novo selpercatinib-resistant CUTC48 cells in the steady state using western blotting. Although both TPC-1 and CUTC48 harbor CCDC6-RET fusion genes, RET phosphorylation was significantly more reduced in TPC-1/SELR (p < 0.05) and CUTC48 (p < 0.01) than in TPC-1 ( ). TPC-1/SELR exhibited a tendency toward increased phosphorylation of EGFR, ERK, AKT and showed significantly increased mTOR phosphorylation (p < 0.05) compared to TPC-1. 
In CUTC48, phosphorylation of EGFR was significantly lower than that in TPC-1 (p < 0.01); however, the phosphorylation of AKT and mTOR was significantly higher than that in TPC-1 (p < 0.05, p < 0.01). Increased AKT and mTOR phosphorylation was observed in both TPC-1/SELR and CUTC48 cells.

3.3

## Expression and phosphorylation of signal transduction-related proteins after 24 h of selpercatinib treatment
Next, we used western blotting to analyze alterations in the expression and phosphorylation of signaling molecules in TPC-1, TPC-1/SELR, and CUTC48 cells after selpercatinib treatment. In TPC-1, 24 h after the addition of selpercatinib, RET, MEK, ERK, AKT, and mTOR phosphorylation was significantly suppressed in a dose-dependent manner (p-RET; p < 0.05, p-MEK, p-ERK, and pAKT; p < 0.01). Thus, selpercatinib inhibited the signal transduction of both MAPK and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways in TPC-1 cells ( ). 
In contrast, although RET, MEK, and ERK phosphorylation was significantly suppressed in the selpercatinib-resistant TPC-1/SELR cells (p-RET; p < 0.05, p-MEK and p-ERK; p < 0.01), similar to the inhibitory effect observed in the parental TPC-1 cells, the phosphorylation of AKT and mTOR, which was enhanced in the TPC-1/SELR cells compared with that in TPC-1 cells in the steady state, was maintained at a high level after selpercatinib treatment.
In CUTC48 cells, selpercatinib inhibited RET phosphorylation in a dose-dependent manner; however, inhibition of MEK and ERK phosphorylation was not substantial. In addition, the phosphorylation of AKT and mTOR was maintained at high levels after selpercatinib treatment. In both TPC-1/SELR and CUTC48 cells, phosphorylation of the AKT-mTOR pathway was maintained after selpercatinib treatment.

3.4

## Everolimus sensitivity of TPC-1, TPC-1/SELR, and CUTC48 cells
As an increase in phosphorylation of the AKT-mTOR pathway was observed in selpercatinib-resistant thyroid cancer cells, we investigated the growth-inhibitory effects of the mTOR inhibitor, everolimus, in these cells ( A). The IC50 values for everolimus were 1.0 ± 0.4 nM for TPC-1, 2.7 ± 2.0 nM for TPC-1/SELR, and 12.2 ± 1.0 nM for CUTC48. Although the sensitivity of selpercatinib-resistant cells to everolimus was lower than that of TPC-1 cells, the difference in relative resistance ratios was considerably smaller than those observed for selpercatinib, and no significant difference was observed. The relative resistance ratios of TPC-1/SELR and CUTC48 when compared with TPC-1 were 2.7 and 12.2, respectively. 

3.5

## Expression and phosphorylation of signal transduction-related proteins after 24 h of everolimus treatment
Everolimus showed substantial growth-inhibitory effects as a single agent against selpercatinib-resistant thyroid cancer cells. Therefore, we analyzed the effect that everolimus treatment has on the expression and phosphorylation of signaling molecules in these cells ( B). At 24 h after administration, everolimus inhibited mTOR phosphorylation in a dose-dependent manner in TPC-1, TPC-1/SELR (p < 0.05), and CUTC48 cells. An increase in AKT phosphorylation was observed in all cells tested 24 h after the administration of 1 nM everolimus.
Everolimus treatment inhibited RET phosphorylation in TPC-1 and TPC-1/SELR cells and suppressed MEK and ERK phosphorylation in TPC-1/SELR cells. In contrast, although EGFR phosphorylation was inhibited in CUTC48 cells, MEK and ERK phosphorylation levels did not change.

3.6

## Genetic sequence analysis of thyroid cancer cell lines
As TPC1/SELR and CUTC48 cells showed high levels of selpercatinib resistance, we performed genetic analysis to examine whether any alterations occurred in the RET fusion gene within these cells. The nucleotide sequence of the CCDC6-RET gene in TPC-1/SELR cells was identical to that of the parental TPC-1 cells. CUTC48 cells also harbored the CCDC6-RET fusion gene, and its sequence was identical to that of the corresponding gene in TPC-1 cells (data not shown). These results ruled out the possibility that structural changes in the target molecule conferred selpercatinib resistance to TPC1/SELR and CUTC48 cells.

## Discussion
Resistance to molecularly targeted agents, including selpercatinib, is a complex process involving multiple factors. In this study, both selpercatinib-resistant TPC-1/SELR and de novo selpercatinib-resistant CUTC48 cells showed elevated AKT and mTOR phosphorylation levels. Furthermore, everolimus, an mTOR inhibitor, inhibited the growth of these cells. These results suggest that activation of the AKT-mTOR pathway may be one of the mechanisms involved in selpercatinib resistance in thyroid cancer cells harboring the RET fusion mutation.
RET inhibitors are mainly used to treat lung and thyroid cancers. To date, genetic analyses of patients who underwent treatment and became resistant to RET inhibitors have been reported more frequently in patients with lung cancer than in those with thyroid cancer. With regard to on-target resistance mechanisms against kinase inhibitors targeting RET, the sensitivity to multi-kinase inhibitors, such as vandetanib and cabozantinib, is known to be impaired by gatekeeper mutations, such as V804 L/M, in RET [ , ].
Selective RET inhibitors (selpercatinib and pralsetinib) have been designed to overcome gatekeeper RET V804 M/L mutations [ , , ]. However, mutations in the G810 residue of RET, which is located on the solvent-front side of the C-lobe, have recently been observed and referred to as solvent-front mutations (SFMs) [ , ]. SFMs have been observed in patients with NSCLC with CCDC6-RET or KIF5B-RET fusion genes and in those with MTC with RET M918T and V804 M double mutations who have become resistant to RET inhibitors; however, this mutation has not been reported in patients with PTC.
Contrastingly, as an off-target resistance mechanism, RET-mutant tumors are thought to acquire resistance to RET inhibitors through activating oncogenic pathways other than RET and its downstream signaling pathways. We observed that lenvatinib-resistant thyroid cancer cell lines showed increased phosphorylation of EGFR, which is not a target of lenvatinib [ ]. In NSCLC with RET fusion gene mutations, oncogenic activation by NRAS mutations has been reported when the cells become resistant to a RET inhibitor [ ]. Moreover, concomitant mutation of driver genes, such as BRAF, KRAS, and NRAS, have been reported in PTC with RET fusion gene mutations; however, whether they are present in “the same cell” or “the different clones of cells in the same tumor” has yet to be assessed [ , ].
In our study, we found no on- or off-target mutations in the two CCDC6-RET fusion gene-positive PTC cell lines that were resistant to selpercatinib. Although no gene mutations were observed, constitutive activation of the AKT-mTOR pathway was observed in selpercatinib-resistant cells established from TPC-1 and in native CUTC48 cells, which are de novo resistant to selpercatinib. Hence, selpercatinib resistance was likely conferred to these thyroid cancer cells through activating molecules that were not targeted by selpercatinib. Recently, Hu et al. reported that TPC-1 cells that survived after long-term culture in the presence of a RET inhibitor did not show any mutations in the CCDC6-RET gene but showed increased expression of Aurora A/B kinases [ ]. Their study results also supported the possibility that resistance to RET inhibitors can occur because of the activation of proliferation pathways that are not directly affected by the driver gene.
Although selective kinase inhibitors have already become a major treatment modality for many cancers, such off-target effects have been reported to induce drug resistance. Off-target effects occur when an inhibitor interacts with unintended kinases or signaling pathways, activating compensatory mechanisms in cancer cells. This adaptation can reduce the effectiveness of a drug and contribute to the development of resistance over time [ , ].
Hence, a strategy that uses a combination of selective kinase inhibitors to inhibit compensatory pathways and the activation of potential escape mechanisms when using a single selective inhibitor has already become one of the standard therapies for several malignancies, including colorectal cancer, breast cancer, and melanoma [ ]. In thyroid cancer, combination therapy with BRAF and MEK inhibitors has been approved for tumors positive for the BRAF V600E mutation, and in particular, significantly improves the overall survival of anaplastic thyroid cancer [ ].
In the present study, we demonstrated that cell lines resistant to the RET inhibitor, selpercatinib, are highly sensitive to the mTOR inhibitor, everolimus. Gild et al. reported that some medullary and differentiated thyroid cancer cells with RET mutations are sensitive to mTOR inhibitors [ ] and that the growth-inhibitory effect of RET inhibitors combined with mTOR inhibitors may be enhanced in these cells. In this study, both thyroid cancer cell lines acquired resistance to selpercatinib, and the cell line that was de novo resistant to selpercatinib showed significant sensitivity to everolimus. These results suggest that if it is possible to efficiently suppress the molecules activated in the signal transduction pathway using a single agent, it may not always be necessary to use a combination of selective targeted drugs.
However, our study observed increased Akt phosphorylation in all cell lines after everolimus administration. When everolimus inhibits mTORC1, S6K1 activity decreases and insulin receptor substrate-1 suppression is lifted, leading to the phosphorylation of PI3K and Akt [ ]. This feedback mechanism might have occurred in the thyroid cancer cell lines we had used in this study.
The limitations of this study are that we did not precisely analyze the interaction of the MAPK and AKT-mTOR pathways; moreover, we did not conduct in vivo studies. We plan to conduct these analyses in the future.
In the present study, we showed for the first time that the AKT-mTOR pathway is activated in RET fusion gene-positive thyroid cancer cell lines with acquired or de novo selpercatinib resistance. In the near future, treatment strategies targeting driver gene mutations are expected to become more common for advanced thyroid cancer. Suppressing the activation of alternative signaling pathways from treatment initiation using a molecularly targeted agent may lead to new treatment strategies that can delay the emergence of resistance to the primary therapeutic agent.
