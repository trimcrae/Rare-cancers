## Abstract
Comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) is implemented to detect actionable gene aberrations and design matched therapies. Although malignant thoracic tumors are commonly detected through respiratory endoscopy, it is questionable whether the small specimens obtained thereof are sufficient for CGP. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the suitability of respiratory endoscopy for sampling primary and metastatic thoracic tumors for CGP. Patients whose specimens were collected through respiratory endoscopy and assessed by pathologists to determine their suitability for CGP at our institution between June 2019 and May 2022 were reviewed retrospectively. The suitability of each procedure as a sampling technique for CGP and, in the cases actually analyzed, the distribution of the detected gene aberration were assessed. In total, 122 patients were eligible for analysis; the median age was 61 (range, 29–86) years, and 71 (58.2%) patients were male. Primary intrathoracic tumors were found in 91 (74.6%) cases, including 84 (68.9%) primary lung cancers; the remaining thoracic metastases of extrathoracic origin included various types. The suitability rates of specimens obtained using conventional bronchoscopy with and without cryobiopsy, endobronchial ultrasound‐guided transbronchial needle aspiration, and medical thoracoscopy were 82.8% (24/29), 70.4% (19/27), 72.9% (35/48), and 100% (18/18), respectively. Of the 96 cases judged suitable, 83 were subjected to CGP, and all but one were successfully analyzed. Finally, 47 (56.6%) patients had at least one actionable gene aberration and eight (9.6%) were treated with the corresponding targeted therapies. In conclusion, specimens obtained through respiratory endoscopy are suitable for CGP; medical thoracoscopy and cryobiopsy in conventional bronchoscopy are particularly useful.

The findings of this study demonstrate that specimens obtained through respiratory endoscopy are suitable for comprehensive genome profiling in the population including primary intrathoracic tumors and thoracic metastases of extrathoracic origin. In particular, medical thoracoscopy and conventional bronchoscopy involving cryobiopsy have proven to be useful, with suitability rates of 100% (18/18 cases) and 82.8% (24/29 cases), respectively.

## Suitability rate for CGP

The suitability rates for CGP of specimens obtained using conventional bronchoscopy, EBUS‐TBNA, and medical thoracoscopy are shown in Figure  , along with the number of cases sampled using each sampling procedure. Of the 56 conventional bronchoscopy cases, four were sampled for endobronchial lesions under direct visualization, while the other 52 were sampled for PPLs. The overall suitability rate was 76.8% (43/56). When conventional bronchoscopy cases were subdivided according to whether cryobiopsy was performed, the suitability rate for cases with cryobiopsy was 82.8% (24/29) compared with 70.4% (19/27) for those without cryobiopsy (p = 0.35). The next most frequent procedure was EBUS‐TBNA in 48 cases, with a suitability rate of 72.9% (35/48). Eighteen medical thoracoscopy cases were reported and confirmed to be suitable (100%).
The results of the pathologists' confirmation of the suitability of the specimens are shown in Table  , divided by the area of the section and the proportion of tumor cell content. Compared with the results of all 122 cases in the upper row, the reduced number of suitable cases in the lower row (i.e., 96 cases) represents the distribution of unsuitable cases. This indicates that all cases with a tumor cell content of less than 20% and most cases with an area of less than 4 mm2 were deemed unsuitable. In nine cases, the tumor cell content was low, and the area of the section was large enough to require macrodissection/microdissection.
 The results in Table  are further subdivided for each procedure and presented in Table  . In conventional bronchoscopy without cryobiopsy, the smallest sectional area (<4 mm2) was the most common reason for deeming the case to be unsuitable. In contrast, the area of the section was generally larger in cases with cryobiopsy but still the smallest of the four cases. EBUS‐TBNA was characterized by the highest tumor cell content (≥50%) in most cases; however, a few cases were deemed unsuitable because of the small sectional area. Medical thoracoscopy combined a large sectional area with high tumor cell content.

## Proportion of cases harboring actionable gene aberrations and targeted therapy
An overview of the results of the CGP tests is shown in Figure  . Of the 96 cases judged to be suitable for CGP tests, 83 were submitted: 77 for NCC Oncopanel and six for F1CDx. CGP was successful in all but one NCC Oncopanel case of malignant melanoma sampled via forceps biopsy from a PPL. The detected gene aberrations in the remaining 82 cases are shown in Figure  . In addition to the most frequent TP53 mutation found in 53 (64.6%) cases, various other mutations were identified at each frequency.
 Finally, 47 (56.6%) of the 83 patients had at least one actionable gene aberration. The breakdown is shown in Figure  , where EGFR and KRAS mutations were the most frequently detected mutations (12 and 10 cases, respectively). ## DISCUSSION
The present study focused on the suitability of respiratory endoscopy as a sampling technique for CGP. Most previous reports on CGP focused on its results and the proportion of patients who received targeted therapies thereafter. In these reports, the types of specimens that had been submitted to CGP were seldom limited and comprehensively included small specimens collected using forceps or needles as well as large surgical specimens. , , Although a few reports mentioned specific sampling methods and devices for NGS, including CGP, none had investigated respiratory endoscopy as a whole. , , , Our results revealed that the suitability rates of conventional bronchoscopy and EBUS‐TBNA for CGP were moderate at 76.8% and 72.9%, respectively, whereas that of medical thoracoscopy was perfect at 100%. Moreover, in patients who underwent conventional bronchoscopy, the suitability rate was higher with cryobiopsy than without (82.8% vs. 70.4%). These cases included not only primary lung cancer but also metastatic thoracic malignancies. Thus, this study provides valuable insights into the suitability of biopsy specimens obtained by respiratory endoscopy for CGP.
Specimens collected using EBUS‐TBNA are often reported to be suitable as representative bronchoscopic specimens for NGS. , Turner et al. reported that EBUS‐TBNA specimens were suitable for CGP, with a success rate of 86.1%. Although the requirements for CGP are different and therefore cannot be precisely compared with those of the previous study, the success rate of EBUS‐TBNA specimens for CGP in our study was 100% (35/35 cases); however, approximately 27% of the specimens were unfit for CGP. Both the area of the section and the proportion of tumor cells are important for performing CGP, as shown in Tables  and . Most EBUS‐TBNA specimens had sufficient tumor cell content (≥30%), but the area of the section was relatively small. We believe that the tumor cell content was higher with EBUS‐TBNA than with other sampling methods for three reasons. First, when TBNA is performed, the stromal component is less likely to be aspirated, resulting in a greater likelihood of cellular components in the specimen. Second, red blood cells, which are frequently contaminated, are not included in the population of the tumor cells and the evaluated area of the section itself as they are not nucleated cells (Figure  ). Third, the specimens were collected via real‐time echography, thus preventing the contamination of specimens outside the lesion. In addition, the specimens were processed in this study in accordance with routine medical practice; in most cases, each collected specimen was placed in a separate formalin bottle, resulting in insufficient sample volumes for CGP.
 To overcome this limitation, we recently introduced IFB, an additional forceps biopsy to be performed after EBUS‐TBNA, and devised methods to combine multiple IFB specimens in a single formalin bottle. Once a tract for inserting forceps is created, specimens can be collected and processed more quickly than with TBNA, and sample volumes can be secured by bringing the specimens together. We encountered some cases in which additional IFB specimens led to successful CGP (Figure  ). More recently, the newly developed transbronchial mediastinal cryoscopy, in which a cryoprobe is inserted, has been reported to provide a high diagnostic yield similar to that of EBUS‐TBNA, as well as a large specimen collection. , This may be a promising method for obtaining samples suitable for CGP in the near future.
Among the EBUS‐TBNA specimens, the suitability rate for CGP was higher in cases of primary intrathoracic tumors than in those with thoracic metastases of extrathoracic origin (77.1% vs. 61.5%). This discrepancy may have resulted from differences between the purpose of respiratory endoscopy and the specimens obtained according to the attending physicians and endoscopists. Especially in primary lung cancers, a sufficient volume of specimens is routinely collected for NGS assay, whereas in metastatic tumors of other malignancies, the emphasis is often on a definitive diagnosis unless specific instructions are given. As a result, some cases have failed to take advantage of the potential for CGP sampling. Therefore, close communication between attending physicians and endoscopists about the purpose of the procedure is important. It is also necessary to educate attending physicians about the utility of respiratory endoscopy for CGP.
In patients who underwent conventional bronchoscopy, specimens obtained by cryobiopsy were more suitable for CGP than those obtained using forceps or aspiration needles. Udagawa et al. reported differences in specimens obtained using forceps and cryobiopsy in relation to precision medicine for lung cancer. Cryobiopsy resulted in larger specimens compared with forceps (15 vs. 2 mm2), a larger proportion of definite histomorphological diagnoses (86% vs. 74%), and a larger yield from DNA and RNA extraction. In addition, unlike other biopsy methods, cryobiopsy allows circumferential biopsy and, when combined together, the advantages may contribute to the success of CGP (Figure  ). Udagawa et al. also reported that the median amount of DNA was 1.60 μg and the success rate of whole‐exome sequencing was 90%. Additionally, they also reported that lower amounts of DNA were extracted from the forceps specimens than from the cryobiopsy specimens (0.58 vs. 1.60 μg), which corroborated the lower amounts of DNA extracted after conventional bronchoscopy without cryobiopsy compared with those extracted after conventional bronchoscopy with cryobiopsy in the present study (735 vs. 943 ng). However, as represented in Table  and Figure  , the minimum amount of extracted DNA after conventional bronchoscopy with cryobiopsy was as low as that without cryobiopsy. The possible reasons for this discrepancy are as follows: (i) the group representing conventional bronchoscopy with cryobiopsy included some cases in which forceps specimens were used to evaluate the suitability for CGP because the cryobiopsy specimens were collected from outside the tumor, and, (ii) to measure the amount of DNA extracted, multiple forceps specimens were sometimes combined, in contrast with the single specimens used for cryobiopsy. Because our study focused on the suitability rates for CGP, a direct comparison with previously reported success rates was not appropriate. Please note that because the specimens deemed to be unsuitable for CGP were not subjected to CGP, our success rate using cryobiopsy specimens was 100%. Nevertheless, even with cryobiopsy, five cases were judged to be unsuitable for CGP as specimens were collected from outside the lesion in all of them. These cases may have influenced the results, thus precluding a statistically significant difference between the conventional bronchoscopy cases with and without cryobiopsy. Although the proportion of tumor cell content may be reduced by the presence of a certain amount of non‐neoplastic tissues in cryobiopsy specimens, it can be controlled to some extent through macro/microscopic dissection as long as tumor tissues can be collected, because the specimens usually have a clear boundary between normal lung parenchyma and tumor tissues. Therefore, it is important to perform cryobiopsy on target lesions; however, conventional 1.9‐mm cryoprobes are thick and stiff and have poor operability in PPLs. New, thinner 1.1‐mm and 1.7‐mm cryoprobes that are easier to operate and have good diagnostic performance for PPLs, respectively, have recently been introduced. , Their replacement will make cryobiopsy sampling more suitable for CGP in the future.
 Medical thoracoscopy enabled sampling with both large sizes and high tumor cell content, leading to a perfect suitability rate for CGP. Because no studies have reported the suitability rates or success rates of CGP with thoracoscopic specimens, we found it difficult to compare our data with data from other studies. The sampling device used in thoracoscopy is forceps, as in bronchoscopy; however, it is often possible to peel off the pleura along with the disseminated tumor cells and obtain large tissue specimens. In some cases, this peeling technique allows for the collection of large amounts of tissue, comparable with cryobiopsy. Additionally, patients who undergo thoracoscopy usually have pleural effusion, which can be collected and sent for cell block preparation, as observed in one of the cases investigated in the present study. The pleural effusion from this case was rich in tumor cells and the cell block prepared from the effusion was successfully subjected to CGP. However, the diagnostic yield of cytology for malignant pleural effusion has been reported to be low, at nearly 60%, and varies according to the tumor type: 6.1% for malignant mesothelioma, 56.0% for lung cancer, and 82.0% when limited to lung adenocarcinoma. Therefore, both pleural biopsy and pleural effusion collection should be considered whenever possible. Medical thoracoscopy can be performed even in patients with minimal pleural effusion by creating an artificial pneumothorax. 

In general, the pathological evaluation of the suitability for CGP based on the area of the section and the proportion of tumor cell content was adequate, with corresponding results in all but one case. In contrast, only eight of 83 patients (9.6%) received targeted therapies according to the results, a lower rate than previously reported, which ranged from 10.5% to 13.4%. , This may be mainly because approximately two‐thirds of the study population had primary lung cancer, for which various driver oncogenes and corresponding targeted therapies have already been introduced into clinical practice. In Japan, because CGP is covered by insurance after the completion of standard therapies, the more approved targeted therapies for known gene aberrations there are, the more difficult it becomes to identify unknown therapeutic targets. In fact, five patients with lung cancer who had previously been treated with targeted therapies were included, whereas only one patient with breast cancer was included outside of lung cancer.
This study had some limitations. First, this study was conducted retrospectively at a single cancer center. Second, not all specimens obtained via respiratory endoscopy during the designated period were evaluated for their suitability for CGP. In other words, most cases with small specimen sizes and/or small numbers of tumor cells described in the pathology report were not considered for CGP in the first place. Consequently, specimens for CGP were not collected at the time of diagnosis in most of these cases, making it impossible to achieve a general evaluation. Nevertheless, it cannot be denied that the suitability of CGP may have been somewhat overestimated. Therefore, to accurately evaluate the efficacy of respiratory endoscopy for CGP, a multicenter prospective cohort study that specifies various items, such as the purpose of the procedure, diagnostic accuracy, submission for CGP, and subsequent treatment is desirable.
In conclusion, specimens obtained through respiratory endoscopy are suitable for CGP; in particular, medical thoracoscopy and cryobiopsy in conventional bronchoscopy are useful. Respiratory endoscopy would be useful not only for primary intrathoracic tumors represented by NSCLC but also for thoracic metastases of extrathoracic origin.
