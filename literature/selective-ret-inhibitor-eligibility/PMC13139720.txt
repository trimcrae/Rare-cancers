## ABSTRACT
Molecular testing has emerged as a pivotal tool for the preoperative assessment of cytologically indeterminate thyroid nodules. In this cross‐sectional study, we evaluated the diagnostic utility of a targeted next‐generation sequencing (NGS) 4‐gene panel, including BRAF

V600E
, TERT promoter mutations, RET fusions, and NTRK3 fusion, for enhancing the cytological diagnosis of thyroid nodules prior to surgical intervention. A total of 827 thyroid nodules subjected to fine‐needle aspiration and subsequent histopathological confirmation were analyzed, among which 773 (93.5%) were classified as malignant or noninvasive follicular thyroid neoplasm with papillary‐like nuclear features (NIFTP). The observed prevalence of molecular alterations was: BRAF

V600E
, 68.3% (526/770); TERT promoter mutations, 10.3% (79/770); RET fusions, 10.3% (79/770); and NTRK3 fusion, 3.9% (30/770). Notably, the 4‐gene NGS panel demonstrated brilliant diagnostic performance for indeterminate cytological nodules (Bethesda categories III–V), achieving a sensitivity of 87.9%, specificity of 96.3%, positive predictive value (PPV) of 99.7%, negative predictive value (NPV) of 35.9%, and overall accuracy of 88.2%. These findings indicate that the targeted NGS 4‐gene panel provides high diagnostic precision in distinguishing benign from malignant nodules. Its implementation offers a cost‐effective, efficient molecular diagnostic strategy that may reduce unnecessary diagnostic procedures and facilitate optimized clinical management.

This multicenter study of 827 thyroid nodules demonstrates that a targeted NGS 4‐gene panel (BRAF^V600E, TERT promoter, RET fusions, NTRK3 fusion) achieves high diagnostic accuracy (88.2%) and PPV (99.62%) for cytologically indeterminate Bethesda III–V nodules, offering a cost‐effective first‐line molecular test in China.

## Introduction
Thyroid nodules represent one of the most common clinical challenges in endocrinology, with a markedly rising incidence over recent decades. This trend is largely attributable to the widespread use of ultrasonography in routine health examinations [ ]. Although ultrasound‐guided fine‐needle aspiration (UG‐FNA) cytology serves as the cornerstone initial diagnostic tool, which significantly improves the differentiation between benign and malignant lesions, it still presents considerable diagnostic limitations [ ]. Approximately 10% ~ 40% of thyroid nodules are classified into indeterminate cytologic categories (Bethesda III, IV, and V), which complicates clinical management due to their heterogeneous malignant potential [ , , , ].
Given these diagnostic challenges, molecular testing has been increasingly integrated as an adjunct to cytological evaluation to improve diagnostic accuracy and guide clinical management. Advances in genetic profiling have led to the identification of numerous actionable mutations in thyroid carcinogenesis, particularly within mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) and phosphatidylinositol 3‐kinase (PI3K) signaling pathways, such as B‐Raf proto‐oncogene, serine/threonine kinase (BRAF)
V600E
, Rat sarcoma (RAS) point mutations, rearranged during transfection (RET), and telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) promoter mutations [ ].

BRAF

V600E
 mutation is highly prevalent in papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) and is often correlated with aggressive clinicopathological features. However, single‐gene testing for BRAF

V600E
 alone exhibits limited sensitivity and limited application in risk stratification of PTC, particularly in carcinomas with subtle nuclear features and high‐grade features, underscoring the need for broader multi‐gene panel testing [ ].
Recent studies have emphasized the superiority of multi‐gene next‐generation sequencing (NGS) panels, which demonstrate higher sensitivity and specificity compared to single‐gene assays, particularly in the evaluation of cytologically indeterminate nodules. Panels such as ThyroSeq and others enable simultaneous detection of a broad spectrum of genetic alterations, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy and potentially decreasing unnecessary surgeries [ , ]. Although these assays have shown promising results in Western populations, thyroid cancer‐related genetic alterations may differ geographically and across different races, limiting the clinical application of these assays in China [ ].
Molecular profiling has become a cornerstone in the evaluation of thyroid nodules, providing not only diagnostic but also prognostic insights. Beyond the well‐characterized BRAF

V600E
 mutation, alterations such as TERT promoter mutations and oncogenic fusions, including ETV6::NTRK3 and RET rearrangements, have been consistently associated with tumor aggressiveness and adverse clinical outcomes. Recent studies have introduced the concept of molecular risk groups (MRG) [ ], in which the coexistence of BRAF

V600E
 and TERT promoter mutations defines a high‐risk category (MRG‐high) [ , , ]. Tumors in this subgroup exhibit markedly increased risks of recurrence and distant metastasis compared with cases harboring either alteration alone. Incorporating MRG stratification into routine molecular testing may therefore enhance risk prediction and facilitate individualized management strategies for patients with thyroid carcinoma.
In this study, we applied an NGS‐based molecular panel to a multicenter Chinese patient cohort, detecting key genetic alterations including BRAF

V600E
 mutation, TERT promoter mutations (C228T and C250T), and ETV6::NTRK3 fusion, CCDC6::RET fusion, and NCOA4::RET fusion. This study aims to determine the diagnostic accuracy of this targeted molecular assay for cytologically indeterminate thyroid nodules and to refine the clinical management strategies of thyroid lesions.

## Materials and Methods
2.1.

## Study Design and Study Population
This cross‐sectional multicenter study analyzed a cohort of 827 patients with thyroid nodules who underwent UG‐FNA biopsies across four tertiary medical centers in China. The study period spanned from April 2023 to September 2024. Inclusion criteria comprised [ ] adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) with thyroid nodules [ ] availability of comprehensive clinical, cytopathological, and molecular profiling data; and [ ] definitive histopathological confirmation following surgical resection. Exclusion criteria involved cases with insufficient NGS data or unverified postoperative histological diagnoses.
A total of 956 patients with thyroid nodules underwent UG‐FNA during the study period. After exclusion of 129 patients due to inadequate FNA samples (n = 42), incomplete molecular test data (n = 58), or lack of histopathological verification (n = 29), 827 patients were included in the final analysis (Figure  ).
 
2.2.

## Sample Collection and Molecular Analysis
Sample collection and molecular testing at the four participating centers were conducted in strict accordance with each hospital's standardized operating procedures (SOPs). Nucleic acids were extracted from 300 μL of the residual homogenized cell suspension from cytology smears using a nucleic acid extraction kit (Shanghai RIGEN Biotech Co. Ltd.; Catalog No.: RJ002T). The extracted nucleic acids were quantified, and their quality was assessed according to the quality control criteria specified in the RIGEN NGS 4‐Gene Thyroid Cancer Detection Kit (Catalog No.: RJ014J).
Samples fulfilling the quality requirement were subjected to analysis using the RIGEN NGS 4‐Gene Detection Kit. All experimental steps, including library preparation, sequencing reaction setup, and bioinformatic processing, were carried out in strict compliance with the manufacturer's protocols. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NextSeq 550Dx platform (Illumina Inc.), and all included sequencing data satisfied the quality thresholds defined by the detection kit.
To ensure methodological consistency and minimize inter‐center technical variation, all participating centers utilized the same batch of extraction kits (which included internal controls) and detection reagents supplied by RIGEN Biotech for identical sample types. This standardization guaranteed uniform testing procedures and analytical criteria throughout the study.
All samples were tested using NGS technologies to detect gene alterations, following rigorous SOPs. The NGS 4‐gene panel was designed to detect key DNA point mutations—specifically BRAF

V600E
 and TERT promoter mutations (C228T and C250T), as well as RNA‐level fusion events, including CCDC6::RET (Exon1‐Exon12), NCOA4::RET (Exon8‐Exon12), and ETV6::NTRK3 (Exon4‐Exon14). The gene targets were selected based on their established relevance in the genomic landscape of thyroid cancer, particularly in Chinese and broader Asian populations [ , , ].

2.3.

## Study Outcomes
The primary outcome of this study was the diagnostic performance of the NGS multigene panel, evaluated through sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and overall accuracy in distinguishing malignant thyroid nodules. Emphasis was placed on nodules with indeterminate cytology (Bethesda categories III–V). The definitive diagnostic reference standard was the final histological diagnosis obtained from surgical resection specimen.

2.4.

## Statistical Analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages. The performance of the molecular test was evaluated based on sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, accuracy, and Youden's index. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to assess overall diagnostic performance. A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

3.

## Results
3.1.

## Patient Cohort and Baseline Characteristics
A total of 827 patients who underwent UG‐FNA and subsequent surgical resection across the four participating centers between April 2023 and September 2024 were included in the final analysis (Figure  ). Baseline demographic and clinicopathological characteristics of the cohort are summarized in Table  . The median age of the patients was 47 years (interquartile range: 37–57), and 71.7% (593/827) were female. Based on cytopathological evaluation using the Bethesda System for Reporting Thyroid Cytopathology (TBSRTC), the nodules were classified as: Bethesda II (1.45%), III (16.69%), IV (4.84%), V (55.02%), and VI (22.01%). Final histopathological examination confirmed 54 (6.53%) benign cases, 3 (0.36%) noninvasive follicular thyroid neoplasm with papillary‐like nuclear features (NIFTP), and 770 malignant cases (93.11%), including 751 PTC, 5 follicular thyroid carcinomas (FTC), 13 medullary thyroid carcinomas (MTC), and 1 anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (ATC). Among the 751 PTC, the classical PTC was the most common subtype (230 cases, 30.63%), followed by follicular variant (5.99%), tall‐cell subtype (0.13%), solid variant (0.13%), and other rare subtypes.
 
3.2.

## Mutation Landscape of Thyroid Nodules
The genetic alteration profiles of benign, NIFTP, and malignant thyroid nodules are summarized in Table  and Figure  . Among malignant nodules, BRAF

V600E
 mutation was the most prevalent alteration, detected in 526 of 770 cases (68.31%), accounting for 69.91% of all PTCs. TERT promoter mutations—including both C228T and C250T hotspots—were identified in 10.26% of malignant nodules. Gene fusions involving RET (CCDC6::RET, 7.01%; NCOA4::RET, 3.25%) and ETV6::NTRK3 (3.90%) were also observed. Neither BRAF nor TERT promoter mutations were detected in benign lesions or NIFTP (Figure  ).
 In addition, among the 770 malignant samples, 35 cases (4.55%) were identified with concurrent BRAF

V600E
 and TERT promoter mutations, corresponding to the MRG‐high subgroup. Although less frequent than isolated BRAF

V600E
 mutations, the presence of these co‐mutations delineates a biologically distinct and clinically aggressive subset of thyroid cancers (Figure  ).
 
3.3.

## Diagnostic Performance in Indeterminate Nodules (Bethesda III–V)
Comparing with single BRAF

V600E
 test, NGS 4‐Gene test exhibits higher diagnostic performance in cytologically indeterminate thyroid nodules (Bethesda III‐V) (Table  ), with a sensitivity of 87.84% (95% CI: 84.96–90.23), specificity of 95.12% (95% CI: 83.86–98.65), PPV of 99.62%, and NPV of 35.14%, along with a Youden index of 0.83. Comparable trends were observed for the other three genes when evaluated individually: each provided incremental contributions to overall sensitivity, although their relatively low prevalence limited their standalone diagnostic utility.
 
3.4.

## Diagnostic Utility Across Malignancy Prevalence Scenarios
As shown in Figure  , we modeled the expected positive and negative predictive values (PPV and NPV) of single BRAF

V600E
 test and the NGS 4‐gene panel across varying disease prevalence. According to the 2023 TBSRTC, the risk of malignancy (ROM) for Bethesda III–V nodules ranges from 22% to 74% [ ]. Within this prevalence spectrum, the single BRAF

V600E
 test, with 68.1% sensitivity and 100% specificity, consistently yielded a PPV of 100%, whereas its NPV declined substantially from 91.3% at 22% ROM to 50.9% at 74% ROM.
In contrast, the four‐gene panel – with 87.8% sensitivity and 95.1% specificity–achieved NPVs of 96.5% and 73.3% at the lower and upper prevalence bounds, respectively. Over the same range, its PPV increased from 83.5% to 98.1%.

## Discussion
The present multicenter cross‐sectional study investigated the clinical utility of a targeted NGS 4‐gene panel (BRAF

V600E
 mutation, TERT promoter mutations, RET fusions, and ETV6::NTRK3 fusion) in the molecular diagnosis of thyroid nodules. Our findings provide evidence that the 4‐gene panel, which represents the most frequently mutated and therapeutically relevant alterations in PTC, can deliver high diagnostic accuracy in real‐world practice. Although formal cost‐effectiveness analyses were not performed in this study, the restricted design of a focused panel covering the most clinically actionable alterations suggests potential economic advantages by reducing unnecessary extensive sequencing or repeat diagnostic procedures. Thus, such a streamlined molecular test may represent a practical and resource‐conscious option for the evaluation of thyroid nodules with indeterminate cytology.
The molecular profile observed in this cohort aligns closely with prior reports, with BRAF

V600E
 identified as the predominant driver mutation in PTC, occurring in approximately 70% of cases. This prevalence is highly concordant with rates reported in large‐scale studies of PTC within the Chinese population, which document a frequency of 71% [ ].
Furthermore, RET fusions (10.52%) and TERT mutations (10.52%) emerged as the second most prevalent genetic alterations in PTC, consistent with prior reports. Xing et al., documented a TERT promoter mutation frequency of 11.3% [ ], while Liang et al., reported RET fusions in 8.45% of cases [ ]. Among fusion genes in thyroid cancer, NTRK fusions rank second only to RET fusions, with ETV6::NTRK3 being the predominant subtype [ ]. In our cohort, ETV6‐NTRK fusions were identified in 3.99% of PTC cases, comparable to the 3.1% incidence previously reported in China [ ].
The NGS 4‐gene panel delivers high diagnostic accuracy for Bethesda III–V thyroid nodules, with a sensitivity of 87.84%, specificity of 95.12%, PPV of 99.62%, and NPV of 35.14%. By comparison, the BRAF

V600E
 test exhibited limited diagnostic value, with reduced sensitivity (66.05%) and low NPV (16.94%), thereby restricting its clinical applicability, particularly in excluding malignancy in indeterminate nodules. In our cohort, although the NPV of the NGS panel was relatively modest (35.14%), its PPV remained consistently high (99.62%). According to the 2023 TBSRTC, the ROM for Bethesda III–V nodules ranges from 22% to 74%, which can represent the prevalence of thyroid cancer in patients with thyroid nodules in China [ ]. When assessed across this prevalence spectrum, the NGS 4‐gene panel demonstrated notable adaptability: its NPV improved from 96.5% at lower prevalence to 73.3% at higher prevalence, while its PPV increased from 83.5% to 98.1%. The consistently high PPV (99.62%) provides a reliable basis for surgical decision‐making in test‐positive patients, thereby reducing unnecessary diagnostic procedures. This feature is particularly valuable in healthcare systems with limited resources, where precise preoperative risk stratification is essential.
The detection rate of MRG‐high tumors (BRAF

V600E
 & TERT promoter co‐mutation) was 4.55%, aligning with published studies that report a relatively low but clinically meaningful prevalence between 3.5% and 5% [ , ]. Meta analysis study has shown that the BRAF&TERT promoter co‐mutation portends more aggressive behavior, higher recurrence risk, and worse outcomes compared with either alteration alone [ ]. Integrating MRG into molecular diagnostic workflows could therefore guide risk assessment and clinical management of thyroid carcinoma. Larger, multi‐center cohorts with long‐term follow‐up are warranted to validate these applications.
A key strength of our approach is its optimal balance between diagnostic performance and cost‐effectiveness. This principle is supported by a recent global cost analysis in advanced non‐small cell lung cancer, which demonstrated that NGS‐based panels become more cost‐effective than single‐gene testing when the number of required biomarkers exceeds, with NGS achieving per‐patient cost reductions of up to 26% in practice scenarios [ , ]. While comprehensive NGS panels—such as ThyroSeq and Afirma Genomic Sequencing Classifier (GSC)—interrogate over 100 genes, their high cost will limit accessibility, especially in resource‐constrained settings. In contrast, our NGS 4‐gene panel could reduce economic burden while maintaining high clinical utility, which is more adaptable for high‐volume testing in China and other developing regions.
Furthermore, the incorporation of the most common three gene alterations indicating targeted therapy significantly enhances its clinical value in making personalized treatment strategies [ , ]. Although RET and NTRK fusions are relatively infrequent (approximately 14.4% in our cohort), they represent critical biomarkers for predicting responses to FDA‐approved targeted agents, including darabinib (a BRAF inhibitor), selpercatinib (a RET inhibitor), and entrectinib (an NTRK inhibitor) [ ]. The current NGS 4‐gene panel reflects the growing emphasis on using molecular profiling to guide clinical management of advanced thyroid carcinoma.
While NGS 4‐gene panel demonstrates strong diagnostic performance, its focused design inherently presents certain limitations. As a targeted assay, it may fail to detect less common oncogenic alterations typically identified by comprehensive NGS panels. In our cohort, 12.5% of histologically confirmed malignancy was 4‐gene wild type. These limitations underscore the importance of adopting a tiered diagnostic approach. The NGS 4‐gene panel may serve as a cost‐effective first line diagnostic tool, while more comprehensive molecular testing could be reserved for challenging or mutation‐wild type cases requiring further risk stratification.
Based on our findings, we recommend implementing a stepwise molecular diagnostic algorithm. The NGS 4‐gene panel should be served as the initial test for all Bethesda III–V nodules, given its high sensitivity and specificity, as well as its advantage in guiding clinical management of PTC. For 4‐gene wild‐type cases with worrisome ultrasonic features, close follow‐up and re‐biopsy are recommended. If necessary, larger NGS panels or RNA‐based classifiers should be rendered as a second‐tier test to identify rare driver alterations.
This study is a large multicenter cohort of 4‐gene NGS analysis using thyroid FNA samples with rigorous histopathological verifications, which provides a clinically relevant evaluation of the panel's performance in real‐world diagnostic settings in China. Nevertheless, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross‐sectional study design induces potential biases, including surgical selection bias and incomplete follow‐up. Second, although the panel demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy in Bethesda III–V nodules, the true false‐negative rate may be underestimated, as only a few 4‐gene wild type cases underwent surgical resection and histopathological verification is unavailable. Third, as a targeted assay, the panel does not include certain rare but clinically significant alterations such as ALK fusions, TP53 mutations or AKT1 [ , , ]. Finally, long‐term follow‐up of 4‐gene wild type lesions is necessary to more accurately assess the panel's wild type predictive value in clinical practice.

5.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, our study demonstrates that the cost‐effective targeted four‐gene panel achieves high diagnostic accuracy in cytologically indeterminate thyroid nodules. Its consistently high PPV provides a reliable basis for recommending surgery in cases with those most common genetic alterations, thereby avoiding unnecessary diagnostic interventions and supporting more rational use of clinical resources. Although rare alterations may not be detected, its role as a practical first‐line molecular test is well justified. Reflexive testing with broader NGS panels and future prospective studies are warranted to further optimize evidence‐based diagnostic pathways for thyroid nodules.
