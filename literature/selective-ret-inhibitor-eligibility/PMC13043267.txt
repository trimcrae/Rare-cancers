## Abstract
Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) is a critical driver gene in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Since 1997, EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs) have evolved from first-generation agents, such as gefitinib, erlotinib, and icotinib, to second-generation agents like afatinib and dacomitinib, now to third-generation agents, including osimertinib, aumolertinib, furmonertinib, befotertinib, rezivertinib, rilertinib, limertinib, lazertinib, mifanertinib for EGFR L858R, sunvozertinib for EGFR exon 20 insertion (20ins), and zorifertinib for EGFR-sensitive mutation with brain metastases. Throughout this evolution, EGFR-TKIs have become the cornerstone of treatment for EGFR-mutant NSCLC, extending beyond advanced stages to encompass the entire disease spectrum, including perioperative and maintenance therapies, with combination therapies further expanding treatment options. These advancements have significantly improved patient survival and quality of life. However, challenges such as acquired resistance remain significant hurdles in achieving long-term disease control. Over the past 30 years, substantial advancements have been made in the comprehensive management of EGFR-mutant NSCLC. This systemic review provides the history of the development of EGFR-TKI therapy for NSCLC from 1997 to 2026, highlighting clinical milestones, emerging therapies, and future directions in this rapidly evolving field.
Keywords: Non-small cell lung cancer, EGFR mutation, EGFR-tyrosine kinase inhibitor, Targeted therapy, Combination therapy, Acquired resistance

## Introduction
In 2022, lung cancer accounted for 2,480,301 new cases and 1,817,172 deaths, making it the leading cause of cancer incidence and mortality worldwide.[ ] Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is the predominant histological subtype, comprising approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases.[ , ] Historically, traditional chemotherapy was the mainstay of treatment for patients with advanced NSCLC, but it provided limited survival benefits. Standard platinum-based chemotherapy typically yields a median overall survival (OS) of only 10–12 months in advanced-stage patients.[ , ] Targeted therapy and immunotherapy have revolutionized the treatment of solid tumors.[ , ] A major breakthrough in NSCLC treatment came with the discovery of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) as a critical therapeutic target. EGFR, also known as human epidermal growth factor receptor (HER) 1 or cellular erythroblastosis virus oncogene homolog 1 (c-erbB-1), is a transmembrane receptor belonging to the v-erb-b erythroblastic leukemia viral oncogene homolog (ErbB) family of receptor tyrosine kinases, and it plays a critical role in regulating key cellular processes, including growth, differentiation, and survival. Upon ligand binding, EGFR undergoes dimerization and autophosphorylation, initiating several intracellular signaling pathways, most notably, the rat sarcoma virus oncogene (Ras)–rapidly accelerated fibrosarcoma oncogene (Raf)–mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway and the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)–protein kinase B (PKB) pathway—that promote cellular proliferation and inhibit apoptosis.[ , , ] Classical sensitive EGFR mutations, which occur in 80%–90% of all EGFR-mutant cases, predominantly involve exon 19 deletions and exon 21 L858R point mutation. The prevalence of EGFR mutations shows significant geographic variability, with much higher frequencies in Asian populations (40–60%) compared to Western populations (10–15%).[ , , , , ] Significant progress has been made in the development of new drugs globally over the past two decades, particularly in the field of lung cancer.[ , , ] EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have become the cornerstone of first-line therapy for advanced NSCLC patients harboring EGFR-sensitive mutations, with multiple generations of EGFR-TKIs now available, including gefitinib,[ , ] erlotinib,[ ] icotinib,[ ] afatinib,[ ] dacomitinib,[ ] osimertinib,[ , ] aumolertinib,[ ] furmonertinib,[ ] befotertinib,[ ] rezivertinib,[ ] rilertinib,[ ] limertinib,[ ] lazertinib,[ ] mifanertinib,[ ] sunvozertinib,[ ] and zorifertinib.[ ] These EGFR-TKIs have dramatically improved survival outcomes compared to the chemotherapy era. Third-generation EGFR-TKIs further improved survival outcomes compared to first- and second-generation EGFR-TKIs. Furthermore, an increasing body of evidence supports the use of EGFR-TKIs in adjuvant, neoadjuvant, maintenance and combination treatment settings, expanding the therapeutic landscape and providing more personalized treatment options for NSCLC patients.[ , , , , , ] Despite these advances, resistance to EGFR-TKIs remains an inevitable and formidable challenge. Numerous resistance mechanisms have been identified, including secondary EGFR mutations, mesenchymal-epithelial transition factor (MET) amplification, and histologic transformation, etc.[ , , , , , , ] Ongoing research continues to uncover novel insights into these resistance pathways, which in turn are driving the development of next-generation therapeutic strategies aimed at overcoming resistance and improving the efficacy of EGFR-TKIs. This systemic review provides the history of the development of EGFR-TKI over the past 30 years from 1997 to 2026, highlighting clinical milestones, emerging therapies, and future directions in this rapidly evolving field [Figure ].
 

## Milestones in Establishing EGFR as a Therapeutic Target
Epidermal growth factor (EGF) was first identified by Dr. Stanley Cohen in the 1960s.[ , , ] In 1975, human EGF (hEGF) was successfully isolated from urine in Dr. Cohen’s laboratory.[ , , ] In the same year in England, Dr. Gregory isolated a substance called urogastrone from urine, which was unexpectedly found to be identical to hEGF in both chemical structure and biological activity.[ ] In 1978, Dr. Stanley Cohen firstly reported EGFR,[ , ] and later in 1980 discovered that EGFR has protein kinase activity dependent on adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and EGF, and undergoes phosphorylation itself.[ , ] In the middle 1980s, a series of studies found that EGFR expression is increased in many types of epithelial tumors, and this typically correlates with a worse prognosis.[ , , , , ] In the 1980s, Dr. John Mendelsohn and Dr. Gordon Sato hypothesized that monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) could block EGF from binding to EGFR, thereby inhibiting receptor activation and preventing cell proliferation.[ ] Stanley Cohen and Rita Levi-Montalcini shared the 1986 nobel prize for physiology or medicine for their research on substances produced in the body that influence the development of nerve and skin tissues and discovery of a bodily substance that stimulates and influences the growth of nerve cells, respectively.

## EGFR-TKIs Monotherapy for Unresectable NSCLC

## First-generation EGFR-TKIs
In chemotherapy era, the prognosis for advanced NSCLC patients remains exceedingly poor. Chemotherapy primarily offers symptomatic relief and limited survival benefits, with a median OS of approximately 6 months for advanced NSCLC patients receiving platinum-based regimens.[ ] The prognosis is especially dismal for patients with progressive disease following one or more lines of chemotherapy, with a median OS of just 16 weeks and a 1-year survival rate of only 16%.[ ] Consequently, there is an urgent need for the development of more effective therapeutic agents for NSCLC.

## Gefitinib
In 1997, ZD1839 (gefitinib), developed by AstraZeneca plc (London, UK), was selected for clinical development.[ ] Preclinical studies have demonstrated that ZD1839 effectively inhibits downstream signaling pathways of the EGFR, induces cell cycle arrest, and inhibits angiogenesis in a dose-dependent and reversible manner.[ , , , ] In the late 1990s, several phase 1 trials demonstrated that ZD1839 was well-tolerated, with favorable anti-tumor activity.[ , , , ] On October 2, 2000, and November 7, 2000, the pivotal clinical trials of the first EGFR-TKI gefitinib (ZD1839, Iressa®)—IDEAL 1 and IDEAL 2—enrolled their first patient, respectively. These trials were instrumental in demonstrating its efficacy as a targeted therapy. In IDEAL 1, gefitinib at doses of 250 mg and 500 mg was administered to NSCLC patients who had received one or two prior chemotherapy regimens, including at least one platinum-based treatment. The overall response rate (ORR) was 18.4% and 19.0%, respectively, with a median OS of 7.6 and 8.0 months.[ ] These findings led to gefitinib’s approval by the Japan Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) on July 5, 2002, for advanced NSCLC treatment. Meanwhile, IDEAL 2, conducted in the United States (U.S.), showed comparable results. Patients who had undergone at least two prior chemotherapy regimens demonstrated ORR of 12.0% and 9.0% at gefitinib doses of 250 mg and 500 mg, respectively, with median OS of 6.5 and 5.9 months.[ ] Based on these findings, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved gefitinib on May 5, 2003 for advanced NSCLC. In 2004, landmark studies found that EGFR mutations are more prevalent in women, adenocarcinoma, and non-smokers. EGFR was discovered to be predictive biomarkers for response to gefitinib, highlighting the importance of molecular profiling in optimizing EGFR-TKI treatment.[ , , , , ] Following the success of IDEAL 1 and IDEAL 2, several additional clinical trials were conducted, including pivotal registration trial for Chinese patients, the Iressa Survival Evaluation in Lung Cancer (ISEL), Second-line Indication of Gefitinib in NSCLC (SIGN), V-15-32, Iressa NSCLC Trial Evaluating Response and Survival versus Taxotere (INTEREST), and IRESSA as Second-line Therapy in Advanced NSCLC—KoreA (ISTANA). In the pivotal registration trial for Chinese NSCLC patients who progressed after chemotherapy, gefitinib demonstrated an ORR of 27%, disease control rate (DCR) of 54.1%, median progression-free survival (PFS) of 3.2 months, and median OS of 10 months,[ ] leading to its approval by the China State Food and Drug Administration (SFDA) for post-line advanced NSCLC treatment on December 6, 2004. The ISEL study, which evaluated gefitinib as a second- or third-line treatment for patients with locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC, did not show a significant survival benefit over placebo, with median OS of 5.6 months vs. 5.1 months (hazard ratio [HR] 0.89 [95% confidence interval {CI}: 0.77–1.02], P = 0.087). However, subgroup analyses indicated potential benefits in non-smokers (median OS 8.9 vs. 6.1 months, HR 0.67 [95% CI 0.49–0.92], P = 0.012) and Asian patients (median OS 9.5 vs. 5.5 months, HR 0.66 [95% CI 0.48–0.91], P = 0.01).[ ] Despite these subgroup findings, the ISEL study’s overall lack of efficacy led to gefitinib was relabeled by the FDA for restricted use in patients already receiving it and obtaining a clinical benefit after the publication of the ISEL study’s results in December, 2004. On August 26, 2010, the FDA requested that AstraZeneca voluntarily withdraw gefitinib from the market. On February 1, 2011, AstraZeneca requested that the FDA withdraw approval of gefitinib, which AstraZeneca characterized as a business decision, effective September 30, 2011. The SIGN[ ] and V-15-32[ ] studies failed to demonstrate the non-inferiority of gefitinib compared to chemotherapy (docetaxel), while the INTEREST[ , ] and ISTANA[ ] studies showed non-inferiority in NSCLC patients previously treated with platinum-based regimens. There remains no definitive consensus on whether gefitinib or second-line chemotherapy offers superior outcomes for NSCLC patients, as study’s results have been inconsistent. Importantly, subgroup analyses hint at specific populations, such as women, patients with adenocarcinoma, and non-smokers, who may derive greater benefit from gefitinib. The exploration of gefitinib as a first-line treatment has been challenging. A phase 2 trial in Japan evaluated gefitinib monotherapy in chemotherapy-naïve patients with advanced NSCLC without EGFR mutation status selection and reported an ORR of 30% with a median OS of 13.9 months. Notably, all four patients with EGFR mutations achieved partial response (PR). Nevertheless, 10% of patients developed fatal interstitial lung disease (ILD), which was deemed unacceptable.[ ] Later phase 2 trials confirmed gefitinib’s efficacy in chemotherapy-naïve patients with EGFR mutations, showing ORRs of 75–78% and median PFS of 8.9–9.7 months.[ , , ] The Iressa Pan-Asia Study (IPASS) established that gefitinib was superior to carboplatin plus paclitaxel as a first-line treatment in selected East Asian patients with advanced pulmonary adenocarcinoma who were non-smokers or former light smokers. The 12-month PFS rate was 24.9% for gefitinib and 6.7% for carboplatin plus paclitaxel (HR 0.74 [95% CI 0.65–0.85], P <0.001). Among EGFR mutation-positive patients, gefitinib demonstrated a longer PFS compared to carboplatin plus paclitaxel (HR 0.48 [95% CI 0.36–0.64], P <0.001). Conversely, in the EGFR mutation-negative subgroup, carboplatin plus paclitaxel yielded a longer PFS (HR 2.85 [95% CI 2.05–3.98], P <0.001). The IPASS trial solidified EGFR mutations as a strong predictor of better outcomes with gefitinib.[ ] Based on the results from the IPASS study, the SFDA approved gefitinib for the first-line treatment of patients with metastatic NSCLC with EGFR-sensitive mutations on November 9, 2010. Despite the First-SIGNAL[ ] study showing no OS benefit of gefitinib as first-line therapy in non-smokers with lung adenocarcinoma, the WJTOG3405[ ] and NEJ002[ ] studies confirmed longer PFS with gefitinib in EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients. The phase 4 IRESSA follow-up measure (IFUM) study demonstrated that first-line gefitinib was effective and well tolerated in Caucasian patients with EGFR mutation-positive NSCLC. The ORR is 69.8%, DCR is 90.6%, median PFS is 9.7 months, and median OS is 19.2 months.[ ] Based on the results of IFUM study, the FDA approved the first-line treatment indication of gefitinib for patients with metastatic NSCLC with EGFR-sensitive mutations on July 13, 2015.

## Erlotinib
Erlotinib (OSI-774, Tarceva®) is another first-generation EGFR-TKI co-developed by Pfizer Inc. (New York, U.S.) and OSI Pharmaceuticals Inc. (New York, U.S.). Erlotinib inhibits the phosphorylation of the EGFR in a dose-dependent, concentration-dependent, and reversible manner, leading to cell cycle arrest and induction of apoptosis.[ , , ] In 2001, the BR.21 study was conducted to compare the efficacy and safety of erlotinib with that of placebo in patients with NSCLC who had experienced failure of standard chemotherapy. The results demonstrated that erlotinib improved OS compared to placebo in patients with stage IIIB or IV NSCLC who had received one or two prior chemotherapy regimens. The median OS was 6.7 months vs. 4.7 months, respectively (HR 0.70 [95% CI, 0.58–0.85]; P <0.001). Multivariable Cox regression analysis revealed that being Asian (HR 0.7 [95% CI, 0.5–0.9], P = 0.01), having adenocarcinoma (HR 0.8 [95% CI, 0.6–0.9], P = 0.004), and never having smoked (HR 0.8 [95% CI, 0.6–1.0], P = 0.048) were independent prognostic factors.[ ] These results led to the approval of erlotinib by the FDA on November 18, 2004, for the treatment of patients with locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC who had progressed after at least one prior chemotherapy regimen. On April 6, 2006, the SFDA approved erlotinib for the treatment of NSCLC patients after the failure of two or more chemotherapies. Subsequent studies compared erlotinib with standard second-line chemotherapy in metastatic NSCLC, including the TITAN, DELTA, HORG, and TAILOR studies. The TITAN,[ ] DELTA,[ ] and HORG[ ] trials showed no survival benefit of erlotinib over chemotherapy in NSCLC without EGFR mutation status selection, while the TAILOR[ ] study found docetaxel superior to erlotinib in EGFR wild-type patients. In the first-line treatment setting, two phase 2 trials showed that the ORR of erlotinib in NSCLC patients without EGFR mutation status selection was 10.0% and 22.7%, respectively.[ , ] Subsequent EURTAC study assessed the efficacy and safety of erlotinib compared with standard chemotherapy in European untreated patients with advanced EGFR mutation-positive NSCLC, demonstrating that the erlotinib group demonstrated a significantly longer median PFS than the chemotherapy group (9.7 vs. 5.2 months, HR 0.37 [95% CI 0.25–0.54], P <0.0001).[ ] Based on the results of EURTAC study, the FDA approved erlotinib as first-line treatment for NSCLC patients with EGFR-sensitive mutations on May 14, 2013. Later on, the ENSURE[ ] and OPTIMAL[ ] studies demonstrated significantly improved PFS with first-line erlotinib compared to chemotherapy in Asian patients with advanced EGFR mutation-positive NSCLC. On March 10, 2017, the China Food and Drug Administration (CFDA), approved the first-line treatment of erlotinib for NSCLC patients with EGFR-sensitive mutations.

## Icotinib
Icotinib (BPI-2009H, Conmana®) is a first-generation reversible EGFR-TKI independently developed by Zhejiang Betta Pharmaceuticals Co., Ltd (Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China). Its median half-life is approximately 6–8 hours, which is shorter than that of gefitinib (48.0 hours) and erlotinib (36.2 hours).[ , , ] The ICOGEN study, the first head-to-head comparison of icotinib and gefitinib in the second-line treatment of NSCLC patients without EGFR mutation status selection. Icotinib was found to be non-inferior to gefitinib in terms of PFS, with a median PFS of 4.6 months vs. 3.4 months, respectively (HR 0.84 [95% CI 0.67–1.05], P = 0.13). Subgroup analysis revealed that patients with EGFR mutations had longer PFS and OS compared to those with EGFR wild-type patients. Icotinib demonstrated a better safety profile compared to gefitinib.[ ] The ICOGEN study underscored the predictive value of EGFR genotyping in the second-line treatment of NSCLC. Based on the results of ICOGEN study, the SFDA approved icotinib for post-line treatment of advanced NSCLC on June 7, 2011. In a phase 4 study, 144 patients with EGFR mutations receiving first-line icotinib treatment, the ORR was 56.3% and the DCR was 95.1%.[ ] Based on the phase 4 study, the CFDA approved icotinib for first-line treatment of advanced NSCLC patients with EGFR-sensitive mutations on November 13, 2014. The CONVINCE trial was the first study to assess the efficacy and safety of first-line icotinib compared with cisplatin plus pemetrexed followed by pemetrexed maintenance therapy in NSCLC patients with EGFR mutations. First-line icotinib significantly improved median PFS in patients with advanced EGFR mutation-positive lung adenocarcinoma (11.2 vs. 7.9 months, HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.43–0.87; P = 0.006), while having a better safety profile compared to chemotherapy.[ ]

## Second-generation EGFR-TKIs
Although first-generation EGFR-TKIs significantly improve OS compared to chemotherapy, the development of resistance is inevitable, typically occurring after a median of 9.7 to 11.2 months after first-line EGFR-TKIs treatment.[ , , ] A defining characteristic of first-generation EGFR-TKIs is their reversible binding exclusively to EGFR. The resistant EGFR T790M mutation increases the affinity of EGFR for ATP, dramatically reducing the efficacy of reversible quinazoline inhibitors like gefitinib and erlotinib.[ ] To address this resistance, second-generation EGFR-TKIs have been developed to enable irreversible binding not only to EGFR but also to other receptors in the ErbB family. This broader inhibition aims to delay or overcome acquired resistance.[ ]

## Afatinib
Afatinib (BIBW2992, Gilotrif®) is the first second-generation EGFR-TKI developed by Boehringer Ingelheim Pharmaceuticals, Inc. (Biberach, Germany). It irreversibly binds to EGFR and HER2, effectively suppressing the kinase activity of both wild-type and activated EGFR and HER2 mutants, including erlotinib-resistant isoforms such as EGFR T790M.[ , ] Afatinib was approved by FDA for the first-line treatment of NSCLC patients with EGFR-sensitive mutations on July 12, 2013. The LUX-Lung 1 study, a phase 2b/3 randomized trial, compared afatinib with placebo in patients with advanced or metastatic NSCLC who had progressed after treatment with erlotinib, gefitinib, or both, in addition to one or two lines of chemotherapy. The study showed that median PFS was significantly longer in the afatinib group compared to the placebo group (3.3 vs. 1.1 months, HR 0.38, 95% CI 0.31–0.48, P <0.0001), but no OS benefit was observed.[ ] The PFS benefit of afatinib compared to chemotherapy in untreated EGFR-mutant NSCLC was further confirmed in the LUX-Lung 3 study[ ] (HR 0.58 [95% CI 0.43–0.70], P = 0.001) and the LUX-Lung 6 study[ ] (HR 0.28 [95% CI 0.20–0.39], P <0.0001). Moreover, the LUX-Lung 7 study demonstrated that afatinib significantly improved median PFS in treatment-naïve patients with EGFR-mutant NSCLC compared to gefitinib (11.0 vs. 10.9 months, HR 0.73 [95% CI 0.57–0.95], P = 0.017). However, subgroup analysis indicated that afatinib did not significantly prolong median PFS for patients with EGFR 19del mutation (12.7 vs. 11.0 months, HR 0.76 [95% CI 0.41–1.44]) and EGFR L858R (10.9 vs. 10.8 months, HR 0.71 [95% CI 0.48–1.06]), compared to gefitinib. Additionally, there was no significant difference in median OS between afatinib and gefitinib in EGFR 19del subgroup (30.7 vs. 26.4 months, HR 0.83 [95% CI 0.58-1.17], P = 0.2841) and EGFR L858R subgroup (25.0 vs. 21.2 months, HR 0.91 [95%CI 0.62–1.36], P = 0.6585). The EGFR 19del mutation demonstrated greater survival benefit compared to EGFR L858R mutation. In the subgroup of patients with brain metastases, afatinib did not show a significant advantage over gefitinib in terms of median PFS (8.4 vs. 9.3 months, HR 1.14 [95% CI 0.64–2.03], P = 0.17) or OS (HR 1.16 [95% CI 0.61–2.21]).[ , ] Afatinib was approved by the CFDA on February 21, 2017 for both post-line and first-line treatment of NSCLC patients with EGFR-sensitive mutations.

## Dacomitinib
Dacomitinib (PF299804, Vizimpro®), a second-generation pan-ERBB inhibitor, developed by Pfizer Inc. (New York, U.S.). It irreversibly inhibits EGFR, HER2, and HER4, targeting both EGFR wild-type and activating mutations, including EGFR T790M resistance mutation.[ , ] Dacomitinib was approved by the FDA on September 27, 2018, for the first-line treatment of NSCLC patients with EGFR-sensitive mutations. In a phase 2 trial comparing dacomitinib to erlotinib in NSCLC patients without EGFR mutation status selection, who had disease progression after one or two prior chemotherapy regimens, dacomitinib demonstrated a longer median PFS compared to erlotinib (2.86 vs. 1.91 months, HR 0.66 [95% CI 0.47–0.91], P = 0.012), with acceptable toxicity.[ ] In the phase 3 NCIC CTG BR.26 study, which assessed dacomitinib vs. best supportive care for NSCLC patients without EGFR mutation status selection, who had received up to three prior lines of chemotherapy and either gefitinib or erlotinib, patients in the dacomitinib group had longer median PFS (2.66 vs. 1.38 months, HR 0.66 [95% CI 0.55–0.79], P <0.0001) and higher ORR (7% vs. 1%, P = 0.001) than those in the placebo group. However, there was no statistically significant difference in OS between the two groups (6.83 vs. 6.31 months, HR 1.00 [95% CI 0.83–1.21], P = 0.506).[ ] The ARCHER 1009 study compared the efficacy of dacomitinib and erlotinib in NSCLC patients without EGFR mutation status selection, who had progression after one or two prior chemotherapy regimens. The results indicated that dacomitinib was not superior to erlotinib in this unselected patient population.[ ] In the ARCHER 1050 study, which compared dacomitinib to gefitinib for the first-line treatment of NSCLC patients with EGFR mutations, dacomitinib significantly improved both median PFS (14.7 vs. 9.2 months, HR 0.59 [95% CI 0.47–0.74], P <0.0001) and median OS (34.1 vs. 27.0 months, HR 0.748 [95% CI 0.591–0.947], P = 0.0155) over gefitinib in the first-line treatment of EGFR mutation-positive NSCLC patients. Dacomitinib also demonstrated superior PFS compared to gefitinib in EGFR 19del subgroup (HR 0.55 [95% CI 0.41–0.75]) and EGFR L858R subgroup (HR 0.63 [95% CI 0.44–0.88]). However, OS superiority was observed only in EGFR L858R subgroup (HR 0.665 [95% CI 0.470–0.941]) and not in EGFR 19del subgroup (HR 0.847 [95% CI 0.618–1.161]).[ , ] Dacomitinib was approved by the China National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) on May 15, 2019 for the first-line treatment of NSCLC patients with EGFR-sensitive mutations.

## Third-generation EGFR-TKIs
Despite the irreversible binding and partial activity against the EGFR T790M mutation of second-generation EGFR-TKIs, afatinib achieves only an improvement in median PFS of less than one month compared to gefitinib in the first-line setting,[ ] while dacomitinib extends median PFS by only 5.5 months.[ ] This limited benefit is likely due to the inability of afatinib and dacomitinib to inhibit EGFR T790M at clinically achievable doses, as dose-limiting toxicities arise from non-selective inhibition of wild-type EGFR. The second-generation EGFR-TKIs can also drive resistance through the acquisition of the EGFR T790M mutation.[ ] Resistance to second-generation EGFR-TKIs typically emerges within a median of 11.0 to 14.7 months after first-line treatment of second-generation EGFR-TKIs.[ , ] Furthermore, the potent inhibition of wild-type EGFR by these agents is associated with notable skin and gastrointestinal toxicities. Afatinib is associated with higher rates of grade 3–4 rash/acne (9.4% vs. 3.1%) and diarrhea (13.1% vs. 1.3%),[ ] while dacomitinib has a higher incidence of grade 3–4 acneiform dermatitis (14% vs. 0) and diarrhea (8% vs. 1%), compared to gefitinib.[ ] Consequently, there remains a significant unmet need for an EGFR-TKI that can more effectively target EGFR T790M-mutant tumors while sparing wild-type EGFR activity.

## Osimertinib
Osimertinib (AZD-9291, Tagrisso®), developed by AstraZeneca plc (London, UK), is the first third-generation, potent, irreversible EGFR-TKI that selectively inhibits both EGFR-TKI sensitive mutations and the EGFR T790M resistance mutation. Compared to first- or second-generation EGFR-TKIs, AZD9291 demonstrates significantly reduced in vitro activity against wild-type EGFR.[ ] In the phase 1 and phase 2 trials, osimertinib demonstrated an ORR of 66% and a median PFS of 11.1 months in NSCLC patients harboring EGFR T790M mutation, who had progressed on prior EGFR-TKI therapy.[ , ] The AURA3 trial confirmed osimertinib’s superiority over platinum plus pemetrexed, with a significantly higher ORR (71% vs. 31%, P <0.001) and median PFS (10.1 vs. 4.4 months, HR 0.30 [95% CI 0.23–0.41], P <0.001), but no difference in OS.[ , ] Based on these findings, osimertinib received accelerated approval by the FDA on November 13, 2015, for patients with EGFR T790M-mutated NSCLC following progression on other EGFR inhibitors. Subsequently, the FLAURA study demonstrated osimertinib’s efficacy as a first-line treatment for EGFR-mutant advanced NSCLC, showing significantly improved median PFS (18.9 vs. 10.2 months, HR 0.46 [95% CI 0.37–0.57], P <0.001) and median OS (38.6 vs. 31.8 months, HR 0.80 [95% CI 0.64–1.00], P = 0.046) compared to standard EGFR-TKIs (gefitinib or erlotinib). A consistent PFS benefit of osimertinib over standard EGFR-TKIs was observed across EGFR 19del (HR 0.43 [95% CI 0.32–0.56], P <0.001), EGFR L858R (HR 0.51 [95% CI 0.36–0.71], P <0.001), and central nervous system (CNS) metastases (HR 0.47 [95% CI 0.30–0.74], P <0.001) subgroups. However, the OS superiority was only observed in EGFR 19del subgroup (HR 0.68 [95% CI 0.51–0.90]), with no significant OS benefit in EGFR L858R (HR 1.00 [95% CI 0.71–1.40]) or CNS metastases subgroup 0.83 [95% CI 0.53–1.30]) subgroups.[ , ] This led to its FDA approval on April 18, 2018 for first-line treatment for the EGFR-sensitive mutation-positive NSCLC patient populations. Osimertinib was approved by the CFDA on March 22, 2017, for patients with EGFR T790M-mutated NSCLC who had experienced disease progression after treatment with prior EGFR inhibitors. Subsequently, on August 29, 2019, osimertinib received approval from the NMPA for the first-line treatment of NSCLC patients with EGFR-sensitive mutations.

## Aumolertinib
Aumolertinib (HS-10296) is a third-generation EGFR-TKI developed by Jiangsu Hansoh Pharmaceutical Group Co., Ltd. (Lianyungang, Jiangsu, China). It selectively inhibits both EGFR-sensitizing and EGFR T790M mutations. Structurally similar to osimertinib, aumolertinib is a pyrimidine-based small molecule but has been further optimized with a cyclopropyl group replacing a methyl group on the indole ring of osimertinib. This modification may confer increased selectivity against the EGFR T790M mutation and improve blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration, as demonstrated by brain-to-plasma concentration ratios ranging from 4.4 to 19.1 in preclinical studies.[ , ] In the APOLLO study, aumolertinib demonstrated an ORR of 68.9% and a DCR of 93.4% in NSCLC patients with EGFR T790M mutations after progression on first- or second-generation EGFR-TKIs. The median PFS and median duration of response (DoR) were 12.4 and 15.1 months, respectively. CNS ORR and CNS DCR were 60.9% and 91.3%, respectively, with a median CNS DoR of 12.5 months.[ ] In the AENEAS study, aumolertinib significantly prolonged median PFS compared to gefitinib (19.3 vs. 9.9 months, HR 0.46 [95% CI 0.36–0.60], P <0.001) as first-line treatment for EGFR-mutant NSCLC. Subgroup analyses demonstrated a consistent median PFS benefit of aumolertinib over gefitinib across key subgroups, including EGFR 19del (HR 0.39 [95% CI 0.28–0.54], P <0.001), EGFR L858R (HR 0.60 [95% CI 0.40–0.89], P = 0.0102), and CNS metastases (HR 0.38 [95% CI 0.24–0.60], P <0.0001) subgroups. The OS data remains immature.[ ] Aumolertinib was approved by the NMPA on March 17, 2020, for patients with EGFR T790M-mutated NSCLC after progression on prior EGFR inhibitors, and on December 14, 2021, for the first-line treatment of advanced NSCLC with EGFR-sensitive or primary EGFR T790M resistance mutations.

## Furmonertinib
Furmonertinib (AST2818) is a third-generation EGFR-TKI developed by Shanghai Allist Pharmaceuticals Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). This trifluoroethoxypyridine-based irreversible EGFR-TKI selectively targets EGFR-sensitizing and resistance mutations (e.g., 19del, L858R, G719X, L861Q, and T790M) while sparing wild-type EGFR.[ ] In the phase 1/2 and phase 2b trials, furmonertinib demonstrated ORRs of 76.7% and 74.1%, respectively, and intracranial ORRs of 70.6% and 65.5% in NSCLC patients with EGFR T790M mutations.[ , , ] The FURLONG study demonstrated that furmonertinib had superior median PFS compared to gefitinib as first-line treatment for EGFR-mutant NSCLC (20.8 vs. 11.1 months, HR 0.44 [95% CI 0.34–0.58], P <0.0001). In subgroup analyses, furmonertinib also showed superior median PFS over gefitinib in patients with EGFR 19del (HR 0.35 [95% CI 0.23–0.53], P <0.0001) and EGFR L858R mutation (0.54 [95% CI 0.37–0.77], P = 0.0006). Additionally, furmonertinib improved median CNS PFS (20.8 vs. 9.8 months, HR 0.40, P = 0.0011) and CNS ORR (91% vs. 65%, P = 0.0277). The OS data remain immature.[ , ] Moreover, fumetinib significantly improve patients’ quality of life compared to gefitinib.[ ] The NMPA approved furmonertinib for EGFR T790M-mutant NSCLC following progression on prior EGFR inhibitors on March 2, 2021, and later expanded its approval on June 28, 2022, for the first-line treatment of advanced NSCLC with EGFR-sensitive or primary EGFR T790M resistance mutations.

## Befotertinib
Befotertinib (D-0316) is a third-generation irreversible EGFR-TKI, developed by Betta Pharmaceuticals Co., Ltd (Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China), that demonstrates high selectivity against both EGFR sensitizing and EGFR T790M mutations.[ ] In a phase 2 trial, befotertinib demonstrated an ORR of 67.6%, intracranial ORR of 55.9%, and a median PFS of 16.6 months in patients with EGFR T790M mutations.[ ] Subsequently, the phase 3 trial demonstrated that befotertinib outperformed icotinib in median PFS as first-line therapy for EGFR-mutant NSCLC (22.1 vs. 13.8 months, HR 0.49 [95% CI 0.36–0.68], P <0.0001). The PFS benefit of befotertinib was observed in the EGFR 19del (HR 0.42 [95% CI 0.29–0.62], P <0.0001) and CNS metastases (HR 0.48 [95% CI 0.28–0.84], P = 0.0086) subgroups, but not in the EGFR L858R subgroup (HR 0.63 [95% CI 0.37–1.08], P = 0.088). The OS data remain immature.[ ] The NMPA approved befotertinib for EGFR T790M-mutated NSCLC after treatment with other EGFR inhibitors failed on May 29, 2023. On September 28, 2023, the NMPA expanded the approval to include first-line treatment for advanced NSCLC patients with EGFR-sensitive mutations.

## Rezivertinib
Rezivertinib (BPI-7711), a third-generation EGFR-TKI developed by Beta Pharma (Shanghai) Co.,Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and Beta Pharma Inc. (Princeton, NJ, U.S.), features an oxygen atom replacing an N-methyl group as the connector between the dimethyl aminoethyl chain and the phenyl ring of osimertinib. Rezivertinib irreversibly and selectively targets EGFR sensitizing mutations and EGFR T790M mutation, with excellent CNS penetration.[ , ] In a phase 2b trial, rezivertinib demonstrated an ORR of 64.6% and a DCR of 89.8% in patients with locally advanced or metastatic EGFR T790M-mutated NSCLC. The median PFS was 12.2 months, with a median OS of 23.9 months. In subgroup analyses, the ORR was 72.4% for EGFR 19del, 51.9% for EGFR L858R, and 57.1% for CNS metastases. The median PFS for patients with EGFR L858R, EGFR 19del, and CNS metastases subgroup was 10.3 months, 12.4 months, and 10.3 months, respectively.[ ] In first-line treatment of locally advanced or metastatic EGFR-sensitive NSCLC, rezivertinib demonstrated an ORR of 83.7%, a median DoR of 19.3 months, and a median PFS of 20.7 months. The ORR for patients with EGFR 19del, EGFR L858R, and CNS metastases was 89.3%, 76.9%, and 83.3%, respectively. The median PFS for these subgroups was 15.2 months, 17.7 months, and 20.7months, respectively. The OS data remain immature. No dose reduction or discontinuation due to treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) was reported.[ , , ] The REZOR study demonstrated that rezivertinib as first-line treatment significantly extended median PFS in NSCLC patients with EGFR-sensitive mutation compared to gefitinib (19.3 vs. 9.6 months, HR 0.48 [95% CI 0.36–0.63], P <0.0001). Rezivertinib also significantly improved median PFS in the EGFR 19del (HR 0.38 [95% CI 0.26–0.57], P <0.0001), EGFR L858R (HR 0.59 [95% CI 0.40–0.85], P = 0.0053), and CNS metastases (HR 0.52 [95% CI 0.34–0.80], P = 0.003) subgroups, compared to gefitinib. Besides, patients with EGFR mutations via tissue sampling detection demonstrated longer median PFS than in those with EGFR mutations via plasma sampling detection (20.7 vs. 16.0 months). The OS data remain immature.[ , ] On May 15, 2024, the NMPA approved rezivertinib for the treatment of NSCLC harboring EGFR T790M mutations who have progressed on or after previous EGFR-TKI therapy. On October 29, 2024, the NMPA approved rezivertinib for the first-line treatment of advanced NSCLC patients with EGFR-sensitive mutations.

## Rilertinib
Rilertinib (SH-1028) is a third-generation EGFR-TKI developed by Nanjing Sanhome Pharmaceutical Co.Ltd (Nanjing, Jiangsu China). It irreversibly and selectively in hibits both sensitizing EGFR mutations and EGFR T790M mutation.[ ] In the phase 2 trial, rilertinib demonstrated an ORR of 60.4% and a DCR of 92.5% in patients with locally advanced or metastatic EGFR T790M-mutant NSCLC. The median PFS was 12.6 months, with a median DoR of 12.5 months. The ORR in subgroups with EGFR 19del, EGFR L858R, and CNS metastases was 71.5%, 36.2%, and 53.8%, respectively. The median PFS for patients with EGFR 19del, L858R, and CNS metastases subgroup was 9.7 months, 13.8 months, and 8.3 months, respectively.[ ] A subsequent phase 3 trial (SHC013-III-01) showed significantly longer median PFS with rilertinib compared to gefitinib (19.3 vs. 9.8 months, HR 0.46 [95% CI 0.33–0.65], P <0.0001). The incidence of ≥Grade 3 TRAEs was 17.3% in the rilertinib group and 22.9% in the gefitinib group, respectively.[ ] The NMPA approved Rilertinib for EGFR T790M-mutated NSCLC, who have progressed on or after previous EGFR-TKI on June 11, 2024, and later expanded its approval on September 3, 2024, for the first-line treatment of advanced NSCLC with EGFR-sensitive mutations.

## Lazertinib
Lazertinib (YH25448, Lazcluze®) is a third-generation selective irreversible EGFR-TKI developed by Yuhan Corporation, (Incheon, Republic of Korea) and Janssen Biotech, Inc., (Horsham, PA, U.S.). It differs from osimertinib by featuring a 4-phenyl-3-dimethylaminomethyl-1-pyrazole group replacing the indole on the pyrimidine ring, and a morpholine substituting the dimethylaminoethyl-N-methyl chain at the 2-position of the phenyl ring.[ ] In a phase 1/2 trial, lazertinib demonstrated an ORR of 54% in NSCLC patients with EGFR-sensitive mutations who had progressed after first- or second-generation EGFR-TKI therapy and developed EGFR T790M mutation.[ ] Subsequent LASER301 study showed that lazertinib significantly extended median PFS compared to gefitinib in the first-line treatment of EGFR-mutated advanced NSCLC (20.6 vs. 9.7 months, HR 0.45 [95% CI 0.34–0.58], P <0.001). The PFS benefit of lazertinib was also observed in EGFR 19del (HR 0.46 [95% CI, 0.33–0.65]), EGFR L858R (HR, 0.41 [95% CI, 0.27–0.62]), and CNS metastases subgroups (HR 0.42 [95% CI, 0.26–0.68]).[ ] Lazertinib received its first approval on 18 January 2021 in the Republic of Korea for the treatment of patients with EGFR T790M mutation-positive locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC who have previously received EGFR-TKI therapy.

## Limertinib
Limertinib (ASK120067) is a third-generation EGFR-TKI developed by Jiangsu Aosaikang Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China), featuring a 2-aminonaphthyl ring replacing the indole ring and a chlorine substituent on the pyrimidine ring. It irreversibly targets both sensitizing EGFR mutations and EGFR T790M mutation. In the phase 2b trial, limertinib demonstrated an ORR of 68.8% and a DCR of 92.4% in patients with locally advanced or metastatic EGFR T790M-mutant NSCLC, with a median PFS of 11.0 months and a median DoR of 11.1 months. The CNS ORR was 56.1%, and the median CNS PFS was 10.6 months.[ ] A phase 3 study demonstated that limertinib significantly prolonged the median PFS of NSCLC patients harboring EGFR-sensitizing mutation compared to gefitinib (20.7 vs. 9.7 months, HR 0.44 [95% CI 0.34–0.58], P <0.0001). Limertinib also showed superior median PFS over gefitinib in EGFR 19del (HR 0.42 [95% CI 0.30–0.58], P <0.0001), EGFR L858R (HR 0.60 [95% CI 0.38–0.93], P = 0.0205), and CNS metastases (HR 0.35 [95% CI 0.22–0.56], P <0.0001) subgroups.[ ] On January 14, 2025, the NMPA approved limertinib for the treatment of EGFR T790M-mutated NSCLC, who have progressed on or after previous EGFR-TKI, and later expanded its approval on April 22, 2025, for the first-line treatment of advanced NSCLC with EGFR-sensitive mutations.

## Mifanertinib
Mifanertinib (mefatinib) is a third-generation EGFR-TKI developed by Hangzhou Zhongmei Huadong Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, Jiangsu, China). Mifanertinib irreversibly inhibits EGFR tyrosine kinase autophosphorylation. The phase 3 study (HDHY-MHTN-III-1907) demonstrated that mifanertinib significantly prolonged the median PFS of NSCLC patients harboring EGFR L858R mutation compared to gefitinib (13.7 vs. 8.3 months, HR 0.55 [95% CI 0.38–0.78], P = 0.0008). At the data cut-off, OS data remained immature.[ ] Based on the positive results of HDHY-MHTN-III-1907, on October 21, 2025, the NMPA approved mifanertinib for the first-line treatment of NSCLC patients harboring EGFR L858R mutation.

## EGFR-TKIs Combination Therapy for Unresectable NSCLC

## EGFR-TKIs combine with chemotherapy
The development of first-line EGFR-TKI combined with chemotherapy has faced numerous challenges. In 2004, two phase 3 trials, INTACT1 and INTACT2, failed to demonstrate a survival benefit when combining gefitinib with chemotherapy, compared to chemotherapy alone, in advanced chemotherapy-naïve NSCLC patients without EGFR mutation status selection.[ , ] The TRIBUTE[ ] and TALENT[ ] studies demonstrate that erlotinib plus chemotherapy did not improve survival compared to chemotherapy alone in chemotherapy-naïve patients with advanced NSCLC without EGFR mutation status selection. Although earlier studies failed to show significant efficacy of EGFR-TKI combination therapy, subsequent studies provided encouraging results particularly in patients with EGFR mutations. The NEJ009 and subsequent studies confirmed that combining gefitinib with chemotherapy provides better survival benefits compared to gefitinib alone in patients with untreated, advanced EGFR-mutant NSCLC.[ , , ] The combination of icotinib with chemotherapy in a first-line setting was shown to significantly improve ORR (77.8% vs. 64.0%, P = 0.043), DCR (91.1% vs. 79.8%, P = 0.031), and median PFS (16.0 vs. 10.0 months, HR 0.59 [95% CI 0.42–0.84], P = 0.003) in NSCLC patients with sensitive EGFR mutations compared to icotinib alone, though no difference was observed in OS.[ ] The FLAURA2 study demonstrated that first-line treatment with osimertinib plus chemotherapy significantly improved median PFS (25.5 vs. 16.7 months, HR 0.62 [95% CI 0.49–0.79], P <0.001) and median OS (47.5 vs. 37.6 months, HR 0.77 [95% CI 0.61–0.96], P = 0.02), compared to osimertinib alone.[ , ] On February 19, 2024, the FDA approved osimertinib in combination with chemotherapy as a first-line treatment for advanced NSCLC with EGFR-sensitive mutations. On June 25, 2024, the NMPA also granted approval for the same regimen and indication. More recently, the AENEAS2 trial showed that aumolertinib plus chemotherapy as first-line treatment significantly prolonged median PFS compared to aumolertinib alone in NSCLC patients with sensitive EGFR mutations.[ ] On November 27, 2024, the NMPA officially accepted the new drug application (NDA) for aumolertinib plus chemotherapy as first-line treatment for NSCLC patients with sensitive EGFR mutations. The FLAURA2 study marks a new era in first-line combination therapy for NSCLC with EGFR-sensitive mutations; however, further studies are required to validate the efficacy of other EGFR-TKI and chemotherapy combinations.

## EGFR-TKIs combine with angiogenesis inhibitors
Blocking EGFR and anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signaling pathways are considered complementary mechanisms to control tumor growth.[ , , , ] Combining EGFR-TKI and angiogenesis inhibitors has been investigated in numerous clinical studies, though challenges persist. Several clinical studies, including those with gefitinib,[ ] erlotinib,[ ] afatinib,[ ] and osimertinib[ , ] combined with bevacizumab or ramucirumab, failed to demonstrate survival improvements compared to EGFR-TKI monotherapy in NSCLC patients. Despite these disappointing results, other studies have shown promising outcomes with the combination of EGFR-TKIs and angiogenesis inhibitor. The ACTIVE study demonstrated that gefitinib plus apatinib had superior median PFS compared to gefitinib plus placebo in advanced EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients.[ ] Furthermore, several other studies, including JO25567, NEJ026, ARTEMIS-CTONG150, and BEVERLY, have demonstrated that erlotinib plus bevacizumab significantly prolonged median PFS compared to erlotinib alone in untreated advanced EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients, with HRs ranging from 0.54 to 0.66.[ , , , , ] Similarly, the RELAY study demonstrated superior median PFS for erlotinib plus ramucirumab compared to erlotinib plus placebo in untreated advanced EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients (19.4 vs. 12.4 months, HR 0.59 [95% CI 0.46–0.76; P <0.0001).[ ] The FL-ALTER study demonstrated that gefitinib plus anlotinib significantly improved median PFS compared to gefitinib plus placebo for untreated EGFR-mutated NSCLC patients (14.8 vs. 11.2 months, HR 0.64 [95% CI 0.48–0.80], P = 0.003).[ ] In the ALTER-L004 study, icotinib plus anlotinib demonstrated an ORR of 68.5%, DCR of 98.2%, median PFS of 15.1 months, and a median DoR of 13.5 months for untreated EGFR-mutated NSCLC patients.[ ] In NSCLC patients with EGFR-mutant and brain metastases, osimertinib plus bevacizumab as first-line treatment demonstrated a CNS ORR of 88% and a median CNS PFS of 26.73 months.[ ] A phase 3 trial evaluating osimertinib with or without bevacizumab as initial treatment for patients with EGFR-mutant NSCLC is ongoing (NCT04181060). While several studies have demonstrated the benefits of combining EGFR-TKIs with angiogenesis inhibitors, the overall efficacy and safety profile of this approach remains complex. A meta-analysis further examined these combinations, revealing that although EGFR-TKIs and angiogenesis inhibitors significantly prolonged median PFS in patients with advanced EGFR-mutant NSCLC, the ORR and OS benefits were not significant, and the combination therapy increased the risk of adverse events (AEs), particularly hypertension and proteinuria. Subgroup analyses suggested that the combination therapy may offer better PFS in smokers, patients with liver metastasis, and those without brain metastases. The smoking and liver metastasis subgroups, as well as the brain metastasis subgroup, may also benefit from a potential OS improvement.[ ] In summary, while combining EGFR-TKIs with angiogenesis inhibitors has shown promising PFS benefits in advanced EGFR-mutant NSCLC, the OS improvement was limited, with an increased risk of adverse events.

## EGFR-TKIs combine with immunotherapy
Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have become a vital component of NSCLC treatment. Preclinical studies have provided a rationale for combining EGFR-TKIs with immunotherapy. For instance, activation of the oncogenic EGFR pathway can upregulate programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression and other immunosuppressive factors, reshaping the tumor microenvironment to promote immune escape and mechanistically linking treatment response to programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) inhibition.[ ] Another study reported that elevated PD-L1 expression is associated with EGFR mutation, and PD-L1-positive patients treated with EGFR-TKIs experienced longer time to progression (TTP) and OS compared to PD-L1-negative patients,[ ] suggesting that the combination of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors and EGFR-TKIs may have synergistic effects in NSCLC therapy. However, conflicting evidence exists; another preclinical study demonstrated that EGFR activation upregulates PD-L1 through the p-ERK1/2/p-c-Jun pathway, and PD-L1 expression mediated by EGFR activation could lead to T cell apoptosis via the PD-L1/PD-1 axis, without showing synergistic tumor-killing effects when EGFR-TKIs and anti-PD-1 mAbs were combined.[ ] Subgroup analysis from several phase 3 trial suggest that EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients may not derive significant benefit from ICIs in post-line treatments.[ , , ] To extend response duration and delay resistance development in EGFR-mutant NSCLC, several clinical trials are investigating combinations of EGFR-TKIs and immunotherapy. In the TATTON study, osimertinib plus durvalumab demonstrated an ORR of 67% in EGFR T790M-positive and 21% in EGFR T790M-negative EGFR-TKI pretreated patients, with an ORR of 70% in EGFR-TKI naïve patients. However, this combination was associated with a high incidence of ILD, reported in 38% of patients (13/34), which was unexpectedly higher than osimertinib or durvalumab monotherapy.[ ] The underlying mechanism contributing to the high ILD incidence remains unknown; as a result, further enrollment in this trial has been permanently suspended. Similarly, a phase 3 trial evaluating osimertinib plus durvalumab compared to osimertinib alone in EGFR T790M-positive NSCLC patients following prior EGFR TKI therapy (NCT02454933) was also suspended. A phase 1b trial reported an ORR of 77.8% with concurrent gefitinib and durvalumab (arm 1) and an ORR of 80% in patients receiving gefitinib monotherapy lead-in for 4 weeks, followed by concurrent durvalumab and gefitinib (arm 2). However, grade 3/4 AEs included alanine aminotransferase elevation (70% in arm 1 and 60% in arm 2) and aspartate aminotransferase elevation (40% in arm 1 and 50% in arm 2).[ ] Another phase 1b trial demonstrated that atezolizumab plus erlotinib showed an ORR of 75%, with grade 3–4 TRAEs was 39%, though no ILD cases were observed.[ ] While preclinical studies suggest potential synergy between EGFR-TKIs and ICIs for NSCLC treatment, clinical studies have yielded limited efficacy, with increased toxicity observed instead. The high incidence of AEs, particularly ILD, highlights the need for further research into underlying mechanisms. Future studies should focus on optimizing combination strategies and refining patient stratification to ensure the balance between efficacy and safety.

## EGFR-TKIs combine with radiotherapy
Preclinical studies have shown that combining EGFR-TKIs with radiotherapy may produce synergistic anti-tumor effects.[ ] However, an initial retrospective study of EGFR-TKIs plus brain radiotherapy in EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients with brain metastases found no survival benefit over EGFR-TKI monotherapy.[ , ] In contrast, several subsequent studies have suggested that the combination of EGFR-TKIs with brain radiotherapy is superior to EGFR-TKI monotherapy for EGFR-mutated NSCLC patients with brain metastases.[ , , , ] While these findings appear promising, they should be interpreted cautiously as they are not derived from randomized controlled trials. Although some phase 2 trials showed promising results with erlotinib combined with whole-brain radiation therapy (WBRT), showing an ORR of 86.00–95.65%,[ , ] a subsequent phase 3 trial found no significant improvement in intracranial PFS or cognitive function compared to WBRT alone in the whole patient population or in EGFR-mutant patients.[ ] For thoracic radiotherapy in NSCLC, some phase 2 trials combining EGFR-TKIs (gefitinib or erlotinib) with thoracic radiotherapy demonstrated promising outcomes, with local control rate up to 96%, median PFS ranging from 10.2 to 14.7 months, and median OS ranging from 20.4 to 21.8 months in advanced NSCLC patients.[ , ] Another phase 2 trial comparing first-generation EGFR-TKI plus stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) vs. first-generation EGFR-TKI alone in stage IV NSCLC patients with EGFR-sensitive mutations who achieved stable disease or PR after three months of EGFR-TKI therapy found that the combination of EGFR-TKI plus SBRT significantly prolonged median PFS (17.6 vs. 9.0 months, HR 0.52 [95% CI 0.31–0.89], P = 0.016) and median OS (33.6 vs. 23.2 months, HR 0.53 [95% CI 0.30–0.95], P = 0.026), compared to EGFR-TKI monotherapy.[ ] Although combining EGFR-TKIs with radiotherapy has shown favorable outcomes in several phase 2 trials, additional evidence from randomized controlled phase 3 trials is needed to further assess efficacy and safety.

## EGFR-TKIs combine with other targeted therapies
While numerous targeted drugs, including EGFR mAbs, MET inhibitors, mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase (MEK) inhibitors, HER2/HER3 inhibitors, PI3K/PKB/mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitors, and Janus kinase (JAK)-signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) inhibitors, have shown promising anti-tumor effects in preclinical studies, incorporating them with EGFR-TKIs as a first-line treatment remains challenging.[ ] Several studies have reported unfavorable outcomes, with combinations such as cetuximab plus afatinib and onartuzumab plus erlotinib failing to improve survival in advanced NSCLC patients.[ , , ] Despite these setbacks, recent studies have shown more promising results. In MARQUEE study, erlotinib plus tivantinib significantly improved median PFS (3.6 vs. 1.9 months, HR 0.74 [95% CI 0.62–0.89], P <0.001) compared to erlotinib plus placebo in advanced non-squamous NSCLC previously treated with one to two systemic regimens. However, median OS was not significantly improved (8.5 vs. 7.8 months, HR 0.98 [95% CI 0.84–1.15], P = 0.81). In exploratory analyses, the subgroup with high MET expression showed a potential OS benefit (HR, 0.70 [95% CI 0.49–1.01]). Among EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients, erlotinib plus tivantinib improved median PFS (13.0 vs. 7.5 months, HR 0.49 [95% CI 0.31–0.77]), with similar median OS between the groups (25.5 vs. 20.3 months, HR 0.68 [95% CI 0.43–1.08]).[ , ] The MARIPOSA study demonstrated that lazertinib plus amivantamab significantly extended median PFS compared to osimertinib alone in previously untreated EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients (23.7 vs. 16.6 months, HR 0.70 [95% CI 0.58–0.85] P <0.001),[ ] leading to the FDA and the NMPA approval of lazertinib plus amivantamab for the first-line treatment of NSCLC with EGFR-sensitive mutations on August 20, 2024, and July 29, 2025, respectively. In summary, despite lazertinib plus amivantamab providing a new first-line treatment option for EGFR-mutant NSCLC, the combination strategy of EGFR-TKIs with other targeted therapies still faces challenges and requires further investigation.

## EGFR-TKIs for Uncommon EGFR Mutations

## EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations
EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations account for 5.8% of all EGFR mutations.[ ] A study on Chinese clinical practice revealed that physicians demonstrate a high awareness of EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations.[ ] Patients with EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations generally have a poorer prognosis compared to those with classic EGFR mutations and show limited efficacy to currently approved EGFR-TKIs targeting EGFR-sensitive mutations. A phase 2 trial demonstrated that combination treatment with afatinib and cetuximab showed promising antitumor activity with a DCR of 54% at 18 weeks and a 32% confirmed ORR for NSCLC harboring an EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations.[ ] Mobocertinib is a first-in-class, potent, oral, irreversible TKI designed to selectively target in-frame EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations in NSCLC. A phase 1/2 trial showed that mobocertinib demonstrated an ORR of 28%, a median PFS of 7.3 months, and a median OS of 24 months in patients with advanced NSCLC who had EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations and had previously received platinum-based chemotherapy.[ ] The FDA and the NMPA approved mobocertinib for the treatment of NSCLC with EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations, whose disease has progressed on or after platinum-based chemotherapy on September 15, 2021 and January 11, 2023, respectively. However, the EXCLAIM-2 trial failed to demonstrate the superiority of mobocertinib as a first-line therapy compared to platinum-based chemotherapy.[ ] On October 2, 2023, Takeda Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan), announced to voluntarily withdraw mobocertinib for the treatment of NSCLC patients with EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations in the U.S., and subsequently initiated the same withdrawal process globally. Mobocertinib was withdrawn for the same indication in China on April 18, 2024. In WU-KONG6 study,[ ] sunvozertinib demonstrated an ORR of 61% in patients with locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC with EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations and progressed from platinum-based chemotherapy. In the first-line setting, the confirmed ORR of sunvozertinib treatment for NSCLC with EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations is 61%.[ , ] On August 22, 2023, the NMPA approved sunvozertinib for the treatment of patients with NSCLC harboring EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations, whose disease has progressed on or following platinum-based chemotherapy, or who are intolerant to platinum-based chemotherapy. Amivantamab, an EGFR-MET bispecific antibody, demonstrated an ORR of 40% and a median DoR of 11.1 months, with tolerable safety in patients with EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations after progression on platinum-based chemotherapy.[ ] The PAPILLON study found that amivantamab plus chemotherapy significantly prolonged the median PFS compared to chemotherapy alone in untreated NSCLC patients with EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations (11.4 vs. 6.7 months, HR 0.40 [95% CI 0.30–0.53], P <0.001).[ ] As a result, the FDA granted the accelerated approval of amivantamab for the treatment of NSCLC with EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations, whose disease has progressed on or after platinum-based chemotherapy on May 21, 2021, and then expanded its first-line treatment indication in combination with chemotherapy on March 01, 2024. Subsequently, on February 11, 2025, the NMPA approved amivantamab in combination with chemotherapy for the first-line treatment of patients with locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC harboring EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations. Currently, several ongoing phase 3 trials are evaluating the first-line treatment of NSCLC patients with EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations, including sunvozertinib (NCT05668988), furmonertinib (NCT05607550), zipalertinib (NCT05973773), YK-029A (NCT05767892), and andamertinib (PLB1004) (NCT06281964). The NDA of andamertinib for the second-line treatment of NSCLC patients with EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations was accepted for regulatory review by the NMPA on May 21, 2025. On February 6, 2026, the NMPA approved furmonertinib for the treatment of locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC harboring EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations, who have progressed on or are intolerant to platinum-based chemotherapy. In summary, while several EGFR-TKIs have been approved for second-line treatment of NSCLC with EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations, their potential as first-line therapies still require confirmation through further phase 3 trials.

## EGFR G719X/ L861Q /S768I mutations
EGFR G719X mutation accounts for 3%; L861Q and S768I mutations account for 1%, respectively.[ ] A retrospective study reported no significant difference in ORR (33% vs. 27.1%, P = 0.499) or DCR (76.5% vs. 87.5%, P = 0.194) between first-generation EGFR-TKI and platinum-based chemotherapy as first-line treatment for NSCLC patients with uncommon EGFR mutations including G719X, S768I, and L861Q. Although first-generation EGFR-TKI demonstrated a better median PFS (7.2 vs. 4.9 months, HR = 0.604, P = 0.0088), the median OS was shorter with first-generation EGFR-TKI (14.3 vs. 20.7 months, HR = 1.759, P = 0.0336), compared to chemotherapy.[ ] G719X/ L861Q /S768I mutations are resistant to first-generation EGFR-TKIs, but exhibit good efficacy with second- and third-generation EGFR-TKIs. A combined post-hoc analysis of LUX-Lung 2, LUX-Lung 3, and LUX-Lung 6 studies demonstrated that afatinib as first-line treatment for NSCLC patients with EGFR G719X, L861Q, and S768I mutations demonstrated ORRs of 77.8%, 56.3%, and 100%, respectively, with median PFS of 13.8 months, 8.2 months, and 14.7 months, respectively.[ ] Based on these results, the FDA expanded afatinib’s first-line treatment indication to include NSCLC patients with non-resistant EGFR mutations, including L861Q, G719X, and S768I, on January 16, 2018. The AFANDA study compared the efficacy of afatinib and dacomtinib in treating uncommon EGFR mutations including G719X and S768I. The results showed that dacomitinib had a higher ORR than afatinib (60.5% vs. 26.7%, P = 0.008), but no significant difference in median PFS (12.0 months vs. 10.0 months, P = 0.305).[ ] In UNICORN study, osimertinib as first-line treatment demonstrated an ORR of 55%, a DCR of 90%, and a median PFS of 9.4 months for NSCLC patients with uncommon EGFR mutations including G719X, S768I, and L861Q.[ ] Similarly, the KCSG-LU15-09 study demonstrated that osimertinib as first-line treatment for NSCLC patients with uncommon EGFR mutations including G719X, L861Q, S768I, demonstrated an ORR of 50%, a DoR of 11.2 months, and a median PFS of 8.2 months.[ ] Mefatinib is a dual inhibitor targeting both EGFR and HER2. In a phase 2 trial evaluating mefatinib as a first-line treatment for NSCLC patients with uncommon EGFR mutations including G719X, S768I, and L861Q, the ORR was 85.7%, and the median PFS was 20.6 months.[ ] On May 12, 2023, mefatinib was included in the breakthrough therapy designation program by the NMPA for the first-line treatment of NSCLC patients with uncommon EGFR mutations. The studies evaluating the efficacy of furmonertinib (NCT05548348) and sutetinib (NCT05168566) on NSCLC patients with EGFR G719X/L861Q/S768I mutations are ongoing. In summary, while first-generation EGFR-TKIs exhibit limited efficacy in NSCLC patients with EGFR G719X, L861Q, and S768I mutations, second- and third-generation EGFR-TKIs have shown promising efficacy, warranting further validation in phase 3 trials.

## EGFR P-loop and αC-helix compressing mutations
EGFR P-loop and αC-helix compressing (PACC) mutations are a recently established concept. In 2021, MD Anderson Cancer Center introduced a novel classification system for EGFR mutations based on structure-function relationship of EGFR mutations on drug sensitivity. This system separated EGFR mutations into four distinct subgroups: classical-like mutations that were distant from the ATP-binding pocket, T790M-like mutations in the hydrophobic core, insertions in the loop at the C-terminal end of the αC-helix in exon 20, and mutations on the interior surface of the ATP-binding pocket or C-terminal end of the αC-helix, which were predicted to be PACC.[ ]
EGFR PACC mutations account for approximately 12.5% of all EGFR mutations. Similar to exon 20 insertion mutations, EGFR PACC mutations can alter the drug-binding pocket, which leads to compromised therapeutic efficacy of EGFR-TKIs.[ , ] The FURTHER study, which evaluating the efficacy of furmonertinib in NSCLC patients with EGFR PACC mutations, demonstrated an ORR of 81.8%, a DCR of 100%, a median PFS of 16.0 months, and a median DoR of 14.6 months.[ ]

## EGFR-TKIs for NSCLC Patients with Brain Metastasis
The BBB poses a significant challenge in the treatment of NSCLC patients with brain metastases. The brain penetration of first- and second-generation EGFR-TKIs is generally limited due to efflux mediated by P-glycoprotein (multi-drug resistance protein 1 [MDR1]) and breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP).[ ] The unbound brain-to-plasma partition coefficient (Kpuu) for these agents is typically below 0.12. Third-generation EGFR-TKIs have been structurally modified to minimize the impact of efflux transporters, with osimertinib and lazertinib being notable examples. Osimertinib and lazertinib exhibit brain Kpuu values of 0.21 and 0.29, respectively, and provide intracranial median PFS of up to 27.6 and 28.2 months, respectively.[ , , ] Besides, aumolertinib, furmonertinib, befotertinib, limertinib, and rezivertinib also showed favorable intracranial efficacy for EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients.[ , , , , , , ]
High concentrations of EGFR-TKIs in intracranial tumors have been shown to confer clinically meaningful benefits in both the prevention and treatment of CNS lesions in NSCLC patients, underscoring the importance of structural modifications to enhance CNS penetration. Zorifertinib, an EGFR-TKI specifically designed to avoid binding to MDR1 and BCRP, achieves a brain Kpuu of approximately 1.1–1.4.[ ] The EVEREST study demonstrated that zorifertinib as first-line treatment for NSCLC with brain metastases significantly outperformed gefitinib or erlotinib, achieving a higher intracranial ORR (74.3% vs. 62.8%) and markedly prolonged intracranial median PFS (15.2 months vs. 8.3 months; HR 0.517 [95% CI 0.390–0.687], P <0.0001).[ ] Based on the results of the EVEREST study, zorifertinib was approved by the NMPA on November 20, 2024, for the first-line treatment of NSCLC patients with sensitizing EGFR mutations and brain metastases. The ESAONA study demonstrated that asandeutertinib (TY-9591) as first-line treatment for NSCLC with brain metastases significantly improved intracranial ORR compared to osimertinib (92.8% vs. 76.1%, P = 0.003).[ , ] The NDA of asandeutertinib for the first-line treatment of NSCLC patients with sensitizing EGFR mutations and brain metastases was accepted for regulatory review by the NMPA on February 6, 2026. In summary, third-generation EGFR-TKIs have shown notable brain penetration, while structurally optimized EGFR-TKI, zorifertinib, have enhanced CNS permeability compared to gefitinib and erlotinib. ## EGFR-TKI for Perioperative Treatment of NSCLC

## Adjuvant treatment
Extensive investigations of EGFR-TKIs in the adjuvant treatment of NSCLC have produced a spectrum of outcomes, with both promising and unfavorable results across different studies. While several studies, including NCIC CTG BR19 in mostly EGFR wild-type NSCLC patients,[ ] RADIANT in mostly EGFR wild-type NSCLC patients,[ ] and afatinib for adjuvant treatment in EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients,[ ] failed to demonstrate significant survival benefits in the adjuvant setting for resected NSCLC, other clinical studies suggest potential efficacy of EGFR-TKIs in this context. The ADJUVANT/CTONG1104 study showed that adjuvant gefitinib significantly prolonged DFS compared to vinorelbine plus cisplatin in patients with completely resected stage II-IIIA (N1-N2) EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients (28.7 vs. 18.0 months, HR 0.60 [95% CI 0.42–0.87], P = 0.0054), although this DFS benefit did not translate into a OS benefit.[ , ] Moreover, the SELECT[ ] and EVAN[ ] studies demonstrated the DFS benefit of erlotinib as adjuvant therapy in EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients compared to chemotherapy. In the EVIDENCE study, 2-year treatment of icotinib significantly improved DFS compared to chemotherapy in patients with EGFR-mutant stage II–IIIA NSCLC following complete tumor resection (47.0 vs. 22.1 months; HR 0.36 [95% CI 0.24–0.55], P <0.0001).[ ] Based on the results of EVIDENCE study, the NMPA approved icotinib for adjuvant treatment in stage II–IIIA EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients after surgery on June 3, 2021. Additionally, the ICWIP study will provide the result of icotinib treatment for 3 years after 4-cycle adjuvant chemotherapy compared to placebo for stage II–IIIA EGFR-mutated lung adenocarcinoma patients.[ ] The ADAURA trial provided compelling evidence that osimertinib significantly improved median DFS in patients with stage IB–IIIA EGFR mutation-positive NSCLC compared to placebo (65.8 vs. 28.1 months; HR 0.27, 95% CI 0.21–0.34). OS was also improved, with a 5-year OS rate of 88% vs. 78% (HR 0.49, 95% CI 0.34–0.70, P <0.001).[ , , ] On December 21, 2020, the FDA approved osimertinib as adjuvant treatment for patients with early-stage EGFR-mutated NSCLC. Later on April 8, 2021, the NMPA approved osimertinib as an adjuvant treatment of patients with early-stage EGFR-mutated NSCLC. The ARTS trial demonstrated that aumolertinib significantly prolonged the median DFS in patients with II–IIIB EGFR mutation-positive NSCLC compared to placebo (not estimable vs. 19.4 months; HR 0.166, 95% CI 0.094–0.294, P <0.001).[ ] Based on the results of ARTS study, the NMPA approved aumolertinib for adjuvant treatment in stage II–IIIB EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients on April 30, 2025. In summary, icotinib, osimertinib, and aumolertinib have established their roles in the adjuvant treatment of EGFR-mutant NSCLC. However, the utility of other EGFR-TKIs in the adjuvant setting requires further investigation.

## Neoadjuvant treatment
Studies for neoadjuvant treatment have been limited to phase 2 trials. A phase 2 trial found that gefitinib as neoadjuvant therapy in stage II–IIIA EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients demonstrated an ORR of 54.5% and a major pathologic response (MPR) of 24.2%.[ ] Several phase 2 trials investigating neoadjuvant erlotinib treatment in patients with stage IIIA EGFR-mutant NSCLC have reported ORRs ranging from 42.1% to 67.4%.[ , , , ] A phase 2 trial evaluating icotinib combined with pemetrexed plus carboplatin in resectable stage II–IIIB EGFR-mutant lung adenocarcinoma reported an ORR of 81.5%, a DCR of 96.3%, and an R0 resection rate of 96.2%.[ ] Moreover, a phase 2 trial showed that afatinib yielded an ORR of 70.2%, an MPR of 9.1%, a pathologic complete response (pCR) rate of 2.0%, a pathological downstaging rate of 57.6%, and an R0 resection rate of 87.9% in patients with EGFR-mutant stage III NSCLC.[ ] In a phase 2b trial, osimertinib as neoadjuvant treatment in resectable stage II–IIIB EGFR-mutant lung adenocarcinoma patients demonstrated an ORR of 71.1% and a R0 resection rate of 93.8%.[ ] Aumolertinib as neoadjuvant treatment demonstrated an ORR and R0 resection rate of 100%, with a pCR rate of 33.3%.[ ] These findings highlight the clinical benefits of EGFR-TKIs as neoadjuvant treatment for EGFR-mutant NSCLC, which deserve validation. Phase 3 trials evaluating gefitinib (NCT03203590) and osimertinib (NCT04351555) as neoadjuvant therapies for NSCLC patients are ongoing. In summary, EGFR-TKIs in the neoadjuvant setting are still in the exploratory phase, with preliminary results from phase 2 trials. However, validation through phase 3 trials is needed to establish their definitive role in neoadjuvant treatment for EGFR-mutant NSCLC.

## EGFR-TKIs for Maintenance Therapy
While pemetrexed has been well-established as an effective maintenance therapy for advanced NSCLC,[ ] the use of EGFR-TKIs in maintenance therapy has undergone extensive investigation. SWOG S0023 study demonstrated that unselected inoperable stage III NSCLC population (adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and other NSCLC) did not have survival benefit with maintenance gefitinib after concurrent chemoradiotherapy and docetaxel consolidation.[ ] The SATURN study showed that erlotinib as maintenance therapy significantly extended median PFS for patients with non-progressive disease following first-line platinum-doublet chemotherapy without EGFR mutation status selection, compared to placebo (12.3 vs. 11.1 months, HR 0.71 [95% CI 0.62–0.82], P <0.0001). This PFS benefit was also observed in patients with EGFR-positive immunohistochemistry, EGFR mutations, and in Asian populations.[ , ] Based on the results of SATURN study, the FDA approved erlotinib as a maintenance treatment for advanced NSCLC patients whose disease had not progressed after four cycles of platinum-based first-line chemotherapy on April 16, 2010. The LAURA study demonstrated that osimertinib significantly prolonged median PFS in patients with unresectable EGFR-mutant stage III NSCLC, without progression during or after chemoradiotherapy, compared to placebo (39.1 vs. 5.6 months, HR 0.16 [95% CI 0.10–0.24], P <0.001).[ ] This result led to the FDA approval on September 26, 2024, for osimertinib as a treatment for patients with unresectable stage III EGFR-mutant NSCLC whose disease has not progressed during or after concurrent or sequential platinum-based chemoradiation therapy. The POLESTAR study demonstrated a significant extension in median PFS with aumolertinib compared to placebo in patients with EGFR mutation-positive, unresectable stage III NSCLC who had not experienced disease progression after definitive chemoradiotherapy (30.4 vs. 3.8 months; HR 0.20 [95% CI: 0.11–0.35], P <0.001). OS was not yet mature.[ ] The POLESTAR study results led to the NMPA approval of aumolertinib on March 10, 2025, for consolidation therapy in patients with EGFR mutation-positive, unresectable stage III NSCLC following definitive chemoradiotherapy. In summary, while EGFR-TKI maintenance therapy has shown PFS benefits, it has not translated into OS improvements. Future studies are needed to optimize maintenance therapy regimens and identify the patient populations most likely to benefit.

## Mechanisms of Resistance to EGFR-TKIs
First-generation EGFR-TKIs, such as gefitinib and erlotinib, reversibly bind to the tyrosine kinase domain of EGFR, inhibiting downstream signaling pathways. Second-generation EGFR-TKIs, such as afatinib and dacomitinib, irreversibly bind to the tyrosine kinase activation region, providing a stronger blockade of signaling pathways. The EGFR-sensitive mutation subtypes mainly include the exon 19 deletions and the L858R mutation in exon 21, which account for approximately 90% of EGFR mutations.[ , , ] There are also G719X mutation in exon 18, S768I mutation in exon 20, and L861Q mutation in exon 21, which are considered sensitive mutations but are relatively rare.[ ] Despite the breakthroughs achieved with EGFR-TKIs for NSCLC patients with EGFR-sensitive mutations, resistance to EGFR-TKIs remains an inevitable challenge for subsequent treatments.[ ] The EGFR resistance mechanisms can be classified into two main categories: primary resistance and acquired resistance [Figure and Table ]. Primary resistance refers to tumors that do not respond primarily to EGFR-TKIs treatment. Certain EGFR mutation, such as EGFR exon 20 insertions, occurred in 4–10% of NSCLC patients resistant to most EGFR-TKIs.[ ] The main reason for the resistance of EGFR exon 20 insertions is that they do not exhibit reduced ATP binding in the kinase domain or increased affinity for EGFR-TKIs.[ ] In addition, several mutations, such as Bcl-2-like protein 11 (BIM, also known as BCL2L11) deletion may decrease the response of patients with EGFR-sensitive mutations to EGFR-TKIs.[ ] Acquired resistance occurs after an initial response to EGFR-TKIs treatment. In 2010, Jackman et al[ ] proposed clinical criteria to identify acquired resistance to EGFR-TKIs. Typically, acquired resistance emerges after a median of 9.2–14.7 months of first- and second-generation EGFR-TKIs therapy.[ , , , , , ] This type of resistance can be further divided into EGFR-dependent and EGFR-independent mechanisms. EGFR-dependent resistance is primarily caused by secondary EGFR mutations, such as the mutations of T790M, D761Y, L747S, G719X, S768I, L861Q and T854A.[ ] Nearly 50% of patients initially respond to first- and second-generation EGFR-TKIs developed EGFR T790M resistance mutation.[ , ] This mutation increases the affinity of the mutant receptor for ATP, thereby preventing drug binding and reducing the efficacy of ATP-competitive EGFR-TKIs.[ ] Third-generation EGFR-TKIs have demonstrated significant clinical success by effectively targeting both EGFR-sensitive mutations and EGFR T790M resistance mutation, which represents a major therapeutic advancement in overcoming acquired resistance to first- and second-generation EGFR-TKIs. The GPS study identified common genomic alterations following first-line osimertinib treatment failure, including TP53 mutations (69.8% vs. 43.6%), cell cycle gene alterations (65.8% vs. 27.5%), EGFR amplification (32.9% vs. 10.1%), MET amplification (30.9% vs. 7.4%), gene fusion events (13.4% vs. 13.4%), PIK3CA amplification (8.1% vs. 1.3%), ERBB2 amplification (7.4% vs. 0.7%), and EGFR C797S mutation (3.4% vs. 4.0%) in tumor tissue and plasma samples, respectively. Regarding MET amplification detection in tissue, optimized next-generation sequencing is an acceptable method, demonstrating 100% sensitivity and specificity. Plasma genotyping serving as an alternative method when tissue samples are unavailable.[ , , , ] The most common EGFR-dependent resistance mechanism to osimertinib is characterized by C797S mutation.[ ] C797S mutation can occur in cis or in trans with EGFR T790M mutation. When EGFR C797S and EGFR T790M coexist on the same allele (in cis), they confer resistance to all generations of EGFR-TKIs. However, when these mutations are located on different alleles (in trans), the tumors maintain sensitivity to the combination of first and third-generation EGFR inhibitors, such as erlotinib plus osimertinib, gefitinib plus osimertinib.[ , , ] The ratio of the in cis to in trans conformations of EGFR C797S and EGFR T790M is approximately 8: 1.[ ] Patients with EGFR C797S mutation but without EGFR T790M mutation showed moderate response to first-generation EGFR-TKIs.[ ] Less frequent EGFR-dependent EGFR mutations, including L718Q, G719X, G724S, L792X, G796X, and L797X, have also been identified as resistance mechanisms to osimertinib.[ , ] EGFR-independent resistance arises through alternative pathways that bypass EGFR signaling, such as PI3K, MET, EGFR and HER2 amplification, epithelial to mesenchymal transition (EMT) and small cell lung cancer (SCLC) histologic transformation.[ , , , ] Among these, MET gene amplification is the most common, accounting for 5% to 20% of cases of acquired resistance to EGFR-TKIs. MET is a transmembrane receptor tyrosine kinase, together with ErbB3, that can continuously activate the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, bypassing EGFR signaling pathways.[ ] The MET exon 14 skipping mutation also plays a crucial role in resistance to EGFR-TKIs by leading to the loss of binding sites for ubiquitin ligase, which results in reduced receptor ubiquitination and sustained activation of MET.[ ] The KRAS, NRAS, and BRAF V600E mutations can lead to the aberrant activation of RAS-MAPK signaling pathway, while mutations or amplifications of PIK3CA may enhance the activation of PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. Both mechanisms abnormally activate the downstream signaling pathways, contributing to the development of resistance to EGFR-TKIs.[ ] EMT is a complex process involving multiple regulatory components and plays a significant role in acquired resistance. The EMT inducer the zinc finger E-box binding protein (ZEB) 1 affects CD70 expression, which is overexpressed in NSCLC with EMT-related EGFR resistance. This overexpression not only promotes cell survival and invasion but also activates signaling pathways associated with acquired resistance.[ , ] Histologic transformation from NSCLC to SCLC has also been observed in 3–15% of patients with acquired resistance to EGFR-TKIs.[ ] Tumor metabolic reprogramming is recognized as a key characteristic of malignant tumors.[ ] Several metabolic pathways, such as lysophospholipid metabolism, fatty acid synthase, and glutathione de novo synthes, serve as critical phenotype transformation in EGFR-TKIs resistance.[ , , , , ] Additionally, epigenetic changes such as promoter methylation and histone modifications also contribute to the development of EGFR-TKIs resistance mechanisms in NSCLC.[ ] Resistance to EGFR-TKIs in NSCLC is a complex and multifactorial process, driven by both EGFR-dependent and EGFR-independent mechanisms, necessitating further research, developing novel agents and new treatment stratiges to overcome these challenges.
 

## Treatment of EGFR-TKIs Resistance
As our understanding of resistance mechanisms advances, research efforts have increasingly focused on overcoming these challenges, resulting in significant progress in the development of targeted therapeutic strategies. A prime example is the emergence of third-generation EGFR-TKIs, which have effectively overcome EGFR T790M-mediated resistance, a major limitation of first- and second-generation EGFR-TKIs. The loss of sensitivity to third-generation EGFR-TKIs treatment emerged mainly due to the EGFR C797S mutation. Several approaches to overcoming this resistance include investigating combination therapies and designing novel drugs. Novel drugs include fourth-generation EGFR-TKIs, EGFR mAbs, allosteric inhibitors, peptides, nanobodies, bispecific antibodies, antibody-drug conjugates, etc.[ , ]

## Targeting EGFR-dependent resistance mechanisms
The EGFR T790M mutation is the most common EGFR-dependent resistance mechanism to first- and second-generation EGFR-TKIs. However, third-generation EGFR-TKIs have effectively addressed this issue, overcoming EGFR T790M resistance with remarkable efficacy. Despite this advancement, a new challenge has emerged: overcoming resistance to third-generation EGFR-TKIs. The most prevalent EGFR-dependent resistance mechanism to third-generation EGFR-TKIs is the EGFR C797S mutation, which has become a major focus of research. In response to this challenge, fourth-generation EGFR-TKIs have been developed to target the EGFR C797S mutation and have shown preliminary efficacy in overcoming this resistance. These drugs, including BBT-176 (NCT04820023), JIN-A02 (NCT05394831), BLU-945 (NCT04862780), TQB-3804 (NCT04128085), BPI-361175 (NCT05329298), BLU-701 (NCT05153408), HS-10375 (NCT05435248), WJ13405 (NCT05662670), BAY2927088 (NCT05099172), BDTX-1535 (NCT05256290), QLH11811 (NCT05555212) are candidates to overcome EGFR C797S resistance. Another strategy to overcome EGFR C797S mutation-mediated drug resistance is to target the allosteric site of EGFR. Current allosteric inhibitors under development include EAI001, EAI045, DDC4002, DDC-01-163, JBJ-07-149, JBJ-04-125-02, and JBJ-09-063.[ , ] However, most of these compounds are still in the preclinical or early clinical stages, and further studies are needed to fully assess their potential. Besides, a pooled analysis of phase 1 and 2 studies (WU-KONG1, WU-KONG2, and WU-KONG15) showed that sunvozertinib demonstrated promising efficacy in treating EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients after resistance to prior EGFR-TKIs. It achieved an ORR of 27.5%, with a notably higher ORR of 55.6% in patients with concurrent EGFR-sensitizing and EGFR T790M mutations.[ ] In parallel with the development of novel drugs, there are ongoing studies into existing drug combinations to overcome resistance. For instance, the combination of osimertinib with gefitinib or erlotinib has shown promising efficacy in patients with EGFR T790M/trans-C797S mutations.[ , ] A retrospective study reported that the combination of brigatinib and cetuximab demonstrated an ORR of 60%, whereas brigatinib combined with chemotherapy resulted in an ORR of only 10% in NSCLC patients with EGFR-activating mutation, EGFR T790M and EGFR cis-C797S mutations following progression on osimertinib.[ ] However, prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and further assess the efficacy of these combinations.

## Targeting EGFR-independent resistance mechanisms
Another important resistance mechanism to EGFR-TKIs relies on aberrant activation of bypass signaling pathways and downstream signaling pathways. Given that MET alterations are key resistance mechanisms, MET inhibitors have been investigated to overcome EGFR-TKI resistance.[ ] A phase 1b/2 trial showed that capmatinib plus gefitinib is a promising treatment option for patients with EGFR-mutated, MET-dysregulated NSCLC, particularly those with MET amplification, with an ORR of 27%.[ ] The INSIGHT study demonstrated that tepotinib plus gefitinib improved PFS and OS compared to standard chemotherapy in patients with EGFR-mutant, EGFR T790M-negative NSCLC with MET overexpression or amplification who had progressed on prior EGFR-TKI therapy.[ ] In the INSIGHT-2 study, tepotinib plus osimertinib showed an ORR of 50.0% in EGFR-mutated NSCLC patients with MET amplification to first-line osimertinib treatment.[ ] The TATTON study showed that osimertinib plus savolitinib had an ORR of 33% in osimertinib-resistant, advanced NSCLC patients with MET amplification.[ ] The MARIPOSA-2 study demonstrated that for NSCLC patients harboring EGFR-sensitive mutations after disease progression on osimertinib, both amivantamab-chemotherapy and amivantamab-lazertinib-chemotherapy improved PFS and intracranial PFS than chemotherapy alone.[ ] Oncogenic fusions, such as RET, ROS1, and ALK, are relatively rare, and studies addressing the overcoming of EGFR-TKI resistance in the context of these fusions is limited. A case report showed that osimertinib, in combination with pralsetinib—an RET inhibitor—demonstrated promising efficacy in overcoming osimertinib resistance caused by a novel intergenic ANK3-RET fusion in EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients, with an DoR exceeding 12 months.[ ] Another retrospective study showed that two EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients with CCDC6-RET fusion, who developed resistance to osimertinib, responded well and tolerated the combination therapy of osimertinib and RET inhibitor pralsetinib (BLU-667).[ ] Another case report identified GOPC-ROS1 rearrangement as an acquired resistance mechanism to osimertinib, with subsequent response to osimertinib plus crizotinib.[ ] In an NSCLC patient with EML4-ALK fusion and EGFR 19del who developed resistance to osimertinib, combination therapy with osimertinib and alectinib was effective and well-tolerated, yielding both intracranial and extracranial responses.[ ] Another case report described a patient resistant to osimertinib, with AGK-BRAF fusion, who showed a PR to treatment with osimertinib and trametinib. However, after five months of treatment, the patient developed intolerable subacute colonic microperforation.[ ] The downstream signaling pathways of EGFR include the MAPK, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, and JAK/STAT pathways, which may also serve as potential targets for overcoming EGFR-TKI resistance. A phase 1b trial demonstrated that in patients with advanced EGFR-mutant NSCLC resistant to osimertinib, combination therapy with osimertinib and selumetinib, a MEK1/2 inhibitor, resulted in an ORR of 42%, with a DoR of 16.6 months.[ ] A retrospective study showed that NSCLC patients with BRAF V600E-mediated resistance to osimertinib treated with a three-drug combination of osimertinib, dabrafenib, and trametinib demonstrated an ORR of 61.5%, a DCR of 92.3%, and a median PFS of 13.5 months.[ ] Regarding PI3K/AKT/mTOR and JAK/STAT pathways, although preclinical studies have suggested that combining dactolisib (a dual PI3K and mTOR inhibitor) or golidocitinib (AZD4205, a highly selective JAK1 inhibitor) with osimertinib may overcome resistance through these pathways, further study is underway, and more clinical studies are needed to validate the efficacy and safety of these combinations.[ , ] The emergence of CDK4/6 amplification has been identified as a biomarker for primary resistance to EGFR-TKIs, including osimertinib.[ ] A case report showed a patient with EGFR 19 del, PIK3CA mutation, and CDK4 amplification demonstrated an ongoing PR for over 10 months after receiving a combination of osimertinib and palbociclib (a CDK4/6 inhibitor) treatment. However, during this period, the patient experienced transient pneumonitis. Limited preclinical and clinical studies exist on the combination of CDK4/6 inhibitors with osimertinib in patients resistant to osimertinib, indicating the need for further investigation.

## Chemotherapy, anti-angiogenesis, immunotherapy, and antibody-drug conjugate
Although numerous EGFR-dependent and EGFR-independent resistance mechanisms to EGFR-TKIs have been identified, 30–50% of these mechanisms remain unknown.[ ] For patients lacking identifiable, targetable resistance mechanisms, targeted therapy is not a viable option, making chemotherapy, anti-angiogenesis, and immunotherapy the most practical approach. The IMPRESS study demonstrated that adding gefitinib to chemotherapy did not improve median PFS (5.4 vs. 5.4 months, HR 0.86 [95% CI 0.65–1.13]; P = 0.27) compared to chemotherapy alone and was detrimental to median OS (13.4 vs. 19.5 months, HR 1.44 [95% CI 1.07–1.94]; P = 0.016) in patients with EGFR-mutant advanced NSCLC who had developed acquired resistance to first-line gefitinib.[ ] A phase 2 trial demonstrated that osimertinib plus chemotherapy as a second-line treatment did not extend median PFS compared to osimertinib monotherapy in EGFR mutation-positive NSCLC patients who experienced disease progression with the emergence of EGFR T790M resistance mutation during first-line EGFR-TKI treatment (14.6 vs. 15.8 months [95% CI 0.51–2.32], P = 0.83).[ ] For NSCLC patients with acquired resistance to gefitinib, erlotinib or afatinib, afatinib combined with bevacizumab demonstrated an ORR of 18.8%, a DCR of 90.7%, and a median PFS of 6.3 months.[ ] A retrospective study demonstrated that the combination of osimertinib and apatinib in EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients who developed osimertinib resistance resulted in an ORR of 12.8% and a DCR of 79.5%.[ ] A phase 2 trial demonstrated that osimertinib plus anlotinib demonstrated an ORR of 48.4%, a DCR of 100%, and a median PFS of 16.7 months for advanced NSCLC with EGFR T790M mutation after failure of prior EGFR-TKIs treatment.[ ] However, the BOOSTER trial demonstrated that median PFS had no difference between osimertinib plus bevacizumab and osimertinib alone for second-line treatment of patients with EGFR-mutant advanced NSCLC and acquired EGFR T790M mutations after failure on previous EGFR-TKI treatment (15.4 vs. 12.3 months, HR 0.96 [95% CI 0.68–1.37], P = 0.83).[ ] A phase 2 trial reported an ORR of 50.0%, a DCR of 87.5%, a median PFS of 7.0 months, and a median OS of 23.5 months with toripalimab plus chemotherapy as a second-line treatment for patients with advanced EGFR-mutant NSCLC who had progressed on first-line EGFR-TKIs and did not harbor the EGFR T790M mutation.[ ] For EGFR-mutant NSCLC who progressed after receiving EGFR-TKIs treatment, the ORIENT-31 study demonstrated that sintilimab plus bevacizumab biosimilar IBI30 plus cisplatin and pemetrexed significantly prolonged median PFS compared to chemotherapy alone (6.9 vs. 4.3 months, HR 0.46 [95% CI 0.34–0.64], P <0.0001).[ ] Based on the results of ORIENT-31 study, the NMPA approved sintilimab in combination with a bevacizumab biosimilar and chemotherapy for the treatment of non-squamous NSCLC with EGFR mutations that have progressed after EGFR-TKI treatment on May 9, 2023. The HARMONi-A study demonstrated that ivonescimab plus chemotherapy significantly improved median PFS with a manageable safety profile in patients who had failed EGFR-TKIs compared to chemotherapy alone (7.06 vs. 4.80 months, HR 0.46 [95% CI 0.34–0.62], P <0.0001).[ ] Based on the results of HARMONi-A study, the NMPA approved ivonescimab plus chemotherapy for the treatment of non-squamous NSCLC with EGFR mutations that have progressed after EGFR-TKI treatment on May 24, 2024. Despite progress in overcoming EGFR-TKI resistance, these strategies failed to significantly prolong OS after disease progression of EGFR-TKIs treatment.[ , , ] Sacituzumab tirumotecan (sac-TMT) is an antibody-drug conjugate targeting trophoblast cell-surface antigen 2. The phase 3 OptiTROP-Lung04 (SKB264-Ⅲ-09) study showed that sac-TMT prolonged median PFS and median OS compared to platinum-based chemotherapy in patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC that had progressed after EGFR-TKI therapy.[ ] Based on the results of SKB264-Ⅲ-09 study, the NMPA approved sac-TMT for the treatment of non-squamous NSCLC with EGFR mutations that had progressed after EGFR-TKI treatment on September 30, 2025. The phase 2 OptiTROP-Lung03 (SKB264-II-08) study demonstrated that sac-TMT increased ORR and prolonged median PFS compared to docetaxel in patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC after previous treatment failure with EGFR-TKI and platinum-based chemotherapy.[ ] Based on the results of SKB264-II-08 study, the NMPA approved sac-TMT for the treatment of non-squamous NSCLC with EGFR mutations that had progressed after EGFR-TKI therapy and platinum-based chemotherapy on March 4, 2025. Future efforts should focus on simultaneously developing new drugs and optimizing drug combinations, while better addressing resistance mechanisms to improve treatment outcomes. ## Management Strategies for EGFR-TKI-Related AEs

## Cutaneous AEs
Common ≥Grade 3 cutaneous AEs associated with EGFR-TKI treatment include rash and paronychia, with the incidences of 2.0–16.2% and 0.4–7.0%, respectively.[ , , , , , , , , ] To optimize the management of cutaneous AEs during EGFR-TKI treatment, patient education prior to treatment is crucial. Preventive measures include regular moisturization of dry skin using a thick, alcohol-free emollient, minimizing sun exposure, applying a broad-spectrum sunscreen with sun protection factor (SPF) ≥15, and avoiding products that may exacerbate skin dryness, such as soaps, alcohol-based products, and long hot showers. For grade 1 cutaneous AEs, continuation of EGFR-TKI therapy is typically appropriate, and no intervention may be necessary. However, topical hydrocortisone or clindamycin can be considered. For grade 2 cutaneous AEs, EGFR-TKI therapy can generally be continued, with treatment options including topical hydrocortisone, clindamycin, or pimecrolimus, along with the addition of oral doxycycline or minocycline. In the event of grade 3/4 cutaneous AEs, discontinuation of EGFR-TKI therapy is recommended, and the management strategy aligns with that for grade 2 AEs, with the addition of intensified methylprednisolone therapy.[ , , ] Multidisciplinary care, including consultation with dermatology, is strongly advised for the effective management of severe AEs.

## Digestive system AEs

## Diarrhea
The incidence of ≥grade 3 diarrhea during EGFR-TKI treatment ranges from 0.9–14.4%.[ , , , , , , , ] For grade 1/2 diarrhea, EGFR-TKI treatment may be continued with appropriate monitoring, while Grade 3/4 diarrhea necessitates dose reduction or treatment discontinuation. Dietary modifications play a crucial role in managing diarrhea. Patients are advised to avoid foods that exacerbate symptoms, such as greasy, spicy, and fried items, as well as foods that are difficult to digest. Ensuring adequate fluid and electrolyte intake is essential to prevent dehydration and electrolyte imbalances associated with diarrhea. Pharmacological management is typically limited to the use of diosmectite and loperamide. In cases where diarrhea persists despite EGFR-TKI dose adjustment and treatment with diosmectite and loperamide, the use of octreotide may be considered.[ , , ]

## Elevated aminotransferase
The incidence of ≥grade 3 elevated aminotransferases during EGFR-TKI treatment ranges from 0–26.3%.[ , , , , , , , ] For patients with grade 1/2 aminotransferase elevations, continuation of EGFR-TKI treatment is generally feasible, alongside the administration of medications for drug-induced liver injury, such as N-acetylcysteine, bicyclol, glycyrrhizic acid preparations, and silymarin. For grade 3/4 aminotransferase elevations, discontinuation of EGFR-TKI therapy is recommended, along with treatment using the same medications as for grade 1/2 elevations. In severe cases, corticosteroid therapy may also be considered. Resuming the same EGFR-TKI or switching to an alternative EGFR-TKI after liver function recovery remains a matter of clinical judgment, as no definitive consensus or guidelines currently exist; an individualized approach is recommended.[ ]

## Other AEs
Other common AEs associated with EGFR-TKI treatment included hematological toxicities (e.g., absolute neutrophil count decreased, white blood cell count decreased, lymphocyte decreased, platelets decreased, and anaemia), systemic effects (e.g., fatigue and decreased appetite), and metabolic disturbances (e.g., electrolyte imbalances). EGFR-TKI-related AEs are usually tolerable and manageable. However, serious AEs, including lung injury (e.g., interstitial lung disease), although occur infrequently, can lead to EGFR-TKI treatment-related deaths and represent a serious concern.[ ] Other potential AEs involve cardiovascular effects, renal abnormalities, epistaxis, and alopecia.

## Discussion
The advent of EGFR-TKI therapy has pioneered a new era in targeted therapy for lung cancer, representing a pivotal breakthrough in the management of EGFR-mutant NSCLC. EGFR-TKIs have dramatically improved OS, extending it to 38.6 months compared to just 10–12 months in chemotherapy era, while also enhancing patients’ quality of life.[ , , ] The incorporation of these therapies into numerous clinical guidelines highlights their essential role as a cornerstone of treatment, solidifying their role as a critical component of standard care for patients with EGFR mutations.[ , , , , , , , , , , , , ] However, limitations remain for first-line EGFR-TKI monotherapy in terms of OS improvement. Although osimertinib has achieved approximately seven months longer OS than gefitinib or erlotinib, this improvement was observed only in the EGFR 19del subgroup and not in the EGFR L858R subgroup.[ , ] Other third-generation EGFR-TKIs such as aumolertinib, furmonertinib, befotertinib, rezivertinib, rilertinib, limertinib, and lazertinib require more mature OS data. In the current treatment paradigm, combination therapies have emerged as a promising avenue for enhancing efficacy. Two phase 3 trials demonstrated that gefitinib plus chemotherapy significantly prolongs both PFS and OS compared to gefitinib alone,[ , ] though did not get approval up to now. The FLAURA2 study reported prolonged PFS and OS with osimertinib plus chemotherapy in the first-line setting,[ , ] this combination has been approved by both the FDA and the NMPA. However, the suitability of osimertinib plus chemotherapy as the standard first-line treatment for all patients with advanced EGFR-mutant NSCLC remains under active debate, particularly with regard to the balance between efficacy and toxicity, as well as the manageability in clinical practice.[ , ] Combining EGFR-TKIs with anti-angiogenic agents has demonstrated PFS improvements;[ , , , , ] however, no approved indications exist for such combinations. Thus, further studies are needed to optimize these regimens. Conversely, EGFR-TKI and ICIs combinations have encountered challenges due to elevated toxicity.[ , ] However, strategic patient selection based on NSCLC immune microenvironment characteristics and advancements in AE management may renew interest in this approach. Additionally, combining EGFR-TKIs with novel targeted agents offers an exciting avenue. For instance, the MARIPOSA study showed that first-line lazertinib plus amivantamab significantly improved PFS over osimertinib,[ ] receiving the FDA and the NMPA approval. Yet, further validation is necessary to assess whether similar benefits can be observed with other EGFR-TKIs. As first-line therapy options expand, optimizing personalized treatment strategies becomes essential.
Rare EGFR mutations, such as exon 20 insertions, continue to be an active study focus. Sunvozertinib, already approved by the NMPA for second-line use due to the promising efficacy,[ ] yet its potential as a first-line treatment still required determination in phase 3 trial (NCT05668988). Additionally, encouraging results from the first-line treatment of mefatinib for uncommon mutations such as G719X, S768I, and L861Q, currently included in the breakthrough therapy designation program by the NMPA, highlight the potential for expanding EGFR-TKI options for uncommon mutations.
Notably, the therapeutic landscape of EGFR-TKIs is evolving toward increasingly precise molecular stratification and personalized intervention. Previous EGFR-TKIs were broadly approved for the treatment of NSCLC harboring EGFR sensitive mutations, yet lacked specificity for distinct molecular subpopulations. In contrast, recent approved EGFR-TKIs has been developed to target highly specific molecular subtypes and unmet clinical needs, marking a critical advancement in precision oncology. For instance, mifanertinib has been approved by the NMPA specifically for NSCLC patients with EGFR L858R mutation, while the NMPA accepted NDA of adonertinib (FHND9041) for the treatment of NSCLC harboring EGFR exon 19 deletions on February 4, 2026. Furthermore, zorifertinib has been approved by the NMPA for NSCLC patients with sensitizing EGFR mutations complicated by brain metastases, and sunvozertinib has been approved for EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations by the NMPA. On February 6, 2026, a new indication for furmonertinib targeting EGFR PACC mutations has been accepted for regulatory review by the NMPA, further expanding the scope of precision stratification. This evolutionary trend reflects a shift toward tailored regimens aligned with specific molecular signatures and clinical features, enabling a more precise patient stratification and more personalized therapeutic strategies.
The emergence of resistance to EGFR-TKIs remains a formidable challenge, limiting their long-term efficacy and necessitating ongoing investigations into the underlying mechanisms of resistance to inform future therapeutic strategies. Recent innovations, including the development of fourth-generation EGFR-TKIs and various combination therapies, demonstrate potential in overcoming resistance. Several fourth-generation EGFR-TKIs, including BBT-176, JIN-A02, BLU-945, TQB-3804, BPI-361175, CH7233163, BI4020, LS-106, JND3229, QLH11811 and AZ7608, are under development, providing new hope for patients. The results from the ORIENT-31[ ] and HARMONi-A[ ] suggest that EGFR-mutant patients indeed benefit from ICIs, highlighting the need for further mechanistic studies to better understand and mitigate AEs observed in prior EGFR-TKI and ICIs combination studies. In perioperative settings, the EVIDENCE and ADAURA studies have established the role of adjuvant EGFR-TKI therapy.[ , , , ] However, neoadjuvant EGFR-TKI therapy remains in early exploration, with phase 2 trials showing potential but requiring phase 3 trials for validation.[ , , , , , , ] The LAURA and POLESTAR study demonstrated that maintenance osimertinib or aumolertinib after chemoradiotherapy significantly improved median PFS in unresectable, stage III EGFR-mutant NSCLC.[ , ] Further studies are needed to determine optimal maintenance durations and explore similar benefits with other EGFR-TKIs.
Over the past 26 years, continuous advancements in EGFR-TKI research have emerged, highlighting the critical importance of targeted therapy and personalized management throughout the treatment course, significantly prolonged survival and improved quality of life for EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients compared to traditional chemotherapy. A multidisciplinary approach is essential, which not only optimizes treatment strategies but also ensures the effective management of AEs, thereby delivering comprehensive care to patients.
