## ABSTRACT
Comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) is useful for optimizing targeted therapy and immunotherapy strategies for thoracic malignancies. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical utility and diagnostic complementarity of the in‐house sequencing platform Rapid‐Neo. We retrospectively analyzed 110 patients with thoracic malignancies who underwent Rapid‐Neo testing. The baseline characteristics, sequencing results, concordance with companion diagnostics (CDx), and clinical outcomes were assessed. Of 110 patients, 100 (90.9%) had primary lung cancer. Rapid‐Neo identified at least one genomic alteration in 99.1% of cases and well‐established driver alterations in 66.0% of lung cancer cases. TMB‐high and MSI‐high statuses were observed in 9.0% and 2.0% of cases, respectively. Among the 90 cases with prior CDx, Rapid‐Neo identified driver alterations in 10.0% of the cases, including EGFR, KRAS, MET, RET, and ERBB2, suggesting its potential to overcome the limitations of conventional CDx tests. High concordance (96.8%) was observed between the Rapid‐Neo and CDx results, finally. In EGFR‐mutant lung adenocarcinoma, high tumor mutation burden (TMB) was associated with a significantly shorter progression‐free survival (PFS) after EGFR‐TKI therapy (HR = 2.58, p = 0.018) and remained an independent prognostic factor in multivariate analysis. Furthermore, among patients receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), favorable genomic markers such as TMB‐high or MSI‐high were associated with prolonged PFS. Rapid‐Neo demonstrated high sensitivity and concordance with CDx, while also identifying actionable driver alterations missed by the initial CDx. Moreover, the genomic markers identified by Rapid‐Neo may provide predictive values for both targeted therapy and immunotherapy responses, supporting their integration into routine clinical decision‐making.

We clarified the utility and challenges of in‐house comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) testing in lung cancer care.

Among the 100 lung cancer cases, eight patients had driver fusions detected by CDx: four ALK fusions, three RET fusions, and one ROS1 fusion, all of which, except for the ROS1 case, were also detected by Rapid‐Neo (Table  ). In the ROS1‐positive case, the tumor cell content estimated from the genomic data was 20%, which was the lowest among all the fusion‐positive cases. This low tumor content was likely the primary reason for the false‐negative results, potentially compounded by the use of a DNA‐based sequencing panel in Rapid‐Neo, which is known to have limited sensitivity for detecting gene fusions compared to RNA‐based assays, in situ hybridization, and immunohistochemistry [ ].
Of the nine cases in which Rapid‐Neo newly identified driver gene alterations, seven involved genes or variants that were not covered by the initial CDx assay (Table  ). These included one EGFR mutation, one KRAS mutation, two RET fusions, one MET Exon14 skipping, and two ERBB2 mutations. One case of MET Exon14 skipping was within the coverage range of the CDx platform but was not detected, suggesting a potential false‐negative result. In another case, CDx using the Oncomine panel failed due to insufficient DNA quantity, whereas Rapid‐Neo successfully identified an EGFR Exon19 deletion. Among the nine patients, seven underwent subsequent secondary CDx testing, which confirmed the presence of the same driver alterations identified by Rapid‐Neo, including alterations in EGFR, RET, MET, and ERBB2. Of the seven cases with confirmed alterations, six received targeted therapy corresponding to the identified driver gene alterations. Partial responses were observed in three patients with either postoperative recurrence or stage IV disease, all of whom achieved progression‐free survival of more than 6 months. Additionally, one patient with stage II disease harboring an EGFR Exon19 deletion received adjuvant osimertinib after surgery and remained recurrence‐free for over 18 months at the time of data collection.
 These findings suggest that Rapid‐Neo has the potential to complement the initial CDx testing by identifying clinically relevant driver alterations that may be missed because of panel limitations, assay failure, or possible false‐negative results in routine clinical practice.

## Concordance With CDx Testing
Of the 110 patients, 95 (86.4%) underwent CDx testing (Table  ). The majority (92 cases, 96.8%) were patients with lung cancer, whereas the remaining three included two cases of carcinoma of unknown primary and one case of adenoid cystic carcinoma. A comparison between the CDx and Rapid‐Neo results for driver gene alterations demonstrated concordance in 92 of 95 cases (96.8%) (Figure  ).
 The three discordant cases included all patients with lung cancer. One involved an ROS1 fusion that was detected by CDx but not identified by Rapid‐Neo, as described above. The remaining two were part of the nine previously reported cases, where driver gene alterations were newly detected by Rapid‐Neo but missed by the initial CDx. The alterations identified by Rapid‐Neo included skipping KRAS G12C and MET Exon14 skipping, respectively. In both cases, it was difficult to determine whether Rapid‐Neo yielded a false‐positive result or CDx failed to detect a true alteration. Notably, both patients had long‐term stable disease without targeted therapy at the time of analysis, and further CDx testing was planned at the time of progression to reassess driver status and guide potential molecularly targeted treatment.
These findings collectively demonstrated that Rapid‐Neo exhibited high concordance with CDx, showing consistent results in 96.8% of the CDx‐tested cases, regardless of whether a driver alteration was present or absent. Among the 95 patients who underwent both tests, only one case (1.1%) involving ROS1 fusion was discordant and was not detected by Rapid‐Neo. This high level of agreement supports the reliability of Rapid‐Neo as a clinical genomic‐testing platform.

## Prognostic Implications for Targeted Therapy
Next, we assessed whether TMB measured using Rapid‐Neo could predict the outcomes of EGFR‐mutant lung adenocarcinoma. Thirty patients harboring major EGFR mutations (either Exon19 deletion or L858R) who subsequently received EGFR‐TKI monotherapy were stratified into high‐ and low‐TMB groups, using the median TMB value of 3.7 mutations per megabase as the cutoff (Table  ). Baseline characteristics showed that TP53 co‐mutations were significantly more common in the high‐TMB group (81.3% vs. 28.6%, p = 0.009). There was also a trend toward a higher frequency of Exon19 deletions in the high‐TMB group (p = 0.06).
 PFS under EGFR‐TKI group was significantly shorter than that in the low‐TMB group (hazard ratio = 2.58, log‐rank p = 0.018) (Figure  ). In the multivariate Cox proportional hazards model adjusted for age, sex, smoking history, stage, EGFR mutation subtype, and TP53 mutation status, high TMB remained an independent risk factor for shorter PFS (hazard ratio = 1.500, 95% confidence interval, 1.026–2.194; p = 0.036) (Figure  ).

## Potential Biomarkers for Immunotherapy
Among EGFR wild‐type cases, we examined 14 patients who harbored at least one ICI‐related genomic factor of interest (TMB‐high, MSI‐high, ARID1A, ARID2, PBRM1 as favorable markers and STK11, KEAP1, PTEN as unfavorable markers) and who received an ICI‐containing regimen (Figure  ). Eleven patients were positive for at least one “favorable” marker, two were positive for at least one “unfavorable” marker, and one had both favorable and unfavorable markers. Among the 11 favorable cases, seven demonstrated a PFS of ≥ 12 months; those with TMB‐high or MSI‐high (five cases) in particular achieved ≥ 24 months of PFS. In contrast, an unfavorable case progressed within 3 months. Two cases with mixed (both favorable and unfavorable) markers also had ≥ 12 months PFS. Notably, among the favorable group, four had negative (< 1%) PD‐L1 tumor proportion score, yet two of them still achieved ≥ 12 months PFS. Although the sample size was small, these preliminary findings suggest that identifying such genomic markers could help predict ICI responses beyond PD‐L1 alone.

## Discussion
Early CGP integration has shown promise in various studies [ , ], and patients who receive targeted therapy based on genomic testing have shown improved outcomes [ ]. Although NGS‐based CGP is indispensable for precision oncology, its implementation in Japan is limited to select institutions because of the high initial costs and the requirement for specialized personnel. Consequently, patients must be referred to centers capable of performing CGP; however, logistical challenges such as geographic distance, insufficient local infrastructure, and workforce shortages often delay timely access to publicly reimbursed CGP testing [ , , ]. Furthermore, under the current reimbursement framework for lung cancer, most patients undergo single‐ or limited‐gene CDx assays such as the Oncomine DX Target Test or the AmoyDx panel, which are used to detect common driver alterations in genes including EGFR, ALK, and ROS1 early in treatment and only become eligible for CGP once standard therapies are exhausted [ , , ]. This often delays the initiation of targeted therapies and may significantly alter the clinical course [ , ]. These challenges, arising from limited resources and restrictive reimbursement policies, are not unique to Japan but are shared by many countries worldwide.
Our institution has initiated in‐house CGP assays to enhance access to CGP tests. Our in‐house system, Rapid‐Neo, demonstrated both high analytical reliability and a major strength in the ability to complement initial CDx, as evidenced by the findings of this study. Rapid‐Neo achieved a 96.8% concordance rate with the CDx results and provided an additional 10.0% diagnostic yield for driver gene alterations that were not detected by the initial CDx. Notably, even when compared to the Oncomine DX Target Test, a small‐panel assay widely used across Japan and the most commonly used initial CDx at our institution, Rapid‐Neo identified additional driver alterations in 6.2% of cases. Many of these alterations involved genes or specific variants that were not covered by the CDx platform, underscoring the limitations of the panel content and technical sensitivity. These findings emphasize the clinical significance of performing CGP, even in patients with negative results in small‐panel assays, because such testing can reveal clinically actionable alterations that may guide molecularly targeted therapies.
Furthermore, Rapid‐Neo enables the simultaneous assessment of TMB, MSI, and multiple genomic biomarkers that are either established or emerging as predictors of therapeutic response to agents such as EGFR‐TKIs and ICIs, which are features that are not typically evaluated by small‐panel assays. In our exploratory analyses, these genomic features demonstrate potential clinical relevance. Consistent with previous reports [ ], a high TMB was associated with inferior PFS in EGFR‐mutant lung cancers treated with EGFR‐TKIs. In the context of ICI therapy, a threshold of 10 mutations per megabase is often used to define TMB‐high status. However, as noted by Offin et al. [ ] TMB tends to be lower in patients with EGFR‐mutant lung cancers than in those with wild‐type EGFR non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). In our cohort, which exclusively included EGFR‐mutant cases, no patient reached the TMB‐high threshold of 10 mutations per megabase. Therefore, following the methodology adopted by Offin et al. we stratified patients within our cohort based on the absolute value of TMB and found clinically meaningful heterogeneity in TMB levels within this oncogene‐defined population. TP53 co‐mutations are adverse prognostic factors in EGFR‐mutant NSCLC [ ]. However, our multivariate analysis indicated that TMB may serve as a more robust predictor of EGFR‐TKI outcomes than TP53 status alone, further supporting earlier findings in the literature.
Similarly, previously reported ICI‐related biomarkers, including high TMB, high MSI, and alterations in ARID1A, ARID2, PBRM1, STK11, KEAP1, and PTEN were linked to outcomes, although our cohort was limited in size [ , , , , ]. Collectively, these results support the potential utility of CGP not only for identifying actionable alterations but also for informing prognostic stratification in clinical practice.
From a systems perspective, the implementation of Rapid‐Neo contributed to the early and broad integration of the CGP within our institution. As discussed later as one of its limitations, Rapid‐Neo lacks a companion diagnostic function; however, it can be performed at any point during the clinical course. This accessibility lowers the threshold for physicians to order testing and may encourage the broader utilization of genomic profiling. Therefore, Rapid‐Neo serves not only as a diagnostic tool but also as a gateway for subsequent CGP testing. Since the launch of Rapid‐Neo, the number of CGP tests submitted under the national insurance system in our department has steadily increased (Figure  ), likely reflecting an enhanced familiarity with genomic medicine among clinicians, stronger motivation to pursue insured CGP, and improved institutional infrastructure for specimen management and data interpretation.
However, a notable limitation is the detection of certain RNA‐level fusion genes using DNA‐based approaches. Optimization with RNA‐based sequencing or sensitive methodologies could further improve the detection rate of gene fusions, such as ROS1. Another limitation is that the test is frequently performed on surgical specimens because of the need to consider the volume of the remaining specimen. Optimizing the specimen extraction method may help minimize specimen loss. Although the Rapid‐Neo test offers high usability, its lack of a companion function necessitates careful consideration when applied to cases with limited remaining specimens. Specifically, if the entire specimen is used for rapid Neo testing, additional companion diagnostic tests may no longer be feasible. Therefore, appropriate case selection is essential.
Taken together, these findings support the feasibility and added value of Rapid‐Neo as a reliable and informative in‐house CGP platform that aligns with and enhances the conventional diagnostic workflow. By enabling early and expanded access to actionable genomic data, Rapid‐Neo has the potential to improve clinical decision‐making, facilitate timely therapy selection, and foster the broad adoption of CGP in real‐world clinical settings. Although the in‐house nature of Rapid‐Neo imposes certain limitations, we believe that it allows for greater flexibility in adapting the panel design to incorporate emerging biomarkers and newly approved therapeutic targets. As such, we consider the routine use of in‐house CGP in daily clinical practice to be a necessary and justifiable investment for maintaining high‐quality personalized cancer care.
In this retrospective study, the in‐house CGP system Rapid‐Neo demonstrated high concordance with CDx tests and showed added diagnostic value by identifying driver gene alterations that were missed by initial small‐panel CDx, including the widely used Oncomine DX Target Test. It also provides additional genomic insights into biomarkers such as TMB, MSI, and immune‐related alterations that are not captured by conventional small‐panel assays. The implementation of an in‐house CGP system can mitigate delays, expand CGP testing to earlier lines of therapy, and refine treatment decisions based on detailed genomic data. Large prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and optimize the CGP workflow for routine clinical practice.
