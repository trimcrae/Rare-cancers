## Abstract

## Background
RET fusions are significant oncogenic drivers, resulting from chromosomal rearrangements of the RET proto-oncogene with various partner genes. Understanding their structural characteristics is crucial for elucidating oncogenic potential and developing targeted therapies.

## Methods
This study analyzed 21,023 tumor samples and 3716 peripheral blood samples from a Chinese pan-cancer cohort using DNA or RNA-targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS). 19,668 tissues underwent DNA-based fusion detection and 1355 NSCLC tissues underwent RNA-based fusion detection using a 733-gene panel. ctDNA-based targeted NGS was also performed on blood samples.

## Results
RET fusions were detected in 1.027 % of tissue samples, predominantly lung and thyroid cancers. Compared to Western populations (87 % in intron 11), Chinese patients show a shift toward the exon 10–11 region, with 30.79 % in intron 10. RET rearrangements were classified into four categories (Simple Reciprocal Inversion, Co-Fusion, Single Fusion-Common, Single Fusion-Rare) with unique mutational profiles and tumor mutational burden scores. RNA-based NGS revealed some DNA-detected rearrangements might not undergo transcription, while Co-Fusion indicated potential simultaneous transcription of multiple RET fusions. An NSCLC patient with KIF5B-RET and ATRNL1-RET co-fusions achieved 15-month progression-free survival on RET-targeted therapy.

## Conclusion
This study underscores the importance of structural insights for developing targeted therapies against RET fusion-driven cancers and highlights the need for further investigation into complex RET fusion mechanisms to better understand RET-driven oncogenesis.

## Introduction
RET fusions, particularly prevalent in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), represent a significant class of oncogenic drivers resulting from the chromosomal rearrangement of the RET proto-oncogene with various partner genes. Understanding the structural characteristics of these fusion genes is crucial for elucidating their oncogenic potential and developing targeted therapies [ , ]. The RET proto-oncogene encodes a receptor tyrosine kinase that plays a crucial role in cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival. Unlike other receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) ligands, RET ligands such as glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF), artemin (ARTN), persephin (PSPN), and neurturin (NRTN) exhibit a distinctive binding mechanism [ , , ]. These GDNF family ligands (GFLs) associate with GDNF family receptors (GFRs). Upon calcium ion binding to the cadherin-like domain of RET, the GFL-GFR complex facilitates the recruitment of RET, positioning two RET monomers in close proximity [ ]. This receptor homodimerization subsequently induces cross-phosphorylation of critical tyrosine residues within the intracellular domains of RET [ ].
In the context of RET fusions, however, the gene undergoes significant structural alterations that lead to constitutive activation of the kinase domain, independent of ligand binding [ ]. A typical RET fusion gene comprises the 5′ portion of a partner gene fused to the 3′ portion of the RET gene, which includes the tyrosine kinase domain. This fusion structure results in several critical changes. Contribution of dimerization domains: most RET fusion partners contribute a dimerization domain, such as a coiled-coil domain, which is essential for the constitutive dimerization of the fusion protein. This dimerization mimics the ligand-induced dimerization of wild-type RET, leading to continuous activation of the kinase domain [ ]. Constitutive kinase activation: The RET kinase domain in these fusions remains intact and is constitutively activated due to the dimerization induced by the fusion partner. This continuous activation drives downstream signaling pathways such as MAPK, PI3K/AKT, and JAK/STAT, thereby promoting oncogenesis [ ]. Loss of regulatory elements: The fusion often results in the loss of regulatory elements that normally control RET activation and localization. Without these regulatory sequences, the fusion protein exhibits altered cellular localization and enhanced signaling capacity [ ]. Gain of function: The fusion not only results in the constitutive activation of RET kinase activity but also often confers new properties on the protein, such as altered cellular localization and interaction with different molecular machinery within the cell. This can lead to the activation of additional oncogenic pathways and contribute to the complexity of cancer phenotypes [ ].For instance, the KIF5B-RET fusion, one of the most common RET fusions in lung cancer, involves the fusion of the coiled-coil domain of KIF5B with the RET kinase domain. This fusion leads to the constitutive kinase activity of RET without the need for ligand binding, thereby driving the proliferation and survival of cancer cells [ ].
RET fusions are not confined to NSCLC but are also found across a spectrum of other cancer types. For example, RET fusions are present in approximately 1–2 % of NSCLC cases and up to 10–20 % of papillary thyroid carcinomas [ , , ]. Additionally, RET fusions have been identified in a variety of other malignancies, including colorectal cancer, breast cancer, and pancreatic cancer, though at lower frequencies [ ] Understanding the structural features of RET fusions is crucial for developing effective targeted therapies. Drugs like selpercatinib and pralsetinib, which inhibit RET's kinase activity, have shown significant efficacy against tumors driven by RET fusions. These therapies target the constitutively active kinase domain, underscoring the importance of structural insights in treatment design [ , , ]. RET fusions, characterized by the fusion of a dimerization domain from a partner gene with RET's kinase domain, drive oncogenic activity. This structural knowledge is essential for developing precise inhibitors targeting the aberrant signaling pathways in RET fusion-driven cancers.

## Materials and methods

## Patients and samples
To elucidate the genomic features of RET fusion across various solid cancers in Chinese patients, this study analyzed 21,023 tumor samples for DNA or RNA-targeted Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) and 3716 peripheral blood samples for ctDNA-targeted NGS derived from a comprehensive Chinese pan-cancer cohort. Tumor tissues were formalin fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE), 19,668 tissues from pan-caner were chosen for DNA-based fusion detection by 733 gene panel (3DMed Onco™ GPS Tissue, China) and other 1355 tissues from NSCLC were chosen for RNA-based fusion detection by 35 gene panel (3DMed Once Core™ Tissue Kit).

## DNA next-generation sequencing for tissue samples
The commercial NGS panel used in the 19,668 pan-cancer cohort analysis was a targeted sequencing panel of 733 genes (3DMed Onco™ GPS Tissue, China). Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections were first assessed for tumor cell content with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, and only samples with over 20 % tumor content were analyzed. The FFPE sections were deparaffinized, digested with lysis buffer and proteinase K at 56 °C overnight, and incubated at 80 °C for 4 h to reverse crosslinks. Genomic DNA was extracted using the ReliaPrep™ FFPE gDNA Miniprep System and quantified with the Qubit™ double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) High Sensitivity (HS) Assay Kit. Libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, California, USA) with a mean depth of 1000X. Raw data from paired samples were mapped to the human genome reference hg19 using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (version 0.7.12). PCR duplicates were removed, and sequence metrics were collected using Picard (v1.130) and SAMtools (version 1.1.19). Variant calling focused on therapeutic, prognostic, or diagnostic regions per the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines. Somatic single nucleotide variants (SNVs) were detected using a custom pipeline based on a binomial test model. Local realignment was performed to identify insertions and deletions (indels). Variants were then filtered according to unique supporting read depth, strand bias, and base quality, ensuring high-confidence calls [ ].

## ctDNA-based targeted panel next-generation sequencing
Blood samples were processed within 2 h of collection by centrifuging in Streck tubes at 1600 g for 10 min at 4 °C. Approximately 5 ml of plasma supernatant was transferred to a new tube and centrifuged again at 16,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove any remaining cells and debris. The supernatant was then used for cell-free DNA (cfDNA) extraction using the QiAmp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. DNA concentration was measured with the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Genomic DNA (gDNA) from white blood cells was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen). Cell-free DNA libraries were prepared with the KAPA Hyper Prep Kit (KAPA Biosystems) following the manufacturer's protocol and were uniquely barcoded with molecular identifiers (UMI). Briefly, 30–60 ng of cfDNA underwent end-repair, A-tailing, and ligation with indexed adapters. The libraries were PCR-amplified, purified for target enrichment, and their concentration and size distribution were determined using a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a LabChip GX Touch HT Analyzer (PerkinElmer), respectively. The captured libraries were then sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina) with 100 bp paired-end reads. All variants were filtered through an automated false positive filtering pipeline to ensure sensitivity and specificity at an allele frequency (AF) of ≥ 1 %. Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and insertions/deletions (indels) were annotated using ANNOVAR against databases such as dbSNP (v138), 1000 Genomes, and ESP6500 (population frequency > 0.015). Only missense, stopgain, frameshift, and non-frameshift indel mutations were retained. Copy number variations (CNVs) and gene rearrangements were detected as previously described [ ].

## Definition of TMB and MSI-H
Tumor Mutational Burden (TMB) was defined as the number of non-synonymous somatic single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and indels per megabase in the examined coding regions, excluding driver mutations. This included missense, silent, stop gain, stop loss, in-frame, and frameshift mutations. All SNVs and indels in the coding regions of targeted genes were considered for this calculation. Microsatellite Instability (MSI) was determined using 100 selected microsatellite loci, with the top 30 loci showing the best coverage used for the final MSI score calculation. An in-house developed R script was employed to analyze the distribution of read counts among different repeat lengths for each microsatellite locus in each sample. The stability of each locus was assessed using the following model:
P(X=ni)=CNinipini(1−pi)Ni−ni, In this model, represents the locus being examined, denotes the cumulative percentage at the cut-point repeat length ( ) of the microsatellite stable (MSS) subtype, is the number of unstable reads, and is the total number of reads for that locus. A locus was considered unstable if the probability ( )was ≤ 0.001. The MSI score was defined as the percentage of unstable loci. Samples with an MSI score of ≥ 0.4 were classified as microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H), while those below this threshold were classified as MSS.

## RNA preparation and next-generation sequencing (NGS)
Compared to DNA-based NGS, RNA-based NGS improves the detection rate of fusion genes by eliminating the impact of introns. For this study, 1355 NSCLC tissue samples were selected for RNA-based fusion detection using the 3DMed Onco™ Core Tissue Kit (3D Medicines, Inc.). DNA and RNA were extracted from the same FFPE samples, either as section curls or slides. Quantities of DNA and RNA were measured to meet minimum test requirements, and cDNA was synthesized from the RNA. The DNA and cDNA were loaded into the Library Cartridge of the 3DMed Onco™ Core Tissue Kit (3D Medicines, Inc.), utilizing RNase H-dependent PCR (rhAmp PCR) technology, which minimizes primer dimers and allows for highly multiplexed panels. The Library Cartridge was processed in the Automated NGS Prep System ANDiS 500 (3D Medicines, Inc.) for amplicon library preparation, involving two rounds of PCR amplifications and purifications. The first round used specific rhAmp primers, followed by magnetic bead purification. The second round added index primers with sample indexes and P5/P7 sequences, followed by another purification. Amplicon libraries for both controls were prepared, quantified, normalized, pooled, and sequenced on the Illumina platform (Illumina, Inc.). Sequencing data were analyzed using TiNAiLab software (3D Medicines, Inc.).

## Results

## Distribution of RET fusion in pan-cancer
From January 2021 to December 2022, DNA-based targeted NGS analysis was performed on 23,384 pan-solid tumor samples from the Chinese population. Among these, 19,668 were tissue samples, with 1.027 % (N = 202) found to have pathogenic RET rearrangements — defined as those that preserve the full-length RET kinase domain, retain the complete set of transcriptional regulatory elements, and occur in a direction consistent with the transcriptional orientation. This included 181 cases of lung cancer, 10 cases of colorectal cancer, 1 case of gastric cancer, and 10 cases of other solid tumors, including breast cancer and ovarian cancer. Additionally, 3716 samples were from peripheral blood, with 0.404 % (N = 15) found to have pathogenic RET rearrangements, including 13 cases of lung cancer, 1 case of colorectal cancer, and 1 case of gastric cancer ( A). Subsequently, we compared the distribution of RET rearrangements across different cancer types ( B). The highest frequency of RET rearrangements was found in thyroid cancer at 4.92 %, followed by lung cancer at 1.75 %, urothelial cancer at 0.29 %, ovarian cancer at 0.28 %, and colorectal cancer at 0.22 %. The overall proportion of pathogenic RET rearrangements across all pan-solid tumor samples was 0.93 % (N = 217). This discrepancy may be related to the varying proportions of different cancer types undergoing NGS testing. Among the 23,384 samples, the majority were from lung cancer, accounting for 89.4 %, followed by colorectal cancer (5.07 %) and thyroid cancer (1.38 %) ( C). This reflects the real-world clinical tendency for NGS testing across different cancer types. Lung cancer, having the most approved targeted therapies, understandably has the highest proportion of multi-gene NGS testing.
 

## Mutational profiles of RET fusion‑positive pan-cancer patients
Genomic alterations were identified in 217 RET fusion-positive samples, encompassing fusions, SNVs, CNVs, and Multi_Hit mutations. The RET fusion-positive cohort comprised 54.4 % females and 45.6 % males, with 67.3 % of the patients being under the age of 65. No MSI-H RET-positive patients were detected, and 10.6 % of the patients exhibited a TMB greater than 10 Muts/Mb. Analysis of the gene mutation landscape revealed that over one-third of the KIF5B-RET cohort had minimal co-mutations, while CCDC6-RET rarely co-occurred with TP53 mutations. The top 10 mutated genes were predominantly different RET fusion variants, with TP53 co-mutation frequency at only 26 %. Other significant co-mutations included MDM2, FRS2, and NKX2–1, each with a frequency of 8 % ( ). Of particular interest were the variations in RET fusion forms, not only in terms of fusion partners but also the nature of the fusions themselves. For instance, some patients exhibited paired classic fusions such as KIF5B-RET and RET-KIF5B, while others showed only the "forward fusion" KIF5B-RET. Additionally, a subset of patients had multiple distinct RET fusions detected simultaneously.
 

## Structural characteristics of RET rearrangements and differences in co-mutation profiles
To explore the characteristics of different pathogenic RET rearrangements from the 217 samples across pan-caner, a comprehensive analysis was conducted to investigate the distribution and frequency of different types of gene rearrangements. The results revealed that conventional 5′ to 3′ rearrangements constitute the majority of RET rearrangements, accounting for 90 % of the detected events. Interestingly, we also identified less common rearrangement types, including 5′ to 5′ and 3′ to 3′ rearrangements. Specifically, 5′ to 5′ rearrangements comprised 4.52 % of the total RET rearrangements, while 3′ to 3′ rearrangements accounted for 5.08 % ( A). These findings highlight the diversity of RET rearrangements present in solid tumors and suggest potential novel mechanisms of RET activation that warrant further investigation. Further analysis revealed that many samples exhibited more than one type of RET rearrangement, indicating the presence of multiple fusion forms. Based on the number and type of fusions, we categorized them into four types: [ ] Simple Reciprocal Inversion, which included both the classic forward fusion and its reverse counterpart and accounted for 37 %; [ ] Co-Fusion, which included two or more RET nonreciprocal rearrangements and accounted for 22 %; [ ] Single Fusion-Common, which included only one rearrangement event with common fusion partners such as KIF5B, CCDC6, or NCOA4 and accounted for 27 %; and [ ] Single Fusion-Rare, which included only one rearrangement event with rare fusion partners other than KIF5B, CCDC6, or NCOA4 and accounted for 14 %. This classification underscored the structural complexity and diversity of RET rearrangements in these samples. Additionally, there was a statistically significant difference in TMB scores among these four groups (P = 0.0195), with the Single Fusion-Rare group exhibiting higher TMB scores ( B). Subsequently, the mutational landscapes of these four types of RET rearrangements were analyzed individually. It was observed that the TP53 co-mutation frequency was remarkably high at 53 % in the Single Fusion-Rare group, while the other three groups exhibited frequencies ranging from 20 % to 23 %. Furthermore, the Simple Reciprocal Inversion group demonstrated co-mutation gene frequencies predominantly below 10 %. In contrast, a more diverse co-mutation profile was identified in the Single Fusion-Rare group, with elevated frequencies of CDKN2A, CDKN2B, and ERBB2 mutations. The Co-Fusion group typically comprised major classic RET rearrangements such as KIF5B-RET or CCDC6-RET, along with some rare RET rearrangement variants ( C).
 

## Structural characteristics of classic RET rearrangements in the Chinese population
Apart from the Single Fusion-Rare group, a significant proportion of classic RET rearrangements were exhibited by the other three groups, particularly the Co-Fusion group. Consequently, a detailed structural analysis of these typical RET rearrangements was proceeded with. The DNA structures resulting from rearrangements between RET and its most frequent fusion partners, KIF5B, CCDC6, and NCOA4, were primarily analyzed and compared. Structurally, the rearrangement between exon 15 of KIF5B (K15) and exon 12 of RET (R12), which accounted for nearly 50 %, was the most common form of KIF5B-RET fusion. The corresponding reverse rearrangement, RET-KIF5B, involving exon 11 of RET (R11) and exon 16 of KIF5B (K16), was the second most common form. For CCDC6-RET fusions, the most frequent form involved the rearrangement between exon 1 of CCDC6 (C1) and exon 12 of RET (R12), with the reverse rearrangement, RET-CCDC6, involving exon 11 of RET (R11) and exon 2 of CCDC6 (C2), being the second most common. In contrast, NCOA4-RET fusions exhibited more variability, with the reverse rearrangement breakpoints being less consistent ( A). Subsequently, we conducted a detailed analysis of the breakpoint locations in the RET gene. Our findings revealed that RET rearrangements predominantly occur in intron 11 (corresponding R12) and intron 10 (corresponding R11), accounting for 54.8 % and 30.79 % of the cases, respectively. Intron 9 (corresponding R10) was the next most common site, representing 5.56 % of the cases ( B). In the Simple Reciprocal Inversion group, the K15:R12//R10:K16 combination accounted for 49.37 %, followed by the C1:R12//R11:C2 combination, which accounted for 17.72 % ( C). In the Co-Fusion group, 75 % of the multiple fusion forms involved classical RET rearrangements paired with rare RET forward or reverse fusions, such as KIF5B-RET//RET-KCNH1 and KIF5B-RET//RARB-RET ( D). Even when the paired rare RET rearrangements included the RET kinase domain, they were mostly in the 5′to 5′ configuration. Further investigation is required to confirm whether these configurations can transcribe the corresponding mRNA and produce functional fusion proteins.
 

## Validation of RET rearrangements in NSCLC samples using RNA-based NGS
Targeted sequencing can utilize either DNA or RNA as input material. However, DNA-based fusion detection is limited by the potential occurrence of fusion breakpoints within intronic regions, necessitating the coverage of large genomic areas. RNA-based NGS offers a significant advantage by using transcripts to detect fusions, thereby overcoming the limitations associated with intronic breakpoints and providing more precise and efficient fusion detection. To further elucidate the structural characteristics of RET rearrangements, pathogenic fusion variants in 1355 NSCLC tissue samples were analyzed using RNA-based NGS detection. The overall prevalence of all fusion variants was found to be 10.26 %, with RET fusions accounting for 1.4 %. Among the RET fusions, Common Single Fusions constituted 68.42 %, and Rare Single Fusions constituted 15.79 %, suggesting that some DNA-detected rearrangements, particularly 5′−5′ or 3′−3′ rearrangements, might not have undergone further transcription. Notably, Co-Fusion accounted for 10.5 %, indicating that multiple RET fusions might have been transcribed simultaneously. Additionally, although Simple Reciprocal Inversion was observed in only one case (5.2 %), it suggested that while the majority of directional fusions did not undergo transcription, this was not absolute. The underlying mechanisms of transcriptional regulation differences in these cases warrant further investigation ( A) . Further analysis of specific RET fusion partners revealed that in the Single Fusion-Common group, KIF5B-RET and CCDC6-RET constituted 47.34 % and 21.05 %, respectively. In the Single Fusion-Rare group, three cases were identified: LOC644634-RET, RASGEF1A-RET, and ERC1-RET. The Co-Fusion group included two cases, both involving classic RET fusions paired with rare fusions: KIF5B-RET//RET-CCDC7 and KIF5B-RET//RET-BPTE//RET-ITPKB. The Simple Reciprocal Inversion group had one case, identified as KIF5B-RET//RET-KIF5B ( B). RET fusions, whether detected by RNA-based NGS or DNA-based NGS, could be categorized into the aforementioned four types, despite the significant differences in their proportions. This suggested that RET fusions inherently include complex fusion types such as multi-fusions. Although the significance of reverse fusion transcription and its impact on forward RET fusions remain unclear, this mechanism represents a new direction distinct from previous studies that focused on rare fusion partners in RET research.
 

## Real-world case study of complex RET fusions
Due to the rarity of RET fusions as driver gene mutations in NSCLC, real-world studies were particularly challenging. Although only one case of complex RET co-fusion was tracked to date, it was hoped that some insights could be gleaned from this instance. This case initially drew attention because both the patient's surgical tissue sample and the post-resistance blood sample revealed multiple RET fusions based on DNA-bsed NGS detection. Specifically, a KIF5B-RET rearrangement was identified, where exons 1–15 of KIF5B were rearranged with exons 12–20 of RET. Additionally, a rare fusion, ATRNL1-RET, was detected, where exons 1–5 of ATRNL1 were rearranged with exons 1–11 of RET ( A-C). For the ATRNL1-RET fusion, the genes involved in the detected rearrangement provided only the 5′ end sequences, which seemingly precludes the formation of a functional protein. However, due to the complexity and specificity of chromosomal structural variations and post-transcriptional processing mechanisms, structural variations at the DNA level cannot accurately predict the presence or absence of the final protein. Unfortunately, this patient did not undergo further RNA-based or IHC-related testing for RET fusions. Nonetheless, insights into the efficacy of complex RET fusions could be gleaned from the patient's medical and treatment history.
 This 63-year-old male patient underwent lobectomy with lymph node dissection in October 2019 after being diagnosed with NSCLC. Pathology revealed invasive adenocarcinoma of the left lower lobe, predominantly micropapillary, measuring approximately 4 * 3 * 3 cm, with vascular and nerve invasion, and tumor spread within the surrounding alveoli. Metastasis was observed in the group 7 lymph nodes. Multiple gene mutations, including RET fusions, were detected in the surgical tissue (3DMed Onco™ GPS Tissue, China). PD-L1 expression was TPS 20 %, microsatellite stability, and the TMB score was 24.58 Muts/Mb. Postoperatively, the patient received four cycles of standard adjuvant chemotherapy. In January 2021, bone metastasis was diagnosed. Due to the inaccessibility of targeted therapies, local radiotherapy combined with six cycles of bevacizumab and paclitaxel was administered, followed by maintenance immunotherapy with camrelizumab. In December 2021, metastatic recurrence in the lungs was discovered. Liquid biopsy and ctDNA analysis confirmed RET fusions identical to previous findings, specifically KIF5B-RET and ATRNL1-RET co-fusions. In January 2022, the patient began targeted therapy with pralsetinib ( D). With RET-TKI targeted therapy, the patient achieved the progression-free survival (PFS) of 15 months, during which significant reduction in lung lesions and a marked decrease in serum carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels were observed ( E). Unfortunately, brain metastasis was detected in March 2023.
This case provides valuable insights: even after multiple lines of treatment, including immunotherapy, RET-targeted therapy managed to control the disease for 15 months, consistent with the results of the ARROW study. The complex RET fusion did not appear to interfere with the efficacy of targeted therapy. It is possible that the ATRNL1-RET rearrangement did not result in a functional fusion protein, leaving the classical RET fusion intact, or that ATRNL1-RET may function through an unknown regulatory mechanism akin to lncRNA. In any case, the complexity of such fusions warrants further exploration, both in theoretical mechanisms and real-world clinical settings.

## Discussion
The study was conducted to highlight the distribution characteristics of RET rearrangements in pan-solid tumors among the Chinese population and to emphasize the mutational profile and structural complexity of these rearrangements.
The results revealing that 1.027 % of cases were found to harbor pathogenic RET rearrangements. The highest frequency of RET rearrangements was observed in thyroid cancer (4.92 %), followed by lung cancer (1.75 %), urothelial cancer (0.29 %), ovarian cancer (0.28 %), and colorectal cancer (0.22 %). Among these patients, 54.4 % were female, 45.6 % were male, and 67.3 % were under 65 years old. Discrepancies in the frequency of RET rearrangements across various cancer types were noted when compared to historical data, with thyroid cancer exhibiting the lower rate but lung cancer at higher rate in our study [ ].
The mutational landscape of RET fusion-positive tumors was characterized, revealing a diverse array of genomic alterations, which the Over one-third of the KIF5B-RET cohort had minimal co-mutations, whereas CCDC6-RET rarely co-occurred with TP53 mutations. The top 10 mutated genes were primarily different RET fusion variants, with TP53 co-mutation frequency at 26 %, and other significant co-mutations included MDM2, FRS2, and NKX2–1 each at 8 %. This was consistent with previous reports that most patients had coexisting aberrations in addition to RET anomalies, commonly in TP53-associated genes, cell cycle–associated genes, the PI3K signaling pathway, MAPK effectors, or other tyrosine kinase families. RET fusions were mutually exclusive with MAPK signaling pathway alterations [ ]. The structural heterogeneity of RET rearrangements was further delineated, with conventional 5′ to 3′ rearrangements predominating, alongside less frequent 5′ to 5′ and 3′ to 3′ rearrangements, highlighting the complexity of RET activation mechanisms in solid tumors. RET rearrangements were classified into four distinct categories—Simple Reciprocal Inversion, Co-Fusion, Single Fusion-Common, and Single Fusion-Rare—revealing significant differences in tumor mutational burden (TMB) scores. The Single Fusion-Rare group was found to exhibit the highest TMB scores. This stratification also highlighted distinct co-mutation profiles, particularly the elevated frequency of TP53 co-mutations in the Single Fusion-Rare group. And, Compared to Western populations, where RET breakpoints primarily occur in intron 11, in Chinese populations, RET breakpoints tend to shift towards intron 10 [ ]. Exons 1–10 and part of exon 11 (amino acids 29 to 635) encode the extracellular region responsible for CLD1, CLD2, CLD3, CLD4, and CRD (which is responsible for physiological receptor dimerization). A portion of exon 11 (amino acids 636–657) codes for the transmembrane region. The bipartite protein tyrosine kinase domains are encoded by a portion of exon 12, exons 13–18, and part of exon 19 (amino acids 658 to 1114). In the Chinese population, RET rearrangements encompass a larger proportion of exon 11, which encodes the transmembrane region and part of the extracellular domain [ ]. The higher frequency of RET breakpoints in intron 10 may influence the structural characteristics of the RET fusion gene and, consequently, the efficacy of targeted therapies, which specifically target the RET kinase domain.
Rearrangements within the coding regions of two genes produce chimeric mRNAs and proteins with novel functions. Chromosomal rearrangements in noncoding regions can juxtapose promoter elements from one gene with the coding region of another, altering downstream gene expression. Cis-splicing of transcriptional read-through products can also generate chimeric mRNAs encoding chimeric proteins [ ]. RNA-based NGS analysis was conducted on 1355 NSCLC tissue samples, providing additional insights into the transcriptional activity of RET rearrangements. The overall prevalence of all fusion variants was 10.26 %, with RET fusions accounting for 1.4 %. Interestingly, the detection rate of RET rearrangements using DNA-based NGS was 1.75 %. The inconsistency between DNA-based NGS and RNA-based NGS in detecting fusion events had garnered significant attention. Previous reports indicated that 10–14 % of fusion events could be identified using RNA-based NGS in samples that tested negative with DNA-based methods [ , ]. In samples tested with both DNA and RNA, discrepancies in fusion detection were also observed. Among 7428 NSCLC patients, 2.9 % (n = 217) had ALK fusions. Of these, 83 % (n = 180) were detected by both DNA and RNA, 15 % (n = 33) were identified by RNA alone, and 2 % (n = 4) were detected by DNA alone [ ]. In a real-world retrospective study of 84,938 pan-cancer cases, only patients with successful RNA- and DNA-seq were included. Among 1945 patients spanning 20 cancer types, 2118 fusion events were identified. Of these, 29.1 % (616) were detected exclusively by RNA-seq, while 4.8 % (101) were identified only by DNA-seq. Notably, 1254 (58 %) of the fusion events had corresponding targeted therapies, and 23 % of these patients had fusions detected only by RNA-seq [ ]. While targeted DNA sequencing panels could detect clinically actionable fusions, technical and analytical challenges often resulted in false negatives. RNA-based whole transcriptome sequencing provided a complementary method that improved the identification of actionable variants, and using both RNA-NGS and DNA-NGS in certain samples or fusion types can better facilitate fusion detection. In our results, the distribution of fusion types suggested that some DNA-detected rearrangements might not undergo transcription, while the presence of Co-Fusion indicated the potential for simultaneous transcription of multiple RET fusions. These rearrangements often result in the formation of chimeric RNAs, which are hybrid transcripts containing exons from two separate genes. Traditionally, they are formed from fusion genes due to chromosomal rearrangements. Advanced sequencing technologies have revealed non-canonical chimeric RNAs formed via trans-splicing or cis-splicing between adjacent genes (cis-SAGe) [ ]. These transcripts might have mediated double-strand breaks through homologous DNA repair, acting like cDNAs. More importantly, chimeric transcripts could have functioned as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) for parent genes, such as the SLC45A3-ELK4 exon 1/2 fusion, which acted as an androgen-responsive chimeric non-coding RNA [ ].
A real-world case study of complex RET co-fusion in an NSCLC patient demonstrated the efficacy of RET-targeted therapy. The patient, with both KIF5B-RET and ATRNL1-RET co-fusions detected via DNA-based NGS, achieved a progression-free survival of 15 months with RET-targeted therapy, despite the complexity of the RET fusion. This case suggested that multiple complex RET rearrangements in the real world needed to be taken seriously, as they might have impacted the efficacy of TKI-targeted therapies. Similar to previous studies on ALK rearrangements, many complex rearrangements detected by DNA-based NGS might not have been transcribed at the RNA level. Consequently, most fusions detected at the RNA level were classical fusions [ ].

## Conclusions
The findings of this study contribute to the understanding of RET rearrangements in pan-solid tumors in a Chinese pan-cancer cohort by elucidating their distribution, mutational profiles, and structural characteristics. The results have implications for the development of targeted therapies and highlight the need for further investigation into the mechanisms underlying complex RET fusions.
