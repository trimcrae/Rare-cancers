## Abstract

## Purpose of Review
Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have become essential agents for managing advanced thyroid cancers, particularly refractory cases that do not respond to conventional therapies. This review emphasizes the key molecular drivers and oncogenic pathways (including the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway) that are therapeutically targetable, providing the rationale for TKI intervention and unraveling the role of key receptor tyrosine kinases, including VEGFR, PDGFR, and EGFR, in the pathogenesis and progression of thyroid cancer.

## Recent Findings
Recent studies comparing multikinase and selective TKIs show distinct differences in efficacy, toxicity, and therapeutic use across thyroid cancer subtypes. FDA-approved TKIs provide clear clinical benefit, but challenges such as drug resistance, limited response durability, and treatment-related toxicities persist. Emerging next-generation inhibitors, pathway-specific approaches, and combination strategies are being developed to address these limitations.

## Summary
TKI therapy has significantly improved outcomes for patients with advanced thyroid cancers by targeting central molecular pathways that drive tumor growth and survival. While resistance and toxicity continue to restrict optimal long-term benefit, ongoing developments in precision oncology and targeted therapeutics offer promising avenues to refine and enhance TKI-based treatment strategies in the near future.

Keywords: Tyrosine kinase inhibitors, Thyroid cancer, MAPK signaling pathway, Targeted therapy, Drug resistance

## Introduction
Thyroid cancer originates from hormone-producing thyroid gland cells, and its incidence has been consistently increasing worldwide [ , ]. It is classified into four major types, which account for approximately 98% of all thyroid malignancies: differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC), which encompasses both papillary thyroid cancer (PTC) and follicular thyroid cancer (FTC); oncocytic thyroid carcinoma/anaplastic (undifferentiated) thyroid cancer (ATC/UTC); and medullary thyroid cancer (MTC) [ ]. PTC is characterized by little colloid, hypercellularity, and enlarged, irregular, crowded nuclei with loss of polarity and presence of psammoma bodies. FTC is usually characterized by hypercellular areas, indistinct cell borders, scant colloid, and mildly enlarged round nuclei. MTC is characterized by high cellular density, ill-defined margins, frequent multi- or binucleated cells, and eccentric nuclei, while ATC is characterized by highly pleomorphic, hyperchromatic cells arranged in sheets or clusters, frequent mitotic figures, and necrotic areas [ ]. ATC, PTC, and FTC originate from thyroid follicular epithelial cells, whereas MTC originates from parafollicular C cells [ , ]. Each of these types consists of various subtypes: PTC comprises occult, encapsulated, follicular, oxyphilic, clear cell, tall cell, and columnar cell types; FTC comprises minimally invasive, widely invasive, and oxyphilic types; MTC comprises classic, encapsulated, papillary, and follicular types; and ATC comprises giant cell, spindle cell, and epitheloid cell types [ ].
PTC is the most prevalent malignant thyroid neoplasm, accounting for up to 80% of all thyroid cancers in countries with sufficient iodine intake. It occurs in all age groups, with its peak incidence noted in the third to fifth decades. These tumors typically grow at a slow pace and are often confined to a single lobe of the thyroid gland. They occasionally metastasize to the cervical lymph nodes and less commonly to the lungs and bones. FTC is the second most prevalent form of thyroid cancer, accounting for approximately 10% of all thyroid malignancies. It is more prevalent in regions with insufficient iodine intake. While FTC can occur across a broad age range, it most commonly affects individuals in their fifth and sixth decades of life. Unlike PTC, FTC rarely spreads to lymph nodes; however, it has a greater tendency to metastasize to distant sites, such as the lungs and bones. On the other hand, MTC is less common, representing less than 4% of thyroid cancers. Its diagnosis can be challenging, as it may metastasize to the lymph nodes, lungs, or liver even before a thyroid nodule becomes clinically apparent. ATC is the most aggressive yet rarest form of thyroid cancer, accounting for less than 2% of all cases. It typically occurs in individuals aged over 60 years and is often thought to evolve from pre-existing PTC or FTC. ATC is characterized by its rapid local invasion into the neck and its tendency to metastasize quickly to distant organs [ , ].
The incidence of thyroid cancer has been steadily increasing globally; this increased detection is largely attributed to the widespread use of diagnostic imaging and enhanced surveillance practices [ , ]. As of 2022, thyroid cancer was identified as the seventh most diagnosed cancer worldwide and the fifth most common cancer among women, with over 821,000 new cases reported. Women are affected nearly three times more often than men. Despite its high incidence, thyroid cancer has a relatively low mortality rate, with 44,000 deaths estimated in 2022, placing it 24th among cancer-related causes of death [ ]. As per projections, the global burden of thyroid cancer will significantly increase by 2030, with a 29.9% increase in incidence and a 67% increase in mortality for both sexes. Notably, the World Health Organization (WHO) African region is expected to experience the sharpest rise, with an anticipated 84.2% increase in incidence and a staggering 100.3% increase in mortality—the highest among all WHO regions [ ].
Exposure of the thyroid gland to ionizing radiation via radiotherapy or nuclear fallout, age, female sex, and family history are risk factors that can increase the prevalence of DTC [ ]. Inherited forms of thyroid cancer and various familial syndromes significantly elevate the risk of developing the disease. Notably, various conditions, such as Cowden syndrome (CS), which is associated with mutations in the phosphatase and tensin homolog gene (PTEN), and multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 (MEN2) are well-established genetic risk factors. Moreover, benign thyroid disorders, often arising from iodine deficiency, have been linked to an increased likelihood of thyroid cancer [ ]. At a molecular level, mutations and chromosomal rearrangements affecting genes involved in cell proliferation-related signaling pathways are frequently observed in many types of thyroid cancer [ ].

## Standard Treatments: Surgery, Radioactive Iodine (RAI) Therapy, and Thyroid-stimulating Hormone (TSH) Suppression
The treatment options mainly depend on the type, size, and stage of the tumor. The three most common and effective treatment options are surgery, RAI therapy, and TSH suppression [ ]. Surgery continues to be the cornerstone of thyroid cancer treatment; however, the specific approach depends on the size and extent of the tumor. According to the latest American Thyroid Association (ATA) guidelines, total thyroidectomy is advised for patients whose primary tumors exceed 4 cm in size, as well as those with lymph node involvement, distant metastases, or evidence of extrathyroidal extension (ETE). In the case of positive lymph node involvement, compartmental lymph node dissection is recommended. For 1–4 cm tumors with no sign of lymph node involvement or ETE, the appropriate course of surgery may be thyroid lobectomy or total thyroidectomy. Lobectomy may be sufficient to treat thyroid tumors smaller than 1 cm, unless there is a clear indication that the contralateral lobe needs to be removed [ , , , ].
Although surgical removal is the mainstay of treatment, RAI therapy and TSH suppression play essential roles in the postoperative management of DTC [ ]. The use of RAI therapy is guided by the patient’s risk of disease recurrence. It is specifically designated for two purposes: (1) residual remnant ablation, aimed at eliminating postoperative normal remnants of the thyroid tissue in order to facilitate follow-up and early detection of relapse, and (2) metastatic thyroid cancer treatment, envisioned to irradiate and eliminate occult micrometastases in order to improve recurrence-free survival [ – ]. TSH suppression is another postsurgical treatment regimen to reduce the risk of recurrence. As TSH secreted by the pituitary gland can promote the growth of thyroid cancer cells, patients can be administered levothyroxine, a synthetic form of thyroxine (T4), to maintain low TSH levels and thereby prevent the growth of any remaining thyroid cancer cells [ ].

## Need for Targeted Therapies in Aggressive/RAI-refractory Thyroid Cancers
The prognosis of thyroid cancer is highly influenced by various factors, such as cancer subtype, tumor size, metastatic spread, patient’s age, and success of surgical intervention. DTC is associated with a favorable outlook, with the 5-year survival rate reaching up to 95% across all age groups and racial backgrounds [ ]. As DTC advances, thyroid cancer cells may lose their ability to take up RAI, thereby worsening the overall prognosis. If metastatic DTC continues to progress despite systemic therapies, such as RAI and TSH suppression, alternative treatment options, including surgery, chemotherapy, and external beam radiation therapy (EBRT), are often considered. However, these interventions are typically associated with significant side effects and do not improve overall survival [ ]. Large tumor size, ETE or metastasis, advanced age, and unfavorable tumor forms, including ATC, are all indicators of a poor prognosis [ ]. Patients with MTC who present with regional lymph node involvement or distant metastases have a lower likelihood of being cured. Thus, MTC accounts for approximately 13% of all thyroid cancer-related deaths [ , ]. MTC and ATC are inherently resistant to RAI therapy and tend to be more aggressive, fast-growing, and life-threatening.
As standard RAI therapy is ineffective in 5%–22% of patients with DTC and surgery is not curative for patients with metastatic MTC, researchers are exploring other ways to treat thyroid cancers. In recent years, mutations in intracellular signaling pathways, including rat sarcoma (RAS), B-Raf proto-oncogene, serine/threonine kinase (BRAF), and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT signaling pathways, have been found to play a key role in thyroid carcinogenesis. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and its receptor (VEGFR) are also associated with the development of aggressive thyroid cancer [ ]. This identification of genetic alterations in thyroid carcinogenesis has led to the emergence of targeted therapies, including tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and PI3K signaling inhibitors, inhibitors targeting gene fusions, immunotherapeutic agents, cancer vaccines, and targeted small-molecule drugs, to inhibit abnormal signaling pathways commonly associated with thyroid cancer [ ]. The use of TKIs has expanded the available therapies for patients with RAI-refractory DTC (RAIR-DTC) [ , ].

## Molecular Pathogenesis of Thyroid Cancer
Aberrant activation of the MAPK and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways plays a pivotal role in the molecular pathogenesis and progression of most thyroid cancers [ ]. The MAPK signaling pathway is commonly activated through point mutations in BRAF and RAS genes, as well as RET/PTC fusion and telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) promoter mutation [ ]. Interestingly, Fagin et al. reported that both poorly DTC (PDTC) and ATC probably originate from preexisting DTC or PDTC and exhibit a high mutation burden [ ]. Although BRAF and RAS oncogenic mutations are predominant drivers in thyroid cancers, TP53, TERT, and EIF1AX mutations have also become frequent with the progression of the disease.

## MAPK Signaling Pathway
The MAPK signaling pathway plays a crucial role in the transmission of extracellular signals to intracellular responses. It can be categorized into three MAPK families in mammalian cells: classical MAPK (also known as ERK), C-Jun N-terminal kinase/stress-activated protein kinase (JNK/SAPK), and p38 kinase [ ]. Activation of the MAPK signaling pathway is closely associated with its pathogenesis and can regulate diverse cellular processes, including uncontrolled cell proliferation, sustained growth, enhanced survival, and dedifferentiation [ ]. Several reports have suggested that approximately 70%–80% of patients with PTC exhibit oncogenic mutations, such as BRAF, RAS, or RET/PTC rearrangement or tropomyosin receptor kinase gene (TRK) mutation [ , , ]. Moreover, patients with FTC are characterized by a high prevalence of RAS and PAX8/PPARɣ oncogenic mutations, while patients with ATC mainly exhibit BRAF and RAS oncogenic mutations [ ]. Ultimately, constitutive activation of the MAPK signaling pathway is triggered by the oncoproteins encoded by these genes with the progression of cancer.

## BRAF Mutations
BRAF, a serine/threonine protein kinase in the Raf family, acts within the MAPK signaling pathway. Upon RAS activation, it activates MEK, resulting in downstream effector activation. Notably, BRAF is constitutively activated by mutations in approximately 7% of all cancers [ ]. Activating point mutations in BRAF, predominantly at codon 600, are a common oncogenic driver. The V600E mutation accounts for 98%–99% of these cases. Albeit less frequent (1%–2%), other BRAF alterations, such as the K601E mutation, small insertions/deletions, or rearrangements, are also noted [ ]. Notably, the V600E variant has been linked to aggressive disease characteristics, such as lymph node metastasis, invasion, and recurrence [ ]. In contrast to the aggressive nature of the common V600E mutation, rare BRAF mutations are primarily found in the follicular variant of PTC and are associated with a favorable prognosis [ ]. The increasing incidence of BRAF mutations in PTC has prompted several hypotheses. One hypothesis is that increased prophylactic iodine intake is a contributing factor; however, direct proof is lacking. Another hypothesis centers on environmental pollutants, suggesting that they induce the BRAF–V600E mutation. Notably, a higher prevalence of this mutation has been noted in individuals living near Mount Etna [ ]. ## RAS Mutations
RAS, a family of GTP-binding proteins upstream of BRAF, regulates cell growth, differentiation, and survival through the MAPK and PI3/AKT signaling pathways. The RAS gene contains three isoforms, KRAS, NRAS, and HRAS, which encode four related proteins: N-Ras, H-Ras, K-Ras4A, and K-Ras4B. RAS proteins cycle between an active GTP-bound state and an inactive GDP-bound state [ ]. The rapid inactivation of activated RAS–GTP protein occurs through a combination of its intrinsic GTPase activity and the catalytic action of cytoplasmic GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs), which facilitate the hydrolysis of GTP to GDP. In numerous human tumors, point mutations within distinct domains of the RAS gene disrupt normal function by either enhancing GTP binding or impairing intrinsic GTPase activity. RAS mutations rank second in prevalence to BRAF mutations in thyroid cancers [ ]. In DTCs, particularly PTCs, NRAS and HRAS are the most frequently mutated RAS genes. These mutations primarily occur at codons 12, 13, and 61, as demonstrated by comprehensive next-generation sequencing data published by The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) study [ ]. The prevalence of RAS mutations in DTCs, specifically KRAS, NRAS, and HRAS mutations, exhibits a distinct pattern. NRAS mutations are the most common, occurring in 8.5% of cases. HRAS mutations are less frequent, occurring in 3.5% of tumors. KRAS mutations are the least prevalent, occurring in only 1% of cases (Fig. ). These percentages align closely with data from the COSMIC database, indicating a consistent pattern across different studies. While RAS is known to be a dual activator of the MAPK and PI3/AKT signaling pathways, in the context of thyroid cancer, RAS mutations appear to favor the PI3/AKT signaling pathway. This preference is indicated by the strong association between RAS mutations and AKT phosphorylation within thyroid tumors [ ].
 

## PI3K/AKT Signaling Pathway
The molecular pathogenesis of most thyroid cancers is characterized by the dysregulation of the MAPK and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways. The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, a key signaling cascade, is notably activated through dysregulated receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs). PI3K signaling is primarily mediated through the PI3K/AKT/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) and PI3K/AKT/PTEN signaling pathways [ ]. The functional involvement of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in sporadic thyroid tumorigenesis was first suggested by the observed upregulation and activation of AKT. Among the three isoforms of AKT, AKT1 and AKT2—unlike AKT3—are predominantly expressed and activated in thyroid cancers [ ]. The serine/threonine kinase mTOR, also known as FRAP1, serves as a key regulator of cell growth. It integrates signals from mitogens, growth factors, and nutrients to modulate protein synthesis and support cell proliferation. The dysregulation of mTOR is implicated in numerous human cancers. mTOR regulates biogenesis by activating p70S6K (also known as ribosomal protein S6 kinase beta-1) and simultaneously inhibiting 4EBP1, collectively resulting in increased protein synthesis and ribosome biogenesis [ ]. Human PTCs have been found to exhibit increased p70S6K levels, indicating higher mTOR activity [ ]. Compared to normal thyroid tissue, thyroid carcinomas exhibit constitutive activation of the mTOR pathway [ ]. The dysfunction of PTEN, a negative regulator of the PI3K pathway, alters PI3K signaling. Germline PTEN mutations cause CS, an autosomal dominant syndrome that predisposes individuals to a higher risk of various cancers, such as breast, endometrial, and thyroid cancers(Porta, 2014). Approximately two-thirds of patients with CS have thyroid malignancies, with FTCs being most frequently identified [ ].

## RET/PTC Mutations
Tyrosine kinase (TK), a cell membrane receptor, is encoded by the RET proto-oncogene. It is located on chromosome 10 (10q11.2) and contains 21 exons. The RET gene undergoes alternative splicing, generating three protein isoforms, RET9, RET43, and RET51, characterized by C-terminal tails containing nine, 43, and 51 amino acids, respectively [ ]. RET is predominantly expressed in the parafollicular C cells of the thyroid gland but is typically absent in follicular cells. However, in follicular cells, RET can get aberrantly activated through chromosomal rearrangements, particularly RET/PTC fusion events [ ]. These genes encode a fusion protein, RET/PTC, in which the intact TK domain of RET is driven by an active promoter from another gene. This results in ligand-independent expression and dimerization of RET/PTC, leading to chronic MAPK signaling stimulation and promoting thyroid tumorigenesis [ ]. RET/PTC1 rearrangement occurs when the TK domain of RET fuses with the CCDC6 (formerly H4) promoter region, causing the RET/PTC protein to become active without ligand binding. Moreover, RET fuses with the promoter region of the NCOA4 gene (also known as ELE1) [ ]. While RET/PTC rearrangements typically differ because of the involvement of distinct partner genes, some variations arise from different breakpoints within the same genes, resulting in chimeras of varying lengths [ ]. In total, 10%–20% of PTCs exhibit clonal RET/PTC rearrangements, which are specific to this tumor type (Fig. ) [ ].
 

## TRK Fusion
The TRK family contains three neurotrophic RTK proteins (TRKA, TRKB, and TRKC), which are encoded by NTRK1, NTRK2, and NTRK3 located on chromosomes 1q21–q22, 9q22.1, and 15q25, respectively [ ]. These receptors are mainly expressed in neuronal tissues and play a key role in human embryonic development and the function of the nervous system; they regulate cellular function through MAPK, PI3K/AKT, and PLC-γ signaling pathways [ , ]. Martin-Zanca et al. first observed somatic fusions involving NTRK (TPM3–NTRK1) in a patient with colorectal cancer in 1986 [ ]. Typically, fusion genes are formed by amalgamating the 3′ sequence of NTRK, containing the kinase structural domain, with the 5′ sequence of an unrelated gene. NTRK fusion genes produce TRK chimeric proteins, which are ligand-independent TRKs, through transcriptional and translational processes responsible for ligand-independent constitutive activation of the kinase structural domain of TRK. This promotes the sustained activation of multiple downstream signaling pathways, followed by the expedition of tumorigenesis and progression [ ]. After the discovery of TPM3–NTRK1 in colorectal cancer, the fusion gene was identified in four out of 10 patients with PTC in 1989 [ ]. The prevalence of NTRK fusion genes is approximately 2.2%–2.3% in patients with thyroid cancer [ , ]. However, 15 NTRK fusion genes (NTRK1: TPM3, TPR, IRF2BP2, FNASC, PPL, DIAPH1, TFG, SSBP2, and SQSTM1; NTRK2: ETV6; and NTRK3: ETV6, EML4, SQSTM1, RBPMS, and VIM) have been reported so far [ ]. Eszlinger et al. identified that NTRK fusion genes are common in patients with RAI-refractory distant metastasis and advanced thyroid cancer [ ]. Notably, they do not usually occur concurrently with other mutations, such as BRAF mutations [ , ].

## TERT Promoter Mutations
TERT is the catalytic domain of telomerase, which adds telomeric repeats to chromosomal ends to maintain genomic stability and chromosomal integrity [ ]. TERT is crucial for telomerase activation during malignant cell transformation [ ]. Thyroid cancer cells frequently exhibit TERT promoter mutations, specifically C228T (chr 5: 1,295,228 C > T) and C250T (chr 5: 1,295,250 C > T). The C228T mutation is the most prevalent, and its frequency increases with tumor aggressiveness, ranging from low in PTC to very high in ATC [ ]. TERT promoter mutations are linked to BRAF–V600E mutations, and their combined presence is noted in a particularly aggressive subset of thyroid tumors, characterized by higher recurrence and mortality rates [ ].

## PAX8/PPARγ Fusion Protein
PAX8, a member of the paired box family of transcription factors, plays an essential role in normal thyroid development. It promotes the survival of thyroid progenitor cells and drives the expression of thyroid-specific genes [ ]. PPARγ, a member of the nuclear receptor family of transcription factors, is best known for its key role in regulating adipogenesis. It also exhibits potential tumor-suppressive effects [ ]. The PAX8–PPARγ fusion protein may facilitate tumorigenesis by either suppressing the tumor-suppressive activity of PPARγ or functioning as a novel proto-oncogenic transcription factor [ ]; it is detected in 30%–40% of patients with FTC [ ].

## Overview of TKIs

## TK
TKIs represent a group of therapeutic agents that interfere with protein kinase signal transduction pathways by employing diverse inhibitors [ ]. TKs are classified into RTKs, non-RTKs (NRTKs), and a small group of dual-specificity kinases (DSKs) that can phosphorylate serine, threonine, and tyrosine residues [ ]. RTKs are transmembrane receptors comprising various families, such as VEGFR, platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR), insulin receptor (InsR), and the erythroblastic oncogene B (ErbB) family, which includes epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) [ ]. TKs regulate signal transduction by phosphorylating specific amino acid residues on target proteins, thereby altering various aspects of cellular behavior. Both RTKs and NRTKs trigger downstream signaling pathways by facilitating the transfer of a phosphate group from nucleoside triphosphates, such as ATP, to the tyrosine residues of target proteins [ ]. These kinases regulate cell growth, migration, differentiation, apoptosis, and death. Aberrant TK activity, either through constitutive activation or through inhibition, can disrupt signaling pathways and induce malignancy and other pathologies [ ]. RTKs are overexpressed in various human cancers, including brain, lung, esophageal, and thyroid cancers [ ].

## VEGFR
VEGF, a potent angiogenic factor, is essential for thyroid cancer development [ ]. It exerts its effects through high-affinity binding to VEGFRs, specifically VEGFR-1 (Flt-1) and VEGFR-2 (KDR) [ ]. VEGF signaling promotes tumor cell migration and invasion, potentially through mechanisms involving dedifferentiation and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) [ ]. Given its established role in tumor growth and metastasis, angiogenesis—particularly mediated through VEGF signaling—has become a key area of focus in thyroid cancer research, with important implications for both prognosis and treatment [ ].

## PDGFR
PDGFs are potent mitogens and chemokines for mesenchymal cells, playing crucial roles in both normal and pathological vascular development [ ]. Five distinct PDGF ligands are currently recognized: PDGFA (PDGF-AA), PDGFB (PDGF-BB), PDGFC (PDGF-CC), PDGFD (PDGF-DD), and the heterodimeric PDGFAB (PDGF-AB) [ ]. Cells express three PDGFR isoforms: PDGFR-αα, PDGFR-ββ, and PDGFR-αβ. Each isoform is capable of interacting with PDGF ligands [ ]. PDGF-D contributes to tumor aggressiveness by activating the Notch and NF-κB signaling pathways [ ] (Ahmad et al. 2011). PDGFC and PDGFRA are highly expressed in thyroid cancers, and patients with elevated PDGFRA expression exhibit significantly lower survival rates [ ].

## EGFR
EGFR, a transmembrane TK receptor, plays a key role in various cancers, including thyroid carcinoma. Its constitutive activation results in increased cell proliferation through the RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, and STAT signaling pathways [ ]. EGFR is expressed in PTCs; its overexpression is linked to dedifferentiation and anaplastic transformation [ ]. Furthermore, the overexpression of EGFR (EGFR-H) is observed in ATCs, FTCs, and MTCs, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target, even in the absence of somatic EGFR mutations [ ].

## Multikinase Inhibitors (MKIs) Versus Selective Kinase Inhibitors
To combat tumor growth driven by hyperactive receptor kinases, inhibiting multiple kinases is often more effective than targeting a single kinase. This can be accomplished by combining selective kinase inhibitors or by using a single MKI [ ]. Selective kinase inhibitors selectively block specific kinases, thereby minimizing off-target effects and potential side effects. Cancer signaling pathways often have redundancies and feedback loops. Blocking one pathway can lead to the activation of alternative pathways, inducing drug resistance. Multitargeted TKIs can overcome this by simultaneously inhibiting multiple crucial pathways.

## Approved TKIs for Thyroid Cancer Treatment
Multiple TKIs have been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) as first-line treatment options for patients who have advanced or metastatic DTC that no longer responds to RAI therapy [ ].

## Sorafenib
Sorafenib was approved by the US FDA in November 2013 as the first multitargeted TKI for treating progressive, advanced RAIR-DTC [ ]. By inhibiting a range of kinases, including C-RAF, B-RAF, VEGFR-1–3, PDGFR-β, RET, c-KIT, and FLT-3, sorafenib disrupts multiple pathways crucial for tumor development. This multikinase inhibitory activity contributes to its ability to inhibit tumor growth, progression, metastasis, and angiogenesis and enhance apoptosis, thereby improving progression-free survival [ ].

## Lenvatinib
Lenvatinib is a multitargeted TKI that acts on VEGFR-1–3, FGFR-1–4, PDGFR-α, RET, and c-KIT [ ], exhibiting particularly strong activity against the FGFR family. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that lenvatinib effectively suppresses tumor growth, tumor-driven angiogenesis, and cancer cell migration and invasion in various thyroid cancer models both in vitro and in xenograft systems.

## Cabozantinib
Cabozantinib inhibits several TKs that mediate tumor growth and angiogenesis in DTC. It suppresses oncogenesis by inhibiting a broad spectrum of RTKs, such as MET, RET, VEGFR-1–3, KIT, TRKB, FLT-3, AXL, and TIE-2, thereby preventing their downstream signaling through phosphorylation [ ]. In a phase I clinical trial, cabozantinib exhibited promising antitumor efficacy in heavily pretreated patients with MTC [ ]. Based on clinical data, both cabozantinib and vandetanib have been approved by the US FDA for the treatment of patients with metastatic or locally advanced MTC that is not amenable to surgical resection [ ].

## Vandetanib
Vandetanib is one of the most used TKIs for managing advanced MTC [ ]. It targets RET, VEGFR-2, and EGFR. However, EGFR signaling can contribute to RET kinase activation. Moreover, RET mutations are frequently observed in MTC. Therefore, the inhibition of EGFR by vandetanib may lead to an unintended increase in cancer cell activity [ ]. The US FDA and EMA have approved the use of vandetanib for the treatment of patients with symptomatic, unresectable, locally advanced, or metastatic MTC [ ].

## Larotrectinib
Larotrectinib, a highly selective and CNS-penetrant TRK inhibitor, was approved by the US FDA in 2018. It is now authorized in over 40 countries for the treatment of TRK fusion-positive cancers in both adults and children [ ]. Larotrectinib is an orally administered, small-molecule, highly selective TRK inhibitor. It is a chiral pyrazolo[1,5-a]pyrimidine derivative featuring a difluorinated benzyl ring, and it exhibits strong selectivity and potency against TRKA, TRKB, and TRKC, with IC50 values in the low nanomolar range in in vitro assays [ ].

## Entrectinib
Entrectinib is a powerful oral TKI that selectively targets gene fusions involving NTRK, ROS1, and ALK genes [ ]. It has been approved by the US FDA and EMA for the treatment of ≥ 12-year-old patients with solid tumors harboring NTRK fusions. Moreover, it has been approved for adult patients with ROS1 fusion-positive non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [ ].

## Selpercatinib
Selpercatinib is a potent, ATP-competitive, and highly selective small-molecule inhibitor of RET kinase. It exhibits nanomolar activity against various RET alterations, including the V804 gatekeeper resistance mutation. Moreover, it exhibits robust and durable antitumor responses in patients with advanced or metastatic RET-altered cancers, regardless of their treatment history. Notably, selpercatinib has also shown efficacy in brain metastasis models and maintained a favorable safety profile across clinical studies [ , ].

## Pralsetinib
Pralsetinib is a highly selective inhibitor that targets RET alterations. It exhibits significant clinical efficacy along with a favorable safety profile [ ]. It has been approved by the US FDA for the treatment of adults with locally advanced or metastatic RET fusion-positive NSCLC, regardless of their treatment history. Moreover, it has shown notable therapeutic benefits in patients with RET-altered thyroid cancers, confirming its potency and specificity as a RET inhibitor.

## Dabrafenib + trametinib
Dabrafenib is a selective, reversible, competitive BRAF inhibitor that binds to the ATP-binding pocket. Trametinib is a selective, reversible inhibitor of MEK1/MEK2 kinase function and activation [ ]. The combination of dabrafenib and trametinib has been found to exhibit antitumor efficacy in patients with BRAF–V600E-mutant metastatic melanoma, NSCLC, and ATC [ ].

## Emerging TKIs in Clinical Trials
Significant advancements in the recent understanding of molecular pathways associated with thyroid cancer have led to the development of numerous medications that target signaling kinases. A range of newly developed drug classes have been identified; these drugs inhibit oncogenic kinases, such as RET/PTC and BRAF–V600E, as well as key signaling kinases, such as VEGFR and PDGFR, which are associated with the proliferation of thyroid cancer cells. In recent years, several small-molecule inhibitors targeting intracellular TKs linked to the aforementioned receptors have received approval from the US FDA and EMA for the treatment of aggressive and treatment-resistant thyroid cancers. Moreover, several multitarget TKIs, beyond their initially intended targets, are evolving as new-generation drugs for the treatment of DTC, MTC, and UTC; they are capable of producing a clinical response and stabilizing disease progression. These multitarget TKIs can interfere with several signaling pathways that support tumor growth and dissemination (e.g., angiogenesis, invasiveness, and metastasis) [ ]. ## Resistance to TKIs

## Mechanisms of Acquired Resistance
Targeted therapies have emerged as a promising advancement for the management of thyroid cancer; however, their long-term success is frequently challenged by the development of acquired resistance. This resistance may be attributed to various mechanisms, such as the development of new or secondary mutations in targeted TKs, e.g., gatekeeper mutations, solvent-front mutations, covalent binding site mutations, or compound mutations; overexpression or amplification of target proteins/genes; induction of compensatory signaling pathways; or modulation of efflux transporter expression. These cellular adaptations empower tumor cells to evade therapeutic pressure, allowing them to grow and survive even in the presence of antitumor drugs [ , , ]. The mechanisms responsible for acquired resistance to TKIs in patients with thyroid cancer remain incompletely understood; however, they are generally classified into two main categories: activation of alternative signaling pathways and mutations in genes directly targeted by TKIs (Table ).
 

## Molecular Alterations Contributing to TKI Resistance

## Resistance to VEGFR Inhibitors
Molecular targeted therapies that inhibit VEGFR TKs have become central to the management of DTC, largely because of their ability to simultaneously target multiple RTKs, such as RET, FGFR, PDGFR, KIT, and MET [ ]. Despite their initial effectiveness, the prolonged use of anti-VEGF therapies often leads to the development of acquired resistance, driven by the activation of alternative signaling pathways that allow tumor cells to bypass VEGF inhibition. The mechanisms responsible for acquired resistance to VEGFR MKI therapy remain poorly understood, particularly in thyroid cancer. Resistance is likely multifactorial and may include alternate mechanisms that allow tumor cells to bypass growth inhibition. These mechanisms include the increased activation of angiogenic signaling pathways, emergence of gatekeeper mutations that block small-molecule inhibitors from binding to the ATP-binding site of the kinase, and activation or upregulation of alternative oncogenic pathways, such as the EGFR signaling pathway [ , ]. For instance, one study identified high FGF2 signaling activity in tumors that were resistant to anti-VEGF therapy [ ]. Zhu et al. further reported that the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, along with the reactivation of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway and phosphorylation of STAT3, could confer resistance to sorafenib [ ]. Furthermore, Wang et al. demonstrated that epigenetic mechanisms contribute to sorafenib resistance, specifically through the involvement of EZH2—a key histone-modifying enzyme known to suppress gene transcription. They also noted significantly higher EZH2 expression in thyroid cancer cell lines and tumor samples than in controls [ ]. Recent studies have established a correlation between EZH2 upregulation and poor clinical outcomes across various cancer types, resulting in the ongoing evaluation of EZH2 inhibitors in clinical trials [ ]. Research on lenvatinib resistance in thyroid cancers has revealed notable cellular and molecular alterations, including a shift toward mesenchymal morphology, activation of survival signaling pathways, and increased expression of the transcription factor genes SNAIL and ZEB1. In one study, resistant cells showed enhanced activity of the nuclear export protein exportin 1 (XPO1) [ ]. Furthermore, the upregulation of FGFR signaling has been implicated in the development of resistance to several VEGFR-targeting agents, including lenvatinib, cabozantinib, sorafenib, and vandetanib [ ].

## Resistance to NTRK Inhibitors
Pooled data have demonstrated that larotrectinib and entrectinib facilitate rapid and sustained disease control in both DTC and ATC. However, resistance has been observed, particularly in NTRK fusion-positive tumors, primarily because of on-target mutations. These mutations mainly include solvent-front mutations, such as TRKA G595R, TRKB G639R, and TRKC G623R [ ]. In addition, resistance-conferring mutations have been less frequently identified in the xDFG activation loop and gatekeeper domain [ , ]. These genetic changes alter the conformation of the kinase structural domain of TRK, impairing drug binding and reducing therapeutic efficacy.
Resistance can also be conferred through off-target or bypass mechanisms, involving alterations in genes outside the NTRK family. These include activating mutations, such as BRAF–V600E, KRAS–G12D, and MET amplification, which can promote escape through the reactivation of the MAPK signaling pathway [ ].

## Resistance to BRAF Inhibitors
Resistance to BRAF inhibitors can be conferred through additional mutations within the MAPK signaling cascade, e.g., the emergence of NRAS–Q61K or KRAS–G12V mutations [ , ]. Studies have also revealed that tumor cells may adapt to BRAF-targeted therapies by upregulating the surface expression of various growth factor receptors, including KIT, MET, EGFR, and PDGFR-β. This upregulation appears to occur in the absence of secondary activating mutations or gene amplifications, suggesting that the increased receptor expression is regulated at the transcriptional or post-transcriptional level [ ]. Montero-Conde et al. reported that BRAF-mutant PTC cell lines acquire resistance to vemurafenib because of the overexpression of the FGFR or ERBB receptor families, thereby reactivating the MAPK pathway downstream of activated RAS [ ].
Another mechanism underlying resistance to BRAF inhibitors involves the gain of wild-type gene copies. For example, Duquette et al. reported the amplification of the MCL1 gene, a member of the BCL2 family involved in the regulation of apoptosis, in patients with PTC who developed resistance to vemurafenib [ ]. Moreover, Bagheri-Yarmand et al. demonstrated that acquired chromosomal polyploidy can contribute to resistance. In a patient with ATC undergoing dabrafenib treatment, the researchers observed triploidy of chromosome 7, which contains the EGFR, RAC1, MET, and BRAF genes. This chromosomal gain led to increased copy numbers and overexpression of these oncogenes in the dedifferentiated tumor, likely enhancing disease progression [ , ]. Evidence also suggests that the overexpression of wild-type RTKs can lead to the reactivation of the MAPK or PI3K/AKT signaling pathways, as long as adequate levels of their corresponding ligands are available [ ].
To determine the mechanisms underlying acquired resistance in thyroid cancer, BRAF–V600E-mutant PTC cells were treated with vemurafenib over an extended period. This exposure led to increased expression of MET receptors, EGFRs, and ERBB receptors, as well as activation of the PI3K/AKT and MAPK signaling pathways [ ]. Whole exome sequencing (WES) further revealed that a subset of these cells had acquired a secondary KRAS–G12D mutation, resulting in enhanced proliferation and invasiveness [ , ]. In addition to RAS point mutations, resistance-associated secondary alterations have been frequently identified in different genes, e.g., PTEN, NF1, NF2, TP53, CDKN2A, RAC1, and RAC1P34R [ , ].

## Resistance to RET Inhibitors
It is well established that acquired mutations within the RET kinase domain significantly confer resistance to selective RET inhibitors by interfering with effective drug binding. These mainly include solvent-front mutations, such as RET–G810, as well as non-gatekeeper alterations in the hinge region (Y806C/N) and the β2 strand (V738A) of the kinase domain [ ].
Secondary RET mutations arising from pretreatment with MKIs include known oncogenic drivers, such as D898Y, G601E, K666E, and V804M, as well as mutations in the ATP-binding site of RET, including G810C/S/R, Y806C/N, and V738A [ ]. While the former mutations generally retain sensitivity to the selective RET inhibitor selpercatinib, mutations at the solvent-front position G810, where bulky residues like arginine, cysteine, or serine are substituted, can sterically hinder the binding of both pralsetinib and selpercatinib.
These G810 substitutions disrupt critical van der Waals interactions essential for inhibitor binding. In particular, Y806 and V738 mutations interfere with selpercatinib binding, while L730 and V738 mutations compromise the hydrophobic interactions necessary for pralsetinib activity [ ]. These structural changes collectively contribute to acquired resistance and underscore the need for next-generation RET inhibitors that can overcome such alterations.

## Resistance to ALK Inhibitors
Crizotinib, an MKI-inhibiting ALK, is useful in patients with EML4–ALK-positive metastatic RAIR-TC; however, resistance is acquired upon prolonged usage. Several mechanisms have been reported to be responsible for this acquired resistance; these include secondary ALK mutations, such as L1196M (gatekeeper mutation), C1156Y, F1174L, G1202R, G1269A, and S1206Y; off-target mechanisms, such as the activation of EGFR by upregulated amphiregulin or TGF-α, activation of HER family proteins (EGFR, HER2, and HER3) by upregulated EGF or NRG-1, and activation of the P2Y purinergic receptor family; KIT and MET gene amplifications; and KRAS mutations [ ].

## Strategies to Overcome Resistance (Use of Combination Therapies and Novel Inhibitors)

## Combination Therapies (Additional Kinase Inhibitors)
A therapeutic approach to address the activation of alternative signaling pathways in resistant cancers involves introducing a second TKI or transitioning to an alternative targeted systemic therapy. This approach aims to block compensatory pathways that drive continued tumor growth. The combination of TKIs and immunotherapeutic agents, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), is emerging as a promising strategy. Many published case series on thyroid cancer have demonstrated that combining targeted therapies with ICIs can offer sustained tumor control, even in patients who previously progressed on targeted monotherapy [ ].
VEGF has been implicated in resistance to immune checkpoint blockade [ ]. The VEGF signaling pathway fosters a hypoxic and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by limiting T-cell infiltration, impairing cytotoxic T-cell activity, and promoting the accumulation of immunosuppressive cells. Hence, targeting VEGF signaling has emerged as a promising approach for enhancing the effectiveness of ICIs. In line with this strategy, the efficacy of the combination of lenvatinib and pembrolizumab was evaluated in patients with progressive DTC who had not previously received VEGFR inhibitors. Among the 30 patients enrolled in that study, 62% achieved a partial response (PR), while 35% exhibited stable disease (SD) [ ]. In a separate study, eight patients with metastatic ATC or DTC received a combination of lenvatinib (24 mg/kg daily) and pembrolizumab (200 mg every 3 weeks). This treatment resulted in PR in four patients and SD in two patients. Notably, no significant grade 3 or 4 toxicities were observed during the course of the therapy [ ].
Another multinational phase 1b trial assessed the efficacy of the combination of cabozantinib (40 mg daily) and atezolizumab (1200 mg every 3 weeks), an anti-PD-L1 antibody, in patients with advanced solid tumors, including DTCs. Similar to lenvatinib, cabozantinib exhibits immunomodulatory properties that can potentially counteract tumor-mediated immunosuppression and improve responsiveness to ICIs. Among the 31 patients enrolled, 58% of the patients had PTC, while 61% of the tumors carried a BRAF mutation. Follow-up data revealed an objective response rate (ORR) of 42%, which included 13 cases of PR and 17 cases of SD. Notably, the disease control rate (DCR) was 97% in that study (30 out of 31 patients), with only one patient lacking a postbaseline assessment. Overall, the first-line treatment of advanced RAIR-DTC with a combination of cabozantinib and atezolizumab could provide durable responses and a high DCR across different subtypes of DTC [ ]. Another clinical trial involving 38 patients with BRAF–V600E-mutant tumors evaluated the combination of atezolizumab, vemurafenib, and cobimetinib, revealing an ORR of 59%, which included nine cases of PR and seven cases of SD [ ]. In a separate study, patients with ATC who were initially treated with lenvatinib or a combination of dabrafenib and trametinib exhibited improved responses when pembrolizumab, a PD-L1 inhibitor, was administered following disease progression. These results suggest that immunotherapy serves as an effective adjunct to enhance the efficacy of kinase inhibitors [ ]. ## Redifferentiation Therapies
Iodine uptake in thyroid follicular cells is mediated by the sodium iodide symporter (NIS), which is located on the basolateral membrane. TSH and other regulatory factors enhance the expression of NIS and its proper localization to the cell membrane. However, in thyroid cancer cells, this regulatory balance is often disrupted. As thyroid cells undergo dedifferentiation (frequently associated with mutations in certain genes, such as BRAF, TERT, and RAS genes), NIS expression at the plasma membrane decreases. Instead, the transporter gets mislocalized to the cytoplasm, where it remains inactive and is unable to facilitate iodine uptake [ , ]. Faria et al. reported that the disruption of E-cadherin-mediated cell–cell adhesion may contribute to the reduced RAI uptake observed in advanced thyroid cancers [ ]. Hence, redifferentiation therapies are often designed to restore proper localization of NIS at the plasma membrane in order to enhance the effect of NIS-mediated RAI therapy. Various strategies have been investigated to enhance NIS expression at the cell surface. Retinoic acid can upregulate the transcription of SLC5A5 (encoding NIS) by activating retinoic acid receptors (RAR/RXR) and reinitiating the expression of thyroid-specific genes, such as TPO and TG genes; however, clinical responses are often modest and transient [ ]. PPARγ agonists, such as rosiglitazone and pioglitazone, act through transcriptional control and modulation of the MAPK and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways, both of which are hyperactivated in dedifferentiated DTC, resulting in the partial restoration of thyroid differentiation markers and NIS trafficking [ ]. Moreover, HDAC inhibitors, including valproic acid and vorinostat, promote the acetylation of histones H3 and H4, causing chromatin relaxation and enhancing the transcriptional accessibility of thyroid-specific promoters, such as NIS, TPO, and TG. This promotes the re-expression and membrane localization of NIS [ ]. Recent research has demonstrated that clotrimazole, an antifungal agent, and ebastine, an antihistamine, can increase RAI uptake in both normal mouse thyrocytes and human thyroid cancer cells. This effect is attributed to the allosteric inhibition of the valosin-containing protein (VCP, also known as p97). Moreover, the research highlighted the crucial role of ARF4 in directing NIS trafficking to the plasma membrane [ ]. Clinically meaningful redifferentiation has also been achieved in patients treated with MAPK pathway and BRAF inhibitors. Various agents, such as dabrafenib, trametinib, and selumetinib, can enhance iodine uptake in thyroid cancer cells by upregulating NIS expression and restoring its functional activity [ , ]. In prior research, treatment with larotrectinib induced redifferentiation in RAI-resistant thyroid tumors with NTRK rearrangements [ ], while treatment with selpercatinib successfully restored iodine uptake in nearly all metastatic sites in a patient whose tumor harbored a RET rearrangement/fusion as the sole genetic alteration [ ]. Although these findings are promising, they are based on individual case studies and are not yet recommended as standard short-term pretreatment strategies before RAI in patients with DTC.

## Adverse Effects and Management
The human genome contains a total of 90 TKs, which include 32 cellular TKs and 56 RTKs [ ]. The US FDA and EMA have approved TKIs for the first-line treatment of advanced or metastatic RAIR-DTCs [ ]. Although some TKIs are very specific to their protein kinases, most of them obstruct multiple kinases, eventually causing several adverse effects, such as cardiovascular adverse effects, hand–foot syndrome, diarrhea, hepatotoxicity, and weight loss.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, TKIs have transformed the treatment of advanced DTCs and RAIR-DTCs by targeting key molecular alterations, such as BRAF, RAS, RET, and TERT mutations. They provide promising alternatives for patients who do not respond adequately to conventional treatments, such as surgery, RAI therapy, and TSH suppression. TKIs have shown particular benefit in managing advanced RAIR-DTC, MTC, and ATC. However, their long-term use is limited by resistance due to secondary mutations or alternative pathway activation, in addition to tolerability issues. These challenges highlight the need for combination approaches, such as immunotherapy and redifferentiation. Advances in understanding thyroid cancer biology continue to drive the development of next-generation TKIs and personalized treatment approaches. Ongoing clinical trials are essential for optimizing therapeutic outcomes.
