## Abstract

## Background
Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a highly lethal cancer with limited therapeutic options. Integration of molecular profiling may enable personalized treatment approaches. We evaluated the clinical utility of molecularly matched treatment (MMT) in a real-life cohort of PDAC patients.

## Patients and methods
A retrospective chart review of clinical/molecular data was carried out, including all PDAC patients with a contributive molecular profile. Survival outcomes were compared across three groups: patients with actionable molecular alterations (MAs) who received MMT (MA/MMT), patients with actionable alterations without MMT (MA/No MMT), and patients without actionable alterations (No MA/No MMT).

## Results
Among 342 patients (median age 61 years; 48% female; 95% Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group 0-1), molecular profiling was carried out using tissue (50%), liquid biopsy (45%), or both (5%). The most common gene alterations were KRAS (84%), TP53 (72%), and CDKN2A (26%). Actionable alterations were found in 69 patients (20%), with 31 (45%) receiving MMT. Targeted therapies included notably olaparib (BRCA2), trastuzumab (HER2), and KRAS G12C inhibitors. Median overall survival (OS) from metastatic diagnosis was significantly longer in the MA/MMT group (32.9 months) compared with MA/No MMT (12.9 months) and No MA/No MMT groups (17.6 months) (P = 0.0008). From initial diagnosis, OS was 34.9, 27.1, and 21.5 months, respectively (P = 0.005). Median progression-free survival from MMT initiation was 5.5 months. The mean growth modulation index was 1.7 in the MA/MMT group versus 0.8 in the MA/No MMT group (P < 0.05). In variate analysis, MMT was correlated with improved OS [hazard ratio (HR) 0.51, P < 0.001 from metastasis; HR 0.59, P < 0.01 from diagnosis].

## Conclusion
To conclude, molecular profiling identified actionable alterations in 20% of PDAC patients. MMT was associated with a significant survival benefit, supporting molecular profiling into routine management, especially with the perspective of KRAS inhibitors.

Key words: pancreatic adenocarcinoma, precision medicine, actionable alterations, KRAS

## Highlights

•Forty-five percent of patients with actionable mutations received MMT.
•MMT significantly improved median OS to 32.9 months versus 12.9-17.6 months in others.
•Targeted therapies included olaparib (BRCA2), trastuzumab (HER2), or KRAS G12C inhibitors.

## Introduction
Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) has a rising incidence and is predicted to become the second cause of cancer-related death in 2030-2040 in Western countries. At diagnosis, PDAC is divided into various subgroups, including resectable tumor (10%-20%), borderline resectable tumor (10%), locally advanced tumor (30%), and metastatic disease (50%). Only resection (upfront or after a neoadjuvant treatment) can cure patients. Still, despite this, only 30% of those resected patients are alive at 5 years. 
In advanced disease, the first-line standard of care is based either on the combination of 5-fluorouracil, (nal)-irinotecan, and oxaliplatine (FOLFIRINOX regimen), or on an alternative treatment combining gemcitabine with nab-paclitaxel. , Both strategies are characterized by a median overall survival (OS) not exceeding 11 months. In 60% of patients, a second line of treatment will be administered at progression, with limited results.
In solid tumors, precision medicine based on next-generation sequencing (NGS) has emerged over the last 20 years. In the first studies, only 15%-20% of patients received a specific targeted therapy based on their molecular profile, with even fewer patients experiencing clinical benefits. , Nevertheless, a prospective randomized trial in breast cancer reported an improvement in progression-free survival (PFS) when combined with a precision medicine approach. They benefited from precision medicine only when molecular alterations (MAs) were classified as level I and II according to the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) Scale of Clinical Actionability of Molecular Targets (ESCAT). 
In pancreatic cancer, KRAS is a key oncogenic driver found at early stages of tumor formation and observed in >90% of pancreatic cancers. This mutation drives the continuous activation of the KRAS-GTP-bound protein, leading to downstream activation of oncogenic pathways, thus promoting cell proliferation and survival. So far, KRAS is not targeted in the clinics, except the KRAS G12C mutation, found in <2% of PDAC patients. Other frequently mutated genes are loss of tumor suppressor genes such as TP53, SMAD4, and CDKN2A, which are not targetable. None the less, in ∼25% of patients, other rare actionable alterations can be found at low frequencies such as AKT, PI3K, FGFR, NTRK, MYC, MET, and NOTCH pathways; amplification of ERBB2; mutations or fusions in ROS1, KIT, BRAF, and RET; DNA damage repair genes such as DDR, BRCA1, BRCA2, and PALB2; as well as microsatellite instability (MSI) tumors. Those actionable alterations are enriched in the KRAS wild-type subgroup. Among these, a minority have been proven effective in PDAC. Notably, olaparib is the only treatment approved explicitly in patients who have responded to first-line platinum-based chemotherapy, in locally advanced and metastatic pancreatic disease with a germline-mutated BRCA1 or BRCA2, as shown in the POLO trial. NTRK fusions, which are rare, can also be targeted with specific inhibitors. 
Recently, Pishvaian et al. found a positive impact of precision medicine on OS in PDAC, using targeted therapies based on actionable alterations in the Know Your Tumor program. They collected tumor samples from 640 patients and found highly actionable genomic alterations in 27% of cases (such as in BRCA1/2 genes or ATM mutations). Few patients (n = 17) received matched therapy with a prolonged PFS compared with patients receiving unmatched therapy. However, one must note that routine molecular profiling is not fully integrated in ESMO guidelines for PDAC management. Recently, an ESMO study evaluated the accessibility to molecular profiling in Europe and showed that in most countries, advanced molecular profiling remains largely inaccessible in routine clinical practice, and is limited to clinical trials, basic research, or tertiary centers. They also noted the increasing cost of these techniques. In the era of potential new active treatments such as rat sarcoma virus (RAS) inhibitors, we report the clinical applicability of precision medicine in PDAC and determine the impact of molecularly matched treatments (MMTs) in patients with actionable MAs.

## Patients and methods

## Patients
All patients diagnosed with histologically confirmed PDAC between 2015 and 2022 and monitored at our institution were considered for inclusion, provided that a comprehensive molecular profile was available as part of the MOSCATO (NCT01566019), MATCH-R (NCT02517892), or STING (NCT0493252) trials. Data collection was carried out through a review of hospital records and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at Gustave Roussy. This retrospective study adheres to the French MR004 framework concerning data protection regulations for non-interventional retrospective health research and received approval from our institutional review board (CSET N°2022-94), in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration.

## Molecular alteration analyses
Molecular profiling was proposed to all eligible PDAC patients as part of their standard clinical management within our institutional precision medicine programs, aiming to identify actionable MAs. Molecular testing was conducted using various targeted panels, including in-house tumor tissue panels and commercially available panels such as FoundationMedicine® (tumor tissue) and FoundationOne Liquid CDx® (Foundation Medicine; Cambridge, MA) (liquid biopsy) ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105899). The classification of MAs in terms of actionability followed the ESCAT guidelines ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105899). Treatments were classified as MMTs if they targeted a specific molecular abnormality, encompassing standard therapies, off-label therapies, or participation in dedicated clinical trials. For homologous recombination deficiency tumors ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105899), MMT was defined as the use of small-molecule inhibitors, such as poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors, while chemotherapies were considered unmatched.

## Clinical outcomes
Only patients who had received at least one line of therapy in the advanced setting were considered for analysis regarding the effects of MMT. To minimize variability due to the timing of molecular profiling and sample collection, the median OS from metastatic diagnosis was determined as the time from starting chemotherapy first-line to death from any cause. Median OS was determined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause. The model was adjusted for stage, line of MMT, and age. Patients were categorized into three groups: those without MAs (No MA/No MMT), those with MAs but without MMT (MA/No MMT), and those who received MMT (MA/MMT). Median PFS was assessed from the start of MMT to disease progression or death. For patients not receiving MMT, the PFS calculation considered the line of therapy following molecular profiling, excluding those who did not undergo treatment after profiling. The growth modulation index (GMI) was calculated as the ratio of PFS during MMT (PFSn) to PFS in the prior line of therapy (PFSn−1). A GMI ≥1.3 was considered indicative of a clinically meaningful benefit, corresponding to a time to progression at least 30% longer than in the previous treatment line. The GMI utilizes the patient as its own control, and can be used to determine whether the new line of treatment brings a clinical benefit, reducing the bias of treatments received in different lines.

## Statistical analysis
Descriptive statistics were carried out using Fisher’s exact test and Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables, while continuous variables were analyzed using two-sample t-tests and Wilcoxon rank sum tests. Kaplan–Meier estimates were used to generate survival curves, with univariate analyses conducted via the log-rank test. Multivariate analyses were carried out using a Cox proportional hazards model, adjusting for known prognostic factors such as age, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) status, disease stage at diagnosis, and presence of liver metastases. All statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio2022.12.0+353 ‘Elsbeth Geranium’ Release.

## Results

## Clinical characteristics
In our cohort, 342 patients with PDAC were included with a contributive molecular profile, of whom 48% were female ( A). Median age at diagnosis was 61 years (54-67 years), and ECOG status was 0-1 in 95% of patients. Pain was the most frequent symptom at diagnosis (48%), followed by icterus (24%). Median CA19-9 at diagnosis was 886 UI/ml (153-5233UI/ml). Among non-metastatic patients at diagnosis (n = 142, 42%), there were 63 (44%) resectable disease, 34 (24%) borderline disease, and 45 (32%) locally advanced disease. Tumors were mainly located in the head of the pancreas (45%), followed by the body (22%) and the tail (20%). Two hundred patients were diagnosed at the metastatic stage, and metachronous metastases occurred in 95% (128) of non-metastatic patients. The liver was the most frequent metastatic site (66% of cases), followed by the peritoneum (18%), lungs (18%), and lymph nodes (15%). The main clinical characteristics are described in .
 

## Molecular profiling
Molecular profiling was carried out on tumor tissue (n = 170, 50%) or liquid biopsy (n = 155, 45%) or both (n = 17, 5%), mostly at metastatic stage (87%). There were no differences in the clinical characteristics based on the technics carried out for molecular profiling ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105899), nor in the main MAs, except for KRAS that was found less frequently when using liquid biopsy (78% versus 90%, P < 0.05) ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105899). The most frequently altered gene was KRAS (n = 288, 84%), followed by TP53 (n = 247, 72%) and CDKN2A (n = 88, 26%). The most frequent KRAS mutation was G12D, found in 120 patients (42% of KRAS mutated patients), followed by G12V (n = 67, 23%) and G12R (n = 36, 13%). The KRAS G12C mutation was found only in 13 patients (5%). Other mutations are detailed in .
 Overall, 69 patients (20%) exhibited a potentially actionable MA according to ESCAT classification at the time of inclusion (ESCAT I-IV). The most common actionable alterations were mutations in DNA repair genes (BRCA2 and PALB2) (n = 26, 8%), KRAS G12C mutation (n = 11), amplification of HER2 (n = 8), BRAF mutations (n = 5), FGFR2 fusion (n = 4), and MSI tumors (n = 3). ESCAT classification was determined at the time of carrying out molecular profiling, and was classified as I in 8% of cases, II in 4% of cases, and III in 6% of cases ( B).
Of these 69 patients, 31 patients (45%) received MMT as a compassionate access or through clinical trials. MTT was usually received as the second line of treatment [median 2 (0-3)]. MTTs received were olaparib (BRCA2 mutations), trastuzumab-based treatment regimen (HER2 amplification), NOTCH 1 inhibitor (in one patient), anti-MEK (in one patient harboring a BRAF mutation), KRAS G12C inhibitors (KRAS G12C), erdafitinib (FGFR2 fusion), protein arginin methyltransferase 5 inhibitor (MTAP deletion), selpercatinib (RET fusion), programmed death-(ligand) 1 inhibitors (MSI and POLE tumors), encorafenib cetuximab (BRAF V600E mutation), zenocutuzumab (NRG1 fusion), and ataxia-telagiectasia and Rad3-related protein inhibitor (BRIP1 alteration) ( ).
 

## Outcomes
To explore the survival benefit of receiving an MMT, we divided patients into three categories: depending on the presence of an MA and whether they received an MMT ( A). In the subgroup of patients who did receive an MMT (MA/MMT), median OS from metastases diagnosis was 32.9 months [95% confidence interval (CI) 26.8 months-not reached (NR)] compared with 12.9 months (95% CI 11.1-30.0 months) for patients with an actionable alteration who did not receive an MMT (MA/No MMT) and 17.6 months (95% CI 15.6.0-19.1 months) for patients with no actionable alterations (No MA/No MMT) (P = 0.0008) ( A). Median OS from initial diagnosis (including non-metastatic stage) was 34.9 months (95% CI 29.5 months-NR) in the MA/MMT group compared with 27.1 months (95% CI 19.3 months-NR) in the MA/No MMT group and 21.5 months (95% CI 19.1-24.9 months) in the No MA/No MMT group (P = 0.005) ( B). Same results were obtained for the subgroup of patients with a molecular profile done on tumor tissue ( A, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105899) and with liquid biopsy ( B, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105899). In the subgroup of patients who had a molecular profile done in second line or less, median OS from metastases diagnosis was 42.1 months (95% CI 20.1 months-NR) compared with 10.4 months (95% CI 7.2 months-NR) for patients with an actionable alteration who did not receive an MMT (MA/No MMT) and 14.5 months (95% CI 11.6-17.1 months) for patients with no actionable alterations (No MA/No MMT) (P < 0.0001) ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105899).
 Median OS from initiation of targeted treatment was 21.6 months (95% CI 14.4 months-NR) ( C). Median PFS from initiation of targeted treatment was 5.52 months (95% CI 4.3-9.2 months) in the MA/MMT group compared with 3.4 months (95% CI 2.4-5.6 months) in the MA/No MMT group and 3.0 months (95% CI 2.5-3.3 months) in the No MA/No MMT group (P = 0.02) ( D). In the subgroup of patients with an actionable alteration (MA/MMT and MA/No MTT), the mean GMI was 1.7 in the MA/MMT group and 0.8 in the MA/No MTT group (P < 0.05) ( ).
In a multivariate analysis, after adjustment for age at diagnosis, stage at diagnosis, KRAS status, and number of previous treatment lines, the hazard ratio (HR) of the OS from metastases diagnosis was 0.51 (95% CI 0.31-0.84, P < 0.001) for the MA/MMT group ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105899). The OS from initial diagnosis was 0.59 (95% CI 0.36-0.97, P < 0.001) after adjustment for the same variables.

## Discussion
Our study highlights the impact of precision medicine in pancreatic cancer, demonstrating that molecular profiling can identify actionable targets in ∼20% of cases, leading to the administration of MMTs that may improve clinical outcomes. This percentage of actionable alterations is quite high, less than in the Pishvaian study (27%) or other studies, , but probably overestimated, with the study being conducted in a tertiary center with access to molecular profiling and specific referral of patients for clinical trials.
Despite the aggressive nature of PDAC, patients receiving MMT demonstrated prolonged survival compared with those who did not receive targeted therapies, as previously shown by Pishvaian in the Know Your Tumor program. In this cohort, several metrics were used to evaluate the efficacy of the precision medicine approach, such as OS but also GMI that utilizes the patient as its own control. Considering that targeted therapies may induce clinical benefits aside from tumor shrinkage, GMI is sometimes used in early drug development to assess the benefit of targeted therapies selected by molecular profiling in patients with advanced refractory cancers, reducing the bias of treatments received in different lines. These findings reinforce the necessity of integrating molecular profiling into standard clinical practice for PDAC patients, especially the application of the ESCAT classification system that was developed recently, proving its utility in clinical practice. However, important barriers such as the reimbursement of the molecular profiling tests and availability of a suitable medicine or drugs (no reimbursement of several treatments and no available clinical trials) must be noted and might limit the generalization of our results. In our study, most patients received targeted agents through enrollment into clinical trials.
The most frequently altered gene in our cohort was KRAS, with KRAS G12D being the predominant mutation, followed by G12V and G12R. Our multivariate analysis revealed that the presence of KRAS mutation was associated with poorer OS, consistent with previous studies. , , While KRAS mutations have traditionally been considered undruggable, recent advancements in anti-RAS therapies, particularly pan-RAS inhibitors or specific codon inhibitors, , , represent a significant breakthrough in the therapeutic landscape of PDAC, with an ongoing phase III trial (NCT06625320). In our cohort, the only targetable KRAS mutation was the KRAS G12C mutation, although they are rare in PDAC. , Moreover, novel therapeutic approaches targeting KRAS in a broader spectrum, including combination strategies with other targeted therapies or chemotherapy, could further revolutionize treatment options for PDAC patients. , 
Importantly, about half of the molecular profiles were carried out through liquid biopsy, as part of patients’ routine management at advanced stages in real-life settings. The use of a mixed tissue/liquid biopsy molecular profiling method in a real-life setting showed that both methods can detect MAs ( and , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105899) without changing the results observed with a benefit for patients receiving targeted therapies. However, this might explain the slight differences with previous studies in identifying actionable alterations, especially gene fusions. Also, we found in our cohort an enrichment in the proportion of KRAS-wt tumors, with 15% of KRAS-wt PDAC compared with 10% in the literature. , Sensibility of liquid biopsy could partly explain these results as shown in , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105899, with a lower representation of KRAS mutation when using liquid biopsy. A recent study showed that CA19-9 levels and hepatic metastases are predictive of KRAS mutation detection in liquid biopsy. However, the results of our real-life study are important since liquid biopsies represent an emerging technology that allows non-invasive serial sampling of tumor material, which might become part of the management of PDAC. 
While our study provides valuable insights into the potential benefits of precision medicine, several challenges persist. These include issues with accessing molecular testing, the restricted availability of targeted therapies for certain molecular subtypes, and the necessity for further validation of novel therapeutic approaches. Future research should focus on expanding access to molecular profiling, refining treatment algorithms, and developing next-generation targeted therapies to improve survival outcomes in PDAC. 
This study has several limitations. Firstly, most patients had undergone multiple prior treatments at the time NGS was carried out, introducing a survival bias, as only those who lived long enough were able to receive molecular profiling. None the less, the use of MMTs was associated with improved survival despite this late intervention and selection bias and similar results were observed, though even more striking, in patients who were treated in the second line or less with a targeted agent. Encouragingly, our institution is increasingly implementing molecular profiling earlier in the disease progression. Secondly, molecular profiling was often conducted through liquid biopsy, a less invasive and more accessible method, though its lower sensitivity remains a drawback. Thirdly, this is a single-center study with a relatively small sample size and limited statistical power, which limits the generalization of the study; however, the findings are consistent with those previously reported by Pishvaian et al. with 17 treated patients. Finally, access to targeted therapies is a critical issue, as patients receiving MMTs were treated with off-label or compassionate-use drugs or were enrolled in clinical trials. This highlights ongoing disparities in health care access across institutions and countries. Detecting actionable mutations without providing corresponding therapies would be ineffective and ethically problematic.
In conclusion, our study emphasizes the importance of precision medicine in PDAC, showing that MMTs can result in significant clinical benefits for patients. The emergence of anti-RAS therapies marks a crucial shift in the PDAC treatment paradigm, providing new hopes for patients with historically difficult-to-treat tumors. Ongoing research efforts will be essential to fully integrate these novel agents into standard clinical practice, thereby enhancing the prognosis for PDAC patients.
