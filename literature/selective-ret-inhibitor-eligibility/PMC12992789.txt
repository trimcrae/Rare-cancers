## Introduction
Genomic profiling is used in the diagnosis, monitoring, and treatment of malignancies. Comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) is a next-generation sequencing (NGS) approach that can test multiple genes simultaneously, potentially saving time, money, and sample material. Costs associated with genomic profiling could be a prohibitive barrier to the widespread implementation of genomic testing in routine clinical practice.

## Methods
A cost calculator was developed to demonstrate the cost difference between targeted panel NGS and individual single-gene testing (SGT). The calculator evaluates the cost of testing for more than 30 biomarkers included in the 2024 update of the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) Scale for Clinical Actionability of Molecular Targets. Sensitivity and specificity data for NGS and SGT, biomarker prevalence, and the number needed to predict (NNP) metric were used to estimate the cost of each test per correctly identified patient (CCIP) in different types of tumors. Calculator results are available in EUR (€) and USD ($), inflated to 2025 values. The calculator generates results for 14 different advanced malignancies.

## Results
At base case in EUR (€), CCIP was lower with NGS than sequential SGT for metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC), advanced prostate cancer, advanced pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), advanced cholangiocarcinoma (CC), advanced soft-tissue sarcoma (STS), advanced thyroid cancer, and cancer of unknown primary origin (CUP). In USD ($) all of the aforementioned tumors except for advanced CC had more favorable CCIPs for NGS, compared with sequential SGT.

## Conclusion
The insights provided by this calculator demonstrate lower costs using NGS compared with SGT for many malignancies, which suggests that opportunities exist for the expanded use of NGS in routine clinical practice for molecular profiling of patients with cancer.

## Key Summary Points

 

## Introduction
Precision medicine uses a patient’s individual genomic profile to construct a tailored therapeutic approach for the management of a disease [ , ]. Biomarker testing-based precision medicine offers potential benefits to patients with rare cancers or tumors that are difficult to treat with conventional therapies, if their tumors have “clinically actionable” genetic mutations [ ]. Clinically actionable genomic alterations are mutations that may respond to treatment with targeted therapies [ ]. Biomarker testing is a component of internationally recognized diagnostic and treatment guidelines in oncology, such as recommendations issued by the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) and American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) [ , ]. ESMO devised a scale to rank specific genomic alterations, according to evidence-based criteria to aid the process of selecting patients for targeted therapies. The ESMO Clinical Actionability of Molecular Targets (ESCAT) scale classifies biomarkers into tiers I–X. The categories are informed by the level of evidence supporting their use in the treatment of specific malignancies [ ].
ESCAT I biomarkers have an established interaction with a drug that is associated with improved clinical outcomes, evidenced by the results of clinical trials. The improved clinical outcomes are associated with a particular tumor type, and a specific genomic alteration. The evidence for ESCAT II biomarkers relates to drugs that exhibit antitumor activity when targeting a particular biomarker; however, the magnitude of the effect is unclear and warrants further investigation [ ].
Tumor-agnostic therapies target genomic alterations which may be present in a wide range of tumors [ ]. Drugs with pan-tumor efficacy include the programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) inhibitor pembrolizumab, the selective tyrosine kinase inhibitors entrectinib, repotrectinib, and selpercatinib, and the selective tropomyosin kinase receptor inhibitor larotrectinib [ – ]. There are numerous examples of clinically actionable alterations which can be targeted with approved therapies, such as high/deficient mismatch repair (MSI-H/dMMR), NTRK1/2/3 fusions, RET, FGFR1/2/3 fusions/mutations, BRAF mutations, and tumor mutation burden-high (TMB-H). MSI-H/dMMR can be targeted with the immune checkpoint inhibitors pembrolizumab and nivolumab [ ]. NTRK1/2/3 fusions can be targeted with larotrectinib or entrectinib [ ], and RET alterations can be targeted with drugs such as selpercatinib and pralsetinib [ ], and FGFR1/2/3 fusions/mutations can be targeted with drugs such as erdafitinib, futibatinib, infigratinib, and pemigatinib [ ]. Finally, mutations in BRAF can be targeted with drugs such as vemurafenib, dabrafenib, and encorafenib [ ], and immunotherapies have efficacy in the treatment of TMB-H tumors [ ].
Next-generation sequencing (NGS) is a highly accurate genomic testing approach which can test multiple genes simultaneously from a single-tissue sample [ – ]. Comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) is a NGS approach that can detect numerous genomic aberrations such as single-nucleotide variants, deletions, translocations/fusions, amplifications [ ], and tumor mutational burden (TMB) which is the total number of somatic variations in a defined region of a tumor genome [ , ].
Single-gene testing (SGT) is a genomic profiling approach that involves the analysis of one gene or a small number of genes at a time [ ]. Common SGT methods include Sanger sequencing, polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and quantitative PCR (qPCR), which are used to detect known mutations or small variants. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) may also be used in this context to assess gene copy number changes or protein expression associated with specific gene alterations [ – ]. The number of potentially identifiable genomic variants differs between SGTs and NGS, because SGTs only detect mutations in the gene being tested and lack the sensitivity to reveal novel mutations within the target gene [ – ].
Recognition of the importance of identifying predictive biomarkers has led to changes in the reimbursement landscape across different geographies [ ]. In 2024, ESMO released a report recommending that NGS be used for patients with advanced cancers in countries where tumor-agnostic targeted therapies are accessible [ ]. ESMO also recommended the use of NGS for patients with advanced (adv.) prostate cancer, non-squamous non-small cell lung cancer (nonSq-NSCLC), cholangiocarcinoma (CC), and ovarian cancers (OC) [ ]. In the USA, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines recommend comprehensive germline and somatic profiling to identify candidates for targeted therapies in indications such as invasive breast cancer and NSCLC [ , ]. NGS testing is not routine in all regions, due to challenges such as lack of reimbursement, standardization, and guidance on use [ ].
Despite the international recognition and proven efficacy of biomarker testing in improving clinical outcomes, lack of understanding of the cost-effectiveness of genomic profiling has hindered its widespread adoption. Addressing this cost-related knowledge gap at the earliest opportunity is crucial to ensure timely access to essential biomarker testing.
There are an increasingly wide range of tumor types for which biomarker testing is recommended, and an ongoing effort to develop targeted therapies based on patients’ and tumor’s genetic characteristics [ ]. The cost-effectiveness of genomic profiling techniques is a feature of clinical decision-making. Cost-effectiveness studies have compared NGS with SGT in indications such as NSCLC and CRC [ , ]. However, reimbursement of biomarker testing is not consistent across geographies [ ]. Lack of robust evidence on the cost-effectiveness of NGS and disparities in funding models impedes access to therapies which may improve patient outcomes [ ].
Stenzinger et al. [ ] developed a cost calculator to compare the cost difference between targeted panel NGS and other multiplex diagnostic advances and SGT. The 2023 cost calculator adopted the CCIP metric to directly compare different tests, accounting for the cost of individual tests and diagnostic accuracy.
This paper introduces an updated version of Stenzinger et al.’s 2023 cost calculator [ ]. The updated calculator captures the full cost of genomic testing including initial setup and maintenance, cost for a given diagnostic test, and the ongoing costs of validated, genomic tests. Released in June 2025 [ ], this updated cost calculator includes the full range of ESCAT I and ESCAT II biomarkers and tumor-agnostic genomic alterations for advanced cancers, based on the 2024 ESMO recommendations.
The objective of this updated cost calculator is to pressure-test the CCIP metric and methodology developed by Stenzinger et al. [ ]. The updated calculator considers factors that affect the achievement of an accurate diagnosis and the cost of individual testing for biomarkers of interest. Comparing CCIP across different types of tests for specific tumors can provide insight into the cost difference between sequential SGT and NGS, the latter of which has the potential to improve turnaround time, reduce sample material needed, and accurately detect genomic alterations that drive advanced malignancies.

## Methods

## Tumor Types, Sensitivity, and Specificity of NGS and Sequential SGT
The scope of the cost calculator covers clinically actionable mutations categorized as ESCAT I or II biomarkers or tumor-agnostic genomic alterations. The model included both DNA and RNA tests analyzed using NGS and the following SGTs: IHC, PCR/qPCR, and FISH. The model calculates the cost of NGS and SGT testing in the following indications: adv. nonSq-NSCLC, squamous NSCLC (Sq-NSCLC), metastatic breast cancer (mBC), metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC), adv. prostate cancer (adv. PC), metastatic gastric cancer (mGC), adv. pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (adv. PDAC), adv. hepatocellular carcinoma (adv. HCC), adv. cholangiocarcinoma (adv. CC), adv. ovarian cancer (adv. OC), adv. soft-tissue sarcoma (adv. STS), adv. gastrointestinal stromal tumor (adv. GIST), adv. thyroid cancer (adv. TC), and cancer of unknown primary origin (CUP).
Data for sensitivity and specificity for 34 genetic targets were extracted from published, multitumor studies for the following diagnostic tests (FISH or chromogenic in situ hybridization [CISH], PCR, Sanger, small/large-targeted panel NGS, and qPCR). Information on the characteristics of the included studies are in the cost calculator, which is accessible from the link at the end of this paper. ## Prevalence, Test Costs, and Simulated Calculation
Data on the prevalence of NGS was sourced from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset (see Supplementary Table  ). TCGA data can be used to identify mutually exclusive genomic alterations. Microsatellite instability high/deficient mismatch repair (MSI-H/dMMR) was assumed to be linked to alterations in MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2
[ , ]. The following tumor-agnostic biomarkers were considered in different advanced cancers: NTRK1/2/3 fusions, RET, FGFR1/2/3 fusions/mutations, BRAF mutations, MSI-H/dMMR, and TMB-H [ – ]. If no specific prevalence for a cancer type could be found, general gene alteration prevalence data was used. For adv. STS, general gene alteration prevalence data without sarcoma subtypes was used. This is due to the lack of specification of adv. STS subtypes in ESMO 2024 recommendations [ ]. For TMB-H, it is assumed that TMB-H is related to genes (TP53, KEAP1, BRAF
) for adv. NonSq-NSCLC [ – ]. The single gene prevalence of TMB-H for each cancer type was added to the corresponding TCGA prevalence due to data paucity for mBC, adv. PC, mGC, adv. HCC, adv. Ovarian, and adv. TC (see Supplementary Table  ). This proxy-based approach was chosen due to differences in requirements for tissue sample testing, fast turnaround times associated with targeted panel testing, minimally invasive methods for the extraction of biological material, and cost-effectiveness of estimating TMB. Aggregated single gene alterations were used for CUP prevalence, because unfavorable CUP is not a specific classification in the TCGA dataset.
Costs were inflated to reflect 2025 values using an inflation tool which displays the time value of money based on historical inflation and consumer price index data [ ]. The costs of sequential SGT and NGS (large panel: broad molecular profiling tests exome and whole genome sequencing) were validated in EUR and USD. Published price estimates for each test were also sourced. Normanno et al. [ ] provided representative estimates of the cost of each test in Europe. For USD, estimates were taken from clinical diagnostic laboratory and 2024 hospital outpatient department payments.
Starting with the most prevalent genomic alteration for a specific tumor type, a simulation of 1000 lab tests was conducted. A second confirmatory test was conducted for patients with a positive result from the initial screening test. The matrices were adjusted according to the sensitivity and specificity of this serial testing approach [ ]. While evaluating each gene alteration sequentially in order of highest to lowest prevalence, all positives (TP + FP) were subtracted from N (the total number of lab tests). This procedure was repeated for individuals with a negative test result until the list of ESCAT I and ESCAT II genomic alterations was exhausted.
The same process was applied to NGS, without repeating the procedures used for sequential SGT. Comparisons of SGT and NGS were conducted using the cost of a test per correctly identified patient (CCIP). Lower values correspond to lower total costs to achieve one correctly identified patient. ## Results

## Cost, Sensitivity, and Specificity of Sequential SGT and NGS Tests
The cost calculator estimates the CCIP for NGS and sequential SGT. The cost difference can then be compared between these two genomic profiling strategies. For IHC, sensitivity was estimated at 89.61%, and specificity at 86.39%. The price of IHC screening was estimated at €290 per test in EUR (€), and $167 in USD ($). Test sensitivity for IHC followed by PCR was 83.31% and specificity was 99.73%. Test sensitivity for IHC followed by FISH was 78.67% and specificity was 99.53%. For FISH, sensitivity was estimated at 87.79% and specificity at 96.56%. The price of FISH was estimated at €795 per test in EUR (€), and $464 in USD ($) [ ]. Sensitivity for PCR/qPCR was estimated at 92.97% and specificity at 97.99%. The price of PCR/qPCR was estimated at €261 per test in EUR (€), and $335 in USD ($). Sensitivity for NGS was estimated at 83.77% and specificity at 96.77%. The price of NGS testing was estimated at €1538 in EUR (€), and $1799 in USD ($) for an up-to-50-gene panel [ ]. ## Discussion
A full understanding of the budget impact of genomic testing is needed to inform clinical decision-making to optimize patient outcomes [ ]. With more therapies being approved for patients with specific mutations, it becomes increasingly important to accurately identify candidates who are most likely to achieve clinical benefit. NGS is a DNA and RNA sequencing technology which can rapidly detect variants and mutations in biological material, positioning this method as a tool for disease diagnosis, prognostication, therapeutic decision-making, and patient follow-up [ ]. NGS was preceded in development by SGT which identifies a specific genomic alteration in one gene [ ]. Comparatively, NGS can detect numerous alterations in a panel of genes but has technical limitations such as inconsistent reliability detecting copy-number changes and triplet repeat changes [ , ]. Clinical integration of NGS has been met with challenges, some of which relate to performance of the tests [ ] and others related to perceived prohibitive costs, lack of reimbursement, failure to refer relevant patients’ biological samples for testing, and inadequate facilities for testing in certain locations [ , ]. Stenzinger et al. [ ] developed the NNP metric and a cost calculator to compare the cost of sequential SGT and NGS to test for ESCAT I and II biomarkers in selected tumors. We updated Stenzinger et al.’s 2023 cost calculator in order to allow for future adaptations to accommodate country and region-specific data in accordance with corresponding guidelines.
Compared with Stenzinger et al.’s 2023 cost calculator which considered ESCAT I & II biomarkers, our updated cost calculator considers the full range of ESCAT I and II biomarkers including five more indications: adv. OC, adv. STS, adv. GIST, adv. TC, and CUP. Results from Stenzinger et al.’s 2023 cost calculator [ ] accommodated a European perspective on the pricing of NGS and SGT methods. Our updated cost calculator has the additional functionality of including a US perspective, with updated information on the pricing and maintenance costs of genomic profiling outside the European market. Thirteen additional biomarkers were included in this cost calculator, which intends to inform cost-effectiveness analyses of a sequential SGT algorithm vs. NGS. Published literature with insights on features of the cost calculator such as specificity and sensitivity of selected tests has been made available and accessible in the updated version of the cost calculator.
This cost calculator showed that CCIP in EUR (€) favored NGS in mCRC, adv. PC, adv. PDAC, adv. CC, adv. STS, adv. TC, and CUP. CCIP in USD ($) for all of the aforementioned tumors except for adv. CC favored NGS over sequential SGT. Biomarker testing using sequential SGT was most expensive for adv. STS, compared with the other tumor types in the cost calculator. Lung tumors have a relatively high prevalence of potentially clinically actionable genetic mutations [ ]. Using a comprehensive approach to identify genomic alterations in pulmonary malignancies can provide clinical benefits, guide treatment decisions, and allow initiation of effective and timely therapeutic interventions [ ]. In this scenario, CGP may be preferable to SGT, regardless of the cost difference. There are also cost differences between CGP and target panels, which test a select set of genes for genomic alterations [ ]. Comparing quality-adjusted life years associated with different types of tests can provide insight for healthcare providers making decisions about distribution and allocation of resources in the oncology setting [ ]. In countries such as South Korea and the USA, cost-effectiveness analyses have found NGS to confer a survival benefit in patients with adv. NSCLC [ , ].
There is a need for greater education on how to interpret and effectively communicate the results of biomarker tests. Lack of expertise in this area has led some health authorities to reject propositions to reimburse costs associated with genomic profiling. The clinical utility of genomic profiling is also assessed differently by payers across geographic regions. These payers may have disparate evidentiary standards for assessing the suitability of NGS tests [ ]. Furthermore, the computer capacity and storage requirements for NGS as well as the expertise required to comprehensively analyze and interpret output affect the applicability of this technology to local settings [ ].
Customizable inputs in the cost calculator allow end users to adjust the model to consider labor, infrastructure setup, and maintenance costs, improving the relevance of results for specific regions. This updated cost calculator is designed to provide clarity on the affordability of NGS and the potential for real-world cost savings using NGS compared with sequential SGT which has been cited as a reason for policy makers and payers’ skepticism about the economic value of NGS [ ]. Future modifications to the cost calculator could introduce settings that make results more applicable to different regions.
Budget impact analyses are valued by regulators and reimbursement authorities reviewing the economic sustainability of implementing interventions in the fields of medicine and healthcare [ ]. Evidence for the cost-effectiveness of NGS in oncology comes from numerous studies in countries such as the USA, UK, Netherlands, Norway, and Thailand [ – ]. Not only do the findings of these studies provide insight into the cost of NGS in different types of tumors but they also specify instances where NGS may not be cost-effective, such as in testing for small numbers of genomic aberrations. However, heterogeneity in study design and differences in the type of NGS evaluated limit the generalizability of the results of these studies. The majority of studies involve the use of targeted panel sequencing, whereas whole genome sequencing and CGP are less often represented in cost-effectiveness studies of NGS [ ]. The results of these studies align with our findings that NGS has the potential to be more cost-effective than SGT in a variety of tumor types in different countries [ , ]. Although CCIP can provide insight into the cost difference for achieving an accurate diagnosis using sequential SGT or NGS, this metric cannot form the basis of decisions on reimbursement or selection of specific therapies for patients with advanced malignancies.
Differences in the CCIP for NGS vs. sequential SGT across the tumors included in this cost calculator are related to the number of genomic targets tested, the prevalence of a specific biomarker in a given tumor type, and individual test characteristics such as sensitivity and selectivity [ ]. There is variability in the adoption of biomarker testing in specific tumors across different countries. For example, between 2011 and 2016, the proportion of patients with adv. NonSq-NSCLC who received a molecular test ranged from 65% to 85% in Germany, Italy, and Spain [ ]. The requirements for acquisition of tissue samples in lung cancer also contribute to differences in testing rates. Compared with tissue biopsy genotyping (TBG), the liquid biopsy genotyping (LBG) method is less invasive, has faster turnaround times for results, and can assess tumor heterogeneity [ – ]. Recognizing these differences, integrating both tissue‑based and liquid biopsy pathways into the modelling framework would allow for a more realistic evaluation of how diagnostic modality, feasibility, and workforce requirements affect the overall cost, and clinical value of broader NGS adoption across different healthcare settings.
The assumed global prevalence of NGS testing for ESCAT I and II biomarkers in adv. STS, adv. GIST, and adv. HCC was less than 20% [ ]. Comparatively for adv. nonSq-NSCLC, the assumed global prevalence of NGS testing for ESCAT I and II biomarkers was 86.93% [ ]. A limitation of the findings in this study is that the results do not consider the adoption rate of biomarker testing in the calculation. The model assumes a population of 1000 receive SGT or NGS equally. The cost calculator provides timely insights on the most appropriate testing method for different tumor types and biomarkers, given that biomarker testing is expected to become more prevalent.
Although testing rates of newer predictive biomarkers are lower than those of established alterations such as EGFR, ALK, and ROS1, the adoption of NGS means more molecular anomalies are being included in routine tests [ – ]. A limitation of Stenzinger et al.’s 2023 cost calculator is the equal weighting of FP and FN in the model, which may not directly predict the impact of these test results on decisions made in clinical practice [ ]. Furthermore, removing TP and FP from the downstream testing pool may bias subsequent yield and cost estimates. A limitation of our cost calculator results from the ordering of sequential tests by prevalence. This approach is methodologically consistent, although it may not fully capture real‑world testing patterns. Creating simulations that follow commonly adopted clinical sequences can increase the validity of the SGT comparator. A key consideration for the clinical meaningfulness of these results is the variability of diagnostic performance for each biomarker class and tumor type.
This cost calculator compares the cost of targeted panel NGS vs sequential SGT, providing CCIP results in the form of deterministic model outputs, PSA with 95% confidence intervals, and one-way sensitivity analysis (OWSA). Including more currencies in a future version of the cost calculator can increase the relevance of the model in different geographic regions. The NGS technology evaluated in the cost calculator is targeted panel NGS. Investigation into the budget impact of different types of NGS is warranted.
Biomarker testing is becoming a prominent feature in guidelines for the diagnosis, treatment, and monitoring of malignancies. The ability of NGS to simultaneously detect multiple genomic alterations is a key advantage that may play a pivotal role in optimizing time to achieve diagnosis and initiation of treatment.

## Conclusion
This paper describes the development of a cost calculator for biomarker testing across 14 different types of tumors. The genomic testing cost calculator demonstrated the cost difference between NGS and SGT using the CCIP metric. This cost calculator is an updated version of the 20023 calculator developed by Stenzinger et al., which is referenced in the paper “Supporting Biomarker-Driven Therapies in Oncology: A Genomic Testing Cost Calculator”. The European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) devised a scale to rank the clinical actionability of specific genomic alterations. Tier 1 and tier 2 genomic alterations on the ESMO Clinical Actionability of Molecular Targets (ESCAT) scale have the highest levels of evidence to support the use of targeted therapies in patients whose tumor profiles align with actionable mutations in these categories. The updated calculator provides results in both EUR (€) and USD ($) and includes the full range of tier 1 and 2 alterations from the 2024 ESMO recommendations. At base case in EUR (€), CCIP was lower with NGS than sequential SGT for seven evaluated malignancies. In USD ($) CCIP was lower with NGS than sequential SGT for six tumor types. The findings of this study highlight the importance of gaining a comprehensive view of the budget impact of different genomic profiling methodologies. The aim of this paper was to validate the CCIP as a suitable metric to compare the cost of different genomic profiling tests across diverse malignancies. Insights in this area can complement decision-making processes that allow architects of regulatory policy in healthcare and diagnostics to choose methods that allow fast and accurate diagnoses. This may increase access to targeted treatments that improve patient outcomes.
