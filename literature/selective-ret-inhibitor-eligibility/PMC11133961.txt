## Abstract
A study to determine the impact of cyclosporine (Neoral), an inhibitor of P‐gp, on the pharmacokinetics of pralsetinib (trade name GAVRETO®) was conducted in 15 healthy adult volunteers. A single 200 mg dose of pralsetinib was administered orally alone and in combination with cyclosporine with a 9‐day washout between treatments. Co‐administration with cyclosporine resulted in a clinically relevant increase in pralsetinib maximum plasma concentration (C
max) and area under the plasma concentration–time curve extrapolated to infinity (AUC0–∞) with associated geometric mean ratios (GMRs) and 90% confidence intervals (CIs) of 148% (109, 201) and 181% (136, 241), respectively. These findings provide insight into concomitant dosing of pralsetinib with inhibitors of P‐gp given the increases in pralsetinib exposure observed when administered with cyclosporine. Based on these results, co‐administration of pralsetinib with P‐gp inhibitors is not recommended. In the event that co‐administration cannot be avoided, it is recommended that the dose of pralsetinib be reduced.

Genentech Inc On Day 1, all subjects were administered an oral dose of 200 mg of pralsetinib (2 × 100 mg capsules) following an overnight fast of at least 10 h. On Day 10, all subjects were administered an oral dose of 200 mg of pralsetinib (2 × 100 mg capsules) after a 600 mg dose of cyclosporine (6 × 100 mg capsules), following an overnight fast of at least 10 h. Each dose was administered with 240 mL of room temperature water. Plasma samples for PK analysis of pralsetinib were collected pre‐dose and 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, 36, 48, 72, 96, 144, and 192 h post‐dose for both treatments. Due to an increase in half‐life of pralsetinib that was previously observed in a study evaluating the effect of co‐administration of itraconazole, additional collections were drawn at 240 and 264 h post‐dose for the treatment of pralsetinib in combination with cyclosporine. Whole blood samples for cyclosporine were collected pre‐dose and 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h post‐dose following cyclosporine administration.

## 
bioanalytical methods
Concentration of pralsetinib in plasma was measured using a validated liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) assay over a concentration range of 2–2000 ng/mL. [13C]‐Pralsetinib‐d3 was used as the internal standard. The precision and accuracy of the assay was satisfactory throughout the study.
Concentration of cyclosporine in blood was measured using a validated LC–MS/MS assay over a concentration range of 5–2000 ng/mL. Cyclosporin‐d4 was used as the internal standard. The precision and accuracy of the assay was satisfactory throughout the study.

## 
PK and statistical methods
Standard noncompartmental analysis methods were used to determine PK parameters for pralsetinib and cyclosporine. PK analyses were conducted using Phoenix WinNonlin software (Certara, Version 8.1.1) and plasma or blood concentrations and PK parameters were summarized using descriptive statistics by treatment. Additionally, statistical analyses were performed using the mixed model approach to determine the geometric least squares means (GLSMs), ratio of geometric mean ratios (GMRs), and corresponding 90% confidence intervals (CIs) of pralsetinib AUC0–t
, AUC0–192, AUC0–∞, and C
max, with actual treatment as a fixed effect and subject as a random effect, for both periods to evaluate the effect of cyclosporine on pralsetinib. Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS Version 9.4. Sample size was based on precedent set by other PK studies and was not based on power calculations. Nevertheless, a sample size of 12 was determined sufficient to detect an expected 1.5‐fold increase in pralsetinib exposure when administered with cyclosporine with at least 80% power, assuming an intra‐subject variability of 39%.

## Safety analysis
Safety assessments occurred from screening through study completion. Safety and tolerability were assessed by the monitoring of adverse events, vital signs, clinical laboratory tests (hematology, biochemistry, and urinalysis) and 12‐lead electrocardiograms (ECGs). The AE severity grading scale for the NCI CTCAE (version 5.0) was used for assessing AE severity.

## Summary of adverse events
Pralsetinib was well tolerated in all subjects. No serious adverse events (SAEs), or AEs leading to study discontinuation were observed. Overall, nine treatment emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were reported by six subjects (40%); three TEAEs were reported following administration of pralsetinib alone and six TEAEs were reported following co‐administration of pralsetinib with cyclosporine. The majority of TEAEs were of Grade 1 severity. One TEAE of Grade 2 severity (ALT increased) in one subject occurred on Day 11 following pralsetinib, and cyclosporine administration was resolved by Day 15 without further action taken. One Grade 3 TEAE of neutropenia occurred in one subject on Day 11 following pralsetinib and cyclosporine administration was resolved by Day 13 without further action taken. The most common TEAE was constipation, experienced by three subjects (20.0%) following administration of pralsetinib alone. All other TEAEs were experienced by one subject each (6.7%) following co‐administration of pralsetinib with cyclosporine and included nausea, ALT increased, AST increased, neutropenia, palpitations, and flushing. All TEAEs were resolved by the end of the study. No unexpected changes or findings were noted from vital signs measurements or 12‐lead ECGs for this study.

## DISCUSSION
Assessment of the P‐gp mediated DDI liability for pralsetinib was conducted based on a number of considerations, including determination of the magnitude of the effect, the resulting clinical relevance, and the appropriate dosing strategy to mitigate any potential effect. The study reported herein had an objective of evaluating the impact of P‐gp inhibition while limiting the contribution of other factors such as CYP3A4 inhibition.
Cyclosporine is an immunosuppressant used to prevent the rejection of transplanted kidneys, hearts, and livers and to treat certain inflammatory diseases. It is a substrate and weak inhibitor of CYP3A4 and a potent inhibitor of P‐gp and other drug transporters, including BCRP. The maximum whole blood concentration of cyclosporine (C
max ~ 2000 ng/mL) achieved following a single 600 mg dose in previously conducted clinical studies , was above the average cyclosporine concentration (~1000 ng/mL) required to inhibit P‐gp, based on the in vitro efflux ratio half‐maximal inhibitory concentration. , Furthermore, in previously conducted clinical DDI studies with known P‐gp and CYP3A4 substrates such as ticagrelor, velpatasvir, and naldemedine, a single 600 mg dose of cyclosporine was shown to exert a substantial P‐gp inhibitiory effect, with AUC ratios of 2.8‐, 2.0‐, and 1.8‐fold, respectively. , , Plasma half‐life of ticagrelor, velpatasvir, and naldemedine was not affected by cyclosporine administration, suggesting that the increased exposure was potentially due to increased P‐gp‐mediated intestinal absorption and gut CYP3A4 inhibition. As cyclosporine has a narrow therapeutic range and can cause systemic hypertension and nephrotoxicity, the risk of which increases with increasing dose and duration of cyclosporine therapy, a single dose of 600 mg was selected for the present study. When administered in combination with pralsetinib in this study, the observed geometric mean C
max for cyclosporine of 2160 ng/mL was sufficient to evaluate the effects of P‐gp inhibition on pralsetinib.
In vitro, pralsetinib is primarily metabolized by CYP3A4 and transporter studies have shown it to be a potential substrate of P‐gp and BCRP. However, inhibition of BCRP is not predicted to have an appreciable impact on the PK of pralsetinib. In BCRP‐MDCK cells, the strong BCRP inhibitor Ko143 decreased the efflux ratio of pralsetinib by 55.4%. While this is marginally above the 50% threshold for a positive effect, in C2BBe1 cells, no change in the pralsetinib efflux ratio was observed in the presence of either Ko143 or fungal toxin fumitremorgin C (a potent and specific inhibitor of BCRP). In contrast, efflux ratios of pralsetinib were inhibited completely in the presence of two P‐gp inhibitors, cyclosporine, or valspodar in MDR1‐MDCK and C2BBe1 cells. In addition, the results from C2BBe1 cell line indicated that P‐gp is the major transporter responsible for the efflux of pralsetinib when both endogenous BCRP and P‐gp are co‐expressed.
Increased exposure of pralsetinib observed when co‐administered with itraconazole in a previously conducted DDI study suggested the role of P‐gp and/or CYP3A4 in disposition of pralsetinib. In addition to its P‐gp inhibitory properties, cyclosporine is a weak to moderate inhibitor of CYP3A4 and BCRP. However, itraconazole demonstrates substantially stronger CYP3A4 inhibition when compared with cyclosporine (the midazolam AUC ratio in combination with itraconazole vs. cyclosporine is 10.8‐fold vs. 1.4‐fold, respectively); hence the contribution of P‐gp inhibition to the increased pralsetinib exposure observed in the previously conducted itraconazole DDI study can be better assessed using cyclosporine as the perpetrator of P‐gp inhibition.
The approved dose of pralsetinib is 400 mg q.d. based on the clinical experience to date, where it exhibits acceptable tolerability in patients following multiple dose administration. For the present study in healthy volunteers, the selected dose of 200 mg is within the range of pralsetinib doses (30–600 mg q.d.) evaluated in the clinical development program where the maximum tolerated dose in patients was found to be 400 mg q.d. When administered to healthy subjects in combination with itraconazole, a single 200 mg dose of pralsetinib was well tolerated with no SAEs observed despite a 251% increase in exposure compared with pralsetinib administered alone. A dose lower than the maximally tolerated dose was selected to be administered in combination with cyclosporine in an effort to accommodate potentially higher pralsetinib exposure due to P‐gp inhibition. Assessment of a DDI with a molecule as a victim can be evaluated at a lower dose, as it will represent a worst case situation for a DDI based on competitive inhibition. Accordingly, the selection of a 200 mg dose level is considered appropriate and the results are translatable to the higher approved dose level of 400 mg, based on the dose proportionality observed previously across the range of doses from 200 to 400 mg following single‐dose administration of pralsetinib in healthy volunteers.
The GMRs corresponding to AUC0–∞, and C
max showed that systemic exposure to pralsetinib increased by 81%, and 48%, respectively, when pralsetinib was co‐administered with cyclosporine compared with pralsetinib administered alone. These results support that co‐administration of a P‐gp inhibitor will contribute to higher pralsetinib exposures that are clinically relevant based on the exposure–safety relationship of pralsetinib and therefore, the use of alternative treatments lacking P‐gp inhibitory activity is recommended. Comparatively, in the previously conducted clinical study with itraconazole, GMRs corresponding to AUC0–∞ and C
max demonstrated that systemic exposure to pralsetinib increased by 251% and 84%, respectively, when pralsetinib was co‐administered with itraconazole. Direct in vitro comparisons of P‐gp inhibition between itraconazole and cyclosporine suggest a similar or slightly higher potency for itraconazole, and the magnitude of P‐gp inhibition is expected to be similar between the two inhibitors. In addition to its P‐gp inhibitory properties, itraconazole is a strong inhibitor of CYP3A4 and the difference in magnitude of the increased pralsetinib exposure with co‐administration of itraconazole relative to co‐administration of cyclosporine could be attributed to CYP3A4‐mediated effects. This is in line with the in vitro studies which showed that pralsetinib is primarily metabolized by CYP3A4. However, considering the low recovery of oxidative metabolites detected in the previously conducted human mass balance study, the differences in the magnitude of increases in exposure observed cannot be explained based on the collective results of the mass balance, cyclosporine DDI, and itraconazole DDI studies alone. Cumulative recovery of drug‐related radioactivity in the mass balance study accounted for 78.6% of the administered dose and a complete accounting of the metabolic profile, including the extent of CYP3A4 metabolism, may be limited.
Further distinguishing the impact of itraconazole versus cyclosporine, co‐administration of itraconazole in the previously conducted DDI study was found to increase the geometric mean terminal elimination half‐life (t
1/2) of pralsetinib by 94% (28.3 h vs. 14.6 h) when compared with pralsetinib administered alone. In contrast, co‐administration of cyclosporine in this study did not appear to impact pralsetinib t
1/2, which was observed to be approximately 16 h regardless of treatment. Considering the role P‐gp plays in limiting drug absorption, , administration of a P‐gp inhibitor could impact bioavailability while having a lesser impact on clearance when compared with an inhibitor of CYP3A4. The negligible effect of cyclosporine relative to itraconazole on t
1/2 and therefore clearance, suggests that the increased exposure of pralsetinib when administered with cyclosporine can be predominantly attributed to increased bioavailability due to P‐gp inhibition alone.
Outcomes from the present study were used to inform a PBPK model that described the magnitude of the DDI from interaction with P‐gp and CYP3A4 inhibitors. Collectively, based on these predictions, the itraconazole and cyclosporine clinical DDI studies, and the exposure–response relationship of pralsetinib, the need for dose modifications for pralsetinib when co‐administered with moderate to strong CYP3A4 inhibitors/inducers, P‐gp inhibitors, and dual inhibitors of P‐gp and CYP3A4 will be assessed.

## CONCLUSIONS
The results of the study reported herein together with a previous study conducted with itraconazole in healthy subjects determined that co‐administration with known inhibitors of P‐gp resulted in significant and clinically relevant increases in pralsetinib exposure. These results support recommendations that the use of concomitant treatments known to inhibit P‐gp should be avoided if possible. If alternative treatments are not available, adjusting the pralsetinib dose from the approved starting dose should be considered.
