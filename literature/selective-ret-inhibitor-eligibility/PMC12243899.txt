In addition to chromosomal rearrangements, FTs can also form through transcriptional and post-transcriptional mechanisms at the RNA level, including alternative splicing and trans-splicing [ , ].
There are several distinct types of FTs, each with unique structural characteristics and formation mechanisms. The most commonly studied are linear FTs, which typically result from chromosomal rearrangements that generate a continuous, linear RNA molecule from segments of separate genes [ ]. These linear FTs often produce chimeric proteins that drive tumorigenesis. Chimeric proteins resulting from gene fusions play a critical role in tumorigenesis by aberrantly activating key signalling pathways. For instance, the PPP1R1B-STARD3 fusion protein has been shown to promote gastric cancer progression via activation of the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway [ ]. Similarly, the ETV6-NTRK3 fusion drives oncogenesis by engaging IGF1R, which activates the RAS-RAF-MAPK and PI3K pathways, particularly in congenital fibrosarcoma and secretory breast carcinoma [ ].
In oncology, linear and circular FTs serve as biomarkers in diagnosis, prognosis, and targeted therapy. For instance, detecting BCR-ABL1 fusion transcripts is essential for diagnosis and follow-up of chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML) [ , ]. In solid tumours, linear fusions such as TMPRSS2-ERG in prostate cancer are being investigated as biomarkers and therapeutic targets [ ]. The high prevalence of FTs has positioned them as promising therapeutic targets in the era of precision medicine. Several drugs target fusion proteins regardless of tumour type. Kinase inhibitors are particularly effective in treating tumours harbouring kinase fusions. Kinase fusions refer to gene fusions involving kinase domains, where the resulting chimeric proteins often exhibit constitutive kinase activity that drives oncogenic signalling, making them ideal therapeutic targets for kinase inhibitors. For example, in 2018, Larotrectinib (Vitrakvi) was approved for treating solid tumours with NTRK fusions. In early 2020, FGFR2 fusions were targeted for cholangiocarcinoma treatment with Pemigatinib (Pemazyre), approved by the FDA. Later in 2020, Pralsetinib (Gavreto) was approved for RET fusion-positive non-small cell lung cancer [ ].
In HCC, early investigations into FTs primarily utilized targeted RT-qPCR panels. For instance, nine candidate fusions were screened in serum samples from 61 HCC patients, and MAN2A1–FER was detected in 100% of cases, and SLC45A2–AMACR, CCNH – C5orf30, ZMPSTE24–ZMYM4, and PTEN – NOLC1 identified in over 55% [ ]. A study of two HBV-associated HCC samples identified 2,354 unique fusion transcripts, yet only 43 (1.8%) were recurrent across four independent cohorts, highlighting the remarkable heterogeneity of HBV-associated FTs and demonstrating that they were selectively expressed in tumour tissues but absent in matched normal liver samples, underscoring the critical role of viral integration in FT generation [ ]. DNAJB1-PRKACA fusion transcripts have been identified in all fibrolamellar carcinoma (FLC) samples, a rare HCC subtype, but have never been observed in adjacent normal liver samples [ ]. Currently, no fusion transcripts are recognized as biomarkers in HCC, highlighting a significant gap in research that warrants further exploration. These insights underscore the need for further investigation into the biological relevance, recurrence patterns, and detectability of FTs in HCC. However, low recurrence rates, limited functional follow-up, and a lack of standardized, cross-platform pipelines remain major limitations to clinical translation.
Although fusion transcripts are commonly observed in cancer cells, they can also be found at low levels in normal cells, where they may play regulatory roles in various biological processes beyond pathological conditions [ ]. For instance, a study [ ] revealed that FTs are present in the normal human cortex and their prevalence increases with age, suggesting a role in transcriptomic diversity and possibly in the ageing process of the brain. Furthermore, research has demonstrated that certain FTs, such as the JAZF1-JJAZ1 fusion, occur in normal endometrial stromal cells through trans-splicing mechanisms, indicating that not all fusion events are products of chromosomal rearrangements associated with malignancies [ ]. These findings underscore the importance of distinguishing between pathological and physiological FTs, emphasize the critical need to identify ‘tumor-specific’ FTs and highlight their potential as biomarkers in HCC.
In this study, we aimed to characterize the landscape of tumour-specific fusion transcripts in HCC and evaluate their potential as biomarkers. First, we comprehensively analyse a meta cohort of eight independent RNA-Seq datasets (n:328) to establish a fusion transcript profile in HCC. Then, we identified tumour-specific fusion transcripts and examined their potential influence on the expression of their 5’/3’ partner genes. Finally, we validated the expression levels of fusion partner genes in HCC tumours and normal samples using in silico (n:436) and wet lab (n:42) patient cohorts.

## Materials and methods

## Dataset selection and preparation
We searched publicly available RNA-seq datasets in GEO [ ], EGA [ ], ENA [ ], ArrayExpress [ ], and SRA [ ]. The search was conducted using the keywords ‘HCC’, ‘hepatocellular carcinoma’, ‘RNA-seq’, ‘next-generation sequencing’, and ‘transcriptome’. We included human FFPE and primary tissue samples while excluding cell lines and non-human datasets. Our meta cohort comprised 328 samples from eight independent RNA-Seq studies spanning 2011–2020, incorporating HCC tumours, normal adjacent liver tissues (NAT), and associated clinical data. Only studies with raw sequencing reads were considered. The final dataset included: GSE77509 (n:40), GSE144269 (n:140), GSE124535 (n:70), GSE55758 (n:16), GSE97214 (n:18), GSE104310 (n:16), GSE33294 (n:6), and GSE63863 (n:22) (Supplementary Table S1). TCGA-LIHC was added for in silico validation, alongside wet lab validation cohorts ( ).
 
 

## Identification and analysis of fusion transcripts in meta cohort samples
RNA-seq datasets from the eight independent studies (meta cohort) were analysed separately. For each study, read quality was assessed using FASTQC [ ], and low-quality reads were removed with Trimmomatic [ ]. Processed reads were then used for fusion transcript analysis with STAR-Fusion (Release v1.9.0) using default parameters. The STAR-Fusion analysis algorithm allowed us to effectively eliminate false positives. It automatically classifies fusion events based on COSMIC, TCGA, and other sources by using the CTAT genome library. Labels such as ‘known fusion’ or ‘paralog artifact’ help to filter out false positives [ ]. STAR-Fusion reports a quantitative measure of FT expression – FFPM (Fusion Fragments Per Million). During the analyses, the FFPM value was used as a strengthening parameter and a filter to account for the heterogeneity of the datasets, and no FT with an FFPM value below 0.1 was included in the lists.
Identified putative fusion transcripts were further analysed in ChimerDB v4.0 [ ], a database containing 67,610 fusion entries collected from multiple sources. This resource provides literature-based evidence and results from three fusion detection algorithms, including STAR-Fusion, applied to ~ 11,000 TCGA samples across 33 cancer types. Using ChimerDB v4.0, we evaluated whether the detected fusion transcripts had been previously reported in HCC or other cancers and identified potential novel fusion events. Furthermore, fusion transcripts of interest were cross-validated using FusionGDB, FusionHub, COSMIC, AtlasGeneticsOncology, and the Tumor Fusion Gene Data Portal.
Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between HCC tumour and NAT samples were identified for each independent study using count tables obtained after STAR alignment. Following alignment with STAR, count tables were generated using the built-in feature of STAR (STAR – quantMode GeneCounts), which directly quantifies reads mapped to annotated genes based on the provided GTF annotation file. Regarding the statistical analysis of expression levels of fusion partner genes, we used the edgeR package (version 4.0.16), where raw count data were normalized using the TMM (Trimmed Mean of M-values) method. Differential expression analysis was conducted with the exactTest function, and resulting p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) method implemented within edgeR. To estimate variability, common and tagwise dispersions were calculated using the estimateCommonDisp and estimateTagwiseDisp functions, respectively. Subsequently, exact tests were performed with the exactTest function to identify differentially expressed genes between tumour and normal samples, with statistical significance set at FDR ≤ 0.05.
To integrate results across multiple datasets, a meta-analysis approach was employed using the metaRNASeq package (version 1.0.7) in R. Fisher’s method was used to combine p-values from individual studies via the Fishercomb function, while the inverse normal method, implemented with the invnorm function, adjusted for differences in sample sizes across datasets.

## Expression analysis of fusion partner genes in the in silico validation cohort
Expression of the 5’and 3’ partners of tumour-specific fusion transcripts in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) dataset was analysed as an in silico validation cohort using UCSC Xena Browser [ ], UALCAN [ ], and cBioPortal [ ], interactive web resources that enable in-depth exploration of TCGA data, including gene expression and clinical correlations ( ).
The HCC cohorts in UALCAN include 371 primary HCC and 50 NAT samples (n:421), while the UCSC Xena Browser dataset contains 377 primary HCC and 59 NAT tissue samples (n:436). Genes of interest were analysed using these platforms, with raw data downloaded and visualizations generated in the R environment.

## Analysis of gene expression levels in patient cohorts

## Patient cohorts
The expression levels of fusion partner genes were examined in a total of 42 tumour and NAT samples from HCC patients. Expression levels of the six most common tumour-specific fusion partner genes were validated in a fresh frozen tissue cohort of 10 Tumour/NAT pairs (n:20). Among the validated FTs, expression of SERPINA1 and H19 was further analysed in another cohort of 11 Tumour/NAT pairs of formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues (n:22) in HCC ( ). The study enrolled adult participants treated at İnönü University Hospital and provided broad written informed consent. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of İzmir Biomedicine and Genome Center (#IBG-GOEK-2024–006). Participants’ data were anonymized as approved by the ethics committee, and anonymization did not distort the scholarly meaning of the study.

## RNA isolation
Total RNA extraction from samples constituting the patient cohort was performed using NucleoZOL reagent (Macherey-Nagel) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Before RNA isolation, FFPE samples were conducted using the deparaffinization method. Fresh frozen tissues stored in liquid nitrogen were homogenized in nucleoZOL. RNA was eluted in DNase/RNase-free ultrapure water, and its concentration was measured using a NanoDrop.

## Quantitative RT-PCR
Expression levels of fusion partner genes were quantified using primers specifically designed for the genes of interest. Primer design was performed using the NCBI Primer Designing Tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/), and their specificity was verified using UCSC In-Silico PCR [ ]. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S2. Briefly, total RNAs were transcribed into cDNA using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific™). qPCR was performed using Luminaris Color HiGreen High ROX qPCR Master Mix (Thermo Scientific™) with the Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR Systems. ACTB or RPL41 was used as an internal control. The 2-ΔCt method was used to indicate the relative expression level of corresponding genes. At least three technical replicates were performed for RT-qPCR experiments.

## Statistical analysis
‘Wilcoxon Signed Rank Test’ was performed in paired tissues using the GraphPad Prism version 8, GraphPad Software, San Diego, California USA, https://www.graphpad.com. p ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance values of the statistical analysis were represented as follows: *p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001, ****p ≤ 0.0001.

## Results

## Fusion transcripts are found more frequently in tumor tissues compared to NAT tissues in HCC patients
To identify HCC-related fusion transcript (FT) lists, we analysed RNA-Seq data from eight independent HCC cohorts using the STAR-Fusion algorithm, as described in detail in the material and methods.
We detected 4268 FTs in HCC and 2762 FTs in paired NAT samples. Across the entire cohort, we identified 6737 distinct FTs. Among these FTs that are expressed in either tumour or NAT tissues, 124 of them exist at least five times (recurrent FTs). Notably, 81 of these recurrent FTs were found exclusively in tumour samples (in this assessment, the presence or absence of these FTs in normal tissues (NATs) was not considered) ( ).
 

## Some novel fusion transcripts are present only in the tumor tissues of HCC patients
Upon analysing the FTs at least five times in tumour samples but absent in paired NAT samples, we identified a total of 15 FTs ( ). We refer to these as ‘tumor-specific fusion transcripts’. Among these, SERPINA1-H19 stands out as the most prevalent fusion transcript in tumours, highlighting its potential for hepatocarcinogenesis.
Then, we examined the functional relevance of these FTs in existing fusion-related databases and investigated whether they had been previously linked with cancer as described in the material and methods section. Our analysis revealed that none of these FTs had been previously classified as cancer-related. On the other hand, we observed that partner genes of the tumour-specific FTs frequently functioned as 5’ or 3’ fusion partners in various cancers, including HCC.

## Partner genes of tumor-specific fusion transcripts are primarily protein-coding genes
Detailed analysis of tumour-specific partner genes revealed that ALB, located on chromosome 4, forms the highest number of distinct fusion events, followed by H19 and IGF2, both of which are located on chromosome 11 and frequently partner with different genes. Additionally, chromosomes 19, 11, and 1 harboured the most recurrent fusion partner genes ( ).
 
Among the identified fusion partners, 72% were protein-coding genes, including ADK, ALB, APOA2, APOC2, APOC4-APOC2, C3, GLUL, IGF2, IGH, MAPK8, MRNIP, MT2A, SERPINA1; 17% were classified as noncoding RNAs, such as AC068756.1, AC132217.1 and H19, and 11% were pseudogenes, represented by AC108690.1 and AL669831.3 ( ). Noticeably, ALB, APOA2, and H19 can be either 3’ or 5’ partners in tumour-specific FTs. H19 and ALB emerged as the most frequently occurring fusion partners (33.33 % frequency in tumour-specific FT list), appearing at either 5’or 3’ positions within fusion transcripts, followed by IGF2 and APOA2 (13.33 % frequency in tumour-specific FT list) ( ).

## Expression of 5’and 3’ fusion partner genes decreased in tumor samples compared to adjacent normal samples
A detailed analysis revealed that certain fusion genes, involving either the 5’or 3’ partner genes, were predominantly detected in tumour samples but were significantly rarer or absent in adjacent normal tissues ( ).
Overall, most of the 5’and 3’ fusion partners exhibited a notable decrease in expression in tumour samples compared to adjacent normal tissues, further supporting the potential role in HCC pathogenesis ( ).
 
We analysed the expression patterns of tumour-specific partner genes across eight different datasets. While not all datasets exhibited regulation in the same direction, the overall trend indicated a significant decrease in the HCC tumours, as illustrated by the red dots in . To gain a comprehensive understanding, we conducted a meta-analysis, which revealed that approximately 50% of the expression changes in these genes were statistically significant across both meta-analysis methods used (fishcomb and invnormcomb). In both, tumour-specific partners showed similar significance values. Therefore, only Fisher’s p-values are reported in .

## In silico validation confirms the downregulation of tumor-specific partner genes
Among the tumour-specific 5’and 3’ fusion partner genes, 11 out of 18 were detected in the TCGA LIHC dataset using the UCSC Xena ( , n:436) and UALCAN (Supplementary Figure S1, n:421) tools. This analysis further revealed that six of these genes, ALB, APOA2, H19, IGF2, MT2A, and SERPINA1, exhibited significant and consistent expression changes across both platforms.
 

## Tumor-specific key partner genes were validated in vitro in HCC patients
The six tumour-specific key partner genes identified in the in silico validation cohort were further validated in vitro. To assess their expression levels, a wet lab validation cohort was analysed using specifically designed primers and RT-qPCR. All six genes exhibited a significant decrease in expression in HCC tumour samples compared to adjacent normal liver tissues ( ).
 

## SERPINA1 and H19 form the most prominent tumor-specific fusion and their fusion alters expression potentially
SERPINA1-H19 was identified as the most frequent tumour-specific fusion transcript in our analysis ( ). Both SERPINA1 and H19 were downregulated in HCC tumours compared to NAT or normal liver tissues across multiple datasets, including our meta cohort, the TCGA in silico validation cohort, and fresh frozen tissue samples (wet lab validation cohort I) (p ≤ 0.05). Given this consistent downregulation, we conducted further studies for SERPINA1 and H19.
 
STAR-Fusion analyses identified the breakpoint regions of the 3’ and 5’ partner genes, allowing us to determine potential sequences of different fusion transcript variants. We identified 16 distinct variants, along with their corresponding junction regions ( , Supplementary Table S3). Two primary structural configurations of SERPINA1-H19 were revealed, each characterized by distinct binding sites. Both SERPINA1 and H19 contain five exons. In both fusion transcript types, SERPINA1 contributes exon 1 and part of exon 2 at the 5’ region, while the 3’ region incorporates H19, which may either contain part of exon 1 along with exons 2, 3, 4, and 5 or solely exon 5 ( ).
To assess the protein-coding potential of SERPINA1-H19, we performed CPAT (Coding Potential Assessment Tool) analysis. Using the recommended threshold of 0.36 [ ], we found that SERPINA1-H19 exhibits low protein-coding potential, indicating a likely non-coding regulatory role ( ). However, in some cases, the fusion transcript displayed a higher probability of translation into a protein or chimeric product, suggesting potential alternative functional roles.
To further validate SERPINA1 and H19 expression, we analysed an independent FFPE tissue cohort (wet lab validation cohort II). Expression analysis confirmed significant downregulation of both genes in HCC tumour tissues compared to normal adjacent tissues (n:22, p ≤ 0.001 for SERPINA1 and p ≤ 0.05 for H19; ). Analysis revealed that TCGA LIHC tumours with low SERPINA1 expression also exhibited significantly lower H19 expression (p ≤ 0.01), suggesting a potential co-regulatory mechanism between these genes ( , n:363).
Furthermore, we investigated the association of SERPINA1 and H19 expression with clinicopathologic parameters, including tumour stages and grades. A marked and significant decrease in SERPINA1 mRNA levels was observed from normal liver tissue to early-stage and low-grade HCC ( ). Notably, low SERPINA1 expression correlated with progressive increases in tumour stage and grade (p ≤ 0.05 for Normal vs. all, Stage I vs. Stage II, Stage I vs. Stage III, Grade I vs. Grade IV, Grade II vs. Grade III, Grade III vs. Grade IV Grade II vs. Grade IV; p ≤ 0.05). Additionally, tumours with low SERPINA1 expression exhibited significantly higher microsatellite instability (MSI) (Supplementary Figure S2A), tumour break load (Supplementary Figure S2B), Regnum hypoxia scores (Supplementary Figure S2C), and fraction genome altered (Supplementary Figure S2D) – factors that are associated with an aggressive phenotype.

## Discussion
Fusion transcripts (FTs) have emerged as critical players in oncogenesis, with growing evidence highlighting their roles in tumour progression, biomarker potential, and therapeutic targeting. Despite their well-documented significance in haematological malignancies and certain solid tumours, their involvement in HCC is relatively underexplored [ , ]. HCC remains a highly challenging disease with a complex aetiology and limited treatment options. The integration of multi-omics technologies has transformed our understanding of HCC, enabling the identification of distinct molecular signatures that drive tumour biology and serve as potential biomarkers for precision oncology [ ]. In this context, characterizing FTs in HCC could provide critical insights into its molecular pathogenesis and facilitate the discovery of novel biomarkers for improved diagnosis and targeted therapies. Despite the growing interest in FTs, studies focusing on HCC-related fusion events remain limited. Most existing research has concentrated on individual FTs [ , ]. To date, only one comprehensive study has integrated multiple human datasets, but it is constrained by a relatively small sample size (~60 HCC and 15 normal tissue samples [ ]). Additionally, some studies have investigated FTs in the serum of HCC patients, highlighting their potential detectability in liquid biopsies [ , ].
In this study, we employed a robust fusion transcript identification approach that enhanced accuracy and ensured clinical relevance by analysing a large meta cohort (n:328) ( ). Unlike previous studies, we focused specifically on tumour-specific fusion transcripts, presenting a comprehensive analysis of the FT landscape in HCC. Our approach not only identified 15 novel tumour-specific FTs ( ) but also explored their impact on fusion partner gene expression ( ). One of the reasons we describe our method as a robust fusion transcript identification approach is our choice to use the STAR-Fusion algorithm. STAR-Fusion achieved the highest accuracy rankings among the tested methods. It consistently demonstrates superior performance across multiple evaluation metrics, including precision, recall, runtime efficiency, and robustness to parameter thresholds. While many tools require high minimum support to maintain accuracy, STAR-Fusion achieves peak performance with just three supporting RNA-Seq fragments and maintains reliable precision and recall across a range of thresholds. In contrast, tools like TopHat-Fusion show a significant drop in accuracy when thresholds are lowered due to increased false positives, limiting their usability in low-expression scenarios [ ]. Its superior performance is attributed in part to its robustness across a range of minimum evidence thresholds, maintaining high precision and recall even at low fragment counts, which exhibit a sharp decline in accuracy below five supporting fragments. Moreover, although Arriba’s ‘high-confidence’ prediction mode increases precision, it does so at the cost of sensitivity, reducing its overall accuracy compared to STAR-Fusion [ ].
To further validate our findings, we utilized an in silico validation cohort consisting of TCGA LIHC samples (n:436) to examine tissue-specific partner genes. Additionally, two wet lab validation cohorts (n:42) were used for in vitro validation ( ). These extensive datasets strengthen the robustness and reliability of our findings, providing the most comprehensive fusion FT profile in HCC.
We observed that most of the 5’and 3’ fusion partner genes were significantly downregulated in our meta cohort ( , n:328). This suggests that FTs may contribute through a ‘loss-of-function’ mechanism, disrupting essential cellular processes. Notably, several key partner genes – ALB, APOA2, IGF2, H19, MT2A, and SERPINA1— are known regulators of HCC pathophysiology. Importantly, this downregulation pattern was further validated in silico and in vitro ( , n:436; Supplementary Figure S1, n:421; , n:42). Albumin (ALB) is a key regulator of hepatic metabolism, and its decreased expression has been associated with poor prognosis and liver dysfunction in HCC patients. Apolipoprotein A2 (APOA2), a liver-derived protein involved in lipid metabolism and a component of high-density lipoprotein (HDL), has been identified as a potential prognostic biomarker in HCC [ ]. Similarly, Insulin-like Growth Factor 2 (IGF2), a critical growth factor regulating cell proliferation, survival, and differentiation, has been shown to drive tumour growth and invasion in HCC when its expression is dysregulated [ , ]. Metallothionein 2A (MT2A) plays an essential role in metal ion homoeostasis, detoxification, and oxidative stress protection. Previous studies have reported that metallothionein (MT) expression is significantly reduced in HCC compared to para-neoplastic and normal tissues [ ]. Additionally, MT2A dysregulation has been implicated in HCC and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) along with ALB and APOA2 [ ]. Our study suggested that decreased expression of these partner genes during hepatocarcinogenesis may result from the formation of tumour-specific fusion transcripts. These findings have provided important insights into the potential roles of FT in HCC and established a fundamental basis for future research studies ( ). In particular, SERPINA1-H19 emerged as the most recurrent fusion transcript, highlighting the need for further investigation ( ).
SERPINA1 encodes Alpha1-antitrypsin (A1AT), a serine protease inhibitor primarily produced in hepatocytes. It is crucial in immune regulation, protease inhibition, and cell homoeostasis [ , ]. SERPINA1 shows potential as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker, with significant correlation with immune checkpoint genes, immune infiltration, and tumour progression in HCC. Low expression of SERPINA1 has been linked to poor differentiation, vascular invasion, and advanced TNM stage in HCC [ ]. Consistently, our findings indicate that tumours with low SERPINA1 expression exhibit significantly higher MSI (Supplementary Figure S2A), tumour break load (Supplementary Figure S2B), Regnum hypoxia scores (Supplementary Figure S2C) and fraction genome altered (Supplementary Figure S2D), further supporting its role in genomic stability and tumour aggressiveness. Notably, the negative correlation of SERPINA1 expression and MSI, hypoxia scores, and genomic instability suggests that its low expression may be linked to a more immunogenic tumour microenvironment, positioning SERPINA1 as a potential marker for predicting immunotherapy response.
Further analysis confirmed a consistent downregulation of both SERPINA1 and H19 in HCC tumours ( , n:22). Additionally, a strong correlation was observed between SERPINA1 and H19 expression, as tumours with low SERPINA1 levels also exhibited significantly lower H19 expression ( , p ≤ 0.01, n:363), suggesting a potential co-regulatory mechanism between the two genes.
H19 is a long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) first identified as an imprinted gene with maternal expression. It is highly expressed during foetal development but is downregulated in most adult tissues, except for muscle. H19 plays context-dependent roles in cancer functioning, either as a tumour suppressor or an oncogene, depending on the cancer type, cellular context, and regulatory interactions [ , ]. Some studies suggest that H19 acts as a tumour suppressor by inhibiting cell proliferation and promoting apoptosis. Notably, H19 knockdown in certain HCC cell lines resulted in increased tumour growth, implying a loss of tumour-suppressive activity [ ]. Moreover, H19 has been implicated in immune regulation within the tumour microenvironment (TME), similar to SERPINA1 [ ].
Our fusion analysis identified 16 distinct variants of the SERPINA1-H19 fusion transcript ( , Supplementary Table S3). Two primary fusion transcript types were identified, where SERPINA1 retains exon 1 and part of exon 2, while H19 contributes either exon 5 or exons 2, 3, 4, and 5 ( ). The SERPINA1 gene consists of three non-coding parts in exon 1 (1A, 1B, and 1C) and four coding exons (2–5). Exon 2 of SERPINA1 is the beginning of the coding region, meaning the loss of exons 3–5 could result in a truncated or non-functional protein or significantly reduced protein production. Additionally, the presence of a premature stop codon might trigger nonsense-mediated decay (NMD), leading to mRNA degradation and preventing the formation of a functional enzymatic region [ ]. These findings suggest that the fusion transcript variants depicted in may significantly alter SERPINA1 transcription and disrupt functional alpha-1 antitrypsin (AAT) protein production.
The loss of H19 exon 1 is a common feature in both types of fusion transcripts ( ), which likely affects H19’s regulatory elements by disrupting transcription factor binding sites [ ]. As a long non-coding RNA, H19 functions as a natural sponge for key microRNAs, including the let-7 family [ ]. Notably, H19 exon 1 encodes miR-675 [ , ], which has been shown to modulate miR-675 levels in response to cellular stressors, such as hypoxia and oncogenic signalling, thereby influencing tumour progression and metastasis [ ]. miR-675 plays a crucial role in regulating tumour suppressors and oncogenes, including RB1 and TP53, and is involved in cell proliferation, apoptosis, and differentiation [ , ].
Thus, fusion-driven exon loss in both SERPINA1 and H19 transcripts may disrupt their biological functions or lead to the formation of novel functional products, thereby influencing hepatocarcinogenesis. Furthermore, if non-functional chimeric proteins derived from FTs are produced in the extranuclear compartment, they may undergo polyubiquitination or misfolding in the endoplasmic reticulum, potentially leading to aggregate formation. Our data reveal a gradual decrease in SERPINA1 and H19 expression from normal to tumour tissues, as well as a progressive decline from early-stage to advanced HCC, highlighting their potential roles in HCC development, progression, and prognosis ( ). In support of this, mutations causing abnormal SERPINA1 conformation have been reported to induce intrahepatic accumulation, contributing to metabolic liver diseases [ ]. These studies show the need for further studies to understand the effects of SERPINA1-H19 fusions on the structure and functions of the corresponding protein and/or noncoding RNA.
Addressing the following limitations is crucial for advancing the translational potential of FTs discovered in this study as biomarkers and/or therapeutic targets in HCC. The main limitation is the heterogeneous nature of the publicly available RNA-seq datasets used in the analysis. These datasets were generated using different platforms, protocols, and sequencing depths and they were not originally designed for the discovery of FTs. To address this limitation, the FTs identified in this study could be further analysed in a well-controlled cohort using standardized protocols and deeper sequencing coverage. Such follow-up investigations would allow for more reliable detection, validation, and functional characterization of candidate fusions under uniform experimental conditions. Moreover, the fusion profiles in our study were derived from tumour tissues and paired adjacent non-tumour (NAT) samples; however, including healthy liver tissues in future analyses could provide a more refined understanding of fusion specificity and its association with disease progression. Furthermore, due to the nature of FTs, where partner genes may be joined at various breakpoints, further experimental validation is needed to confirm the precise fusion junctions and their expressed protein products.

## Conclusion
This study suggests that FTs may lead to the functional loss of the partner genes, thereby playing a role in hepatocarcinogenesis and correlating with a worse prognosis in HCC. This study provides a novel perspective on the fusion transcript landscape in HCC, identifying tumour-specific FTs. SERPINA1-H19 emerged as a key FT candidate for further investigation with consistent downregulation of partner genes and association with HCC progression. Future studies should focus on understanding the mechanistic roles of these fusion transcripts and evaluating their potential as biomarkers or therapeutic targets in HCC.
