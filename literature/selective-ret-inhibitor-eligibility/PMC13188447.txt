CDKs comprise a family of serine/threonine kinases (CDK1–20) characterised by a bilobed structure: an amino-terminal N-lobe that mediates ATP binding and substrate recognition, and a carboxy-terminal C-lobe containing the phosphorylation-sensitive T-loop essential for catalytic activity [ , ]. Upon cyclin engagement at the PSTAIRE motif, CDKs undergo structural reorientation that exposes the T-loop threonine, allowing its phosphorylation by CDK-activating kinase (CAK) [ ]. Once activated, CDK–cyclin complexes interact with their substrates to regulate key cellular processes, including (i) cell cycle progression (CDK1–6), (ii) gene transcription (CDK7–13, CDK19–20), and (iii) both (CDK7, 10, 11) [ ].
Within this network, the CDK4/6–cyclin D complex functions as the principal conduit linking extracellular mitogenic cues to the cell cycle machinery [ ]. This connection makes the activation of CDK4/6–cyclin D an Achilles’ heel for cellular homeostasis. Although multiple layers of regulatory pathways control the activation of the complex, CDK4/6–cyclin D is among the most frequently dysregulated pathways in malignancy, with genetic or functional alterations detected in up to 90% of human cancers [ ].
Given this central role, CDK4/6 inhibition holds significant therapeutic potential. The clinical development of CDK4/6 inhibitors, including palbociclib, ribociclib, and abemaciclib, has reshaped the management of hormone receptor (HR)-positive breast cancer [ ]. Their success has heightened interest in extending CDK4/6 inhibition to other tumour types; however, broader application remains constrained by several challenges, including intrinsic or acquired resistance, limited therapeutic efficacy in certain tumour contexts, and dose-limiting haematologic toxicity [ ]. These limitations have intensified efforts to refine CDK targeting strategies, particularly by reducing activity against CDK6, a key regulator of haematopoietic proliferation, with the aim of improving the therapeutic window while maintaining effective CDK4 pathway suppression. Recent advances in medicinal chemistry have produced the first selective CDK4 inhibitors (AU2-94, atirmociclib, BGB-43395, and RGT-587), which demonstrate promising anticancer activity and renewed interest in next-generation therapeutic development [ , ].
This review summarises three decades of progress in understanding the biological foundations of the CDK4/6–cyclin D axis, examining how this pathway has been therapeutically exploited, and evaluating the clinical landscape shaped by CDK4/6 inhibitors. It highlights ongoing limitations and discusses emerging strategies to overcome them. Finally, the review looks forward to the next phase of the field: the rise of selective CDK4 inhibitors and their potential to redefine the cell-cycle-targeted therapy.

## CDK4/6 and D-type cyclins: from cellular homeostasis to tumourigenesis

## Activation of CDK4/6 by D-type cyclins
Among cell-cycle CDKs (CDK1, CDK2, CDK4, and CDK6), CDK4 and CDK6 function as key effectors of mitogenic signalling through their association with D-type cyclins (D1, D2 and D3) [ ]. Upon propagation of mitogenic signals, D-type cyclins are upregulated to convey extracellular signals to the cell cycle machinery [ ]. D-type cyclins interact with phosphorylated p21 (Y76) or p27 (Y88/89), utilizing their nuclear localization signals (NLS) to enter the nucleus [ ], where the p21/p27–cyclin D forms a ternary complex with CDK4 or CDK6 [ ].
p21/p27 are also essential for activating CDK4/6. Unlike other CDKs, which can be fully activated by cyclin binding, the association with cyclin Ds is insufficient to properly configure the CDK4 activation loop. This occurs because D-type cyclins bind to CDK4 primarily via the N-terminal cyclin-binding factor (CBF), which misaligns the C-lobes of both proteins, causing disorientation of CDK4’s activation loop [ ]. The association of p21 or p27 with the CDK4-cyclin D1 complex is essential for rotating the N-lobe of CDK4 toward cyclin D, thereby exposing the T172 residue for CAK-mediated phosphorylation [ , , ]. CDK4 also has an additional phosphorylation site (P173), which is targeted by proline-directed kinases and JNK. These additional phosphorylation events are critical for the timely activation of CDK4 [ , ].
Owing to the structural similarity between CDK4 and CDK6, CDK6 is activated through a comparable p21/p27-assisted, CAK-dependent mechanism (T177) [ , ]. However, the lack of a proline residue in its T-loop reduces CDK6’s reliance on T-loop phosphorylation. Accordingly, CDK6 can also be activated by alternative mediators, contributing to biological functions that are distinct from those of CDK4 [ ].

## Substrate recognition by the CDK4/6-cyclin D complex
Once activated, CDK4/6-cyclin D transiently forms an enzyme-substrate complexes. Substrate recognition primarily occurs through an interaction between the hydrophobic patch of cyclin D and the RXL motif of the substrate, which is a conserved peptide sequence consisting of arginine (R), leucine (L), and any other residue (X) [ ]. This step is not exclusive to cyclin D and can be observed in other cyclins [ ]. CDK4/6-cyclin D also recognizes their substrates through a C-terminal helix docking site, which is present in some, but not all, substrates. Unlike for the RXL motif, interaction with the C-terminal helix docking site is specific for D-type cyclins [ ]. So far, sixty-eight potential substrates have been identified for CDK4/6-cyclin Ds. Among them, retinoblastoma (RB) protein is the primary and best-characterized target. RB interacts with CDK4/6-cyclin D through both the RXL motif and the C-terminal helix docking site, leading to its mono-phosphorylation at multiple residues, including S780, S795, S807/811, and T826 [ , ].
Beyond RB, several additional substrates have been identified for the CDK4/6-cyclin D complex, some of which are expressed at low levels or only under specific physiological or pathological conditions [ , ]. Notably, not all CDK4 and CDK6 substrates are identical. Despite their high structural homology, CDK4 and CDK6 differ in their activation mechanisms and in their affinity for cyclin D family members, which influences their substrate preferences. For example, CDK6’s specific affinity for cyclin D3 involves it in processes such as splicing regulation, T cell development, and haematopoiesis [ – ]. CDK6 also functions as a transcription regulator through its distinct regulatory domains within the N-terminal LYN homology domain and C-terminal helical region. These domains facilitate interactions with transcription factors, including STAT3, VEGF-A, c-Jun, and RUNX1, as well as with chromatin modifiers and DNA promoter regions [ – ]. A list of these transcription factors is summarized in Table  . Together, these additional properties distinguish CDK6 from CDK4 in specific cellular contexts and highlight their partially divergent biological functions.

## Biological function of the CDK4/6-cyclin D complex
The interaction of CDK4/6-cyclin D with their substrates drives different intracellular activities, which traditionally categorised as canonical (RB-dependent) and non-canonical (RB-independent) functions. This classification arose from the earlier belief that RB was the sole substrate of CDK4/6-cyclin D.

## The canonical function: Cell cycle regulation via the RB/E2F axis
RB is the central substrate of CDK4/6–cyclin D complexes. During early G1 phase, mono-phosphorylation of RB by CDK4/6-cyclin Ds disrupts its repressive association with E2F transcription factors through two coordinated mechanisms: (i) relieving RB-mediated inhibition of E2F and (ii) preventing RB-dependent recruitment of HDACs to E2F-regulated promoters [ ]. This relief enables E2F1 to interact with the promoters of CCNE1/2, which encode cyclin E family members.
In late G1, E-type cyclins bind to CDK2, leading to RB hyperphosphorylation and an expansion of E2F1 transcriptional output, promoting the expression of A-type and B-type cyclins, CDK1, TK1, DNA polymerase-α, and topoisomerase IIα [ ]. The two-step RB phosphorylation during the G1 phase ensures orderly progression of the cell cycle [ ]. Once G1 ends, cells progress through subsequent phases in which other CDK–cyclin complexes orchestrate cell proliferation. ## The non-canonical roles of the CDK4/6-cyclin D complex

Cell proliferation

The regulation of cell proliferation by the CDK4/6-cyclin D complex can also be mediated by interacting with TGF-β and SMAD family members [ ]. Under non-proliferative conditions, TGF-β signalling enforces SMAD2 and SMAD3 to make a complex with SMAD4, preventing the expression of proliferation-related proteins [ ]. The CDK4/6-cyclin D complex, particularly CDK4-cyclin D1, abrogates the TGF-β/SMAD signalling by phosphorylating SMAD2 and SMAD3 at the SMAD linker region (T8, T178, and S212), decreasing their association with SMAD4 [ , , ] (Fig.  A). This disruption further leads to MYC upregulation and cell proliferation [ , ].
Fizzy and cell division cycle 20-related 1 (FZR1), also known as CDH1, is another substrate of CDK4/6-cyclin D1 involved in cell proliferation (Fig.  A) [ ]. Similar to RB, FZR1 has conserved phosphorylation sites (S36, S40, S151, T32) that are targeted by CDK4-cyclin D1, leading to S-phase progression [ ]. FZR1 inactivation by CDK4-cyclin D1 also stabilizes the SCFSkp2 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, promoting proteasome-mediated degradation of the Cip/Kip family of CDK inhibitors and facilitating cell cycle progression [ ].
CDK6 and D-type cyclins can also regulate cell proliferation through kinase-independent mechanisms [ ]. Structural analyses revealed that the C-terminal region of cyclin D contains an LxxLL motif and a distinct repressor domain that facilitate coactivator recruitment [ ], and HDAC binding [ ], respectively. Through these interactions, cyclin D function as transcriptional modulators, either enhancing or repressing gene expression [ ]. For example, in breast epithelial cells, cyclin D1 recruits steroid receptor co-activators and the histone acetyltransferase PCAF to enhance the transcription signature of ERα [ , ] (Fig.  B). Conversely, it suppresses the anti-proliferative nuclear receptor PPAR-γ by recruiting HDAC1/3 to its response elements or inhibiting p300, a PPAR-γ co-activator [ – ] (Fig.  B). Additional transcription factors and nuclear receptors that interact with cyclin D1 are listed in Table  .
In a kinase-independent manner, CDK6 also regulates cell proliferation in the haematopoietic system. Under physiological conditions, CDK6/cyclin D3 promotes HSPC exit from quiescence and re-entry into the cell cycle via the canonical, RB-dependent pathway [ ]. Under haematopoietic stress, when HSPCs shift toward increased self-renewal to replenish stem-cell pools, CDK6 acts in a kinase-independent manner by binding to the promoter of early growth response protein 1 (EGR1), enhancing the proliferative capacity of HSPCs [ ]. Additionally, CDK6 interacts with the Runt domain of RUNX1, disrupting its binding to DNA and C/EBPα and thereby impairing transcription of myeloid lineage specific genes, ultimately sustaining cell proliferation [ ].

Senescence

Cellular senescence is a protective mechanism that halts cell proliferation under conditions such as DNA damage or oxidative stress [ ]. The CDK4/6-cyclin D1 complex counteracts senescence through interaction with FOXM1 and DNMT1 [ ].
FOXM1 has a transcriptional signature reminiscent of E2F1 that prevents senescence by driving cell cycle progression [ , ]. CDK4/6-cyclin D1 enhances FOXM1 stability and transactivation by phosphorylating both its DNA-binding and transactivation domains [ ] (Fig.  C). This event promotes cell cycle progression, enhances replicative immortality by upregulating BMI1, β-catenin, and SOX, and sustains cell survival under oxidative stress by expressing catalases and MnSOD1 [ ].
The regulation of DNMT1 by CDK4/6-cyclin D1 is mediated through both the RB/E2F/HDAC axis and post-transcriptional regulation [ ]. In an RB-independent manner, CDK4/cyclin D1 phosphorylates DNMT1, leading to its stabilization and protection from autophagy-mediated degradation [ ]. DNMT1 upregulation subsequently represses HBP-1 expression, potentiating cell proliferation by facilitating cyclin D1 expression and other mitogenic mediators [ ] (Fig.  D).

Cell survival

The involvement of CDK4/6-D-type cyclins in regulating cell survival has been observed under conditions of oxidative stress and DNA damage, particularly within the haematopoietic compartment, where CDK6–cyclin D3 phosphorylates glycolytic enzymes such as pyruvate kinase M1/M2 (PKM2) and phosphofructokinase (PFKP). This event redirects glycolytic by-products (glucose-6-phosphate and 3-phosphoglycerate) into the pentose phosphate and serine biosynthesis pathways, which enhance the production of antioxidant molecules that mitigate oxidative damage (Fig.  E) [ ].
Another mechanism by which the CDK4/6-Cyclin D complex regulates cell survival involves phosphorylation of methylosome protein 50 (MEP50), a co-activator of protein arginine N-methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5). CDK4/cyclin D1 phosphorylates MEP50 at three sites (T5, S265, and S306), with T5 being critical for enhancing PRMT5 methyltransferase activity. The MEP50/PRMT5 complex dimethylates arginine residues on histone H3 and H4, leading to downregulation of cullin4A and cullin4B. Since these proteins degrade chromatin licensing and DNA replication factor 1 (CDT-1), their suppression stabilizes CDT-1, thereby prolonging cell survival during the S-phase fidelity checkpoint [ ] (Fig.  F). The activated MEP50/PRMT5 complex also upregulates MDM4, suppressing p53 activity and further promoting cell survival [ – ]. Beyond cell survival, the MEP50/PRMT5 complex also facilitates the G1-S transition by regulating E2F1 methylation [ ].
Cyclin D1 also prolongs cell survival following DNA damage [ ]. During DNA damage, CDK2-cyclin A prevents homologous recombination (HR) by phosphorylating BRCA2 (S3291), thereby impeding RAD51 association with DNA breaks [ ]. This event drives the cells toward apoptosis to prevent the propagation of damaged genome [ ]. Cyclin D1, independent of CDK4 or CDK6, binds to the C-terminal domain of BRCA2 (amino acids 20–90), interfering with cyclinA-CDK2-mediated phosphorylation, allowing RAD51 to repair DNA [ , ] (Fig.  G). Cyclin D1 also acts as a scaffold, directly recruiting RAD51 to DNA breaks. RAD51 is also a direct transcriptional target of E2F1, indicating that the CDK4/6–cyclin D complex could influence DNA repair through its canonical RB-dependent pathway [ ]. Beyond RAD51 and BRCA2, cyclin D1 and D3 interact with other DNA repair mediators, including PCNA and RFC [ – ], to maintain genome integrity (Table  ).

Cellular metabolism

Lysosomes are metabolic regulatory hubs that balance anabolism and catabolism in response to nutrient availability. Under nutrient-deprived conditions, lysosome biogenesis increases to preserve cellular energy by degrading proteins, recycling cellular components, and activating autophagy [ , ]. Lysosome biogenesis is inversely linked to cyclin D1 abundance, accelerating during the S and G2/M phases when cyclin D1 levels are lowest [ ]. Interestingly, CDK4/6-cyclin D1 suppresses lysosome biogenesis during G1 by phosphorylating the transcription factors TFEB and TFE3, promoting their export from the nucleus [ ] (Fig.  H).
In parallel, CDK4/6–cyclin D1 promotes anabolic processes by activating mTORC1. Under amino acid deprivation, the folliculin (FLCN)-FNIP complex suppresses Rag GTPases, preventing mTORC1 activation at the lysosomal surface [ ]. Phosphorylation of FLCN by CDK4/6–cyclin D1 relieves this inhibition [ ]. This complex also phosphorylates TSC2 (S1217 and S1452), a negative regulator of mTORC1, further sustaining mTORC1 activity [ ] (Fig.  H). Activated mTORC1 enhances protein synthesis and blocks lysosome biogenesis through TFEB degradation. These findings clarify why mTORC1 activity peaks during the G1 phase [ ].
CDK4 also promotes anabolic metabolism by phosphorylating and inhibiting AMPKα, either alone or in complex with cyclin D1. Since AMPKα negatively regulates acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC), its inhibition stimulates malonyl-CoA production and fatty acid production, while also impairing lipid oxidation through suppression of carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (CPT1) [ ] (Fig.  I). AMPKα inhibition additionally activates mTORC1, further amplifying metabolic signalling [ ]. Taken together, the participation of CDK4/6-cyclin D1 in regulating cell metabolism ensures the production of essential proteins required for cell growth and proliferation.

 

Immune system

The role of the CDK4/6–cyclin D complex in immune regulation was first identified in murine models, where CDK6 and cyclin D3 deficiency was shown to reduce the population of double-positive thymocytes, a group of T-cell precursors [ ]. Subsequently, it was found that the CDK6/cyclin D3 complex interacts with the NOTCH and PI3K/Akt pathways to regulate T cell maturation [ , ]. These findings highlight the essential role of CDK6–cyclin D3 in lymphoid development.
In parallel, the CDK4/6 and D-type cyclins complex also participates in immune responses, by controlling lymphocyte clonal expansion. This function is regulated through the canonical RB-E2F1 axis. Following antigen exposure, the TCR/CD28 signalling upregulates cyclin D and CDK4/6 expression to facilitate T cell expansion [ ]. The exceptionally high proliferation rate observed during clonal expansion, characterized by a doubling time of less than 10 h, highlights the importance of CDK4/6-cyclin D in maintaining cell cycle fidelity in activated lymphocytes [ ].
Moreover, to prevent T cell exhaustion during the immune response, the CDK4/6-cyclin D1 complex represses programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1) expression through both RB-dependent and RB-independent mechanisms [ , ]. In the RB-dependent mechanism, CDK4/6-cyclin D1 phosphorylates S249/T252 residues of RB, enabling it to suppress PD-L1 expression by inhibiting NF-kB [ ]. In the RB-independent mechanism, CDK4/6-cyclin D1 promotes PD-L1 degradation by phosphorylating the transcription factor speckle type BTB/POZ protein (SPOP) at Serine 6 [ ] (Fig.  ).
In addition to modulating effector T cells, these molecules also inflame the immune response environment by producing cytokines, a process primarily mediated by CDK6. Nuclear CDK6 directly interacts with inflammatory modulators such as bromodomain protein ANCCA/ATAD2 and SOCS family members to induce cytokine synthesis [ ]. Independent of cyclin D1, CDK6 further facilitates NF-κB activation by phosphorylating the nuclear scaffolding protein TRIP6 [ ] and p65 (S536) [ ]. These mechanisms explain why CDK6-deficient mice release fewer cytokines in response to LPS lethal shock [ ].
At the final stage of the immune response, the CDK4/6-cyclin D1 complex transitions to an immune suppressive role, preventing unnecessary responses. This function is mediated by the differentiation of effector T cells into regulatory T cells (Tregs) (Fig.  ). Although the precise mechanism remains unclear, this process may be mediated by DNMT1 stabilization or FOXP3 upregulation [ , ]. Consistently, the higher expression of CDK4/6 and cyclin D in Tregs compared to effector T cells highlights their essential role in Treg differentiation [ , ]. Beyond Tregs, CDK6, but not CDK4, also dampens effector T cell activity by downregulating NFAT4 through an RB-independent mechanism (Fig.  ). Given the role of NFAT in IL-2-mediated T cell activation, its inhibition by CDK6 impairs effector T cell’s function [ ].
Taken together, CDK4/6 and cyclin Ds, either acting independently or as part of a complex, regulate immune responses at multiple stages, spanning T-cell development and activation through to T-cell exhaustion under immunosuppressive conditions.

 

 

## Regulation of CDK4/6-cyclin D complex
Having explored the biological functions of CDK4, CDK6, and D-type cyclins, it is clear that their regulation is essential for maintaining cell homeostasis. This regulation is mediated through multiple processes, including transcriptional repression and ubiquitin-mediated degradation of cyclin D, as well as inhibition of CDK4/6 by the INK4 and CIP/KIP family proteins.

## Regulation of cyclin D levels
The dynamic regulation of cyclin D levels is essential for controlling CDK4/6 activity. As labile proteins with short half-lives (10–60 min, depending on the cell type) [ ], D-type cyclins’ levels rapidly decline upon mitogen withdrawal, primarily through a combination of transcriptional repression and ubiquitin-mediated degradation.
For ubiquitin-mediated degradation, D-type cyclins must first be labelled, transported to the cytoplasm, and then processed by the ubiquitin-proteasome system [ ]. GSK-3β plays a key role in labelling cyclin D1 by phosphorylating the T286 residue [ ]. Additionally, p38/SAPK2 and Mirk/Dyrk1B phosphorylate T286 and T288, respectively [ , ]. Once phosphorylated, D-type cyclins bind to the nuclear exportin CRM1 and are transported to the cytoplasm [ ], where they undergo polyubiquitination by SCF (Skp-Cullin-F-box) complex, β-transducin repeat-containing protein (β-TrCP), CRL4AMBRA1 (also known as CRL4DCAF3), and the APC/C [ , ].
Among E3 ubiquitin ligases within the SCF complex, FBXO4 is a well-characterized F-box containing protein that ubiquitinates cyclin Ds. Its E3 ligase activity is dependent on homodimerization, which is driven by GSK-3β-mediated phosphorylation (S12), a modification that coincides with cyclin Ds phosphorylation during the G1–S transition. The FBXO4 homodimer interacts with αB-crystallin and recognizes phosphorylated T286 on cyclin D, targeting it for ubiquitination [ ]. The added polyubiquitin chain directs cyclin D to the 26S proteasome, where it is unfolded, translocated into the catalytic core, and degraded into small peptides [ ]. ## Regulation of CDK4/6 activity
Alongside cyclin D1 degradation, CDK4/6 kinase activity is also suppressed to prevent unnecessary cell proliferation. Following a decline in intracellular levels of D-type cyclins, monomeric CDK4/6 proteins bind to cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors, which include the INK4 family (p16INK4A, p15INK4B, p18INK4C, p19INK4D) and CIP/KIP family (p21, p27, and p57) [ ].

INK4 family

The INK4 family consists of four members that selectively inhibit CDK4 and CDK6. Among them, p16INK4A, encoded by CDKN2A, serves as the primary inhibitor. The expression of p16INK4A increases upon termination of the G1 phase through both transcriptional and post-translational mechanisms. Interestingly, the RB/E2F1 axis is one of the regulators that upregulates p16INK4A in a feedback loop to ensure proper cell cycle progression [ ].
At the post-translational level, p16INK4A undergoes modifications at three phosphorylation sites and one methylation site, which influence the protein’s stability and interaction with CDK4/6 [ ]. Specifically, phosphorylation at S140 enhances protein stability [ ] and at S157 increases protein affinity for CDK4/6 [ ]. In contrast, S8 phosphorylation weakens its affinity for CDK4/6 [ ], and PRMT6-mediated methylation of R138 interferes with S140 phosphorylation, further impairing p16INK4A binding to CDK4/6 [ ].
Once expressed, p16INK4A binds to monomeric CDK4/6 via ankyrin repeat (AR) motifs. This interaction is critically dependent on R24 in CDK4 and R31 in CDK6, to the extent that mutations in these residues attenuate the interaction of CDK4/6 with p16INK4A [ , ]. Upon binding, p16INK4A induces allosteric changes in CDK4/6, which distort both their cyclin-binding interface and the ATP-binding pocket. Moreover, this interaction disrupts the association of CDK4/6 with heat shock protein 90 (HSP90) and its co-chaperone CDC37, preventing their proper folding [ ]. As a result of these conformational changes, CDK4 and CDK6 lose their affinity for the complexes of D-type cyclins with p21/p27 [ ]. This loss of affinity further releases bound p21/p27, which in turn inhibit the CDK/cyclin complexes, preventing cell cycle progression [ ].

CIP/KIP family

Another protein family that suppresses the catalytic activity of CDK4/6 is the CIP/KIP family, comprising p21, p27, and p57. Members of this family are general inhibitors of CDK-cyclin complexes, which allosterically prevent T-loop activation by binding to the kinase inhibitory domain (KIDs) of CDKs [ ]. Importantly, these proteins exhibit dual regulatory effects toward the CDK4/6-cyclin D complex, facilitating both its assembly and inhibition.
p21, encoded by CDKN1A, has four phosphorylation sites, including T57, S130, T145, and S146, which are important for its regulatory effects on CDK4/6-cyclin D. While unphosphorylated p21 prevents the catalytic activity of CDK4 by preventing T-loop phosphorylation, JNK1/2-mediated phosphorylation of p21 (S130) activates CDK4 [ ]. The intracellular concentration of p21 also determines its influence on CDK4-cyclin D. At high concentrations, which is mainly during the G0 phase, p21 suppresses CDK4/6-cyclin D; meanwhile, lower intracellular concentrations active the kinase complex [ ].
p27 and, to a lesser extent, p57 are other family members that interact with cyclin E(A)/CDK2, cyclin D/CDK4/6, and cyclin B/CDK1 [ , ]. Like p21, the unphosphorylated p27 also allosterically prevents the CDK4/6-cyclin D activation and converts cells to a quiescence status [ ]. To stabilize the CDK4/6-cyclin D complex, p27 undergoes several stages of phosphorylation by Akt (T157 and T198) [ ] and non-receptor tyrosine kinases (Y74, Y88, and Y89) [ , ].
For p57, the intracellular concentration determines its regulatory effect on the CDK4/cyclin D complex, with lower levels promoting complex assembly and higher levels inhibiting its activity [ ]. Other factors that enforce p57 to inhibit CDK4-cyclin D1 include p38-mediated phosphorylation of T143 [ ] and p57 homodimerization [ ].

 

## CDK4/6-cyclin D and tumourigenesis

## Mechanisms leading to CDK4/6-cyclin D activation in cancer cells
A glimpse into the biological functions of CDK4/6 and cyclin D1 clearly reveals that any aberrations in the regulation of this axis disrupt the delicate balance required for cellular homeostasis, ultimately leading to tumourigenesis [ ]. Notably, dysregulation in the CDK4/6-cyclin D/RB/E2F1 axis is found in over 90% of human cancers, often resulting from genetic alterations or the activation of mitogenic pathways (Fig.  ) [ ].

Genetic alterations

The list of genetic alterations affecting the CDK4/6-cyclin D axis in different cancers is provided in Table  . These alterations include gene amplification/overexpression of the main components (CDK4, CDK6, and cyclin D1-3); deletions or epigenetic alterations of the negative regulators (p16INK4A and p15INK4B); and deletion or loss-of-function mutations of RB1 [ ] (Fig.  ). Co-occurring genomic alterations are also common, particularly among genes located within the same chromosomal locus. A notable example is the 9p21.3 tumour suppressor locus, where CDKN2A and CDKN2B frequently undergo homozygous co-deletion, resulting in simultaneous loss of p16INK4A and p15INK4B. This event represents one of the most prevalent tumour suppressor deletions and has been widely reported in several cancers, including NSCLC [ ], glioblastoma [ ], and pancreatic adenocarcinoma [ ]. Other recurrent co-occurring genetic alterations include co-amplification of CDK4 and MDM2, which has been reported in sarcoma [ ] and neuroblastoma [ ]. Importantly, cooperative alterations are not restricted to genes located in close genomic proximity. For example, concurrent alterations in CDK4 and CDKN2A have been reported in glioma, suggesting that dysregulation of the CDK4/6 pathway can arise through multiple genomic mechanisms [ ].
Rare genetic alterations also contribute to constitutive activation of CDK4/6-cyclin D1 in cancer cells. These comprise alterations known as D-cyclin activating features, which enhance the transcription of D-type cyclins or prevent their proteasomal degradation. Examples include the t(11;14) (q13;q32), focal amplification of CCND2/3, and mutations in the 3’ untranslated region of CCND1, which promote D-type cyclin expression [ ]. Moreover, mutations that substitute P287 with threonine or serine, replace T286 with alanine, and a 12-base pair in-frame deletion that removes amino acids 289–292, have been observed to prevent cyclin D1 degradation [ , ]. Similarly, mutations in components of the ubiquitin-proteasome system, such as FBXO4 and CRL4AMBRA1, also disrupt cyclin D1 degradation, leading to its nuclear accumulation [ , ] (Fig.  ).
A group of rare mutations also disrupt CDK4/6 inhibition by p16INK4A (Fig.  ). These include a point mutation in CDK6 and a miscoding mutation in CDK4 (R24C), both of which attenuate the sensitivity of these CDKs to p16INK4A in neuroblastoma and familial melanoma [ , ]. The t(9;22) translocation in childhood ALL also results in deletion of the CDKN2A/B locus, leading to downregulation of p16INK4A and p15INK4B [ ].
An interesting aspect of these genetic alterations is their tissue specificity. Different tissues preferentially display distinct alterations within the CDK4/6-cyclin D/RB/E2F1 pathway [ ]. For instance, CDK4 hyperactivation is more common in solid tumours of epithelial origin, whereas overexpression/amplification of CDK6 is more prevalent in mesenchymal tumours, such as haematologic malignancies [ , ]. The amplification/overexpression of cyclin D1 in breast carcinoma may, in part, be attributed to its unique interaction with ERα, which promotes cell proliferation, survival, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, and invasion [ , ]. These cancer-specific genetic alterations imply that the consequences of mutations are tailored to the tumour type [ ].

Mitogenic signalling pathways

In addition to genetic alterations, oncogenic signalling pathways such as RAS, MAPKs, PI3K/AKT, and JNK can hyperactivate the CDK4/6-cyclin D1 axis [ , , ]. Although these pathways ultimately converge on the same downstream output, they do so through distinct mechanisms. For example, the MAPK pathway directly enhances CCND1 transcription [ ], whereas PI3K/AKT pathway stabilises cyclin D1 by inhibiting GSK-3β-mediated degradation and promoting its translation [ ] (Fig.  ). Or, in hormone-dependent cancers like breast cancer, steroid hormone receptors such as ERα can upregulate cyclin D1 expression by binding to its promoter, facilitating Sp1 recruitment to GC-rich motifs, and engaging cAMP-responsive elements (Fig.  ) [ ].
Translocations and mutations can likewise drive aberrant activation of the CDK4/6–cyclin D axis, particularly in haematologic malignancies such as leukaemia, splenic marginal zone lymphoma, and B-cell lymphocytic leukaemia [ , ]. In AML, FLT3 internal tandem duplication (ITD) mutations promote the RAS/PI3K and SRC-family kinase signalling, elevating cyclin D1 and CDK6 expression (Fig.  ) [ ]. AML-associated alterations, including the t(9;11) MLL-AF9 fusion and NUP98-rearranged translocations, can also upregulate CDK6 in leukaemia cells [ , ].

## Function of CDK4/6-cyclin D in cancer cells
Once hyperactivated in cancer cells, the CDK4/6-cyclin D1 complex drives tumourigenesis by regulating cell proliferation, sustaining cell survival, and facilitating metastasis as well as angiogenesis [ ]. An overview of these key mechanisms is provided in Fig.  .

Regulating cell proliferation and survival

The regulation of cell proliferation and survival is a central function of the CDK4/6-cyclin D complex during tumourigenesis. Regulating the expression of proliferation-related genes like CCNE1/2, CCNA1/2, CCNB1, and MYC, or sequestering p21/p27 from the CDK2-cyclin E complex are the main mechanisms through which this complex mediates cell proliferation [ ]. The CDK4/6-cyclin D complex also promotes cell proliferation and survival through non-canonical substrates. Key examples include CDK4/cyclin D1-mediated suppression of the TGF-β/SMAD pathway in breast and colorectal cancers [ , ], phosphorylation of FZR1 (S714) in breast cancer [ ], FOXM1 stabilisation in NSCLC [ ] and melanoma [ ], stabilization of DNMT1 in prostate cancer and sarcoma [ ], and regulation of aldo-keto reductase (AKRC) family in breast cancer [ ] (Fig.  ).
Beyond kinase-dependent roles, cyclin D1 also supports cancer cell survival and proliferation through kinase-independent mechanisms. For example, in breast cancer, cyclin D1 directly interacts with the hormone-binding domain of ERα, increasing its affinity for oestrogen-response elements, amplifying ERα-mediated transcriptional signature [ , ]. In prostate cancer, cyclin D1 suppresses androgen receptor (AR) activity, an effect that appears counterintuitive but ultimately promotes the emergence of an aggressive, androgen-independent phenotype known as castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) [ ]. Cyclin D1 also modulates transcriptional networks in lung cancer by interacting with p300 and repressing RUNX3 and its downstream effector p21 [ ]. CDK6 likewise exerts kinase-independent functions in haematologic malignancies, where it enhances leukemogenesis by upregulating p53 antagonists such as PRMT5, PPM1D and MDM4, and by activating mitogenic pathways through interactions with FLT3 and PIM1 [ , ].
DNA repair is another cellular process through which cyclin D1 regulates cell survival, especially during cancer treatment. Cyclin D1 activates both homologous recombination and non-homologous end joining, particularly in RB-deficient cancers, where it is dispensable for cell proliferation [ , , ]. In CRPC cells, for example, cyclin D1 promotes genomic stability by recruiting the ATM/DNA-PKcs/RAD51 repair machinery to sites of radiotherapy-induced DNA damage to prevent cell death (Fig.  ) [ ].

Regulating cell migration and invasion

Metastasis, a critical step in tumour progression, requires coordinated changes in tissue architecture and cell motility. The involvement of E2F1 in these processes has brought renewed attention to the CDK4/6–cyclin D1 axis [ ] (Fig.  ). Notably, this complex promotes metastatic behaviour through both RB-dependent and RB-independent mechanisms. In breast and prostate cancer, the CDK4/6–cyclin D1 complex promotes epithelial–mesenchymal transition by stabilising SNAIL1 and SLUG via phosphorylation of the deubiquitinase DUB3 (S41) [ ] and through direct interaction with AR [ ].
CDK4/6–cyclin D1 also contributes to metastatic dissemination by modulating extracellular matrix remodelling. It regulates matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-2, MMP-3, and MMP-9) through multiple mechanisms, including phosphorylation of filamin A (S2152 and S1459) [ ], suppression of the Rho-GTPase pathway and TSP-1 [ ], upregulation of RAC1 [ , ], and transcriptional cooperation with c-Jun and SP1 [ ] (Fig.  ). Together, these mechanisms enhance invasive capacity and support metastatic progression.

Regulating angiogenesis

Angiogenesis is another process influenced by CDK4/6–cyclin D1. Clinical observations suggest that elevated cyclin D1 or CDK6 expression correlates with increased vessel density in lung carcinoma and TNBC patients, respectively [ , ].
Molecular investigation outlines that cyclin D1, independent of RB or CDK4/6, regulates angiogenesis. In endothelial cells, it enhances VEGF expression by suppressing TSP-1 or by phosphorylating STAT3 (Fig.  ) [ ]. Consistent with these findings, CCND1 knockout diminishes vessel density in colorectal and prostate cancer xenografts [ , ].
CDK6 also contributes to pro-angiogenic signalling, although the precise mechanism remains incompletely defined. Current evidence suggests that CDK6 upregulates VEGF expression through kinase-independent interactions with STAT3, c-JUN or AP-1 (Fig.  ) [ ].

 

 

## CDK4/6 Inhibition: a therapeutic strategy in cancer
The involvement of the CDK4/6-cyclin D1 complex in tumourigenesis, together with its widespread dysregulation in human cancers, indicates that targeting this complex could offer therapeutic benefit. Gene-targeted models provided compelling support for this concept: loss of CDK4 or cyclin D1 markedly reduced the incidence of skin and breast tumours, even in the presence of potent oncogenic drivers such as HER2 or Neu-Ras [ – ]. Similarly, deletion of CDK4, cyclin D1, or cyclin D3 delayed tumour growth in xenograft models of HER2⁺ breast cancer, T-ALL, and NSCLC [ , ]. However, the severe toxicities observed in these genetic models underscored the need for pharmacological strategies capable of modulating kinase activity without compromising essential physiological functions [ ].
The first pharmacological attempts involved pan-CDK inhibitors that broadly targeted both cell-cycle and transcriptional CDKs. Although agents such as flavopiridol (alvociclib), roscovitine, milciclib, and dinaciclib demonstrated potent anti-tumour activity, their clinical development was limited by dose-limiting toxicities [ ]. These challenges prompted a shift toward designing compounds with greater specificity, and the extensive medicinal chemistry led to the discovery of highly selective CDK4/6 inhibitors, including palbociclib, ribociclib, and abemaciclib [ ]. Since then, several additional CDK4/6 inhibitors have entered clinical development. Approved agents and those currently under clinical evaluation are summarised in Tables  and , respectively.

## A brief overview of the characteristics of CDK4/6 inhibitors
Palbociclib (Ibrance/PD-0332991), the first-in-class CDK4/6 inhibitor developed by Pfizer [ , ], shows potent selectivity for CDK4/cyclin D1, CDK4-cyclin D3, and CDK6-cyclin D2 with the IC50 of 11 nM, 9 nM, and 15 nM, respectively. Plasma concentrations after oral dosing of palbociclib show a 26-hour half-life and appear sufficient to suppress RB phosphorylation with once-daily administration [ ]. Multiple clinical trials, including PALOMA-1/TRIO-18, PALOMA-2, and PALOMA-3, evaluated the efficacy of palbociclib in combination with aromatase inhibitors and selective oestrogen receptor degraders (SERDs) in patients with HR+/HER2− metastatic breast cancer. Palbociclib was approved by the FDA for use in combination with endocrine therapies (ET) for the treatment of HR+/HER2− metastatic breast cancer [ – ].
Ribociclib (LEE011), developed by Novartis, exhibits potent inhibition of CDK4 and CDK6, with IC50 values of 10 nM and 39 nM, respectively [ ]. Like palbociclib, the clinical development of ribociclib has focused on treating HR+/HER2− breast cancer. Three key clinical trials, MONALEESA-2, MONALEESA-3, and MONALEESA-7, demonstrated the therapeutic benefit of ribociclib in combination with letrozole or fulvestrant in ER+ breast cancer. Consequently, ribociclib received FDA approval for the treatment of HR+ advanced breast cancer, in combination with ET. It has also been approved as adjuvant therapy with ET for patients with early-stage HR+/HER2− breast cancer [ ].
Abemaciclib (LY2835219), developed by Eli Lilly, is one of the most potent inhibitors against CDK4-cyclin D (IC50 = 0.6–2 nM) and CDK6-cyclin D (IC50 = 10 nM) but also inhibits CDK2-cyclin A/E (IC50 ~ 504 nM), CDK5/p25 (IC50 ~ 355 nM), and CDK9-cyclin T1 (IC50 ~ 57 nM) [ , ]. Beyond CDKs, abemaciclib also targets GSK-3α/β (IC₅₀ = 8.67 nM) and CaMKII isoforms with the IC50 value of 3.5 nM (CaMKIIβ), 2.6 nM (CaMKIIδ), and 52 nM (CaMKIIγ) [ ]. Pharmacokinetic studies have revealed that abemaciclib has a shorter half-life than the other two inhibitors in humans (~ 17 h) and undergoes saturable absorption. Therefore, a twice-daily regimen is required to maintain continuous RB inhibition and optimise drug exposure [ – ]. Abemaciclib has been approved for use as a single agent or in combination with ET in first-line and adjuvant treatment settings for breast cancer [ ].
Another feature of abemaciclib is its ability to cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB). Unlike palbociclib and ribociclib, which are substrates of ATP-binding cassette efflux transporters, abemaciclib inhibits these transporters [ ]. This unique ability increases its concentrations in cerebrospinal fluid, making it a promising therapeutic candidate for brain tumours and brain metastases [ ].
Trilaciclib (G1T28, Cosela) is a short-acting and intravenous CDK4/6 inhibitor developed by G1 Therapeutics. With potent activity against CDK4 (IC₅₀ = 1 nM) and CDK6 (IC₅₀ = 4 nM), trilaciclib induces transient G1 arrest in haematopoietic progenitor cells, thereby protecting these cells from chemotherapy-induced toxicity [ – ]. Effective myeloid protection requires that G1 arrest is established before exposure to cytotoxic agents and is reversible thereafter to avoid prolonged myelosuppression; trilaciclib was designed and dosed to achieve this profile [ ].
The myeloid-protective effect of trilaciclib has been or is being evaluated in RB-deficient tumours (SCLC and TNBC) and RB-proficient cancers, including pancreatic cancer, HNCSS, oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, Ewing sarcoma, and colorectal cancer. In extensive stage SCLC, trilaciclib 240 mg/m2 was administered intravenously within four hours prior to chemotherapy and effectively reduced the chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression in randomized trials, leading to its FDA approval as a myeloid-protective agent in this setting [ ].
Dalpiciclib (SHR6390), developed by Jiangsu Hengrui Medicine, inhibits CDK4 and CDK6 with IC50 values of 12.4 nM and 9.9 nM, respectively [ , ], and has been evaluated as a single agent and in combination (NCT04293276; NCT04997798). In the phase III DAWNA-2 trial (an aromatase inhibitor + dalpiciclib) in patients with HR⁺/HER2⁻ advanced breast cancer, the combination improved PFS [ ]. Dalpiciclib received its first approval in China in December 2021 for use in combination with fulvestrant in HR⁺/HER2⁻ advanced breast cancer after ET progression.

Other CDK4/6 inhibitors

The therapeutic success of frontier CDK4/6 inhibitors has driven intense efforts to develop novel agents with improved efficacy and selectivity. These agents are currently at various stages of preclinical and clinical evaluation, with detailed data available for some and only limited information is available for others identified exclusively through clinical trial registries, such as for BEBT-209 (NCT06047184), HS-10,342 (NCT04060511; NCT05044988), XH-30,002 (NCT05927844), P-276-00 (NCT00407498), CS-3002 (NCT04162301), PF-06804103 (NCT03284723), and TY-302 (NCT04433494).
FCN-437c is an oral CDK4/6 inhibitor currently under clinical evaluation for the treatment of HR+ breast cancer. Early-phase studies in HR⁺/HER2⁻ breast cancer and advanced solid tumours demonstrated that FCN-437c is well tolerated (NCT04488107; NCT03951116), exhibits a lower risk of cardiotoxicity (NCT06290466), and does not require dose adjustment in cases of mild hepatic impairment (NCT06620731). The combination of FCN-437c with fulvestrant or letrozole in HR⁺/HER2⁻ breast cancer patients achieved an ORR of 40.9%, with 12-month PFS and OS rates of 67.7% and 95.5%, respectively (NCT05004142). FCN-437c has advanced to phase III studies in combination with fulvestrant and anastrozole in breast cancer (NCT05438810; NCT05439499).
FLX925 (AMG 925, Amgen) is an inhibitor of FLT3 and CDK4/6, with IC50 ~ 2 nM, 3 nM, and 8 nM against FLT3, CDK4, and CDK6, respectively. Preclinical studies demonstrated anti-tumour activity of FLX925 against FLT3 mutant AML and colon adenocarcinoma models [ ]. The compound advanced into a phase I clinical trial in patients with relapsed/refractory AML (NCT02335814); however, no reported progression into later clinical trials has emerged to date.
Tibremciclib (BPI-16350), developed by Betta Pharmaceutical, inhibits CDK4-cyclin D1 (IC50 = 1.1 nM), CDK6-cyclin D1 (IC50 = 0.4 nM), CDK4-cyclin D3 (IC50 = 4.2 nM), and CDK6-cyclin D3 (IC50 = 10.2 nM). Preclinical studies have demonstrated robust anti-tumour activity in colorectal cancer and leukaemia xenograft models, and its ability to penetrate the BBB [ ]. Tibremciclib is well tolerated, exhibits dose-proportional plasma exposure with a half-life of 35–51 h, supporting a once-daily dosing regimen (NCT03791112). In a phase III TIFFANY trial (NCT05433480), tibremciclib plus fulvestrant improved median PFS from 5.6 to 16.5 months in patients with HR+/HER2− locally advanced or metastatic breast cancer who have progressed on ET.
Birociclib (XZP-3287), developed by Xuanzhu Biopharmaceutical, is currently in phase III clinical trials for metastatic HR+/HER2− breast cancer patients whose disease has progressed after ET (NCT05077449; NCT05257395). A phase II study has suggested a twice-daily administration improved PFS in breast cancer patients (NCT04539496).
MM-D37K, a synthetic peptide developed by MetaMax, was designed to mimic the CDK4-inhibitory function of p16INK4a through a derived functional motif. As a peptide-based agent, it would require parenteral administration. Acting as a p16 surrogate, MM-D37K has demonstrated CDK4/6 inhibition and anti-tumour activity in preclinical models, including those lacking endogenous p16 function. Given the acceptable safety in preclinical xenograft models, the compound has been proposed for advancement into early-phase clinical trial [ ].
Auceliciclib (AU3-14), developed by Aucentra Therapeutics, is a highly potent CDK4/6 inhibitor with Ki values of 0.5 nM for CDK4-cyclin D1 and 0.5-3 nM for CDK6-cyclin D1/3. Preclinical pharmacokinetic studies demonstrated favourable BBB permeability, with unbound brain-to-plasma ratios 2–4 times higher than those of abemaciclib, supporting its development for brain tumours [ ]. A Phase I/IIa trial (ACTRN12621000479808) of auceliciclib as a monotherapy in patients with advanced solid tumours, and in combination with temozolomide for recurrent/refractory high-grade glioma including glioblastoma, has been completed. Auceliciclib was well-tolerated with minimal haematological toxicity, a key differentiator from other CDK4/6 inhibitors, and demonstrated dose-dependent increased absorption and pharmacokinetics. Among the evaluable patients, > 42% achieved stable disease, including a few patients with disease control lasting > 24 weeks. These findings support further clinical development of auceliciclib in brain tumours and other malignancies [ ].
Lerociclib (G1T38; GB491), developed by G1 Therapeutics, is an oral CDK4/6/9 inhibitor with activity against CDK4/cyclin D1 (IC₅₀ = 1 nM), CDK6/cyclin D3 (IC₅₀ = 2 nM), and CDK9/cyclin T1 (IC₅₀ = 28 nM) [ , ]. In a phase I/II study in HR+/HER2− metastatic breast cancer (NCT02983071), lerociclib plus fulvestrant produced efficacy comparable to that of other CDK4/6 inhibitors while demonstrating a lower incidence of grade 3/4 neutropenia and reduced gastrointestinal toxicity. Two ongoing phase III trials i.e., LEONARDA-1 (NCT05054751) evaluating lerociclib plus fulvestrant, and LEONARDA-2 (CTR20212139; NCT05851014) assessing lerociclib plus letrozole, are investigating its efficacy in HR⁺/HER2⁻ breast cancer. Beyond breast cancer, lerociclib has also been assessed in combination with osimertinib in EGFR-mutant NSCLC (NCT03455829).
Narazaciclib (ON123300, HX301), developed by Onconova and Hanmi, is a multi-kinase inhibitor with potent activity against CDK4, CDK6, PIM1 and PI3K-δ with IC50 ~ 3.9 nM, 9.8–10 nM, 47 nM and 36 nM, respectively. Preclinical studies have demonstrated anti-tumour activity in mantle cell lymphoma [ ] and AML [ ]. Clinically, narazaciclib has been evaluated in clinical trials for endometrial cancer (NCT05705505) and advanced solid tumours (NCT05731934). A study assessing its combination with dexamethasone in multiple myeloma was initiated but subsequently terminated (NCT06351644).

 

 

## An overview of CDK4/6 inhibitors in clinical trials
This section reviews the clinical trial landscape in greater depth, highlighting the pioneering agents that established CDK4/6 inhibition as a major therapeutic paradigm. To date, more than 800 clinical trials have investigated CDK4/6 inhibitors across diverse tumour types and treatment settings, both as monotherapies and in combination strategies. The key findings that have shaped our current understanding of CDK4/6 inhibitor efficacy are summarised below.

## Trends and selection criteria in clinical trials
A major challenge in the clinical development of CDK4/6 inhibitors is identifying patient populations most likely to achieve sustained benefit. Currently, enrolment strategies often prioritise tumours with intact RB protein, inactive CDKN2A, or overexpression/amplification of CDK4/6 or cyclin D1 [ ]. However, these criteria alone have proven insufficient for reliably predicting therapeutic response. Co-existing genomic alterations and pathway aberrations can markedly modify sensitivity to CDK4/6 inhibition. For instance, TP53 mutations have been associated with attenuated responses to CDK4/6 inhibitors [ ], and recent evidence suggests that TP53 loss or MDM2 amplification may compromise long-term disease control. These findings are consistent with preclinical models demonstrating that p53-deficient cancer cells escape CDK4/6 inhibitor-induced G1 arrest by reactivating CDK2-dependent proliferation and evading senescence [ ].
The lack of robust molecular selection criteria has also contributed to an uneven distribution of clinical trial activity across tumour types. Breast cancer accounts for most studies (68.4%), followed by lung cancer (4.9%) and ovarian cancer (3.7%) (Fig.  ). The dominance of breast cancer reflects the high frequency of CCND1 amplification/overexpression and the critical role of cyclin D1-CDK4/6 signalling in tumour progression and endocrine resistance [ ]. Within this domain, HR+/HER2− subtype overwhelmingly drives trial activity, whereas HER2⁺ disease and TNBC have been less frequently explored (Fig.  ). Historically, TNBC was deprioritised due to early signals suggesting limited benefit (NCT01037790; NCT01394016); however, emerging data now show that the luminal androgen receptor (LAR) subtype exhibits meaningful vulnerability to CDK4/6 inhibition [ ], broadening therapeutic possibilities for a group traditionally managed with intensive cytotoxic regimens.
Collectively, these trends highlight an urgent need for more refined, biologically driven patient-selection strategies. Several ongoing trials (NCT02154490; NCT03195192; NCT05452213; NCT06561022; NCT06630325) now incorporate genomic profiling and molecular stratification to optimise enrolment and identify biomarkers predictive of durable response. Such approaches will be essential for defining the true therapeutic scope of CDK4/6 inhibitors across diverse malignancies.

 

## Trends in clinical trial regimens
Across breast cancer studies, most trials (95.2%) have evaluated CDK4/6 inhibitors in combination regimens with ET, HER2-targeted agents, targeted therapies, immunotherapies, chemotherapies, and radiotherapy (Fig.  ). Among these, ET-based strategies constitute the most intensively investigated approach.
Endocrine therapy.
CDK4/6 inhibitors are most frequently combined with ET, including oestrogen receptor modulators, aromatase inhibitors, SERDs, and luteinising hormone-releasing hormone (LH-RH) agonists, to suppress ER-driven proliferation (Fig.  ). This strategy has been assessed across first-line, second-line, neoadjuvant, and adjuvant settings.

## 1) First- and second-line treatment
Pivotal trials such as PALOMA-2, PALOMA-3, MONALEESA-2, MONALEESA-3, MONALEESA-7, MONARCH-2, MONARCH-3, and DAWNA-2 have consistently shown that adding a CDK4/6 inhibitor to ET nearly doubles PFS in patients with HR+/HER2− metastatic breast cancer [ ]. Notably, these combinations achieve PFS outcomes comparable to chemotherapy, but with a milder toxicity profile (NCT02028507; NCT03839823), reinforcing their application as preferred treatment options.
Given these successes, CDK4/6 inhibitors have also been explored in HER2-enriched tumours, where intrinsic endocrine resistance is common. A pooled analysis from the MONALEESA reported improved OS in HER2+ breast cancer treated with ribociclib [ ]. However, benefit remains uncertain, as a retrospective analysis of palbociclib did not replicate this finding [ ]. The ongoing phase III HARMONIA trial (NCT05207709) is directly comparing ribociclib and palbociclib, both with ET, to clarify their relative activity in the HER2-enriched subtype.

## 2) Adjuvant treatment
CDK4/6 inhibitors combined with ET have also been assessed as adjuvant therapy in early-stage HR+ breast cancer. This strategy was motivated by the substantial OS improvements seen with CDK4/6 inhibition in metastatic disease.
The monarchE trial (NCT03155997) showed that abemaciclib plus ET significantly improved invasive disease-free survival (iDFS), with greatest benefit in node-positive and high-risk patients, leading to FDA approval of adjuvant abemaciclib for this population [ ].
Similarly, the NATALEE trial (NCT03701334) reported that ribociclib plus ET reduced the risk of recurrence in stage II/III HR+/HER2− breast cancer. With four years of follow-up, 62.8% of patients completed the scheduled three-year ribociclib course, achieving an iDFS rate of 88.5% compared with 83.6% for ET alone. Unlike monarchE, NATALEE enrolled a broader, intermediate- to high-risk population, which supported FDA approval for ribociclib across a range of early-stage disease [ ].
The resulting differences in adjuvant indications reflect the distinct designs of the monarchE and NATALEE trials. Abemaciclib remains restricted to high-risk and node-positive disease, while ribociclib is approved for a broader population. This wider indication is particularly advantageous for intermediate-risk patients with lymph-node involvement, who would otherwise require axillary dissection [ ]. Owing to these differences, a new study (NCT04565054) is assessing abemaciclib plus ET in patients with intermediate- to high-risk clinical or genomic risk.
In contrast, palbociclib has not demonstrated adjuvant benefit. The PALLAS (NCT02513394) and PENELOPE-B (NCT01864746) trials reported no improvement with palbociclib plus ET in patients with stage II–III ER+/HER2− disease or residual disease after neoadjuvant chemotherapy. These findings align with the lack of statistically significant OS benefit observed in metastatic trials (NCT01740427; NCT01942135). Nonetheless, several studies, including Appalaches (NCT03609047), POLAR (NCT03820830), and TRAK-ER (NCT04985266), continue to investigate whether selected subgroups might derive adjuvant benefit. Adjuvant trials of dalpiciclib (NCT04842617) and TQB3616 (NCT05780567) in HR+/HER2− disease are also underway.
3) Neoadjuvant treatment: Multiple clinical trials have evaluated the neoadjuvant use of CDK4/6 inhibitors in combination with ET, including PALLET (NCT02296801), NeoPalAna (NCT01723774), SAFIA (NCT03447132), neoMONARCH (NCT02441946), coopERA (NCT04436744), FELINE (NCT02712723), and CORALLEEN (NCT03248427). Several studies, most notably neoMONARCH (NCT02441946), PALLET (NCT02296801), and FELINE (NCT02712723), have reported greater reductions in Ki67 and higher rates of complete cell cycle arrest (CCCA) with CDK4/6 inhibitors plus ET compared with ET alone. The single-arm DANCER trial also demonstrated robust biological responses with dalpiciclib plus an aromatase inhibitor in luminal B/HER2− disease: 86.7% patients achieved CCCA at two weeks, and 60% demonstrated partial responses at the time of surgery (NCT05640778).
Neoadjuvant CDK4/6 inhibition has also been benchmarked against chemotherapy. The NeoPAL and CORALLEEN trials reported palbociclib- or ribociclib-based regimens achieved efficacy comparable to standard neoadjuvant chemotherapy, while offering more favourable safety profiles [ , ].
Despite these encouraging findings, the clinical value of neoadjuvant CDK4/6 inhibition remains uncertain. In PALLET trials, although the addition of palbociclib to letrozole reduced tumour cell proliferation, it did not improve pathological complete response, a finding attributed to its limited induction of apoptosis. This highlights a potential limitation that the cytostatic nature of CDK4/6 inhibitors may compromise their capacity as neoadjuvant therapy, particularly in slow-growing tumours [ ]. Moreover, several studies, including NeoPalAna, neoMONARCH, and coopERA, have reported Ki67 rebound after treatment discontinuation, raising concerns regarding the durability of response [ ].
Taken together, these observations underscore the need for long-term follow-up and refined biomarker-driven patient selection to better define the role of CDK4/6 inhibitors in the neoadjuvant setting [ , ].

HER2-directed therapies

In HER2-expressing breast cancer, interest has increasingly focused on combining CDK4/6 inhibitors with HER2-targeted agents, particularly in luminal ER+/HER2+ tumours where crosstalk between ER and HER2 signalling contributes to therapeutic resistance. The monarcHER trial (NCT02675231) demonstrated that the triplet regimen of abemaciclib, trastuzumab, and fulvestrant significantly improved PFS compared with chemotherapy. While the PFS improvement was limited to the triplet regimen, updated analyses showed that both the triplet and the doublet of abemaciclib plus trastuzumab conferred an OS advantage, with the largest gains seen in luminal HER2+ disease [ ].
Similarly, the phase II PATRICIA trial (NCT02448420) reported that the triplet regimen of palbociclib, trastuzumab, and letrozole improved PFS in both ER+/HER2+ and ER−/HER2+ metastatic breast cancer, with the greatest benefit observed in luminal subtypes. These findings prompted expansion cohorts in patients with HR+/HER2+ luminal disease, comparing the palbociclib-trastuzumab-ET combination with physician’s-choice trastuzumab regimens. In this setting, the palbociclib-based combination yielded longer median PFS and higher ORR (18.9% vs. 8.3%).
Building on these findings, the phase III PATINA trial (NCT02947685) is evaluating the addition of palbociclib to trastuzumab and ET in patients with HER2-positive metastatic breast cancer. Preliminary data indicate a substantial improvement in median PFS (44.3 vs. 29.1 months). Additional trials exploring palbociclib in combination with HER2-directed therapies are also ongoing (NCT05969184; NCT03304080).
Ribociclib has also shown encouraging activity. In a phase Ib/II study (NCT02657343), ribociclib plus trastuzumab was well tolerated. Interim data from the ongoing DETECT V trial (NCT02344472) suggest that the triplet of ribociclib, goserelin, and HER2-targeted therapy yielded milder toxicity while achieving comparable PFS and OS outcomes to chemotherapy. Additional trials are investigating ribociclib in combination with other HER2-directed agents, such as trastuzumab emtansine, trastuzumab, and pertuzumab (NCT06481956; NCT05900206).
Dalpiciclib has recently been tested in HER2+ metastatic breast cancer. In the DAP-Her-01 trial (NCT04293276), dalpiciclib plus pyrotinib improved PFS, with a greater therapeutic benefit observed in HR-negative patients. Likewise, the PLEASURABLE trial (NCT03772353) reported strong activity of dalpiciclib-pyrotinib-ET with a median PFS of 22.0 months, PRR of 70.2%, and clinical benefit rate of 87.2%. Based on these findings, the follow-up DAP-Her-02 trial (NCT05328440) is comparing triplet regimens tailored to HR status: dalpiciclib-pyrotinib-fulvestrant in HR+/HER2+ tumours versus dalpiciclib-pyrotinib-inetetamab in HR−/HER2+ disease.
Given their ability to cross the BBB to varying degrees, CDK4/6 inhibitors are also being explored for brain metastases, although results have been mixed. Palbociclib has shown intracranial activity in a small patient cohort [ ]. In a trial of palbociclib, tucatinib, and letrozole (NCT03054363), patients with brain metastases achieved a median PFS of 10.1 months, compared with 8.7 months in those without CNS involvement. Another interim analysis reported that a quadruple regimen of palbociclib, fulvestrant, trastuzumab, and pyrotinib was safe and yielded clinically meaningful systemic and intracranial responses with a median PFS of 10.6 months [ ]. However, other studies such as NCT02774681 found no benefit from palbociclib plus trastuzumab. Similar discrepancies have been observed with abemaciclib. Although abemaciclib achieved substantial drug concentrations in the CNS, its combination with trastuzumab did not elicit intracranial response in HER2+ patients [ ]. Collectively, these data suggest that effective CNS activity may require co-administration of CNS-penetrant HER2-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors to provide synergistic therapeutic benefit [ ].

Targeted inhibitors

Combination strategies involving targeted agents represent the second most common approach in CDK4/6 inhibitor trials in breast cancer, accounting for 18.9% of studies. Nearly half of these (9.6%) evaluate dual PI3K and CDK4/6 blockade (Fig.  ), reflecting strong mechanistic rationale that inhibiting the CDK4/6–cyclin D1 axis can restore sensitivity to PI3K inhibitors and overcome adaptive resistance in PIK3CA-mutant cancers [ ]. Several clinical trials have investigated CDK4/6 inhibitors with PI3K pathway inhibitors, including buparlisib (NCT02154776), copanlisib (NCT03939897), and capivasertib (NCT04862663). Among these, the PIPA trial (NCT02389842) reported encouraging activity from the triple combination of palbociclib, fulvestrant, and taselisib in ER+/HER2−, PIK3CA-mutant advanced breast cancer. However, regimens incorporating first-generation PI3K inhibitors with CDK4/6 inhibitors have been limited by dose-limiting toxicities, including severe hepatobiliary events, infections, and high-grade neutropenia (NCT02088684).
To address these limitations, attention has shifted toward mutant-selective PIK3CA inhibitors and degraders, which spare wild-type PI3K and offer improved tolerability. The most clinically advanced is inavolisib, a PIK3CA-mutant degrader that has demonstrated robust efficacy (NCT03006172; NCT04191499; NCT03424005) and has recently received FDA approval in combination with palbociclib and fulvestrant for PIK3CA-mutant breast cancer. Following this success, multiple trials are investigating next-generation allosteric PIK3CA inhibitors, including RLY-2608 (NCT05216432; NCT01872260), STX-478 (NCT05768139), RLY-5836 (NCT05759949), and LOXO-783 (NCT05307705) in tumours enriched for PIK3CA mutations or PTEN loss.
Beyond PI3K pathway inhibition, a range of targeted agents have been evaluated in combination with CDK4/6 inhibitors. These include venetoclax (NCT03900884), olaparib (NCT03685331), niraparib (NCT04481113), fluzoparib (NCT05891093), bevacizumab (NCT06307249), hydroxychloroquine (NCT05953350; NCT04523857), xentuzumab (NCT03099174), ZEN003694 (NCT05372640), binimetinib (NCT04494958; NCT05564377), VS-6766 (NCT05608252), and belinostat (NCT04315233). Most studies remain in early clinical phases, and efficacy signals have not yet emerged. Nevertheless, establishing safety, tolerability, and optimal scheduling will be essential to determine whether co-targeting complementary pathways can broaden or deepen responses to CDK4/6 blockade.

Immunotherapy

Immune-checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) represent another promising class of agents being combined with CDK4/6 inhibitors, particularly in immune-cold tumours, where insufficient baseline T-cell infiltration limits anti-tumour immune surveillance. The rationale for this strategy derives from the immune-modulatory properties of CDK4/6 inhibitors, which include enhanced tumour antigen presentation, increased immunogenic cell death, activation of CD8⁺ T cells, expansion of memory T cells, infiltration of NK-cells and B-cells, and suppression of Tregs and myeloid-derived suppressor cells [ – ].
CDK4/6 inhibitors have been evaluated with various ICIs, including pembrolizumab (NCT02779751; NCT02778685; NCT06027268), avelumab (NCT04360941; NCT03147287; NCT03573648; NCT04841148), nivolumab (NCT04075604), ONO-4578 (NCT06570031), atezolizumab (NCT03280563; NCT04802759), adebrelimab (NCT06149130; NCT06599216), and LY3300054 (NCT02791334). Most studies remain in early clinical phases and results are still emerging.
Although preliminary findings indicate potential improved efficacy of ICIs, toxicity remains a major challenge. The NEWFLAME trial reported a high incidence of severe immune-related adverse events, such as transaminase elevation and interstitial lung disease or pneumonitis, when abemaciclib was combined with nivolumab and ET [ ]. These toxicities may be driven by elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g. IL-11 and TNF) and profound Tregs suppression; while enhancing anti-tumour immunity, they may also trigger immune activation in normal tissue [ ].
Additional safety concerns related to haematologic and hepatotoxic toxicities. Neutropenia is an on-target effect of CDK4/6 inhibitors and may be exacerbated by ICIs [ ]. Moreover, as both classes undergo hepatic metabolism, their combination can increase the risk of liver injury [ ]. These mechanistic liabilities have manifested clinically: combination regimens such as ribociclib plus spartalizumab in HR+/HER2– breast cancer were halted due to hepatotoxicity [ ], and the neoadjuvant application of palbociclib, nivolumab, and anastrozole was not pursued further because of grade 3/4 hepatotoxicity (NCT04075604). Similar toxicity-related closures have occurred in HNSCC (NCT03938337), glioma (NCT04220892), and HCC (NCT03781960) trials. These collectively highlight the need for careful toxicity monitoring and optimised dosing strategies when combining CDK4/6 inhibitors with ICIs.

Chemotherapy

The combination of CDK4/6 inhibitors with chemotherapy has been explored in a relatively small number of clinical trials. Conceptually, enthusiasm has been tempered by concerning mechanistic antagonism, as CDK4/6 inhibition induces G1 arrest, theoretically reducing the efficacy of S- or M-phase-dependent cytotoxic agents [ ]. However, no antagonistic interaction has so far been observed. Instead, preclinical studies indicate that CDK4/6 inhibitors may enhance chemosensitivity by suppressing the E2F1 transcription programme and facilitating chemotherapy-induced apoptosis [ ]. Several clinical trials have incorporated CDK4/6 inhibitors into standard chemotherapy regimens across multiple malignancies. In breast cancer, combinations have been tested with paclitaxel (NCT01320592; NCT02599363), cisplatin (NCT02897375), capecitabine (NCT02754011), and docetaxel (NCT02599363).
Another rationale for combining CDK4/6 inhibitors with chemotherapy is to protect HSPCs, a strategy that has been tested with the transient CDK4/6 inhibition by trilaciclib [ ]. When administered prior to chemotherapy, trilaciclib induces a reversible G1 arrest in HSPCs and has demonstrated mitigation of myelosuppression in patients with SCLC (NCT04902885; NCT02499770; NCT02514447; NCT03041311). This approach has also been investigated in TNBC prior to carboplatin-gemcitabine treatment. Unlike in SCLC, trilaciclib did not show a significant myeloid-protective effect in TNBC patients; however, its pre-administration improved OS, suggesting a potential synergistic effect between trilaciclib and chemotherapy (NCT04799249; NCT05112536). Part of this benefit may reflect the immunomodulatory properties of CDK4/6 inhibition when combined with chemotherapy [ ]. Further studies will clarify the impact of trilaciclib on chemotherapy outcomes in TNBC (NCT05112536; NCT04799249; NCT05978648).

Radiotherapy

The ability of CDK4/6 inhibitors to penetrate the BBB, together with evidence of radiosensitizing activity in preclinical models [ ], has increased interest in their combination with radiotherapy, particularly for patients with brain metastases. However, toxicity remains a major concern. Case reports and small series have documented severe radiation-associated adverse events, including fatal (grade V) pneumonitis following palbociclib administration months after thoracic irradiation and radiation-recall dermatitis triggered by abemaciclib in previously irradiated HR+/HER2− breast cancer patients [ , ].
Despite these, several studies indicate that concurrent administration of CDK4/6 inhibitors and radiotherapy can be delivered safely, with acceptable tolerability and no unexpected toxicities [ – ]. To minimize potential risk, Bosacki et al. recommended holding CDK4/6 inhibitors for approximately five drug half-lives before and after radiation exposure [ ]. Incorporating such precautions, ongoing trials are now assessing CDK4/6 inhibition and radiotherapy in patients with brain metastases (NCT04923542).

## Current application of CDK4/6 inhibitors in clinical practice
Based on promising clinical trial evidence, three CDK4/6 inhibitors, palbociclib, ribociclib, and abemaciclib, have been approved for the treatment of HR+/HER2− metastatic breast cancer as first- and second-line therapies. The incorporation of CDK4/6 inhibitors into routine practice has increased rapidly; by 2022, these agents were included in approximately 72.2% of treatment regimens for HR+/HER2− metastatic disease [ ]. Their use has been associated with a 21% reduction in the risk of death in ET-resistant disease and a 27% reduction in ET-sensitive patients [ ]. Currently, the main factor limiting CDK4/6 inhibitor use is organ failure or significant impaired hepatic function, in which case chemotherapy remains the preferred alternative [ ].
Although all three CDK4/6 inhibitors demonstrated comparable improvements in PFS for metastatic breast cancer, treatment selection in clinical practice is primarily guided by aligning each agent’s toxicity profile with patient comorbidities. Palbociclib remains widely used, in part due to its earlier approval and established real-world experience [ ]. For the ET partner, aromatase inhibitors are generally preferred in the first-line setting, whereas fulvestrant and, increasingly, SERDsS are used following progression or demonstrated endocrine resistance [ ].
Beyond the metastatic setting, CDK4/6 inhibitors have expanded into early-stage HR+/HER2− breast cancer. Abemaciclib is approved as adjuvant therapy for high-risk, node-positive disease [ ], and ribociclib has recently received approval for use in early-stage HR+/HER2– breast cancer. Additionally, trilaciclib has gained approval as a myeloprotective agent when administered prior to etoposide/carboplatin- or topotecan-containing regimens in SCLC.

## Challenges of CDK4/6 inhibitors

## Dose-limiting toxicity
The toxicity profiles of CDK4/6 inhibitors reflect intrinsic differences in target selectivity, pharmacokinetics, and tissue distribution [ ]. Haematologic toxicity, particularly neutropenia, is the most frequent and clinically significant adverse effect associated with this drug class [ ]. Neutropenia is commonly graded as follows: grade I-II (absolute neutrophil count (ANC) < 1000/mm³), grade III (500 < ANC < 1000/mm³), and grade IV (ANC < 500/mm³) [ ]. Given the role of CDK6 in myeloid progenitor proliferation [ ], inhibitors with greater CDK6 inhibition, such as palbociclib and ribociclib, produce pronounced cytopenia, whereas abemaciclib, being slightly more CDK4-selective, causes relatively milder myelosuppression [ , ].
Gastrointestinal toxicity is a common adverse effect of abemaciclib [ ]. In the monarchE trial, 82.2% of patients who received adjuvant abemaciclib experienced diarrhoea, which in some cases reached grade III severity. Nevertheless, this toxicity is manageable with antidiarrhoeal medication or dose adjustment [ ]. Mechanistically, abemaciclib’s gastrointestinal effects arise from its broader kinase inhibition, including activity against CDK9, GSK-3β, and CaMKIIα/γ, which are implicated in epithelial renewal and intestinal motility [ ].
Abemaciclib has also been associated with an increased risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE), particularly when administered with tamoxifen [ ]. Therefore, premenopausal patients, who more frequently receive tamoxifen, should undergo careful VTE assessment prior to receiving abemaciclib.
Ribociclib carries specific restrictions when combined with QT-prolonging medications such as tamoxifen (NCT02278120; NCT01958021). Mechanistic studies reveal that ribociclib downregulates KCNH2 (encoding the hERG potassium channel) while upregulates SCN5A and SNTA1 (encoding Nav1.5 and the scaffolding protein syntrophin-α1, respectively), collectively disrupting cardiac electrophysiological balance and contributing to cardiotoxicity [ ]. Although this is typically reversible and manageable with dose adjustments, it necessitates electrocardiographic monitoring during the first two treatment cycles [ ].
In addition to early-onset toxicities, CDK4/6 inhibitors are associated with delayed toxicities, including drug-induced hepatic toxicity and metabolic disturbance. Hepatic toxicity, characterized by elevation in alanine and aspartate aminotransferase, is generally reversible upon drug discontinuation [ ], but its incidence varies across agents, occurring most frequently with ribociclib, followed by abemaciclib, and less commonly with palbociclib [ ]. Although the precise mechanisms remain under investigation, CYP3A4-mediated hepatic metabolism may generate reactive or immunogenic intermediates capable of inducing hepatocellular injury. In the MONALEESA-2 trials, several cases exhibited autoimmune-like pattern, suggesting an immune-mediated component. Furthermore, ribociclib inhibits key hepatic transporters, including the bile salt export pump and certain basolateral efflux systems, potentially increasing the risk of cholestasis and contributing to the jaundice observed in some patients [ ]. These risks underscore the need for regular liver function monitoring, particularly during prolonged therapy in adjuvant settings [ ]. The concomitant administration of CYP3A4 inhibitors should also be avoided [ ].
Metabolic toxicity is another important, though less frequent, consequence of long-term CDK4/6 inhibition. Pancreatic b-cells rely on CDK4 for proliferation and maintenance of insulin-producing capacity [ , ]. Preclinical evidence shows that prolonged palbociclib exposure can lead to of pancreatic b-cells degeneration, hypoinsulinemia, glucose intolerance and hyperglycaemia. Although clinically rare, this toxicity warrants attention, particularly in younger patients and those with pre-existing metabolic risk factors [ ].

## Development of drug resistance
Clinical studies indicate that 25–35% of patients with HR+/HER2– metastatic breast cancer have experienced disease progression following CDK4/6 inhibition [ ]. Resistance can be intrinsic, characterised by minimal or no clinical benefit and recurrence within approximately three months, or acquired, emerging after an initial period of disease control [ , ]. In the PALOMA-2 trial, more than 30% of patients developed acquired resistance to palbociclib plus letrozole within two years, with this figure rising to over 70% after 40 months (NCT01740427).
Multiple ongoing clinical studies are employing circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) profiling and integrated multi-omic analyses to elucidate the mechanisms driving resistance (NCT04653740; NCT03281902; NCT03195192). Current findings suggest that acquired resistance emerges primarily through two overarching mechanisms: (i) rewiring of the cell cycle machinery, often involving genetic or transcriptional alterations affecting key regulators of cell cycle such as RB1, CDK2, or cyclin E; and (ii) activation of compensatory survival pathways, which can bypass CDK4/6 dependency and maintain proliferative signalling. Other mechanisms, although less frequently observed, also contribute to therapeutic failure. These include lysosomal sequestration of CDK4/6 inhibitors, which reduces effective intracellular drug concentration [ , ], epigenetic remodelling affecting E2F1 target gene expression [ ], and microenvironmental adaptations that promote proliferative escape [ ]. Collectively, these processes underscore the complexity of resistance and highlight the urgent need for new strategies capable of overcoming or preventing resistance to CDK4/6 targeted therapy.

(i) Rewiring of cell cycle machinery

Alterations in RB1 and BRCA2

As the central downstream effector of CDK4/6-cyclin D signalling, loss of RB protein is a well-established mechanism of resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors. In the PALOMA-3 study, 4.7% of HR+ breast cancer patients treated with palbociclib plus fulvestrant acquired RB1 mutations at the time of disease progression [ ]. In another study, acquired RB1 alterations were detected in ctDNA from patients receiving palbociclib or ribociclib. These included splicing-site substitutions in exons 8 and 22, exon 19 deletion, exon 3 insertion, and the H483Y mutation in exon 16, none of which were presented in baseline specimens [ ].
Evidence also suggests a correlation between germline BRCA2 mutations and RB1 allele deletion in mediating resistance. Analyses of tumour samples from patients with CDK4/6 inhibitors resistance revealed a strong association between germline BRCA2 mutations and deletion or loss of the RB1 allele. Given the proximity of the BRCA2 and RB1 loci on chromosome 13, BRCA2 germline mutations may predispose the adjacent RB1 to additional somatic alterations during treatment. This genomic vulnerability provides a mechanistic rationale for the shorter PFS observed in patients with germline BRCA2 mutations when treated with CDK4/6 inhibitors and ET [ ].

Alterations in cyclin E1/2-CDK2

Overexpression of E-type cyclins is a key mechanism of resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors [ , ]. Clinical evidence from the POP (NCT02008734) and PALOMA-3 (NCT01942135) trials demonstrated that CCNE1 and CCNE2 overexpression is associated with reduced responsiveness to palbociclib as monotherapy and in combination with fulvestrant. Similar findings have been reported in NeoPalAna (NCT01723774) and MONALEESA-7 (NCT02278120) trials, showing cyclin E1 upregulation in patients developed resistance to palbociclib and ribociclib. Notably, CCNE2 amplification was detected in 14.6% of biopsies obtained from patients with acquired resistance to CDK4/6 inhibition, further illustrating the clinical relevance of cyclin E pathway activation [ ].
Increased CDK2 expression has also been linked to CDK4/6 inhibitor resistance. Both clinical and preclinical models have shown that cancer cells with elevated levels of CDK2, E-type cyclins, or p16INK4A can bypass the G1 arrest induced by CDK4/6 inhibitors, sustaining proliferation and limiting long-term benefit [ ]. This occurs through activation of the cyclin E–CDK2 complex, which phosphorylates RB and other substrates independently of CDK4/6, effectively restoring E2F-driven S-phase entry.

Alterations in cyclin D and CDK6

Dysregulation of D-type cyclins and CDK4/6 can also contribute to resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors, although their roles appear less well established than those of RB1 loss or cyclin E-CDK2 activation. Nevertheless, several acquired alterations observed in resistant tumours implicate this axis in therapeutic escape.
Data from the NeoPalAna, PALOMA-1, and PALOMA-3 trials have reported CCND3 upregulation and CCND1 amplification in tumour biopsies obtained from patients who progressed on therapy [ ]. However, these alterations have not shown a consistent or robust correlation with clinical outcomes, suggesting that their contribution to resistance may be context-dependent or secondary to broader signalling changes [ ].
Amplification or upregulation of CDK6 has also been detected in preclinical models of acquired resistance to abemaciclib and ribociclib [ , ]. To date, however, recurrent CDK6-activating mutations have not been identified in patient samples. Instead, CDK6 overexpression in clinical settings appears to arise primarily from hyperactivation of mitogenic pathways, including enhanced PI3K/Akt, FGFR, or JAK/STAT signalling, which collectively increase CDK6 transcription and promote bypass of CDK4/6 blockade [ ].

Alterations in other cell cycle-related proteins

Kinase rewiring represents another mechanism by which cancer cells evade CDK4/6 inhibition. Preclinical studies have identified the upregulation of CDK7, c-Myc, WEE1, and AURKA as mediators of resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors [ , ]. Consistently with these findings, AURKA amplification, a key regulator of mitotic spindle formation and G2-M transition, has been detected in 27% of HR+ breast cancer patients who developed resistance to CDK4/6-based therapies [ ].
Genomic alterations in MYC also appear to contribute to therapeutic escape. In the MONARCH-3 and nextMONARCH-1 trials, acquired MYC mutations were found in 5% and 9% of patients, respectively, who progressed on abemaciclib [ ]. Similarly, the MONALEESA-7 trial (NCT02278120) reported MYC upregulation in tumour samples obtained at the time of resistance to ribociclib.
Mechanistic studies suggest that c-Myc promotes resistance by expressing the E3 ubiquitin ligase KLHL42, which targets RB for proteasomal degradation, thereby functionally mimicking RB protein loss of function. This mechanism explains the observed correlation between MYC amplification and RB loss-of-function across various tumour types [ ].

(ii) Activation of compensatory oncogenic signalling pathways

Alterations in the FGFR1 and RAS GTPase pathways

Activation of the FGFR1 and RAS pathways has been documented in patients who developed resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors. The PALOMA-3 and MONALEESA-2 trials have identified FGFR1 amplification as a mechanism associated with resistance to palbociclib and ribociclib, respectively [ , ]. In SCLC, secretion of FGF ligands induces resistance by upregulating CDK6, cyclin D1, and cyclin E1 [ ]. Emerging mutations in the RAS GTPase family, e.g. KRAS (G12D and Q61L), HRAS (K117N), as well as amplification of NRAS, have also been identified in approximately 10% of HR+ breast cancer patients with acquired resistance to palbociclib plus fulvestrant [ ]. Once activated, FGFR1 and RAS signalling recruit downstream PI3K/Akt/mTOR and MAPK pathways to re-establish the proliferative drive needed to bypass CDK4/6 inhibition [ , ].

Alterations in the PI3K and MAPK pathways

Both the MAPK and PI3K signalling cascades have been implicated in acquired resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors. In prostate adenocarcinoma and metastatic CRPC, constitutive MAPK activation was observed in 47% of patients and correlated with loss of sensitivity to palbociclib [ ]. Increasing evidence from clinical and clinicopathogenomic studies also indicates that alterations in PIK3CA, AKT1, and PTEN frequently co-occur in tumours progressing on CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy, with PTEN loss showing the strongest association with rapid progression and endocrine resistance [ ].
Acquired loss of PTEN has been identified in ER+/HER2− breast cancer patients who developed resistance to ribociclib (NCT01872260). PTEN protein loss sustains Akt activity, promoting the assembly of the CDK4/6-cyclin D1 complex by p27 phosphorylation [ ]. Acquisition of AKT1 amplification or AKT1 activating mutation (W80R) has also been found in post-progression biopsies from HR+ breast cancer patients [ ].
Beyond these, mTOR hyperactivation and upregulation of PDK1, have been shown in preclinical models of resistance [ , ]. Although these alterations have not yet been confirmed in clinical samples, their emergence in resistant cell lines suggests their potential biological relevance.
Alterations in other mediators: FAT1, TGF-β, and ESR1
Beyond classical mitogenic pathways, alterations in other regulators can also enable bypass of CDK4/6 inhibition. Rare loss-of-function mutations in FAT1 disrupt the Hippo axis, leading to nuclear accumulation of YAP/TAZ and consequent CDK6 upregulation [ ]. Clinically, missense mutations or biallelic inactivation of FAT1 have been associated with reduced efficacy of CDK4/6 inhibitors in ER+ breast cancer [ ].
Dysregulation of oestrogen signalling may also influence resistance. Because ER signalling regulates cyclin D1 transcription, alterations in ESR1 could reduce the effectiveness of CDK4/6 inhibitors, however, clinical evidence remains inconclusive. In the MONALEESA-2, MONARCH-2, and PALOMA-3 trials, baseline ESR1 mutations did not predict PFS on CDK4/6-based therapies. In contrast, end-of-treatment ctDNA analysis from PALOMA-3 revealed emergence of ESR1 Y537S mutation in 12.8% of patients, suggesting that while pre-existing ESR1 mutations do not impair CDK4/6 inhibitor efficacy, their acquisition during therapy may contribute to disease progression [ ].

## Strategies to overcome the limitations of CDK4/6 inhibitors
Optimising the clinical utility of CDK4/6 inhibitors requires strategies that both prevent or delay drug resistance and expand the therapeutic window by minimising haematologic toxicity.

## Strategies to overcome drug resistance
Preventing disease recurrence following CDK4/6-based therapies requires a two-pronged approach: (i) identifying reliable biomarkers that can predict early resistance or treatment failure; (ii) tailoring subsequent therapy according to the patient’s evolving molecular profile.

i) Identifying biomarkers

Reliable biomarkers capable of forecasting the response duration to CDK4/6 inhibitors are essential for personalised therapy. One promising biomarker is thymidine kinase-1 (TK1), a key enzyme in the pyrimidine salvage pathway and a surrogate marker of cell proliferation. Several studies (NCT01322893; NCT05977036; NCT02549430; NCT01723774; NCT03439046; NCT02536742) have shown that serum TK1 activity correlates with clinical outcomes with levels rising as resistance emerges [ ]. However, because TK1 primarily reflects proliferative status rather than specific genomic alterations, it is not sufficient for identifying actionable mutations or pathway aberrations driving therapeutic escape. Thus, liquid biopsy-based tools, such as CTC, ctDNA, ctRNA, and whole blood RNA, offer more information and a non-invasive approach to longitudinal molecular monitoring.
A landmark example is the PADA1 trial (NCT03079011), which utilized ctDNA to detect emerging ESR1 mutations during first-line therapy with palbociclib plus aromatase inhibitors. Patients with newly detected mutations were switched to fulvestrant before disease progression, resulting in improved PFS compared with remaining on initial therapy. This proof-of-concept study prompted other trials to incorporate liquid biopsy-based molecular stratification to optimise first- and second-line therapy (NCT04289974; NCT03065621; NCT05826964; NCT05452213; NCT05362760). Among these, the TIRESIAS trial (NCT04660435) is analysing ctDNA from patients receiving CDK4/6 inhibitor plus aromatase inhibitor to identify biomarkers of resistance across treatment lines. Similarly, the PALPETBIO trial (NCT03560856) was designed to investigate genomic and transcriptomic changes in patients treated with palbociclib plus fulvestrant after progression on first-line therapy, aiming to characterize resistance mechanisms in the second-line setting.

ii) Selecting an appropriate second-line treatment

With advances in biomarker discovery and genomic profiling, current clinical guidelines for metastatic breast cancer increasingly emphasise molecularly informed second-line therapy. Specifically, testing for somatic PIK3CA, AKT1, and PTEN alterations, ESR1 mutations, and germline BRCA1/2 status is recommended to guide subsequent treatment selection [ ]. Despite these recommendations, real-world decision-making is often more complex. In patients with aggressive disease, visceral crisis, or significant comorbidities like impaired organ function, clinicians may have to prioritise chemotherapy even when actionable alterations are present. In tumours with PI3K/Akt pathway alterations: incorporation of PI3K/Akt/mTOR inhibitors

Based on clinical observations showing that PI3K pathway alterations contribute to disease progression during endocrine and CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy [ , ], multiple clinical trials have evaluated PI3K pathway targeted agents following treatment progression. These include the SOLAR-1 (NCT02437318), BYLieve (NCT03056755), CAPitello-291 (NCT04305496), TAKTIC (NCT03959891), VICTORIA-1 (NCT05501886), and additional studies assessing alpelisib (PI3Kα inhibitor), capivasertib (AKT inhibitor), ipatasertib (AKT inhibitor), everolimus (mTOR inhibitor), and gedatolisib (pan-PI3K/mTOR inhibitor).
The SOLAR-1 and BYLieve trials established alpelisib plus fulvestrant as a second-line treatment for PIK3CA-mutated breast cancer following progression on ET (Fig.  ). Since alpelisib is associated with hyperglycaemia, careful patient selection is essential, particularly in patients with pre-existing diabetes or elevated baseline glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c).
Another key study in this setting is the CAPItello-291 trial, which evaluated capivasertib in combination with fulvestrant in patients with HR+/HER2− advanced breast cancer who experienced disease progression after aromatase inhibitor therapy, with or without prior exposure to CDK4/6 inhibitors. In patients harbouring PI3K/Akt pathway alterations, this combination significantly improved median PFS compared to placebo plus fulvestrant group (3.1 vs. 7.3 months). These results led to the FDA approval of capivasertib in combination with fulvestrant for HR+/HER2− advanced breast cancer with PI3K/Akt pathway alterations following prior ET [ ].

In tumours harbouring ESR1 mutations: incorporation of SERD

The PADA-1 trial (NCT03079011) was the first randomised study to demonstrate that a ctDNA-guided early switch from aromatase inhibitor to fulvestrant, while continuing palbociclib, can delay progression in patients with emerging ESR1 mutations (Fig.  ). These findings support early molecularly guided intervention for ESR1-driven resistance rather than waiting for overt clinical progression. The clinical activity of SERDs was subsequently established in the EMERALD (NCT03778931) and EMBER-3 (NCT04975308) trials, which led to the regulatory approval of elacestrant and imlunestrant for patients with HR+/HER2−, ESR1-mutant advanced or metastatic breast cancer after ET [ , ].
Building on this concept, the phase III SERENA-6 trial (NCT04964934) showed that switching to camizestrant while continuing the same CDK4/6 inhibitor significantly prolonged PFS compared with continued aromatase inhibitor therapy (16.0 vs. 9.2 months), further supporting early intervention strategies targeting ESR1-driven resistance.

In tumours harbouring germline BRCA1/2 mutations: incorporation of PARP inhibitors

For patients with germline BRCA1/2 mutations, current clinical guidelines recommend PARP inhibitor monotherapy (olaparib or talazoparib) following progression on first-line therapy (Fig.  ) [ ]. Although several clinical trials have compared PARP inhibitors with CDK4/6 inhibitors in the first-line setting (NCT03685331; NCT04481113; NCT05891093), less is known about their combination in managing disease progression after progression on CDK4/6 inhibitor in ER+ breast cancer. Ongoing efforts include a phase II trial (NCT05594095) evaluating dalpiciclib plus fluzoparib in patients previously treated with CDK4/6 inhibitors, which may clarify the therapeutic potential of combining CDK4/6 and PARP blockade in the second-line setting.

In tumours with low HER2 expression: HER2-directed antibody–drug conjugate therapy

Although classified as HER2-negative, a substantial proportion of metastatic HR-positive breast cancers exhibit HER2-low or HER2-ultralow expression. Following disease progression on ET and CDK4/6 inhibitors, treatment has traditionally relied on chemotherapy, which generally confers limited benefit. In this setting, trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd), a HER2-directed antibody–drug conjugate carrying a topoisomerase I inhibitor payload, has demonstrated superior clinical activity compared with standard chemotherapy.
In the phase III DESTINY-Breast06 trial, T-DXd significantly prolonged PFS versus physician's-choice chemotherapy in patients after endocrine-based therapy. On the basis of this finding, T-DXd received FDA approval in January 2025 for unresectable or metastatic HR-positive, HER2-low or HER2-ultralow breast cancer that has progressed on one or more lines of ET in the metastatic setting [ , ].
At present, there is no clinical evidence directly evaluating the combination of T-DXd with CDK4/6 inhibitors. Given that HER2-targeted therapies combined with CDK4/6 inhibition have shown favourable activity in HER2-positive breast cancer [ ], such strategies may warrant further translational and clinical investigation.

In tumours with CDK2-cyclin E amplification or overexpression: incorporation of CDK2 inhibitors

Given the role of CDK2-cyclin E activation in driving resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors [ , ] and preclinical evidence showing that CDK2 inhibition suppresses the growth of CDK4/6-resistant models [ – ], multiple early-phase trials are exploring CDK2-directed therapy (NCT04553133; NCT06188520; NCT05867251; NCT05735080); however, the absence of an approved CDK2 inhibitor remains a major limitation in this area.
Beyond selective CDK2 inhibitors, interest is growing in triplet CDK2/4/6 inhibitors simultaneously targeting cell cycle components. Examples include:

Ebvaciclib (PF-06873600) is a first-in-class CDK2/4/6 inhibitor with low nM potency against CDK2 (IC50 = 0.09 nM), CDK4 (IC50 = 0.13 nM), and CDK6 (IC50 = 0.16 nM) [ ]. In an early-phase clinical trial, the combination of ebvaciclib and fulvestrant demonstrated preliminary clinical activity in endocrine-resistant HR+ breast cancer, including partial responses in patients previously treated with CDK4/6 inhibitors (ORR 6.7%), suggesting potential activity in CDK4/6 inhibitor–resistant disease. Given this finding and favourable safety profile (NCT03519178), ebvaciclib has progressed to phase II studies.
Culmerciclib (TQB3616) is currently undergoing phase III clinical evaluation in HR-positive/HER2-negative breast cancer, including in combination with fulvestrant for previously treated advanced disease (NCT05375461) and in the adjuvant setting in combination with endocrine therapy (NCT05780567) [ , ]. In a randomized phase III trial, culmerciclib plus fulvestrant significantly improved PFS compared with fulvestrant alone (16.6 vs. 7.5 months), supporting further clinical investigation of concurrent CDK2/4/6 inhibition in this setting [ ]. Culmerciclib has also been investigated as monotherapy or in combination with other agents such as anlotinib, abiraterone, or a PD-L1 monoclonal antibody in liposarcoma (NCT05496569), lung cancer (NCT04924192), CRPC (NCT05156450), and digestive system tumours (NCT05139082). Some of these exploratory studies appear to have outdated or uncertain registry status and should therefore be interpreted with caution.
Eciruciclib (BPI-1178) has shown promising safety and anti-tumour efficacy in combination with fulvestrant in HR+/HER2– breast cancer after endocrine therapy (NCT04282031).
RGT-419B, a third-generation CDK inhibitor with potency against CDK2, 4, and 6, is currently undergoing early clinical evaluation as monotherapy or in combination with hormonal therapy in patients with CDK4/6 inhibitor–pretreated, HR+/HER2− locally advanced or metastatic breast cancer (NCT05304962).
R547 is a multi-kinase inhibitor targeting CDK1 (IC50 = 2 nM), CDK2 (IC50 = 3 nM), and CDK4 (IC50 = 1 nM) [ ]. It has been investigated in a completed phase I study in patients with advanced solid tumours (NCT00400296).

While targeting CDK2 is conceptually appealing, broad inhibition of interphase CDKs may increase toxicity; some evidence also suggests that cancer cells may retain proliferative capacity even in the absence of CDK activity [ ]. These uncertainties underscore the need for careful patient selection and trial design.

In tumours with FGFR1 amplification: incorporation of FGFR1 inhibitors

For tumours that acquire FGFR1 amplification, FGFR1-targeted agents represent a potential second-line option. Clinical studies have investigated erdafitinib and rogaratinib in patients whose disease progressed after treatment with an aromatase inhibitor plus CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy (NCT03238196; NCT04483505). Both trials reported promising PFS improvement with a triplet regimen combining palbociclib, fulvestrant, and erdafitinib or rogaratinib, without significant toxicities. In another trial (NCT04024436), futibatinib plus fulvestrant was investigated in patients with FGFR1-amplified breast cancer previously treated with CDK4/6 inhibitor–based therapy. Although the study was terminated, published results indicated that the combination was tolerated despite treatment-related adverse events. These findings suggest that further investigation of FGFR-targeted approaches may be warranted.

Targeting other signalling pathways

Given the involvement of aurora kinase and CDK7 in resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors and ET, several clinical trials are evaluating these pathway inhibitors as potential second-line treatment options [ , ]. Aurora kinase inhibitors have shown promise. In a phase I/II study, erbumine (LY3295668) demonstrated a favourable safety profile and durable anti-tumour activity as monotherapy, prompting its further evaluation in combination with ET in patients who progressed on CDK4/6 inhibitors (NCT03955939). Another Aurora kinase inhibitor, alisertib, has also been assessed as monotherapy and in combination with fulvestrant, but the activity was modest, and most patients discontinued due to disease progression (NCT02860000).
Targeting CDK7 has also emerged to address transcriptional escape mechanisms. Several CDK7 inhibitors, including samuraciclib, SY-5609, and Q901, have progressed to phase I–II clinical evaluation in combination with SERDs in metastatic HR+/HER2− breast cancer previously treated with CDK4/6 inhibitors (NCT05963984; NCT04802759; NCT04247126; NCT05394103). However, clinical evidence in this area remains immature. For example, SY-5609 combined with fulvestrant achieved disease stabilisation in approximately 42% of heavily pretreated patients (NCT04247126). Samuraciclib plus fulvestrant demonstrated a median PFS of 3.7 months in an early-phase study, while the preliminary data from the MORPHEUS trial showed comparable clinical activity between samuraciclib–giredestrant and giredestrant monotherapy (NCT04802759). A phase I trial (NCT03134638) assessing SY-1365 in HR+ breast cancer following progression on CDK4/6 inhibitor plus ET, was also terminated early. Collectively, findings from the clinical studies indicate that limitations related to therapeutic window, tolerability, and optimal dosing of CDK7 inhibitors must be resolved before this approach can be positioned to overcome CDK4/6 inhibitor resistance.
Beyond these approaches, multiple trials have explored additional pathways to address resistance to CDK4/6 inhibition, including bosutinib, a Src/Abl tyrosine kinase inhibitor (NCT03854903), TTI-101, a STAT3 inhibitor (NCT05384119), and hydroxychloroquine, an autophagy inhibitor (NCT04841148).

Continuous administration of CDK4/6 inhibitors

The strategy of continuing CDK4/6 inhibition beyond progression, either by switching to another CDK4/6 inhibitor or by maintaining the same agent, has also been explored in several trials, including MAINTAIN (NCT02632045), PACE (NCT03147287), PALMIRA (NCT03809988), postMONARCH (NCT05169567), and TRINITI-1 (NCT02732119). To date, the results remain mixed, creating uncertainty about whether patients benefit from continuing CDK4/6 blockade after the development of resistance.
The PACE and PALMIRA trials showed no PFS benefit from continuing palbociclib after disease progression on palbociclib-containing therapy. In contrast, the MAINTAIN trial found that switching to ribociclib plus fulvestrant significantly improved PFS in patients whose disease progressed on CDK4/6-based therapies. Similarly, another study showed that replacing palbociclib with abemaciclib after progression provided clinical benefit [ ], prompting the postMONARCH trial to investigate switching to abemaciclib after progression on palbociclib or ribociclib.
Taken together, these findings suggest that CDK4/6 inhibitor continuation may offer some benefit in selected settings, particularly when switching to mechanistically variable agents. However, larger randomized trials are needed to determine whether continuous dosing or agent-switching represents a viable second-line treatment strategy following resistance to initial CDK4/6 inhibitor-based therapy.
iii) Antibody–drug conjugates in later-line treatment of HR+/HER2−
breast cancer
Following progression on endocrine-based therapy, treatment options for HR+/HER2− metastatic breast cancer have historically been limited, with chemotherapy representing the main therapeutic approach. More recently, antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) have emerged as important later-line options. Sacituzumab govitecan is a Trop-2–directed antibody–drug conjugate (ADC) that delivers SN-38, the active metabolite of irinotecan, to tumour cells [ ]. In the phase III TROPiCS-02 trial, sacituzumab govitecan significantly improved PFS compared with physician's-choice chemotherapy in patients with heavily pre-treated HR+/HER2− metastatic breast cancer [ ]. Exploratory biomarker analyses suggested that clinical benefit was observed across multiple molecular subgroups, including tumours with PI3K pathway alterations, ESR1 mutations, and BRCA1/2 alterations, and was not restricted by HER2-low status [ ]. Subsequent analyses further demonstrated a meaningful OS advantage compared with chemotherapy [ ]. Consistent findings were also reported in the phase III EVER-132-002 study in Asian patients, further supporting sacituzumab govitecan as an effective later-line therapeutic option for this setting [ ]. These data led to the FDA approval of sacituzumab govitecan for adults with unresectable locally advanced or metastatic HR+/HER2− breast cancer who have received endocrine-based therapy and at least two additional systemic therapies in the metastatic setting.

 

## Strategies to avert toxicity of CDK4/6 inhibitors
Haematologic toxicity, particularly neutropenia, remains a major limitation of CDK4/6 inhibitors, especially for palbociclib and ribociclib. To minimise cytopenia while preserving target inhibition, intermittent dosing schedules have been adopted. Palbociclib is administered either 125 mg daily on a 21/7 schedule or 200 mg daily for 14 days followed by a 7-day drug holiday [ , ]. Ribociclib is similarly given at 600 mg on a 21/7 schedule [ ]. Although these schedules reduce the risk of abrupt neutrophil nadirs, cumulative toxicity remains a persistent challenge [ ].
This limitation was clearly shown in the PALOMA-3 trial (NCT01942135), where only 5% of patients required dose reductions within the first two cycles, but this increased to 40% with prolonged treatment. Comparable trends have been observed across the MONALEESA trials [ ]. Neutropenia risk is further amplified in combination regimens. For example, in metastatic CRPC (NCT02494921), combining docetaxel with intermittent ribociclib resulted in grade IV neutropenia and febrile neutropenia, and even with dose reductions and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor support, grade III neutropenia remained in 37% of patients.
To manage high-grade neutropenia, current guidelines recommend temporary treatment deferral for grade III neutropenia, and interruption plus dose reduction for grade IV neutropenia, or for grade III neutropenia accompanied by fever or infection. Importantly, only two dose reductions are permitted; further reductions necessitate discontinuation of CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy [ ].
Dose adjustments, however, may compromise therapeutic benefit by shortening effective treatment duration. This was evident in the adjuvant palbociclib study (NCT02513394), where high-grade neutropenia led to treatment discontinuation in 27% of patients, an interruption that likely contributed to the absence of clinical benefit in this setting. In contrast, the clinical success of abemaciclib in certain settings may partially reflect its continuous dosing schedule, allowing sustained target engagement [ ].
Collectively, these findings highlight the urgent need for strategies that not only maintain anti-tumour efficacy but also improve tolerability. As discussed earlier, the haematologic toxicity of CDK4/6 inhibitors is largely attributable to on-target CDK6 inhibition [ ]. Reducing activity against CDK6 may therefore improve the haematologic safety profile. However, progress toward CDK4-selective inhibitors has long been hindered by the high amino-acid homology between CDK4 and CDK6 within their ATP-binding domains, making isoform-specific inhibition difficult to achieve.
Recent advances have now yielded several CDK4 inhibitors: AU2-94 and atirmociclib, both represent a promising approach to widening the therapeutic window of CDK4/6 inhibitors by reducing haematologic toxicity without compromising efficacy [ , ].
AU2-94 (CDDD2-94) is a first-in-class CDK4 inhibitor that demonstrated approximately 60-100-fold greater potency for CDK4/cyclin D1 (Ki = 1–2 nM) over CDK6/cyclin D3 (Ki = 121–279 nM). Kinome-wide profiling across 369 human kinases further confirmed its exceptional selectivity [ ]. Compared with palbociclib, AU2-94 induces substantially milder reductions in lymphocyte or neutrophil counts, supporting an improved haematologic safety profile [ ]. Preclinical studies have shown marked anti-tumour efficacy in multiple animal models, including ovarian cancer, breast cancer, colorectal cancer, and glioblastoma [ , ].
Notably, AU2-94 also exhibits myeloprotective effects when administered prior to chemotherapy in RB-deficient TNBC models. These findings suggest potential applications not only in RB-proficient tumours, which rely on CDK4 activity, but also in settings traditionally considered refractory to CDK4/6 inhibition due to RB loss [ ].
Atirmociclib (PF-07220060), developed by Pfizer, inhibits CDK4 with approximately 20-fold selectivity for CDK4/cyclin D1 over CDK6/cyclin D3. Preclinical studies have shown that atirmociclib effectively suppresses HR+ breast cancer cell proliferation while exerting minimal effects on CD34+ HSPCs, suggesting potentially improved haematologic safety. Twice-daily administration of atirmociclib reduced tumour growth in multiple breast and prostate cancer xenograft models, both as monotherapy and in combination with fulvestrant, enzalutamide, CDK2 inhibitors, and anti-PD-1 agents [ ].
Clinically, atirmociclib is being evaluated in multiple trials for breast cancer including combinations with ET (NCT06206837; NCT06465368; NCT04557449; NCT06760637), a CDK2 inhibitor (NCT05262400), a PI3K inhibitor (NCT05216432), and a KAT6 inhibitor (NCT04606446). Combination with fulvestrant is also being tested as a second-line option in patients who have progressed on CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy. Early-phase clinical data indicate that atirmociclib is well tolerated and associated with promising PFS (NCT06105632), although larger studies are required to define its clinical benefit.
Additional selective CDK4 inhibitors are also entering early clinical development. BGB-43395 is a novel selective CDK4 inhibitor that has shown superior anti-tumour efficacy compared to atirmociclib in preclinical models [ ] and has recently entered early-phase clinical evaluation in HR+/HER2− breast cancer and solid tumours (NCT06253195; NCT06120283). Similarly, RGT-587 (GDC-0587) is undergoing phase I investigation in patients with breast cancer previously treated with CDK4/6 inhibitors (NCT07214662).
These emerging agents highlight the growing interest in CDK4-selective therapeutic strategies, and the substantial industry investment in this area reflects the increasing recognition of CDK4 as a promising therapeutic target.

## Future directions: move toward selective CDK4 inhibitors
A major objective for the next generation of CDK4/6-directed therapies is to extend their therapeutic reach beyond HR+ breast cancer into a broader spectrum of malignancies. Achieving this will require strategies that mitigate haematologic toxicity, thereby improving tolerability, enabling prolonged treatment duration, and supporting more effective combination regimens. In this context, selective CDK4 inhibitors represent a promising direction; however, their long-term success depends on resolving a central biological question: to what extent can CDK6 compensate for selective CDK4 inhibition and limit therapeutic efficacy [ ]?
Addressing this issue requires a deeper understanding of tumour-specific dependency on CDK4/cyclin D1 versus CDK6/cyclin D3. Analyses from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) show that CDK4 and CCND1 are broadly overexpressed across many solid tumours, whereas CDK6, CCND2, and CCND3 generally display similar or lower expression relative to normal tissues (Fig.  ) [ ]. In a recent complementary integrative profiling, cancers have been classified into four groups based on their reliance on CDK4 and CDK6: CDK4-dependent, CDK6-dependent, dual CDK4/6-dependent, and CDK4/6-independent tumours [ ]. Such stratification frameworks may serve as a practical tool for identifying tumour types most likely to benefit from selective CDK4 inhibition, while minimising the likelihood of intrinsic CDK6-mediated resistance and toxicity [ ].
Despite these advances, additional mechanistic and translational studies are required to determine whether selective CDK4 inhibitors can sustain durable responses in tumours capable of expressing or upregulating CDK6. Understanding the long-term influence of CDK6 on treatment responses is particularly important for anticipating acquired resistance. Notably, clinical sequencing studies have not identified recurrent CDK6 gene alterations in patients with acquired resistance, suggesting that compensatory increases in CDK6 activity likely arise from upstream oncogenic signalling rather than direct genetic mutation [ ]. Elucidating these regulatory circuits will be essential for sustaining the therapeutic efficacy of CDK4-selective strategies.
Collectively, a more refined understanding of CDK4 and CDK6 biology may ultimately enable their expression levels and pathway activity to serve as stratification biomarkers, guiding when dual CDK4/6 inhibition is necessary and when CDK4-specific inhibition offers comparable efficacy with superior safety and tolerability.
