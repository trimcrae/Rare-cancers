## Abstract
Pralsetinib, a potent and selective inhibitor of oncogenic RET fusion and RET mutant proteins, is a substrate of the drug metabolizing enzyme CYP3A4 and a substrate of the efflux transporter P‐gp based on in vitro data. Therefore, its pharmacokinetics (PKs) may be affected by co‐administration of potent CYP3A4 inhibitors and inducers, P‐gp inhibitors, and combined CYP3A4 and P‐gp inhibitors. With the frequent overlap between CYP3A4 and P‐gp substrates/inhibitors, pralsetinib is a challenging and representative example of the need to more quantitatively characterize transporter‐enzyme interplay. A physiologically‐based PK (PBPK) model for pralsetinib was developed to understand the victim drug–drug interaction (DDI) risk for pralsetinib. The key parameters driving the magnitude of pralsetinib DDIs, the P‐gp intrinsic clearance and the fraction metabolized by CYP3A4, were determined from PBPK simulations that best captured observed DDIs from three clinical studies. Sensitivity analyses and scenario simulations were also conducted to ensure these key parameters were determined with sound mechanistic rationale based on current knowledge, including the worst‐case scenarios. The verified pralsetinib PBPK model was then applied to predict the effect of other inhibitors and inducers on the PKs of pralsetinib. This work highlights the challenges in understanding DDIs when enzyme‐transporter interplay occurs, and demonstrates an important strategy for differentiating enzyme/transporter contributions to enable PBPK predictions for untested scenarios and to inform labeling.

## Study Highlights

WHAT IS THE CURRENT KNOWLEDGE ON THE TOPIC?

There is frequent overlap between CYP3A4 and P‐gp substrates/inhibitors, and it is challenging when trying to differentiate the contribution of CYP3A4 versus P‐gp for victim drugs based on in vitro data or even clinical drug–drug interaction (DDI) data.

WHAT QUESTION DID THIS STUDY ADDRESS?

Whether physiologically‐based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling could be used to deconvolute clinical DDI data and delineate CYP3A4 and P‐gp contribution for a victim drug (i.e., pralsetinib), and subsequently predict the effect of other inhibitors/inducers on the PKs of pralsetinib with confidence to inform drug labeling.

WHAT DOES THIS STUDY ADD TO OUR KNOWLEDGE?

Through PBPK modeling of clinical DDI data with sensitivity analyses and scenario simulations, key parameters associated with CYP3A and P‐gp contribution can be determined with good mechanistic rationale, including the worst‐case scenarios to allow further model application.

HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE DRUG DISCOVERY, DEVELOPMENT, AND/OR THERAPEUTICS?

This work highlights the challenges in understanding DDIs when enzyme‐transporter interplay occurs, and demonstrates an important strategy for differentiating enzyme/transporter contributions to enable PBPK application to replace clinical studies.

## INTRODUCTION
Physiologically‐based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling is a powerful approach combining physicochemical and drug‐related properties with drug‐independent physiological system information to describe a drug's absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME). The use of PBPK modeling to quantitatively predict drug–drug interactions (DDIs) has been rising in recent years, and regulatory agencies have issued DDI guidance highlighting the use of such modeling approaches. , There is more confidence in predicting DDIs involving drug‐metabolizing enzymes, and in particular those involving cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYPs), whereas the modeling of transporter‐mediated DDIs, especially with transporter‐enzyme interplay, is generally considered more complicated due to knowledge and translational gaps causing limited confidence. In this paper, we describe a comprehensive work to predict both enzyme‐ and transporter‐mediated DDIs for pralsetinib using a unique PBPK modeling approach.
Pralsetinib is a potent and selective oral inhibitor of oncogenic RET fusion and RET mutant proteins. In vitro data suggest that pralsetinib is a substrate of the enzyme isoform cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4) and a substrate of the efflux transporter P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp); therefore, its pharmacokinetics (PKs) may be affected by co‐administration of potent CYP3A4 inhibitors and inducers, P‐gp inhibitors, and combined CYP3A4 and P‐gp inhibitors. A two‐part clinical study (study BP42861) was conducted to evaluate the effect of itraconazole (ITZ), a known strong CYP3A4 inhibitor that also may demonstrate P‐gp inhibition, and rifampin (RIF), a strong CYP3A4 inducer, on the PKs of pralsetinib in healthy volunteers. The PK results from the study showed that the area under the plasma concentration‐time curve (AUC) and the maximum plasma concentration (C
max) of pralsetinib increased by ~250% and ~84%, respectively, when co‐administered with ITZ, and the AUC and the C
max of pralsetinib decreased by ~68% and ~30%, respectively, when co‐administered with RIF.
To help delineate the contribution of CYP3A4 versus P‐gp to pralsetinib's disposition, an additional phase I clinical study (study GP43162) was conducted as a postmarketing requirement to evaluate the effect of cyclosporin A (CsA), a known P‐gp inhibitor, on the PKs of pralsetinib in healthy volunteers. PK results from the study showed that pralsetinib's AUC increased by ~80% and C
max increased by ~48% when co‐administered with CsA.
Although these clinical DDI study data provided confirmation of the involvement of both CYP3A4 and P‐gp to pralsetinib's disposition, more definitive quantification to delineate the contribution of CYP3A4 and P‐gp just based on clinical data is still very challenging due to substantial knowledge gaps, limited understanding of the translation of in vitro transporter data, and the lack of clear selective CYP3A4 versus P‐gp substrates and inhibitors due to the extensive substrate/inhibitor overlap. , , Therefore, a mechanistic modeling approach was proposed to rationalize the clinical observations and more quantitatively delineate CYP3A4 versus P‐gp contribution to pralsetinib's disposition, which can allow prediction of untested scenarios and ultimately support potential dose justification and inform drug labeling.
The aim of this work was to develop a PBPK model to determine the contribution of CYP3A and P‐gp in pralsetinib's disposition through simulations of the clinical observed CYP3A4/P‐gp mediated DDIs among pralsetinib‐CsA, pralsetinib‐ITZ, pralsetinib‐RIF, and ultimately to use the developed PBPK model to predict the effect of other inhibitors and inducers on the PKs of pralsetinib. The learnings and worst‐case scenario approach taken here present a way to overcome current challenges, and it can be applied to DDI risk assessment for other CYP3A4/P‐gp dual substrates and/or other compounds with transporter‐enzyme interplay involved in the future.

## METHODS
The Simcyp Population‐Based ADME simulator (version 21, release 1; Sheffield, UK) with a Simcyp healthy volunteer population (“Sim‐Healthy Volunteers”) was used to construct the PBPK model and perform the PK and DDI simulations. The approach for the pralsetinib PBPK model development and application in DDI predictions is illustrated in Figure  and the processes for each step are detailed in the following subsections.
 

## 
PK model development and verification
The PBPK model describing pralsetinib's ADME was developed using a mixed bottom‐up and top‐down approach where the data used in model development included both in vitro and clinical data. The key parameters input in the pralsetinib PBPK model are shown in Table  and described below.
 Pralsetinib's absorption after oral dosing was described using the Simcyp advanced dissolution, absorption, and metabolism model with measured physicochemical and biopharmaceutical data (Table  ). Further details will be presented in an additional paper. Briefly, the particle population balance diffusion layer model was used. To evaluate potential supersaturation of pralsetinib, the compound was evaluated in the dynamic in vitro absorption system Tiny‐TIM (The TIM Company, Delft, The Netherlands). Following a 400 mg dose in the fasted state in this system, pralsetinib concentrations in the small intestine compartment of the system were quantified, resulting in a pralsetinib concentration of ~320 μg/mL (exceeding the intrinsic solubility at the measured pH). The bile micelle:buffer partition coefficients were fit to this observed solubility value. A supersaturation model (Simcyp first order model 2) was fit to maintain concentrations close to this measured concentration.
Whereas an absolute oral bioavailability study for pralsetinib has not been performed, following administration of a single dose of 200 mg dose of pralsetinib with a high‐fat meal, the mean C
max of pralsetinib increased by 104% and the mean AUC0‐INF increased by 122%. If it is assumed that pralsetinib oral bioavailability with a high fat meal approaches 100%, the results from the food effect study suggest that pralsetinib's fasted oral bioavailability likely does not exceed ~40–50%, and an F = 0.5 was used to estimate the clinical volume of distribution and clearance.
The volume of distribution at steady state (V
ss) was predicted using the Rodgers and Rowland (2007) method and a scalar for the tissue: plasma partitioning was applied to match the predicted V
ss with that estimated from clinical data. A full PBPK distribution model was utilized to model the P‐gp contribution in related organs and allow input of the P‐gp intrinsic clearance (CLint) parameter in the gut and liver. It should be noted though, that the Rodgers and Rowland method does not account for transporter‐mediated distribution. V
ss estimated using this method needs to be carefully assessed. In this case though, the impact is minimal as the predicted liver Kpuu did not significantly change with or without P‐gp inclusion (liver Kpuu =1.5 vs. 1.6, respectively).
To assign the clearance pathways, the total observed apparent clearance determined from the population PK analysis was used along with the assumed F = 0.5, to estimate a total intravenous clearance (CL) of 8.05 L/h. The estimated total CL was then partitioned into renal CL, based on the fraction of drug excreted unchanged in the urine as measured in the human mass balance study, and hepatic CL (further assigned to CYP3A4 clearance entered as CLint CYP3A4 and additional human liver microsome [HLM] CLint as described in DDI model development section). Based on in vitro data, pralsetinib may also be a minor substrate of CYP1A2, CYP2D6, and UGT1A4. These options were examined as alternate inputs for the HLM CLint, but did not have an impact on model development or application results.
Verification of the pralsetinib PK model was done using PK data from the pralsetinib‐alone arms of the pralsetinib‐ITZ, pralsetinib‐RIF studies. The simulated single‐dose pralsetinib PK profile and parameters (C
max, time to the maximum concentration, and AUC) at 200 mg and 400 mg were compared with the observed PK data from the pralsetinib‐ITZ and pralsetinib‐RIF studies, respectively. Briefly, a total of 10 trials with 25 subjects per trial were simulated, with an age range of 22–55 years (proportion of women: 0.24) for 200 mg, and an age range of 21–54 years (proportion of women: 0.20) for 400 mg.

## 
DDI model development and verification
To examine pralsetinib as a victim of DDIs, the key parameters associated with the contribution of P‐gp and CYP3A4, the P‐gp CLint, and the fraction metabolized by CYP3A4 (fmCYP3A4), respectively, needed to be determined. Although in vitro data suggested that pralsetinib was a possible P‐gp substrate and CYP3A4 substrate, the kinetic parameters (e.g., K
m/J
max or CLint) for P‐gp and value for fmCYP3A4 were not determined due to the limitations of the in vitro studies. Therefore, the final values of these key parameters were determined to be those in the PBPK modeling simulations that could best capture the observed clinical DDIs among pralsetinib and CsA, ITZ, and RIF.
The fmCYP3A4 was entered as enzyme kinetic data, and the CYP3A4 CLint was derived from Simcyp's retrograde calculator based on different values of fmCYP3A4 tested in the simulations. The P‐gp CLint values were input in the gut and liver compartments in the model. The same value of P‐gp CLint were used because despite different units of μL/min in the gut and μL/min/million cells in the liver, the gut CLint is further scaled to μL/min/cm2 by the model based on the area of the transwell used for in vitro experiments (in this case, assumed to be 1). It has been standard practice with other compound models in Simcyp to assume that 1 cm2 = 1 million cells with respect to P‐gp abundance and activity.
For the perpetrators (CsA, ITZ, the metabolite of ITZ [hydroxy‐itraconazole, OH‐ITZ], and RIF), the default PBPK models in the Simcyp (version 21) compound library that have been validated by Simcyp were used without modification. The CsA model was validated for DDI prediction using a clinical study with repaglinide as a victim (OATP1B1, CYP2C8, and CYP3A4 substrate) and the AUC ratio (AUCR) was slightly underpredicted (predicted/observed AUCR = 0.69). The ITZ model (solution formulation) has been previously used successfully for proprietary datasets (communication from Simcyp—there is a lack of published DDI studies due to the recent introduction of the formulation). Before conducting DDI simulations between pralsetinib and these perpetrators, further verification of simulated perpetrator PKs for CsA, ITZ, and OH‐ITZ were conducted using the available clinical data measured in DDI studies conducted here. Data from RIF were not available, and the default validated Simcyp model (multiple dose) was used.
As CsA is a known P‐gp inhibitor and its effect on P‐gp substrates can be more pronounced in the gut, as reflected in the C
max change of a substrate (whereas the AUC change of a substrate can be caused by CsA P‐gp and CYP3A4 inhibition), the pralsetinib‐CsA DDI simulations were primarily used to determine pralsetinib's P‐gp CLint based on whether the observed C
maxR could be accurately captured. Whereas, because ITZ is a known strong CYP3A4 inhibitor and routinely used for the determination of fmCYP3A4 for many drugs, pralsetinib‐ITZ DDI simulations were conducted to first estimate the maximum fmCYP3A4 required to capture the observed pralsetinib‐ITZ DDI. Following this, the P‐gp CLint and fmCYP3A4 estimated from the initial separate DDI simulations were combined into the pralsetinib model, and verification simulations using all three observed DDIs data (pralsetinib‐CsA, pralsetinib‐ITZ, and pralsetinib‐RIF) were conducted. The values of P‐gp CLint and fmCYP3A4 in the model that could capture all three observed DDI study data were the input parameters in the final pralsetinib PBPK model and used in the PBPK application. The pralsetinib‐RIF DDI simulation was used as a confirmation of the input parameters primarily derived from the pralsetinib‐CsA and pralsetinib‐ITZ DDIs given that there is generally less confidence in induction modeling (compared to inhibition), and the RIF concentrations in our clinical study were not measured for model verification.
The DDI simulations were set up according to the clinical study designs and all trials were run in the fasted state. Briefly, for the pralsetinib‐CsA DDI simulation, a single dose of 600 mg CsA was co‐administered with 200 mg pralsetinib. A total of 10 trials with 15 subjects per trial, with an age range of 23–55 (proportion of women = 0.133), were simulated. For the pralsetinib‐ITZ DDI simulation, 200 mg ITZ was dosed b.i.d. on day 1 followed by q.d. dosing on days 2–14, and 200 mg of pralsetinib was co‐administered on day 4. A total of 10 trials with 25 subjects per trial, with an age range of 22–55 years (proportion of women = 0.24), were simulated. For the pralsetinib‐RIF DDI simulation, 600 mg RIF was dosed q.d. for 16 days, and 400 mg of pralsetinib was co‐administered on day 9. A total of 10 trials with 25 subjects per trial, with an age range of 21–54 years (proportion of women = 0.20), were simulated.

## Sensitivity analyses and scenario simulations
Sensitivity analyses on the parameters of P‐gp CLint and fmCYP3A4 and scenario simulations of different combinations of the two parameters were conducted to ensure that these key parameters could best describe the observed data with good mechanistic rationale, and that the worst‐case scenarios were covered. A sensitivity analysis on a range of P‐gp CLint (10–100) was first conducted to examine the C
maxR and AUCR sensitivity in predictions of the pralsetinib‐CsA DDI, without considering the CYP3A4 contribution. A similar sensitivity analysis with the P‐gp CLint range of 10–100 was also conducted for the pralsetinib‐ITZ DDI, however, the AUCR was expected to be underpredicted when only considering the ITZ P‐gp inhibition without its CYP3A4 inhibitory contribution.
A sensitivity analysis was also conducted on a range of fmCYP3A4 (0.30–0.85) first for its effect on the simulated pralsetinib‐ITZ DDI with and without considering P‐gp to determine the maximum CYP3A4 contribution in pralsetinib's elimination (no P‐gp contribution), and then to account for the fmCYP3A4 with consideration of P‐gp. A similar sensitivity analysis with the fmCYP3A4 range of 0.30–0.85 was conducted for the pralsetinib‐CsA DDI.

## 
PBPK model application
The pralsetinib PBPK model with the final key parameters (P‐gp CLint and fmCYP3A4) that could best capture the clinical DDI data with CsA, ITZ, and RIF, was applied to predict various untested DDI scenarios for pralsetinib as a victim drug. The magnitude of the effect of moderate CYP3A4 inhibitors (without P‐gp inhibition [fluconazole] and with P‐gp inhibition [verapamil and diltiazem]), moderate CYP3A4 inducers (efavirenz and phenobarbital), and a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor without P‐gp inhibition (voriconazole), on pralsetinib PKs were predicted. The default PBPK models validated in Simcyp compound library (V.21) for fluconazole, verapamil, efavirenz, and phenobarbital were used directly in the DDI simulations. For diltiazem, the P‐gp inhibition (not included in Simcyp default diltiazem model [V.21], although clinically known) was added into the model (P‐gp Ki = 0.01 uM) based on verification simulations with diltiazem and a known P‐gp substrate, digoxin, as detailed in the Supplementary Material  . For voriconazole, a Simcyp validated PBPK model (included for the first time in Simcyp version V.22) was obtained and used after verification in V.21 for this purpose. Details on the simulations run can be found in Table  . For all simulations, a total of 10 trials with 10 healthy volunteers per trial were simulated (age: 20–50, 0.5 women), and pralsetinib was co‐administered with the perpetrators.

## RESULTS

## Pralsetinib PK model development and verification
The parameters describing pralsetinib ADME in the PBPK model are presented in Table  . The final PBPK absorption model predicted Fa values at 200 mg and 400 mg are 0.51 and 0.50, respectively; these values appear to be consistent with the assumed oral bioavailability based on the food effect study results and are also in‐line with the estimated pralsetinib Fa from the mass balance study (additional details can be found in the Supplementary Material  ). The CLiv is 8.05 and V
ss is 2.64.
The developed PBPK model was used to simulate pralsetinib's PKs at 200 mg and 400 mg, and the simulated PK profiles and parameters were compared with the observed data from the pralsetinib‐alone arms of the pralsetinib‐ITZ and pralsetinib‐RIF DDI studies, respectively. The PBPK model was able to describe the observed concentration‐time PK profiles of both doses, as shown in Figure  , and captured the range of observed PK parameters, as shown in Table  .
 

## Perpetrator PK simulation and verification
Simulations of the perpetrator (CsA, ITZ/OH‐ITZ) PKs using the Simcyp default compound files (V.21) were conducted to further verify that the simulated PK could capture the observed data in the clinical DDI studies here. As shown in Figure  and Table  , the simulated cyclosporine PK using the Simcyp default compound model was able to capture the cyclosporine blood concentration‐time profile and blood PK parameters observed in the pralsetinib‐CsA clinical DDI trial.
The itraconazole (fasted solution model) and hydroxy‐itraconazole PBPK models in Simcyp compound library were used to simulate the ITZ and OH‐ITZ concentration‐time profiles and plasma PK parameters observed in the pralsetinib‐ITZ clinical DDI trial with 200 mg itraconazole solution administered. Given the known ITZ and OH‐ITZ concentration variability between subjects, it was not surprising to see that the simulations with a 200 mg itraconazole dose overpredicted the observed OH‐ITZ concentrations in this DDI study (Figure  , Table  ). Accurately capturing the observed perpetrator PK data, that is, of both ITZ and OH‐ITZ is crucial for DDI simulations because both are strong CYP3A4 inhibitors. An arbitrary dose of 175 mg itraconazole allowed the simulation to best capture the observed data in this DDI study (Figure  , Table  ), and therefore was used for the DDI simulations.

## Pralsetinib DDI model verification
The two key parameters for the pralsetinib DDI model, P‐gp CLint and fmCYP3A4, were determined and verified as described in the Section  , and the estimated value for P‐gp CLint was 15 (in both gut and liver) and for fmCYP3A4 was 0.83 (Table  ). The pralsetinib PBPK model with these parameters was confirmed to be able to reasonably capture the observed DDI data (Table  , Figure  ) from all three clinical DDI assessments for CYP3A4 and P‐gp involvement. The final values of these two parameters were further examined and confirmed through sensitivity analyses and scenario simulations, and the potential cause of the underprediction of AUCR in the pralsetinib‐CsA DDI was elucidated (see next section and Section  ).
 

## Sensitivity analyses and scenario simulations

## Sensitivity analyses of pralsetinib P‐gp CLint
 with scenario simulations of the pralsetinib‐cyclosporine and pralsetinib‐itraconazole DDIs

Sensitivity analyses on a range of P‐gp CLint values (10–100) for its effect on the simulated DDIs were conducted. For pralsetinib‐CsA DDI, in the absence of CYP3A4 (fmCYP3A4 = 0), the value of P‐gp CLint of 15 could best capture the observed pralsetinib C
maxR (Figure  , Table  ), representing the maximal P‐gp contribution primarily in the gut (as no CYP3A contribution is considered). This was confirmed in a scenario of having a P‐gp CLint = 15 input in only the gut, and not in the liver, and deactivating the liver P‐gp resulted in a minor simulated C
max ratio change from 1.48 to 1.43 (Table  ). Without considering CYP3A4 contribution, a P‐gp CLint of ~50 was needed to capture the AUCR, and, as expected, the CLint of ~50 would significantly overpredict the C
maxR (Figure  , Table  ).
 For the pralsetinib‐ITZ DDI, the P‐gp CLint in the range of 10–100 was not able to capture observed AUCR without considering the CYP3A4 contribution (Table  ), which was expected because ITZ is more of a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor and the observed DDI with ITZ, especially for a 3.5‐fold increase in AUC, should be mainly from CYP3A4 inhibition.

## Sensitivity analyses of pralsetinib fmCYP3A4 with scenario simulations of the pralsetinib‐cyclosporine and pralsetinib‐itraconazole DDIs

Sensitivity analyses on a range of fmCYP3A4 (0.30–0.85) for its effect on the simulated pralsetinib‐ITZ DDI were conducted with and without considering the involvement of P‐gp. In the absence of P‐gp (CLint = 0), the value of fmCYP3A4 at 0.85 could best capture the observed pralsetinib AUCR, representing the maximum CYP3A4 contribution in pralsetinib's elimination (as no P‐gp contribution is considered in the simulation; Figure  , Table  ). To account for the contribution of ITZ P‐gp inhibition to the observed DDI, a sensitivity analysis of fmCYP3A4 with consideration of P‐gp (CLint = 15, defined from CsA DDI study) was also conducted. Adding P‐gp increased the predicted C
maxR by nearly 30% (from 1.26 to 1.62; Table  , Table  ). At fmCYP3A4 of 0.83 with the P‐gp CLint of 15, the observed AUCR and C
maxR could be best recovered in the PBPK simulation.
When the sensitivity analysis on fmCYP3A4 (0.30–0.85) was applied in the simulation of the pralsetinib‐CsA DDI, with consideration of P‐gp (CLint = 15), again, at fmCYP3A4 of 0.83, the model simulation could capture the observed DDI. On the other hand, in the absence of P‐gp, no interaction was predicted (Figure  , Table  ). This result confirmed that the CsA DDI (especially C
maxR) is mainly caused by P‐gp inhibition. It should be noted that the AUCR of the pralsetinib‐CsA study was mildly underpredicted due to the inhibition of CYP3A4 by CsA likely not being accurately captured in the current CsA model.

## Pralsetinib PBPK model application
The verified pralsetinib PBPK model was then used to predict pralsetinib DDIs with moderate CYP3A4 inhibitors (with and without P‐gp inhibition), moderate CYP3A4 inducers, and a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor without P‐gp inhibition; the results are summarized in Figure  and Table  .
 The effect of fluconazole (moderate CYP3A4 inhibitor that does not inhibit P‐gp) on pralsetinib PK is predicted to be mild, with AUC and C
max ratios predicted to be 1.71 and 1.15, respectively. The effect of verapamil and diltiazem (moderate CYP3A4 inhibitors that inhibit P‐gp) on pralsetinib PK is predicted to be mild, with AUCRs predicted to be 2.08 and 1.84 and C
maxRs predicted to be 1.60 and 1.68 for verapamil and diltiazem, respectively. The effect of efavirenz and phenobarbital (moderate CYP3A4 inducers) on pralsetinib PKs is predicted to be mild, with AUCRs predicted to be 0.55 and 0.68 and C
maxRs predicted to be 0.82 and 0.86 for efavirenz and phenobarbital, respectively. The effect of voriconazole (strong CYP3A4 inhibition that does not inhibit P‐gp) on pralsetinib PKs is predicted to be mild–moderate with AUC and C
maxRs predicted to be 2.22 and 1.20, respectively.

## DISCUSSION
As a combined substrate of the P‐gp transporter and CYP3A4 enzyme, the risk of pralsetinib DDIs with known P‐gp and CYP3A4 inhibitors/inducers was assessed using a well‐designed PBPK modeling approach. The pralsetinib PBPK model was developed and verified based on existing clinical PK data, followed by clinical DDI data, to determine the P‐gp and CYP3A4 contribution in pralsetinib's disposition and the model was then applied to predict the magnitude of DDI between pralsetinib and additional CYP3A4/P‐gp perpetrators. The authors believe this case example helps advance the emerging, challenging area of transporter‐enzyme interplay between P‐gp and CYP3A4 using clinical data with a PBPK modeling approach. , 

Although in vitro data suggested that pralsetinib was a possible P‐gp substrate and CYP3A4 substrate, the kinetic parameters (e.g., K
m/J
max or CLint) for P‐gp and value for fmCYP3A4 were not determined due to the limitations of the in vitro studies. This highlights a commonly cited challenge for transporter substrates, where the current strategy is to use compartmental models to take into account unbound substrate concentrations at the transporter binding site along with flux, however, extensive in vitro data (i.e., multiple time and multiple concentration data) is needed, and even then, there can still be a gap in in vitro‐in vivo extrapolation. For example, previous work attempted to use in vitro P‐gp J
max/K
m data for three P‐gp substrates and found that scaling factors were required for the J
max values in order to accurately recover the plasma concentration‐time profiles. In addition, for bosutinib, a CYP3A4/P‐gp substrate, fmCYP3A4 was estimated as near unity based on in vitro phenotyping data and the human mass‐balance study, and scaling factors were used for the in vitro P‐gp J
max. Whereas, for pralsetinib, additional metabolic enzymes besides CYP3A4 cannot be excluded based on both in vitro data and the human mass balance study (where ~21% of drug was not recovered), leading to the additional challenge of simultaneously determining fmCYP3A4.
Given these limitations, the mechanistic‐based PBPK simulations of the clinical observed DDIs among pralsetinib and CsA, ITZ, and RIF provided estimates of P‐gp CLint and fmCYP3A4. The P‐gp CLint was primarily determined based on the pralsetinib‐CsA DDI simulations capturing the C
maxR without considering CYP3A4 involvement, which represents the maximum P‐gp contribution. It should be noted that the AUCR of the pralsetinib‐CsA study was mildly underpredicted due to the inhibition of CYP3A4 by CsA not being accurately captured in the current CsA model using existing in vitro data. Although CsA was considered as a moderate CYP3A4 inhibitor, clinical DDI data for CsA with a known probe CYP3A4 substrate (such as midazolam) was not available to verify the CsA CYP3A4 inhibition Ki input included in the model. A hypothetical simulation of midazolam‐CsA DDI in healthy volunteers showed very minimal effect of CsA on midazolam PK (predicted AUCR = 1.08, C
maxR = 1.02), in agreement with the lack of CsA CYP3A4 inhibition effect predicted in the pralsetinib‐CsA DDI simulation when P‐gp is not considered. Whereas the current work focused on capturing the C
maxR in the pralsetinib‐CsA DDI simulations to determine P‐gp CLint, sensitivity analyses on the CsA CYP3A Ki were also conducted to capture the observed AUCR. It was found that to better capture the pralsetinib‐CsA AUCR with P‐gp included (AUCR predicted = 1.57 vs. observed = 1.79), the CsA CYP3A4 Ki needed to be decreased by 100× (to 0.0089 μM), which is significantly lower than the totality of the reported in vitro data. This remains a challenge in the absence of CsA verification data with prototypical CYP3A4 substrates. Nevertheless, the underestimation of AUCR in pralsetinib‐CsA DDI simulation due to the uncertainty of CsA CYP3A4 inhibition Ki did not affect our assessment overall because the fmCYP3A4 contribution was mainly determined from the pralsetinib‐ITZ DDI, whereas the pralsetinib‐CsA DDI simulation focused on C
maxR prediction to define the maximum P‐gp contribution.
The initial pathway assignments were estimated using Simcyp retrograde calculator, and the final assignments for each pralsetinib elimination pathway were confirmed to align with or could be explained by existing data. The simulated percentage of pralsetinib excretion in the urine (2.2%) and feces (60%) align well with the observed human mass balance study data, with the biliary clearance via P‐gp being reflected in the simulated feces pathway. Although the simulated metabolism contribution (~40% from the liver and gut) is higher than the human mass balance study findings, the low recovery (21.4% missing) in the clinical study could explain the discrepancy. The higher metabolism assignment likely represents a worst‐case scenario for the DDI risk assessment.
Although the model validation using three clinical studies data provided good confidence in using this model to predict DDI for untested scenarios, the overlap between CYP3A4/P‐gp inhibitors proved to be another challenge when selecting drugs to simulate untested DDI scenarios to inform drug labeling. Ultimately, fluconazole and voriconazole were selected as perpetrators that cause moderate or strong CYP3A4 inhibition, respectively, without P‐gp inhibition. These were selected as literature data have reported no in vitro inhibition of P‐gp up to 250 μM and minimal effects were observed when co‐dosed with the sensitive P‐gp probe substrate edoxaban. , , In addition, although diltiazem and phenobarbital were included here to predict a moderate inhibitor/inducer DDI with pralsetinib, additional model validation for these two drugs is needed as only one clinical DDI study with selective substrates for both compounds were available and used for model verification. It should also be noted that the potential P‐gp induction by rifampin, phenobarbital, and efavirenz were not considered for this purpose, as there is limited clinical data to available to validate the P‐gp induction, and only CYP3A4 induction simulations were needed to support the drug labeling. Despite these caveats, a simulation was conducted (data not shown here) with P‐gp induction data included in the RIF model, which also led to reasonable predictions of the observed pralsetinib‐RIF DDI and further verified that the fmCYP3A4 and P‐gp CLint included in the pralsetinib model are most relevant.
In conclusion, the PBPK approach used here with observed clinical DDI data allowed assessment of the P‐gp and CYP3A4 contributions to pralsetinib's disposition which drive the potential DDIs for pralsetinib as a victim drug. The key parameters determined likely represent the worst‐case scenario, which assure high confidence in the risk assessment of DDIs for pralsetinib with other untested CYP3A4 inhibitors/inducers, or combined P‐gp and CYP3A4 inhibitors. The perspectives and approach shared with this work can help the field to further increase our confidence in delineating complex transporter/enzyme contributions for new chemical entities to enable predictions of untested DDI scenarios.
