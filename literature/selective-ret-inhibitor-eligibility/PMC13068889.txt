Analyzing public databases, we tested the hypothesis that RTK function in normal cell could predict for TKI activity in the corresponding cancer. Classes of alterations for 16 RTK (missense mutations, amplifications, translocations) were analyzed in 17 different solid tumors, and tested as biomarkers of TKI efficacy in pivotal trials. Single agent TKI activity was minimal in cancers bearing RTK amplification. TKI activity was found correlated with the type RTK mutation but also to the functional role of the RTK in normal cell. A missense mutation of an RTK correlated with TKI efficacy when the RTK exerts an important function in the corresponding normal cell. Conversely, translocations were tissue-agnostic biomarkers, independent of RTK function in normal cells. These results provide guidance for the selection of cancers for development of novel targeted treatments.

## Introduction
In the last decades, targeted therapies with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have transformed the management of several cancers that harbor activating alterations of the target receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) – . Single agent TKIs demonstrated improved survival as compared to previous standards in molecular subsets of some advanced and localized cancers with RTK mutations. Activating missense mutations (e.g., in KIT, PDGFRA, EGFR), amplifications (e.g., ERBB2), or chromosomal translocations encoding for fusion genes (e.g. in ALK, ROS1, RET, NTRK1-2–3, CSF1, PDGF, FGFRs…) are biomarkers for responses to TKI therapy in one or sometimes several types of cancers , , , , ). The question explored in this work is: why cancers bearing a similar mutation are not equally responding to the same TKI in the clinic?
The first generation of TKIs improved survival in a specific cancer type or with known RTK genomic alterations. For instance, imatinib was demonstrated active in chronic myelogenous leukemia, then in KIT and PDGFRA mutated gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GIST) . For some targets, such as EGFR and ALK, several generations of inhibitors subsequently demonstrated clinical activity and improved survival as compared to previous standards – .
In parallel, it became clear that several of these TKIs were also active in other cancer types presenting similar molecular alteration in the same kinase , , , e.g. KIT mutations in melanoma , ALK translocations in inflammatory myofibroblastic Tumors (IMT) . The concept of tissue-agnostic targeted therapies has garnered considerable interest recently, and a methodology to classify targeted therapies as tissue-agnostic has been proposed by ESMO (ETAC-S) taking into consideration the number of cancer types and proportion of patients showing antitumor activity .
However, the clinical benefit of these TKIs in different cancer types bearing similar molecular alteration of a RTK varies considerably across cancer type. Some TKI seem to have similar level of clinical activity across a variety of cancer types, e.g. NTRK inhibitors , , . Others show striking variability of clinical activity in cancers sharing similar RTK genomic alterations , . For example, patients with metastatic melanoma (or thymomas) bearing KIT exon 11 mutations treated with imatinib had an overall response rate (ORR) of 21% and a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 4–6 months, while patients with advanced GIST harboring the same KIT mutations experienced an ORR of 60–70% and a median PFS over 40 months , – . Similarly, phase 1 or basket trials that tested a given TKI across different cancer types harboring similar genomic alterations of RTK showed a wide range of clinical responses (Table ).
A conceptual framework better capable to predict the expected therapeutic benefit of single-agent TKI treatment for cancers harboring genomic alterations of a RTK is still lacking. The striking variability of responses in the presence of a similar genomic alteration implies that other biological factors, beyond the mere presence of RTK genomic alterations, influence the clinical efficacy of TKIs against a cancer. RTK genomic alterations can act as strong oncogenic drivers in some cancers, whereas they function as passengers—clonally propagated but without major oncogenic effect—in other cancers – . Additional biological parameters have to explain the variable clinical activity of TKI across different cancer types.
The aim of the present work was to investigate biological parameters that could account for the variable clinical activity of TKIs across cancer types harboring comparable RTK alteration. We tested whether the expression and/or physiological function of the given RTK for the development or maintenance of the normal cell lineage correlated with the clinical activity of TKI in corresponding cancer cell bearing a genomic alteration of the RTK. To address this question, we selected a set of 16 commonly mutated RTKs in 17 different cancer types and analyzed 4 distinct groups of public datasets (Fig.  ) looking for a possible link between expression and/or function of these RTKs in the normal cells and TKI activity in the corresponding cancer cell. 

## Results

## Activity of TKI in 17 cancers types bearing RTK genomic alterations
We reviewed the clinical activity of TKIs in 17 common and rare cancers harboring genomic alterations of one of 16 RTKs in the published literature. The median PFS and objective response rates (ORR) in 43 cohorts representing unique combinations of a specific class of genomic alteration (e.g. translocation) of a RTK (e.g. EGFR) in a cancer type (e.g., lung adenocarcinoma) are presented in Figs.  a and . These data were obtained from 35 unique clinical trials of the best-in-class TKI in which RTK alteration was an inclusion criterion (detailed in Methods, Fig. , and Table ). “Best in class” refers to the TKI yielding the best progression free survival in a given cancer type across clinical trials in the same indication. Most of these data were obtained from phase I-II phase II trials, with four phase III trials available (Table ). Ten cohorts gathered cases from various cancer types in basket trials were analyzed collectively as ‘miscellaneous’ cohort. 
The median PFS of single-agent TKIs across these 43 cohorts varied considerably, with a median of 5.5 months (range 1.3–68 months) and a median ORR of 30.5% (range 0–96%). PFS and ORR were highly correlated (Spearman r = 0.87, p < 10–13, Figs.  a, and a). The median PFS was used as criteria of antitumor activity for the subsequent analyses hereunder.

## Clinical activity of TKI in the 3 classes of genomic alterations.
The efficacy of TKIs was analyzed for each RTK (for all 3 classes of alterations), cancer type and for the class of the genomic alteration (for all RTK) (Figs.  b and c and ).
We observed no significant differences of median PFS across the 16 RTK target (Fig.  b, Kruskal–Wallis p = 0.074) nor across of the 17 cancer types (Fig. b; Kruskal–Wallis p = 0.1). A comparative analysis of median PFS across the 3 classes of alterations is shown in Fig.  c. The median PFS observed with single agent TKI in cancers bearing translocations involving an RTK was significantly longer as compared to those with missense mutations (Wilcoxon p = 0.0067, Fig.  c). Similarly, the median PFS observed with TKIs in cancers with missense mutations of RTK was superior to that of cancers bearing amplifications (Wilcoxon p = 0.012; Fig.  c). We conclude that single agent TKI provide overall longer PFS in cancers bearing a target RTK activated by gene fusions as compared to those in which RTK are activated by missense mutations. Single agent TKI have limited clinical activity in cancers bearing RTK gene amplification.

## Clinical activity of TKI and frequency of RTK alterations in 17 cancers types
The frequency of RTK alterations within each of the 17 advanced cancers type selected was investigated using data from the MSKCC-IMPACT cohort. This cohort includes 7,147 patients spanning 816 unique combinations of 17 adult advanced solid cancers, 16 RTKs, and three types of gene alterations (16*17*3 = 816, detailed in Methods Fig.  a). All three classes of alterations (missense mutations, translocations or amplifications) were observed in one or more of the N = 17 cancers for 14 of the 16 selected RTKs (Fig.  a). Each individual RTK alteration (eg. EGFR missense mutation, or a translocation involving ALK) was rare in individual cancer types, but were observed in multiple cancer types (Fig.  a). Generally, translocations were less frequent; missense mutations were significantly more common than amplifications and translocations (Fig.  b). No correlation was observed between the frequency of an RTK alteration in a given cancer type and the clinical activity of TKI in this cancer (Fig.  c, Spearman r = 0.27, p = 0.08). 

## Clinical activity of TKI and expression of RTK in corresponding normal cell
We then tested whether the expression of RTK genes in normal tissues could correlate with the efficacy of a TKI in the corresponding cancer; For this, we used mRNA sequencing data from the GTEx database (See Methods). The mRNA expression values for RTK genes in normal cells or tissues ranged from 0 to 357 transcripts per million (TPM) (Fig.  a). The mRNA expression of RTK in normal tissues was not found correlated to the frequency of RTK alterations in the corresponding cancer (Fig.  b). It was alsonot correlated to the clinical activity of TKI (as evaluated by median PFS) in the corresponding cancers (Fig.  c, Spearman r = 0.29). 

## Clinical activity of TKI and function of RTK in corresponding normal cells
The function of RTKs in the corresponding normal cell lineages from which cancers arise was analyzed by integrating information from the public genetic database Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man® (OMIM) database, as well as murine knockout and knock-in model .
The function of RTKs on normal cell development and maintenance was classified in four categories: strong, probable, absent, or unclear (detailed in Methods, Tables & , and presented graphically in Fig.  a). 
Some RTK have limited and cell lineage specific functional roles eg. ROS1, Flt3 and FGFR4 whose loss is respectively impacting the normal functions of epididymal, leucocyte and hepato/cholangiocytes lineages with no reported impact in other lineages (Fig.  a, Table ). Conversely, MET had pleiotropic physiological role on a multiplicity of cell lineages, with broad organ impairment in KO mouse model.
As expected, the level of expression of the RTK in the corresponding normal adult tissue was higher when the functional role of the RTK in the normal cell was probable or strong (Fig.  b). The functional role of RTK in the normal cell was not correlated to the prevalence of the alteration in the cancer cell (Fig.  c). The functional role of RTK in the normal cell was not correlated to TKI efficacy (median PFS) in the corresponding cancer bearing a genomic alteration of the RTK (pooling the 3 classes of alterations together) (Fig.  d). The result was different however when the different classes of mutations were considered separately.

## Impact of RTK alteration class and physiological function on TKI clinical activity
The clinical activity of single agent TKI varied according both 1) to the class of RTK mutation and 2) to the functional role in corresponding normal cells.
First, the clinical activity of TKI (median PFS) was superior in cancers with high incidence of missense RTK mutations (Spearman r = 0.78, p = 0.00008). This was not observed in the subgroup with RTKs activated by translocations nor for those with RTK gene amplifications (r = 0.35 p = 0.25 and r = 0.18 p = 0.59, respectively (Fig.  a). 
The level of RNA expression of RTKs in the corresponding normal tissue was inversely correlated with median PFS of TKIs in the corresponding cancers for the translocation subgroup (r = − 0.74, p = 0.003), while no trend was observed for the missense mutation or RTK amplification subgroups (r = − 0.22 p 0.36 and r = − 0.26 p = 0.45 respectively, Fig.  b).
In cancers bearing an activating missense mutation of the RTK, the function of the RTK in normal tissues (as evaluated by the 4 categories strong, probable, absent, or unclear) was strongly correlated with TKI activity in the corresponding cancers (Fig.  c). Conversely, for the subgroup with RTK translocations, no correlation between the function of RTK in normal cells (which was absent in all but two cases) and TKI activity was observed. None of the translocations involving an RTK gene were observed in cancers derived from normal cells where the RTK have strong functional role and only one with a probable role (Fig.  c). The same was observed for the group with RTK amplification where single agent TKI was anyway limited (Fig.  c).

## Discussion
Somatic alterations of RTKs genes, encoding for an activating protein, are predictive biomarkers for TKI efficacy in some cancer types, but not others . The biological mechanisms accounting for these differences across cancers are not well understood.
In this study we investigated the hypothesis that the physiological role of the RTK in the corresponding normal cell lineage could predict for the activity of the TKI in the corresponding cancer cell bearing an activating mutation of the RTK. TKI acting on activating RTK represent a simple model to test this question. We wanted to test this hypothesis on this focused, limited set of enzymes, ie. tyrosine kinases and their inhibitors, before exploring further this concept on other signaling pathway. Analyzing a series of public datasets, we show that single agent tyrosine kinase inhibitors are active in 1) cancer cells bearing a RTK mutated activated by missense mutations if the normal RTK has an important physiological function in the normal cell counterpart, and 2) in cancer cells bearing a fusion gene involving the target RTK regardless of RTK role in the corresponding normal cell. In the second case, the RTK is generally not expressed and not physiologically involved in the normal cell counterpart.
Four types of public databases were analyzed: 1) databases presenting the prevalence of somatic mutations of RTK in different cancers, selecting 16 common RTK and 17 different cancer types, 2) databases presenting the expression of the RTK in the normal cell counterpart (GTEX), 3) databases describing the phenotypic impact of the kinase loss or activation (OMIM) in the normal lineage, and 4) the database of clinical trials (Pubmed), selecting the best in class TKI when several TKI and trials were available.
We tested whether: 1) the frequency of a given RTK alterations in a cancer type, 2) the expression of the RTK in the corresponding normal tissues, and/or 3) the physiological role of RTK in tissue development and maintenance correlated to the clinical activity of a TKI in corresponding cancer type bearing genomic alteration of the RTK.
Clinical activity was assessed using the median PFS in the principal pivotal clinical trial of a best-in-class TKI. PFS and ORR were highly correlated in this dataset of studies. Even though the PFS may be influenced by the number of lines and inclusion criteria, most of the pivotal studies were conducted in relatively homogenous patient populations in terms of PS, organ function, and lines. The physiological role in the normal cell counterpart was categorized in 4 pragmatic categories (strong, probable, absent, unknown), whose definition was based on the analysis of the literature, depending on the impact of gene knock-out or overexpression in the normal cell lineage or tissue. The prevalence of RTK alteration and the physiological role in the corresponding normal cell correlated to TKI clinical activity only for cancers with RTK missense mutations. As example, TKIs targeting KIT, EGFR, and RET activated by missense mutations were effective in cancer types where these RTKs play a role in normal cell development (e.g., Cajal cells, lung epithelium, or thyroid cells). This was not explained simply by RTK expression in the tissue.
This is not observed when RTK were activated by amplifications or translocations. None of the RTK partners in translocations/fusion proteins played a strong role in the normal cell counterpart. Only one played a probable role. For the most clinically active TKI (as judged by the median PFS) the target RTK had not role in the corresponding normal cell. The level of expression of the target RTK are actually significantly lower in the corresponding normal tissues. When RTKs are partners in fusion proteins, they act as strong drivers regardless of the cancer type and the cell of origin. TKIs are effective across various cancer types bearing translocations involving RTK. Most often these RTK have low levels of expression and no functional role in the normal tissue. Notable examples include ALK, TRK, and ROS1, which have limited roles in the development and function of most normal cells. TKIs targeting these fusion proteins demonstrate a broad clinical efficacy in a variety of cancers .
Finally, cancers bearing RTK amplifications show poor response to single-agent TKIs, with no correlation between the prevalence of amplification or the role of the RTK in normal tissue.
Taken together, these results provide a framework to understand better the relevance of activating mutations of tyrosine kinases, but also possibly other signaling proteins in oncogenesis. It provides a guidance for the development of TKI for the treatment of cancers bearing missense mutations or fusions of RTK genes. Although rare, cancers bearing fusion genes encoding for a functional RTK are the best candidates for drug development, along with cancers bearing missense mutations of RTK playing an important physiological role in the normal cell counterpart. These are the two situations where cancer treatments are agnostic. It may be interesting to explore this concept with other therapeutic targets (serine threonine kinases, transcription factors…) as the therapeutic armentarium against activated mutated proteins of cancer cells develops.
These results open perspectives for further clinical research. It will be interesting to test this model in a prospective setting, in particular in basket studies allowing for the inclusion of variable cancer types with a diversity of activating mutations.
This study has however several limitations. These include reporting biases in published data, variability in the performance of different generations of TKI, and incomplete clinical reports for rare cancer subtypes. Also, the studies analyzed may differ in terms of inclusion criteria, and timing for evaluation. However, pivotal studies include patient populations in good clinical conditions, with normal end organ functions; response evaluation intervals mostly ranged from 6 to 8 weeks. This reduces the heterogeneity of these data inherent to cross trial comparison. Of note PFS was found highly correlated with response rate in this dataset confirming the relevance of this chosen endpoint. Another limitation is that assessments of RTK function in normal tissues were sometimes approximated due to limited information on the different databases. The functional impact of a specific gene of a tissue or cell lineage in adults may not necessarily be associated to the phenotype identified in the presence of a germline disorder. This was the hypothesis we chose to identify only the most relevant functions of a given RTK, as judged by the phenotype of animals or humans with activated or inactivated gene. The 4 groups classification (strong, probable, absent, unclear) was created pragmatically. It has limitations which led us to create the group “unclear”. While possibly oversimplifying, it offered the opportunity to classify and dichotomize RTK and tissues. It may be refined in the future when more knowledge on functional impact of RTK is available at adult age. The absence of correlation between expression of the RTK by bulk RNAseq in the normal cells and TKI efficacy may be related to an heterogeneity of expression of the RTK gene in normal cells. The nature (codons…) of missense mutations may strongly influence response to TKI as demonstrated eg. with KIT in GIST or EGFR in lung cancer. This heterogeneity is not addressed in the present study. Combination treatment, with chemotherapy or IO are also increasingly used in the clinical and were not assessed in this work in order to better identify the individual effect of the TKI on the given cancer. Given the exploratory nature of the study, multiple testing were performed to explore the questions, with no correction integrated. Finally, this study focused on single-driver genes, though it is increasingly evident that additional mutations and pathway activations contribute to TKI response or resistance – .
Despite these limitations, this work shows that the efficacy of TKI in cancers with genomic alterations of the target RTK depends on RTK’s physiological role in normal tissues for missense mutations. For cancers where RTKs were activated by translocations, TKIs are active regardless of the normal function of the RTK. Indeed, most tissue agnostic cancer therapies are directed against fusion genes, with few refractory patients. Agnostic therapies against activated missense mutated proteins, such as the serine threonine kinase BRAF, have less consistent activity across cancer types, supporting the present observation , , . Future clinical research evaluating single-agent kinase inhibitors, in particular with an agnostic approach, could focus on cancers with kinase translocations, regardless of tissue origin, and in cancers with missense mutations where the kinase plays a physiological role in the tissue. These results provide a guidance for clinical research in precision medicine and support research on routine genomic screening for these rare alterations in advanced cancers.

## Materials and methods

## Selection of RTKs, cancer types and TKI
Sixteen different RTKs (genes EGFR, ERBB2, KIT, Flt3, PDGFRA, NTRK1-2–3, ROS1 ALK, RET, MET, FGFR1-2–3-4) most frequently reported as therapeutic targets in clinical oncology were selected for this study. Genomic alterations considered as significant variant included missense mutations (single nucleotide variations or indels resulting into missense mutations), amplifications, and translocations. Each alteration was considered as pathogenic and targetable and variant calling and filtering method was specific to each data source and is detailed in each database or trial (referred in Table for clinical trial data and in MSK-IMPACT publication for RTK alteration prevalence) .

## Prevalence of RTK gene alterations in cancers from public databases
We investigated the landscape of RTK alterations across the 17 advanced cancers with cohorts of 100 patients or more , based on data from MSKCC-Impact study through www.cbioportal.org – for the 17 advanced solid tumor with cohorts larger than 100 patients: bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA), breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), glioblastoma (GBM), gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GIST), head and neck squamous carcinoma (HNSC), kidney renal carcinoma (KIRC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma (OV), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD), skin melanoma (SKME), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), and uterus corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC). We collected the numbers and percentage per cancer types and types of mutations of missense mutations, amplifications and copy number amplifications for each of the selected RTK in each cancer subtypes. Besides we integrated the clinical activity of TKI in any reported cancer type.

## Selection of clinical trials reporting TKI activity
Only TKI used as single agents in cancers bearing specific activating mutation of the target RTK were selected. The clinical activity of TKI vs RTK-alteration the literature was reviewed on PubMed, extracting PFS and objective response rates (ORR) in clinical trials investigating antitumor activity of single agents in progressive metastatic/advanced cancers. Maintenance and adjuvant studies were excluded. Small molecule TKIs used in clinic without selection on a genomic biomarker (eg. VEGFR1-2–3 TKIs used as antiangiogenics), or in combination with cytotoxics, or immunotherapy were excluded. The search code for each RTK with formulas including (RTK name) AND (cancer) AND (tyrosine kinase inhibitor) in Title/Abstract with a filter on clinical trials and exclusion of combination or maintenance trials, from January 2000 to August 2024. We screened a total of 764 articles in August 2024, in compliance with the PRISMA statement (Fig. ). No RTK antibody is used as single agent on cancers bearing an activating mutation of the target kinase. We therefore focused on single agent therapy using small molecule TKI targeting a genetically altered RTK. For trials investigating several TKI in a similar RTK-alteration*cancer types, we selected the largest study reporting the most efficient TKI, the best in class, mostly latest generation TKI (eg. osimertinib was retained for EGFR mutations in lung adenocarcinomas, over gefitinib, erlotinib phases III or mobocertinib phase II which reported lower efficacies) to minimize the risk of ignoring an efficacy signal due to issues related to TKIs pharmacokinetics or pharmacodynamics. When the same TKI was tested in several settings in the same cancer type, we selected the first line rather than to pre-treated setting. We selected the independent response review data rather than investigator assessment when both where available. A minimum of 5 patients per RTK*cancer type cohort was requested. The cohorts evaluated had similar types of RTK alterations within similar cancer types, except for basket trials, which included a small number of diverse cancer types that could not be broken down into individual cancer types and were collectively labeled as ‘miscellaneous’. RTK from the same family were reported together if they were merged in the same cohort in trials (eg: NTRK1-2–3 or FGFR1-2–3). At the end of the selection process, each RTK was associated with the best-in -classTKI for the groups of activating mutations (fusion genes, missense mutation, amplification) for one cancer type. While heterogeneity is observed across trials for inclusion criteria and timing of evaluation, it is important to note that most trials included patients patients with PS 0 to 1, with response evaluation at 6 to 8 weeks intervals.

## Assessing efficacy of a TKI against a mutated RTK in given cancer type
Overall, efficacy assessment retained 35 phase III, II, and I clinical trials covering 43 “RTK*cancer types” conditions. This literature analysis covered 33 RTK in specific cancer types settings and 10 RTK*miscellaneous histology cohorts. The efficacy results of TKI tested in the basket studies are integrated in the analyses when the “cancer type”/histotype is not integrated eg in Figs.  , , , Fig. ). They are not included when correlations with the cancer types are tested, ie in Figs.  , and .
Best in class agents were selected, ie. TKI yielding the best progression free survival in a given cancer type, with cross trial comparison. Progression free survival was the criteria chosen for the determination of TKI efficacy, as it is more consistently reported and less variable than response rate in different cancer types. In the specific case of recent immature data were ORR was given and median PFS was not reached, we reported the lower threshold of 95% confidence interval (95% CI) of the median PFS: this was the case for instance for publication of LIBRETTO-001 trial (NCT03157128), selpercatinib in RET mutations in thyroid medullary carcinomas (MTC) or RET fusions in THYR published in August 2024 . The clinical approval status of TKI in RTK alteration-informed situations—whether tumor specific or tumor-agnostic—was checked on the US FDA website (www.FDA.gov ).

## Gene expression of RTK in normal tissues
Gene expression of each RTK in corresponding normal cell lineages was assessed in publicly available RNA sequencing (bulk RNA-seq) data obtained from the GTEx Portal on 26/06/2024 (www.gtexportal.org). The bulk tissue RNA expression data are expressed in transcripts per million (TPM) from healthy tissues for the corresponding cancer type as follows: Skin with-sun exposure for SKME, lung for LUAD or LUSC, breast epithelial cells – mammary tissue for BRCA, colon – sigmoid mucosa for COAD, stomach for STAD, bladder mucosa for BLCA, liver for CHOL, kidney cortex for KIRC or kidney renal papillary carcinoma (KIRP), ovary for OV, esophagus mucosa for ESCA, uterus endometrium for UCEC, prostate epithelial cells for PRAD, brain hippocampus median for GBM, thyroid epithelial cells for thyroid cancers, a mean of muscle-skeletal cell and cultured fibroblast for non-muscular myofibroblastic tumors such as inflammatory myofibroblastic tumors (IMT) or gastric wall mesenchymal cells for GIST and a mean of all above mentioned for miscellaneous histologies and pan-cancer trials.

## Functional role of the RTK in the normal tissue or cell lineage
To classify the physiological role of selected RTKs in normal cell lineages in vivo in human, we screened Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man® (OMIM) database (www.omim.org), and PubMed for human or mouse genetic disorders with RTKs gain- or loss-of-function. The OMIM database was used to determine the phenotypic impact of RTK gain- or loss-of-function, in humans bearing germline mutations of the given gene and in murine models (Table ). We also searched Medline using “RTK name” AND “germline mutations” OR “mouse Knock-out” (KO) OR “mouse Knock-in” (KI). Overall, we analyzed the published evidence on the physiological impact of RTK on organ development, function and maintenance using: i) inherited human germline alterations considering gain- and loss- of function mutations reported in several unrelated families when available, and ii) engineered or spontaneous KO or KI murine models—RTK modulations in vitro on cell lineages were excluded. Human germline alterations were documented for EGFR, PDGFRA, KIT, RET, FGFR1-2–3, NTRK1-2–3 with specific impact on relevant cell lineages, but not for FGFR4, FLT3, MET and ROS1. Murine genetically modified models also enabled to evaluate RTK impact on normal lineages. Some early embryonic or post-natal lethality of RTK gene KO models were reported (e.g., in MET, FGFR1-2, ERBB2, TRKC, EGFR KO mice), which might have masked putative delayed impacts (e.g., ERBB2 KO models allowing to identify phenotypic neural and cardiac dysfunction, and failing to detect a later putative role on mammary development). Conversely, some RTK KO models show specific lineage impairments (e.g., unusual white coating with KIT KO, skeletal defects with FGFR 1–2-3 alterations) whereas others show less significant non-vital consequences, relying on investigator focus or systematic reporting (the impact on prostate, breast, salivary glands, or thymus are rarely described). From this literature analysis, we classified the role of each RTK in different lineages into four categories (Table ). 1) “strong” for important impact of loss- or gain-of-function (documented in both human and mouse models) 2) “probable” for documented impact (documented in either human or mouse models), 3) “absent” for no impact in reliable models and 4) “unclear” when the information was unclear because of absent of description in human or mouse model. Indeed, this absence was considered as informative and not just lacking, possibly hidden by an early pleotropic or vital effect hiding subsequent organ development (e.g., PDGFRA or ERBB2) inducing a putative lack of information notably for adult non-vital organs).

## Statistics
Correlation between continuous variables was performed using the Spearman correlation test due to the non-normal distribution of the data. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used for comparisons involving more than two groups, while the Wilcoxon test was used for pairwise comparisons, both due to the non-normal distribution of the data. The median PFS, prevalence of genomic alterations of individual RTK genes, and RNA expression of RTKs in normal tissues were graphically summarized using boxplots to depict the distribution of data points (i.e., minimum, maximum, range, median, first, and third quartiles). These analyses were exploratory and not designed to confirm controlled prospective hypotheses: a significant threshold of p < 0.05 was therefore adopted. All analyses were conducted using R software version 4.4.1.
