## Abstract
Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) is the second most common primary liver malignancy and is characterized by aggressive biological behavior and poor prognosis. Surgical resection remains the only potentially curative treatment; however, most patients are ineligible for surgery at diagnosis, and recurrence rates remain high even after curative resection.
In recent years, locoregional therapies including transarterial chemoembolization, hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy, and radiotherapy have achieved promising clinical outcomes and are increasingly incorporated into multimodal treatment strategies alongside surgery and systemic therapy. Liver transplantation has also shown potential benefits in carefully selected early-stage cases. Meanwhile, advances in targeted therapy and immunotherapy—including phase III trials of gemcitabine–cisplatin combined with PD‑1/PD‑L1 inhibitors—have further supported the development of personalized, multidisciplinary treatment strategies in advanced disease.
The rapid development of genomic sequencing and tumor microenvironment research has enabled molecular classification of ICC, providing valuable guidance for the selection of targeted and immune-based therapies. Importantly, molecular profiling is now also influencing patient selection for resection and transplantation within multimodal treatment pathways, and recent data support fluoropyrimidine-based adjuvant therapy and emerging perioperative chemotherapy strategies in resectable biliary tract cancers. Nevertheless, certain treatment strategies, particularly in the context of multimodal integration, require further validation through large-scale prospective clinical studies.
This review summarizes recent progress in molecular classification, local and systemic therapeutic approaches, and highlights how molecular profiling and biomarker-guided strategies contribute to personalized, precision, and multimodal treatment of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma.

## Introduction
Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) ranks second among primary liver malignancies, following hepatocellular carcinoma, and accounts for approximately 20% of all liver cancers [ , ]. The incidence rate of ICC in China is increasing annually [ ]. Currently known risk factors associated with the development of ICC include fibrocystic disease, primary sclerosing cholangitis, intrahepatic bile duct stones, cirrhosis, viral hepatitis, parasitic infection, obesity-related steatohepatitis, diabetes, exposure to carcinogens, and certain genetic disorders [ ]. These factors can induce chronic inflammation, resulting in bile duct epithelial damage and bile stasis, which in turn promote the initiation and progression of ICC [ ].
The classification of ICC usually reflects the distinct biological behaviors and invasive abilities of tumors. With the development of gene sequencing technology and the deepening of immune microenvironment research, numerous actionable gene mutations have been gradually identified. Therapeutic agents targeting these molecular alterations have demonstrated encouraging clinical efficacy [ , ]. Thus, progress in molecular typing of ICC provides important guidance for targeted therapy and immunotherapy, and is closely associated with tumor diagnosis, treatment selection, and prognosis.
Radical surgical resection is currently the only possible treatment for ICC, however, only 20%–35% of patients can achieve R0 resection [ ]. For these patients, the 5-year postoperative survival rate is merely 20%–35% [ ]. With the continuous refinement of surgical techniques and the introduction of locoregional treatment modalities such as hepatic artery embolization, hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC), and thermal ablation, significant advances have been achieved in ICC management. Meanwhile, novel approaches including immunotherapy and targeted therapy are emerging as effective options, and phase III trials have established gemcitabine–cisplatin combined with PD‑1/PD‑L1 inhibitors as a new first-line standard in advanced biliary tract cancer. In addition, randomized data now support fluoropyrimidine-based adjuvant therapy and suggest that perioperative gemcitabine–cisplatin may improve outcomes in selected resectable biliary tract cancers. Consequently, personalized and comprehensive multimodal treatment, integrating surgical, local, and systemic therapies according to tumor subtype, molecular characteristics, and individual patient factors, has become a new treatment paradigm.
This article reviews recent advances in the molecular classification and comprehensive treatment of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, with a particular focus on how molecular profiling and emerging perioperative and systemic strategies can be integrated into rational treatment sequencing and multidisciplinary care.

## Molecular classification of ICC
Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) presents with multiple subtypes that can be classified into three major types according to their growth patterns and morphological characteristics: mass forming type, periductal infiltrative type, and intraductal growth type [ ]. Among these, the mass-forming type is the most common [ ]. Anatomically, bile duct tumors are further categorized into large bile duct and small bile duct types based on location and duct size. The small bile duct type usually exhibits a mass-forming growth pattern and is located in the peripheral liver tissue, whereas the large bile duct type tends to grow infiltratively along the bile ducts [ ].
With the advancement of genome sequencing technology and omics analysis, the molecular characteristics of ICC have been further clarified. Several actionable genetic alterations have been identified, including KRAS mutations, TP53 mutations or deletions, *IDH1/2* mutations, FGFR2 gene fusions, and rarer alterations involving NTRK, ERBB2 (HER2), MET, BRAF, and NRAS [ ]. A recent study reported that nearly half of ICC patients undergoing gene sequencing harbored potentially targetable genomic mutations [ ]. Correlating molecular subtypes with specific gene alterations provides valuable guidance for targeted therapeutic strategies, with molecularly matched therapies associated with improved prognosis and longer survival [ ].
Large and small bile duct types are strongly correlated with distinct gene mutation patterns. Large bile duct ICC typically exhibits high-frequency KRAS and TP53 mutations, whereas small bile duct ICC commonly features *IDH1/2* mutations and FGFR2 fusions [ ]. This provides important guidance for the clinical application of targeted therapy, and currently targeted therapy targeting *IDH1/2* mutations and FGFR2 fusions have shown significant efficacy [ , ]. Consequently, the traditional pathological classification of ICC has been expanded through molecular profiling, allowing for more individualized treatment selection.
 Further research has proposed additional molecular classifications. One study categorized ICC into two molecular classes: the inflammatory type, characterized by activation of inflammatory signaling pathways, cytokine overexpression, and STAT3 activation; and the proliferative type, marked by oncogenic signaling activation [ ]. Based on DNA copy number variations and gene expression, the proliferative subtype can be divided into three subclasses—P1, P2, and P3—with P1 enriched in KRAS mutations and poor prognosis, and P3 associated with EGFR pathway activation.
Other researchers have proposed classifications centered on proteogenomic features, dividing ICC into inflammatory, stromal (interstitial), metabolic, and differentiated types. These subtypes also correlate strongly with specific mutations: KRAS mutations are frequent in inflammatory type, TP53 mutations in metabolic type, and FGFR2, BAP1, and *IDH1/2* mutations in differentiated type [ ]. Such molecular classifications are increasingly valuable for guiding targeted treatment. For instance, mutan TP53 has been linked to resistance to gemcitabine chemotherapy in preclinical models [ ].
Recent studies incorporating transcriptomic and immune microenvironment analyses have refined ICC categorization further. One approach distinguishes inflammatory and non-inflammatory ICC subtypes, with the former enriched in KRAS mutations and T-cell depletion, and the latter characterized by *IDH1/2*, BAP1, and FGFR2 alterations [ ]. Job et al. [ ] further classified ICC into four immune microenvironment-based subtypes: immune-desert, immunogenic, myeloid, and stromal. Each demonstrates differential responses to treatment—cytotoxic or immunomodulatory agents may benefit immune-desert subtypes; myeloid subtypes could respond to bisphosphonates or CSF-1 receptor inhibitors; and stromal subtypes may benefit from antifibrotic therapy.
In summary, ICC classification has evolved from conventional histopathology to molecular and tumor microenvironment-based subtyping. These refined classification systems enable more precise treatment selection, enhance the efficacy of targeted and immune therapies, and represent critical steps toward personalized and precision medicine. Nonetheless, challenges remain, including the limited availability of targeted drugs for some mutations and the high cost of gene sequencing. As molecular subtyping becomes more accessible and integrated into treatment planning, it will further improve the accuracy of therapy selection and patient prognosis.

## Rationale and scope of molecular testing
Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) constitutes 8.2–15% of primary liver malignancies and represents the second most common hepatic cancer after hepatocellular carcinoma [ – ]. Despite advances in surgery and systemic therapy, approximately 70–80% of patients present with unresectable or metastatic disease and have a median survival of less than one year [ ].
Molecular profiling has redefined ICC as a genetically heterogeneous entity characterized by distinct driver mutations and immune microenvironment patterns [ , , ].
Both the NCCN Biliary Tract Cancers Guidelines (Version 2.2025) and the Chinese national clinical practice guideline on biliary tract cancers (2024) recommend molecular profiling / biomarker testing for patients with unresectable or advanced disease to identify actionable targets and guide precision therapy [ , ].
Approximately 40–50% of ICC cases harbor targetable alterations—including FGFR2 fusions, *IDH1/2* mutations, BRAF V600E variants, HER2 amplifications, KRAS mutations, and NTRK or RET fusions—underscoring the clinical importance of routine molecular profiling [ , ] .

## Recommended testing techniques
The testing methodologies outlined in Table  are essential for reliable detection of clinically actionable biomarkers and should, when possible, be implemented within a combined DNA‑ and RNA‑based NGS platform.
 Expert consensus recommends a combined DNA + RNA NGS approach to simultaneously capture point mutations, insertions/deletions, copy-number changes, and gene fusions [ – ]. When NGS is unavailable, a tiered strategy using traditional techniques is advised. For example:

FGFR2 fusions are best detected by RNA-NGS or FISH [ – ].
IDH1 and KRAS mutations can be identified by PCR-based methods [ ].
HER2 status should be assessed with IHC, confirmed by FISH or NGS for equivocal cases [ ].
BRAF V600E screening can use the VE1 IHC antibody, with positive results validated by NGS or PCR [ ].

## Clinical significance of key targets

## FGFR2 fusions → FGFR inhibitors
FGFR2 fusions are typical of small-duct ICC and predict response to FGFR blockade. Pemigatinib and futibatinib significantly prolong progression-free survival in previously treated FGFR2-rearranged ICC [ , ].

## IDH1 mutations → Ivosidenib
Detected in 10–20% of small-duct ICC, IDH1 mutations are therapeutically targetable. The phase III ClarIDHy trial demonstrated improved PFS and disease control with ivosidenib [ , ].

## BRAF V600E → BRAF/MEK inhibitors
Although present in < 5% of ICC, BRAF V600E is linked to poor prognosis but shows robust response to combined BRAF and MEK inhibition [ – ].

## HER2 Amplification/Overexpression → Anti-HER2 therapy
HER2 alterations occur in 1.8–8.0% of cases and respond to trastuzumab-based or trastuzumab-deruxtecan regimens [ – ] .

## NTRK / RET fusions → selective inhibitors
Though rare (< 2%), these fusions show dramatic responses to larotrectinib, entrectinib, or selpercatinib [ – ].

## KRAS mutations → prognostic marker
KRAS variants, particularly G12C and G12D, associate with aggressive phenotypes and limited response to standard therapy [ , ].

## MMR/MSI-H → immunotherapy sensitivity
MSI-H or dMMR tumors demonstrate enhanced benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitors such as pembrolizumab [ – ].

## Emerging targets
NRG1 / NRG2 fusions and DDR gene defects (*BRCA1/2*, ATM, PALB2) represent novel opportunities for precision therapy and trial enrollment [ – ].

## Challenges and future directions
Despite clear therapeutic benefits, barriers to routine implementation remain. High costs, regional inequities in testing availability, and limited access to targeted agents constrain clinical impact [ , ]. Intratumoral heterogeneity may yield sampling bias and false-negative results, underscoring the need for liquid biopsy integration and repeat testing in progressive disease [ – ].
Future work should prioritize standardized testing algorithms, novel biomarker validation, and resistance mechanism monitoring through ctDNA profiling. Next-generation FGFR inhibitors and rational combination strategies—such as IDH plus PD-1 blockade—represent promising research directions. With increasing accessibility of NGS and liquid biopsy, molecular testing is set to become an integral component of routine ICC management, enabling precise, evidence-based therapeutic selection and improved patient outcomes.

## Surgical treatment

## Radical surgical resection
Surgical treatment remains the only potential curative option for intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC). The primary goal is to achieve R0 resection while preserving adequate liver volume and preventing postoperative liver failure [ ]. However, due to the insidious onset and lack of specific clinical symptoms, most patients are already in an advanced stage at diagnosis and are not eligible for curative resection. In a clinical cohort study, only about 32% of patients were eligible for curative resection after comprehensive evaluation. The main reasons for unresectability were multifocal liver tumors (54%), locally unresectable disease (29%), and distant metastasis (13%). Therefore, a thorough preoperative assessment is essential, and the ability to achieve R0 resection remains the most critical prognostic determinant.
Patients who underwent radical resection had a median survival of 36 months, whereas those unable to undergo curative surgery had a median survival of only 9 months [ , ]. This stark survival benefit for surgical candidates is corroborated by contemporary data, which shows that patients with localized disease (the surgical cohort) have a median overall survival of 24.3 months and a 5-year survival rate of 28.2%, drastically higher than the 3.0% 5-year survival for patients with distant disease [ ]. For patients who undergo radical resection, outcomes can be even more favorable, with the 5-year survival rate reaching up to 63% in those achieving R0 resection with negative lymph nodes [ ].
For patients requiring extensive liver resection, preoperative liver function reserve must be carefully evaluated to prevent serious postoperative complications such as liver failure. Prognosis is also affected by resection margin width and lymph node status. Studies have shown that wider resection margins correlate with longer survival, with margins greater than 5 mm identified as an independent predictor of improved outcomes [ ]. Thus, surgeons should aim for the widest possible margin while maintaining sufficient remnant liver volume.
The necessity of lymph node dissection during ICC surgery remains controversial [ ]. Some studies have reported that lymphadenectomy does not significantly improve survival [ , ], but it provides important prognostic information and aids in postoperative treatment planning. Therefore, it is generally recommended that lymph node dissection be included as part of radical surgical management.
In summary, hepatectomy remains the cornerstone of curative treatment for ICC. A meticulous preoperative evaluation is required to balance complete tumor removal, adequate margin width, and preservation of liver function. Attention to these factors is crucial for minimizing postoperative complications and improving long-term outcomes.

## Liver transplantation
Liver transplantation has always played an important role in the comprehensive treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma, demonstrating favorable therapeutic outcomes and prolonged survival. However, in earlier reports, patients with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) experienced high recurrence rates and poor prognoses following transplantation [ ]. Consequently, liver transplantation was traditionally not recommended as a standard treatment for ICC.
More recent evidence has begun to challenge this view. A retrospective study reported that liver transplantation following systemic chemotherapy achieved a 5-year overall survival rate of 83.3% and a 5-year recurrence-free survival rate of 50% [ ]. Another study found that patients with very early-stage ICC (single tumor ≤ 2 cm) had significantly lower recurrence rates after liver transplantation than those with advanced disease (single tumor > 2 cm or multifocal lesions). The cumulative recurrence rates at 1, 3, and 5 years in the very early ICC group were 7%, 18%, and 18%, respectively, compared with 30%, 47%, and 61% in the advanced ICC group (P = 0.01). Similarly, the 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates were 93%, 84%, and 65% for very early ICC, versus 79%, 50%, and 45% for advanced ICC (P = 0.02) [ ].
These findings suggest that, with advances in perioperative management and multimodal treatment strategies, liver transplantation may no longer be an absolute contraindication for selected ICC patients. In particular, liver transplantation combined with neoadjuvant therapy has shown potential to improve prognosis in appropriately selected cases [ , ]. Furthermore, several centers have reported encouraging outcomes in locally advanced ICC patients who underwent transplantation after achieving disease stability with neoadjuvant chemotherapy [ ] .
Nonetheless, this approach remains limited by factors such as donor organ shortages and the lack of large-scale, prospective clinical validation. Therefore, while liver transplantation may be considered a promising option for highly selected early-stage or downstaged ICC patients, it is not yet recommended as a routine treatment for ICC.

## Perioperative systemic therapy (Adjuvant and Neoadjuvant)
Patients with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) face a high risk of recurrence even after R0 resection, and 5-year survival rates remain modest despite technical advances in surgery [ , ]. Consequently, treatment is increasingly moving from a purely “surgery-first” approach toward a multimodal perioperative strategy aimed at eradicating micrometastatic disease and biologically selecting patients most likely to benefit from major hepatectomy.

## Adjuvant chemotherapy
The standard of care for adjuvant therapy has evolved primarily based on two large phase III trials. The BILCAP study randomized 447 patients with resected biliary tract cancer (BTC) to receive capecitabine or observation. Although the intention-to-treat analysis for overall survival narrowly missed statistical significance, the prespecified per-protocol analysis showed a clear survival benefit (median overall survival 53 vs. 36 months; HR 0.75) [ ]. These results led to the widespread adoption of adjuvant capecitabine after curative-intent resection of BTC, including ICC.
More recently, the phase III ASCOT (JCOG1202) trial further refined adjuvant management, particularly in East Asia. This study showed that S-1 significantly improved overall survival compared with observation in resected BTC (3-year survival 77.1% vs. 67.6%; HR 0.69; p = 0.008) [ ]. By contrast, earlier gemcitabine-based adjuvant trials, including BCAT and PRODIGE 12–ACCORD 18, did not demonstrate a survival benefit [ , ]. Reflecting these data, major guidelines now recommend adjuvant capecitabine (globally) or S-1 (in parts of Asia) for patients fit for systemic therapy, with particular emphasis on those with high-risk features such as lymph-node positivity, R1 margins, or multifocal disease [ , ].

## Neoadjuvant and conversion strategies
For patients with borderline resectable or high-risk resectable ICC, neoadjuvant therapy is increasingly utilized to improve R0 resection rates and assess tumor biology—effectively serving as a “stress test” before irreversible surgery. The phase III GAIN trial (AIO-HEP-0110), reported in 2025, provides the first randomized evidence supporting a perioperative approach in BTC. In this study, patients with resectable BTC were randomized to perioperative gemcitabine plus cisplatin versus immediate surgery. The perioperative arm achieved nearly doubled median overall survival (27.8 vs. 14.6 months; HR 0.46; p = 0.04) and a higher R0 resection rate (62.5% vs. 33.3%) [ ]. However, the trial was closed early due to slow accrual and included a heterogeneous BTC population rather than ICC alone; thus, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Importantly, recent data specific to ICC has reinforced this strategy; a 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis confirmed that neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by surgery was associated with improved overall survival compared to upfront surgery in selected patients with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma [ ].
For initially unresectable, liver-confined ICC, locoregional strategies such as hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC) combined with systemic chemotherapy have emerged as promising conversion options. A phase II study of floxuridine-based HAIC plus systemic gemcitabine and oxaliplatin reported a partial response rate of 58% and allowed conversion to resection in a subset of patients, resulting in a median overall survival of 25 months [ ].
Looking forward, the integration of molecular profiling into the perioperative setting represents the next frontier. While current standards rely on cytotoxic chemotherapy, ongoing trials are evaluating the addition of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors (such as durvalumab or pembrolizumab) to neoadjuvant regimens. Early feasibility work is also exploring whether patients with FGFR2 fusions or IDH1 mutations might benefit from targeted neoadjuvant approaches, though this remains experimental. At present, neoadjuvant and conversion strategies should be implemented within experienced multidisciplinary teams, preferably in clinical trials.

## Locoregional therapy
Most patients with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) are diagnosed at an advanced stage and are therefore ineligible for curative surgical resection. For these patients, locoregional therapy modalities play a particularly important role in disease management. With the continuous advancement of locoregional therapeutic techniques, these interventions have become an essential component of the treatment strategy for unresectable ICC.
Currently, a variety of locoregional therapy options are available, including transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), transarterial radioembolization (TARE), hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC), thermal ablation, and external beam radiotherapy.

## Transarterial locoregional therapies
Transarterial therapies deliver therapeutic agents directly into the tumor-supplying artery via a catheter. These approaches maximize local tumor exposure while minimizing systemic toxicity. The two main modalities are transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) and transarterial radioembolization (TARE), which employ different mechanisms - chemotherapy with ischemic embolization versus localized radiation, respectively.

## Transarterial chemoembolization (TACE)
TACE involves injecting chemotherapy agents such as cisplatin, doxorubicin, mitomycin, or irinotecan into the tumor’s arterial supply using iodized oil or drug-loaded microspheres as carriers. Studies have reported an objective response rate (ORR) ranging from 10% to 50% in ICC patients following TACE [ , ].
A phase II clinical trial [ ] (NCT01648023, DELTIC) demonstrated that irinotecan-loaded microsphere TACE combined with systemic chemotherapy using gemcitabine and cisplatin achieved superior outcomes compared with systemic chemotherapy alone, significantly improving progression-free survival (PFS) (31.9 months vs. 10.1 months, P = 0.028) and overall survival (OS) (33.7 months vs. 12.6 months, P = 0.048). Furthermore, TACE combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors enhanced survival and treatment tolerance compared with systemic chemotherapy alone [ ].
Prophylactic TACE administered after surgery has also been shown to significantly improve postoperative survival [ ]. As an essential locoregional therapy, TACE achieves substantial therapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic adverse reactions. When combined with systemic chemotherapy or other modalities, it can further improve patient prognosis [ ].

## Transarterial radioembolization (TARE)
TARE involves the infusion of glass or resin microspheres carrying yttrium-90 (Y-90) into the arterial supply of the tumor, where beta radiation induces localized tumor damage. Studies have demonstrated that TARE combined with chemotherapy achieves an ORR of 39% and a disease control rate of 98% at 3 months, with a PFS of 14 months and a median overall survival (mOS) of 22 months. Approximately 22% of patients were successfully downstaged to surgical resection [ ].
TARE combined with chemotherapy not only improves the prognosis of patients with unresectable ICC but also provides conversion opportunities for surgical intervention, making it a valuable option for advanced or conversion therapy settings [ ]. Recent prospective studies confirm that TARE plus chemotherapy achieves better outcomes than chemotherapy alone and remains effective even in patients previously treated with chemotherapy [ , ]. However, further large-scale clinical trials are required to validate these results.

## Hepatic Arterial Infusion Chemotherapy (HAIC)
HAIC involves the continuous infusion of chemotherapy drugs through a catheter into the arterial blood supply of the tumor, resulting in a significantly higher local drug concentration compared to systemic administration. This approach enhances the cytotoxic effect on tumor tissue while minimizing systemic toxicity.
Studies have shown that in patients with multifocal ICC, the median survival and 5-year survival rates of those receiving fluorouracil-based HAIC are not significantly different from those undergoing hepatic resection [ ]. However, in patients with multifocal disease requiring extensive hepatic resection, the procedure carries a higher risk of surgical complications and postoperative liver failure [ ]. Therefore, HAIC may serve as a feasible alternative to surgery in appropriately selected patients. Cercek et al. [ ] conducted a phase II clinical trial using fluorouracil-based HAIC in combination with systemic gemcitabine and oxaliplatin. The study demonstrated a disease control rate of 84%, partial response rate of 58%, 6-month progression-free survival (PFS) rate of 84%, and a median overall survival (mOS) of 25 months. Based on these results, HAIC, when combined with systemic chemotherapy or other modalities, has shown superior efficacy compared with chemotherapy or arterial embolization alone [ ].
HAIC represents a feasible and effective treatment option, particularly for patients with insufficient residual liver volume or impaired hepatic reserve who are unsuitable for surgical resection.

## Thermal ablation
Thermal ablation is a minimally invasive locoregional therapy guided by imaging modalities such as ultrasound or computed tomography (CT). An ablation probe is inserted to the tumor site, where thermal or cryogenic energy induces coagulative necrosis and destruction of tumor tissue. Commonly used ablation techniques include radiofrequency ablation (RFA), microwave ablation (MWA), and cryoablation.
A retrospective study reported that the 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year overall survival (OS) rates of ICC patients treated with thermal ablation were 82.4%, 42.1%, and 28.5%, respectively. The procedure demonstrated particularly favorable safety outcomes in tumors smaller than 3 cm. In contrast, older age, multiple lesions, and larger tumor size were associated with shorter OS after ablation [ ].
A multicenter cohort study further indicated that when adequate margins (> 5 mm) are achieved, both surgical resection and thermal ablation yield comparable prognoses [ ]. Therefore, for recurrent or small (< 3 cm) lesions, thermal ablation is often preferred due to its safety profile and ability to avoid surgical complications while maintaining treatment efficacy. Additionally, it preserves liver parenchyma, allowing for future interventions in the event of recurrence or new lesions [ ].
However, for larger tumors, ablation may leave residual viable tissue, and radiofrequency ablation is generally not recommended for lesions with large diameters. Nonetheless, for patients who are not candidates for surgical treatment, ablation remains an important and effective locoregional therapeutic option [ ].

## Radiotherapy
Radiotherapy utilizes high-energy radiation or particle beams to eradicate tumor cells. It is primarily indicated for patients with localized tumors that are not amenable to surgical resection, aiming to delay disease progression and alleviate symptoms such as pain.
Among ICC patients who are ineligible for surgery, those receiving high-dose radiotherapy demonstrate a significantly higher 3-year local control rate compared with patients treated with low-dose radiation. High-dose radiotherapy has been shown to improve both local control and overall survival (OS) in unresectable ICC [ ]. One study reported a median radiation dose of 58.0 Gy, achieving a 2-year local control rate of 94.1% and a 2-year OS rate of 46.5% [ ].
Furthermore, radiotherapy combined with targeted therapy, immunotherapy, or chemotherapy has yielded promising therapeutic outcomes and good tolerability in ICC patients [ ].
Although radiotherapy is not the first-line treatment, it remains an essential option for advanced-stage ICC, particularly in cases with local recurrence or metastasis where surgical intervention is not feasible. Respiratory motion during treatment can cause minor instability in the irradiation field, leading to potential exposure of surrounding normal tissues.
However, with advances in radiotherapy techniques and motion management, these risks can be minimized. Radiotherapy therefore represents a valuable component of comprehensive treatment strategies for ICC, offering disease control and symptomatic relief while contributing to individualized therapeutic approaches.

## Systemic therapy
Systemic therapy is the mainstay of treatment for patients with unresectable or metastatic intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) and plays an increasingly central role in conversion and perioperative strategies. Over the past decade, the treatment landscape has evolved from gemcitabine–cisplatin alone to routine use of chemo‑immunotherapy in the first‑line setting, with molecularly targeted agents and fluoropyrimidine‑based regimens reserved for subsequent lines of therapy.

## Chemotherapy
For over a decade, gemcitabine plus cisplatin was the global standard of care based on the ABC‑02 trial, which demonstrated a survival benefit over gemcitabine alone (median OS 11.7 vs. 8.1 months; HR 0.64; p < 0.001) [ ]. This doublet remains the backbone of current combinatorial strategies.
More recently, the integration of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has redefined first‑line therapy. The phase III TOPAZ‑1 trial showed that adding durvalumab to gemcitabine–cisplatin significantly prolonged overall survival compared with chemotherapy alone (median 12.8 vs. 11.5 months; HR 0.80), with durable responses and a manageable safety profile [ ]. Similarly, the global phase III KEYNOTE‑966 study demonstrated that pembrolizumab combined with gemcitabine–cisplatin improved median overall survival (12.7 vs. 10.9 months; HR 0.83; p = 0.0034) without new safety signals [ ]. On the basis of these data, PD‑1/PD‑L1–based chemo‑immunotherapy—particularly durvalumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin, and more recently pembrolizumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin where approved—is now recommended as the preferred first‑line option for fit patients with unresectable or metastatic biliary tract cancer, including ICC.
Alternative chemotherapy regimens remain relevant for specific populations or where access to immunotherapy is limited. The Japanese FUGA‑BT (JCOG1113) trial established gemcitabine plus S‑1 (GS) as non‑inferior to gemcitabine–cisplatin [ ], while the KHBO1401‑MITSUBA trial demonstrated higher response rates with a triplet regimen of gemcitabine–cisplatin–S‑1 (GCS) [ ]. Intensive triplets such as gemcitabine–cisplatin–nab‑paclitaxel have shown high response rates (45%) and meaningful conversion surgery rates, albeit with increased toxicity [ ]. Conversely, for patients seeking to minimize hospital visits or in whom infusional therapy is less feasible, capecitabine plus oxaliplatin (CAPOX) serves as a practical, non‑inferior alternative to gemcitabine–oxaliplatin (GEMOX) [ ].
Collectively, these findings highlight the ongoing optimization of systemic chemotherapy for ICC. Despite potential toxicities, chemotherapy remains an essential therapeutic modality for unresectable and recurrent disease. In modern clinical practice, integrating chemotherapy with targeted therapy, immunotherapy, or locoregional treatments is a key strategy to enhance treatment efficacy and overall outcomes. ## Targeted therapy
Molecular profiling has transformed the management of ICC, identifying actionable alterations in approximately 40–50% of patients. Key targets include FGFR2 fusions, IDH1 mutations, BRAF V600E, HER2 amplification or overexpression, and NTRK/RET fusions [ , , ]. Targeted agents are generally deployed in the second‑line setting after progression on gemcitabine‑based chemotherapy.

## FGFR2 fusions
Occurring in 10–15% of ICC, predominantly in the small‑duct subtype, FGFR2 fusions are well‑validated therapeutic targets. In the phase II FIGHT‑202 study, pemigatinib achieved an objective response rate of 35.5% and a median overall survival of 21.1 months in previously treated patients with FGFR2‑rearranged cholangiocarcinoma [ ]. Infigratinib produced an objective response rate of 23.1% and a median overall survival of 12.2 months in a similar population [ ]. The irreversible covalent inhibitor futibatinib (FOENIX‑CCA2) achieved a median overall survival of 21.7 months and has shown activity in some patients with acquired resistance mutations after prior reversible FGFR inhibitors, underscoring the need to understand resistance mechanisms when sequencing FGFR‑directed therapies [ ].

## IDH1 mutations
Present in up to 20% of ICC, IDH1 mutations are targeted by ivosidenib. The phase III ClarIDHy trial confirmed that ivosidenib significantly improved progression‑free survival versus placebo (2.7 vs. 1.4 months; HR 0.37) and increased disease control, establishing it as a standard second‑line option for IDH1‑mutant ICC [ ].

## Emerging targets
HER2‑positive biliary tract cancers (by amplification or overexpression) have seen major advances with the antibody–drug conjugate trastuzumab deruxtecan, which showed robust activity in the DESTINY‑PanTumor02 trial [ ]. BRAF V600E–mutated tumors respond to combined BRAF and MEK inhibition with dabrafenib plus trametinib [ ]. NTRK and RET fusions, although rare, are sensitive to tumor‑agnostic inhibitors such as entrectinib and selpercatinib, which have demonstrated high response rates and durable benefit across solid tumors [ , ].
Overall, targeted agents such as FGFR2 and IDH1 inhibitors are typically used in the second‑line or later setting after failure of gemcitabine–platinum–based chemotherapy in patients with good performance status. Early, comprehensive molecular profiling is therefore essential to ensure that suitable candidates are identified in time to benefit from these therapies.

## Immunotherapy
Beyond first‑line chemo‑immunotherapy, single‑agent ICIs have limited activity in unselected ICC but can be highly effective in specific biomarker‑defined subgroups. The KEYNOTE‑158 and KEYNOTE‑028 studies showed that while overall response rates to pembrolizumab are low in unselected biliary tract cancers, durable responses can be achieved in tumors with microsatellite instability‑high (MSI‑H) or mismatch repair‑deficient (dMMR) status, supporting tumor‑agnostic approval in these settings [ , ].
Investigational strategies are focused on overcoming the immunosuppressive microenvironment characteristic of many ICCs. Combinations such as pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib have shown promising synergistic activity in refractory biliary tract cancer, with an objective response rate of 25% and a disease control rate of 78% in a phase II study [ ]. At the same time, transcriptomic and microenvironment‑based classifications have identified distinct immune subtypes of ICC—such as immune‑desert, myeloid‑rich, and inflamed phenotypes—which may differentially respond to checkpoint inhibitors and rational combinations [ , ]. These insights are beginning to guide the design of tailored immunotherapy strategies, although they are not yet routinely used in clinical decision‑making.

## First‑line
For fit patients (ECOG performance status 0–1), gemcitabine–cisplatin combined with a PD‑1/PD‑L1 inhibitor—most commonly durvalumab based on TOPAZ‑1, and more recently pembrolizumab based on KEYNOTE‑966—represents the preferred first‑line approach where approved [ , ]. The choice between durvalumab‑ and pembrolizumab‑based regimens is largely driven by regulatory status, reimbursement, and institutional experience, as both have demonstrated a modest but clinically meaningful survival advantage over gemcitabine–cisplatin alone.

## Second‑line (targeted therapy)
Upon progression, comprehensive molecular profiling is strongly recommended. Patients with FGFR2 fusions, IDH1 mutations, BRAF V600E, HER2 amplification/overexpression, or NTRK/RET fusions should be considered for matched targeted therapies, which generally offer better tolerability and quality of life than cytotoxic salvage regimens [ , , , , – , , , , ]. Sequencing among targeted agents (e.g., switching from reversible to covalent FGFR inhibitors after resistance) should be guided by the resistance profile where possible, potentially utilizing liquid biopsy (ctDNA) to identify emergence of resistance mutations.

## Second‑line (chemotherapy)
For patients without actionable driver mutations (approximately 50–60% of ICC), the ABC‑06 trial established FOLFOX (fluorouracil plus oxaliplatin) plus best supportive care as the standard second‑line regimen, offering a survival benefit over best supportive care alone (median OS 6.2 vs. 5.3 months; HR 0.69) [ ]. The NIFTY trial identified liposomal irinotecan plus fluorouracil and leucovorin as an emerging alternative, improving progression‑free and overall survival compared with fluorouracil and leucovorin alone [ ]. Selection between these regimens depends on prior toxicities, performance status, and patient preference.

## Summary
Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) is characterized by aggressive biological behavior and remains associated with poor prognosis and significant impairment of patient quality of life. Surgical resection continues to be the only potentially curative option; however, the majority of patients present with unresectable disease, and even those who achieve R0 resection experience high recurrence rates.
To improve outcomes, various therapeutic strategies including conversion therapy, local interventions, and systemic treatments have been developed and increasingly integrated into multidisciplinary care. Clinical studies have demonstrated encouraging results, although treatment responses vary substantially, particularly with targeted therapy and immunotherapy. This heterogeneity is largely attributable to differences in molecular alterations and the tumor immune microenvironment. Advances in molecular and omics-based classification have enabled more accurate stratification of ICC and guided the rational selection of targeted and immune therapies, thereby promoting individualized treatment and improved efficacy with reduced toxicity.
Despite these advances, several challenges persist, including the limited availability of effective drugs for rare molecular subtypes, the lack of robust predictive biomarkers, therapeutic resistance, and the high cost and uneven accessibility of genomic testing. In addition, important clinical questions remain unresolved, such as the optimal extent of lymphadenectomy, the indications and timing for liver transplantation, the best perioperative regimen for resectable disease, and the most effective way to integrate locoregional therapies with systemic chemo-immunotherapy and targeted agents. Resistance mechanisms—such as secondary FGFR2 mutations after FGFR inhibition, metabolic plasticity in IDH1-mutant tumors, and immune escape in immune-cold or stromal-rich microenvironments—further limit the durability of current treatments and highlight the need for next-generation inhibitors and rational combination strategies. Nevertheless, emerging evidence continues to support the clinical value of molecular-guided targeted and immunotherapies, and ongoing research is expected to yield novel therapeutic agents and reliable biomarkers that can optimize precision treatment strategies.
With the continued advancement of genomic sequencing, multi-omics integration, and refined molecular classification, alongside evolving surgical, locoregional, and systemic approaches, the management of ICC is moving toward a fully personalized and multidisciplinary model. Importantly, molecular profiling is increasingly influencing surgical decision making, candidate selection for transplantation, and the design of multimodal treatment pathways, including perioperative and conversion strategies. These developments, together with improved understanding of resistance and tumor microenvironmental subtypes, hold promise to further improve prognosis, minimize treatment-related toxicity, and enhance long-term survival and quality of life for patients with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma.
