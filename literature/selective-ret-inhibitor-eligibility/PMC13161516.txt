## Clinical impact of genotype‐timed surgery

RET‐guided timing has been associated with an earlier stage of disease at diagnosis (Figure  ). In a study by Raue et al., the proportion of patients who presented with localized stage I–II MTC increased from 11% to 32% after the introduction of genetic testing, whereas the biochemical remission rate rose from 20% to 43%. Schreinemakers et al. reported a five‐fold reduction in lymph node metastasis (4% vs. 20%). 

 The postoperative outcomes mirror these stage shifts. In Norway, biochemical remission among presymptomatic carriers with early stage disease increased from 50% to 89% after systematic genotyping. The same study demonstrated that the proportion of screened individuals already harboring invasive MTC at surgery fell from 100% to 39%, underscoring that molecular testing enables the prevention of invasive cancer, rather than a cure, in individuals who never developed clinically manifest disease. In contrast, US data revealed that persistent disease (69% vs. 39%) and distant metastases (55% vs. 31%) were substantially more frequent when surgery was performed in the pretesting era. Even when differences in recurrence did not reach statistical significance, the directional benefit of genotype‐guided intervention remained consistent.

## Balancing benefits and potential long‐term consequences
However, early thyroidectomy is not without its costs. Lifelong thyroid hormone replacement is mandatory, and pediatric surgery carries psychological, metabolic, and quality‐of‐life implications that must be addressed through longitudinal follow‐up. These potential sequelae are the reason why current practice emphasizes variant‐specific risk stratification rather than indiscriminate prophylactic surgery in all mutation carriers.

## Integration into tumor board decision‐making
American Thyroid Association and European Thyroid Association guidelines now mandate germline RET genotyping in all patients with MTC, , not merely for diagnosis but also as the foundation of the surgical strategy. The surgeon’s task is to decide when genotyping should occur and how extensive it should be based on the biologic behavior predicted by the genotype. The family discussed in this virtual tumor board article illustrates the evolution of germline RET testing and surgical decisions. The proband and his brother, who were diagnosed after disease manifestation, underwent reactive surgery and developed persistent or metastatic disease. In contrast, their children, identified solely through genetic testing, underwent thyroidectomy before malignant transformation and have never developed clinical MTC.
The surgical approach to asymptomatic RET carriers has evolved from genotype‐based, prophylactic thyroidectomy to calcitonin‐guided, preemptive surgery. This paradigm recognizes a window of opportunity for safe surveillance, particularly for moderate‐risk mutations like p.Cys609Ser. Deferring surgery until calcitonin becomes elevated avoids surgical morbidity—permanent hypoparathyroidism, nerve injury—and delays lifelong levothyroxine therapy. In this family, the proband's children might have been managed differently today, with surgery deferred for a decade or more. This contrast illustrates both the power of genetic testing and the ongoing refinement of surgical timing: achieving a cure while minimizing the surgical footprint and preserving quality of life.

## MEDICAL ONCOLOGY PERSPECTIVE

## From indolent surveillance to precision targeted therapy
From the perspective of medical oncologists, MTC represents a uniquely heterogeneous disease course. Many patients experience long periods of biochemical progression without structural disease, whereas others develop aggressive, rapidly progressive metastases. Therefore, the central challenge is not simply how to treat but when to treat, balancing the risks of premature systemic therapy against the consequences of delayed intervention. Before the molecular era, systemic treatment options were limited, largely empirical, and associated with modest benefits and significant toxicity. , , The introduction of germline RET genotyping allowed oncologists to distinguish RET‐driven disease biology and paved the way for targeted therapies that anchor the modern management of MTC.

## Indications for initiating systemic therapy and circulating tumor DNA
Not all patients with metastatic MTC require immediate treatment. Many individuals, including the proband in this tumor board, exhibit prolonged periods of biochemical progression with radiographically stable disease and minimal symptoms. In such patients, careful surveillance is often appropriate. Systemic therapy is generally reserved for patients with radiographic disease progression, symptomatic metastatic disease, threatened organ function, or rapidly rising calcitonin and CEA levels with short doubling times. This approach reflects the natural history of MTC, in which tumor burden and clinical behavior guide treatment initiation. The variable behavior of metastatic MTC is well illustrated by this patient's 11‐year period of stable liver disease followed by the eventual emergence of bone metastases. Although such prolonged stability is at the favorable end of the spectrum, it is not unique. The biologic basis for this variability is incompletely understood but likely reflects tumor grade, specific germline RET mutation, host factors, and tumor heterogeneity.
This uncertainty underscores the need for better biomarkers to guide therapeutic timing. Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) analysis has emerged as a promising tool for detecting molecular progression before radiographic changes, monitoring treatment response, and identifying emerging resistance mutations. Detection rates of ctDNA in advanced MTC vary considerably, depending on the assay used, mutation profile, and disease status. Recent studies indicate that ctDNA is detectable in a majority of patients with advanced or progressive disease. For example, Ciampi et al. identified RET‐mutated ctDNA in 88.9% of patients who had progressive, high‐tumor‐burden MTC but in only 44.4% overall in a cohort of patients with advanced MTC and in 0% of those who had stable disease, indicating that detection is highly dependent on disease activity and burden. 

However, the integration of ctDNA into clinical decision‐making raises fundamental questions: Should we treat molecular progression before radiographic progression? What threshold of ctDNA increase justifies intervention? Does earlier therapy improve long‐term outcomes? These questions remain unanswered, highlighting the central dilemma in managing indolent malignancies—decisions made today may only be proven right or wrong a decade or more later. Until prospective data are available, ctDNA should be considered a complementary tool to conventional imaging and biochemical markers, not a replacement for them. In this patient, ctDNA monitoring could theoretically have provided earlier warning of impending bone metastases, but whether this would have altered management, given his asymptomatic status and lack of cord compromise, remains uncertain.

## Evolution of systemic therapy: From multikinase inhibitors to selective RET inhibition
For many years, systemic therapy has relied on multikinase inhibitors, such as vandetanib and cabozantinib, which target RET among several other kinases. Although these agents improved progression‐free survival, their use was limited by off‐target toxicities, including hypertension, diarrhea, fatigue, QT prolongation, and hand–foot syndrome. Dose reductions and treatment discontinuation were common, particularly in patients with indolent diseases.
The therapeutic landscape has changed dramatically with the development of highly selective RET inhibitors, most notably selpercatinib and pralsetinib. An important principle from clinical trials is that selective RET‐inhibitor efficacy is independent of whether the RET mutation is germline or somatic. In the LIBRETTO‐001 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT03157128), response rates were 77% (germline) and 71% (somatic) ; and the ARROW trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT03037385) demonstrated response rates of 79% and 69%, respectively. This has two clinical implications: (1) patients with known germline RET mutations (like this family) are excellent candidates for selective RET inhibition without need for additional tumor testing; and (2) patients with sporadic MTC must undergo somatic tumor testing to determine eligibility. In both populations, selective RET inhibitors have transformed outcomes, producing durable responses and substantially improved tolerability compared with traditional multikinase inhibitors. These agents were designed specifically to inhibit RET signaling, producing deeper and more durable responses with substantially improved tolerability. , Consequently, selective RET inhibition is now considered the preferred first‐line systemic therapy for patients with progressive, unresectable, or metastatic RET‐mutant MTC.

## Integrating molecular results into long‐term management
Germline RET sequencing actively informs therapeutic decision‐making. In patients with documented germline RET mutations, medical oncologists can anticipate the availability of highly effective targeted therapies at the time of disease progression. This knowledge allows greater confidence in adopting initial surveillance strategies for patients with asymptomatic or indolent metastatic diseases. This approach is exemplified by the proband discussed in this tumor board report: despite the presence of metastatic involvement, his prolonged disease stability and absence of symptoms justified extended observation, supported by the expectation that potent targeted therapy remains available when progression occurs.
In contrast to germline testing, which identifies inherited mutations, somatic RET testing is performed directly on tumor tissue to detect acquired mutations present only in cancer cells. Approximately 50% of sporadic MTCs harbor somatic RET mutations, most commonly M918T, that predict response to selective RET inhibitors. In this patient, somatic testing was not performed because germline testing had already confirmed RET‐driven disease.
Thus the role of medical oncologists has evolved from managing late‐stage complications to orchestrating long‐term, molecularly informed disease control. Germline RET testing allows therapy to be individualized not only by stage and tumor burden but also by the underlying oncogenic driver, transforming MTC from a disease of limited options into a model of precision oncology. Therefore, in a modern tumor board, systemic therapy decisions are inseparable from molecular diagnosis, surgical timing, endocrine surveillance, and genetic counseling. The case presented here exemplifies this. Indeed, the molecular identification of the germline RET mutation structured the surgical course, guided family screening, enabled prolonged safe surveillance, and established a clear therapeutic roadmap for future disease progression.

## RADIATION ONCOLOGIST PERSPECTIVE

## The role of radiation in a molecularly stratified disease
From the perspective of radiation oncology, MTC occupies a distinct position among endocrine malignancies. Unlike differentiated thyroid cancers, MTC is not iodine‐avid and thus does not benefit from radioactive iodine therapy. Consequently, the role of external‐beam radiotherapy (EBRT) in MTC has traditionally been limited to select, high‐risk, palliative, or oncologic emergency scenarios rather than serving as a routine component of primary management. 

The introduction of germline RET molecular testing has not diminished the importance of radiation therapy, but it has refined its indications. In the contemporary era, radiation oncology may contribute primarily in three settings: (1) adjuvant therapy for patients with high‐risk locoregional disease; (2) definitive local control for unresectable or incompletely resected tumors; and (3) palliation/oncologic emergencies of symptomatic metastatic sites, particularly bone and central nervous system lesions. , 

## Adjuvant and locoregional disease control
Evidence supporting the use of postoperative EBRT in MTC remains limited. Although retrospective studies have suggested a potential improvement in locoregional control among patients with high‐risk features—such as gross extrathyroidal extension, extensive lymph node involvement, extranodal extension, or microscopically positive surgical margins—the available data are derived from relatively small, heterogeneous cohorts. Importantly, no randomized controlled trials have demonstrated the benefit of this strategy, and the extent to which EBRT translates into meaningful clinical benefit remains uncertain. Consequently, the routine use of adjuvant radiation in MTC remains controversial.
Within the virtual tumor board framework, RET germline genotyping may provide additional clinical context, although its role in guiding radiation decisions is not clearly established. Patients harboring aggressive variants, such as M918T or certain codon 634 mutations, may present with more advanced locoregional disease and could theoretically benefit from intensified local control strategies in individualized cases. However, in patients with lower risk genotypes who undergo complete surgical resection, the potential morbidity associated with EBRT frequently outweighs its uncertain benefit. Therefore, decisions regarding adjuvant radiation require careful multidisciplinary deliberation, integrating surgical findings, disease burden, molecular context, and patient‐specific factors.

## Palliation and oligometastatic control
Radiation oncology plays a central role in the management of patients with metastatic MTC. Bone metastases are common and frequently cause pain, instability, and neurologic compromise in oncological emergencies. EBRT offers effective palliation, improves quality of life, and can prevent catastrophic skeletal events. , Stereotactic body radiation therapy has further expanded the therapeutic repertoire, allowing for the precise treatment of oligometastatic lesions with high local control rates and limited toxicity. , , , 

The proband discussed in this tumor board exemplifies this paradigm. Despite the development of skeletal lesions identified on PET‐CT imaging, the absence of symptoms, absence of structural instability, and no epidural spinal cord compression supported continued surveillance and medical management, with radiation therapy reserved for future symptom development or local disease progression. This strategy reflects the contemporary integration of radiation therapy into a broader, molecularly guided treatment plan.

## Integration with targeted systemic therapy
The availability of highly effective, selective RET inhibitors has altered the sequencing of local and systemic therapy. In patients with widespread metastatic disease, systemic RET inhibition serves as the principal disease‐modifying intervention, with radiation deployed strategically for local control or palliation. Close coordination between medical and radiation oncology is essential to optimize sequencing, minimize overlapping toxicities, and maximize durable disease control outcomes.
Thus, in the molecular era, radiation oncology for MTC is no longer reactive but strategically selective. It functions as a precision local therapy embedded within a comprehensive, genotype‐informed treatment plan, enhancing disease control, alleviating symptoms, and preserving the quality of life across the full trajectory of this complex disease.

## MEDICAL GENETICIST PERSPECTIVE
From the medical geneticist’s perspective, this case illustrates the full clinical power of germline RET molecular testing, not merely as a diagnostic tool for an individual patient but as the initiating event in a multigenerational prevention strategy. Once the proband’s germline RET p.Cys609Ser pathogenic variant was identified, the clinical focus immediately expanded from tumor management to family‐based risk reduction in the proband’s family.
Cascade testing identified four additional RET p.Cys609Ser carriers across generations. The proband's brother, unscreened until age 37 years, presented with metastatic MTC (preoperative calcitonin, 2450 pg/mL) and required total thyroidectomy with bilateral neck dissection; he has persistent disease and is now on selpercatinib. In contrast, all screened carriers who underwent prophylactic thyroidectomy remain disease‐free. All have undetectable calcitonin and receive normal annual surveillance. This multigenerational pedigree powerfully illustrates that RET testing and cascade screening can convert a lethal hereditary cancer into a preventable condition.
Beyond the risk of thyroid cancer, germline RET testing also establishes lifelong surveillance obligations for MEN2‐associated conditions. All mutation carriers in this family now undergo structured monitoring for pheochromocytoma and primary hyperparathyroidism, which may remain clinically silent until life‐threatening complications occur. Early identification of these manifestations is as critical as thyroid cancer prevention and underscores the systemic nature of MEN2 management.
Importantly, the genetic result also provided prognostic clarity for the proband. Knowledge of the specific RET variant informed expectations regarding disease behavior, metastatic risk, and long‐term management, allowing the clinical team to integrate surveillance, surgical planning, systemic therapy sequencing, and family counseling into a unified care strategy for the patient.
In this context, medical geneticists are important professionals in the tumor board. Germline RET testing becomes the pivot point around which individualized treatment, family prevention, and multigenerational risk management are organized. This case demonstrates that modern genetic oncology enables not only earlier diagnosis but also the conversion of lethal hereditary diseases into preventable conditions for future generations.

## CONCLUSION
The case presented in this virtual tumor board encapsulates the dual realities of MTC management in the molecular era, as shown in Figure  . The proband’s diagnosis at the metastatic stage illustrates the consequences of delayed detection, whereas the timely prophylactic surgery performed in his children exemplifies the life‐altering potential of precision prevention strategies. Germline RET molecular testing has reshaped the clinical paradigm of hereditary MTC, shifting care from late‐stage intervention to early identification and targeted prevention of the disease.
 Considering 4 decades of evidence, the virtual tumor board discussed the integration of germline RET testing, which has transformed diagnosis from reliance on phenotypic suspicion to the certainty offered by genotypic confirmation of germline RET mutations in MTC. This transition has lowered the age at which surgical intervention is performed, enabled preemptive management before malignant transformation, and improved recurrence‐free survival rates. This represents a foundational reorganization of care, in which multidisciplinary decision‐making anchored in molecular data becomes more proactive than reactive.
The evolution of germline RET testing in MTC offers a powerful blueprint for precision oncology: a model that integrates molecular discovery with clinical strategy, empowers families through prevention, and anchors the management in evidence‐based risk stratification. The story of the family at the center of this virtual tumor board is thus more than illustrative; it is a call to action for society. Precision oncology must strive for an outcome in which the patient’s prognosis reflects biologic risk rather than geographic or socioeconomic factors.

## PATIENT PERSPECTIVE
Reflecting on my medical journey, the moment I finally received a molecular diagnosis marked a turning point that reshaped my understanding of the illness. For many years, the absence of a clear explanation for what was happening in my body created a constant feeling of uncertainty. The diagnosis did not change the past, but it finally gave a name and structure to an experience that had been confusing and frightening for so long.
When the germline RET mutation was identified, it had a profound emotional impact, not only on me but also on my family. Learning that my children were carriers was initially overwhelming and painful. However, the clarity provided by testing quickly transformed fear into a sense of direction and control over the disease. Through the process known as cascade testing, each family member can understand their own risk. Making the decision for my children, who had no symptoms, to undergo preventive surgery was one of the hardest choices of my life. However, it was also unmistakably correct. Knowing that the disease was stopped before it could develop and seeing them cured before their lives were touched by cancer has brought me a level of peace that I could not have imagined at the beginning of this journey.
Based on this personal experience, I strongly believe that access to molecular testing should not depend on luck, geography, or financial circumstances. My family’s outcome was possible only because the test was available to us, and I cannot ignore the fact that many others never received this chance. If public agencies and policymakers made genetic testing more widely accessible, especially for families with a known history or signs of hereditary conditions, many lives could be changed as ours have been. For me, this testing did more than identify a mutation; it broke a cycle that had silently passed through generations of my family. Expanding access would allow countless other families to experience the possibility of early intervention, prevention, and hope.
