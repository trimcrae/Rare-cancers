## Abstract
Regional differences in approved drug dosages and administration are shaped by complex factors, including clinical data, prior regulatory decisions, and region‐specific considerations. This study investigated how such factors are associated with variations in approved doses across the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA), European Medicines Agency (EMA), and Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) in the context of recent global regulatory practices. We analyzed 111 new molecular entities approved in the United States and Japan from 2017 to 2023, incorporating EMA data where available. Dose concordance, defined as agreement between regions on the labeled approved dosing regimen for the same indication, was observed in 77% between PMDA and FDA and 84% between EMA and FDA, higher than rates reported in earlier studies. Nevertheless, significant regional differences remain. Qualitative analysis suggested that discordance in approved doses was associated with a small number of high‐level factors, including differences in benefit–risk perspectives, approaches to dose determination, population‐related considerations. Quantitative analysis indicated associations with trial design and submission timing. In a subset of 11 products in which FDA approval preceded approvals by other agencies and involved FDA‐led dose modifications, patterns were observed that were consistent with potential interdependence in regulatory decision making, while sponsor‐driven harmonization and shared evidence bases may also underlie these patterns. These findings suggest that early regulatory engagement and international coordination may support efficient dose selection, although attention must be paid to differing benefit–risk assessment considerations across regions.

## Study Highlights

WHAT IS THE CURRENT KNOWLEDGE ON THE TOPIC?

Although several comparative studies have examined regulatory outcomes across agencies, only a few have systematically analyzed the regional differences in approved dosages or the underlying factors driving these differences, particularly in the context of recent global drug development.

WHAT QUESTION DID THIS STUDY ADDRESS?

This study investigated regional differences in the approved dosages of new drugs approved in the United States and Japan from 2017 to 2023, incorporating EMA data where available, and explored factors associated with observed divergences.

WHAT DOES THIS STUDY ADD TO OUR KNOWLEDGE?

By focusing on the regulatory reasoning behind dose decisions and sponsor‐driven factors, such as clinical data package composition and submission sequencing, this study provides new insights into how regulatory agencies evaluate dosage and administration, revealing region‐specific approaches and potential interdependencies that have not been systematically analyzed before.

HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY OR TRANSLATIONAL SCIENCE?

The high concordance in the approved dosages is suggestive of increasing globalization of clinical development and advances in clinical pharmacology and translational science. The remaining regional differences underscore the need to account for distinct benefit–risk perspectives while fostering further collaboration and convergence across regions.

In today's globalized pharmaceutical research and development landscape, regulatory reviews conducted by national or regional authorities are becoming increasingly interdependent, with decisions in one country often influencing those in others. Against this backdrop, the value of harmonization and collaboration among regulatory authorities has become more significant than ever before. 

At the same time, understanding the differences in regulatory expectations and benefit–risk assessment considerations remains critical for promoting globally aligned development while addressing region‐specific needs. Dosage and administration, in particular, are central reflections of benefit–risk considerations across regions, serving as key determinants in regulatory approval decisions and among the elements most likely to vary across countries. , 

Although several comparative studies have examined regulatory outcomes across agencies, they have primarily focused on approval status itself, often drawing on perspectives within regulatory bodies. , , To date, only a few have systematically investigated the underlying considerations driving differences in key labeling elements, and no comprehensive analysis has clarified the nature of interdependence among authorities, particularly in the context of recent global development environments. Focusing on dosage and administration enables examination of regional decision differences and the dynamics of interdependence.
Therefore, this study examined dosage and administration to identify interregional differences in approved regimens and the underlying factors. We selected the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA), and European Medicines Agency (EMA) because they offer a high degree of overlap in approved drugs and provide publicly available review information, enabling meaningful comparisons.
Although limited to these three agencies, each represents distinct institutional, cultural, and geographic backgrounds that may influence regional differences in dosage decisions. These differences may arise from several factors, including: (i) demographic, genetic, and clinical context differences, such as variations in pharmacokinetics (PK), exposure–response relationships (efficacy and safety) due to intrinsic or extrinsic factors, or treatment practices; (ii) regulatory priorities and benefit–risk perspectives, with some regions prioritizing minimizing risks, whereas others emphasize maximizing benefits , ; (iii) review approaches, where some agencies value inferential judgment and flexibility, whereas others adopt more rigid and consensus‐oriented processes, with prior literature suggesting that decision‐making and reasoning styles vary across cultures , ; (iv) reliance on local data or prior foreign regulatory decisions. In practice, dosage decisions may also exhibit path dependence, wherein earlier regulatory outcomes shape subsequent outcomes, especially under constraints such as limited data or compressed timelines. Even when a public health rationale supports developing an alternative dose, sponsor‐side costs, timing pressures, and conflicts with global development plans often limit the frequency of pursuing such paths.  To reflect real‐world conditions, we examined drugs approved for the same indications across regions without restricting the analysis to simultaneous submissions. Differences in submission content, influenced by prior sponsor–regulator interactions, are expected to affect review outcomes. Accordingly, we collected data on approval timing, variations in clinical data packages, and, when available, history of dose selection.
This study aimed to contribute to the practical advancement of global drug development by offering insights that support the establishment of approaches tailored to meet the health needs of populations across countries. By focusing on decision making around dosage and administration, an area central to benefit–risk assessments, this research aims to contribute to a broader understanding of regulatory review processes and support further development in the systematic analysis of regulatory decision making.

## METHODS
From the new molecular entities (NMEs) approved in both Japan and the United States between 2017 and 2023, including those approved via expedited pathways, we excluded vaccines, imaging/diagnostic agents, topical drugs, fixed‐dose combinations, and co‐packaged products, resulting in 116 eligible products. Comparisons were made based on the first approved indication; of nine products with differing first approved indications, four became comparable via subsequent label expansions, while five remained non‐comparable and were excluded. This left 111 NMEs for analysis. Among these, 105 were also authorized by the EMA for the same indications as of March 8, 2025; five of these were approved outside the 2017–2023 window but were included in the analysis.
For the United States and Japan, sponsor‐submitted and approved dosages were collected from public review reports. For the EMA, since submitted and approved dosages are not consistently disclosed, the dosage listed in the Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPC) as of March 8, 2025, was used as the approved dosage. When EMA review reports explicitly noted a different dosage from that in the SmPC, we prioritized the review report's description. We cross‐checked FDA, PMDA, and EMA review reports to confirm the plausibility of EMA‐approved dosages.
We extracted attributes potentially influencing dosage differences from review reports, including product characteristics, clinical trials supporting dose rationale, submitted clinical data packages, and modality (a full list of variables is shown in Table
 ). Therapeutic areas were identified using Citeline's Pharmaprojects. Products whose PMDA review reports explicitly concluded differences in PK and/or systemic drug exposure between Japanese and non‐Japanese populations were flagged (Ethnic PK difference = 1).
 Among these attributes, differences in submitted clinical data packages were assessed by comparing the Phase 2 and Phase 3 studies (dose‐finding and confirmatory trials) included in each region's submission with those in the FDA package, categorized as Identical (all trials the same), Partially Overlapping (some trials differ), or Regionally Distinct (no trials in common). For regression analysis, this was simplified into a binary variable, “data package discordance,” coded as 0 for Identical and 1 for the others. As discrepancies in data packages often correspond to differences in approval timing, approval timing was also considered in interpretation. We incorporated these contextual variables to address issues of confounding by sponsor submission strategy and sequencing; however, this does not separate regulatory interdependencies from sponsor‐driven submission strategies. Accordingly, observed temporal patterns across agencies should be interpreted with caution and treated as descriptive associations rather than evidence of regulatory‐led concordance.
We conducted logistic regression using regional dose discordance (1 = discordant, 0 = concordant) as the dependent variable, and attributes described above as independent variables. See Table
 for the definitions of dose discordance. The results were reported for all discordance (any type of difference) and initial dose discordance, which showed the most robust and interpretable associations.
 For qualitative analysis, we identified factors contributing to regional dose differences based on review report descriptions for the 30 products with observed discrepancies. We also collected key review issues related to dose justification from review reports beyond these 30 products and used them for case analyses and discussion.
The FDA's approval timing was generally the earliest, and the dosages were frequently modified during review (see ; ref. [ ]). To examine whether and how FDA outcomes influenced other agencies and whether this influence differed between the PMDA and EMA, we conducted qualitative comparative analysis (QCA). , QCA is a Boolean minimization method effective for analyzing complex causal relationships in small‐N studies. , , From the data set of 111 NMEs, we included products for which: (1) the FDA disagreed with the sponsor's proposed dose, resulting in dosage modification at approval, and (2) FDA approval preceded that of the PMDA or EMA, enabling assessment of subsequent alignment or divergence. We did not distinguish whether alignment with the FDA‐approved dose arose from sponsor actions or independent agency decisions but treated the final concordance or discordance as the outcome.
Data management, statistical analyses, and QCA were performed using R (version 4.4.3).

## RESULTS
The timing of approval by each regulatory authority is shown in Figure

Compared with the FDA, approval occurred later by a median of 181 days (interquartile range [IQR]: 48–284 days) in the EMA and 188 days (IQR: 53–645.5 days) in the PMDA. The observed variation in approval lag was deemed sufficient to analyze interdependencies.
 Because approved dosing regimens directly inform clinical use, (i) overall discordance (any difference) was treated as clinically meaningful by design, without assuming uniform clinical impact across cases. To aid interpretation, concordance rates were additionally examined for (ii) initial dose discordance (excluding maintenance dose differences) and (iii) overall population discordance (excluding differences in dose stratification). Discordance in overall population was observed in 14 of 111 products (13%) in the PMDA–FDA comparison and 11 of 105 products (10%) in the EMA–FDA comparison (Table

 At the FDA, approved doses differed from sponsor submissions in 14 of 111 drugs (12%): eight following FDA reanalyses with new recommendations, five due to removal of dosing options for efficacy or safety considerations, and one aligning with a clinically tested regimen. An additional 5 drugs were approved with postmarketing requirement/commitments (PMR/PMC) for alternative doses, none of which led to a label change as of March 8, 2025. At the PMDA, differences occurred in 12 of 111 drugs (11%), mostly aligning with clinically tested regimens (10 drugs), and in two cases, involving removal of dosing options.
For the 30 products with regional dosage differences, factors contributing to these discrepancies were identified from review report contents (Table

The main drivers included (1) differences in safety‐related risk evaluation, (2) divergent approaches to dosage decisions, particularly whether to adopt model‐informed dosing or doses evaluated in clinical trials, (3) considerations of ethnic differences in PK or exposure‐matching among body weight‐based subpopulations, (4) varying expectations for efficacy‐supporting evidence, and (5) other contextual factors such as local clinical practices. Dosage concordance and discordance were compared across products, reviews, and data characteristics (Table

For the EMA–FDA comparison, discordance was less frequent in oncology, more frequent when the FDA altered the dose, and more frequent when the EMA approval preceded the FDA approval.

 Of the 111 NMEs, 11 met the criteria for QCA analysis (Figure

For the PMDA, a sufficient condition for alignment with the FDA was either (i) a long approval gap combined with the absence of ethnic PK differences or (ii) an FDA modification that involved dose exclusion (removal of a proposed dose) rather than addition (introduction of a new dose). This path showed perfect consistency (1.0) and coverage (1.0), accounting for all 11 cases in this data set. For the EMA, a sufficient condition was the combination of (i) an FDA change that involved addition rather than exclusion, and (ii) a direction of change that was dose reduction. While the consistency was 1.0, the coverage was 0.75, suggesting that other unexplored patterns may also explain the EMA alignment. All derived paths shared key elements (PMDA: “long gap & no PK difference” + “not an addition”; EMA: “dose reduction” + “addition”), confirming shared logical structures (Table

## DISCUSSION
Differences in regulatory decisions across regions are influenced by complex factors, including region‐specific considerations and prior regulatory decisions. This study builds on these themes by analyzing the factors associated with regional differences in approved dosages. Among new drugs approved in Japan and the United States between 2017 and 2023, the dosage concordance rate between PMDA and FDA was 77%, and that between EMA and FDA was 84%. These rates are higher than those previously reported, , in which an approximate concordance was ~60% between Japan and the United States based on maximum‐dose comparison. This shift likely reflects the evolving global development environment. Nonetheless, notable regional differences were also observed.
Qualitative (case‐based) analysis of the 30 discordant cases identified major drivers of these regional differences. Although all discordance was treated as clinically meaningful by design, its clinical implications differed in mechanism and scope across cases, including divergent approaches to safety risk evaluation and dosage decisions (model‐based vs. clinically studied dosages), population‐related considerations (e.g., ethnic differences in PK, exposure‐matching among body weight‐based subpopulations), and varying expectations for efficacy‐supporting evidence, and clinical context. Dose differences driven by explicitly identified ethnic PK differences accounted for a small proportion of cases (four of 111 products; 3.6%). By contrast, broader body size‐related considerations and other drivers were more frequently observed, and may have practical clinical implications through effects on exposure and/or efficacy and safety. Complementary quantitative analysis (logistic regression) indicated that the composition of trials included in the application package, submission timing, and therapeutic area were associated with regional differences. Taken together, these results suggest that such differences are more likely when there are multiple viable dosing options or differing interpretations of the benefit–risk balance, especially in non‐oncology indications. Because the analyses are observational, these associations should be interpreted descriptively rather than causally. Nonetheless, the observed patterns provide a basis for hypothesis generation regarding how regulatory priorities and development strategies may be associated with regional dose discordance.
Case analyses suggest interdependencies through which early decisions may shape subsequent outcomes. For example, in the case of dupilumab, EMA and PMDA adopted the FDA‐approved dose after the sponsor accepted FDA's recommendation to remove a dose option, resulting in harmonized dosing. By contrast, divergence arose when FDA recommended a new dose that was not adopted by EMA or PMDA, as seen with selpercatinib (not adopted by PMDA), lenacapavir, and rozanolixizumab (not adopted by EMA and PMDA). In other cases, EMA's actions altered the outcomes; for niraparib, FDA's initial 300 mg approval was later revised after EMA raised safety concerns. This led to protocol changes, a lower‐dose submission to PMDA, and eventual label updates by both FDA and EMA—an example of iterative harmonization. For osilodrostat, FDA retained the original label while EMA recommended a lower starting dose for Asian populations, which Japan adopted, resulting in partial but persistent divergence (Table

These examples demonstrate that early decisions can serve as reference points, with convergence emerging through negotiation and evidence accumulation, whereas divergence remains when regional considerations predominate. At the same time, concordant outcomes in these cases cannot be attributed to a single mechanism and may reflect a combination of sponsor‐driven harmonization, shared data packages, convergent scientific standards, and regulatory alignment rather than direct regulatory reliance. These review‐sequence dynamics were further explored using QCA and regression analyses to assess whether review sequence was systematically associated with discordance.
Differences in the review approaches of the FDA, EMA, and PMDA appear to have contributed to the observed divergence in approved doses. The FDA reviews often include reanalyses that explore alternative doses and actively applies model‐informed drug development (MIDD), reflecting a proactive stance seeking new evidence from existing data. , In our data set, sponsor‐submitted doses were modified during FDA review in 14 cases (12%), including eight based on FDA reanalyses with new dosing recommendations. Logistic regression showed that discordance between EMA and FDA was less likely when EMA approval followed FDA approval. By contrast, FDA–PMDA discordance was not significantly associated with the order of approval, indicating that PMDA decisions may be less influenced by FDA actions than EMA decisions. QCA and case analyses indicated that the EMA outcomes were more often aligned with FDA outcomes when a lower dose was added, but not when existing options were removed. Notably, most drugs with divergent decisions listed the greatest number of tiers in the EMA's approved doses (Table
 ), suggesting a tendency to retain or approve multiple dosing options. PMDA's approved dose was more likely to align with the FDA's when the approval gap was large, no ethnic PK differences were noted, or FDA's revisions involved excluding rather than adding a new dose. The sponsor‐submitted doses were modified during PMDA review in 12 cases (11%), primarily aligning with the regimens that had been administered in clinical trials. These relative differences suggest that, taken together with prior work, the FDA favors model‐based dose selection that emphasizes clarity and simplicity and often seeks streamlined regimens. By contrast, EMA tends to accommodate complexity and design more inclusive dosing regimens that preserve multiple options when clinically justified. The PMDA places importance on hypothesis‐testing frameworks and relies heavily on prior clinical dosing experience at the proposed dose, making it more cautious in accepting doses supported only by modeling and simulation. While these are relative rather than absolute tendencies and should not be overgeneralized beyond this sample, and acknowledging that multiple overlapping mechanisms are involved, they offer insights into differing regulatory approaches observed in recent drug approval.
Structural and systemic differences in regulatory practices between the FDA and PMDA help explain the divergent dose decisions. At the FDA, discipline‐specific reviewers independently analyze raw data, and their findings are reflected in detailed review documents, fostering flexible decision making and an analytic style that emphasizes generating new options. By contrast, PMDA relies on consensus‐based evaluations; review reports do not typically incorporate agency‐conducted reanalyses or reflect individual reviewers' views, nor does the agency actively propose new alternative doses. This reflects a holistic orientation that emphasizes stability and reliance on clinically tested evidence. Another feature of the US system is that the dosing labels are updated relatively frequently after approval, including via PMR/PMC for dose optimization; among oncology NMEs with such requirements, approximately 43% led to meaningful label changes. This reflects a willingness to revise the dosage post‐approval in response to new evidence, whereas Japan generally avoids changes once approval is granted and instead aims to ensure robustness at approval. However, this approach is flexible. With fremanezumab—approved in Japan more than a year after the United States and Europe—a simulation‐based dosing regimen was accepted, supported by model‐predicted reductions in migraine days and additional evidence from other patient populations, indicating a readiness to accept changes when justified.
The influence of ethnic PK and body weight differences on regulatory outcomes was evident, but nuanced. Case‐based analysis identified ethnic PK differences as a factor in regional dosage differences for four of the 111 products (erenumab, osilodrostat, opicapone, and vibegron). By contrast, regression analysis suggested that regional dosage differences were more consistently associated with contextual factors, such as data package composition, submission timing, and therapeutic area, rather than with interethnic PK differences alone. The PMDA review reports suggest that for most products, ethnic differences in drug exposure are not clinically meaningful. This aligns with an earlier observation that ethnic differences in PK often fall within the range of inter‐individual variability and do not necessarily lead to different dosage recommendations. Caution is warranted when interpreting these findings, however, as clinical development strategies may themselves have been shaped by earlier expectations or findings regarding ethnic or demographic variability. In cases where ethnic PK differences led to observable safety concerns, authorities introduced risk communication or postmarketing surveillance, even without changing doses. For example, the PMDA identified higher exposure to berotralstat in Japanese populations than in non‐Japanese populations, raising QT prolongation concerns, and mandated targeted risk communication for patients with predisposing factors. For tirzepatide, a greater increase in heart rate was observed among the Japanese participants. Through exposure–response analyses stratified by ethnicity, the FDA concluded that the effect stemmed from greater sensitivity rather than higher exposure. The PMDA also acknowledged this difference and added corresponding safety warnings to the labeling. Regarding body weight differences, some drugs initially developed in Asia (e.g., baloxavir marboxil) have incorporated higher weight dosing categories (≥ 80 kg) when expanded to Western countries. There are also examples in which Western regulators advise weight‐based dosing, leading to differentiated regimens for individuals with higher body weight. By contrast, this study found no instances in which drugs with Western‐led development later introduced to Asian countries incorporated low‐weight categories tailored for Asian populations. Given that most global developments originate in the West, these findings suggest a potentially missed opportunity to tailor dosing based on body weight differences between Western and Asian populations.
This study has implications for future drug development and regulatory review. Regional discordance was more likely when multiple viable dosing options existed, suggesting that earlier scientific advice or shared evidentiary expectations may help clarify which evidence is relevant for decision making, potentially reducing late‐stage divergence. Although anticancer drugs showed fewer regional dosage differences than other therapeutic areas between 2017 and 2023, expectations for dose optimization continue to evolve, and divergent cases were still observed, highlighting the need for early scientific advice across therapeutic areas. In a subset of cases, later agencies aligned with FDA‐led dose modifications while retaining agency‐specific considerations, underscoring the benefits of process transparency and anticipation for sponsors. As simultaneous global submissions and initiatives such as Project Orbis expand, and policy changes, including FDA cost‐reduction measures, may reshape review practices, efficient use of regulatory and development resources remains essential. This study further highlights potential differences in benefit–risk considerations among regulatory authorities, which can drive divergent outcomes even within collaborative reviews. While differences rooted in national interests or regulatory frameworks may warrant context‐specific evaluation, the scientific foundations underpinning these decisions are shared across jurisdictions, as emphasized in International Council for Harmonization of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH) guidelines E4, E5, E8, and E17, with draft guidance such as ICH M15 signaling ongoing harmonization. Strengthening early dialogue around shared scientific principles, particularly dose–exposure–response relationships for efficacy and safety, may also contribute to clearer and aligned expectations regarding the use of model‐informed approaches, supporting more efficient global development and regulatory review while better serving patient health worldwide.
Our findings should be interpreted within the limitations of the study. To leverage the detailed FDA and PMDA review reports, we focused on drugs approved in both the United States and Japan, including those approved in the EU. This design may introduce sampling bias in the EMA–FDA comparison, as submissions rich in Japanese data could affect the EMA review outcomes and dose concordance. The proportion of products initially developed in Europe may also have been underestimated, potentially biasing the assessment of inter‐agency interdependencies. Second, the extent to which each authority was aware of evolving positions of others during development and submission could not be determined from public sources, highlighting the difficulty of directly observing path dependence and real‐time inter‐agency influence.
In conclusion, this study examined regional differences in approved drug dosages in the United States and Japan from 2017 to 2023, incorporating EMA data where available. Concordance between PMDA and FDA and EMA and FDA were higher than previously reported, although meaningful regional differences remained. Divergent regulatory approaches to safety evaluation, decision making, and evidence standards were identified as drivers of discordance, while study design and submission timing were descriptively associated with its occurrence. Structural patterns were observed consistent with potential interdependence, whereby earlier regulatory decisions served as reference points for subsequent reviews. These findings suggest that early regulatory engagement and international coordination can help promote scientific soundness and efficiency in dose selection, although attention must be paid to the underlying differences in benefit–risk assessment considerations.
