CaiJingshuangMDData curationFormal analysisInvestigationMethodologyResourcesSoftwareValidationVisualizationWriting - original draftWriting - review & editingThe Central Laboratory, Quanzhou First Hospital Affiliated to Fujian Medical University, 1028 Anji South Road, Quanzhou, Fujian, 362000, China , Despite the regulatory approval of several biomarkers, such as PD-L1 level, tumor mutation burden (TMB), along with microsatellite instability-high/deficient DNA mismatch repair, for the prediction of response to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), identifying patients who will derive meaningful benefit from these therapies remains a challenge. 
The receptor tyrosine kinase rearranged during transfection (RET) is essential for the development of diverse tissues. Oncogenic activation of RET occurs primarily through 2 mechanisms: germline/somatic mutations and chromosomal rearrangements. These gain-of-function modifications drive cancer cell proliferation, growth, and survival via multiple signaling pathways and are linked to an increased risk of distant metastasis. , Emerging evidence indicates that dysregulation of RET signaling significantly influences tumor immune responses. Specifically, RET contributes to immune evasion by inducing a pro-inflammatory transcriptional program that may modulate the tumor immune phenotype. Additionally, activated RET has been shown to govern Wnt signaling, which is implicated in the recruitment of antigen-presenting cells. These characteristics of the RET mutations suggest their potential utility as a cancer immunotherapy biomarkers.
Because of the rarity of RET-mutant tumors, individual studies have typically included only a limited number of patients. A pooled analysis of existing data may therefore yield valuable insights with practical implications for clinical decision-making. In light of the increasing availability of public datasets, we conducted a systematic investigation into the attributes of RET mutations and their interaction with clinical outcomes in ICI-treated patients. Furthermore, we carried out comprehensive genomic and immunologic analyses to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms in RET-mutant tumors.

## Methods

## Data collection
Information on RET mutations and clinicopathological attributes in ICI-treated patients was collected from 9 published cohorts. Among them, melanoma was investigated in 7 studies, , lung cancer in 4 studies, , , , and multiple tumors in 2 studies. , Genomic profiling was predominantly performed using whole-exome sequencing (WES) in 8 studies, while one study utilized the MSK-IMPACT targeted sequencing panel for multiple tumor types. Immune checkpoint inhibitor therapies evaluated across the cohorts included anti-CTLA-4 agents (n = 5 studies) , , , , and anti-PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors (n = 7 studies). , , 
The genomic and transcriptomic data, along with clinical attributes from 10 953 patients across 33 cancers in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) cohort (https://gdc.cancer.gov/about-data/publications/pancanatlas) were analyzed. In this study, the nonsynonymous mutations were defined as missense, frameshift, nonsense, nonstop, splice site, and translation start site changes. Frameshift deletions, frameshift insertions, and splice site mutations are categorized as truncating mutations. In-frame mutations include in-frame deletions and in-frame insertions. The analyzed key immunogenomic characteristics included silent and non-silent mutation rates, single-nucleotide variant (SNV)- and indel-derived neoantigens, lymphocyte and leukocyte fractions, CD8+ T cell abundance, tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) regional fraction, T cell receptor (TCR) and B cell receptor (BCR) richness, and Shannon diversity indices for both TCR and BCR. These parameters were calculated as previously described. 
The expression levels of related genes were scaled separately based on the mutation status, and the median values were derived to calculate the expression differences. Statistical analysis for comparisons between 2 groups was conducted using the Wilcoxon test. Clinical response was assessed based on Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors v1.1. Patients achieving complete response (CR) or partial response (PR) were classified as responders, while those with stable disease (SD) or progressive disease (PD) were categorized as non-responders. Overall survival (OS) data were extracted directly from the original publications.

## Immune signature evaluation
The enrichment levels of 29 established immune signatures were quantified using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA), implemented via the “GSVA” package in R. 

## Microenvironment cell population analysis
The abundance of tumor-infiltrating immune and stromal cell populations was estimated through the Microenviroment Cell Populations-counter (MCP-counter) method. This approach quantifies the presence of 9 immune cell types as well as 2 stromal cell populations. 

## Deciphering mutational signatures
The “deconstructSigs” package was deployed to carry out non-negative matrix factorization analysis of carcinoma evolutionary mutations and patterns. Based on cosine similarity, we conducted a comparison between the retrieved mutation patterns and the Catalogue of Somatic Mutations in Cancer (COSMIC).

## Statistics
Survival outcomes were analyzed via the Kaplan-Meier method, and disparities between groups were ascertained with the log-rank test. Hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed using the Cox proportional hazards model. Associations between categorical variables were evaluated using the Kruskal-Wallis test, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, or Chi-square test, as appropriate. All data processing and analyses were performed with R software (v4.2.1) and MedCalc (v18.2.1). Two-sided P value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## 
RET mutation and immunotherapy outcomes
To evaluate the impact of RET mutation on ICB efficacy, we analyzed a discovery cohort comprising 1406 patients with 6 tumor types across 8 datasets ( ). The cohort included patients diagnosed with lung cancer (n = 791), melanoma (n = 574), bladder urothelial cancer (n = 27), head and neck cancer (n = 12), anal cancer (n = 1), and sarcoma (n = 1). The WES data were used to determine mutational status. Among these patients, 76 (5.41%) harbored RET mutations, which were significantly related to improved OS (HR = 0.71; 95% CI, 0.54-0.94; P = .03; ).
 In the validation cohort, we analyzed 1524 patients dignosed with 9 tumor types: Lung cancer (n = 344), melanoma (n = 313), bladder urothelial cancer (n = 211), renal cell carcinoma (n = 143), head and neck cancer (n = 129), esophagogastric cancer (n = 118), glioma (n = 116), colorectal cancer (n = 109), and breast cancer (n = 41). Rearranged during transfection mutation status was ascertained via the MSK-IMPACT panel, an FDA-approved comprehensive genomic profiling assay. Among these patients, 50 (3.28%) carried RET mutations and exhibited significantly prolonged OS (HR = 0.42; 95% CI, 0.29-0.60; P = .001; ).
Collectively, in 2930 ICI-treated patients with 11 tumor types, RET mutations were associated with a 40% reduction in mortality risk (HR = 0.60; 95% CI, 0.48-0.75; P < .001; ). Furthermore, patients harboring RET-mutant tumors demonstrated a significantly increased objective response rate, unlike those with RET-non-mutant tumors (44.9% vs. 25.7%; P < .001; ). For the evaluation of the performance of diverse features as biomarkers in ICB, we applied both univariate ( ) and multivariate Cox analyses ( ). Following the adjustment for potential confounding variables, including age, sex, cancer type, drug type, TMB, and mutations in TP53, POLE, and BRCA, the RET mutation status remained an independent prognostic biomarker.

## The molecular characteristics of RET-mutant tumors
Analyzing the TCGA cohort revealed that among 10 953 patients across 33 cancer types, RET mutations were identified in 274 individuals (2.50%), with mutation frequencies varying significantly across tumor types ( ). A total of 311 distinct RET mutations were detected: 232 (74.60%) missense mutations, 42 (11.50%) fusion mutations, 25 (8.04%) truncating mutations, 10 (3.22%) splice mutations, and 2 (0.64%) in-frame mutations ( ). No significant associations were observed between RET mutation and disease-free survival (HR = 0.78; 95% CI, 0.54-1.14; P = .25), disease-specific survival (HR = 0.87; 95% CI, 0.67-1.13; P = .32), or OS (HR = 0.87; 95% CI, 0.70-1.08; P = .23; ). Altogether, the RET mutation was not broadly prognostic for survival in cancer but may specifically predict response to immunotherapy rather than serving as a general prognostic biomarker.
 

## 
RET mutation and intrinsic immune responses
The intrinsic immune characteristics primarily encompassed elevated tumor immunogenicity, activation of antigen-processing machinery, and overexpression of co-stimulatory molecule. The mutation loads, including non-silent mutation rate, silent mutation rate, and TMB, were significantly increased in RET-mutant tumors ( ). The key immune checkpoints, CTLA-4 and PD-1/PD-L1, displayed significant overexpression in RET-mutant tumors ( ). Dysregulation of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) level is a well-established mechanism of tumor immune escape. The RET mutation was connected with enhanced levels of several MHC-correlated antigen-presenting molecules and co-stimulatory factors ( ).
 

## Mutational signatures associated with RET mutation
It was well established that a complex and diverse array of mutational signatures could be discovered in human normal tissues and cancers when evaluating the whole-genome and whole-exome sequencing data. Somatic mutation genomic landscapes are documented in the COSMIC database, which is a public resource offering annotation, data mining, analysis, and visualization tools. Here, we explored whether there were any distinct mutational signatures associated with the clinical outcomes in RET mutant patients who were treated with ICIs. As shown in and , the incidences of single base substitution (SBS) 7d (known etiology, ultraviolet light exposure), SBS10b (POLE mutation), SBS31 (platinum chemotherapy), SBS32 (azathioprine therapy), SBS42 (haloalkane exposure), and SBS86 (unknown chemotherapy) changed significantly in RET-mutant tumors. Among them ( and ), SBS10b was linked with improved OS (HR = 0.84; 95% CI, 0.71-0.98; P = .03), and its frequency in RET-altered tumors was significantly increased compared with that in RET-unaltered tumors (36.5% vs. 26.1%; P < .001). Conversely, SBS7d (HR = 1.53; 95% CI, 0.92-2.55; P = .04), SBS31 (HR = 1.31; 95% CI, 0.97-1.77; P = .04), SBS32 (HR = 1.73; 95% CI, 1.00-2.99; P = .01), SBS42 (HR = 1.47; 95% CI, 0.95-2.25; P = .03), and SBS86 (HR = 1.33; 95% CI, 0.98-1.81; P = .04) represented negative predictors. In addition, their frequencies in RET-altered tumors were downregulated significantly.
 

## 
RET mutation and extrinsic immune landscape
Key extrinsic immune characteristics include immune cell infiltration, elevated BCR/TCR diversity, tumor cell immunogenicity, and overexpression of immune-stimulatory molecules and chemokines.  Rearranged during transfection mutation was associated with higher immune cell infiltration based on (1) DNA methylation array-evaluated leukocyte fractions; (2) CIBERSORT-estimated lymphocyte fraction; and (3) RNA-sequencing-determined TIL fraction ( ). Tumor mutations have the ability to give rise to neoantigens that are specifically recognized by T/B cells with antigen-specific TCRs/BCRs. As shown in , neoantigen abundances (including SNV and Indel neoantigens) and TCR/BCR diversity (estimated by TCR/BCR Shannon index and richness) were significantly increased in RET-mutant tumors. The expression levels of 11 major cell types estimated by MCP-counter ( ) and 29 immune signatures quantified by ssGSEA ( ) clearly manifested enriched immune cell populations as well as enhenced immune activities in RET-mutant cancers. Furthermore, the RET mutation was related to overexpression of most chemokines and their receptors ( ) and immune stimulators ( ).

## Discussion
Although the oncogenic drivers can influence both the local and systemic immune landscapes, their implications in immunotherapy remain poorly understood. , Here, we systematically evaluate the interaction between RET mutation and immunotherapy efficacy in roughly 3000 individuals with various tumor types. Our results demonstrate that RET mutation status serves as an independent biomarker for improved clinical outcomes in pan-cancer ICB therapy. Additionally, multi-omic analysis on intrinsic and extrinsic immune responses manifested that the RET mutation was associated with improved tumor immunogenicity, increased immune cell infiltration, and improved immune responses. Our study was retrospective and had other limitations. However, considering that the RET mutation could decrease the risk of death by 40%, these findings have clinical relevance.
While the precise mechanisms linking RET mutation to immunotherapy response remain incompletely understood, emerging evidence suggests that activation of RET signaling has a profound impact on tumor immunity. Prior studies have shown that RET proteins can drive a pro-inflammatory transcriptional program that promotes immune escape. , This may occur by activating STAT1/3 and NF-κB, key transcription factors that are central regulators of immune responses. , Activated RET can also regulate Wnt signaling, which is involved in recruiting antigen-presenting cells. Additionally, RET can induce a series of pro-inflammatory molecules, including cytokines (CSF-1, IL-1, GM-CSF, SPP1/OPN, and G-CSF), chemokines (CXCL8, SDF1, CCL2, and CCL20), and matrix-degrading enzymes and adhesion molecules. , Many of these molecules are chemotactic for immune cells and may contribute to shaping the tumor immune microenvironment. Here, by comparing all the key features involved in intrinsic and extrinsic immune responses, our data demonstrate that RET-mutant tumors show a unique immune landscape that differs greatly from RET-unaltered tumors. Furthermore, analysis of mutation signatures against the COSMIC database identified 6 potential tumor-associated antigens, which may partially account for the improved clinical outcomes observed in patients harboring RET-altered tumors. Despite these insights, the full functionality of RET in modulating anti-tumor immunity and its implications for immunotherapy response remain to be fully elucidated and warrant further mechanistic investigation.
Because of the low prevalence of eligible patients, the anti-tumor activity of ICI-based combination treatments in RET-mutant tumors has been incompletely investigated. Previous evidence has shown that radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or even immunotherapy can trigger immunogenicity in cancer patients. , Therefore, combining ICIs with these modalities may potentially expand the number of patients who derive clinical benefit. In line with this hypothesis, 2 ongoing phase 3 randomized clinical trials are currently evaluating the efficacy of ICI combination therapy in the first-line treatment of RET-mutant lung cancer. The LIBRETTO-431 (NCT04194944) is comparing selpercatinib with platinum-based chemotherapy (carboplatin or cisplatin plus pemetrexed), administered with or without pembrolizumab. Similarly, ACCELE-RET (NCT04222972) is assessing pralsetinib versus platinum-based regimens—pemetrexed plus platinum for non-squamous histology or gemcitabine plus platinum for squamous histology—with or without pembrolizumab. These 2 trials share similar designs and will guide future clinical practice.
There are several limitations in this retrospective study. First, the eligible cohorts were conducted at various medical centers, and the researchers exhibited subjectivity in recording clinical outcomes, especially objective response rate (ORR). Our results are subject to any biases or errors derived from the original investigators. Second, although our data imply that patients with RET could benefit more from immunotherapy compared with patients without RET, confirmation through prospective, head-to-head comparative trials is necessary to validate this association. Third, the association between RET mutation and immunotherapy outcomes was affected by various confounding factors. However, due to the rarity of RET mutation (only 126 individuals among approximately 3000 enrolled patients), we could not explore all the potential factors in the subgroup analysis based on clinicopathological and molecular characteristics, such as different mutation subtypes of RET. Even in subgroup analysis conducted, like the association between ICB efficacy and RET mutation in different tumor types, the statistical power was limited because of the small number of patients included. Further in-depth investigations are needed to confirm these results. Fourth, the toxicity profile is a critical factor in treatment decision-making; however, comprehensive safety analyses could not be conducted due to the lack of detailed adverse event reporting in the included cohorts.
To conclude, our comprehensive analysis of intrinsic and extrinsic immune features demonstrates that the RET mutation is related to improved tumor immunogenicity, increased immune cell infiltration, and improved immune responses. These findings support the classification of RET-mutant tumors as immunologically “hot,” correlating with favorable clinical outcomes in ICI-treated patients. This study provides a foundation for refining clinical strategies, guiding treatment decisions, and ultimately enhancing outcomes for patients with RET-altered tumors.
