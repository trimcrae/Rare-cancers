## Abstract
Kinase fusion genes were the most targeted fusion gene group among multiple major cellular gene groups. Kinase inhibitors disrupt aberrant signaling cascades and inhibit tumor progression, yet the specific mechanisms of action of the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved inhibitors in the context of kinase fusion oncoproteins remain largely unknown. This gap limits our ability to develop personalized therapies and next-generation kinase inhibitors. To address this, we developed a novel in silico pipeline for predicting 3D structures of kinase fusion proteins and performing structure-based virtual screening. This approach enables large-scale structural annotation and drug screening across pan-cancer kinase fusions. We present KinaseFusionDB, available at https://compbio.uth.edu/KinaseFusionDB, a comprehensive knowledgebase providing functional annotation of 7680 kinase fusion genes, 1399 predicted fusion protein structures, predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT)-based confidence scoring, and virtual screening data using FDA-approved kinase inhibitors. Our analysis revealed that most predicted structures showed high pLDDT scores (pLDDT >70) within conserved kinase domains. Structural alignment with known Protein Data Banks demonstrated shared structural motifs despite variation in fusion breakpoints. Virtual screening results highlighted repurposing opportunities and isoform-specific binding preferences. KinaseFusionDB is a valuable resource for investigating kinase fusion structure–function relationships and guiding the design of personalized and next-generation kinase inhibitor therapies.
Keywords: kinase, fusion gene, fusion protein, 3D structure, virtual screening, next-generation kinase inhibitor

## Introduction
In cancer, three big mechanisms result in the oncogenic activation of kinases ( ). First, through the overexpression (up-regulation) or amplified copy numbers of the wild-type kinases. Second, through the gain of functional mutations of kinases. Last, through the chromosomal rearrangement of the kinase gene (kinase fusion gene). Gene fusion is a unique hallmark of the cancer genome [ ]. Kinase fusion genes are the most targeted fusion gene group among all known fusion genes in cancer [ ]. Kinase domain–retained fusion proteins can activate kinase functions. For example, BCR::ABL1 fusion gene between BCR (BCR activator of RhoGEF and GTPase) and ABL1 (ABL proto-oncogene 1, nonreceptor tyrosine kinase), expressed in ~95% of chronic myeloid leukemia (CML, 20% of adult leukemia) and ~20% of acute myelogenous leukemia (AML, 80% of adult leukemia), causes uncontrolled cell proliferation due to constitutive expression of the kinase domain of ABL1. The EML4::ALK fusion protein between EML4 (EMAP like 4) and ALK (ALK receptor tyrosine kinase) can form an ALK dimer without activating an exogenous ligand, activating ALK and its downstream RAS (RAS proto-oncogene, GTPase family)/ERK (mitogen-activated protein kinase 1)/STAT3 (signal transducer and activator of transcription 3)/mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin kinase) and other signaling pathways [ ]. The promotion of cell proliferation and invasion, as well as the inhibition of apoptosis, leads to the occurrence of nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [ ]. Kinase fusion genes, including ROS1 (ROS proto-oncogene 1, receptor tyrosine kinase) and RET (ret proto-oncogene), were also observed in the NSCLC. Over 60% of juvenile pilocytic astrocytomas (JPAs), the most common type of pediatric low-grade glioma (pLGG), contain a tandem duplication leading to a fusion between KIAA1549 and BRAF (B-Raf proto-oncogene, serine/threonine kinase) that results in the loss of the auto-inhibitory domain of BRAF and the constitutive activation of its kinase domain [ ]. NTRK (neurotrophic receptor tyrosine kinase) gene fusion is highly seen in rare tumors such as mammary secretory carcinoma, mammary analog secretory carcinoma of the salivary gland, congenital infantile fibrosarcoma (fusion prevalence is almost 100%), and other rarer tumors. NTRK fusion is present in ~40% of high-grade nonbrainstem pediatric gliomas [ ]. There were many designed/approved kinase inhibitors designed to inhibit these kinases. Kinase inhibitors block the uncontrolled signal transduction in diverse mechanisms relating to the amplified kinase function context in cancer [ , ]. However, there are many ongoing efforts to develop next-generation kinase inhibitors per activated kinase mechanisms for precision medicine, and we often see announcements of the approval of existing kinase inhibitors for another cancer type. There are multiple databases providing information on the biology and interactions of wild-type kinases. For example, KLIFS [ ] is a resource of kinase–ligand interactions through structural analysis of kinase catalytic domains of wild-type kinases. KinaseMD [ ] is a database focused on annotating kinase domain hotspot point mutations. Kinomer [ ] is a resource for classifying and comparing eukaryotic protein kinases across various species. Kinomer emphasizes the evolutionary relationships and classification of kinases. RTKdb [ ] focuses on receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) across multiple metazoan species, providing a valuable resource for comparative and evolutionary studies of these transmembrane receptors. RTKdb offers insights into the evolution and sequence diversity of RTKs. PKIDB [ ] focuses on cataloging approved protein kinase inhibitors (PKIs). Even though the kinase fusion genes are the most targeted fusion gene group among all known fusion genes in cancer, none of these resources provides detailed annotations and interpretations based on the kinase fusion genes/proteins context in multi-scale, which is an urgent need for kinase fusion breakpoint-based drug development in cancer.
 To develop effective personalized therapeutics and next-generation kinase inhibitors, we must fully understand the detailed mechanisms of action and contexts of individual kinase inhibitors in kinase fusion oncoproteins [ ]. However, this has not been done yet due to the lack of fusion protein sequences and fusion protein 3D structures [ ]. For this, we recently released the functional annotation of human fusion genes and predicted fusion protein sequences through FusionGDB versions 1 and 2 [ , ]. We also released the predicted fusion protein 3D structures through FusionPDB [ ], which includes ~3.5 K predicted fusion protein 3D structures of the manually curated fusion genes using AlphaFold2 [ ]. However, FusionPDB’s first version did not include fusion protein isoform structures; it only focused on the longest isoforms of individual fusion proteins and did not cover all kinase fusion proteins. Most of all, there was no kinase fusion gene/protein-focused knowledgebase. With our expertise in functional annotation of individual kinase fusion genes/proteins and prediction of fusion protein 3D structures [ ], in this study, we share our newly developed resource of human kinase fusion genes/proteins, KinaseFusionDB. We integrated up-to-date RNA exon junction breakpoint loci information of 7680 kinase fusion genes from FusionGDB2 [ ], Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) [ ], and catalogue of somatic mutations in cancer (COSMIC) [ ] ( ). Open reading frame (ORF) annotation of the breakpoints and gene isoforms found ~1400 kinase fusion proteins. Kinase domain retention search, fusion protein 3D structure prediction, diverse assessment evidence of the predicted 3D structures of kinase fusion proteins, comparison between our fusion protein structures and known Protein Data Benk (PDB) structures, comparison between individual kinase fusion protein isoform structure pairs, predicted best active site information, known active sites of 3D structure present in the PDB database, PubMed Abstract and full-text search, and clinicaltrials.gov search between all human kinase fusion oncoproteins and 72 FDA-approved kinase inhibitors, virtual screening against 72 FDA-approved kinase inhibitors, a gene–gene network composed by potentially affected targeted phosphorylated genes, and related diseases and drug information would provide novel knowledge and potential context for prioritizing the kinase inhibitors by utilizing the whole 3D structures of kinase fusion proteins, not relying on the partial structure-based drug screening approach.
KinaseFusionDB is the only resource one can follow for annotating the central dogma of the human kinase fusion genes on a multi-scale basis. KinaseFusionDB will help enhance our knowledge of kinase fusion genes/proteins and provide the foundation of new therapeutic strategies for personalized medicine. The impact of this work will be broader than we think in the bio/health/drug scientific communities. Diverse cancer and drug research communities will routinely use it.

## Materials and methods

## Fusion gene and fusion protein information
We have obtained a comprehensive dataset of 7680 kinase fusion genes sourced from FusionGDB2 [ ], CCLE [ ], and COSMIC [ ]. For these fusion genes, we have the information of sample ID or expressed sequence tag (EST) ID, 5′-gene, chromosome, breakpoint, strand and 3′-gene, chromosome, breakpoint, strand information, and full-length fusion transcript sequences. These fusion genes represent combinations of genes A and B, resulting from chromosomal rearrangements that create fusion transcripts. Among these 7680 fusion genes, a detailed analysis was conducted to identify those that maintain in-frame coding sequences, leading to the selection of 1840 in-frame fusion genes. These in-frame fusion genes could finally make fusion amino acid sequences based on the GRCh37 human reference genome from the result of ORFfinder [ ]. The methods of using the fusion protein sequences from multiple genomic breakpoints and multiple gene isoforms are described in the FusionGDB2.0 study as follows. Two different genes can form fusion genes with multiple breakpoints based on multiple gene isoforms. Therefore, we considered all gene isoforms at each breakpoint. To help identify and validate fusion genes, we focused on the in-frame fusion genes. For more reliable fusion genes, we checked the distance between the two breakpoints in case of intra-chromosomal rearrangements and created fusion sequences when those genes were >100 kb apart. We also selected fusion genes when both breakpoints aligned at the exon junction. To call each exon sequence of the given breakpoints, transcription start/end sites, and coding DNA sequence (CDS) start/end sites, we used the nibFrag utility from UCSC Genome Browser based on ENCODE hg19 genome structure. The next critical step involved assessing whether the kinase domain was retained in the resulting fusion proteins. This domain retention is essential for the functional activity of kinase proteins. After this rigorous selection process, 1399 unique kinase fusion amino acid sequences were identified. Among these, 973 sequences were classified as 3′-kinase fusion proteins, where the kinase domain is located at the C-terminal end, and 449 sequences were classified as 5′-kinase fusion proteins, where the kinase domain is at the N-terminal end. This careful filtering ensures that the final dataset includes only those fusion proteins most likely to retain kinase activity, making them viable targets for further study in cancer research.

## Functional annotation of fusion proteins
To assign the functional or gene categories, we integrated cancer genes, tumor suppressors, epigenetic regulators, DNA damage repair genes, human essential genes, kinases, and transcription factors. In each gene group, we checked the retention and ORFs of the main protein functional features. There are 13 features belonging to the ‘region’ category, including ‘calcium binding’, ‘coiled coil’, ‘compositional bias’, ‘DNA binding’, ‘domain’, ‘intramembrane’, ‘motif’, ‘nucleotide binding’, ‘region’, ‘repeat’, ‘topological domain’, ‘transmembrane’, and ‘zinc finger’. To perform the protein functional feature retention search, we first downloaded the GFF (General Feature Format) format protein information of 10 651 UniProt [ ] accessions from UniProt for 10 619 genes involved in 15 030 fusion genes [ ]. UniProt provides the loci information of 39 protein features, including 6 molecule processing features, 13 region features, 4 site features, 6 amino acid modification features, 2 natural variation features, 5 experimental info features, and 3 secondary structure features. Since such feature loci information was based on amino acid sequence, the genomic breakpoint information was converted into the amino acid level while considering all UniProt protein accessions, ENST isoforms, and multiple breakpoints for each partner. We used the GENCODE gene model of human reference genome v19 50 to map each feature to the human genome sequence. For the 5′-partner gene, we considered the protein feature to be retained in the fusion gene if the breakpoints occurred on the 3′-end of the protein feature. On the contrary, if a protein domain was not entirely included in the fusion amino acid sequence, we reported that such fusion genes did not retain that protein feature. Similarly, for the 3′-partner gene, we considered the fusion gene to have retained the protein feature if the breakpoints occurred on the 5′-end of the protein feature region.

## 3D structure prediction of fusion proteins and Ramachandran plots
We used AlphaFold2 [ ] to predict the complete 3D structures for 1422 kinase fusion proteins. Of the 1399 unique kinase fusion proteins, 449 were classified as 5′-kinase fusion proteins and 973 as 3′-kinase fusion proteins, with 22 proteins common to both categories. We utilized the graphics processing unit (GPU)-based Linux version of AlphaFold2, adhering to the step-by-step instructions provided in AlphaFold2’s GitHub repository. FASTA files containing the amino acid sequences for all 1399 fusion proteins were submitted to AlphaFold2 for structure prediction. For sequence alignment, we downloaded several large databases, totaling 2.2 TB, including UniRef90, UniProt [ ], UniClust30, small_bfd, PDB_seqres, PDB_mmcif, PDB70, params, MGnify, and BFD. The model parameters provided by AlphaFold2 were employed for the predictions. The output directory contained the predicted PDB structures, ranked based on model confidence from ranked_0.pdb to ranked_4.pdb.
Ramachandran plots are generated for each kinase fusion protein to evaluate the conformational angles of the protein backbone. This analysis helps assess the quality of the predicted 3D structures by identifying regions of the protein that adopt favored, allowed, or disallowed conformations, which is essential for validating the structural predictions. We used a Python script with the help of the RamachanDraw library.

## Preparation of protein and active site identification
The predicted PDB structures of proteins may have missing atoms, incomplete connectivity information, and water molecules that require removal or preprocessing before using the protein’s 3D structure for virtual screening. All 1399 predicted protein structures were preprocessed to address these issues using the Protein Preparation Wizard in Schrödinger [ ] (Release 2022-2, LLC, New York, NY, 2021). This preprocessing involved removing water molecules, assigning bond orders, and adding hydrogen atoms to the crystal structure of the receptor molecule. A restrained minimization was performed using an RMSD constraint and the OPLS force field. To identify the active sites of all 1399 predicted proteins, we employed Schrödinger’s SiteMap [ ] (release 2021-4). SiteMap calculates a site score for each predicted active site based on several factors: site size, solvent exposure, hydrophobicity, hydrophilicity, and hydrogen bond donor and acceptor potential.

## Receptor grid generation and virtual screening
The grid size defines the volume of a receptor’s active site within which a ligand can explore potential binding interactions during docking. The grid around the receptor was generated using the GLIDE [ ] module in the Schrödinger suite. The grid dimensions were determined based on the active sites identified by SiteMap. Virtual screening is a computational method used in drug discovery to identify small molecules most likely to bind to target receptors. For this study, we utilized FDA-approved ligand libraries from the free database of commercially-available compounds for virtual screening (ZINC) [ ]. These ligand libraries were prepared for virtual screening using Schrödinger’s LigPrep [ ] module. Subsequently, in silico screening and docking analyses were performed on all kinase fusion protein targets against the FDA-approved ligands using the Glide module in the Schrödinger package (release 2021-4).

## Multiple sequence alignment of fusion protein sequences
We provide multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) for kinase fusion proteins to compare the sequences across different isoforms or related fusion proteins. This alignment helps identify conserved regions, which may be crucial for the function or stability of the protein, and variable regions that could influence the protein’s interaction with other molecules or its role in disease. MSA of kinase fusion protein sequences was performed using ClustalW [ ]. We generated visual plots for each kinase fusion protein alignment. A Python script using Matplotlib created these plots, sorting sequences by length. This visualization step allowed for an intuitive inspection of sequence conservation and variability across the aligned kinase fusion protein isoforms.

## Comparison of fusion protein isoforms (root mean square deviation, secondary structure, relative accessible surface area)
We perform a comparative analysis of different isoforms of kinase fusion proteins by calculating the RMSD, secondary structure elements, and relative accessible surface area. We conducted Fisher’s exact tests for each type of secondary structure, considering a P-value <.05 as indicative of a statistically significant alteration. These comparisons help understand the structural differences between isoforms, which may affect their function and interaction with other proteins or drugs.

## Comparison of fusion protein sequences/structures with known sequences/structures from Protein Data Bank
The predicted structures of kinase fusion proteins are compared with known structures available in the PDB [ ]. This comparison helps identify similarities and differences that could influence the function of the fusion proteins or their potential as drug targets.

## Active sites, confident structure selection through pLDDT distributions between active versus nonactive sites
We identify and annotate the active sites of kinase fusion proteins using computational tools such as SiteMap. Active site prediction is crucial for understanding the potential binding regions for inhibitors and designing drugs specifically targeting these sites. We use pLDDT scores to assess the confidence of the predicted structures, particularly focusing on the active sites versus nonactive regions. This allows us to select the most reliable structures for further analysis, ensuring that the predicted active sites are accurate and relevant for drug discovery. By using R scripts, we highlighted the active site regions. To differentiate active from nonactive sites in kinase fusion proteins, we annotated with an ‘ActiveSite’, indicating whether a site was active or nonactive based on SiteMap predictions. Violin plots were then generated using ggplot2 to visualize the distribution of pLDDT scores for active versus nonactive regions. Only significant plots, where the active site pLDDT median was higher than the nonactive site saved for further analysis. This process enabled the identification of regions within kinase fusion proteins that are likely to be structurally and functionally significant.

## Interacting kinase inhibitor information
We used QikProp (Schrödinger Release 2021-4: QikProp, Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, 2021) to provide detailed information on kinase inhibitors predicted to interact with the fusion proteins. This includes data on variety of pharmaceutically relevant properties—octanol/water and water/gas log Ps, log S, log BB, overall CNS activity, Caco-2 and MDCK cell permeabilities, log Khsa for human serum albumin binding, and log IC50 for HERG K +− channel blockage, helping researchers to identify promising candidates for targeted therapy.

## PubMed search of drug/disease-related clinical trials and kinase-substrate information
To complement the in silico findings, we searched PubMed and clinical trials using the terms of individual kinase fusion protein names and 72 FDA-approved kinase inhibitor names. We performed the PubMed abstract search and PubMed full-text search for 1399 kinase human fusion genes with all combinations of 72 kinase inhibitors. After manual curation, we found 4959 records from 2912 PubMed abstracts between 91 unique kinase fusion gene IDs and 62 FDA-approved kinase inhibitors. For these found kinase fusion genes and 62 kinase inhibitors, we further searched clinicaltrials.gov. After manual curation, we found 542 clinical trials (NCT IDs) among 10 kinase fusion genes and 20 kinase inhibitors.

## Drug and disease information
For all genes involved in these curated fusion genes, drug–target interactions (DTIs) were extracted from DrugBank [ ] (January 2021, version 5.1.8) with the duplicated DTI pairs excluded [ ]. All drugs were grouped using Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification system codes. We also searched the PubMed literature evidence on the drugs that were reported as inhibiting or targeting individual fusion genes using this search expression, ‘((BCR [Title/Abstract]) AND ABL1 [Title/Abstract]) AND fusion [Title/Abstract]) AND drug [Title/Abstract]’. For the disease information, we utilized the MeSH terms from our found PubMed records. We also used DiseaseNet and MyCancerGenome.

## Database architecture
The KinaseFusionDB system is based on a three-tier architecture: client, server, and database. It includes a user-friendly web interface, Perl’s DBI module, and MySQL database. This database was developed on MySQL 3.23 with the MyISAM storage engine.

## Results

## Revisit the kinases that have FDA-approved kinase inhibitors in the pan-cancer kinase fusion gene in human
From a recent review paper [ ], we had a kinase inhibitor list approved to target major kinases by the FDA, as shown in . However, the most targeting for these kinases was based on the wild type but enhanced kinase function or single-nucleotide variants in the kinase domain. Only a few targeted the kinases in the fused form with other partner genes, like some kinase inhibitors targeting ABL [ ], ALK [ ], and RET [ , , ]. For these known kinase inhibitors in , we added a column listing the kinase fusion proteins corresponding to individual major kinases. We intended to explain the need to explore kinase fusion proteins and their potential targeting approaches or strategies. In this study, we will present a comprehensive analysis of kinase fusion genes and their corresponding kinase fusion proteins alongside an overview of current scientific knowledge. Moreover, we will evaluate and rank 72 FDA-approved kinase inhibitors based on their potential efficacy against these fusion-driven cancers.
 

## Previous research and clinical trials on the kinase fusion genes and FDA-approved kinase inhibitors
To provide a comprehensive overview of the current research landscape, we present an analysis of recent studies, investigations, and clinical trials focused on kinase fusion genes and FDA-approved kinase inhibitors. These include an up-to-date summary of relevant research papers and clinical trials ( ). For example, we searched PubMed abstracts and full-text using individual kinase fusion gene names and 72 FDA-approved kinase inhibitors. We checked whether these kinase fusion gene names and inhibitors were mentioned in PubMed’s abstract and full text. We manually curated the found abstracts. As a result, we found 2912 articles that described the relationship between 91 kinase fusion genes and 62 FDA-approved kinase inhibitors. Most targeted kinases here belonged to the tyrosine kinase group (TK) ( ). A continuously increasing number of studies have been reported since the first designed and approved kinase inhibitor, imatinib, for the BCR::ABL1 fusion gene in 2001. The top 10 most PubMed reported kinase fusion genes with the FDA-approved kinase inhibitors were BCR::ABL1, EML4::ALK, FIP1L1::PDGFRA between FIP1L1 (factor interacting with PAPOLA and CPSF1) and PDGFRA (platelet derived growth factor receptor alpha), KIT::PDGFRA between KIT (KIT proto-oncogene, receptor) and PDGFRA, ETV6::NTRK3 between ETV6 (ETS variant transcription factor 6) and NTRK3, KIF5B::RET between KIF5B (kinesin family member 5B) and RET, CD74::ROS1 between CD74 (CD74 molecule) and ROS1, CCDC6::RET between CCDC6 (coiled-coil domain containing 6) and RET, STRN::ALK between STRN (striatin) and ALK, and NUP214::ABL1 between NUP214 (nucleoporin 214) and ABL1. The top 10 most PubMed-reported FDA-approved kinase inhibitors in targeting kinase fusion genes were imatinib, crizotinib, dasatinib, nilotinib, ponatinib, alectinib, bosutinib, ceritinib, lorlatinib, and entrectinib. More details are available in .
 From clinicaltrials.gov, we searched the condition, intervention, and description sections with a combination of the kinase fusion gene name and kinase inhibitors mentioned in the PubMed search article. shows the statistics across the years. There were 542 CNTIDs (clinicaltrials.gov ID) between 10 kinase fusion genes and 20 FDA-approved kinase inhibitors. Among kinase fusion genes, the BCR::ABL1 has the longest history of disease–drug development and the largest reported clinical studies. The next frequent one was EML4::ALK, the second most well-known solid tumor fusion gene since the next-generation sequencing era. CD74::ROS1, first reported in lung cancer in 2012, has also gained high attention. KIAA1549::BRAF is frequently identified in pediatric low-grade glioma. Overall, BCR::ABL, EML4::ALK, CD74::ROS1, ETV6::NTRK3, and KIAA1549::BRAF were reported in the clinical trials using the FDA-approved kinase inhibitors. Imatinib, dasatinib, nilotinib, bosutinib, ponatinib, asciminib, crizotinib, ruxolitinib, sorafenib, larotrectinib, axitinib, and gilteritinib were reported in the clinical trials targeting kinase fusion genes. More details, including the clinical trial status, phase, and starting dates, are available in .

## Impact of kinase fusion genes for activation of kinase expression and downstream affected genes and pathways
The frequency analysis of kinase fusion genes across all fusion genes in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and CCLE datasets reveals critical insights into the expression and distribution of these fusions across different cancer types. As shown in , kinase fusion genes represent a substantial portion of fusion events in TCGA and CCLE, respectively. TCGA and CCLE showed that 5 ~ 55% and 6 ~ 24% of fusion genes of individual cancer types of TCGA and CCLE had kinase fusion genes. For example, in TCGA, out of 1097 fusion events observed in BRCA (breast cancer), 422 cases (~38.5%) were kinase fusion genes. This substantial proportion across pan-cancer highlights the tumorigenic roles of the kinase fusion genes and presents the opportunity for targeted therapeutic interventions. Similarly, stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) and lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) also showed a high proportion of kinase fusion genes out of all fusion genes as 178/516 and 212/418, respectively. These pie charts emphasize the widespread impact of kinase genes across multiple cancer types, making them key targets for cancer therapies. Other cancer types, such as ovarian cancer, prostate cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, bladder cancer, and skin cutaneous melanoma, also depicted a varying degree of kinase genes, underlining the complexity and heterogeneity of kinase involvement in cancer progression across different malignancies. Similarly, CCLE shows the involvement of kinase genes in various cancer cell lines. For example, lung, breast, and brain cancer had 53/338, 22/120, and 22/248 cells with kinase fusion genes, highlighting the importance of kinase signaling in cancer cell survival and proliferation.
To identify the impact of the kinase fusion genes, we investigated the downstream affected genes by individual kinase fusion genes in their cancer type. We performed the differential expressed gene analysis between fusion-positive (FP) cell lines and their matched normal tissue samples as the fusion-negative (FN) cell lines (Wilcoxon t-test, marked as * for P < .05, ** for P < .01, *** for P < .001). on the box plots in . Among 771 up-regulated genes in 23 different tissue types, there were 20 kinases, 27 transcription factors, 56 oncogenes, 204 CGC genes, and 58 IUPHAR drug target genes. ( ). Notably, ABL1, ALK, and BCR of BCR-ABL1 in leukemia and EML-ALK in lung cancer showed highly significant fold changes (P < .001) in their cancer types, directly showing the impact of kinase fusion genes to enhance the oncogenic function of kinases.
 Using these 771 affected up-regulated genes, we investigated the enriched biological pathways per cancer and visualized them using the Cytoscape’s ClueGO module [ ]. The network map in highlights significant biological processes, such as in lung cancer, axonogenesis, regulation of adenylate cyclase activity, and the ERBB2 signaling pathway, with different colored clusters representing distinct functional groups. Each node represents a Gene Ontology (GO) term, and the size reflects the term’s significance, while the connections indicate shared genes between processes. The ERBB2 signaling pathway, a known oncogenic driver in lung cancer, is frequently altered through mutations, gene amplification, or protein overexpression. It contributes to lung tumor development and progression by dysregulating key cancer-related genes [ ]. Furthermore, the adenylate cyclase (ADCY) gene family, encoding Adenylyl Cyclase (AC) proteins, plays crucial roles in cell proliferation, apoptosis, and immune escape [ , ]. Notably, AC compounds are already used in treating diseases like neurodegenerative disorders and asthma [ ]. Likewise, in breast cancer, the map highlights key biological processes such as mRNA transcription by RNA polymerase II, regulation of transcription initiation, and cellular response to estrogen stimulus, which are crucial for understanding gene regulation mechanisms in breast cancer. In the hematopoietic and lymphoid tissue (HALT), notable biological processes include regulation of lymphocyte differentiation, immunoglobulin production, and synaptic vesicle endocytosis, which are crucial for immune cell development and function. Lymphocyte differentiation progresses from hematopoietic stem cells in bone marrow to lymphocyte maturation in secondary lymphoid tissues, with key processes including regulation of lymphocyte differentiation [ ]. The map also highlights processes like DNA replication maintenance of fidelity and regulation of mitotic metaphase/anaphase transition, key to cell cycle regulation and immune response. In the central nervous system (CNS) and stomach tissues, upregulated genes are involved in positive regulation of epithelial cell differentiation and regulation of postsynaptic membrane neurotransmitter receptor levels, respectively.

## Overview of KinaseFusionDB, a base resource studying kinase fusion protein
KinaseFusionDB provides five functional annotations of 7680 kinase fusion genes and 23 functional annotations of ~1399 kinase fusion proteins across 17 categories with user-friendly visualization. The main features of KinaseFusionDB annotation categories are summarized below.

The ‘Kinase fusion gene summary’ category shows a basic fusion gene summary following multiple annotations for each fusion partner gene. It provides an overview of the fusion gene with detailed annotations for each fusion partner, including gene symbol, chromosomal location, and UniProt [ ]Accession IDs. It uses the University of California, Santa Cruz (UCSC) genome browser [ ] to show the most frequently reported breakpoint information and all known breakpoints of individual partners in the gene structures.
The ‘Kinase fusion gene sample information’ category shows the sample or cell list that expressed the kinase fusion gene. It integrates data from multiple resources, such as the COSMIC and CCLE databases, displaying breakpoints for each kinase fusion partner gene with corresponding genomic coordinates.
The ‘Kinase fusion ORF analysis’ category provides the in-frame fusion gene information after ORF annotation, such as gene isoform IDs, breakpoints, fusion mRNA sequence length, and fusion protein sequence length.
The ‘Kinase fusion amino acid sequences’ category provides the fusion amino acid sequences of all fusion isoforms.
The ‘Multiple sequence alignment of all fusion protein isoforms’ category provides the multiple sequence alignment among all predicted fusion protein isoform sequences using CLUSTAL 2.1 [ ]. This alignment helps compare the sequence similarity and identify conserved regions across all fusion isoforms.
The ‘Kinase fusion protein functional features’ category provides the retention information of 39 protein features due to the formation of fusion proteins with a link to an in-house visualization web tool for functional features of fusion genes, FGviewer [ ]. This tool allows users to explore functional impacts at the DNA, mRNA, protein, and pathogenic levels.
The ‘Kinase Fusion Protein Structures’ category offers predicted 3D structures of kinase fusion proteins, available in PDB and CIF formats, with embedded Mol* visualization directly on the webpage for interactive exploration [ ]. For individual 3D structures, we also can get the basic fusion information, including gene isoform ID, breakpoint, fusion protein sequence, and breakpoint position on the fusion protein sequence.
The ‘Comparison of fusion protein isoforms’ category provides the superimposed relationship between one-to-one comparisons among all fusion protein isoform 3D structures. This category also provides a secondary structure comparison for each pair of fusion protein isoforms.
The ‘Comparison of fusion protein sequence/structures with known sequences/structures’ category compares protein sequences and 3D protein structures between our fusion protein prediction and known ones from PDB search using the multiple sequence alignment and superimposing through chimera 1.15 [ ].
The ‘pLDDT score distribution’ category provides the pLDDT score distribution across fusion protein sequence length with the best active site marking and differential analysis of the pLDDT scores between active and nonactive sites. This result can provide background to choose confident 3D structures. Violin plots are used to visualize the distribution of predicted active site scores, highlighting variations between active and non-active sites across the kinase fusion proteins.
The ‘Potential active site information’ category provides information on the best active sites predicted using SiteMap [ , ] of the Schrodinger package per individual kinase fusion protein isoforms. For the comparison, this category also provides the known binding site residues of the found PDBs.
The ‘Ramachandran plot of kinase fusion protein structure’ category provides the Ramachandran plot of individual predicted kinase fusion protein 3D structures for assessment.
The ‘Virtual screening results’ category provides the top 20 interacting FDA-approved kinase inhibitors of individual kinase fusion protein isoform structures using the Glide [ ] tool of the Schrodinger package. ## Needs of whole 3D structures of kinase fusion proteins
Kinase fusion proteins are the primary targeted oncoproteins in human cancer. However, due to the lack of whole 3D protein structures, further analyses were not available. From PDB, we searched individual kinase fusion gene names as BCR::ABL1, and we could find only eight fusion protein–related PDB files. shows the multiple sequence alignments and superimposed structure in 3D of the found PDB files with their corresponding kinase fusion proteins for BCR::ABL1, CCDC6::RET, CD74::ROS1, EML4::ALK, and LMNA::NTRK1 between LMNA (lamin A/C) and NTRK1. The comparison of known PDB structures with our predicted kinase fusion protein structures highlights the limitations of relying solely on existing PDB data for structural insights. The superimposed 3D structures reveal that while some regions of the kinase fusion proteins align with existing PDB entries, these alignments are often incomplete and do not extend across the fusion breakpoints. This incomplete coverage means that the current PDB structures are insufficient for fully understanding the conformational dynamics of the entire fusion protein. Our approach, which involves predicting the whole 3D structures of these fusion proteins, overcomes this limitation by providing comprehensive structural models that include both sides of the breakpoint. Considering multiple isoforms of these kinase fusion proteins can help better understand the structural aspects of the entire fusion protein. These models, validated by favorable Ramachandran plots and pLDDT score distribution checking, offer a more accurate and complete picture of the kinase fusion proteins’ structural characteristics, which are essential for developing targeted therapies. Furthermore, in antibody-based therapies, knowing the 3D structure of both the antibody and the target antigen is essential for designing treatments that bind specifically to disease-related proteins. Similarly, enzyme therapies rely on an accurate understanding of the enzyme’s structure to enhance its catalytic activity or modification for therapeutic applications. By predicting the entire 3D structures of kinase fusion proteins, targeted treatments can be more effectively developed for precision medicine [ ]. All predicted 3D structures of kinase fusion proteins are available in the website of KinaseFusionDB.

## Discussion
Previous studies on fusion genes have primarily concentrated on lower-level biological entities such as DNA breakage, fusion transcripts, and a limited number of fusion proteins. This focus is mainly due to the restricted understanding of fusion protein sequences, domain retention, and structures. To address this gap, our earlier work on FusionGDB and FusionGDB 2.0 provided systematic functional annotations of human fusion genes. In this study, we integrated all known breakpoints of kinase fusion genes from FusionGDB2.0, COSMIC, and CCLE. Then, we annotated ORF. For the in-frame cases, we made the full-length transcript sequences. We input these kinase fusion mRNA sequences into ORFfinder and chose the longest ORF-based protein sequences. In total, we identified 973 tail kinase fusion proteins and 449 head kinase fusion proteins, further expanding the potential for targeted cancer research.
The development of targeted therapies against specific cancer-driving molecules has led to significant progress in cancer treatment, starting with the early success of Gleevec, the first small-molecule inhibitor against the kinase ABL of BCR-ABL fusion protein. Today, numerous targeted therapies have received FDA approval for cancer treatment. However, the initial effectiveness of these therapies is frequently undermined by the tumor’s development of resistance, often due to the acquisition of new mutations in the target molecule or through alterations in the tumor cells’ epigenome, transcriptome, or kinome.
KinaseFusionDB is the only knowledgebase providing deep functional and structural annotation on the kinase fusion genes/proteins in human cancer. KinaseFusionDB represents a significant advancement in cancer research by offering a comprehensive and systematic repository dedicated to kinase fusion proteins. Unlike other databases, KinaseFusionDB provides extensive, detailed information and analytical tools designed explicitly for exploring kinase fusion genes and proteins, which are crucial targets in cancer therapeutics. KinaseFusionDB offers diverse data for each kinase fusion protein, including summaries of the kinase fusion genes and their sample information, detailed ORF analyses, and complete amino acid sequences. This depth of information ensures that researchers have access to all relevant aspects of kinase fusion proteins in one centralized location, facilitating more informed and efficient research. One of the unique features of KinaseFusionDB is its provision of 3D structural data for kinase fusion proteins, along with pLDDT scores and detailed Ramachandran plots. These features allow for a more precise understanding of the fusion proteins’ structural dynamics and potential functional sites. The database also includes a comparative analysis of fusion protein isoforms and comparisons with known structures from the PDB. This level of structural detail is essential for researchers aiming to understand the details of kinase fusion proteins and their roles in oncogenesis.
The fusion protein is a monomer, but the MSA and template searching steps are done like a multimer prediction problem. There are multiple studies to predict the multimer protein prediction by enhancing the multiple sequence alignment part and template process part [ , ]. However, all tools eventually used AlphaFold2 in the protein 3D structure step. Most of all, the fusion protein is not the multimer, and usually, kinase fusion proteins retain only one kinase domain. Out of ~936 kinase fusion genes, only 12 retained two (multi) kinase domains from each partner. Most of all, in , we described the better-structured protein regions of the representative kinase fusion proteins in terms of the pLDDT and protein lengths. Therefore, currently, utilizing the AlphaFold2 is the best way for us to predict the 3D structures of fusion proteins. In the near future, we also plan to enhance AlphaFold2 to predict the fusion protein 3D structures by creating personalized genome-based fusion protein sequences and utilizing the unmasking approach of the template process step.
KinaseFusionDB is crucial for understanding the structural dynamics of kinase fusion proteins, particularly in terms of how conformational changes impact inhibitor binding. As reported, the structural differences between ABL kinase binding to imatinib and dasatinib, the Asp-Phe-Gly (DFG) motif’s position (DFG-in or DFG-out), and activation loop orientation can alter drug compatibility. Our database enables the detailed analysis of these key structural elements in fusion kinases, aiding in predicting active and inactive conformations [ ]. KinaseFusionDB provides functional annotations of kinase fusion proteins and includes multiple sequence alignments of all fusion protein isoforms. This enables users to identify conserved and divergent regions, which are critical for understanding the functional implications of these proteins in cancer. As reported by Raisinghani et al. in 2024, MSA is important in predicting the structural diversity of kinase protein isoforms [ ]. It highlights that shallow subsampling of MSA can effectively predict active conformations and their heterogeneity. At the same time, methods like randomized sequence scanning can further enhance the prediction of diverse conformational states, particularly for complex fusion kinase proteins. MSA-based approaches allow better representation of the structural ensembles and functional states of these proteins, which is critical for accurate modeling and drug targeting. The database compares the sequences and structures of kinase fusion proteins with those of known proteins, offering insights into potential functional similarities and differences. A particularly novel aspect of KinaseFusionDB is its integration of virtual screening results, which identify potential small molecules that could target kinase fusion proteins. The database also links these findings to existing drugs, related diseases, and ongoing clinical trials, thus providing a bridge between in silico findings and practical therapeutic applications. This feature is vital for researchers seeking to repurpose existing drugs or to discover new therapeutic candidates for treating cancers driven by kinase fusion proteins.
To ensure biologically meaningful and mechanism-driven virtual screening, we limited our analysis to the active sites predicted by the SiteMap tool from the Schrödinger suite. Further, we refined our selection by focusing exclusively on those active sites within the fusion proteins’ kinase domains. These domains represent well-established druggable regions critical to the oncogenic activity of kinase fusion proteins. By anchoring our docking efforts within these functionally relevant regions, we aimed to enhance the reliability of our screening results and prioritize inhibitors with higher potential for biological efficacy. Through Insilco, we prioritize the screened molecules based on their binding score and Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion (ADME) properties by following the Lipinski rule of five. We also checked the off-target effects by docking the same molecules to the wild type of fusion partner protein to ensure that it’s not affecting normal cell functioning. While a detailed mechanistic interpretation of each protein–inhibitor interaction is beyond the scope of this resource paper, our approach establishes a rational framework that can be further explored in future experimental and mechanistic studies.

In our virtual screening analysis against the BCR–ABL1 fusion protein, nine FDA-approved kinase inhibitors—lapatinib, pazopanib, pexidartinib, dacomitinib, infigratinib, larotrectinib, nilotinib, belumosudil, and crizotinib demonstrated notably higher docking scores as compared to asciminib, suggesting strong predicted binding affinities. Our findings highlight several alternative compounds with promising in silico interaction profiles. These results suggest potential repurposing opportunities and warrant further experimental investigation to evaluate their therapeutic relevance in the context of kinase fusion–driven cancers. These results provide preliminary computational validation of our virtual screening approach and reinforce the utility of KinaseFusionDB for identifying biologically plausible drug–fusion protein interactions. While a detailed mechanistic interpretation of each protein–inhibitor interaction is beyond the scope of this resource paper, our approach establishes a rational framework that can be further explored in future experimental and mechanistic studies.
While KinaseFusionDB offers valuable knowledge for kinase fusion proteins, it is essential to acknowledge certain limitations. The in silico nature of the predictions and virtual screenings means that the accuracy of these results depends on the algorithms and models used, such as AlphaFold2. However, the accuracy of AlphaFold2 is claimed to be equivalent to the experimentally validated structure. Additionally, the current scope of KinaseFusionDB is focused on specific types of kinase fusion proteins, and while extensive, it may not encompass all potential fusion events or mutations. Expanding the coverage and refining the predictive models will be key to maintaining its relevance and accuracy as the database evolves.

## Conclusion
To support the structural and functional exploration of kinase fusion proteins, we developed KinaseFusionDB. This comprehensive resource integrates multi-level annotations for 7680 kinase fusion genes and 1399 fusion proteins across 17 biologically relevant categories. The database provides extensive gene-level and isoform-level annotations, including fusion gene summaries, expression in samples or cell lines, ORF analysis, and amino acid sequences. Structural annotations encompass predicted 3D models of fusion proteins with interactive visualization, secondary structure comparisons, and sequence/structure alignment with known PDB entries. Functional features include retention of critical protein domains, pLDDT-based structural confidence scoring, active site prediction, and Ramachandran plots. KinaseFusionDB further supports drug discovery by offering virtual screening results for FDA-approved kinase inhibitors, literature-mined drug associations, and clinical trial data. Integrated tools like FGviewer enable users to visualize the functional impact of fusions across DNA, RNA, and protein levels. Collectively, KinaseFusionDB serves as a robust platform for the systematic interpretation of kinase fusion proteins in both basic and translational cancer research.

## Key Points
KinaseFusionDB is the only resource for the comprehensive functional annotation of human kinase fusion genes on a multi-scale.
KinaseFusionDB provides all protein sequences and 3D structures of human kinase fusion protein isoforms.
KinaseFusionDB provides the landscape of therapeutic records of kinase fusion proteins.
KinaseFusionDB will help develop enhanced precision medicine targeting kinase fusion proteins.
