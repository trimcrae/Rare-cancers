## Abstract
Cancer genomic profile (CGP) testing, which is covered by the national health insurance system in Japan, has been introduced as a routine clinical practice. However, the effects of CGP testing on prognoses remain unclear. Drug accessibility rates and prognoses after CGP testing were retrospectively investigated in 713 patients who underwent CGP testing examined by our molecular tumor board between November 2019 and October 2022,. Overall survival (OS) was examined using the log‐rank test and the Kaplan–Meier method. The median age of patients (326 males and 387 females) was 58 years (12–85 years). CGP testing revealed one or more gene mutations in 681 cases (95.5%), among which actionable gene mutations were detected in 439 (61.6%). Although treatment options were recommended for 285 cases (40.0%) by the molecular tumor board, only 45 received treatment based on their gene mutations. During the median observation period of 8.6 months, 351 (49.2%) patients died of the exacerbation of existing diseases. No significant differences were observed in OS between patients treated with and without genomically matched therapy (p = 0.285). According to clinical responses to treatment based on gene mutations, median OS was significantly longer in patients who achieved partial response and stable disease (26.5 months; 95% CI 14.4–38.6) than in those with progressive disease and not evaluated (9.8 months; 95% CI 5.8–13.8, p = 0.013). Responses to treatment based on gene mutations may improve prognoses, and it is important to increase the drug accessibility rate after CGP testing.
Keywords: advanced solid tumor, cancer genomic profile test, clinical response, genomically matched therapy, precision cancer medicine

We herein first reported the prognostic impact according to the effect of genomically matched therapy after CGP testing for advanced or metastatic solid tumors in clinical practice under the Japanese national health insurance system. Although 40.0% of patients were recommended for treatment based on their gene mutations in our molecular tumor board review after CGP testing, only approximately 6.3% received this treatment. However, when treatment based on gene mutations was initiated and partial response or stable disease treatment effects were achieved, OS was significantly prolonged.

## INTRODUCTION
Approximately one million individuals are estimated to develop cancer each year, with nearly one in every two developing cancer during their lifetime. Cancer genomic medicine, which enables more effective and efficient diagnosis, treatment, and prevention, is attracting increasing attention. In the United States, then president Barack Obama announced the “Precision Medicine Initiative” in his State of the Union address in January 2015, and in January 2016, the vice president, Joe Biden, led the launch of the “Cancer Moonshot” project, which aimed to develop new diagnostic and treatment methods to overcome cancer. In the UK, Genomics England, established by the UK Department of Health in 2013, is implementing the “100,000 Genomes Project” to analyze the whole genome of patients with cancer and rare diseases. In December 2018, Genomics England announced the completion of the 100,000 Genomes Project analysis. In October of the same year, the UK Minister of Health announced the goal of conducting one million whole‐genome analyses over the next 5 years.
In Japan, three types of cancer genomic profile (CGP) tests (OncoGuide NCC Oncopanel System, FoundationOne CDx Cancer Genome Profile, and FoundationOne Liquid CDx Cancer Genome Profile) entered full‐scale applications under the national health insurance system. , , , In accordance with the Clinical Practice Guidance for Next‐Generation Sequencing in Cancer Diagnosis and Treatment (edition 2.1), CGP tests covered by the national health insurance system include those for patients with solid tumors for whom no standard treatment is available or for whom standard treatment has been completed (including those who are expected to complete such treatment). , , In addition, for the purpose of consolidating precision oncology treatment with quality control and assurance in designated hospitals, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare originally designated 13 cancer genomic medicine core hospitals, 32 hub hospitals, and 203 cooperative hospitals in April 2023. , 

Cancer genomic profile testing uses next‐generation sequencing and other technologies to simultaneously analyze a large number of cancer‐related genes, which is expected to assist in the selection of treatment strategies, thereby improving cancer treatment outcomes. A pilot study reported that 27% of patients with recurrent cancer achieved a 30% increase in progression‐free survival with treatment selected based on tumor molecular profiles from that with their previous treatment. Similar findings from a retrospective study showed prolonged progression‐free survival and overall survival (OS) in cancer patients who entered a phase 1 trial based on a molecular alteration identified in their tumors. However, in the SHIVA trial, the first clinical trial on molecularly targeted drugs for the off‐label treatment of heavily pretreated metastatic cancer, the use of molecularly targeted agents outside their indications did not prolong progression‐free survival over that with treatment selected by physicians in heavily pretreated patients with cancer. 

In Japan, the Trial of Onco‐Panel for Gene‐profiling to Estimate both Adverse events and Response (TOP‐GEAR) project, a study using CGP testing, reported patient access to therapeutic agents after standard treatment in Japan. Between May 2016 and May 2017, approximately 50% of patients who participated in the project and were able to undergo a genetic analysis were found to have abnormalities in actionable genes, and 25 (13.4%) received therapeutic drugs tailored to their individual gene mutations. Although CGP testing is being implemented in Japan under the national health insurance system, low rates of drug accessibility after CGP testing have been reported, and, thus, the prognostic impact of CGP testing remains unclear.
Therefore, the present study investigated the clinical usefulness and prognostic relevance of CGP testing at our institute under the national health insurance system in Japan.

2.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1.

## Patients
Among 713 patients who underwent CGP testing at our institution between November 2019 and October 2022, those with metastatic cancer whose treatment plan was discussed by the molecular tumor board within the study period were retrospectively examined, except for patients whose analysis failed. In Japan, insurance coverage for CGP tests is restricted to patients with advanced solid tumors exhibiting disease progression during standard therapy or those for whom there are no appropriate standard treatments, including patients with rare cancers and carcinomas of unknown primary origin.
The present study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our institution (2021–1195), and data were collected in compliance with the ethical requirements of our institution.

2.2.

## Pathological assessment
All cases for which CGP testing was requested were evaluated by pathologists to ensure that the submitted specimens contained the required tumor volume for CGP testing, and if the minimum tumor volume specified for each CGP test was not secured in the requested specimen, CGP tests were not submitted.

2.3.

## Cancer genomic profile testing
FoundationOne CDx Cancer Genomic Profile, FoundationOne Liquid CDx Cancer Genomic Profile, and the OncoGuide NCC Oncopanel System were used in this study. FoundationOne CDx Cancer Genomic Profile (Foundation Medicine) is a CGP platform that applies next‐generation sequencing to in vitro diagnostics with a hybrid capture‐based target enrichment approach and whole‐genome shotgun library construction to identify gene substitutions, insertions and deletions (indels), copy number alterations, and select rearrangements. FoundationOne CDx Cancer Genomic Profile/FoundationOne Liquid CDx Cancer Genomic Profile detects alterations in 324 genes, including all coding exons of 309 cancer‐related genes (substitutions, indels, and copy number alterations) and select intronic regions of 36 commonly rearranged genes. In addition, FoundationOne CDx Cancer Genomic Profile/FoundationOne Liquid CDx Cancer Genomic Profile simultaneously profiles the tumor mutation burden (TMB) and microsatellite instability status.
The OncoGuide NCC Oncopanel System detects mutations in 124 genes and evaluates TMB affecting the treatment effects of immune checkpoint inhibitors while differentiating the mutations that cancer patients are born with (germline mutations) and those present only in cancer cells (somatic gene mutations). Consequently, in some cases, test results may facilitate the diagnosis of hereditary cancers. 

The drug accessibility rate and prognostic impact of CGP testing for advanced or metastatic solid tumors in clinical practice were evaluated.
The pathologist selected representative blocks from stored formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) specimens of patients and estimated the tumor content in HE specimens. Between 10 and 25 slices with a thickness of 5 μm were prepared to achieve a tumor area of at least 16 mm2 for the OncoGuide NCC Oncopanel System and 25 mm2 for FoundationOne CDx Cancer Genomic Profile, which are the volumes required for analyses, and submitted for CGP testing.

2.4.

## Molecular tumor board (Expert Panel)
The molecular tumor board of our institute discussed reports from CGP testing in conjunction with the Center for Cancer Genomics and Advanced Therapeutics and generated final reports for patients. The molecular tumor board consists of physicians specializing in cancer pharmacotherapy; those with expertise in genetic medicine; genetic counselors; pathologists; physicians with expertise in molecular genetics and cancer genomic medicine; and those with expertise in bioinformatics. Evidence levels for therapeutic efficacy were categorized as A–F, following the Japanese Society of Medical Oncology/Japan Society of Clinical Oncology/Japan Cancer Association guidelines. 

2.5.

## Definition of actionable and druggable genomic alterations
Actionable gene mutations were defined as follows: predictive evidence level D and more and high TMB (after the approval of pembrolizumab by FDA). We defined druggable gene mutations as genes for which treatment was recommended by the molecular tumor board, including approved drugs, investigational new drugs, and off‐label drugs. Genomically matched therapy was defined as actual therapy that patients received based on the results of CGP testing.

2.6.

## Statistical analysis
SPSS ver. 29.0 (IBM Corporation) was used for statistical analyses. In a univariate analysis, OS rates were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method with the log‐rank test. In a multivariate analysis, a Cox regression analysis was used. p < 0.05 was considered to indicate significance.

3.

## RESULTS
3.1.

## Patient characteristics
The CONSORT diagram of the present study is shown in Figure  . The characteristics of the 713 patients investigated in detail are listed in Table  . Between September 2019 and October 2022, 713 patients underwent CGP testing, and the results obtained were evaluated by the molecular tumor board in our hospital. The most common cancer was colorectal cancer in 143 patients (20.1%), followed by pancreatic cancer in 112 (15.7%), ovarian cancer in 59 (8.3%), breast cancer in 57 (8.0%), biliary tract cancer in 56 (7.9%), uterine cancer in 43 (6.0%), and soft tissue sarcoma in 36 (5.0%). The median age was 58 (range: 12–85) years, and there were 326 (45.7%) and 387 (54.3%) male and female patients, respectively. A total of 661 (92.7%) patients had an ECOG‐PS of 0 or 1. Among 713 patients, 611 (85.7%) underwent CGP testing with FoundationOne CDx Cancer Genomic Profile, 95 (13.3%) with FoundationOne Liquid CDx Cancer Genomic Profile, and 7 (1.0%) with the OncoGuide NCC Oncopanel System. Specimens from a primary site and metastatic site were used for CGP testing in 425 patients (59.6%) and 190 (26.6%), respectively. The median observation period was 8.6 (range: 0.1–38.1) months.
 
3.2.

## Drug accessibility rate and clinical responses
Among the 713 patients investigated in detail, actionable gene mutations were identified in 439 (61.6%). Although the molecular tumor board recommended treatment according to gene mutations for 285 patients (40.0%), only 45 (6.3%) received treatment based on gene mutations (Figure  ).
 Details on the gene mutations in and treatment selected for the 45 patients who received treatment according to their gene mutations are shown in Table  . Genomically matched therapy was initiated for 45 (6.3%) patients, most of whom had ovarian cancer (N = 7), followed by colorectal cancer (N = 6), pancreatic cancer (N = 6), breast cancer (N = 5), and lung cancer (N = 5). Forty‐five patients received treatment according to genetic mutations, among which 22 were in sponsor‐initiated clinical trials, six were in investigator‐initiated clinical trials, 16 were being treated by approved drugs, and one was being treated by an off‐label drug.
 Clinical response rates are shown in Table  . The overall clinical response was a partial response (PR) in 16 patients (35.6%), stable disease (SD) in 10 (22.2%), progressive disease (PD) in 17 (37.8%), and not evaluated (NE) in 2 (4.4%). No patient achieved a complete response (CR) in this study.
 There were 240 patients who were recommended treatment based on their gene mutations by the molecular tumor board but did not receive it. The most common reason for failure to receive treatment was the lack of a recommended clinical trial slot (77 patients), followed by worsening of the disease when gene panel test results were revealed (51 patients). Other reasons for failure were that 72 patients continued to receive prior therapy after gene panel test results were known, and 24 were screened for clinical trials but did not meet eligibility criteria.

3.3.

## Prognostic analysis of treatment based on gene mutations
The median observation period was 8.6 months from obtaining consent for CGP testing. There were 351 (49.2%) deaths related to cancer as the primary disease.
There were 22 deaths in patients who received treatment based on their genetic mutations and 329 deaths in those who did not. Kaplan–Meier plots for OS are shown in Figures  , , and .
 No significant differences were observed in prognosis between patients with or without a recommended treatment proposed by the molecular tumor board (median OS 14.3 months, 95% confidence interval; 11.7–16.3 vs. 14.0 months, 95% CI; 11.7–16.3, p = 0.597) (Figure  ).
The median survival time of patients who received genomically matched therapy (N = 45) was 18.0 months (95% CI, 7.9–28.1), while those of patients who did not receive genomically matched therapy (N = 668) 14.2 months (95% CI, 12.4–16.0) (Figure  ). No significant differences were noted in OS between patients who received genomically matched therapy and those who did not (p = 0.285).
The prognosis of patients based on responses to treatment according to their genetic mutations is shown in Figure  . According to clinical responses, the median OS was significantly longer in patients who achieved PR and SD (26.5 months; 95% CI 14.4–38.6) than in those with PD and NE (9.8 months; 95% CI 5.8–13.8, p = 0.005).
According to the ESCAT classification I/II, there were nine patients who received genomically matched therapy with ESCAT I/II, ERBB2 amplifications (N = 1) and KRAS (N = 1) in non‐small‐cell lung cancer, ERBB2 hotspot mutations in breast cancer (N = 1), FGFR2 fusions in cholangiocarcinoma (N = 1), ERBB2 amplifications in colorectal cancer (N = 3), BRCA1 (germline mutation) in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (N = 1), and tumor mutational burden‐high in salivary cancer (N = 1). The prognosis of patients with ESCAT I/II genomic alterations is shown in Figure  . The median survival time of patients who received genomically matched therapy with ESCAT I/II (N = 9) was 19.8 months (95% CI, 3.2–36.4), while those of patients who did not receive genomically matched therapy (N = 668) was 14.2 months (95% CI, 12.4–16.0). No significant differences were noted in OS between patients who received genomically matched therapy with ESCAT I/II and those who did not (p = 0.653).
 In our study, two patients died within 1 month, and 23 patients died within 2 months due to disease progression after consent for CGP testing was obtained. There were 224 patients who were unable to switch to the any other subsequent therapy after the molecular tumor board (death within 2 months after submission of the CGP testing, 23 patients; failure to continue treatment due to disease progression, 106 patients; and continuation of previous treatment, 95 patients). When analyzed excluding these cases, there was no significant difference in OS between those who received genomically matched therapy (18.0 months; 95% CI, 7.9–28.1) and those who did not (15.1 months; 95% CI, 12.8–17.4) (p = 0.810).
We also analyzed the prognosis after CGP testing in each cancer type and found no significant differences were noted in OS between patients who received genomically matched therapy and those who did not in any of the cancer types.

4.

## DISCUSSION
We herein reported the prognostic impact of CGP testing for advanced or metastatic solid tumors in clinical practice under the Japanese national health insurance system. Although 40.0% of patients were recommended treatment based on their gene mutations in our molecular tumor board review after CGP testing, only approximately 6.3% received this treatment. In the gene panel test conducted under the national health insurance system, no significant differences were observed in prognosis regardless of whether the molecular tumor board recommended treatment or treatment selected according to gene mutations was implemented. However, when treatment based on gene mutations was initiated and PR or SD treatment effects were achieved, OS was significantly prolonged. The present results indicate that the use of molecularly targeted agents prolongs OS, even in heavily pretreated patients with recurrent advanced solid cancer.
Regarding drug accessibility rates, as for the current status of CGP testing under the national health insurance system in Japan, 805 patients underwent CGP testing between June and October 2019, and 10.9% of these patients were then treated based on their gene mutations. Furthermore, according to a report from core hospitals for cancer genomic medicine, among the 747 cases (11 hospitals, June 2019 to January 2020) and 2294 cases (12 hospitals, February 2020 to January 2021) on which CGP testing were conducted, 28 (3.7%) and 176 (7.7%) patients received genomically matched therapy, respectively. In the present study, the drug accessibility rate was consistent with previously reported values.
As described above, in the SHIVA trial, the use of molecularly targeted agents outside their indications did not prolong progression‐free survival over that with treatment selected by physicians in heavily pretreated patients with cancer, and several key points may explain the overall negative finding of the SHIVA trial. They analyzed multiple treatment groups composed of many molecular alterations in patients with various tumor types and histologies. Furthermore, molecularly targeted agents were mostly used as single agents in their study, which often led to reduced efficacy through the development of resistance. Moreover, spatial and temporal intrapatient heterogeneity was not taken into consideration in their study. They also included heavily pretreated patients, which reduced the likelihood that molecularly targeted agents will be effective. 

Recently, to prioritize genomic alterations for treatment decision, scales of evidence for genomic alterations have been developed. ESMO has created ESCAT6, a scale that includes six levels ranked according to the level of evidence of the matched genomic alteration/drug. Level I means that the drug matched to the genomic alteration has been proved to be effective; level II means that the drug matched to the genomic alteration has been associated with preliminary evidence of efficacy. Beyond level II, the efficacy of the matched drug/genomic alteration is hypothetical at best. In patients with metastatic breast cancer, after a median follow‐up of 21.4 months (90% CI: 17.9–27.6), analysis of the primary end point in patients with ESCAT I/II showed a significantly longer progression free survival (PFS) in the targeted therapy arm (9.1 months; 90% CI: 7.1–9.8) than in the maintenance chemotherapy arm (2.8 months; 90% CI: 2.1–4.8, p < 0.001). 

Although limited information is currently available on prognoses after CGP testing under the national health insurance system in Japan, Ide et al. reported that in clinical practice, genomically matched therapy after CGP testing was administered to 12.2% of patients, and the OS of these patients was longer than that of patients who did not. Our study was conducted by a single‐center molecular tumor board, and we analyzed the drug accessibility rate, detailed reasons for failure to administer new treatment such as clinical trials, response rate of genomically matched therapies, and prognostic analysis of genomically matched therapies, especially according to the responses rate of genomically matched therapies, which has not been undertaken in previous reports. This is an important study in that it provides novel results and uses real‐world data from Japan.
The present results suggested that even in patients with recurrent advanced solid tumors who were expected to complete standard treatment under the national health insurance system, when treatment according to gene mutations was implemented and PR or SD treatment effects were achieved, the prognosis of patients appeared to be improved. However, the following issues are considered to have contributed to the low treatment attainment rate after the implementation of CGP testing in Japan. The indication for CGP testing covered by the national health insurance system in Japan is restricted to patients with advanced solid tumors exhibiting disease progression during standard therapy (including those expected to complete treatment) or patients for whom there are no appropriate standard treatments, including those with rare cancers and carcinoma of unknown primary origin. , , Even though CGP testing is now limited to after the completion of standard treatment due to insurance requirements in Japan, experts in each field examined the findings of CGP testing in detail in a molecular tumor board and provided new treatment options, such as those available in clinical trials, to 275 patients (38.6%). However, 240 patients for whom treatment was recommended according to their gene mutations by the molecular tumor board did not receive this treatment. Of these patients, 51 (21.3%) were unable to undergo genomically matched therapy due to disease progression after CGP testing. Therefore, the present study revealed that the benefits of CGP testing may not be fully extended to patients with metastatic breast cancer due to the indication for CGP testing covered by the national health insurance system in Japan.
Furthermore, there are not enough available slots for clinical trials. In the present study, 77 (32.1%) of patients who were recommended treatment according to their genetic mutations were unable to receive treatment due to the lack of available slots in clinical trials. To enroll patients in the limited number of clinical trial slots and receive treatment after CGP testing, it is important to know the status of clinical trials quickly and accurately. However, detailed information on clinical trials is not available and not shared sufficiently.
The present results suggest that CGP testing conducted at the appropriate time and based on the efficacy of previous therapy, the patient's general condition, and patient preference will increase the drug accessibility rate. To address these issues, it may be important to submit CGP testing at earlier treatment stages. In compliance with the treatment guidelines of academic societies for each cancer type, it is important to consider CGP testing at the start of the first‐line treatment for rare cancers and at the start of the second‐line treatment for other cancer types.
In addition, in settings where access to new clinical trials and clinical studies is limited, the gathering of detailed information on the progress of clinical trials, in close collaboration with the attending physician, is critical for increasing treatment reach rates. It is essential to collect information, including inquiries to investigators at other hospitals before the review meeting, mainly by coordinators of cancer genome medicine in collaboration with other professions, and to inform the attending physician of the details of the candidate trial immediately after the molecular tumor board.
The limitation in this study is that the ratio of patients who received genome‐matched therapies after CGP panel testing might be lower than expected in some previous reports. The proportion of patients who received genomically matched therapies by drug and therapy type in our study was as follows: clinical trials, 28 patients (investigational drugs, 27 patients [3.8%], NCCH1901, 1 patient [0.1%]); off‐label use, 1 patient (0.1%), and approved drugs, 16 patients (2.2%). Compared to previous reports, the following several factors could be attributed to the low drug accessibility rate after CGP testing in our study. The first reason would be the low ratio of patients receiving NCCH1901 because we were unable to conduct the NCCH1901 study at our hospital. The second reason was that there was only one patient receiving genomically matched therapy as off‐label use because the treatment with off‐label use requires the patient to pay all treatment costs, including the targeted therapies, under Japanese insurance. The third reason might be the low ratio of patients receiving “approved drugs.” Since the purpose of this study was to evaluate the usefulness of the CGP testing in clinical practice, we strictly defined cases in which treatment according to the gene mutation was implemented based on the results of the CGP testing. Cases in which treatment according to the gene mutation were implemented based on the results of companion tests, for instance, known before the CGP testing were excluded from the “cases in which treatment was achieved” in this study. Furthermore, our molecular tumor board recommended only those biomarkers that have been established to correlate a gene mutation with the efficacy of a therapeutic agent as “treatment according to the gene mutation. For example, cases in which mTOR inhibitors were administered to luminal breast cancer patients with PIK3CA mutations, as shown in a previous report, are not counted as cases in which genomically matched therapies were administered.
Another limitation is that the impact on PFS had not been evaluated. It is very important to evaluate the impact of CGP testing on PFS in heavily pretreated patients with recurrent advanced solid cancer under the national health insurance system. The study design such as the SHIVA study, in which molecularly targeted agents and treatment at the physician's choice were selected after CGP testing was performed, would allow us to evaluate the impact of CGP testing on PFS precisely. However, in this study, the results of CGP testing did not immediately change their treatments. That is, in clinical practice, there are some patients who cannot be treated after CGP testing due to worsening of their disease, patients who continue their previous treatment regardless of the results of CGP testing, and patients for whom no treatment according to their gene mutation could be found. The impact of CGP testing on PFS under the national health insurance system should be considered in future studies.
Regarding the prognosis according to gene mutations for each cancer type, since the number of cases in each cancer type in our study was small, further studies are needed to evaluate whether treatment according to gene mutation after CGP testing improves the prognosis and to focus on identifying biomarkers that can improve prognosis in each cancer type.
In conclusion, we herein demonstrated the prognostic impact of CGP testing for advanced or metastatic solid tumors in clinical practice. Genomically matched therapy after CGP testing was administered to 6.3% of patients. OS was significantly prolonged when treatment according to gene mutations was implemented and PR or SD treatment effects were achieved.
