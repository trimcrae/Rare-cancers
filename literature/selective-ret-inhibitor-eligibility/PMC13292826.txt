## Abstract

## Objectives
Molecular testing is increasingly used in thyroid cancer (TC) to complement diagnostics and guide systemic therapy choices. However, real-world data on its clinical impact remain limited. We evaluated the molecular landscape, clinical actionability, and outcomes of matched therapy in patients with advanced TC.

## Methods
We retrospectively assessed 103 patients with advanced TC who underwent panel-based molecular testing with potential treatment consequences at Radboud University Medical Center, Nijmegen, the Netherlands, between 2014 and 2024. Alterations were classified using the European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO) Scale for Clinical Actionability of molecular Targets (ESCAT), and details on matched treatment and follow-up were collected.

## Results
Molecular testing was performed at diagnosis (51.5%; primarily anaplastic TC) or upon disease progression (48.5%; primarily differentiated and medullary TC). Most frequent alterations were TP53 (39.8%), NRAS (17.5%), BRAF (16.5%), PTEN (16.5%), and RET (11.7%). Potential targetable alterations (ESCAT level I–III) were detected in 62.1% of patients, and 32.0% harbored ESCAT level I or II alterations considered clinically actionable. Matched systemic therapy was initiated in 16 patients and demonstrated an objective response rate of 56.3% and a disease control rate of 68.8%. Durable responses were predominantly observed in patients with ESCAT level I or II alterations (BRAF, RET, and NTRK). Matched therapy was associated with a trend toward longer overall survival (123.0 vs 57.0 months; P = 0.058).

## Conclusion
Molecular testing frequently revealed targetable alterations in patients with advanced TC, and matched therapies yielded meaningful clinical benefit. These findings underscore the value of precision medicine in TC and support broader implementation of molecular-guided treatment strategies.

## Introduction
Molecular testing has been increasingly used to complement the diagnosis of solid tumors, including thyroid cancer (TC), and to enable precision medicine ( ). With the growing availability of targeted therapies, molecular testing has become essential to select patients who may benefit from these treatments. In clinical care for advanced tumors, gene-specific panels using next-generation sequencing (NGS) are often applied to identify a range of common targetable molecular alterations observed in cancer patients.
European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO) guidelines broadly define the potential indications for molecular testing in advanced TC to facilitate these precision treatments ( , ). However, molecular testing is often limited by technical or financial constraints and inconsistent availability of specific drugs. This also leads to large heterogeneity of clinical applications in real-world practices that were highlighted in a recent publication ( ). While molecular testing is routinely performed in advanced TC clinical care, the real-world molecular outcomes and clinical consequences are unclear. This hampers the standardized and rational integration of molecular testing into clinical guidelines, thereby limiting treatment options for patients with unmet medical needs.
Systemic treatment options for advanced TC have evolved from multikinase inhibitors (MKIs) to highly selective targeted agents (Supplementary Table 1 (see section on given at the end of the article)). In the Netherlands, the availability of these drugs has influenced molecular testing practices beyond research purposes. Testing largely takes place in centers of expertise and has historically been reserved for cases in which the results are expected to impact clinical management. However, until recently, no specific guideline was available to define testing indications. The most recent 2024 Dutch clinical practice guidelines recommend performing molecular testing in patients with differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) showing progression on first-line systemic therapy, following the availability of the selective kinase inhibitors in the Netherlands ( ).
At Radboud University Medical Center, Nijmegen, we perform somatic molecular testing for clinical purposes in TC since approximately 2014. More recently, the molecular testing practice has changed and is now often incorporated in the routine patient care for patients with advanced TC. However, since no standardized protocols are available, this has mostly been done on a case-by-case basis upon deliberation within the multidisciplinary team. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of how the previous and current practices have resulted in clinical benefit for the patients is crucial. Currently, there is no study assessing the current practices and their outcome in the Netherlands, nor are there any data on the prevalence of specific targetable genetic alterations in patients with advanced TC in the Netherlands.
The current study examines the patterns, outcomes, and therapeutic consequences of molecular testing in patients with advanced TC in a real-life setting, following an approach restricted to the cases where this might lead to a treatment that was available, reimbursable, and potentially clinically meaningful for the patients. We retrospectively collected data from patients diagnosed with TC in our tertiary referral center that underwent molecular testing. We aimed to identify the molecular landscape in our cohort, examine the potential targetability of the genetic alterations according to the current ESMO Scale for Clinical Actionability of molecular Targets (ESCAT) ( ), and evaluate response patterns in patients receiving matched systemic therapy.

## Methods

## Patient selection
We retrospectively collected clinical data from all patients that underwent molecular testing at the Radboud University Medical Center, Nijmegen, the Netherlands, between April 1, 2014, and April 1, 2024. Eligible cases were identified through a search of the hospital pathology database. Following multidisciplinary deliberation or at the request of the treating physician, molecular testing was performed to aid in diagnosis or to guide systemic therapy options. The focus of the current study was on the latter. Therefore, the results from cases sequenced for initial diagnosis are described in the text, but were excluded from the final analyses. Data were collected from patient records and included patient demographics, clinicopathological information, and details about the molecular testing and potential systemic therapy administration. Data cutoff for patients who received matched therapy was November 2024. Disease progression was assessed by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) 1.1 and was reported as indicated in the patient records, without performing an additional review of the imaging ( ). Records were excluded if clinical or molecular data were incomplete, if the primary tumor was of non-thyroid origin, or if the molecular test was performed for hereditary tumor syndromes not involving TCs.

## Study approval
This study was approved by the Local Ethics Committee (Commissie Mensgebonden Onderzoek Arnhem-Nijmegen: 2024-17308). As part of our patient care system, individuals were explicitly given the option to permit or decline the use of their clinical data for research purposes. Patients provided informed consent electronically during routine hospital registration. Only data from patients who consented to research use were included.

## Molecular testing
DNA and/or RNA was derived from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor tissue by the local routine pathology laboratory. NGS was performed on a NextSeq 500 or a NovaSeq 6000 (both from Illumina), using one of seven validated NGS panels available through the nationwide Dutch pathology network (PALGA) ( ). All panels were targeted NGS panels analyzing mutations, copy number variations, microsatellite instability (MSI) status, and gene fusions (Supplementary Table 2). These panels vary slightly in gene content and technical specifications, but all cover key cancer-related genes relevant for diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making. Panel selection was based on local availability and clinical indication at the time of testing. Supplementary Table 3 details the mutations detected in our cohort and their distribution across the different panels. PD-L1 expression was assessed by immunohistochemistry using tumor proportion score (TPS). Positivity was defined as TPS ≥ 1%. PD-L1 expression data were included when available as additional information in patients who underwent NGS-based molecular profiling.

## Targetability of genetic alterations
Clinical actionability was assessed using the ESMO Scale for Clinical Actionability of molecular Targets (ESCAT) (Supplementary Table 3) ( ). Briefly, level I includes alterations with proven clinical benefit in prospective trials. Level II includes emerging genetic variants that are investigational with evidence of clinical benefit, requiring further validation. Level III includes hypothetical targets with benefit demonstrated in other tumor types with similar alterations. Level IV is supported only by preclinical or in silico evidence, without clinical data. In this study, ESCAT levels I–III were considered potentially targetable and ESCAT levels I–II were considered clinically actionable.

## Statistical analysis
Survival analyses and descriptive statistics were performed using SPSS, version 30.0 (IBM). Baseline characteristics are presented as number (n) and percentage or as median with interquartile range (IQR). The log-rank test was used to assess statistical significance between patients treated or not treated with matched therapy. Kaplan–Meier estimates were used to assess overall survival (OS), and hazard ratios (HRs) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards models. Objective response rate (ORR) was defined as the proportion of patients who achieved a best overall response of complete response (CR) or partial response (PR) according to RECIST 1.1. Disease control rate (DCR) included patients with a response (CR or PR) or stable disease (SD). OS was calculated from the date of diagnosis to the date of death or last follow-up.

## Results

## Study design and molecular test cohort
From 2014 to 2024, 143 patients underwent molecular testing at our center ( ). In 31 cases, the test was used to complement initial diagnosis, performed in Bethesda V nodules to confirm a papillary TC (PTC) diagnosis. Nine cases were excluded due to incomplete patient file (n = 4), the tumor was of non-thyroid origin (n = 2), or the molecular test was performed solely for confirmation of hereditary tumor syndromes (n = 3). Ultimately, molecular testing with potential consequences for systemic therapy was performed in 103 patients.
 Most patients underwent a total thyroidectomy (74.8%), followed by radioactive iodine therapy for most patients with DTC. Palliative radiotherapy either to control tumor or to alleviate symptoms was given to 48.5% of the patients ( ). Prior to molecular testing, most patients received no systemic therapy (70.9%), especially in the anaplastic thyroid cancer (ATC) cohort (100.0%). One previous line of systemic therapy was administered in 17 patients (16.5%), and two lines in 13 patients (12.6%).
 Molecular testing was performed either at initial diagnosis due to advanced-stage disease (51.5%), predominantly in anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (ATC), or during follow-up for progressive disease (48.5%), primarily in DTC and MTC. Material for NGS was mainly derived from the primary tumor, through fine-needle aspiration (8.7%), through histological needle biopsy (15.5%), or from the resected primary tumor (54.4%). Metastases were less frequently sampled, either via a histological biopsy (14.6%) or as a resected metastasis (6.8%).

## Molecular landscape in advanced TC
The molecular landscape of the total cohort is summarized in . At least one genetic alteration was detected in 87.1% of all patients, and gene fusions were detected in 10.2% of tested cases. Overall, the most frequent genetic alterations were identified in TP53 (n = 41, 39.8%), NRAS (n = 18, 17.5%), BRAF (n = 17, 16.5%), PTEN (n = 17, 16.5%), and RET (n = 12, 11.7%). Papillary thyroid cancer (PTC) (n = 23) was primarily driven by somatic BRAF mutations (n = 13, 56.5%), NRAS mutations (n = 4, 17.4%), and RET or NTRK fusions (n = 4, 17.4%). Patients with follicular TC (FTC; n = 7) harbored mutations in NRAS (n = 2), PTEN (n = 3), and TP53 (n = 3). Similarly, the oncocytic TC (OTC; n = 5) cohort presented mutations in PTEN (n = 2) and TP53 (n = 3). Patients with poorly differentiated TC (PDTC; n = 15) showed mutations in PDGFRA, ATM, or KEAP (each n = 1), next to TP53 (n = 7) or PTEN (n = 4). The ATC cohort (n = 38) showed the most diverse mutational profile. Mutations that were predominantly detected were in TP53 (n = 28, 73.7%), NRAS (n = 10, 26.3%), PTEN (n = 8, 21.1%), and CDKN2A (n = 5, 13.2%). Moreover, less frequent alterations were observed in HRAS, ATM, CHEK2, BRCA2, NOTCH1, PDGFRA, EGFR, and MSH6 (each n = 1). BRAF mutations were identified in three ATC patients (7.9%). Notably, microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) was observed in two ATC patients. RET mutations dominated the MTC cohort (n = 15), with 12 patients (80.0%) harboring RET alterations, including one germline mutation. Finally, PD-L1 assessed by immunohistochemistry (IHC) was positive in 21 out of 45 cases (46.7%).
 In 18 out of 22 detected genes, alterations co-occurred at least once, which is visualized in the co-occurrence network ( ). Overall, 37 patients (35.9%) had co-occurring events. TP53 mutations, which are the most prevalent, occasionally co-occurred with alterations in PTEN, NRAS, and BRAF. Alterations in RAS genes (NRAS, HRAS, and KRAS), BRAF, and RET were mutually exclusive. Notably, we identified a patient with PTC, who harbored a co-occurring NRAS mutation and an ETV6–NTRK3 fusion. One ATC patient had co-occurrent mutations in MSH6, NF1, BRCA2, ATM, PTEN, and TP53, despite lacking MSI-H status. Furthermore, PIK3CA co-occurred with BRAF in two cases and with NRAS in one case.
 

## Clinical actionability
The genetic variants were ranked according to the ESCAT. Potential targetable variants (ESCAT I–III) were detected in 64 out of 103 patients (62.1%), and 32.0% of all patients harbored a level I or II alteration that is considered clinically actionable ( ). ESCAT level I alterations included mutations in BRAF and RET fusion in ATC (10.5%), mutations in RET in MTC (80%), and all detected fusions in PTC (17.4%) (total ESCAT I alterations of cohort; 19.4%). The only level II alteration was BRAF in PTC (56.5%) (total level II; 12.6%), and the remaining targetable alterations were classified level III (n = 31, 30.1% of total cohort). Multiple potentially targetable alterations (ESCAT I–III) were observed in 5 out of 64 patients (7.8%). A matched therapeutic approach was initiated in 13 out of 64 patients (20.3%), all within clinical trial settings at the time. Reasons for not initiating matched therapy among patients with potential targetable alterations included patient preferences (n = 4), poor performance status (n = 11), drug unavailability (n = 25), or failure to meet indication criteria due to limited disease progression (n = 8). Patients who did not receive matched therapy due to drug unavailability often received a MKI (lenvatinib or sorafenib for DTC; vandetanib for MTC). The availability of matched targeted therapies and MKIs in the Netherlands for ESCAT level I and II alterations is depicted in Supplementary Fig. 1.

## Matched therapy outcomes
In some cases, matched therapies were administered beyond alterations classified as ESCAT levels I–III, based on broader clinical judgment or clinical trial eligibility. An additional three patients received matched therapies for alterations not classified as ESCAT I–III (CHEK2; ESCAT IV; PD-L1 expression), bringing the total number of treated patients to 16 (Supplementary Table 4). Of the 16 treated patients who received matched therapy, five were treated in the first-line setting (all ATC), three in the second-line setting, and eight in the third-line setting. An ORR of 56.3% and a DCR of 68.8% were observed across the entire cohort. The median progression-free survival (PFS) was 3.0 months (IQR: 2.0–13.5 months), the median overall survival (OS) from treatment initiation was 17.0 months (IQR: 11.3–24.3 months), and the median treatment duration was 13 months (IQR: 4.3–18.5 months). At the end of data collection, 9 out of 16 patients (56.3%) were alive.
An overview of treatment outcomes for patients receiving matched therapy is presented in . Ten patients were treated for at least 12 months. PRs were observed across all treatment categories, except among patients with CHEK2 or NRAS alterations. At the time of data cutoff, six patients were still receiving matched therapy with ongoing durable responses, including three patients with MTC treated with RET inhibitors. Eight patients discontinued therapy due to disease progression, and two because of toxicity. Patients with ESCAT level I or II alterations (BRAF, RET, and NTRK, n = 10) demonstrated more durable responses than patients with lower level alterations (MSI-H, NRAS, and CHEK2, n = 4), indicated by DCRs of 90.0 and 50.0%, respectively. Among the three patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), the patient with MSI-H status experienced the most durable response, which was ongoing at data cutoff.
 The median OS was longer in patients receiving matched therapy compared with those who did not (123.0 months (95% CI: 111.3–134.7) vs 57.0 months (95% CI: 41.0–73.0); ). The log-rank test showed a trend toward improved survival with matched therapy, although not statistically significant (P = 0.058). Cox regression analysis estimated a HR of 0.47 (95% CI: 0.21–1.05, P = 0.066). These survival analyses were unadjusted for histological subtype, disease stage, or prior treatments.

## Discussion
In the present study, molecular testing performed selectively after multidisciplinary deliberation and according to the availability of drugs at the time of testing identified a potentially targetable alteration according to the ESCAT levels I–III in 62.1% of patients with TC. For 32.0% of the patients, this represented an ESCAT I or II alteration, representing clinically actionable variants with demonstrated (level I) or investigational (level II) evidence. The patients who received matched therapy had an ORR of 56.3% and a DCR of 68.8%, which underscores the relevance and clinical benefits of precision oncology in advanced TC, and its potential incorporation into standard care.
The mutational profiles in our cohort were predominantly alterations in TP53, NRAS, BRAF, PTEN, and RET, consistent with previous molecular-based studies in advanced TC ( , , , ). The 62.1% ESCAT I–III targetability rate in this study is comparable to the 66.9% reported in a recent ESCAT-based analysis of patients with advanced TC, although the latter did include patients with MTC ( ). Together, these findings support a consistently high prevalence of clinically actionable targets in advanced TC when applying standardized ESCAT criteria. In contrast, higher targetability rates of 68–79% have been reported in studies using alternative frameworks to define actionability ( , ). For example, Ma et al. applied a broader definition of targetability by including OncoKB level 4 alterations in genes such as PTEN or CDKN2A, which represent biologically relevant events, but lack clinical evidence ( ).
Notably, the current study identified that patients harboring alterations in ESCAT level I or II have improved clinical outcomes compared with those with higher ESCAT levels treated with matched therapies, demonstrated by the durable responses observed in the BRAF-, RET-, and NTRK-targeted cohorts. These findings support the clinical relevance of ESCAT and its applicability in treatment planning, especially if therapies targeting level I or II alterations are available ( ). While these observations align with ESMO guidelines for systemic therapy in advanced TC, and with those of the ESMO Precision Medicine Working Group, real-world implementation remains challenging because of limited drug availability and uncertainty regarding the optimal timing for initiating matched therapy ( , ). In our study, all matched treatments were administered within clinical trial frameworks, such as the Dutch Drug Access Protocol (DAP) and Drug Rediscovery Protocol (DRUP), which highlight the need for broader access to targeted agents ( , ).
To further guide the use of precision medicine in routine care for patients with TC, timing of the NGS and sampling are a matter of debate. In our series, molecular testing was performed in patients with DTC, including those refractory to radioiodine, when the first-line systemic therapy had failed, the second-line therapy was unsuccessful, or unacceptable toxicity occurred. As selective kinase inhibitors became available, molecular testing is now often performed when systemic treatment is first considered. The latter is also the case for patients with sporadic MTC. Conversely, in patients with ATC, molecular testing is done almost always at diagnosis, since the rapid access to a potential targeted treatment is crucial for survival ( ). Therefore, in ATC, rapid polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based assays or IHC may be initially used to rapidly assess the BRAF V600E mutation. Simultaneously, NGS should be initiated to validate the rapid testing results and to identify any other targetable alterations. In metastatic DTC, it is not clear which patients benefit from early molecular testing, as the disease can remain stable or show only minimal progression for several years. On the other hand, testing later while there is progression on a MKI might give additional insights into resistance mechanisms and potential new therapeutic targets, particularly when a new biopsy from a metastatic site is available. A later biopsy may therefore capture newly emerged subclonal mutations and provide a more accurate reflection of current tumor heterogeneity. Since new biopsies cannot always be sampled, testing when systemic therapy is being considered with the best available tissue would be the most rational choice.
Treatment decisions after molecular testing are not standardized. Current ESMO guidelines mention that individualized therapy should be considered for advanced DTCs, but offer no further details on optimal treatment sequence ( ). This gap is likely attributable to the lack of clear clinical data comparing matched therapy in first-line versus later-line settings and to the inconsistent availability of NGS panels and matched therapies across different geographical regions. On the one hand, precision drugs are associated with less toxicity and often higher response rates. To illustrate, RET-driven MTCs have demonstrated improved survival when treated with the RET inhibitor selpercatinib compared with MKIs vandetanib or cabozantinib ( ). However, the occurrence of RET-inhibitor-resistant clones has been described in patients with MTC treated primarily with selpercatinib, resulting in progressive disease ( ). While our study was not designed to establish causality, the longer overall survival in patients receiving matched therapy suggests that precision-based approaches may provide clinical benefit in real-life applications. The exact clinical context to maximize this effect still needs to be clarified in further prospective studies.
Future research should also explore improving response rates through more intensive personalization and strategies for combination therapy. Our study identified co-occurring mutations of PIK3CA alongside BRAF. These patients may be suitable for rational combination therapies, targeting the altered proteins and potential resistance mechanisms simultaneously. This approach was adopted by the I-PREDICT trial, which administered matched combination therapies guided by NGS, using up to three agents simultaneously, and demonstrated both efficacy and safety ( ). Moreover, ICIs have been used in ATC, with ORRs varying between 16 and 19%, which warrants personalization strategies ( , , , ). Biomarkers for sensitivity are PD-L1 expression (via IHC) or MSI status (via NGS), with MSI-H showing higher predictive value than PD-L1 ( , , ). In our series, one ATC case with MSI-H showed a durable response, whereas two PD-L1-positive ATC cases did not respond, suggesting that MSI-H may be a stronger predictive marker in this context, although data remain limited. Larger cohorts are needed to conclusively compare the predictive values of MSI status and PD-L1 expression in ATC patients and patients with other forms of advanced TC.
This study has some limitations. First, variation in molecular testing panels over the 10-year period, including differences in panel size, may have influenced detection rates, particularly for rare alterations. However, common molecular alterations in proteins such as BRAF, RET, and P53 were included in most panels. Second, the number of patients who received matched therapy was relatively small, which reduced the statistical power to detect significant differences in survival outcomes. As this was a real-world study, survival was considered a secondary outcome. Finally, survival analyses were not adjusted for potential confounding variables, such as histological subtype, performance status, disease stage, or prior treatments, due to the small sample size, leaving the possibility of residual confounding.
In conclusion, this study demonstrates that molecular testing can identify actionable alterations in many patients with advanced TC. Matched therapies were associated with clinical benefit and prolonged survival. These findings support the integration of precision oncology into routine clinical practice and highlight the need for standardized timing and broader access to matched therapies.
