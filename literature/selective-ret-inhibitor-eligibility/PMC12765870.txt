Radioactive-iodine refractory (RAI-R) differentiated thyroid cancers (DTCs) represent a critical subset of thyroid cancers (TCs) due to their poor prognosis, even when managed with conventional treatment strategies. The 2025 American Thyroid Association (ATA) guidelines suggested strong and supplemental criteria for RAI-R DTCs including: (1) absence of 131I uptake on a post-radioactive-iodine (RAI) scan in the setting of known diseases on structural or 18F-fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose positron emission tomography (18FDG-PET) imaging; (2) disease progression less than 6 months after administrating therapeutic RAI; (3) no uptake present on a diagnostic RAI scan in the presence of otherwise detectable disease; (4) uptake is present in some, but not all, tumor foci on post-RAI scan [ ]. The outcomes of an RAI scan are influenced by imaging preparation procedures and protocols. Moreover, therapeutic response to RAI is often heterogeneous, reflecting a wide spectrum of responsiveness among patients. Importantly, RAI avidity in DTCs can be modulated by pharmacological interventions. Therefore, the definition of RAI-R DTCs remains dynamic and is expected to evolve further, driven by advances in RAI imaging techniques and the development of neoadjuvant therapies that can resensitize DTCs to RAI [ ]. RAI-R DTC is caused by the loss of key biological characteristics in its originating thyroid follicular cells, notably the iodide-metabolizing machinery [ ]. The use of targeted pharmacologic agents prior to therapeutic RAI administration is a fascinating strategy for elevating the differentiation status of DTC, thereby restoring RAI avidity and improving tumor response to RAI [ ].
Over the past decades, substantial progress has been made in elucidating the molecular pathogenesis of RAI-R DTC, highlighting the role of genetic and epigenetic alterations and dysregulated signaling pathways [ , ]. Among these, genetic alterations in the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)-AKT signaling pathways, caused by acquired point mutations such as BRAF or RAS, or by gene fusions involving RET or neurotrophic tyrosine receptor kinases (NTRKs), are major drivers of pathogenesis and RAI refractoriness through diminished expression or mislocalization of the sodium/iodide symporter [ , ]. The Cancer Genome Atlas Research Network reported that BRAF-like TCs exhibit a lower differentiation score and reduced expression of iodidemetabolizing genes, accompanied by higher activation of the signaling pathway, whereas RAS-like TCs retain a comparatively preserved differentiation machinery [ ]. Meanwhile, the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway is activated by the inactivation of the tumor suppressor gene phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) or by activating mutations in phosphatidylinositol- 4,5-bisphosphate 3-kinase catalytic subunit alpha (PI3KCA) and AKT1 [ , ]. Clinical trials for redifferentiation therapy in RAI-R DTC have primarily focused on inhibition of the MAPK signaling pathway, targeting BRAF and mitogen-activated extracellular kinase (MEK), as well as targeting upstream oncogenic drivers such as RET and NTRK fusions. In a clinical trial conducted by Ho et al. [ ], they reported that selumetinib increased 124I uptake in RAI-R DTC, with greater efficacy observed in tumors with NRAS mutation than in those with BRAF mutation, yielding partial responses (PRs) to the RAI therapy. In addition, Taprogge et al. [ ] and Wadsley et al. [ ] also confirmed that selumetinib resensitized RAI uptake in more than 30% of patients with RAI-R DTC. However, the results of the phase III clinical trial were disappointing, as selumetinib failed to demonstrate its effect on the restoration of RAI uptake [ ]. These heterogeneous results indicate that although selumetinib exhibits potential in restoring RAI avidity in some patients with RAI-R DTC, the degree of RAI uptake restoration appears to be variable. Trametinib, another MEK inhibitor, demonstrated restoration of RAI uptake in 22 of 25 patients with RAI-R DTC, especially those with RAS mutation, with nine PRs and six cases of stable disease at 6 months after RAI administration (NCT02152995) [ ]. However, Leboulleux et al. [ ] reported that a 6-week course of trametinib monotherapy was not highly effective RAI uptake in patients with RAS-mutated RAI-R DTC, neither a consequent RAI therapy revealed a low response rate. Therefore, further studies are needed to clarify the efficacy of MEK inhibitors in redifferentiation therapy for RAI-R DTC. Dabrafenib [ ] and vemurafenib [ ], specific BRAF inhibitors, was effective in restoration of RAI uptake by approximately 60%, with vemurafenib achieving tumor regression in three of four patients with RAI-R DTC. Moreover, because BRAF inhibition triggered negative feedback leading to the increased expression level of erb-b2 receptor tyrosine kinase 3 (ERBB3), vemurafenib combined with CDX-3379, which is an anti-ErbB3 monoclonal antibody, was used for enhancing RAI uptake in a clinical trial [ ]. The combination therapy of dabrafenib and trametinib induced a redifferentiation effect in patients with BRAF-mutated RAI-R DTC, restoring RAI uptake [ ]. Larotrectinib, an NTRK inhibitor, has attracted considerable attention with its redifferentiation effect in RAI-R DTC. Groussin et al. [ , ] reported successful cases with administration of larotrectinib in patients with RAI-R DTC characterized by echinoderm microtubule-associated protein-like 4 (EML4)-NTRK3 or ETV6-NTRK3 gene fusion. Lee et al. [ ] demonstrated that larotrectinib increased RAI uptake in a pediatric TC harboring a translocated promoter region (TPR)-NTRK1 fusion. However, similar to BRAF mutation, the co-occurrence of NTRK gene fusion and telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) promoter mutation may contribute to RAI resistance by lowering thyroid differentiation score [ ]. Encouraging tumor regression after RAI therapy following re-sensitization of RAI avidity has been reported in patients with RET fusion-positive RAI-R DTC treated with a RET inhibitor, selpercatinib [ , , ]. In addition, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have been explored in clinical trials for their ability to enhance RAI avidity in RAI-R DTC. Apatinib, a vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2) inhibitor, has shown promising potential, indicating that its synergistic effect with RAI could lead to a reduction in tumor size and metabolic activity [ ]. Pazopanib, a multikinase inhibitor, has also been evaluated in clinical trials; however, no significant increase in RAI uptake was observed after its administration [ ]. A summary of key clinical evidence for redifferentiation therapies in RAI-R DTC is provided in , and an overview of genetic alterations related to RAI refractoriness in DTCs along with the TKIs applied to redifferentiate RAI-R DTCs is presented in .
 Current clinical research in RAI-R DTC is advancing along two major precision trajectories. The first focuses on redifferentiation therapy, which aims to restore RAI avidity and re-enable effective RAI therapy utilizing MAPK-pathway inhibition (e.g., BRAF±MEK or MEK alone) [ , , - , ]; Ongoing trials are exploring this approach (e.g., NCT05182931, NCT07010393). The second direction emphasizes driver mutation-targeted therapies employing selective inhibitors—such as RET and NTRK inhibitors—in patients whose tumors harbor corresponding fusions (e.g., NCT03157128, NCT03037385, NCT02576431, NCT07010393) [ , - , ]. A crucial adjunct to both strategies is the integration of advanced nuclear imaging (e.g., I-124 positron emission tomography/computed tomography [124I-PET/CT], I-123 single photon emission computed tomography/computed tomography [123I-SPECT/CT]) and individualized dosimetry (e.g., NCT04619316, NCT06443866, NCT07092514), enabling quantitative assessment of whether redifferentiation therapy successfully restores therapeutically meaningful RAI uptake.
Although promising, clinical evidence supporting the use of redifferentiation strategies in patients with RAI-R DTCs remains limited, as most studies have enrolled small patient cohorts. Another challenge is the difficulty in discerning whether the observed therapeutic efficacy stems from the pharmacologic redifferentiation achieved prior to RAI therapy or from the direct effect of the re-differentiating drugs (e.g., TKI), an issue requiring resolution. To date, no randomized trials have demonstrated significant improvements in overall survival or progression-free survival, and only one trial has documented a case of complete remission (CR) [ ]. Moreover, differentiation rates vary markedly across completed studies, even among those evaluating the same therapeutic agents. Future research should focus on selecting patients with DTC most likely to benefit from the adjuvant redifferentiation therapy combined with RAI therapy. This will require careful screening for optimal candidates based on oncogenic molecular alterations and other pre-treatment factors such as pathology, tumor burden, [18F]-FDG avidity, and biochemical results (e.g., thyroglobulin), etc. Equally important are the optimization of drug dose and duration of re-differentiating drugs, as well as the management of their adverse effects.
As tumor genomic profiling is increasingly adopted in clinical practice, it will be helpful to gain insights on whether a genotype–phenotype correlation predicts RAI-R DTC. As tumor genomic analysis becomes increasingly integrated into clinical practice, it is crucial to determine whether specific genotype–phenotype associations influence the effectiveness of redifferentiation therapy in RAI-R DTC. The Adjuvant Selumetinib for differentiated Thyroid cancer, Remission after RAI (ASTRA) phase III trial (NCT01843062) evaluated whether adjuvant use of selumetinib with RAI could improve CR rates in high-risk no-gene selected patients with DTC [ ]. Although selumetinib can enhance RAI avidity by inhibiting the MAPK pathway, the study demonstrated no significant CR benefit compared with placebo (40% vs. 38%). These findings underscore the importance of tumor genomic context—particularly the presence of BRAF or RAS mutations, or NTRK and RET fusions—indicating that a one-size-fits-all redifferentiation strategy is unlikely to be effective. For high-risk patients, particularly those with RAI-R DTC who have not undergone molecular profiling, future clinical management should prioritize comprehensive genomic characterization to enable genotype-specific redifferentiation therapy selection rather than empiric use of kinase inhibitors as redifferentiation agents.
Redifferentiation therapy using TKIs followed by RAI therapy has several advantages over conventional TKI therapy alone: (1) intermittent short-term TKI therapy over continuous therapy [ ]; (2) lesser adverse effects and lower costs by short-term TKI use as demonstrated in ongoing clinical trials (NCT07092514, NCT06482086); (3) better therapeutic effect of RAI in tumors shrunken by adjuvant TKI use; (4) can be applied before tumor progression; (5) can be applied in DTCs with RAI uptake as well to enhance degree of RAI avidity, as investigated in ongoing studies (NCT05668962, NCT01947023).
The 2025 ATA guidelines recommend considering redifferentiation therapy for selected patients with RAI-R DTC harboring targetable mutations. Consequently, more clinical data demonstrating the effectiveness of pharmacologic redifferentiation is expected to be accumulated soon. This progress is anticipated to accelerate the adoption of redifferentiation therapy as a standard treatment strategy for RAI-R DTC, ushering in a new era of precision medicine driven by collaborative efforts between clinicians and researchers.
