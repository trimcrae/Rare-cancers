## Introduction
Only type I MET tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) are approved for treating MET-altered NSCLCs. Preclinically, type II TKIs, such as cabozantinib, can rescue progression on type I TKIs. This phase 2 trial (NCT01639508) evaluated the activity of cabozantinib in patients with MET-dependent lung cancers, including TKI-pretreated cancers.

## Methods
This phase 2 trial with a Simon two-stage minimax design treated patients with metastatic, MET-altered lung cancers with cabozantinib (60 mg daily) until progression or intolerable toxicity. The primary end point was objective response rate (ORR). We prespecified that cabozantinib would be considered a useful agent if at least a 20% ORR was observed. Secondary end points included progression-free survival, overall survival, and safety.

## Results
We enrolled 28 patients, 23 patients (82%) with only a MET exon 14 alteration, two patients (7%) with MET amplification, and three patients (11%) with concurrent MET exon 14 alteration and amplification. There were 24 patients (86%) previously treated with a type I MET TKI. The ORR was 20% (5/25 assessable patients; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 8.9%–39.1%), with five partial responses (duration ranged from 4 to 39 mo). Four of five responders were type I MET TKI pretreated. The median progression-free survival and overall survival were 4.5 (95% CI: 3.3–5.7) months and 7.2 (95% CI: 2.9–11.5) months, respectively. Dose modification and discontinuation occurred in 64% (18/28) and 7% (2/28) of patients, respectively.

## Conclusion
This trial met its primary end point. Importantly, we demonstrated that cabozantinib, a type II MET TKI, could benefit patients with MET-altered lung cancers previously treated with type I MET TKIs.

## Introduction
The MET pathway is deregulated in many lung cancers. MET mutation or amplification may sensitize select tumors to tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) therapy. Crizotinib is a multikinase MET inhibitor that first established the activity of MET inhibition in MET-driven cancers. Subsequently, the type I MET-selective TKIs capmatinib, tepotinib, and savolitinib demonstrated robust efficacy in treatment-naive MET-exon 14 altered and MET-amplified NSCLCs and were approved or guidelines-listed for the treatment of MET-dependent lung cancers. , However, resistance ultimately develops, underscoring the need for alternative treatment strategies.
Switching to a MET inhibitor with a distinct mechanism of action may help overcome acquired resistance. Type I TKIs bind MET in the active conformation. In contrast, type II TKIs bind MET in the inactive conformation and have been found to rescue disease progression on type I TKIs in preclinical models. , , , , Case reports or series of clinical responses to type II TKI therapy after type I TKI progression have likewise been published. , Cabozantinib is a type II multikinase inhibitor with anti-MET activity that may abrogate type I MET TKI resistance. To date, no prospective clinical studies that assess its efficacy in this population have been published.
To address this gap, we conducted a single-arm, phase 2 trial to evaluate the efficacy and safety of cabozantinib in patients with advanced NSCLCs harboring MET alterations, including TKI-pretreated cancers. This phase 2 trial represents the first prospective evaluation of the activity of a type II MET TKI on a clinical trial after progression on a type I MET TKI, elucidating the potential utility of sequential TKI therapy that leverages TKI type switching.

## Methods

## Study Design and Patients
This was a single-institution, single-arm, Simon two-stage phase 2 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov number NCT01639508). Patients enrolled had metastatic or unresectable NSCLC harboring a MET alteration, including MET overexpression, amplification, or mutation (fusions were permitted if these involved MET exon 14 skipping). Notably, although this cohort technically allowed the accrual of cancers with NTRK or AXL alterations, only MET-altered cancers were ultimately enrolled. Additional eligibility criteria included a Karnofsky performance status of greater than 70%, adequate organ function, and measurable disease as defined by the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version (v.) 1.1. Patients with nonmeasurable disease were allowed with sponsor approval.
Patients with treated or asymptomatic brain metastases were eligible, and there were no restrictions on the number of previous lines of therapy, including treatment with other MET TKIs. For additional eligibility criteria, see the . The study was conducted in accordance with the Good Clinical Practice guideline and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided written informed consent before participation. The study was funded in part by Exelixis.

## Procedures and Assessments
MET exon 14 alterations and amplifications were assessed using a next-generation sequencing assay on either tumor tissue or circulating tumor DNA. For tissue-based testing, DNA-based assays (FoundationOne CDx or MSK IMPACT) were used. Circulating tumor DNA was assayed using MSK ACCESS, Guardant 360, or Resolution Bio ctDx. In addition, RNA-based detection was permitted through anchored multiplex polymerase chain reaction using the MSK Solid Fusion Panel. Samples sequenced by MSK-IMPACT had MET copy number gains further evaluated using Fraction and Allele-Specific Copy Number Estimates derived from Tumor Sequencing (FACETS). MET immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed with the SP44 clone (Ventana), and the H-score method was used to evaluate MET expression. 
Patients received cabozantinib tablets at a dose of 60 mg orally daily in continuous 28-day cycles until disease progression according to RECIST v.1.1 or unacceptable toxicity. Post-progression treatment was allowed if patients had ongoing clinical benefit at the investigator's discretion. Dose interruptions and reductions (to 40 mg daily and then to 20 mg daily) were permitted to manage adverse events. Tumors were assessed by computed tomography (CT) at baseline, 4 weeks after treatment initiation, and every 8 weeks thereafter. Central nervous system imaging was not required. Adverse events were assessed using Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 4.0, and all patients who received at least one dose of cabozantinib were included in toxicity evaluations.

## Study End Points
Objective response rate (ORR; complete or partial response) was the primary end point, as assessed according to RECIST v.1.1. Progression-free survival (PFS; time from first dose of cabozantinib to radiologic progression or death, whichever occurred first), overall survival (OS; time from initiation of treatment to death for any cause), and safety were secondary end points. Patients were considered assessable for the primary end point if they received at least one dose of cabozantinib and had a baseline CT scan followed by at least one subsequent scan at a protocol-defined time point. Three patients who did not meet these criteria were replaced as per protocol.

## Statistical Analysis
The study was based on a Simon two-stage minimax design. Assuming a response rate of 10% (null hypothesis, H0) and a desired response rate of 30% (alternative hypothesis, H1), with a type I error of 10% and power of 90%, 16 patients were necessary in the first stage of this design. If at least two responses were observed, an additional nine patients would be enrolled in the second stage. At the end of the study, the primary end point would be met if five or more patients demonstrated an objective response of 25 assessable patients treated. Kaplan-Meier plots were used to determine PFS and OS.

## Results
Between September 2014 and January 2024, 28 patients were enrolled. The data cutoff date was June 6, 2024. Of enrolled patients, 25 were eligible for assessment of the primary end point ( ); three patients were ineligible due to the inability to complete required follow-up imaging and were replaced as prespecified. Specifically, two patients withdrew consent before the first radiologic assessment and one patient discontinued treatment early due to an adverse event (fatigue). All 28 patients were included in the toxicity analysis.

## Patients
Patient characteristics are found in . The median age was 68 (range 38–85) years. Furthermore, 23 patients (82%) had a MET exon 14 alteration only (exon 14 skipping mutation, n = 21; MET fusion resulting in exon 14 skipping, n = 2). Two patients (7%) had MET amplification only (including one patient with a concurrent ROS1 fusion), and three patients (11%) had a concurrent MET exon 14 alteration and MET amplification. There were 22 patients (79%) who had adenocarcinoma, and two patients (7%) each had pleomorphic carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and adenosquamous carcinoma. In addition, 24 patients (86%) had received a previous MET TKI (crizotinib, n = 15; capmatinib, n = 7; tepotinib, n = 2). Also, 20 (71%) and 15 (54%) patients had received one or more previous lines of chemotherapy and immunotherapy, respectively. Nine patients (32%) had brain metastases at baseline. 

## Activity
Five patients (20%) had a confirmed partial response, 15 patients (60%) had stable disease, and three patients (12%) experienced disease progression as their best overall response. No complete responses were observed. Two patients had no measurable disease (bone metastases and pleural effusion) and were assessed based on non-target lesion evaluation according to RECIST v.1.1. The ORR was 20% (95% CI: 8.9%–39.1%) in the 25 assessable patients, meeting the primary study end point. Most patients demonstrated a reduction in target lesion size ( A and B). All responses were identified by 3 months (response duration range 4–39 mo). All patients with a partial response had cancers harboring MET exon 14 skipping mutation (one had concurrent MET amplification) and maintained a duration of treatment exceeding 6 months ( ). Tissue samples for MET IHC were available for 20 patients. All samples exhibited MET expression, with more than 50% of cells staining positive ( A and ). H-scores ranged from 110 to 300 among patients who achieved a partial response, and no correlation between MET IHC H-score and treatment response was observed. 
No responses were observed among patients with MET amplification alone or MET fusion. One patient with MET amplification also harbored a concomitant ROS1 fusion and experienced disease progression as their best overall response. MET amplification analysis using FACETS was performed on 20 patients. FACETS defined MET amplification as a copy number greater than 6, MET gain as a copy number between 3 and 5, and “MET negative” (cases without amplification or gain) as a copy number less than 2. Among patients with samples that underwent FACETS analysis, three had MET amplification, eight had MET gain, and nine were “MET negative” ( A and ). Among patients with a partial response, one had MET amplification with a FACETS-level MET copy number of 10, two had MET gain with a copy number of 3, and two were “MET negative.” Regarding previous treatments, all patients with a partial response had been previously treated, with a median of two previous lines of therapy (range 1–3). Four of the five patients with an objective response had received MET TKI therapy previously: two with crizotinib, one with tepotinib, and one with capmatinib. Regarding intracranial activity, among nine patients with baseline brain metastases, seven had undergone previous brain radiotherapy, and the remaining two patients progressed rapidly and did not undergo central nervous system imaging, preventing a reliable assessment of cabozantinib’s intracranial efficacy in this cohort.
At the time of analysis, all patients had discontinued cabozantinib, and disease progression or death had occurred in 26 (93%) of the 28 patients ( A and B). The median duration of treatment was 4.6 (95% CI: 1.2–8.0) months. Notably, one patient with a partial response had a treatment duration of 39 months, one patient with stable disease had a treatment duration of 17 months, and the two patients with nonmeasurable disease had treatment durations exceeding 20 months. The median PFS was 4.5 (95% CI: 3.3–5.7) months, and the median OS was 7.2 (95% CI: 2.9–11.5) months ( A and B). 

## Side Effects
A total of 28 patients were evaluated for toxicity. All-causality adverse events found in more than or equal to 20% of patients are listed in . Two grade 5 events occurred, one due to a fall and one related to coronavirus disease 2019, neither were considered related to the treatment. No treatment-related deaths were observed. Three patients (7%) discontinued treatment due to TRAEs (palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia, n = 2; fatigue, n = 1), and 18 patients (64%) required dose reductions ( ). Treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) were mostly of grade 1 or grade 2 ( ). The most frequent TRAEs were fatigue (39%), diarrhea (39%), palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia (36%), anorexia (36%), and hypophosphatemia (32%). The most common grade 3 or higher TRAEs were hypophosphatemia (14%), hypertension (11%), and lipase increased (11%). 

## Discussion
This study met its primary end point and is the first phase 2 trial to report the activity of a type II MET TKI in patients with MET-dependent lung cancers including those who were TKI pretreated. Phase 2 data in MET-dependent lung cancers have not been reported for the type II TKIs merestinib, and foretinib, and the phase 2 data for the type II inhibitor glesatinib in MET-dependent lung cancers did not include TKI-pretreated tumors. The ORR for cabozantinib was 20% and the rate of primary progression was low (12%) in this heavily pretreated population (>70% of patients had two or more previous lines of systemic therapy, including chemotherapy and immunotherapy). An exceptional responder received cabozantinib for more than 3 years.
Although the 20% response rate was lower than that of other targeted therapies in NSCLC, response rates with other MET TKIs are generally lower compared with type Ia and Ib inhibitors, such as crizotinib, capmatinib, tepotinib, savolitinib, vebreltinib, and gumarontinib. Similar efficacy was reported with amivantamab, a bispecific antibody targeting EGFR and MET, which demonstrated an ORR of 19% and a median PFS of 5.4 months in patients pretreated with MET inhibition in the CHRYSALIS study. Furthermore, cabozantinib had a similarly modest ORR of 28% in RET-altered lung cancer, where a median duration of response of 4.7 months, PFS of 5.5 months, and OS of 9.9 months was reported. The activity of cabozantinib in RET-altered lung cancer was eventually surpassed by selective RET inhibitors (selpercatinib and pralsetinib) that achieved higher ORRs and longer durability. This implies that rational drug design may eventually generate a type II inhibitor with superior features that translate to increased clinical benefit.
The need to generate a next-generation type II MET inhibitor is even more important when we consider that this prospective phase 2 trial provided the utility of sequential TKI therapy in MET-dependent cancers. Unlike in EGFR-mutant, ALK fusion-positive, ROS1 fusion-positive, and NTRK fusion-positive lung cancers for which all approved TKIs that have been used in a serial fashion (e.g., alectinib followed by lorlatinib) are type I inhibitors, the ability of cabozantinib to rescue disease progression after previous treatment with capmatinib, tepotinib, and crizotinib highlights the utility of starting with a type I TKI and later switching to a type II TKI on progression in selected cases. Cabozantinib offers a distinct mechanism of action by binding to the inactive conformation of MET, overcoming resistance to type I inhibitors. Other series have revealed that on-target type I resistance mutations (MET D1228N) may be abrogated by type II inhibition, although these mutations were not observed in TKI-pretreated cancers in this series.
The side effects observed were consistent with previous reports for cabozantinib in other cancers. Many of the toxicities can be attributed to off-target effects related to the ability of cabozantinib to inhibit other kinases such as RET, ROS1, and VEGFR2. This multikinase inhibition likely contributes not only to the side effects of cabozantinib but potentially to its activity as well, considering that the inhibition of other kinases (e.g., VEGFR2 and thus angiogenesis) has been found to be independently active in lung cancer.
This trial included a variety of MET alterations, such as exon 14 skipping mutations, amplifications, and fusions. Owing to the diversity of these alterations, we incorporated additional methodologies, including FACETS analysis and MET IHC, to better evaluate the tumors and their potential response to treatment. Although MET IHC may be relatively insensitive at detecting MET exon 14 alterations or MET amplifications, its effectiveness in predicting response to TKIs remains uncertain. Regarding amplification, there are no widely accepted clinical thresholds for defining an amplification threshold through next-generation sequencing, and standardized guidelines for the assessment and reporting of MET amplification are still lacking. To address this, we used FACETS analysis, which enabled a more refined calculation of allele-specific copy numbers, adjusted for tumor purity. Nonetheless, neither method appeared to be a strong predictive biomarker for cabozantinib response, recognizing the small number of patients treated and the exploratory nature of these analyses.
This study was conducted at a single center, limiting its generalizability, and a heterogeneous population of diverse MET alterations was included. The trial was initially designed in 2011 during a period when no MET inhibitors were approved for patients with NSCLC and MET alterations, and type Ib inhibitors (e.g., capmatinib and tepotinib) had not yet been introduced into clinical studies. In addition, baseline brain scans were not required for enrollment, although magnetic resonance imaging of the brain with contrast was recommended for patients with untreated asymptomatic brain metastases. Notably, 32% of patients (nine patients) had brain metastases detected at diagnosis. The absence of a dedicated evaluation of cabozantinib’s central nervous system activity represents an additional limitation, and the potential presence of undetected brain metastases in patients without baseline imaging may have influenced clinical outcomes. Nevertheless, case reports have described intracranial responses to cabozantinib, suggesting potential central nervous system activity that warrants further exploration. 
In summary, cabozantinib was active in MET-altered lung cancers. Although the trial met its primary end point, the ORR of 20% underscores the need to develop new type II MET inhibitors. Due to its alternative type II binding mode and its wide availability, cabozantinib can be useful particularly in the treatment of type I TKI resistance.
