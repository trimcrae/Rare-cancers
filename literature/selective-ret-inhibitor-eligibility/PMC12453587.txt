## SUMMARY
RET is a receptor tyrosine kinase that plays important roles in development, cancers, and Parkinson’s disease. Here, we identify immunoglobulin superfamily member 10 (IGSF10) as a RET antagonist. We show that Ewing sarcoma depends on IGSF10 and that IGSF10 prevents RET-mediated activation of cdc42, a Rho family G protein and a key regulator of Ewing sarcoma growth as well as cell migration. We demonstrate that IGSF10 binds RET and GAS1, a cell surface RET inhibitor, and assembles an inhibitory RET-GAS1 complex, preventing a stimulatory RET-GFRA complex. IGSF10 mutations are associated with delayed puberty, and IGSF10 is shown to be necessary for the proper migration of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) neurons. We show that the IGSF10-RET-GAS1-cdc42 pathway regulates migration of GnRH neurons and that IGSF10 mutants linked to delayed puberty are defective in RET-cdc42 regulation. These results reveal a critical role of IGSF10 as a RET antagonist in Ewing sarcoma and GnRH neurons.

## INTRODUCTION
RET is a receptor tyrosine kinase involved in many diverse processes, including the development of the central and peripheral nervous systems and the kidney, neural crest cell migration, and spermatogenesis. – The activation of RET by point mutations or gene rearrangements is implicated in a variety of cancers. – There is also evidence that RET plays a tumor suppressor role in some cancer types. , RET also plays an important role in dopamine neurons and is considered a therapeutic target for Parkinson’s disease. The GDNF family ligands (GDNF, neurturin, artemin, and persephin) trigger hetero-tetramerization of RET and a stimulatory GFRA family coreceptor, leading to RET autophosphorylation and downstream signaling. In addition, growth and differentiation factor 15 (GDF15) activates RET through hetero-tetramerization of RET and GDNF family receptor α-like (GFRAL). GAS1 is a GPI-anchored cell surface protein structurally similar to the stimulatory RET coreceptors, GFRA1–4 and GFRAL, but functions as an inhibitor of RET tyrosine kinase signaling. – The N-terminal cysteine-rich domain of GAS1 directly binds RET and inhibits downstream signaling. The biological role of GAS1 as a RET inhibitor is not well-characterized.
Immunoglobulin superfamily member 10 (IGSF10) is a large, secreted protein whose functions are poorly understood. In 2016, IGSF10 mutations were associated with hereditary delayed puberty, and IGSF10 was shown to stimulate the migration of gonadotropin-releasing hormone-producing neurons (GnRH neurons), although the mechanism of IGSF10 action had not been clarified. The IGSF10A1672T variant was also found in combined pituitary hormone deficiency. Low IGSF10 expression is linked to more aggressive lung and breast cancers. – A frameshift mutation in IGSF10 was found in a case of a hereditary skeletal disorder, cleidocranial dysplasia, and IGSF10 knockdown was shown to inhibit alkaline phosphatase activity and the formation of mineralized nodules by MC3T3-E1 pre-osteoblast. IGSF10 was also found to be induced in rat primary calvaria cells by mechanical stress. However, how IGSF10 exerts these activities and plays roles in diseases is unknown.
GnRH neurons secrete GnRH, which acts on the anterior pituitary gland to release luteinizing hormone and follicle-stimulating hormone, playing a central role in the initiation of puberty and the regulation of reproductive functions. Interestingly, GnRH neurons arise outside the central nervous system, from the nasal placode. During embryonic development, GnRH neurons migrate from the nasal placode into the forebrain. , Improper migration of GnRH neurons results in hypogonadotropic hypogonadism and delayed puberty. , Multiple proteins/genes have been identified to regulate GnRH neuron migration and, when mutated, are linked to hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, including IGSF10. , , 
Ewing sarcoma is a cancer of bone and soft tissue in children and young adults, characterized by a chromosomal translocation that fuses EWS and an ETS family transcription factor, most commonly FLI1. – EWS-FLI1 is the primary driver of this cancer. – Because current cytotoxic chemotherapies are not improving the survival of those with metastatic or recurrent Ewing sarcoma cases, there is a need for novel and more effective targeted therapies. Since Ewing sarcoma is driven by EWS-FLI1, targeting EWS-FLI1 is a rational approach to treat this cancer. Despite many attempts, EWS-FLI1-targeted therapy has not been developed to date. A promising alternative is to identify and target the molecular vulnerabilities created by EWS-FLI1. Previous research on Ewing sarcoma predominantly focused on how EWS-FLI1 regulates intracellular events such as gene transcription and mRNA splicing; little is known about how EWS-FLI1 impacts the extracellular environment.
In this study, we show that IGSF10 is a RET antagonist, facilitating the assembly of an inhibitory RET-GAS1 complex while preventing the formation of a stimulatory RET-GFRA complex. We demonstrate that RET inactivation by IGSF10 plays important roles in Ewing sarcoma growth and GnRH neuron migration.

## RESULTS

## Ewing sarcoma depends on IGSF10, a target of EWS-FLI1
To assess how EWS-FLI1 impacts the Ewing sarcoma secretome, we compared the proteins secreted from EWS-FLI1-silenced A673 Ewing sarcoma cells and those from control A673 cells. One of the high-confidence proteins that displayed a significant alteration in abundance upon EWS-FLI1 silencing was IGSF10, which exhibited a 57-fold decrease after silencing EWS-FLI1 ( and ; ). We found that IGSF10 transcript levels are also reduced upon EWS-FLI1 silencing ( ). Conversely, lentiviral expression of EWS-FLI1 in human mesenchymal stem cells, putative cells of the origin of Ewing sarcoma, – induced IGSF10 mRNA and protein levels ( ). Using chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP), we were able to show that endogenous EWS-FLI1 in A673 cells binds to the IGSF10 gene promoter ( ). A previous ChIP-sequencing analysis also identified IGSF10 as one of 1785 high-confidence EWS-FLI1-binding sites in A673 and SK-N-MC Ewing sarcoma cells. These results suggest that IGSF10 is a transcriptional target of EWS-FLI1. Consistent with this, IGSF10 is highly expressed in Ewing sarcoma tumors, patient-derived xenograft (PDX) tumors, and cell lines compared with human mesenchymal stem cells, rhabdomyosarcoma tumors, and synovial sarcoma tumors ( ).
siRNA-mediated IGSF10 knockdown in A673, EW8, TC32, and ES-1 Ewing sarcoma cell lines arrested cell proliferation, which was rescued by IGSF10-expressing conditioned medium (CM) ( ; Dharmacon IGSF10 SMARTpool siRNAs; proliferation assessed by the IncuCyte live-cell imaging system; IGSF10 knockdown was verified by immunoblotting at the top), suggesting that these Ewing sarcoma cells depend on extracellular IGSF10. To complement the established cell lines, we dissociated a Ewing sarcoma PDX tumor (NCH-EWS-1) and derived tumor cells, which we termed PDX1. IGSF10 knockdown arrested the proliferation of PDX1 tumor cells, which was rescued by IGSF10 CM ( ). IGSF10 knockdown by shRNAs ( ) also inhibited anchorage-dependent growth ( ), anchorage-independent growth ( ), and xenograft tumorigenicity ( ) of Ewing sarcoma. These results demonstrate that Ewing sarcoma depends on IGSF10.

## IGSF10 is a RET antagonist
We noticed that IGSF10 knockdown results in the upregulation of several genes reported to be activated by RET – in A673 cells ( ). IGSF10 knockdown stimulated autophosphorylation of RET on tyrosine 905, which was reversed by IGSF10 CM, suggesting that IGSF10 normally inhibits RET ( ). We then tested the possibility that IGSF10 is a ligand for RET. C-terminally FLAG-tagged IGSF10 was produced by transfection in 293T cells and incubated for 0 (no treatment), 1, 4, or 8 h with A673 cells transfected with control siRNA or RET siRNA. After washing to remove unbound IGSF10-FLAG, cells were lysed, and the binding of IGSF10-FLAG to cells was assessed by anti-FLAG immunoblotting. As shown in , IGSF10-FLAG is bound to control siRNA-transfected A673 cells, and this binding is abolished by RET knockdown, indicating that RET serves as the IGSF10 receptor in Ewing sarcoma. Furthermore, IGSF10-FLAG specifically co-immunoprecipitated with RET extracellular domain ( ). Importantly, we found that growth arrest of Ewing sarcoma cells induced by IGSF10 knockdown can be rescued by concurrent RET knockdown ( ) or by two distinct RET kinase inhibitors ( and ), suggesting that IGSF10 normally maintains Ewing sarcoma growth by inhibiting RET. IGSF10 C-terminal region (1646–2623), but not IGSF10 N-terminal region (1–1645), co-immunoprecipitated with RET extracellular domain ( ). IGSF10 C-terminal region (1646–2623) is composed of ten immunoglobulin-like domains (Ig3–12), and we found that Ig3–5, Ig6–8, and Ig9–12 display co-immunoprecipitation with RET extracellular domain ( ). We used isothermal titration calorimetry to determine the Kd of interaction between RET extracellular domain and Ig3–5, Ig6–8, and Ig9–12 to be 56, 128, and 250 nM, respectively ( – ).

## IGSF10 prevents cdc42 activation by RET, thereby maintaining Ewing sarcoma growth
We recently reported a critical inhibitory role for the Rho family G protein, cdc42, in Ewing sarcoma. cdc42 disassembles the BAF chromatin remodeling complex and degrades key BAF subunits, leading to suppression of EWS-FLI1 transcriptional activity, which depends on the BAF complex and growth arrest in Ewing sarcoma. Because RET activates cdc42, one of the effectors of RET, – we assessed how IGSF10 affects active cdc42 levels using GST-PAK1 pulldown assays. IGSF10 knockdown strongly activated cdc42 and Rac (increased GTP-bound cdc42 and Rac) in Ewing sarcoma cells ( ). IGSF10 knockdown also induced RET autophosphorylation on tyrosine 905, 1015, and 1062 and reduced the protein levels of the BAF complex subunits (BRG1, BAF155, and BAF47) ( ). A RET kinase inhibitor, pralsetinib, blocked cdc42 activation and BAF complex subunit degradation upon IGSF10 knockdown ( ), indicating that IGSF10 inactivates cdc42 and maintains the protein levels of BAF complex subunits by antagonizing RET. Consistent with the degradation of the key BAF complex subunits, IGSF10 knockdown also reduced the expression of EWS-FLI1 target genes ( ). Importantly, growth arrest of Ewing sarcoma cells by IGSF10 knockdown was rescued by a cdc42 inhibitor, ML141 ( ), indicating that IGSF10 normally inactivates cdc42 to maintain Ewing sarcoma growth.
A RET agonist, GDNF, induced RET autophosphorylation and activation of cdc42/Rac, Akt, and ERK in Ewing sarcoma cells; however, these responses were abolished by IGSF10 ( and ). GDNF also arrested Ewing sarcoma proliferation, which was rescued by IGSF10 ( ). These results indicate that IGSF10 is able to block GDNF-induced RET signaling. While GDNF-RET signaling activates ERK and Akt ( ), the blockade of ERK and Akt pathways by pharmacological inhibitors does not rescue growth arrest induced by IGSF10 knockdown in Ewing sarcoma cells ( and ). Similarly, the blockade of mTORC1 pathway by torin 1 did not rescue IGSF10 knockdown-induced growth arrest ( ). These results suggest that among the effectors activated by RET, cdc42, but not ERK, Akt, or mTORC1, serves as a critical downstream target of IGSF10 in regulating Ewing sarcoma growth.
Collectively, these results indicate that IGSF10 maintains EWS-FLI1 transcriptional output and Ewing sarcoma growth through inactivation of the RET-cdc42 axis (model in ).

## IGSF10 delayed puberty mutants are defective in growth signaling in Ewing sarcoma
IGSF10 mutations are associated with hereditary delayed puberty. , – Two recurrent mutations displaying complete penetrance are R156L and E161K, located in the N-terminal leucine-rich repeat domain of IGSF10. The growth arrest of Ewing sarcoma cells by IGSF10 knockdown was rescued by wild-type IGSF10; the R156L mutant was less effective, and the E161K mutant was severely defective in rescuing growth ( – ). Furthermore, unlike wild-type IGSF10, the E161K mutant was unable to suppress RET autophosphorylation and cdc42 activation induced by IGSF10 knockdown in A673 cells ( ). The protein levels of wild-type and mutant IGSF10 in the CM used for the rescue experiments were comparable ( ). These results indicate that delayed puberty-linked IGSF10 mutants display defects in growth signaling in Ewing sarcoma and suggest a common mechanistic basis for IGSF10 signaling in Ewing sarcoma and delayed puberty.

## GAS1 functions as a co-receptor for IGSF10-RET signaling
GAS1 is a GPI-anchored cell surface protein structurally similar to the stimulatory RET co-receptors, GFRA1–4 and GFRAL, but functions as an inhibitor of RET tyrosine kinase signaling. – The N-terminal cysteine-rich domain of GAS1 directly binds RET and inhibits downstream signaling. In A673 cells, IGSF10, RET, GAS1, and GFRA2 are expressed at relatively high levels ( ). We found that GAS1 knockdown results in the activation of RET tyrosine kinase ( ), activation of cdc42 ( ), degradation of the BAF complex subunits ( ), and growth inhibition ( ) in Ewing sarcoma cells. Thus, GAS1 knockdown phenocopies IGSF10 knockdown in Ewing sarcoma.
The ligand binding assays in A673 cells showed that the binding of IGSF10-FLAG to A673 cells is abolished by GAS1 knockdown ( ), like RET knockdown ( ), suggesting that GAS1 functions as a co-receptor for IGSF10. Using co-immunoprecipitation, we found that the IGSF10 N-terminal leucine-rich repeat domain binds to GAS1 ( ) and, importantly, the IGSF10 mutations associated with delayed puberty, R156L and E161K, reduced or abolished GAS1 binding ( ), suggesting the functional importance of IGSF10-GAS1 interaction.
Using immunoprecipitation of endogenous proteins in A673 cells, we determined that RET co-immunoprecipitates with GAS1 and IGSF10, which is substantially reduced by IGSF10 knockdown ( ). Upon IGSF10 knockdown, RET instead co-immunoprecipitated with GFRA2, a stimulatory RET co-receptor ( ). A similar result was obtained using PDX1 Ewing sarcoma tumor cells ( ). These results suggest that IGSF10 assembles an inhibitory RET-GAS1 complex and prevents the formation of a stimulatory RET-GFRA complex.
Ligand-binding assays revealed that IGSF10 binds to 293T cells expressing RET and GAS1, but not RET and GFRA2 ( , top) while GDNF binds to 293T cells expressing RET and GFRA2, but not RET and GAS1 ( , bottom), indicating the differential requirement of RET co-receptors, GAS1 and GFRA2, for IGSF10 and GDNF binding.
Using isothermal titration calorimetry, we determined the Kd of interaction between IGSF10 (1–281) and GAS1 to be 376 nM ( ).
GAS1 harbors two cysteine-rich domains (G1 and G2) followed by a C-terminal unstructured tail. Previous studies demonstrated that RET and palmitate-modified sonic hedgehog bind the G1 domain , while cholesterol-modified sonic hedgehog binds the G2 domain of GAS1. Using GAS1 deletion mutants lacking G1, G2, or C-terminal tail, we found that IGSF10 (1–281) is able to bind to all GAS1 deletion mutants ( ), suggesting that multiple domains in GAS1 are involved in IGSF10 binding, though the precise mode of GAS1 – IGSF10 interaction warrants further investigations.

## Role of IGSF10-RET-GAS1 signaling in GnRH neurons
GN11 mouse GnRH neuronal cell line is a widely used model for GnRH neurons. , Exogenous IGSF10 has been shown to stimulate the migration of GN11 cells, although the mechanism of IGSF10 action remains unclear. We used secretome proteomics to characterize the proteins secreted from GN11 cells and found abundant secretion of IGSF10 ( ), indicating that IGSF10 is endogenously secreted from GnRH neurons.
IGSF10 knockdown in GN11 cells resulted in the activation of RET and cdc42/Rac ( ). IGSF10 knockdown did not reduce the protein levels of BAF complex subunits in GN11 cells ( ). Using Transwell migration assays, we found that IGSF10 knockdown inhibits GN11 cell migration, which was rescued by human wild-type IGSF10 ( ). IGSF10 E161K mutant was defective in rescuing GN11 cell migration ( ). IGSF10 knockdown also inhibited GN11 cell proliferation, which was rescued by wild-type IGSF10 ( ). IGSF10 E161K mutant was severely defective in rescuing proliferation ( ).
Inhibited migration and proliferation of GN11 cells upon IGSF10 knockdown were rescued by concurrent RET knockdown, a RET kinase inhibitor, or a cdc42 inhibitor ( – ). These results suggest the critical role of the IGSF10-RET-cdc42 pathway in regulating GN11 cell migration and proliferation.
GAS1 knockdown in GN11 cells activated RET and cdc42/Rac ( ). GAS1 knockdown inhibited the migration and proliferation of GN11 cells, which was rescued by a RET kinase inhibitor or a cdc42 inhibitor ( – ). Therefore, GAS1 knockdown phenocopied IGSF10 knockdown in GN11 cells.
These results suggest that IGSF10-RET-GAS1-cdc42 signaling mediates migration and proliferation of GN11 cells.
To complement the mouse GN11 GnRH neuronal cell line, we used mouse primary GnRH neurons in nasal explants. In nasal explants, GnRH neurons migrate out of the nasal pits and continue to migrate into the periphery of the explant. Using this model system, antisense oligonucleotides against IGSF10 and control randomers were applied when GnRH neurons and olfactory axons had just begun to leave the main tissue explant. Two days later, treated and control explants were harvested for either PCR measurement of relative expression levels of IGSF10 in GnRH neurons from randomer- and antisense-treated explants or stained for GnRH (NiDAB, blue) and peripherin, a marker for olfactory axons (DAB, brown), GnRH neurons counted, and migration distance measured ( ). PCR revealed a decrease in IGSF10 of 71% in antisense-treated explants. Analysis of staining showed no difference in GnRH neuron number (p = 0.56; randomer = 117 ± 13 [n = 16]; antisense = 122 ± 17 [n = 12]). A significant difference between the groups was found in the location of GnRH neurons (χ2
p = < 0.0001; χ2 = 51.300). In explants in which IGSF10 was knocked down, GnRH neurons remained closer to the explant compared to those treated with randomers. GnRH neurons in antisense-treated explants had significantly more GnRH neurons 100–400 μm from the midline tip and significantly fewer neurons >900 μm from the midline tip than neurons in explants treated with the randomer ( ).
To further verify the role of IGSF10 in GnRH neurons, we used FNC-B4-hTERT human GnRH neuronal cells. , IGSF10 was readily detectable in these GnRH neuronal cells, and IGSF10 siRNA knockdown resulted in activation of RET and cdc42/Rac ( ). IGSF10 knockdown inhibited the migration and proliferation of FNC-B4-hTERT cells, which was rescued by IGSF10 CM ( and ). These results suggest that the role of IGSF10 is also conserved in human GnRH neurons.
Collectively, these findings suggest that IGSF10 functions as a RET antagonist by facilitating the assembly of an inhibitory RET-GAS1 complex, which plays critical roles in regulating Ewing sarcoma growth and GnRH neuron migration (model in ).

## DISCUSSION
In this study, we revealed that IGSF10 is an antagonist of RET ( ). IGSF10 prevents cdc42 activation by RET ( ), and this activity of IGSF10 plays critical roles in Ewing sarcoma growth ( ) and GnRH neuronal migration ( ). Mechanistically, IGSF10 facilitates the assembly of an inhibitory RET-GAS1 complex and prevents the formation of a stimulatory RET-GFRA complex ( ). IGSF10 mutants linked to delayed puberty display defects in growth signaling in Ewing sarcoma and migration as well as growth signaling in GN11 GnRH neurons ( and ). We determined that IGSF10 is an EWS-FLI1-induced dependency in Ewing sarcoma ( ).
While IGSF10 mutations were linked to hereditary delayed puberty in 2016, the mechanism of action of IGSF10 has been an enigma. This study uncovered that IGSF10 is a RET antagonist and demonstrated that RET inhibition by IGSF10 is important in Ewing sarcoma growth and GnRH neuronal migration. It is intriguing that Ewing sarcoma and GnRH neurons are both dependent on the IGSF10-RET pathway. Although the cells of origin for Ewing sarcoma have not yet been determined, neural crest cells or neural crest-derived mesenchymal stem cells have been proposed as the cells of origin. , GnRH neurons are also partially derived from the neural crest. , , It is possible that the shared IGSF10-RET pathway in Ewing sarcoma and GnRH neurons reflects their common origin in the neural crest. In this regard, it is interesting that RET plays an essential role in the proliferation and migration of neural crest-derived cells. – , 
Mutational activation of RET, most commonly by the M918T mutation, causes multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2B (MEN 2B), characterized by mucosal neuroma, medullary thyroid carcinoma, adrenal pheochromocytoma, and Marfanoid habitus. Approximately 40% of patients with MEN 2B are reported to display delayed puberty. RET hyperactivation caused by M918T mutation and by IGSF10 mutations may be the common basis for aberrant GnRH neuron migration and delayed puberty observed in patients with MEN 2B and IGSF10 mutation carriers.
RET knockout mice display defective migration of sympathetic neurons. Inactivating RET mutations in humans cause Hirsch-sprung disease, which is characterized by defective migration of neural crest-derived cells to developing gut. , While these findings suggest a pro-migratory role for RET, our results suggest that RET hyperactivation upon IGSF10 suppression inhibits GnRH neuronal migration. Abnormality in GnRH neurons has not been reported in mice deficient for RET, GFRA, or GDNF family ligands. It is possible that RET activity needs to be fine-tuned within a proper range for proper migration of neurons. It is also possible that RET plays different roles in the migration of different neuronal subtypes.
RET was originally identified as an oncogene activated by DNA rearrangement. , Somatic rearrangements of the RET gene generating constitutively active RET fusions are found in 10%–20% of papillary thyroid carcinoma and 1%–2% of non-small-cell lung cancers. Increased expression or activity of RET is also observed in multiple cancer types, including breast, pancreas, prostate, and myeloid malignancies. Interestingly, low IGSF10 expression is linked to more aggressive lung and breast cancers. – IGSF10 may function as a tumor suppressor in these cancers by inhibiting RET. While these studies suggest oncogenic roles for RET, RET also appears to play tumor suppressive roles in some cancer types. The RET gene promoter is frequently methylated in colorectal cancers, , and inactivating RET mutations are also identified in these cancers. In neuroblastoma, RET expression or RET activation by GDNF was shown to induce neuronal differentiation. , This study adds Ewing sarcoma to the list of cancers in which RET plays tumor-suppressive roles.
The identification of the IGSF10-RET axis as a critical dependency in Ewing sarcoma provides an opportunity for therapeutic targeting. Since RET signaling plays an important role in maintaining dopaminergic neurons, , several structurally distinct classes of RET agonists have been developed as potential therapeutics for Parkinson’s disease and other neurodegenerative diseases. These include BT, HUS, and Q compounds. – These RET agonists and their derivatives can be developed to bypass IGSF10 and activate RET in Ewing sarcoma. Although the above-mentioned oncogenic roles for RET raise a concern about secondary tumorigenesis, chronic increase of the RET ligand, GDNF, did not enhance tumor formation in mice, suggesting that agonist-induced activation of wild-type RET may not promote secondary tumor formation. Alternatively, monoclonal antibodies and compounds to block IGSF10-RET interaction can be developed. Although EWS-FLI1 is the primary driver of Ewing sarcoma, targeting EWS-FLI1, an intracellular transcription factor, has been unsuccessful. Since the IGSF10-RET axis is extracellular, it is likely more accessible for therapeutic targeting. Therefore, it will be important to evaluate the efficacy of targeting the IGSF10-RET axis in order to develop targeted therapy.

## Limitations of the study
While the present study uncovered that IGSF10 functions as a RET antagonist regulating Ewing sarcoma growth and GnRH neuron migration, the study is largely limited to cell culture models. The role of IGSF10 in Ewing sarcoma tumors in vivo needs to be further evaluated. Although the lack of genetically engineered mouse tumor models for Ewing sarcoma is a well-known obstacle, the Ewing sarcoma tumor models in other model organisms could be used, and the analysis of human Ewing sarcoma tumor samples can also be informative. To evaluate the role of IGSF10 in GnRH neuron migration in vivo, IGSF10 knockout and mutant knockin in mice and other model organisms can be used. While our signaling/molecular cell biology data amply support the role of the IGSF10-RET-GAS1 complex in Ewing sarcoma and GnRH neurons, direct biochemical evidence for the three-way complex is still lacking. IGSF10 is a large (2623 amino acids) protein, and the RET-binding regions are located at its C terminus, while the GAS1-binding region is located at its N terminus. We have not been able to express and purify full-length IGSF10, and hence, the reconstitution of the three-way complex was not feasible. In the future, the reconstitution of the three-way complex and its structural studies will provide important mechanistic insights into the role of IGSF10 as a RET antagonist.

Female 5 – 6-week-old C.B.17SC scid−/− mice were used. All mice were housed in a pathogen-free vivarium in the University of Texas Health Science Center at San Antonio. Mice were randomly allocated to treatment groups. Blinding of the researcher measuring tumor size was employed. The animal research method was reviewed and approved for humaneness by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Texas Health Science Center at San Antonio. Because Ewing sarcoma tumors are not known to be sex hormone-responsive or otherwise display sex-dependent differences, we believe it is unlikely that IGSF10 dependency of xenograft tumor growth differs between female and male mice.

## METHOD DETAILS

## Transfection, viral infection, and cell treatment
Calcium phosphate co-precipitation was used for transfection of 293T cells. Lentiviruses were prepared by transfection in 293T cells following System Biosciences’ protocol, and the cells infected with lentiviruses were selected with 2 μg/mL puromycin for 48 h. IGSF10 cDNA clone (RC215458) was purchased from OriGene. cDNAs for EWS-FLI1, IGSF10, RET, and GAS1 were cloned into pcDNA3 mammalian expression vector (Invitrogen/Thermo Fisher) or pCDH1-puro lentiviral expression vector (System Biosciences). The target sequences for shRNAs are as follows: FLI1 C terminus shRNA, AACGATCAGTAAGAATACAGAGC; luciferase shRNA, GCACTCTGATTGACAAATACGATTT; IGSF10 shRNA-1, CAGGCGTATATCACTGTATAA; IGSF10 shRNA-2, CCACAAATAGTCATCAGACAT; and control scrambled shRNA, CCTAAGGTTAAGTCGCCCTCG. ## Protein sample preparation and proteomic analysis
The preparation of secreted protein samples, mass spectrometry analysis, and proteomics data processing were performed essentially as described. , Cells were washed six times with culture medium without serum. Subsequently, cells were cultured in medium without serum for 24 h and the culture supernatant was harvested. The supernatant was centrifuged, filtered through a 0.45-μm filter (Millipore), and concentrated using 3,000-Da cut-off Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filter Units (Millipore).
The proteins in each sample were fractionated by SDS-PAGE using a Criterion XT 12% gel that was electrophoresed for 2 cm and then stained with Coomassie blue. Each gel lane was divided into six slices, and the proteins in each slice were reduced in situ with TCEP [tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine] and alkylated in the dark with iodoacetamide prior to treatment with trypsin (Promega; sequencing grade) in 40 mM ammonium bicarbonate overnight at 37°C. The resulting tryptic peptides were analyzed by HPLC-ESI-tandem mass spectrometry on a Thermo Fisher LTQ Orbitrap Velos Pro mass spectrometer in combination with an RSLC NANO HPLC (Thermo Scientific/Dionex). Mascot (v2.8.0.1; Matrix Science) was used to search the spectra against a combination of the UniProt Mouse database [UniProt_Mouse 20161004 (57,951 sequences; 26,573,730 residues)] plus a database of common contaminants (247 sequences; 128,130 residues). Search parameters included: peptide mass tolerance, ±20 ppm; fragment mass tolerance, ±0.8 Da; fixed modification, carbamidomethylation (cysteine); variable modifications, acetylation (protein N terminus), oxidation (methionine) and deamidation (glutamine and asparagine); enzyme, trypsin with one missed cleavage allowed. The Mascot data files were combined using the MudPIT option in Scaffold (v5.0.1; Proteome Software) and subjected to subset searching by X! Tandem [CYCLONE (2010.12.01.1)] followed by cross-correlation of the Mascot and X! Tandem data. The thresholds for acceptance of peptide and protein assignments in Scaffold were 95% and 99%, respectively, with a minimum of two peptide required, resulting in a protein-level FDR of 2.1%. The mass spectrometry proteomics data were deposited in the ProteomeXchange Consortium via MassIVE partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD052271 (MSV000094771).

## Isothermal titration calorimetry
Recombinant RET extracellular domain (R&D, 10302-CR-050), GST-IGSF10 Ig(3–5), GST-IGSF10 Ig(6–8), GST-IGSF10 Ig(9–12), recombinant GAS1 (R&D, 2636-GS-050), and GST-IGSF10 (1–281) were dialyzed (MWCO 3.5 kDa) against ITC buffer (PBS, pH 7.4) for 12–14 h at 4°C. All ITC measurements were performed on a MicroCal PEAQ-ITC instrument (Malvern Panalytical) at 25°C. The concentration of protein samples was 1.46 μM IGSF10 Ig3–5, 25.4 μM IGSF10 Ig6–8, 35 μM IGSF10 Ig9–12, 6.65 μM RET, 3.91 μM IGSF10 (1–281), and 39.1 μM GAS1. The experimental settings include one 0.4 μL injection followed by nineteen 2 μL injections with a 150 s interval. Data was analyzed with MicroCal PEAQ-ITC ORIGIN V7.0 analysis software using the one-set-of-sites binding model. Three technical replicates were performed for an error estimation.

## Cell proliferation and xenograft tumorigenicity assays
Anchorage-dependent cell proliferation was assessed by IncuCyte live-cell imaging system (Essen BioScience/Sartorius). The IncuCyte system monitors cell proliferation by analyzing the occupied area (% confluence) of cell images over time. At least four fields from five wells were assayed for each experimental condition. The cell seeding density was 2000 cells per well in a 96-well plate. For each assay, biological replicates were performed to confirm the reproducibility of results.
Anchorage-independent cell proliferation was evaluated by soft agar colony formation assays. A673 cells were infected with lentiviruses expressing IGSF10 shRNA-1, IGSF10 shRNA-2, or control scrambled shRNA and were selected with 2 μg/mL puromycin. Four days after infection, 4×103 cells were plated in soft agar. The soft agar cultures were composed of two layers: a base layer [3 mL in a 60-mm dish; DMEM/10% fetal bovine serum/0.6% noble agar (A5431, MilliporeSigma)/penicillin/streptomycin] and a cell layer (2 mL in a 60-mm dish; DMEM/10% fetal bovine serum/0.3% noble agar/penicillin/streptomycin). Colonies were grown for three weeks and counted. Colonies (>50 cells) were scored by randomly counting 9 fields per dish.
For xenograft tumorigenicity assays, cells were subcutaneously injected into the flanks of SCID mice (1.5×106 cells/injection, five mice/group). Tumor growth was monitored weekly using a caliper. While it is not possible to predict the effect size, we chose the sample size of five mice per group based on our prior experience with xenograft experiments. Mice were randomly allocated to treatment groups. Blinding of the researcher measuring tumor size was employed. The animal research method was reviewed and approved for humaneness by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Texas Health Science Center at San Antonio.

## Transwell migration assays
Migration assays were performed in modified Boyden chambers (Corning Transwell, 8 mm pore size, MilliporeSigma). Cells were seeded at 5 × 104 cells/200 μL serum free-DMEM medium in the upper chamber. The lower chamber was filled with DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. After 20-h incubation, the membrane was gently removed from the chamber and the cells on the upper surface were removed using cotton swabs. Cells on the lower surface that migrated through the membrane were fixed with 50% methanol and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. The migrated cells were counted using ImageJ.

## Knockdown of IGSF10 in primary GnRH neurons maintained in nasal explants
Nasal explants were generated, treated with two different antisense oligonucleotides (CTATCTCACCTGCAAGGACTGC and GGCAGTCACAGGTCCATGGGTT) designed to knockdown IGSF10 expression or a randomer oligonucleotide (CACAGA GGGTGTGGACGCCAAA) with the same length and GC content. Post hoc analysis of the total number of GnRH neurons as well as the distance the cells had migrated away from the main explant tissue was performed.

## Relative expression levels of IGSF10 in GnRH neurons from treated explants
GnRH neurons were plucked from explants transfected with either randomer or antisense oligomers (n = 2 each). Two tubes containing 5–10 GnRH neurons/tube were obtained from each randomer treated explant and 4 tubes from each antisense treated explant. cDNA was prepared from each tube using Maxima H Minus Reverse Transcriptase Kit, cDNA from 2 tubes of each group were pooled and serially diluted (neat, 1:2, 1:4, 1:8) to assess relative expression levels. PCR was performed using β-tubulin and IGSF10-specific primers, and the signal intensity across the dilution series analyzed, normalized to β-tubulin expression.

## Immunocytochemistry
Rabbit polyclonal anti-GnRH-1 (SW-1; RRID:AB_2629221), and mouse anti-peripherin (7C5, Stem Cell) were used. Staining was performed using standard double avidin–biotin horseradish peroxidase chromogen procedures using nickel-intensified 3-diaminobenzidine (NiDAB) and DAB.

## Nasal explant migration assays
Explants from 3 different culture dates were transfected at 3div, then 2 days later fixed and stained for GnRH to assess overall GnRH neuronal migration. Measurements were performed on each half, giving 1–2 measurements/explant. In control vs. antisense explants, the total number of GnRH neurons was counted (n > 10 explants per group). Distances that the GnRH neurons had migrated from the outer rim of the midline cartilage of the explant were measured in 100 μm zones, except the last zone which contained cells that had migrated greater than 1 mm from the explant. Treatment groups were then compared with a two-way ANOVA to test for differences in cell number, and differences in migration behavior were analyzed using Chi-Squared.

## QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed with Prism (GraphPad Software, version 10.2.3). Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Two-tailed Student’s t tests were used to calculate the p-values. The results were considered significant when p < 0.05. The number of replicates, independent samples, and animals is indicated in the figure legends.
