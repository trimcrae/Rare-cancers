## Abstract

## 
Objective

To analyse at our institution the criteria for selecting a first-line therapy for patients with advanced radioiodine-refractory thyroid cancer and their clinical responses, safety and survival outcomes.

## 
Patients and methods

We extracted data from 69 consecutive patients referred to Federico II University Hospital from September 2016 to September 2024, among whom 44 patients were treated with TKIs as first-line treatment and outside any clinical trial, and form the basis of this report.

## 
Results

Thirty-one (71%) patients were treated with the antiangiogenesis inhibitor lenvatinib and 13 (29%) were treated with selective tyrosine kinase inhibitors (s-TKIs). Among the latter, eight patients were treated with dabrafenib + trametinib (DT), two patients were treated with selpercatinib because of contraindications to lenvatinib, and three patients received DT as redifferentiation therapy. A RECIST partial response was observed in 28% of patients treated with lenvatinib, in 63% of those treated with DT and in one of the two patients treated with selpercatinib. Grade ≥3 adverse events occurred in 13 (42%) patients treated with lenvatinib and only in 1 (9%) patient treated with DT. Progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival rates at 1 year were 72% and 83% in lenvatinib-treated patients and 69% and 83% in DT-treated patients, respectively. In both selpercatinib-treated patients, the PFS at data cut-off was 10 months. No treatment-related deaths were observed.

## 
Conclusion

S-TKIs permitted tailoring systemic treatment based on disease location, tumour volume and patient comorbidities, achieving satisfactory tolerance and outcomes in selected patients with an actionable driver mutation and with contraindications to angiogenesis inhibitors or candidates for redifferentiation therapy.

## Introduction
Systemic treatment with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) is the most effective treatment for advanced radioactive iodine-refractory (RAI-R) thyroid cancer. Ten years ago, two multikinase inhibitors (MKIs), sorafenib ( ) and lenvatinib ( ), were approved by medical regulatory agencies for advanced thyroid cancer patients as first-line treatment based on the results of large phase 3 trials. Their efficacy is mainly based on the inhibition of several kinase receptors that promote tumour neoangiogenesis, but their use is associated with multiple adverse events. Currently, selective TKIs (s-TKIs) are marking a further step forward in improving both the tolerance and the survival of patients with oncogene-addicted cancers by selectively inhibiting a specific oncoprotein encoded by a tumour driver mutation ( ).
A high percentage of actionable driver gene alterations exist in RAI-R thyroid cancer, including the BRAFV600E mutation, RET fusion genes and rarer BRAF, NTRK and ALK fusion genes ( ). To inhibit the BRAFV600E mutant-dependent signalling, the BRAF inhibitor dabrafenib was used alone and in combination with the MEK inhibitor trametinib in a recent phase 2 study that reported RECIST objective response rates of 35 and 30%, respectively ( ). Importantly, the selective inhibition of BRAFV600E has also been shown to redifferentiate cancer cells with the restoration of iodine uptake in RAI-R thyroid cancers. In a phase 2 study of 24 patients with BRAFV600E-mutated progressive RAI-R differentiated thyroid cancer, RAI treatment after redifferentiation therapy with dabrafenib + trametinib (DT) induced a 6-month partial response (PR) rate of 38% ( ). In a phase 2 trial of selpercatinib – a selective inhibitor of RET – on 34 subjects with RET fusion-positive advanced differentiated thyroid cancer, 88% of cases achieved an objective response, including seven complete responses ( ). Some case reports have also shown a promising redifferentiation potential of specific inhibitors of RET or TRK fusion genes ( , ), which is currently the subject of an ongoing phase 2 study (NCT05668962).
In this paper, we report our institutional experience of the changes in the clinical management of patients with RAI-R differentiated thyroid cancer brought by the availability of s-TKIs, which were prescribed as first-line therapy based on our own experience and indirect data from the medical literature. Lenvatinib is the only TKI to have received to date reimbursability from the Italian National Health System, in June 2016, with the indication as first-line treatment for progressive and/or symptomatic advanced refractory differentiated thyroid cancer. In our institution, we started to prescribe s-TKIs in the first-line outside clinical trials in January 2021 through a case-by-case request to the Campania Region Health Authority in patients with an actionable driver mutation, based on the presence of contraindications to treatment with antiangiogenic MKIs. In this setting, we analysed the clinicopathological characteristics of the patients who received MKIs or s-TKIs, their toxicity profiles and the impact of MKIs and s-TKIs on patients’ outcomes in our clinical practice.

## Methods

## Patients and data collection
Sixty-nine patients were referred for advanced RAI-R differentiated thyroid cancer at Federico II University Hospital between September 2016 and September 2024. Clinical charts were examined to collect patients’ demographic and clinical data (gender, age, date of cancer diagnosis, cumulative RAI activity, date of TKI initiation, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status at TKI initiation, date of death or last follow-up visit), pathological information (TNM staging according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer VIII edition, primary tumour size, extrathyroidal extension, lymphovascular invasion and tumour mutational status), radiological assessment and treatment details. Only the 44 patients who started TKI treatment outside clinical trials after September 2016 at our department were included in the study and form the basis of this report.
Patients were followed up depending on initial stage and tumour progression every 3–12 months from surgery, with serum thyroglobulin determinations and standardized imaging techniques. Neck lymph nodes were evaluated with ultrasonography; mediastinal and lung metastases with contrast-enhanced computed tomography scans; brain, liver and bone metastases with contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging. Positron emission tomography with fluoro-deoxyglucose was used for the detection of tracer-avid metastases. This standardized imaging work-up permitted an accurate evaluation of the tumour volume and of its progression over time. The criteria for classifying a disease as refractory to RAI were those indicated in the most recent guidelines ( ), namely, non-avid RAI metastases at first RAI treatment or loss of RAI avidity at subsequent treatments; presence of RAI uptake in only some lesions but absence in others, and disease progression despite RAI avidity. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethical committee of Federico II University Hospital ‘Comitato Etico Campania 3’ (protocol no. 152/2023).

## Systemic treatments
In all patients with advanced disease, selection criteria for systemic treatment initiation were based on the presence of clinically relevant symptoms and/or disease progression according to RECIST version 1.1 ( ). The recommended lenvatinib dose at treatment initiation was 24 mg once daily, and lower starting doses (≤14 mg/day) were prescribed in the presence of tumour lesions at risk of bleeding or fistulization and/or comorbidities at risk of complication despite optimized medical treatment. DT was started in all patients at full doses of 150 mg twice daily + 2 mg once daily, respectively. Redifferentiation treatment included the administration of DT over a period of 42 days ( ), combined with a fixed RAI activity of 150 mCi on day 35 after two injections of recombinant human thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH). Selpercatinib was administered at a full dose of 160 mg twice daily. For all three treatment regimens, dose reductions in the presence of adverse events were performed according to the manufacturer’s recommendations.
Selection criteria for choosing s-TKIs over MKIs in patients with actionable driver mutations were as follows: locally advanced disease at high risk of tracheal and/or oesophageal bleeding or fistulization; lung metastases with documented bronchial infiltration at high risk of bleeding or fistulization; comorbidities at high risk of decompensation because of treatment-related toxicities despite optimized medical treatment. Redifferentiation therapy was proposed to all BRAFV600E-mutated patients who fulfilled the following eligibility criteria: ECOG performance status 0–1; previous cumulative RAI activity <600 mCi; RECIST progression within 12 months prior to treatment; distant metastases in the lung only and a maximum size of metastases not exceeding 3 cm ( ). For the administration of redifferentiation therapy, we included only cases with the BRAFV600E mutation based on the results of the MERAIODE phase 2 trial, and excluded patients with RAS mutations based on the results from the same study ( , ). Of note, the Italian Drug Agency (Agenzia Italiana del Farmaco) has not yet approved the indication of DT for the treatment of BRAFV600E-mutated non-anaplastic thyroid cancer, and has approved the use of selpercatinib in RET-fused thyroid cancer only in the second treatment line. Therefore, after written informed consent, the treatment of patients who received DT or selpercatinib in the first line has been approved at our request by the ethics committee of the patient’s respective local health authorities, in accordance with the legislation of the Campania Region.

## Tumour mutational analysis
The molecular analysis was performed following two different approaches: until December 2021, samples were analysed with a real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)-based approach using the EntroGen Thyroid Cancer Mutation Analysis Panel Kit (EntroGen, Inc., USA) on the QuantStudio 5 platform (Applied Biosystems, USA; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). This kit detects the most common clinically relevant mutations in BRAF, KRAS, NRAS, HRAS and the RET/PTC1-3, PAX8/PPARγ aberrant fusion transcripts ( ). From January 2022, molecular analysis was carried out by our next-generation sequencing custom panel, called Nexthyro, on the Genexus™ system (https://www.genexus.com; Thermo Fisher Scientific), built to cover 265 clinically relevant hotspot mutations across 15 genes (BRAF, EIF1AX, GNAS, HRAS, IDH1, KRAS, NF2, NRAS, PIK3CA, PPM1D, PTEN, RET, DICER1, CHEK2 and TERT promoter) and 161 clinically relevant fusion genes (RET, BRAF, NTRK, PAX8/PPARγ, ALK and ROS1) ( ). Data analysis was carried out adopting Genexus™ proprietary bioinformatic pipeline on the Genexus Software (1.0).

## Outcome measures and adverse events
All patients were followed up with clinical examinations that included ECG, blood and urine tests 7–14 days after TKI initiation, then monthly for the first 3–6 months, and every 1–3 months thereafter, depending on clinical evolution. The first imaging after TKI initiation was performed for each patient at 1–2 months with a standardized work-up, then every 3–6 months depending on structural and clinical response. Treatment efficacy was assessed according to RECIST v1.1. Progression-free survival (PFS) was calculated from the time of the first TKI administration to tumour progression or death. The PFS duration of patients who were on treatment at the time of the data cut-off was censored at the data cut-off. Overall survival (OS) was calculated from the time of first TKI administration to death. The OS duration of patients who were alive or lost to follow-up at the time of data cut-off (September 1st, 2024) was censored on the date of the last time the patient was known to be alive.
Adverse events were classified according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0 and were treated according to the most recent guidelines for toxicity management and supportive care. All patients had a phone connection to our institution to promptly communicate in case of adverse events.

## Statistical analysis
All variables were analysed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test to assess normality before data analysis. Medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) were used for non-normally distributed data. Continuous variables, including age at diagnosis and at TKI initiation, primary tumour size and cumulative RAI activity, were analysed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact probability test. The Kaplan–Meier method and log-rank test were used for the analysis of survival outcomes. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The statistical analyses were conducted using the GraphPad Prism version 10.1 software (https://www.graphpad.com/updates/prism-10-1-1-release-notes).

## Results

## Patient characteristics
From September 2016, we have consecutively treated 44 patients in our department for advanced RAI-R differentiated thyroid cancer in the first line with TKIs ( ). The median age at primary cancer diagnosis was 62 years (53–68), and the male-to-female ratio was 1.3. All 44 patients had been referred to surgery, but in six cases (14%), a complete thyroidectomy was not feasible. The median primary tumour size was 4.1 cm (2.4–6.9). Seventeen cases (39%) presented with distant metastases at surgery, of whom 12 were aged more than 55 years. In four patients (9%) RAI was not administered due to persistent high-volume thyroid tissue after surgery. Forty patients received a median cumulative RAI activity of 309 mCi (150–470), and 19 cases (48%) were considered RAI-R at the first post-treatment whole-body scan.
 Mutational status was investigated in 31 of the 44 patients (71%) and was systematically performed from January 2021. Among these 31 cases, 16 (52%) had a BRAFV600E mutation, three (10%) had an NRAS mutation, two (6%) had concomitant BRAFV600E and TERT promoter mutations, two (6%) had a RET fusion, one (3%) had concomitant BRAFV600E and TP53 mutations, one (3%) had a BRAFD594Y mutation, one (3%) had a TP53 mutation and in five (16%) cases no driver mutation was found.

## First-line systemic treatments
The median age at TKI initiation was 68 years (61–75). Thirty-one patients (71%) were treated with lenvatinib, and 13 patients (29%) were treated with s-TKIs, of whom 11 were treated with DT and two were treated with selpercatinib. From September 2016 to December 2020, lenvatinib was the only first-line administered TKI and was given to 19 patients, either at a full initial dose of 24 mg/day in 13 patients or at a reduced dose (≤14 mg/day) in the other 6 patients for a high risk of bleeding/fistulization or comorbidities. A first-line treatment with a s-TKI, specifically DT, was administered to the first patient in January 2021. Since then, 25 patients initiated a systemic treatment, of whom 12 (48%) received lenvatinib and 13 (52%) s-TKIs. Lenvatinib was given at an initial dose of 24 mg/day in six patients and at a reduced dose (≤14 mg/day) in the other six patients due to a high risk of bleeding/fistulization or comorbidities but who had no actionable driver mutations. DT combination was administered in three cases with locally advanced disease at risk of bleeding/fistulization, in two cases with tracheobronchial metastases at high risk of bleeding/fistulization, in three cases with comorbidities considered to be at high risk of decompensation in relation to increased risk of treatment-related toxicity to angiogenesis inhibitors (one case with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) complicated by coronary artery disease, one case with poorly controlled T2DM complicated by diabetic macroangiopathy and one case with ulcerative colitis subjected to total colectomy), and three cases eligible for redifferentiation therapy. Selpercatinib was administered in one patient with locally advanced disease at risk of bleeding/fistulization and in one patient with hypertensive heart disease at high risk of decompensation.
TNM staging, age at diagnosis, ECOG performance status and age at systemic treatment initiation, cumulative dose of RAI and rate of synchronous distant metastases were not relevant factors in the choice of treatment between s-TKI and lenvatinib ( ). However, s-TKI-treated patients presented with a higher rate of incomplete primary surgery (31 vs 6%; P = 0.053), a smaller tumour size at histology (3 (2.1–3.7) vs 5.5 (2.8–7) cm; P = 0.007), and a lower number of metastatic sites per patient (1.5 vs 2.2; P = 0.02) compared to lenvatinib-treated patients. Specifically, lenvatinib-treated patients presented a higher rate of bone metastases (42 vs 8%; P = 0.03), and a higher rate of metastases outside mediastinal lymph nodes, lung and bone (26 vs 0%; P = 0.04) compared to s-TKI-treated patients.

## Treatment-related adverse events
All patients treated with lenvatinib had at least one treatment-related adverse event of any grade ( ). The mean number of adverse events per patient who started lenvatinib at 24 mg and in those who started at ≤14 mg was 5.6 and 3.9, respectively (P = 0.1). All 19 patients treated with lenvatinib at 24 mg had to reduce the dose due to treatment-related adverse events, while dose reduction was necessary only in 4 of the 12 patients who started lenvatinib at ≤14 mg. Grade 3 adverse events occurred in 47% of patients treated with 24 mg and 33% of those treated with ≤14 mg daily. The most frequent adverse event was fatigue, which occurred in 71% of patients (in 19 cases at grade 1–2 and in three cases at grade 3), followed by hypertension in 61% (in 16 cases at grade 1–2 and in three cases at grade 3), weight loss in 58% (in 13 cases at grade 1–2 and in five cases at grade 3), decreased appetite in 58% (in 16 cases at grade 1–2 and in two cases at grade 3) and increased TSH in 55% (in all 17 cases at grade 1–2). Three patients (10%) had to discontinue lenvatinib because of toxicity: one patient because of recurrent grade 3 proteinuria 5 months after treatment start and two patients because of life-threatening adverse events (colonic perforation in one case and acute heart failure in one case, occurring 3 and 10 months after treatment start, respectively). No fatal adverse events were observed.
 Seven of the 11 patients (64%) treated with DT showed treatment-related adverse events. Fever occurred in six patients (55%) at grade 1–2, and fatigue occurred in three patients (27%) at grade 1–2. One patient (9%) also had grade 1 anorexia and weight loss, and one patient (9%) had a grade 3 folliculitis. A reduction in dabrafenib treatment dose to 75 mg twice daily was only necessary in this one patient. Both patients who received selpercatinib had an increase in TSH levels. One patient also presented with grade 1–2 fatigue, anorexia and weight loss, and selpercatinib dose was reduced to 120 mg twice daily.

## Treatment outcomes
The median follow-up from TKI initiation was 17.5 months (11–44). At data cut-off, 24 patients (54.5%) were alive, 11 (25%) had died of disease-related causes, 7 (16%) had died from causes unrelated to cancer and 2 (4.5%) were lost to follow-up.
In lenvatinib-treated patients, the median follow-up from treatment initiation was 25 months (range: 13–49 months). At data cut-off, 12 out of 31 patients (39%) were alive, 11 (35%) died of disease-related causes, 7 (23%) died for causes unrelated to cancer (two from stroke, two from infections, one from pneumonia, one from Alzheimer’s disease and one from heart disease) and 1 (3%) was lost to follow-up. Kaplan–Meyer estimates of PFS and OS at 1 year were 72% and 83%, respectively ( and ). Median PFS and OS were 24 and 46 months, respectively. Best treatment responses according to RECIST v1.1 were evaluable in 29 of the 31 lenvatinib-treated patients, while one patient refused to undergo follow-up imaging and one patient died before the first follow-up imaging. The median time to best treatment response was 5 months (3–7). Among the 29 evaluable patients, PR was observed in 8 cases (28%), stable disease (SD) in 20 cases and progressive disease in 1 case ( ). According to the initial lenvatinib dose (24 mg/day in 19 patients and ≤14 mg/day in 10 patients), a PR was observed in five and in three patients, respectively, an SD was observed in 13 and seven patients, respectively, and a progression occurred in one patient treated with an initial dose of 24 mg/day. Four patients with a BRAFV600E-mutated cancer that progressed under lenvatinib received second-line treatment with dabrafenib: the disease continued to progress in two cases, while a SD was obtained in the other two cases for 10 and 3 months, respectively.
 Median follow-up from DT treatment initiation was 14 months (range: 9–19 months). At data cut-off, 10 of the 11 patients were alive, and one patient was lost to follow-up. DT treatment was administered on a long-term continuous basis in eight patients ( ) and with the purpose of redifferentiation in the remaining three patients. In the eight continuous-treated patients, the best response was PR in five (63%) and SD in three cases ( ). The median time to best treatment response was 4 months (2–12.5). Kaplan–Meier estimates of the PFS and OS rates at 1 year in these eight patients were 69% and 83%, respectively. Disease progression occurred in two patients after 4 and 8 months, respectively, who then received lenvatinib as second-line treatment. All three patients who received redifferentiation therapy had a lung miliary metastatic spread that was not measurable according to RECIST. In all three cases, RAI uptake in the lungs was observed on post-treatment scintigraphy, and all three patients achieved a SD, with durations at data cut-off of 10, 18 and 22 months, respectively.
 Two patients with RET-fused thyroid cancer received selpercatinib as the first-line treatment, and at data cut-off, both were alive. One case showed PR with a decrease of −40% in tumour volume 4 months after treatment start, while the other had SD. The duration of response at data cut-off was 10 months in both cases.

## Discussion
Lenvatinib has demonstrated high efficacy in a placebo-controlled phase 3 trial in patients with advanced RAI-R thyroid cancer, irrespective of BRAF mutational status ( ), and therefore has the highest strength of recommendation in current guidelines ( ). S-TKIs are considered as another feasible option in first-line treatment, but with a lower level of recommendation since they were not tested within placebo-controlled or head-to-head comparative studies in the context of RAI-R thyroid cancer ( ). Nevertheless, the availability of s-TKIs has markedly changed the therapeutic management of patients affected by RAI-R differentiated thyroid cancer. This change particularly involved patients who are exposed to an additional risk of acute bleeding or fistulization if subjected to antiangiogenic MKIs therapy, e.g. those with tumour invasion of the larynx, tracheobronchial tree, oesophagus and/or main vessels; and patients with comorbidities with increased risk of fatal adverse events or treatment-related toxicity that may compromise therapeutic adherence to an inadequate level. Starting from when the administration of s-TKIs became feasible outside clinical trials, we have preferred to administer a full-dose s-TKI in the first line rather than low-dose lenvatinib to all patients with actionable mutations and contraindications to antiangiogenic MKIs or comorbidities with increased risk of treatment-related toxicity.
How would they have been managed if s-TKIs had not been available? In cases of increased risk of fatal adverse events with antiangiogenic MKIs and non-feasibility of locoregional treatments ( ), it is current clinical practice to start antiangiogenic MKIs at lower dosages than those recommended, and then - if possible - gradually increase to the maximum tolerated dose ( , ). In our series, approximately 60% of the patients that initiated treatment with lenvatinib received the full initial dose of 24 mg daily, and objective response rates and toxicity events associated with lenvatinib were comparable to those reported in other real-life clinical studies ( , , ). Similarly, patients who received DT or selpercatinib on a continuous basis showed toxicity profiles comparable to those reported in larger phase 2 trials but RECIST objective response rates in the upper ranges documented in the existing literature ( , ). This could be at least partially explained by the low percentage of patients with bone metastases, which are an unfavourable prognostic factor ( ).
Notably, patients who received s-TKIs in our series had an increased risk of treatment-related adverse events with antiangiogenic MKIs, which would have led us otherwise to administer lenvatinib at lower doses than those recommended. The issue with starting treatment at lower doses is that this can reduce its efficacy while risking to induce the adverse events. In a randomized phase 2 trial, patients who started lenvatinib at 18 mg had indeed a lower RECIST objective response rate compared to those who received an initial dose of 24 mg, despite similar rates of adverse events ( ). To reduce treatment-related toxicities, an option could be to plan a so-called ‘drug holiday’, i.e. scheduled treatment discontinuation periods after the occurrence of a given adverse event, in order to minimize the appearance of further adverse events ( ). However, data on the planned drug holidays approach are derived from non-randomized observational trials and present selection biases that require randomized studies to be addressed. Finally, if the objective is to delay disease progression, few real-life studies reported that even a low-dose treatment may provide clinical benefit while limiting the rate of toxicity ( , ).
The observation that kinase inhibitors are also able to restore iodine uptake in a non-iodine-avid tumour has already increased the potential therapeutic scope of RAI treatment. Most of the ongoing redifferentiation trials and the MERAIODE study ( ) are based on a single cycle of time-limited TKI administration associated with high-activity RAI treatment, and whether this can be repeated in case of therapeutic success at the first cycle remains to be demonstrated. The main advantage associated with redifferentiation therapy is to achieve a potentially long-lasting tumour response that allows the patient to delay the initiation of continuous TKI-based therapy. In light of this, in our clinical practice, redifferentiation therapy is offered to all patients who fulfil the eligibility criteria proposed in the MERAIODE study. All three patients in this study who underwent redifferentiation therapy had progressive disease that required systemic treatment, and all three had significant clinical benefit that resulted in a SD for 10, 18 and 22 months, respectively.
The retrospective nature of the analysis is a limitation of this study. Selection bias may have occurred, but all consecutive patients who were referred to our centre were enrolled in this study, without any exclusion. In addition, the criteria for administering s-TKIs vs MKIs have been predefined on the basis of the available scientific evidence and were the same for all TKI-treated patients. Moreover, there were no cases where we could not prescribe an s-TKI to a patient who was considered suitable for this treatment (e.g. for reimbursement issues). The small sample size does not allow definitive conclusions to be drawn regarding the safety and efficacy of s-TKIs. However, the survival and toxicity rates we found are similar to those observed in larger studies, and therefore, it seems that our real-life data can be considered reliable. Importantly, in our patient series, there were no treatment-related deaths.
In conclusion, efficacy and risk of treatment-related adverse events are central to the decision to initiate or not a systemic treatment with TKIs. It is possible that for patients with contraindications to angiogenesis inhibitors or for those who prefer not to worsen their quality of life, this balance will end up in postponing the initiation of antiangiogenic MKIs ( , ). In this context, the possibility of using s-TKIs that have a better safety profile and maintain satisfactory therapeutic efficacy may be a viable option. In our study, no patient who was treated with s-TKIs because of pre-existing comorbidities or tumour-associated bleeding risk showed adverse events that required treatment discontinuation. Importantly, none of these patients had to delay the initiation of therapy, which was administered at the full starting dose in all patients. However, s-TKIs may cause serious adverse effects ( ), indicating a careful patient monitoring as for the antiangiogenic MKIs. Prospective studies are needed to clarify which is the best therapeutic sequence among kinase inhibitors to be adopted.
