## Background
The tumor microenvironment (TME) exerts a profound influence on the progression of cancer cells. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), the most abundant cell population within the TME, exhibit a complex, dual role. On the one hand, TAMs promote inflammation and help eliminate cancer cells; on the other hand, they often adopt an anti-inflammatory role that contributes to the evolution of cancer cells. Furthermore, the programmed cell death protein 1/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) signaling pathway plays a critical role in both the adaptive and innate immune responses. This study aims to understand the roles of TAMs and the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling network in esophageal cancer.

## Methods
We conducted a systematic review of published data using the Medline (PubMed), Scopus, and Cochrane databases. We included articles that investigated the coexistence of TAMs and the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway in esophageal cancer. Studies that evaluated the clinical prognosis of patients with elevated levels of PD-1-positive TAMs were also incorporated.

## Results
Six articles comprising a total of 822 patients were included in the review. The data indicate a positive correlation between PD-L1 expression and TAMs infiltration. Additionally, patients with high levels of PD-1-positive TAMs tend to have a worse prognosis compared with those with lower levels.

## Conclusions
TAMs play a crucial role in regulating the PD-1/PD-L1 network and the progression of esophageal cancer. Further studies are necessary, however, to clarify the roles of TAMs and the PD-1/PD-L1 network in esophageal cancers.

## Introduction
Esophageal cancer is one of the most aggressive types of cancer, with a 5-year overall survival rate of only 21.6%. Specifically, esophageal cancer is the seventh most frequently diagnosed cancer and the sixth leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. , , Interestingly, the incidence rates of esophageal cancer vary significantly across different geographical regions, with certain high-incidence areas commonly referred to as the ‘esophageal cancer belt.’ The regions with the highest incidence include northern Iran, Central Asia, northern China, southern and eastern Africa, and northern France. Conversely, North America has the lowest incidence of diagnosed esophageal cancer cases. 
Esophageal cancer presents in two histological types: squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and adenocarcinoma, each characterized by unique etiology, pathology, tumor location, treatment approaches, and prognosis. Esophageal adenocarcinoma is typically located in the lower esophagus, while SCC is found at or above the tracheal bifurcation. SCC is associated with a poorer prognosis and is characterized by earlier lymphatic spread. , In recent years, there has been significant progress in the treatment of esophageal cancer with the use of both targeted therapy and immunotherapy. 
Identifying and targeting the molecular subtypes of esophageal cancer has led to the development of new treatments. For instance, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-positive status has resulted in the introduction of anti-HER2 therapy. Specifically, the ToGA trial was the first randomized, prospective phase III trial to evaluate the efficacy and safety of trastuzumab, a HER2 inhibitor, in HER2-overexpressing advanced gastric and esophagogastric junction adenocarcinoma. Furthermore, the phase II HERXO trial evaluates the addition of chemotherapy to trastuzumab in first-line therapy for patients with HER2 overexpression. As a result of these trials, the first-line therapy for these patients is trastuzumab combined with chemotherapy. , , 
Moreover, there has been a remarkable increase in the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) across different types of cancers. ICIs have been shown to improve survival rates not only in patients with microsatellite instability (MSI), but also in patients with microsatellite stability (MSS). For patients whose tumors harbor MSI-H, pembrolizumab is the approved ICI. Based on the phase III CheckMate 648 and CheckMate 649 trials, the combination of nivolumab and ipilimumab has been approved as first-line therapy for patients with both SCC and adenocarcinoma. , 
Other types of therapies, such as anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) treatments, also play a role in the treatment of esophagogastric adenocarcinoma. For instance, ramucirumab, a VEGFR2 antibody, is approved as a subsequent therapy option after progression following first-line chemotherapy. , Additionally, there are several targeted therapies approved for the subsequent treatment of esophageal cancer, such as dabrafenib and trametinib for patients with BRAF V600E mutations, and selpercatinib for tumors with RET gene fusions. , , 
Moreover, advancements in understanding the immune system and its role in cancer progression have led to identifying other molecular markers that influence prognosis. Tumor cells are adapted to evade immune surveillance, which in turn is essential for their survival, progression, and metastasis. Tumor cells achieve this by suppressing the antitumor immune response through the release of various soluble factors, which in turn attract and influence the differentiation and activation of stromal cells to support tumor growth. , 
The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a vital role in cancer development, with various cell types within the TME contributing to cancer progression. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which appear to be the most abundant cell type in the TME, influence cancer progression. TAMs exhibit a range of phenotypes, spanning a spectrum of phenotypic variation, with the M1 and M2 types representing the two extremes. , , ,

On one hand, the M1 type is pro-inflammatory and responsible for the destruction of cancer cells, while on the other hand, the M2 type is anti-inflammatory and promotes tumor progression. , , More specifically, the M1-like phenotype is induced by toll-like receptor ligands, such as bacterial lipopolysaccharide, or by Th1 cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), and colony-stimulating factor 2 (CSF2). M1-like macrophages have a high antigen-presenting capacity and secrete reactive oxygen species (ROS) as well as cytokines like interleukin 6 (IL-6), IL-12, IL-23, and TNF-α, which are linked to microbicidal and pro-inflammatory activities. Because of these functions, they are often referred to as ‘fight’ macrophages and are associated with a favorable prognosis in the context of cancer. , , , 
On the other hand, M2-like macrophages are polarized by Th2-derived cytokines such as IL-4, IL-10, IL-13, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), or prostaglandin E2. These macrophages are often referred to as ‘repair’ or ‘fix’ macrophages due to their role in promoting tissue repair through immune tolerance, tissue remodeling, debris scavenging, and immune modulation. In the context of cancer, M2-like macrophages support angiogenesis by secreting adrenomedullin and vascular endothelial growth factors and express immunosuppressive molecules like IL-10, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), and TGF-β, which contribute to tumor growth. Therefore, they are considered ‘friends’ of cancer cells. , , 
 shows how macrophages polarize across two different spectrums. 
Immune checkpoints are also expressed on the surface of TAMs, with programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) being present on TAMs and interacting with PD-L1 on cancer cells. The interaction between PD-1 and its ligands (PD-L1/PD-L2) is critical for self-recognition and immune regulation, preventing immune cells from attacking normal tissues. This mechanism, however, can also be exploited by cancer cells to evade immune detection and promote tumor survival. , 
When this interaction occurs, two tyrosine residues within an immunosuppressive motif in the intracellular domain of TAMs are phosphorylated. This leads to the phosphorylation of two tyrosine phosphatases, SHP1 and SHP2. Subsequently, dephosphorylation of myosin IIA occurs, resulting in the deactivation of TAMs. , For this reason, it is important to understand how TAMs are polarized and their relationship with PD-1/PD-L1 pathway.
The aim of this review is to examine the relationship between the infiltration of TAMs and the expression of PD-L1 in esophageal cancer cells, and to investigate the prognosis of patients in relation to the level of PD-1-positive TAMs expression.

## Methods

## Search strategy
We used a deliberately broad search strategy to ensure comprehensive coverage of all studies examining the relationship between TAMs and the PD-1/PD-L1 axis. Our search algorithm included common synonyms related to esophageal cancer, TAMs, and the PD-1/PD-L1 axis. The algorithm used was as follows: (oesophageal OR oesophagous OR esophageal OR esophageal adenocarcinoma) AND (cancer OR carcinoma OR neoplasm OR tumor) AND (TAMs OR tumor-associated macrophages OR macrophages OR tumor-infiltrating macrophages) AND (PD-1 OR PD-L1 OR PDL1 OR PD1 OR programmed cell death protein 1 OR programmed death ligand 1). We applied this algorithm to three databases (Medline/PubMed, Scopus, and Cochrane) to ensure comprehensive retrieval of all relevant literature for our study.

## Study inclusion and exclusion criteria
We included all articles that involved studies with more than one esophageal cancer patient and investigated the correlation between the presence of TAMs and PD-L1 expression in cancer cells. Studies assessing the prognosis of patients with PD-1-positive TAMs were also eligible for review. Conversely, we excluded case reports, studies involving non-human tissues, and any studies where the full text was not available in English.

## Article selection and data extraction
Initially, we screened titles and abstracts, excluding articles that did not meet our criteria. Following this, a detailed review of the remaining articles was carried out to assess their relevance and suitability for our study ( ). The extracted data were checked for accuracy and entered into an electronic database, which included details such as the paper title, author, publication year, patient gender, mean and median age, sample size, histological type, T-stage, presence of infiltrated lymph nodes, N-stage, metastasis status, AJCC 8th edition staging, systemic therapy, type of surgery, patient follow-up, and TAMs and PD-L1 cross tables.

## Correlation of TAMs and PD-L1
All of the identified articles reported a correlation between TAM infiltration and PD-L1 expression. Whether these subtle differences in TAMs expression play a crucial role in immune targeting or immune escaping, and how they might influence the outcomes of immunotherapy, remains an important area of research. Currently, there are no prospective randomized clinical data available to provide insights into this relationship. Further studies are needed to elucidate the implications of TAM expression in the context of immunotherapy effectiveness and patient responses. , , , 
In the study by Chen et al., the PD-1H was highly expressed in macrophages (Rho 0.155, P value = 0.038). Moreover, in the study by Jeremiasen et al., significant associations were observed between CD163-positive macrophage infiltration and PD-L1 expression on tumor cells (P value = 0.035), as well as between MARCO-positive macrophages and PD-L1 expression on both tumor and immune cells (P value = 0.009). Finally, in the study by Wu et al., the PD-L1 expression was detected in 33.33% of patients, primarily showing a stroma-only pattern. Moreover, this expression was positively associated with CD163-positive cells (P value < 0.001). In 74.07% of PD-L1-positive specimens, PD-L1-positive CD163-positive cells were more frequently colocalized with CD4-positive T cells than with CD8-positive T cells. Additionally, 83.95% of all specimens had a greater infiltration of CD4-positive T cells compared with CD8-positive T cells. Although in the study by Hatogai et al., the correlation between PD-L1 and CD204-positive/CD68-positive macrophages was not statistically significant (P value = 0.470), it was the only study that did not yield a result.
It is believed that TAMs are recruited from both tissue-specific embryonic macrophages and resident macrophages derived from monocytes, with the latter significantly contributing to the overall population of TAMs. Macrophages can be differentiated based on elevated levels of various cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and other signals from tumor and stromal cells. M1 macrophages secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-12, TNF-α, chemokine ligand 1 (CXCL-10), and IFN-γ. They also produce high levels of nitric oxide synthase, which metabolizes arginine to nitric oxide. In contrast, M2 macrophages secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-4, IL-10, and IL-13, and they express elevated levels of arginase-1, CD206, and CD163. , 
Finally, M2-TAMs secrete high levels of TGF-β1, activating the TGFβR1-Smad2/3 pathway, which promotes and maintains the stemness characteristics of esophageal SCC cells, leading to decreased sensitivity to cisplatin. These patients showed poorer prognoses and increased expression of stemness markers. , 

## Prognosis of PD-1-positive TAMs
In the study by Li et al. it was found that PD-1 is expressed in TAMs, and there is a correlation between PD-1 expression in TAMs and survival. Specifically, a positive association with disease progression was observed. Similarly, the study by Lu et al. concluded that PD-1 high TAMs were associated with a worse prognosis compared with PD-1-negative TAMs. PD-1 high TAMs were found to have significantly shorter median disease-free survival (DFS) and median overall survival (mOS) (mDFS: 13.8 versus 10.2 months, P < 0.0001; mOS: 25.3 versus 19.0 months, P < 0.0001). The study also suggested that PD-1 expression in TAMs could potentially serve as a prognostic factor. 
Additionally, an analysis of cytokine expression in TAMs revealed that PD-1-positive TAMs exhibit higher levels of CD206 and lower levels of CD64 and HLA-DR compared with PD-1-TAMs. Tumor-derived exosomes were found to be responsible for the up-regulation of PD-1 and CD206, along with the reduction of expression of CD64 and HLA-DR in monocytes. PD-1-positive TAMs were also shown to express elevated levels of the M2 marker IL-10 and lower levels of the M1 marker IL-12. 
The study by Lu et al. discovered that CD68-positive PD-1-positive TAMs are present at higher levels in esophageal cancer cells compared with normal cells. These TAMs expressed higher levels of M2 markers, indicating that they predominantly exhibit the M2 phenotype. 
In the study by Li et al., an investigation into the biological function of TAMs concluded that PD-1-positive TAMs impair CD8-positive T-cell proliferation. Additionally, an investigation on the role of high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) in the induction of PD-1-positive TAMs in esophageal cancer cells found that HMGB1 expression was significantly higher in esophageal SCC compared with normal cells. Specifically, exposure of monocytes to HMGB1 resulted in increased CD206 expression, decreased HLA-DR and CD64 expression, enhanced suppressive function, up-regulated IL-10 expression, and reduction of IL-12 expression. 
Moreover, it was discovered that CD68-positive PD-1-positive TAMs are present at higher levels in esophageal cancer cells compared with normal cells, expressing higher levels of M2 markers and predominantly exhibiting the M2 phenotype. Following this discovery, the study examined the use of anti-PD-1 therapy in regulating TAMs. TAMs were found to express various phagocytosis-related proteins, including MERTK, AXL, TIMD4, and MARCO, along with enhanced phagocytic activity due to the increased expression of these proteins. Finally, Lu et al. concluded that anti-PD-1 treatment can redirect cultured macrophages from an M2 to an M1 phenotype.

## Discussion
TME is a key factor in the progression of cancer cells, and further research into its influence is essential. TAMs, which are the macrophages in TME, are the most abundant cell population and their role is complex. TAMs can be either polarized as M1 phenotype and be pro-inflammatory, which kills cancer cells, or may be polarized as M2 phenotype, which is anti-inflammatory and contribute to the evolution of cancer cells. Unfortunately, TAMs appear to be predominantly polarized toward the M2 phenotype, which contributes to more aggressive and detrimental tumor behavior.
In one of our previous reviews, we investigated the role of TAMs and PD-1 networking in gastric cancer. In this review, we go further, evaluating the role in other cancers of the upper gastrointestinal tract, more specifically esophageal cancer, a very aggressive type of cancer. Here, we discovered that there are very few relevant studies about this role in esophageal cancer. We identified only four articles that investigated the correlation of the infiltration of TAMs and the expression of PD-L1 in esophageal cancer cells. The studies found a positive correlation, which, unfortunately, had a negative impact on patient survival. , , , Moreover, in this review, we identified articles that examined the prognosis of patients whose cancer expressed PD-1-positive TAMs. We identified only two relevant articles on PD-1-positive TAM expression, representing a very small sample of patients for such an important clinical question, with the prognosis of those patients to be poor. , 
Specifically, the function of TAMs is regulated by two categories of signals. The first category includes ‘eat me’ signals that activate macrophage phagocytosis. In contrast, the second category consists of ‘do not eat me’ signals, which include PD-1. ‘Eat me’ signals on tumor cells include antigens recognized by Fc receptors (FcRs), calreticulin interacting with LRP1 on phagocytes, and SLAMF7, although the mechanism of SLAMF7 remains unclear. Conversely, tumor cells evade phagocytosis by using ‘don’t eat me’ signals such as CD47, PD-L1, and B2M, which bind to receptors like SIRPα, PD-1, and LILRB1 on phagocytes. is a schematic representation of ‘eat me’ and ‘don’t eat me’ signals. Therapeutic antibodies targeting these pathways are being explored as potential cancer immunotherapy strategies. These signals serve to inhibit macrophage phagocytosis, as those two studies showed that the expression of PD-1 in TAMs eliminates the phagocytosis and provokes the evolution of cancer cells. , , 
Therefore, the use of TAMs as prognostic and diagnostic biomarkers offers significant potential. TAMs have already shown considerable effectiveness as diagnostic markers in various cancers, including multiple myeloma, breast cancer, prostate cancer, and pancreatic cancer. Additionally, TAMs appear to be promising prognostic indicators, providing valuable insights into patient outcomes in lung cancer, esophageal SCC, and bladder cancer. , , As prognostic biomarkers, this will be helpful in the decision of therapy or not and also to the decision of what therapy may be better. , , , , , Moreover, the studies show that the anti-PD-1 therapy may be beneficial not only for the inhibition of immune checkpoints of T-cells but also to the repolarization of TAMs in order to be more efficacious to compete with the cancer cells. , , 
It will be very promising if there is a potential to find out therapeutic strategies which focus on targeting TAMs infiltration. These approaches generally aim to block TAMs recruitment to tumors, either by inhibiting their recruitment or promoting their direct elimination. Additionally, some strategies seek to reprogram TAMs from a pro-tumorigenic polarization to enhance their antitumor functions. , , 
As a result, several phase I and II clinical trials are currently underway. It is essential to deepen our understanding of the role of TAMs and their relationship with PD-1/PD-L1 to develop effective treatments. , , For instance, a MATINS trial which is a phase I/II trial evaluates the role of bexmarilimab. Bexmarilimab inhibits Clever-1 and this leads to the activation of macrophages and after that to the activation of the adaptive immune system. Moreover, there are few trials that evaluate the combination therapy to have a better outcome. 
The initial aim of this review was to carry out a statistical meta-analysis of the available data. Due to the heterogeneity of the articles and the absence of precise data relevant to our question, however, this analysis was not feasible. Consequently, it is essential to conduct new research that accurately compares PD-L1 expression, TAMs infiltration, and the prognosis associated with PD-1-positive TAMs.

## Conclusion
To conclude, the correlation between the infiltration of TAMs and the expression of PD-L1, as well as the expression of PD-1 in TAMs, appears to play an important role in the progression of cancer cells. Moreover, these factors may serve as both prognostic indicators and treatment targets. Relevant data are limited, however, and more studies must be conducted to understand how TAMs function and their relationship with the PD-1/PD-L1 network.
