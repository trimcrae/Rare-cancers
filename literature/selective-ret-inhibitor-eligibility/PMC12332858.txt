## Abstract
Phase 3 trials of neoadjuvant, perioperative, and adjuvant immune checkpoint inhibitors combined with chemotherapy (ICI-CT) in resectable early-stage NSCLC (eNSCLC) have reported that all three approaches confer an event-free or disease-free survival benefit over CT alone, with acceptable safety profiles. All three strategies are approved standards of care for eNSCLC. This review provides a detailed analysis of these phase 3 ICI-CT trials and addresses the considerations regarding the selection of each approach, including protocol schema and baseline patient and tumor differences, preoperative staging, surgical outcomes, efficacy end points, safety, treatment disposition, and the programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) efficacy biomarker. The differences between regimens and study populations among these ICI-CT trials hamper cross-trial comparisons and highlight the need for head-to-head trials. Patients achieving pathologic complete response with neoadjuvant ICI-CT have better survival outcomes irrespective of subsequent treatment, but the optimal number of preoperative ICI-CT cycles needed to achieve pathologic complete response has not been defined. The choice between a neoadjuvant or perioperative versus adjuvant treatment approach involves a risk-benefit assessment of the potential for preoperative attrition to surgery, postoperative attrition to ICI-CT, and the anticipated toxicity profile. Current limitations of invasive lymph node staging mean that adjuvant ICI remains an important treatment strategy, but preoperative node staging is imperative. Future studies that identify the safety and toxicity contributions of each treatment phase in perioperative trials will confirm whether a pre- or postoperative ICI approach is superior, whether there is added benefit to adjuvant after neoadjuvant ICI-CT, and which patients will benefit the most from each approach.
Keywords: Early-stage lung cancer, Immune checkpoint inhibitors plus chemotherapy, PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitor therapy, Pre- and postoperative treatment, Treatment strategy

## Introduction
Phase 3 cancer immunotherapy trials in resectable early-stage (eNSCLC) involve three distinct treatment approaches: neoadjuvant (preoperative), perioperative (pre- and postoperative), and adjuvant (postoperative). Regimens involving immune checkpoint inhibitors combined with chemotherapy (ICI-CT) have been approved for each approach ( ). With the lack of comparative head-to-head phase 3 trials, the debate regarding the optimal approach continues.
 This comprehensive review provides a detailed analysis of the phase 3 ICI-CT trials in eNSCLC and addresses multiple considerations regarding the selection of each approach: protocol schema and patient and tumor differences, efficacy end points (survival and pathologic regression), surgical outcomes (preoperative attrition to surgery, complete resection rates, and resection extent), safety, treatment disposition, and molecular- and biomarkers of efficacy.

## Protocol Schema and Baseline Patient and Tumor Differences Among Immunotherapy Trials
Key notable differences in ICI-CT duration (number of cycles), sequence, and timing relative to surgery define each treatment approach. The neoadjuvant and perioperative studies involved concurrent ICI-CT; the adjuvant trials evaluated sequential CT followed by ICI.
The neoadjuvant and perioperative trials entailed preoperative concurrent ICI-CT for up to 3 (neoadjuvant) or 3–4 cycles (perioperative trials) ( ). , , , , The perioperative trials also required up to 1 year of adjuvant ICI. The neoadjuvant-only CheckMate 816 study included an additional dual ICI, CT-free cohort (ipilimumab and nivolumab) ; however, the role of this regimen in eNSCLC treatment is unclear, and it is included primarily for completeness. The results of three global (AEGEAN, KEYNOTE-671, , and CheckMate77T ) and two Chinese perioperative trials (Neotorch and RATIONALE-315 ) have been reported ( , ). IMpower030 (NCT03456063) is another ongoing phase 3 perioperative ICI trial with pending results.
 In the global adjuvant ICI trials, 1 year of ICI was given after four or fewer cycles of CT. At least one CT cycle was mandatory in IMpower010 ; CT was optional in KEYNOTE-091 and BR.31 but was received by most patients. IMpower010 and KEYNOTE-671 required cisplatin-based CT, whereas CT was selected by the investigators in the other phase 3 trials. Additional phase 3 adjuvant ICI trials with pending results are ANVIL (NCT02595944), ALCHEMIST Chemo-IO (NCT04267848), NADIM-ADJUVANT (NCT04564157), and LungMate-008 (NCT04772287). 
The study populations differed among the trials. Patients with known classic EGFR-mutated (L858R or exon 19-deleted) and ALK-rearranged tumors were generally excluded per protocol except from KEYNOTE-671, IMpower010, and KEYNOTE-091 ( ). Notably, patients with planned pneumonectomy or clinical invasive T4 at screening were excluded only from AEGEAN. The patients’ disease and tumor characteristics also differed between the pre- and postoperative ICI trials ( ). The neoadjuvant and perioperative trials included fewer patients with nonsquamous and stage III NSCLC than the adjuvant ICI trials, and Neotorch reported outcomes in stage III patients only. Finally, the definition of tumor resectability for stage III NSCLC substages remains ill-defined. These variations not only confound the comparability of results across trials, but also fuel the debate regarding which patients are the best candidates for preoperative ICI-CT versus chemoradiation with consolidation ICI.
The timing of treatment arm randomization within the trial schema also resulted in the selection of different patient populations. The adjuvant trial design inherently selected fitter patients because randomization occurred after complete resection (R0) and after CT in IMpower010 and BR.31. In contrast, randomization occurred before preoperative ICI-CT in the neoadjuvant and perioperative trials. In addition, perioperative study designs have limitations, including preoperative attrition to surgery and patients’ inability to start or complete the planned adjuvant therapy.
When the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Oncologic Drugs Advisory Committee reviewed the perioperative AEGEAN trial data in July 2024, concerns were raised that the trial design did not allow for individual evaluation of the two treatment phases and that overtreatment with perioperative regimens might result in unnecessary toxicities without additional clinical benefit. The FDA voted unanimously to request assessments of the efficacy contribution of each treatment phase in NSCLC perioperative regimens going forward, using alternate, multiarm, sequential multiple assignment randomized, and rerandomization study designs. , Future trials will likely require three or four arms to exhibit treatment phase-specific contributions to efficacy and toxicity. 

## Efficacy in Neoadjuvant, Perioperative, and Adjuvant ICI Trials
The efficacy differences seen between the pre- and postoperative ICI-CT approaches are likely to reflect the differences between the study designs, patient populations, and regimens, underlining the need for head-to-head trials comparing treatment approaches.
The primary efficacy data from the eNSCLC phase 3 ICI-CT trials are summarized in , with longer follow-up data detailed in . Perioperative KEYNOTE-671 was the only phase 3 trial in eNSCLC to include overall survival (OS) as a co-primary end point ( ) and is the only study to date to have reported a statistically significant OS benefit (hazard ratio [HR] 0.72; p = 0.0052 at the 3-year update) ( ). However, KEYNOTE-671 also had the shortest median event-free survival (EFS) in the control arm of all the studies. Nevertheless, the Kaplan-Meier OS curves for the active and control treatment arms seem to separate after 12 to 18 months in many of these trials; in some, the OS differences between treatment arms may reach statistical significance after longer follow-up (see versus ). After 5 years of follow-up, the final analysis of CheckMate-816 revealed that OS was significantly improved (p = 0.0451) with neoadjuvant ICI-CT versus CT alone ( ). Positive OS trends were also observed in the adjuvant IMpower010 study’s programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)–positive population at the 5-year follow-up (stratified HR 0.77 [95% confidence interval 0.56–1.06]), with an even stronger trend seen in the PD-L1 greater than or equal to 50% group (unstratified HR 0.44 [95% confidence interval 0.25–0.74]) ( ). Nevertheless, until head-to-head findings emerge, the primary end point data from completed phase 3 ICI-CT trials in eNSCLC can be reviewed by treatment approach.

## Neoadjuvant Versus Adjuvant ICI-CT Approach
In melanoma, preoperative ICI has led to superior survival outcomes over postoperative ICI in phase 1B, 2, and 3 , clinical trials. Neoadjuvant ICI also expanded more antitumor T-cell clones than adjuvant ICI. These findings provide evidence that preoperative ICI may elicit a better immune response than postoperative ICI because of the intact primary tumor and lymph nodes. However, trials comparing pre- versus postoperative ICI have not yet been performed in eNSCLC, precluding definitive conclusions regarding the superior approach. The neoadjuvant CheckMate 816 trial and the perioperative KEYNOTE-671 trial have both reported statistically significant OS differences between CT-ICI and CT alone (p = 0.048 and p = 0.0052, respectively). As such, the controversy regarding whether adjuvant ICI after neoadjuvant CT-ICI is beneficial remains unresolved. To determine the individual contributions of the different treatment phases to overall efficacy, the phase 3 SWOG-Alliance PROSPECT-LUNG study in resectable stage II to IIIB NSCLC will randomize patients preoperatively to receive perioperative versus adjuvant ICI. 
In primary analyses of the neoadjuvant and perioperative ICI-CT trials, the EFS HRs exhibited the greatest benefit in the PD-L1 greater than or equal to 50% subgroups (0.24–0.71, compared with 0.65-0.85 in PD-L1 <1% subgroups) ( ). Although Neotorch had the lowest EFS HR among the intention-to-treat populations (0.40) ( ), these data were from stage III patients only. 
In the adjuvant trials, the respective disease-free survival (DFS) HRs and DFS rates for PD-L1 all-comers at the primary analysis cutoff dates were 0.76 and 73% at 1.5 years in KEYNOTE-091, 0.79 and 70% at 2 years in IMpower010, and 0.89 and 67% at 2 years in BR.31. The BR.31 trial of adjuvant durvalumab versus placebo after CT did not meet its primary end point. Adjuvant ICI efficacy was most apparent in the PD-L1–positive cohort of IMpower010 (DFS HR 0.66) : 5-year DFS rates were 53% and 65% in the PD-L1 greater than or equal to 1% and greater than or equal to 50% subgroups, respectively ( ). By contrast, DFS improvement versus placebo did not correlate with increasing PD-L1 expression in KEYNOTE-091 or BR.31 ( ). Overall, these data highlight the unmet need for ICI combination strategies that have better efficacy in PD-L1–negative eNSCLC subgroups.

## Perioperative Versus Neoadjuvant ICI Approach
Subgroup analyses of neoadjuvant versus perioperative ICI approaches also exhibited efficacy differences between patient subgroups. The ICI-CT arm of CheckMate816 included patient cohorts that might potentially have received less benefit from neoadjuvant ICI-CT, such as those with American Joint Committee on Cancer seventh edition TNM stage IB–II NSCLC (EFS HR 0.87), and tumor cell PD-L1 expression less than 1% (EFS HR 0.85) ( ). In the global perioperative trials (AEGEAN, KEYNOTE-671, and CheckMate77T ), the EFS HRs in the subgroups that had performed poorly in CheckMate 816 suggest that these patients seemed to benefit from neoadjuvant and adjuvant ICI compared with control. EFS HRs ranged from 0.65 to 0.81 in stage II (eighth edition), 0.46 to 0.71 in the squamous cell carcinoma histologic structure, and 0.73 to 0.77 in the PD-L1 less than 1% subgroups. It remains an open question whether the improvements in EFS HRs over CheckMate 816 seen in all three global perioperative trials reflect an impact of adjuvant ICI.
Pathologic complete response (pCR) may potentially be a surrogate end point of survival and, thus, reflects the therapeutic efficacy of the neoadjuvant phase of a perioperative study. However, tumor sampling methodology (i.e., whole versus partial tumor bed sampling to determine pCR), which is rarely described in detail, may influence the results, and potentially confound the interpretation of this efficacy end point. Nevertheless, patients who achieved pCR after neoadjuvant ICI-CT had better survival outcomes, irrespective of subsequent treatment. , , 
The EFS Kaplan-Meier curves in the neoadjuvant and perioperative studies , exhibited varying levels of separation between ICI-CT and CT, suggesting that the adjuvant ICI phase may be most beneficial in patients who do not achieve pCR. However, only a study comparing outcomes with both treatments in patients with and without pCR, such as the planned phase 3 CLEAR-INSIGHT trial, can answer this question definitively. Using four arms, CLEAR-INSIGHT will stratify patients by pCR status after standard-of-care neoadjuvant ICI-CT and R0, then randomize the pCR cohort to adjuvant ICI versus surveillance and the nonPCR cohort to adjuvant ICI versus combination ICI. This direct comparison of perioperative versus neoadjuvant ICI stratified by pathologic response will reveal whether adding adjuvant to neoadjuvant ICI will prolong survival, and whether nonpCR patients will benefit most from the escalation in adjuvant treatment.
A post hoc patient-matched analysis of CheckMate 816 patients who completed surgery versus CheckMate 77T patients who completed surgery and greater than or equal to one cycle of adjuvant ICI could not provide definitive answers regarding the benefit of adjuvant after neoadjuvant ICI. The analysis excluded the patients in CheckMate 77T who survived surgery but were too ill for adjuvant ICI, possibly biasing the results in favor of CheckMate 77T.

## Pathologic Regression as an Efficacy End point
The optimal number of preoperative ICI-CT cycles needed to achieve pCR has not been defined. In CheckMate 816, with 3 or fewer cycles of neoadjuvant ICI in the ICI-CT arm, the pCR rate was 24%, a 22% difference from CT alone ( ). In the global perioperative trials (AEGEAN, KEYNOTE-671, and CheckMate77T ), 4 or fewer cycles of neoadjuvant ICI-CT were given, and the pCR rates (17%–25%, a 13%–21% difference versus CT) were similar to that in CheckMate 816. In the neoadjuvant phase 2 neoSCORE study of two versus three cycles of sintilimab-CT, the pCR rates were 19% versus 24%, respectively (p = 0.66). 
Along with establishing the optimal number of ICI cycles that yields the highest pCR rates, given that approximately 80% of patients do not achieve a pCR with currently approved neoadjuvant ICI-CT, improving on this outcome will be important in future trials of novel neoadjuvant ICI combinations.

## Surgical Outcomes

## Preoperative Attrition to Surgery
Preoperative attrition to surgery—the proportion of patients not undergoing intended primary lung cancer resection—is scrutinized in neoadjuvant and perioperative ICI trials. In the neoadjuvant and perioperative trials reviewed here, the preoperative rates of attrition to surgery were 16% to 22% in the ICI-CT arms and 19% to 27% in the CT arms ( ). , , , , , , , This rate is not substantially higher than the 4% to 18% of patients who did not undergo surgery in historical neoadjuvant CT-only phase 2 and 3 trials ( ). Hence, preoperative attrition to surgery occurs regardless of the intended pre- or postoperative systemic therapy. In the phase 3 NATCH trial of preoperative versus adjuvant CT, patients were randomized preoperatively either to surgery alone, neoadjuvant CT followed by surgery, or surgery then adjuvant CT; surgery attrition rates were 9.5%, 9.1%, and 9.6% in the respective arms. 
 Reasons for preoperative attrition include adverse events (AEs) or disease progression, but these reasons were uncommon in the ICI-CT trials ( ). AEs accounted for 1% to 6% of attrition in the ICI-CT arms versus 0% to 4% in the CT arms, whereas disease progression accounted for 3% to 7% in the ICI-CT arms versus 10% to 15% in the CT arms. Other reasons preventing surgery (e.g., patient refusal, being unfit for surgery, cancelation by the surgeon) occurred in 7% to 12% in the ICI-CT arms versus 8% to 15% in the CT arms of the perioperative ICI trials. , , , , , , , These reasons may represent potentially preventable causes of surgical attrition.
To provide more information about potential surgical complications and, thus, help manage patient care, prospective data collection in clinical trials is needed to assess perihilar and lobar or mediastinal adhesions after neoadjuvant therapy. Proposed scales for intraoperative quantification of surgical complexity after neoadjuvant therapy have been previously recommended by our group. 

## Complete Resection Rates
Tumor and lymph node resectability should ideally be decided before initiating any systemic therapy. Completeness of tumor resection is an important surgical end point, and the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer has described a Resection (R) Classification system. R0 is defined by microscopically cancer-free resection margins, systematic or lobe-specific systematic nodal dissection and the absence of extracapsular lymph nodal cancer and metastasis in the highest mediastinal lymph node station. A revised R classification has been proposed for the ninth edition TNM staging system. 
The R0 rates in the ICI-CT arms of the neoadjuvant and perioperative trials were generally high and were 100% in the adjuvant trials because R0 was an eligibility requirement ( ). Among the neoadjuvant and perioperative trials, CheckMate 816 had the lowest R0 rates. However, the R0 rates in CheckMate 816 varied widely by study site location (North America 65%, Europe 90%, Asia 90%), which may reflect differing adherence to the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer definition of R0. Other explanations may include variations in patient selection or the intent to downstage to make “borderline resectable” disease resectable.

## Extent of Resection (Types of Surgery)
Most surgeries in these ICI trials were lobectomy or pneumonectomy ( ). In CheckMate 816, the frequency of pneumonectomy differed between the preoperative ICI-CT (11%–17%) and CT arms (21%–25%) ( ). However, in the perioperative trials, no appreciable difference in pneumonectomy rates was seen between the preoperative ICI-CT (7%–11%) and CT (8%–14%) arms ( ). Notably, although AEGEAN excluded patients who required pneumonectomy at baseline, a similar proportion of patients in the respective arms (7% and 8%) underwent pneumonectomy.

## Safety
The rate of treatment-related AEs (TRAEs) with preoperative ICI-CT was comparable to that of CT alone ( ). However, ICIs can also lead to a unique set of immune-related adverse events with variable occurrence, time to onset, and duration after treatment cessation. The variable immune-related AE reporting in ICI clinical trials is suboptimal, and a standardized reporting protocol that accounts for tolerability, management, and reversibility is needed to aid safety comparisons of ICI regimens and treatment approaches. 
 Nevertheless, the safety profiles were acceptable across these phase 3 ICI-CT trials, with low discontinuation rates ( ). Both pre- and postoperative ICI-CT had low mortality rates similar to those in control arms. In the perioperative trials, TRAEs were reported separately for both the neoadjuvant and adjuvant periods only for KEYNOTE-671 and CheckMate 77T, making it difficult to conclusively elucidate whether this treatment approach led to increased toxicity compared with neoadjuvant- or adjuvant-only ICI-CT. However, the lower rates of grade 3 or higher all-cause AEs (22%–34%) in the ICI arms of the adjuvant trials , , may reflect CT then ICI given sequentially, compared with ICI-CT given concurrently in the neoadjuvant and perioperative ICI trials, , , , , , , , in which the rates of grade 3 or higher all-cause AEs were 41% to 70%. As new combination treatment strategies are considered, it will be important to understand the TRAE profile by pre- versus postoperative periods and the associated mortality rates.

## Treatment Disposition
One advantage of a preoperative treatment approach is that almost all patients will start—and most will complete—systemic therapy. In the neoadjuvant and perioperative trials reviewed here, preoperative ICI-CT or CT alone was initiated in nearly all patients (98%–100%), and all intended preoperative ICI-CT cycles were completed by ≥75% ( ). By contrast, postoperative ICI with or without CT in the perioperative trials was initiated by a smaller proportion of patients (62%–74%), and all intended postoperative cycles were completed by an even smaller proportion (24%–47%). In the adjuvant trials, almost 100% of patients started postoperative therapy because the trial designs required it after R0; 52% to 65% of patients completed adjuvant ICI.
 As previously mentioned, initiating systemic treatment preoperatively is associated with the risk that some patients may not undergo surgical resection—whichever modality is initiated first is more likely to be completed. Hence, the choice of treatment approach should be considered in light of the anticipated safety profile and the likelihood of initiation and completion of the pre- or postoperative treatment periods.

## Considerations for Selecting a Neoadjuvant, Perioperative, or Adjuvant ICI Approach

## Invasive Staging for N1 and N2 Lymph Nodes (“Surprise N1 and N2” Metastasis)
Based on the Society of Thoracic Surgeons database, the frequency of invasive mediastinal lymph node staging is low—43% for stage IB or higher and 52% for central NSCLC tumors. Large meta-analyses of preoperative endobronchial ultrasound with transbronchial lymph node staging in patients with clinically N0 or N1 NSCLC exhibited only 37% to 49% sensitivity in detecting unsuspected N2 or N3 lymph node metastasis. , Thus, preoperative invasive lymph node staging has limitations that include underutilization and poor sensitivity to detect unexpected (especially N1) nodal metastasis that can lead to postoperative “surprise N1 or N2 disease.” Nevertheless, preoperative invasive mediastinal staging remains imperative for optimal outcomes. Regardless of which treatment approach is superior, adjuvant-only ICI-CT will remain an essential treatment approach because of the underutilization and inadequacies of current invasive lymph node staging techniques. , , 

## Real-World Data on Pre- Versus Postoperative Treatment Disposition
Clinicians may have an inherent preference to perform surgery first, followed by adjuvant systemic therapy. A Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results Program registry study (2010-2015) reported that most patients with stage II (58%), IIIA (58%), and IIIB (43%) NSCLC received adjuvant therapy, compared with neoadjuvant (5%, 8%, and 9%, respectively) or perioperative therapy (6%, 16%, and 10%, respectively). A U.S. Flatiron Health Research Database study (2019–2022) reported that surgery rates were 30% and 10% among stage IIIA and IIIB eNSCLC patients, respectively, and adjuvant treatment was given to 76% and 75% of patients with stage IIIA and IIIB NSCLC, respectively. However, these databases have the limitation of being on the basis of surgical patterns before (or on the cusp of) the approvals for perioperative treatment regimens.
At present, two adjuvant ICI regimens after R0 have been approved: adjuvant atezolizumab in PD-L1-selected stage II to IIIA tumors on the basis of IMpower010 and adjuvant pembrolizumab regardless of PD-L1 status on the basis of KEYNOTE-091. , , Additional adjuvant ICI trials in eNSCLC are ongoing or have pending results ; real-world adjuvant ICI data should also be scrutinized.
Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) was evaluated in KEYNOTE-671. No differences from baseline in patient-reported outcome scores were observed between treatment arms in the neoadjuvant or the adjuvant phases. This suggests that, compared with neoadjuvant CT, and surgery alone, adding perioperative pembrolizumab maintained HRQoL in both the neoadjuvant and adjuvant setting. Apart from these findings, more evidence on the impact of adjuvant ICI on HRQoL from current and future trials may be a valuable addition to understanding the impact on patients.

## Systemic Therapy Attrition
CT has a role in eNSCLC treatment, regardless of whether concomitant ICI is given pre- or postoperatively. However, CT is more likely to be completed when given preoperatively, as reported by the NATCH trial, in which preoperative CT was received by 97% and postoperative CT by 66% of intention-to-treat study population patients—an attrition rate of 31%. Phase 2 trials of neoadjuvant ICI monotherapy versus combined ICI-CT reported superior rates of objective response, major pathologic response, and pCR with ICI-CT versus ICI alone. In KEYNOTE-091, the importance of CT for optimal adjuvant ICI efficacy was suggested by the DFS HR of 1.25 in the 14% of patients who received adjuvant pembrolizumab without CT. , Previously, in the era of CT, oncologists, and patients could choose to opt out of systemic treatment because of the poorer risk-benefit ratio, which led to a greater potential for not initiating adjuvant CT. With ICI, the risk-benefit ratio has improved, and patients should be encouraged to comply with treatment if adjuvant therapy is the chosen therapeutic approach.

## PD-L1 as an Efficacy Biomarker
Tumor cell PD-L1 expression is an important biomarker of ICI efficacy. In the neoadjuvant CheckMate-816 trial, EFS HRs improved as the level of PD-L1 expression increased: 0.85 (PD-L1 <1%), 0.58 (PD-L1 1%–49%), and 0.24 (PD-L1 ≥50%) ( ). In the perioperative trials, not all studies exhibited this trend and EFS HRs ranged from 0.65 to 0.80 (PD-L1 <1%), 0.31 to 0.76 (PD-L1 1%–49%), and 0.26 to 0.71 (PD-L1 ≥50%). , , , A meta-analysis of 43 trials of neoadjuvant therapy that included more than 5400 patients with resectable NSCLC found that neoadjuvant ICI-CT was superior to neoadjuvant CT across surgical, pathologic, and efficacy outcomes. Patients with tumor PD-L1 levels less than 1% had an EFS benefit with neoadjuvant ICI-CT versus neoadjuvant CT, but this did not translate into an OS benefit. The EFS benefit was even greater in patients with PD-L1 levels of 1% to 49% and greater than or equal to 50%. A limitation of this meta-analysis is that it also included perioperative trials and therefore some patients received adjuvant therapy, which could have confounded the EFS and OS findings.
In the adjuvant IMpower010 trial, the OS and DFS HRs improved as the PD-L1 expression level increased ( , ). The OS HR directly correlated with the PD-L1 expression level in stage II–IIIA patients, with the 5-year analysis revealing OS HRs of 0.47 in patients with PD-L1 greater than or equal to 50% and 0.77 in those with PD-L1 greater than or equal to 1%. In contrast, PD-L1 performed inconsistently in the adjuvant KEYNOTE-091 and BR.31 trials. Although pathologic responses and long-term OS were independent of PD-L1 expression in some phase 2 neoadjuvant ICI trials, , tumor cell PD-L1 expression remains the most relevant biomarker of ICI efficacy at present.

## Driver Mutations or Alterations and Molecular Testing
EGFR and ALK alterations and PD-L1 status should be tested whenever systemic treatment is considered for eNSCLC. , Among the phase 3 ICI-CT trials in eNSCLC, the AEGEAN, KEYNOTE-671, IMpower010, and KEYNOTE-091 studies included patients with known EGFR or ALK alterations, but their small numbers compared with those having wild-type EGFR or ALK hamper meaningful interpretation of EFS and DFS in these subgroups. , , , The compelling benefits in DFS with adjuvant alectinib for ALK-rearranged NSCLC (ALINA trial) and OS with adjuvant osimertinib for EGFR L858R or exon 19–deleted eNSCLC (ADAURA) indicate the importance of testing these biomarkers before systemic treatments are started and that these agents, rather than ICI, should be used to treat eNSCLC with these alterations.
Other driver mutations or alterations with known poor responses to ICI-CT should be tested in eNSCLC because more data are needed to identify patients who have biomarkers or driver mutations that will respond to ICI in the optimal setting. Phase 3 adjuvant targeted therapy trials such as LIBRETTO-432 (selpercatinib), APEX (aumolertinib), and FORWARD (furmonertinib; NCT04853342) are ongoing.
The current platforms used to evaluate circulating tumor DNA in these neoadjuvant or adjuvant ICI studies had suboptimal negative predictive value, and the data , do not support de-escalating ICI on the basis of circulating tumor DNA results.

## Medical Operability and Surgical Resectability
Thoracic surgeons and other specialists assess two key factors: the patient's ability to undergo surgery (medical operability) and whether the tumor and lymph nodes can be successfully removed (surgical resectability). Medical operability is determined by pulmonary, cardiovascular, and other comorbidities that define patient performance and organ function. Surgical resectability is on the basis of tumor and lymph node imaging and staging, which help predict whether the tumor can be fully removed. When choosing between a pre- or postoperative approach, both medical operability and surgical resectability should ideally be defined preoperatively, preferably in a multidisciplinary team approach. , , , Patients discussed at multidisciplinary tumor boards have better stage classification and initial management plans, and higher rates of treatment and guideline adherence, all of which contribute to improved cancer outcomes. 
In conclusion, neoadjuvant, perioperative, and adjuvant ICI-CT are all approved and effective treatment approaches that improve EFS and DFS compared with CT and have acceptable safety profiles. All three strategies are accepted standards of care for eNSCLC. Several major questions remain to be answered in future clinical trials ( ). Differences in regimens and study populations among these phase 3 trials confound cross-trial comparisons and highlight the need for head-to-head trials. To identify the optimal strategy, particularly in light of the recent requirements from the FDA Oncologic Drugs Advisory Committee for studies to identify the efficacy and toxicity contributions of each treatment phase in perioperative trials, alternate study designs, and multiarm trials, such as PROSPECT-LUNG and CLEAR-INSIGHT, will be required. These and other studies will help define whether a pre- or postoperative ICI approach is superior, whether there is added benefit to adjuvant after neoadjuvant ICI-CT, and more importantly, which patients will benefit most from each approach. Because of surgeon and patient preferences and the limitations of preoperative invasive lymph node staging that include underutilization and poor sensitivity, adjuvant ICI-CT therapy will remain an important strategy. Nevertheless, the risks of nodal metastasis mean that preoperative mediastinal lymph node staging remains imperative. The choice between a pre- versus postoperative approach, thus, involves a risk-benefit assessment of factors that include the potential for preoperative attrition to surgery, postoperative attrition to systemic therapy, and the anticipated toxicity profile. The multidisciplinary tumor board has a vital role in this process to improve patient outcomes. Finally, the plethora of treatment options in the eNSCLC landscape further increases the need for multidisciplinary team decision-making, particularly as our understanding of biomarkers and molecular testing continues to evolve.
