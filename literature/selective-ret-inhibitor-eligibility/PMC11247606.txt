## Abstract
Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer death and includes two major types: non‐small‐cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small‐cell lung cancer (SCLC), accounting for 85% and 15% of cases, respectively. Non‐small‐cell lung cancer harboring actionable driver mutations is generally treated with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) molecularly targeting individual oncogenes. Although TKIs have greatly contributed to better clinical outcomes, acquired resistance to them inevitably occurs. Histologic or lineage transformation is a rare but well‐documented off‐target mechanism associated with acquired resistance, and has been identified in settings following treatment with multiple different TKIs and other drugs. It includes neuroendocrine transformation, squamous cell transformation, and epithelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition. Here, we review the clinicopathologic features of transformed tumors and current understanding of the key genetic alterations and biologic mechanism of lineage transformation in NSCLC, particularly TKI‐triggered transformation.
Keywords: histologic transformation, lineage plasticity, NSCLC, SCLC, tyrosine kinase inhibitor

## INTRODUCTION
Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer death worldwide. It includes two major types: non‐small‐cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small‐cell lung cancer (SCLC), accounting for 85% and 15% of cases, respectively. , Among NSCLCs, adenocarcinoma is the most frequent subtype, with a 5‐year survival rate of approximately 20%. Unlike squamous cell carcinoma, the other subtype, adenocarcinoma, is less linked to smoking history. The major predisposition to this disease is oncogenic activation of driver genes such as epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene (KRAS), and MET proto‐oncogene (MET). The discovery of such driver oncogene alterations has greatly contributed to the advancement of lung cancer treatment. Currently, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) targeting these oncogenes are generally used as a standard therapy for patients who harbor such genetic alterations. Although these therapeutic advances have led to marked improvements in both progression‐free and overall survival, emergence of resistance remains inevitable. As the spectrum of available therapeutic approaches has broadened, the complexity of the mechanisms underlying acquired drug resistance has become increasingly apparent. The simplest and most well‐established mechanism of resistance to targeted therapies is on‐target alteration. Genetic changes in the driver oncogene itself, such as T790M mutation in EGFR
 or second‐site mutations in ALK driver fusion , cause constitutive activation of downstream signaling pathways even in the presence of inhibitors. On the other hand, activation of bypass signaling pathways, such as MET amplification, is a common off‐target resistance mechanism attributable to alternative pathway activation or downstream activation. In addition to genetic or transcriptional alteration, an increasing number of studies have shown that lineage transformation of tumor cells also leads to TKI resistance, whereby tumor cells transdifferentiate to neuroendocrine carcinoma or squamous cell carcinoma, or undergo epithelial‐to‐mesenchymal transformation (EMT) (Figure  ). Here, we summarize what is currently known about common genetic alterations and potential molecular mechanisms of acquired therapeutic resistance attributable to lineage switching of tumor cells in NSCLC.
 1.1.

## Neuroendocrine transformation
The most well‐recognized form of lineage transformation is that from NSCLC to SCLC histology, which was first described as a mechanism of TKI resistance in EGFR‐mutant NSCLC in 2006. Small‐cell lung cancer transformation has been found in 3%–14% of patients who develop resistance to first‐ and second‐generation EGFR‐TKIs, , and 5%–20% of patients who acquire resistance to the third‐generation EGFR‐TKI, osimertinib, , , , indicating that SCLC transformation can occur irrespective of the EGFR‐TKI generation. A multicenter retrospective cohort study undertaken in Japan involving patients with EGFR‐mutant NSCLC who received EGFR‐TKIs, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), or cytotoxic chemotherapy indicated that 59 of 2624 (2.2%) cases underwent neuroendocrine transformation. It was noteworthy that neuroendocrine transformation occurred not only after TKI treatment but also after use of ICI and cytotoxic agents.
Transformed SCLC (t‐SCLC) mimics de novo SCLC morphologically and immunohistochemically and is characterized by small cells with a high N/C ratio, high mitotic activity and expression of neuroendocrine (NE) markers such as chromogranin A and synaptophysin (Figure  ). , , , Although the founder EGFR mutation is genomically preserved in t‐SCLC, EGFR protein expression is lost, indicating that t‐SCLC is no longer dependent on EGFR oncogenic signaling. , , The majority of t‐SCLCs harbor mutation of both TP53 and RB1, similarly to de novo SCLC. , , , However, combined loss of TP53/RB1 function is reportedly necessary, but not sufficient, for SCLC transformation, suggesting that there are additional pivotal molecular mechanisms beyond TP53/RB1 loss. PIK3CA is one of the most common recurrent mutations in SCLC, having been observed in 1/4 cases of SCLC transformation in the study that first reported it. Subsequent studies also identified it in 20%–29% of pretransformed tumors. , , Offin et al. reported enrichment of mutations or amplifications of SMYD1, NOTCH2, ELF3, CCNE1, MYC, CREBBP, and PTEN in pretransformed tumors, with a lower frequency of PIK3CA alterations. 
TERT amplification was also reported in 4/6 cases of t‐SCLC. More recently, integrative analysis of 3600 SCLC cases revealed that 107 SCLC samples harbored activating mutations in the EGFR kinase domain typically associated with NSCLC. It was suggested that these EGFR‐mutant SCLCs represented putative transformed SCLCs, and that the cohort mirrored the genomic landscape of de novo SCLC, with RB1 and TP53 mutations present in the majority of samples. Notably, amplification and gain‐of‐function alterations in PIK3CA, NFKBIA, NKX2‐1, and CCNE1 and loss‐of‐function mutations in RBM10, IRS2, and GNAS were significantly enriched in EGFR‐mutant t‐SCLCs. We reviewed such genes that had been previously identified as key genomic alterations in de novo SCLC and t‐SCLC , , , , , , , (Table  ). Among them, 18 gene alterations were commonly observed in both de novo SCLC and t‐SCLC, reflecting a high similarity between them (Figure  ). These mutations were especially enriched in genes related to the NOTCH pathway and PI3K/AKT pathway, suggesting that deficiency of those pathways might play some role in SCLC transformation (Figure  ). Quintanal‐Villalonga et al. analyzed genomic alterations in pre‐ and posttransformed specimens and suggested that whole‐genome duplication events and chromosomal 3p loss might represent a potential risk for SCLC transformation. Additionally, hypermutation caused by the apolipoprotein B mRNA editing enzyme, catalytic polypeptide‐like (APOBEC), was frequent in clonal branches associated with incipient SCLC transformation, , although any biological links between these findings remain elusive.
 Although these genomic changes might prime or predispose tumors to undergo transformation, the process of SCLC transformation itself seems to be driven by transcriptional reprogramming. Quintanal‐Villalonga et al. have indicated that epigenomic reprogramming upon transformation leads to altered DNA methylation of key TF‐binding motifs, driving expression phenotypes that are observed during histologic transition. Although a fundamental mechanistic understanding of lineage plasticity and SCLC transformation is still lacking, multiple factors have been proposed as potentially important pathways in SCLC transformation. For instance, fibroblast growth factor 9 (FGF9) has been reported to functionally induce SCLC transformation. Ectopic introduction of FGF9 into Tp53‐ and Rb1‐null murine adenocarcinoma cells resulted in acquisition of a SCLC‐like phenotype. Moreover, recent multi‐omics analysis using a cohort of combined histology samples including both adenocarcinoma and SCLC revealed that alterations in the WNT, PI3K/AKT, and Notch pathways and the PRC2 complex were significantly enhanced in the SCLC components. Of note, pharmacologic inhibition of the PI3K/AKT pathway delayed tumor growth and SCLC transformation in an EGFR‐mutant patient‐derived xenograft model, suggesting that the PI3K/AKT signaling axis may mediate acquired resistance to EGFR‐TKIs through SCLC transformation. 

Transformation of NSCLC to SCLC has also been described in patients with ALK‐positive and ROS1‐positive NSCLC who received TKIs, but less frequently than in patients with EGFR‐mutant NSCLC. , , , , , , , , , , Lin et al. reported that the frequency of SCLC transformation was 0%–0.08% after acquisition of resistance to second‐generation ALK‐TKIs, and 2.7% after acquisition of resistance to lorlatinib, a third‐generation ALK‐TKI. We speculate that newer‐generation TKIs can cause SCLC transformation more frequently because target specificity of the drugs is higher than those of early‐generation TKIs, resulting in higher incidence of off‐target resistance mechanism including histologic transformation rather than on‐target mechanism. Also, through analysis of serial biopsy and autopsy specimens, they described SCLC transformation in a patient with ROS1‐positive adenocarcinoma who had received multiple ROS1‐TKIs such as crizotinib and lorlatinib. Furthermore, SCLC transformation was reported in a patient with a RET‐rearranged lung adenocarcinoma that relapsed after pralsetinib therapy, showing TP53/RB1 inactivation and an APOBEC signature similar to that of EGFR‐mutant t‐SCLC. 

Large‐cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC) is another histologic entity that falls under the spectrum of high‐grade neuroendocrine carcinoma but is distinct from SCLC. Large‐cell neuroendocrine carcinoma is characterized by a high mitotic count and cytologic features of NSCLC such as organoid nesting, peripheral palisading, and rosette‐like structures, as well as immunohistochemical positivity for NE markers, similarly to SCLC. Histologic transformation from adenocarcinoma to LCNEC is a very rare resistance mechanism that has been reported in patients treated with EGFR‐TKIs or ALK‐TKIs. , , , , Due to the limited number of cases examined so far, the molecular features associated with LCNEC transformation remain poorly understood, but unlike SCLC there is variability in the baseline TP53/RB1 status.
Neuroendocrine transformation includes SCLC and LCNEC transformation, both of which have been identified in EGFR‐mutant and ALK‐positive lung adenocarcinoma. Although TP53/RB1 deficit is a major risk factor predisposing tumors to undergo SCLC transformation, further molecular and functional studies are needed to clarify the fundamental biological mechanism responsible for neuroendocrine transformation.

## Squamous cell transformation
Another category of lineage transformation observed at the time of acquired resistance to targeted therapies for lung cancer is transformation of adenocarcinoma to squamous cell carcinoma. This is characterized by changes in morphology and a transition of immunohistochemical staining from adenocarcinoma markers (napsin A and TTF1) to squamous cell markers (p40 and p63). Squamous cell transformation has been identified in 1.1%–14% of patients with EGFR‐mutant adenocarcinoma who develop resistance to EGFR‐TKIs. , , , There are few case reports of squamous cell transformation in patients with ALK‐positive lung adenocarcinoma showing relapse after ALK‐TKIs, , , , , and its frequency and clinical significance have not been clarified. Interestingly, sarcomatoid transformation has also been reported in ALK‐positive patients after ALK‐TKI therapy. , Moreover, histologic conversion from adenocarcinoma to squamous cell carcinoma has also been observed without any identifiable genomic resistance mechanisms in patients who acquire resistance to KRAS G12C inhibitors. The authors histologically examined repeat tissue‐biopsy samples of tumors from 10 patients (9 with NSCLC and 1 with colorectal cancer) obtained at the time of disease progression during adagrasib treatment, and found that 2 cases of NSCLC had transformed to squamous cell carcinoma during the course of treatment with KRAS G12C inhibitors, representing quite a high frequency.

Notably, genomic analysis of four specimens showing adenocarcinoma‐to‐squamous transformation during EGFR‐TKI treatment revealed that PTEN loss, PIK3CA mutation, and LKB1 gene deletion were common to all. Consistently, depletion of the LKB1 gene led to squamous transformation in a murine Lkb1‐deficient model of mixed adenosquamous cell carcinoma. 
SOX2 amplification was also shown to cause squamous differentiation in preclinical murine models, , being concordant with the detection of SOX2 amplification in 40% of squamous cell carcinomas but not in adenocarcinomas. Multi‐omics analysis using four pretransformation adenocarcinomas, seven posttransformation squamous cell carcinomas, a nontransformed adenocarcinoma, and a de novo squamous cell carcinoma revealed that mutations in TBX3, MET, and RBM10 were enriched in the pretransformation adenocarcinomas relative to the nontransformed adenocarcinoma. At the transcriptional level, increased expression of genes related to the PI3K/AKT, MYC, and PRC2 signaling pathways was found in the squamous‐transformed component of either mixed‐histology or posttransformation tumors, whereas concurrent activation of PI3K/AKT and MYC induced squamous features in preclinical models of EGFR‐mutant adenocarcinoma. Moreover, pharmacologic inhibition of EZH1/2 in combination with osimertinib prevented squamous relapse in a patient‐derived EGFR‐mutant xenograft model, and inhibition of EZH1/2 or PI3K/AKT signaling resensitized resistant squamous‐like tumors to osimertinib, suggesting that these pathways might be functionally associated with squamous cell transformation. However, the mechanism responsible for such transcriptional reprogramming has yet to be unequivocally determined.
 
1.3.

## Epithelial‐to‐mesenchymal transformation
Epithelial‐to‐mesenchymal transformation is another mode of lineage plasticity that has been known to contribute to acquisition of resistance to TKIs. Although the definition of EMT is less clear than that of neuroendocrine transformation or squamous cell transformation, it is determined by morphologic changes and a shift in the expression of mesenchymal markers (e.g. vimentin) and epithelial markers (e.g. E‐cadherin). Suda et al. found that EMT developed as a mechanism of resistance to erlotinib, where expression of E‐cadherin was downregulated and that of vimentin and ZEB‐1 was upregulated without any genetic alteration. Similarly, EMT was observed in EGFR‐mutant NSCLC tissues that had acquired resistance to EGFR‐TKIs through mechanisms other than secondary mutation, T790M, or MET bypass activation. Treatment with a histone deacetylase inhibitor moderately resensitized the resistant cells to erlotinib, indicating that epigenetic reprogramming is relevant to this specific event rather than genetic changes. AXL receptor tyrosine kinase has been shown to facilitate EMT in TKI‐resistant cells. Increased activation of AXL and evidence of EMT have been demonstrated in multiple in vitro and in vivo EGFR‐mutant lung cancer models with acquired erlotinib resistance in the absence of EGFR T790M or MET activation, identifying AXL as a potential therapeutic target to overcome EGFR‐TKI resistance in NSCLC. Transforming growth factor‐β (TGF‐β) is one of the most well‐understood potent EMT inducers, being also involved in acquisition of resistance through EMT. Transforming growth factor‐β stimulation downregulates SOX2 and induces EMT accompanied by increased TKI tolerance, which can interfere with ectopic SOX2 expression. Although details of the mechanism are unclear, SOX2 silencing increases vimentin expression and suppresses BCL2L11, suggesting that cross‐talk between SOX2 and TGF‐β signaling can directly control cell plasticity and resistance to TKIs. Furthermore, activation of the YAP/TAZ and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways, as well as ZEB1, TGF‐β, and AXL, has been observed in patient‐derived cells showing EMT features. Intriguingly, these cells were susceptible to WNT‐TNKS‐β‐catenin inhibitors, indicating a possible strategy for overcoming EMT‐mediated resistance. Moreover, activation of AURKA and AURKB has been independently reported in EGFR‐TKI resistant models displaying the EMT phenotype. , As inhibition of AURKA and AURKB enhances the effects of EGFR‐TKI treatment in multiple preclinical models, these kinases might also be associated with the biological process of EMT.
Similarly, EMT has been reported as a mechanism of resistance to ALK‐TKIs. , , , Downregulation of E‐cadherin and upregulation of vimentin were observed in 5 out of 12 cases of ceritinib‐resistant ALK‐positive NSCLC, suggesting that EMT occurred during the process of resistance acquisition. Interestingly, however, 3 of those 5 cases harbored a secondary mutation in the ALK kinase domain as an on‐target resistance mechanism, suggesting that both genetic and phenotypic change can occur concomitantly. These observations could imply that EMT does not always cause resistance directly, but is merely a consequence of the process.

## Mixed histology and tumor heterogeneity
Although lineage plasticity is a plausible mechanism for histologic transformation, observation of tumors with mixed histology has led to another hypothesis that pretreatment tumor heterogeneity results in dominance of SCLC after treatment due to clonal selection (Figure  ). Nicholson et al. analyzed 100 cases of surgically resected SCLC and found that 28% of them also showed NSCLC histology. Additionally, a retrospective review of NSCLC slide preparations from 11 patients who had been initially diagnosed as having pure NSCLC and had later acquired resistance to EGFR‐TKI through SCLC transformation revealed that 8 samples indeed showed a combination of both adenocarcinoma and SCLC histology, 2 showed suspected combined histology, and only 1 sample was in fact pure adenocarcinoma before EGFR‐TKI treatment. After TKI treatment, all 11 samples were diagnosed as transformed SCLC but adenocarcinoma was also present in 5 of them. These data support the hypothesis that pretreatment tumor heterogeneity results in a transformed SCLC phenotype. Quintanal‐Villialonga et al. individually investigated clinical samples showing histological combinations of adenocarcinoma and SCLC, as well as adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma, to gain insight into factors related to lineage plasticity. , These studies were based on the concept that a combined histology represents an intermediate state of histologic transformation.
 On the basis of the WHO classification, a carcinoma that contains even a very small amount (<10%) of SCLC components should be diagnosed as a “combined SCLC”, whereas a diagnosis of “adenosquamous carcinoma” requires the presence of at least 10% of both adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma components. A diagnosis of “pleomorphic carcinoma” also requires that at least 10% of the histology should be a spindle cell or giant cell carcinoma component. This means that even if the diagnosis is adenocarcinoma, it might include squamous cell or sarcomatoid components even though their proportion may be low (<10%). It is also important to note that areas of combined histology in a biopsy specimen may be missed due to sampling error or bias. As the areas evident in a biopsy specimen are limited in comparison to a surgically resected tissue specimen, there is always the possibility that combined histology will be missed. These diagnostic and clinical limitations suggest that the actual frequency of combined histology would be higher than what is actually observed. Finally, the presence of mixed histologic components prior to TKI or other treatment raises the question of whether driver oncogene inhibition is required for the process of histologic transformation. Indeed, spontaneous histological transformation of NSCLC to SCLC and LCNEC was recently reported, although the mechanism remains unknown. In order to clarify the extent to which the timing and potency of oncogene inhibition drive lineage transformation, further research efforts are needed.

## CONCLUSION
In this review, we have discussed three representative categories of lineage transformation that can lead to drug resistance: neuroendocrine transformation, squamous cell transformation, and EMT. Due to the lack of reliable biomarkers detectable by DNA sequencing of liquid biopsy samples, lineage transformation can be easily missed if repeat tissue biopsy is not carried out for histologic analysis at the point when TKI resistance has developed. Furthermore, although no promising treatments are yet available for histologically transformed tumors, enrollment for some clinical trials designed for transformed SCLC has begun. For appropriate selection of patients, rebiopsy at the time of resistance acquisition is essential. Further translational and clinical investigation is required to gain a fuller understanding of the fundamental biology of lineage transformation and develop a novel roadmap for future therapy.
