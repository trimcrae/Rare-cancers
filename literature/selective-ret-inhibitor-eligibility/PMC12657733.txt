Overarching precision oncology randomized trials comparing molecularly guided therapy with standard care had yielded disappointing results. , 
The ROME trial now provides what the field has lacked: randomized evidence that comprehensive genomic profiling coupled with expert guidance improves outcomes in patients with advanced solid tumors. This multicenter phase II study of 400 patients demonstrated superior overall response rates (17.5% versus 10%, P = 0.029) and progression-free survival (hazard ratio 0.66, P = 0.0002) with tailored therapy compared with standard care. 

## Why ROME succeeded where SHIVA failed
The contrasting results of ROME and the earlier SHIVA trial reflect critical methodological differences. SHIVA, published in 2015, failed to show progression-free survival benefit for molecularly targeted therapy. That study used limited genomic panels and often relied on archival tissue obtained years before enrollment. ROME required fresh metastatic tissue biopsies at disease progression, employed comprehensive next-generation sequencing of both tissue and blood, and mandated systematic molecular tumor board (MTB) review of all cases. 
This distinction matters. Tumors evolve under therapeutic pressure, acquiring new alterations while shedding others. Treating patients based on outdated molecular profiles is akin to navigating with an old map. ROME’s approach captured the tumor’s current biology, not its historical state. The trial also demonstrated the value of expert multidisciplinary interpretation. Among 897 patients undergoing MTB review through 127 weekly meetings, treatment selection often required navigating competing biomarkers, conflicting pathway interactions, and variant allele frequencies. This complexity defies simple algorithmic matching and argues for specialized expertise in treatment selection.

## Clinical implications and limitations
The ROME trial establishes that comprehensive genomic profiling should be considered for patients with advanced solid tumors, particularly those with rare malignancies or limited standard options. , The benefit was especially pronounced in specific molecular subgroups: patients with microsatellite instability-high tumors achieved 57% response rates with immunotherapy versus 0% with standard care, while BRAF-mutant tumors showed 65% 12-month progression-free survival with targeted therapy.
While there are considerations to keep in mind, the study offers encouraging insights. A measurable improvement in response rate (7.5 percentage points) was achieved, reflecting meaningful progress. Although an overall survival benefit was not demonstrated, the high crossover rate (59%) from standard care to genomically guided therapy underscores the growing confidence in precision oncology. Importantly, the fact that 41% of control patients did not cross over, often due to rapid clinical decline, highlights an urgent opportunity: by accelerating access to genomic-guided treatments, we can better serve patients before their condition deteriorates. This reinforces the real-world value and potential of precision oncology when delivered in a timely manner.
Infrastructure requirements pose additional challenges. ROME’s success required coordinated molecular profiling, rapid turnaround, expert MTB evaluation, and access to multiple targeted agents, resources unavailable in many settings. The median time from consent to treatment and the complexity of MTB coordination represent barriers to widespread implementation.

## The randomization debate
The ROME trial’s completion raises a provocative question: Do we need more randomized trials in precision oncology? The answer is nuanced.
For therapies targeting strong molecular drivers in biomarker-selected populations, response rates often far exceed those with standard chemotherapy, potentially rendering randomization impractical or unethical. Larotrectinib’s 79% response rate in NTRK fusion-positive cancers or selpercatinib’s 85% response rate in RET fusion-positive lung cancers provide evidence that arguably surpasses what randomization could offer. For rare alterations, NTRK fusions occur in ∼1 in 300 patients, traditional randomized trials become logistically impossible.
Nevertheless, ROME’s randomized design proves valuable at this particular moment in precision oncology’s evolution. It definitively addresses skeptics who questioned whether molecularly guided therapy could outperform standard care with contemporary methods. The trial establishes that when executed properly, with fresh tissue sampling, comprehensive profiling, and expert guidance, precision oncology delivers measurable benefit. Moving forward, the field will likely rely increasingly on alternative evidence frameworks: real-world data registries, platform trials with adaptive designs, and synthetic control arms derived from comprehensive datasets. , These approaches can maintain rigor while accelerating patient access, particularly for rare molecular subsets where traditional randomization is impractical. 

## Lessons for the field
The ROME trial’s success in the contemporary era offers several critical lessons for precision oncology. Firstly, tissue matters, both timing and source. The requirement for fresh metastatic biopsies at disease progression, rather than relying on archival samples from initial diagnosis, proved essential. Tumors evolve under therapeutic pressure, and treatment decisions must reflect current biology, not historical snapshots.
Secondly, comprehensiveness trumps convenience. The integration of next-generation sequencing across both tissue and liquid biopsies, combined with broad genomic panels, identified actionable alterations that limited testing would have missed. Single-gene or narrow-panel approaches are inadequate for optimal patient selection. 
Thirdly, expertise cannot be algorithmic. The systematic MTB review of all cases demonstrated that optimal treatment selection requires nuanced interpretation of complex scenarios, multiple biomarkers, competing pathways, and quantitative thresholds that simple matching algorithms cannot navigate. The involvement of multidisciplinary expertise significantly enhanced treatment quality beyond what biomarker–drug databases alone could achieve.
Fourthly, timing is crucial. The 41% of control patients who deteriorated before crossing over to genomically guided therapy underscores precision oncology’s temporal vulnerability. Earlier implementation in the treatment trajectory, perhaps at initial diagnosis or first progression, may be necessary to maximize clinical impact.
Fifthly, the trial validates precision oncology at a pivotal moment with contemporary technologies and accumulated knowledge absent from earlier negative studies. It definitively establishes that molecularly guided therapy can outperform standard care when executed with modern methods, providing the foundational evidence needed to move the field from experimental to standard practice.
Finally, modest absolute benefits despite strong biological rationale reveal persistent gaps in our understanding of resistance mechanisms, tumor heterogeneity, and microenvironmental influences. These limitations define the research agenda ahead and underscore that validation is not optimization.

## Conclusion
The ROME trial represents a milestone in precision oncology, providing robust randomized evidence for comprehensive genomic profiling and expert-guided treatment selection. While challenges remain, including modest absolute benefits, infrastructure requirements, and lack of survival improvement, the trial validates decades of investment in molecular medicine. The journey from SHIVA’s negative results to ROME’s positive outcomes demonstrates the importance of methodological rigor in translating biological insights into clinical benefit. Several priorities emerge from ROME. Firstly, earlier implementation of genomic profiling in the treatment trajectory may capture more patients before clinical deterioration. Secondly, integration of emerging technologies, circulating tumor DNA monitoring, artificial intelligence-assisted MTB decisions, and comprehensive multi-omic profiling may enhance treatment selection. Thirdly, equitable access to molecular profiling and MTB expertise must be addressed to prevent precision oncology from exacerbating health care disparities. The trial also highlights gaps in our understanding. Why do only 17.5% of patients respond to genomically matched therapy? Improved understanding of resistance mechanisms, tumor heterogeneity, clonal evolution, and microenvironment interactions will be essential to increase benefit.
The ROME trial demonstrates that all roads in precision oncology: genomic profiling, molecular tumor boards, biomarker-driven selection, can lead to improved outcomes when properly integrated. Precision oncology has moved from aspiration to evidence-based practice. The challenge now shifts from validation to democratization, ensuring that every patient who might benefit can access the promise of genomically guided therapy, not just those at academic centers with the requisite infrastructure and expertise.
