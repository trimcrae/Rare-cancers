Concurrent RET/EGFR mutations have been reported in patients with NSCLC who develop resistance to EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors but are even less frequent in treatment-naïve patients. Consequently, there is no standard treatment for RET/EGFR-mutated NSCLC. We report a case of RET/EGFR mutant NSCLC successfully treated with the oral, potent, highly selective RET inhibitor selpercatinib (160 mg daily for 28-day cycles) in an ongoing phase II study in Chinese patients with NSCLC (LIBRETTO-321). The patient, a female nonsmoker, was diagnosed with de-novo left lung adenocarcinoma with neuroendocrine differentiation, and a RET fusion was detected by next-generation sequencing testing. The patient had two tumors in the pleura, a third in the subcarinal lymph node, and a nontarget tumor in the pleura. Pleural biopsy analysis confirmed a RET fusion KIF5B (K15;R12) and an EGFR exon 19 deletion. The patient achieved a partial response (PR) with selpercatinib (absence of target tumors in pleura and reduction in the size of lymph node tumor). The PR persisted for 14.7 months, with disease progression in the nontarget lesion in the pleura and a new lesion in the liver (the PR had persisted), resulting in the discontinuation of selpercatinib. The only notable adverse event was grade 3 elevated transaminase, that was effectively managed by dose reduction. These data may support the use of selpercatinib in patients with RET/EGFR co-mutated NSCLC.

## Introduction
Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI) targeting activating oncogenic mutations in the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) are an important therapeutic strategy for nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [ ]. Gene fusion alterations in rearranged during transfection (RET), a transforming proto-oncogene encoding a receptor tyrosine kinase, have also been identified as potent oncogenic drivers in patients with NSCLC [ ], including in Chinese patients [ ]. NSCLC is among the most common cancers harboring RET fusions [ ], which occur in 1–2% of patients with NSCLC [ ] and ~1.5% of Chinese patients with NSCLC [ , ]. Among patients with NSCLC, RET fusions generally occur most frequently in Asian females, younger patients, patients who have never smoked or who are/were light smokers, and those with adenocarcinomas [ , ]. Indeed, patients with NSCLC with RET fusions are strikingly similar to those with NSCLC with EGFR mutations, who also tend to be female, of East Asian ethnicity, have never smoked, and have adenocarcinoma histology [ ].
RET fusions generally arise in the absence of other oncogenic drivers [ ]; however, the co-occurrence of other driver oncogenes, including EGFR, has been reported in RET-altered cancers, and studies have shown that RET fusion alterations may arise as a mechanism of EGFR-TKI resistance [ ]. Therefore, concurrent RET and EGFR mutations occur most frequently in patients who are treatment-experienced and are very rare in patients who are treatment-naïve [ ]. In China, due to the high prevalence of EGFR mutations among patients with NSCLC, there is a relatively high number of patients with treatment-naïve NSCLC with co-occurring EGFR and RET mutations, presenting a unique opportunity to study this poorly understood patient subgroup.
Selpercatinib (LOXO-292) is an oral, potent, highly selective RET inhibitor. It was approved in the USA (Retevmo) for the treatment of locally advanced and metastatic RET fusion-positive NSCLC, and in Europe (Retsevmo) for the treatment of advanced RET fusion-positive NSCLC, based on the robust and durable efficacy observed in patients with advanced RET fusion-positive NSCLC in the global, phase I/II LIBRETTO-001 study [ , ]. In patients with treatment-naïve NSCLC (n = 69 evaluable), the objective response rate (ORR) assessed by an independent review committee (IRC; primary endpoint) after a median follow-up of 20.3 months was 84% [95% confidence interval (CI): 73.3–91.6]. Median duration of response (DOR) was 20.2 months (95% CI: 13.0–not estimable), and median progression-free survival (PFS) was 22.0 months (95% CI: 13.8–not estimable) [ ]. In patients who had previously received platinum-based chemotherapy (n = 247 evaluable), the ORR was 61% (95% CI: 54.7–67.2), with a median DOR and PFS of 28.6 months (95% CI: 20.4–not estimable) and 24.9 months (95% CI: 19.3–not estimable), respectively, after a median follow-up of 24.7 months [ ].
Selpercatinib has also been approved in China for the treatment of adults with locally advanced or metastatic RET fusion-positive NSCLC, based on the results of the LIBRETTO-001 and -321 studies. LIBRETTO-321, an ongoing, multicenter, open-label, phase II study [ ], is the first study undertaken to evaluate selpercatinib in Chinese patients with RET fusion-positive NSCLC [ ]. The ORR assessed by IRC after a median follow-up of 9.7 months was 69% in the overall population of patients with centrally confirmed RET fusions (n = 26), 88% in patients who were treatment naïve (n = 8), and 61% in patients who had been previously treated (n = 18). The median DOR was not reached; however, 94% of responses were ongoing at 9 months. Selpercatinib had a manageable safety profile and was generally well tolerated in the LIBRETTO-001 and -321 studies [ – ]. We report the case of a Chinese patient who participated in the LIBRETTO-321 study with advanced, treatment-naïve NSCLC harboring both a RET fusion and an EGFR mutation.

## Case and methods (LIBRETTO-321)

## Patients
LIBRETTO-321 is an ongoing, multicenter, open-label, phase II study being undertaken to evaluate the efficacy and safety of selpercatinib in Chinese patients with advanced RET-altered solid tumors (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04280081) [ ]. Briefly, adults with advanced NSCLC were eligible for enrollment into the study based on the detection of a RET alteration in tumor tissue at a local laboratory (KIF5B/CCDC6/NCOA4-RET fusion or other alteration) or a central laboratory (KIF5B/CCDC6/NCOA4-RET only). RET fusions were detected at a certified local laboratory by PCR or next-generation sequencing (NGS), at a central laboratory using the AmoyDx 9-in-1 PCR assay (Amoy Diagnostics Co., Ltd.), or both. Patients with RET alterations and an additional known oncogenic driver that could cause resistance to selpercatinib (e.g. an EGFR mutation) were enrolled into a separate cohort. Eligible patients had progressed on, or were intolerant to, standard therapy (chemotherapy or immunotherapy, or both), had declined or were deemed unsuitable for such therapy, or had tumors for which no standard therapy was available. Patients were also required to have an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–2 and not to have previously received a selective RET inhibitor. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles described in the Declaration of Helsinki, Good Clinical Practice guidelines, Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences International Ethical Guidelines, and country and local regulations. All protocols were approved by the institutional review board or independent ethics committee at each investigative site. All patients provided written, informed consent prior to any study-related procedures. Consent for publication was also provided by the patient described in this case.

## Treatment and assessments
Patients received oral selpercatinib 160 mg twice daily (bid) in 28-day cycles until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, withdrawal of consent, or death. Treatment could be continued beyond disease progression at the investigator’s discretion. Radiologic tumor assessments were performed at baseline, Week 4 (optional), Week 8, then every 8 weeks until Week 48, and every 12 weeks thereafter. All patients underwent baseline brain imaging using MRI or contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) if MRI was contraindicated. Objective response assessed by the IRC (primary endpoint) was evaluated according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors v1.1, and responses were confirmed ≥4 weeks after they were first observed. Adverse events were monitored until 28 days after the last selpercatinib dose and graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events v5.0. The data cutoff for the case presented was 31 May 2022.

## Case details
A 55-year-old female presented to her local hospital in February 2020 with a 2-month history of coughing, white, sticky sputum that was difficult to clear, and shortness of breath after exercise. The patient had profuse sweating, but no fever or hemoptysis, no chest tightness, pain, dizziness, or headache, and no history of smoking. The patient was hospitalized for further evaluation and chest CT showed extensive pleural effusion in the left lung. Thoracentesis with the removal of 1.9 L pleural fluid significantly reduced the pleural effusion. A follow-up CT scan revealed irregular thickening of the left pleura with extensive pleural effusion on the left side and left lower pulmonary inflation insufficiency. The patient also had lymphadenopathy in the left heart septal angle area. Thoracoscopic pleural biopsy with hematoxylin and eosin staining showed a malignant tumor in the left lung. Immunohistochemical analysis of the biopsy specimen was positive for thyroid transcription factor 1, cytokeratin, low molecular weight cytokeratin, synaptophysin, plasma chromogranin A foci, and cytokeratin 20; and negative for p40 and natural killer CD56. The Ki-67 index was +40%. Bronchoscopy of the right lung was unremarkable; however, the lumen of the intrinsic segment of the left upper lobe was narrow and the mucosa was congested and swollen with no obvious neoplasm. These findings were consistent with left lung adenocarcinoma with neuroendocrine differentiation. Brain MRI and emission CT of the bone showed no obvious abnormalities.
NGS testing using a 520-gene panel (BNR 520, OncoScreen Plus, Burning Rock Biotech, China) was conducted at a local laboratory to guide treatment. NGS results showed a RET-KIF5B fusion (K15:R12) and a variable shear TP53 mutation in exon 6 (c.560-1G > A) with CDKN2A and CDKN2B copy number deletions. MUTYH (p.E479*) and BRCA1 (c.134 + 3A > C) germline pathogenic variations were also detected. On the basis of the results of the local RET test, the patient, who had an ECOG performance status of 1, was enrolled in the LIBRETTO-321 study. Further analysis of the pleural biopsy tissue at a central laboratory (Amoy PCR) confirmed RET fusion KIF5B (K15;R12) and an EGFR exon 19 deletion (Fig. ). Baseline CT revealed two tumors in the pleura with diameters 23 mm and 37 mm, and a third tumor in the subcarinal lymph node measuring 17 mm in diameter (sum of target lesion diameters [SOLD]: 77 mm) (Fig. ). There was an additional nontarget tumor in the pleura.
 The patient started first-line treatment with selpercatinib 160 mg bid in April 2020. On day 29 of treatment, the patient experienced grade 3 AST and ALT elevations, which while not considered serious, were considered related to the study drug. The selpercatinib dose was reduced to 80 mg bid and transaminase elevations resolved to grade 1 within 2 weeks. The selpercatinib dose was then progressively increased to 120 mg bid and 160 mg bid at approximately monthly intervals without re-occurrence of the transaminase elevations. At the first tumor assessment after 8 weeks’ treatment, a partial response (PR) was achieved based on IRC review, with the best change in SOLD of –93.5%. There was an absence of target tumors in the pleura and reduction in the size of the subcarinal lymph node tumor. The IRC confirmed a CR in the target lesion during the tumor evaluation 9 months after initiating treatment, with an overall response of PR, based on a non-CR/nonprogressive disease (PD) response in the nontarget lesion. The DOR was 14.7 months. Selpercatinib was discontinued in August 2021 due to disease progression – the CR in the target lesion had persisted, as had the non-CR/non-PD response in the nontarget lesion, but there was a new lesion in the liver. Second-line treatment with bevacizumab, carboplatin, and pemetrexed was initiated; however, the patient died due to NSCLC within 3 months of discontinuing selpercatinib. ## Discussion
We present a report of a female patient with NSCLC who was a treatment-naive nonsmoker who harbored a co-existing RET fusion and EGFR mutation and was treated with selpercatinib in the LIBRETTO-321 study. The patient achieved a durable response; a PR that persisted for 14.7 months. The patient had grade 3 elevations in transaminase levels, which were successfully managed with dose reduction.
To our knowledge, this is the first report of the clinical and genetic characteristics of a treatment-naïve patient with concurrent RET fusion and EGFR mutations and indicates that selpercatinib monotherapy may represent a useful treatment option for these patients. PCR testing revealed a positive RET fusion from Cycle 20 and a positive EGFR mutation from Cycle 26, suggesting that the RET fusion was the main driver of gene alteration. It should be noted that there was a discrepancy between the NGS and PCR EGFR mutation results for this patient. We suggest this is likely due to the different limits of detection for the BNR 520 and Amoy 9-1 PCR kits: 1% and 2%, respectively. This means only gene alterations with a minor allele frequency ≥2% will be detected by the BNR 520 compared with ≥1% by the Amoy PCR kit. A further explanation for this discrepancy may be tumor heterogeneity. Unfortunately, re-testing could not be conducted as there was no remaining tissue.
RET fusions have been reported in patients who have previously received EGFR-TKI therapy, and it has been postulated that RET fusions may represent a mechanistic pathway for acquired EGFR-TKI resistance [ , ]. RET fusion may be an acquired resistance pathway in patients resistant to EGFR TKIs, although this has only been shown in a limited number of patients [ , ]. The role of RET fusions in patients who are EGFR-TKI naïve is less well established, with limited information regarding the clinical and genetic characteristics of these patients. Data suggest that RET fusions are found at a lower frequency in patients who are treatment-naïve compared with those who have previously received EGFR TKIs [ ]. In patients with lung adenocarcinomas, RET fusions occur commonly with concurrent CDKN2A and CDKN2B loss (29.8% and 25%, respectively) and with a relative paucity of TP53 mutations [ ], which follows a similar genetic profile to the patient in the present report, who also had CDKN2A and CDKN2B copy number deletions. Overall, the impact of co-occurring mutations with RET fusions, including oncogene-driven subgroups such as EGFR mutations, ALK, and ROS1, is undetermined and represents an area for further research [ ].
The LIBRETTO-321 study allowed enrollment of patients with RET and EGFR mutations and provides rare and highly valuable data from a patient with treatment-naïve NSCLC and RET/EGFR co-mutations. The current National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guideline for the management of patients with NSCLC recommends EGFR TKIs for patients with NSCLC and EGFR mutations [ ]. In addition, the NCCN guidelines recommend RET TKIs for patients with NSCLC and RET rearrangements [ ]; however, the NCCN guidelines do not recommend specific therapies for patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC who acquire RET fusions after becoming resistant to EGFR TKIs. In the absence of a standard of care, the combination of an EGFR TKI and RET TKI seems a reasonable clinical approach, and limited data from four patients suggest that it is effective [ ]. An ongoing open-label, multidrug, phase II platform study (ORCHARD; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03944772) will prospectively evaluate the efficacy of targeted combination strategies in patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC who develop resistance to EGFR TKIs, including those with acquired RET fusions; however, for patients with RET and EGFR co-mutations at diagnosis, there is currently no standard of care and little evidence to guide treatment. Therefore, the case presented here provides valuable information for clinicians. Current research suggests EGFR does not play a role in selpercatinib resistance [ ]; however, the potential contribution of EGFR mutations to selpercatinib resistance in patients with a RET/EGFR co-alteration, compared with patients with a RET fusion only, has not been investigated. In addition, it is unknown if EGFR mutations change dynamically during selpercatinib treatment. Further circulating tumor DNA follow-up is required to answer these questions.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, our case report provides some of the only data available on the genetic and clinical characteristics of a treatment-naïve patient with NSCLC and RET fusions co-occurring with EGFR mutations. Selpercatinib, an oral, potent, highly selective RET inhibitor, was associated with robust and durable efficacy in this patient and was well tolerated, with a manageable safety profile. Therefore, data from the LIBRETTO-321 study support further exploration of selpercatinib in this patient population. Further research is required to understand the most effective treatment approaches for patients with co-occurring RET fusion and EGFR mutations and to identify resistance mechanisms and treatment options for patients with co-mutations who progress following selpercatinib treatment.
