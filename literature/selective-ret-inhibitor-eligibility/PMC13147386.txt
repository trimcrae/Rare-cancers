## Highlights
•This review for the first time systematically synthesizes the latest advances in multimodal technologies of thyroid cancer.
•This review also discusses RNA-based therapy as a promising emerging therapeutic modality in thyroid cancer.
•The review introduces intraoperative fluorescence navigation technology, which can reduce surgical complications and effectively integrate diagnosis and treatment.
Keywords: Thyroid cancer, Multimodal strategies, Diagnosis, Treatment

## Abstract
Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) is the most common type of thyroid cancer, and its incidence has been increasing recently. Although the 5-year survival rate for PTC remains stable, the prognosis for medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) and the highly aggressive, rare anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (ATC) is less favorable, underscoring the need for improved risk assessment. Additionally, there are still no effective treatments for MTC and ATC, emphasizing the urgency of developing personalized therapies. In this context, creating more precise diagnostic and treatment methods is essential. This review highlights recent advances in innovative diagnostic and therapeutic technologies for thyroid cancer and compares them with traditional approaches. It particularly focuses on how combining various radionuclides with positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) facilitates innovative diagnostic and treatment solutions. The review also explores optical imaging for early tumor detection and real-time therapy guidance, as well as nanotechnology's role in transforming light into heat and free radicals—driving progress in both medicine and nanomaterials science. Moreover, it discusses RNA-based therapies as a promising, emerging approach that broadens treatment options for patients with thyroid cancer. Additionally, the review introduces intraoperative fluorescence navigation technology, which can reduce surgical complications and effectively integrate diagnosis and treatment.

## Introduction
Thyroid cancer is the most common endocrine cancer, making up about 4 % of all cases. Although it affects all genders, the incidence rate among women is higher than men and it ranks fifth among female tumors [ ]. Well-differentiated thyroid cancers that originate from follicular cells include papillary, follicular (roughly 4 %), and oncocytic (about 2 %) types, with papillary being the most prevalent, accounting for approximately 84 % of thyroid cancer [ ]. More aggressive cancers derived from follicular cells include poorly differentiated thyroid cancer (around 5 %), which has a high mortality rate, and anaplastic thyroid cancer (about 1 %), a rare and highly aggressive tumor. Medullary thyroid cancer (approximately 4 %), arising from parafollicular C cells, is a less common neuroendocrine malignancy [ , ].
Thyroid diagnosis mainly depends on neck ultrasound (US) and fine-needle aspiration [ ]. However, over the past three decades, the incidence of thyroid cancer has steadily risen, making it one of the top seven cancers worldwide, primarily due to the widespread use of imaging tests [ ]. As a result, societal guidelines recommend against routine thyroid ultrasound in patients of all ages without symptoms or risk factors [ ]. Additionally, fine-needle aspiration (FNA) is an invasive procedure that heavily relies on the operator's skill. Approximately 15–30 % of FNA cytology results are indeterminate [ ]. And neck ultrasound heavily rely on operator expertise and its comparatively low detection rate for patients with positive lymph node metastasis. A recent meta-analysis indicates that ultrasound demonstrates high specificity in predicting lymph node metastasis in thyroid cancer; however, its sensitivity for detecting metastasis in central and lateral lymph nodes remains modest. Conversely, computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) excel in identifying advanced lesions, cervical lymph nodes, and metastases in other organs. These imaging techniques aid in surgical planning but are typically not used during initial screening [ ].
The main treatments for thyroid cancer include thyroidectomy, radioactive iodine therapy, and TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) suppression. For more aggressive types, such as anaplastic thyroid carcinomas (ATC), options include cytotoxic chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy [ ]. Some differentiated thyroid cancers (DTCs) develop resistance to iodine-131, especially during treatment; iodine-131-resistant thyroid cancer with distant metastasis has a poor prognosis, with a median survival of 2.5 to 3.5 years [ ]. Research shows that thyroid cancer is associated with several factors. 1) Amplification or increased copy number of oncogenes, especially those encoding receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs); 2) Somatic gene mutations such as BRAF-V600E mutation, RET and RAS mutations; 3) Chromosomal translocations and 4) Non-coding RNA imbalance including abnormal miRNA, lncRNA and circRNA. Disruption of the MAPK pathway caused by oncogenic BRAF, RAS and RET fusion mutations is the main genomic feature of PTC, ATC and RET-mutant MTC [ ]. Thus, some small molecule inhibitors specifically targeting overactive or mutated cell surface binding receptors have shown promising outcomes. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) such as lenvatinib and sorafenib are FDA-approved for progressive, RAI-refractory metastatic DTC [ , ]. Other systemic therapies, including the BRAF inhibitor dabrafenib, mTOR inhibitor everolimus, NTRK inhibitors, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), are being evaluated in clinical trials [ ].Two RET inhibitors, selpercatinib and pralsetinib, are approved for metastatic MTC [ ]. ATC is highly aggressive, and single treatments often fail, necessitating multimodal strategies like surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapy. However, responses vary, and adverse effects or toxicity limit some patients' tolerance [ ]. Furthermore, cancers also evade the attack of targeted drugs by modifying key functional genes and activating bypass or feedback overexpression of other receptors, thereby generating drug resistance and significantly weakening the therapeutic effect [ , ].
To address these challenges, we propose a multimodal approach to identify high-risk groups and design a personalized treatment plan. Advances in imaging and combined diagnostic and treatment methods have the ability to distinguish the malignant disease from the indolent one, enabling more personalized therapies for aggressive cases. The primary aim of this review is to examine novel multimodal technologies for risk analysis and precise treatment critically. ( )
 

## Molecular imaging technologies

## Radionuclide-based PET-CT imaging
Functional imaging plays a vital role in clinical practice, particularly in diagnosing and locating thyroid cancer [ ]. SPECT fusion imaging combines detailed anatomical and functional data with image attenuation correction, allowing for the accurate detection of thyroid tumors and metastases. Whole-body scans (WBS) using small doses of 131I or 125I are used to evaluate iodine uptake before 131I therapy after thyroid cancer surgery. Additionally, FDG-PET-CT is essential for assessing the spread of thyroid cancer, predicting patient outcomes, and is the preferred method for detecting RAI-resistant thyroid cancer [ ].
Recently, multimodal imaging has gained popularity for diagnosis, particularly as research into radioactive drug carriers advances. While some radionuclides, such as iodine and radium, naturally target specific organs, most require specialized carriers to reach disease sites for imaging and treatment. These carriers can be designed to target tumor cells, the tumor microenvironment, or proteins that are highly expressed in tumors. For example, peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT), which targets somatostatin receptors (SSTR), and 18F-DOPA, a radioactive form of L-3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine, have shown success in imaging and treating RAI-R differentiated thyroid cancer and medullary thyroid cancer. Additionally, radioactive drugs targeting fibroblast activation protein (FAP) are currently in phase I and II clinical trials. Other transporters under study include C-X-C chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4), cholecystokinin-2 receptor (CCK2R), and prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA), all of which are at different stages of clinical research.
Radioactive isotopes release gamma (γ) rays, positrons (β+), alpha (α), and beta (β-) particles. For instance, 99mTc emits γ rays and is used in SPECT imaging, while 18F emits positrons for PET scans. Various isotopes are utilized in thyroid cancer diagnosis and treatment. For example, I-124 PET aids in staging and dose planning for differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC), while F-18-FDG PET/CT helps stage high-risk DTC and evaluate less differentiated or undifferentiated thyroid cancers [ , , ]. All agents share a common structure—composed of a ligand, a linker or a chelating agent, and radiopharmaceutical—and are classified into three groups according to radiopharmaceutical: (a) the same isotope used for both imaging and therapy(e.g. low-dose isotopes like 177Lu and I-131 for initial imaging, followed by high-dose therapy); (b) the same isotope with different numbers of neutrons (e.g. 86Y for PET, 90Y for therapy); and (c) different isotope for diagnosis and treatment(e.g. 68Ga for imaging, 177Lu for therapy). Unlike chemotherapeutic drugs, radioactive therapies target tumor cells remotely by causing DNA damage, such as single-strand breaks (SSB) and double-strand breaks (DSB), within tumor tissues or nearby cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME). Beta particles lose energy as they pass through matter and need to accumulate in high concentrations within the target tissue for effective therapy. Compared to alpha particles, beta particles inflict less damage on normal tissues expressing the target receptor, which explains their wider clinical application [ ]. Hence, functional imaging is crucial in the management of thyroid cancer. PET-CT, especially FDG-PET/CT, is preferred for assessing tumor spread, prognosis, and RAI-resistant cases, as well as staging high-risk or poorly differentiated cancers. SPECT fusion imaging allows precise localization, and whole-body scans assess iodine uptake. Recent advancements in multimodal imaging employ targeted carriers (such as SSTR and FAP) to deliver radionuclides. These radiopharmaceuticals generally share a similar design and can be categorized into four main groups. Their primary therapeutic effect involves inducing DNA damage, with beta-particles currently being more prevalent in clinical practice than alpha-particles.

## I131, I124, I123 and NIS
Solute carrier (SLC) transporter proteins play a crucial role in transferring small molecules, including hormones, drugs, neurotransmitters, and nutrients, across cell membranes. The human genome comprises over 400 SLC members, including glucose transporter 1(GLUT1) and Na+-I symporter (NIS), with NIS being the most well-known. NIS is explicitly found in thyroid tissue and can concentrate substances into cells, whether they are endogenous or exogenous. Tumors, which need more nutrients, often exhibit increased uptake transporter levels, aiding in diagnosis and differentiation [ ]. Since the 1940s, iodine-131 has been used for both diagnosing and treating thyroid cancer, marking it as the first actual therapeutic diagnostic agent targeting NIS in DTC and normal thyroid tissue. NIS transports two sodium ions and one iodine ion into the cell, utilizing the sodium gradient across the membrane, which is driven by the transmembrane sodium concentration [ ]. Its activity and expression in thyroid follicular cells are mainly regulated by thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) through the cAMP pathway. Additionally, Refractory radioactive iodine (RAI)-resistant DTC has a poor prognosis, mainly due to the activation of core signaling pathways such as MAPK/PI3K, multiple levels including epigenetic silencing and microRNA regulation, which jointly suppress the thyroid-specific gene network——especially the NIS, ultimately leading to the loss of NIS expression and/or function (membrane localization) [ ].
For over 70 years, I-131 has been used primarily in diagnosis, particularly for DTC. With advancements in technology, I-123, I-124, and I-131 have become common for gamma scanning. However, using I-131′s β energy for diagnosing DTC poses radioactive toxicity risks. I-124 PET/CT offers benefits such as a half-life of 4.2 days, high spatial resolution, and utility in lesion-based dosimetry, but both I-123 and I-124 require cyclotrons for production, which are expensive and not widely available. Consequently, new imaging agents targeting DTC via NIS are being developed. 99mTc ferricyanide produces γ rays at 140 keV——an ideal energy for gamma imaging——without β rays, and is cost-effective, making it suitable for clinical use. Its features have made 99mTc ferricyanide a common reagent in routine imaging. Recently, 18F-labeled tetrafluoroborate has been synthesized and identified as a specific NIS substrate; it is still under development [ ]. The development of new NIS-targeted imaging agents with improved physical properties and clinical potential, such as 99mTc ferricyanide and 18F-tetrafluoroborate, is increasingly important due to the radiation toxicity and production difficulties associated with radioactive iodine. In clinical practice, the core principle of I-131 (iodine-131) in differentiating benign from malignant thyroid tumors lies in the significant difference in the ability of normal thyroid cells and cancer cells to take up iodine. Normal thyroid cells actively take up iodine from the blood to synthesize hormones, while this ability decreases or disappears in malignant tumors. Therefore, the radioactivity at the nodules is lower than that of the surrounding normal tissues or completely lost, which is referred to as a cool or cold nodule. In conclusion, the colder a nodule is, the greater the likelihood that it is malignant [ ].

## Glucose transporter 1(GLUT1) and 18F-FDG tumor imaging
GLUT1 is a low oxygen-responsive transport protein that promotes unidirectional transport of glucose and mannose, galactose, glucosamine, and other hexoses. However, Glucose is the primary substrate. GLUT1 is expressed in almost all tissues, and its high expression is often found in tissues with high cellular glucose metabolism. In normal tissues, the highest expression of GLUT1 is found in red blood cells and endothelial cells that form the blood-brain barrier [ ].
In the 1920s, Warburg and his colleagues observed that tumors absorb more glucose than normal tissue. Moreover, unlike normal cells, which generate energy through oxidative phosphorylation of glucose, most cancer cells produce lactic acid from glucose fermentation. This phenomenon is known as the "Warburg effect" and forms the basis for the development of radioactive glucose analogs, such as 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG), as cancer imaging agents [ ]. The principle of 18F-FDG PET for staging is that higher glucose uptake generally indicates larger or more aggressive tumors, with high-grade tumors typically taking up excess glucose to support their growth. Therefore, a higher FDG signal around the lesion correlates with a more advanced stage of the cancer [ ]. As shown in , (a-c), combining PET/CT scans, 18F-FDG PET can non-invasively determine tumor location and metabolism and aid in staging. Currently, 18F-FDG PET is widely employed for diagnosing, staging, and monitoring treatment progress in high-risk DTC, low-differentiated, undifferentiated thyroid cancer, and MTC. For iodine-avid diseases, 124I PET is the most sensitive method for detecting small recurrent lesions [ ]. However, imaging iodine-resistant recurrent DTC mainly relies on 18F-FDG PET [ ]. Studies have shown that dedifferentiation of DTC may lead to a loss of NIS expression and increased glucose uptake. Additionally, glucose uptake is elevated in inflammatory areas, which can lead to a relatively high false-positive rate for 18F-FDG [ ]. In clinical practice, 18 F-FDG PET/CT can also serve as a prognostic indicator for RAI treatment response, which is of great significance for patients with metastatic DTC. High 18 F-FDG uptake functions as an independent adverse prognostic factor and negatively impacts overall survival. Furthermore, CT has been combined with 18F-FDG PET to enhance imaging, but 18F-FDG PET remains mainly used clinically to confirm new lesion findings, detect metastases of old lesions, and stage existing lesions [ ]. In summary, GLUT1, a widely expressed glucose transporter, is highly associated with the Warburg effect in tumors and forms the basis for 18F-FDG PET imaging. This technique, which reflects tumor invasiveness through increased glucose uptake, has become a crucial tool for diagnosing, staging, and monitoring high-risk, low-differentiated, undifferentiated thyroid cancers, and MTC. It is especially valuable for iodine-resistant recurrent DTC and can also serve as an independent adverse prognostic indicator for RAI treatment response. Nevertheless, caution should be exercised when interpreting results that may be caused by false positives due to inflammation.
 

## Somatostatin receptor (SSTR) and peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT)
SSTRs are a family of seven-transmembrane G protein-coupled receptors consisting of five distinct subtypes: SSTR 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5. They are found on the cell membranes of over 70 % of neuroendocrine tumors (NET) and thyroid cells and can mediate the endocytosis of external substances into cells [ ]. Typically, medullary carcinoma originates from the thyroid NET, with SSTR2 highly expressed, whereas normal thyroid cells generally do not express it. Therefore, molecular imaging targeted SSTA is the preferred method for diagnosing the primary lesion of medullary carcinoma, locating lymph node metastasis, and distant metastasis [ ]. Natural somatostatin is rapidly broken down by enzymes, resulting in a very short half-life of less than 3 min after intravenous injection. To address this, various somatostatin analogs have been developed to extend their biological activity, with octreotide being a key example, possessing a half-life of 2 h and a high affinity for SSTR2. Octreotide (TATE) is produced by oxidizing the terminal alcohol of octreotide to a carboxyl group (COOH), which enhances its affinity for SSTR2. Additionally, efforts are underway to develop SSTR inhibitors by reversing the chirality of the first and second residues of the octapeptide. Unlike traditional SSTR agonists, which do not internalize, SSTR antagonists have a higher affinity for receptor binding sites than agonists [ ].
68Ga is chelated with various agents, such as DOTA, and is attached to SSA to create a radioactive, targeted nuclear diagnostic agent. Binse et al. assessed the diagnostic effectiveness of 68Ga-DOTATOC PET/CT in 15 DTC patients who had elevated serum thyroglobulin levels and harmful iodine and 18F-FDG PET/CT results. 68Ga-DOTATOC was detected in all DTC patients (3/3) and in the single ATC patient. Additionally, the serum Tg levels of tumors that absorbed 68Ga-DOTATOC showed a higher trend [ ]. Research showed the diagnostic efficacy of 68Ga-DOTATATE for MTC is lower than that of 18F-DOPA, but its diagnostic and therapeutic potential make it a promising option. And more clinical trials are needed to verify the accuracy and effectiveness of its diagnosis and treatment [ ]. Additionally, its therapeutic function is facilitated by switching the radioactive label, enabling the destruction of tumor cells in SSTR-high patients through targeted therapy using Lu 177 and 90Y-labeled somatostatin (SSA), known as PRRT [ ].
PRRT, a targeted therapy, has gained attention because it selectively targets tumor cells while sparing healthy tissue, resulting in fewer side effects and personalized efficacy compared to traditional radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Unlike targeted therapy that acts on the cellular signal transduction level, PRRT does not rely on specific signaling pathways. It directly induces DNA damage by using the β rays released from radioactive nuclides. it generally involves radioactive somatostatin analogues composed of three parts: synthetic analogues such as octreotide or Tyr 3-octreotide, chelators like DTPA or DOTA, and radionuclides like 177Lu and 90Y [ , ]. Qingxing Liu et al. studied 28 patients with progressive, somatostatin receptor-positive MTC who received PRRT using 177Lu or 90Y from September 2003 to June 2019. Responses were assessed via RECIST 1.1 and molecular imaging (European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer). Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to determine progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) from the start of treatment, with Cox regression identifying factors related to these outcomes. Results showed a disease control rate of 56 % after 77 cycles, with a partial response in 12 % and stable disease in 44 % at 3–4 months post-treatment. Median OS and PFS were 63.7 and 10.1 months, respectively, exceeding typical survival expectations [ ]. Another clinical trial evaluated the therapeutic effect and outcome of PRRT in patients with somatostatin receptor-positive metastatic MTC. The results indicated that patients with a calcitonin doubling time (CtnDT) exceeding 24 h could achieve better remission after PRRT treatment, suggesting it as a promising treatment for a specific population [ ]. A meta-analysis evaluated the therapeutic effect and prognosis of PRRT on differentiated thyroid cancer. The conclusion indicated that PRRT had a good therapeutic effect, with an objective response rate (ORR) of 11.48 % - 24.52 % and a disease control rate (DCR) of 61.47 % - 67.26 %. It is a potential and safe strategy for treating MTC and DTC [ ]. The use of PRRT for targeted therapy of thyroid cancer is a rapidly evolving field. However, PRRT is relatively new in the field of thyroid cancer, and further exploration is needed in optimizing imaging techniques and dosimetry studies. Moreover, more clinical trials are required to achieve clinical translation.

## FAP and FAPI tumor imaging
TME is a complex system comprising the extracellular matrix (ECM), immune cells, fibroblasts, progenitor cells, endothelial cells, and signaling molecules, and it is vital in tumor development. Tumor-associated fibroblasts (TAFs) are central components of the connective tissue surrounding epithelial cancer cells, accounting for approximately 90 % of malignant connective tissue. They promote tumor growth, migration, immune evasion, and resistance to radiation and chemotherapy by secreting immunosuppressive factors and mediators [ ]. Fibroblast activation protein (FAP), a 170 kDa member of the DPP4 family, functions as a type II transmembrane serine protease that typically forms homodimers. FAP is highly expressed on most CAFs, serving as a specific marker for malignant tumors. It participates in enzyme regulation and signal transduction, showing high expression across various tumors and low levels in healthy tissues. Therefore, after injection of the radiolabeled FAPI, this tracer specifically binds to FAP on the surface of CAFs, which are extremely abundant in the malignant tumor microenvironment, and FAPI imaging typically shows extremely high uptake [ ]. Consequently, FAP inhibitors (FAPI) are extensively studied as diagnostic and therapeutic targets, especially in cancer diagnosis and lymph node metastasis staging through radioactive labeling for PET/CT [ ].
A radiopharmaceutical diagnostic agent labeled with 68Ga and 18F FAPI has recently emerged as a promising marker, offering an effective alternative for functional tumor imaging. A study showed that 68 Ga-DOTA-FAPI-04 PET/CT could effectively detect lymph node and distant metastases in 87.5 % of RAIR-DTC patients [ ]. Another latest study compared the diagnostic value of 68Ga-FAPI-04 and 18F-FDG PET/CT in lymph node metastasis of thyroid cancer. The results showed that there was no significant difference between 68Ga-FAPI-04 and 18F-FDG in detecting primary lesions and LNM in PTC but 68Ga-FAPI-04 may have potential advantages in the diagnosis of head and neck tumors. However, due to the experiment being conducted on a small population, its effectiveness still needs further verification [ ]. Another study compared the performance of 68Ga-FAPI-04 and 18F-FDG PET/CT in the initial staging and recurrence detection of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) among 77 individuals. The results indicated that 18F-FDG and 68Ga-FAPI-04 PET/CT had comparable detection efficiencies for primary tumors (100 %) and recurrences (62.5 %). However, 68Ga-FAPI-04 PET/CT demonstrated higher specificity and accuracy in preoperative staging assessment. In terms of follow-up, 68Ga-FAPI-04 was on par with 18F-FDG, demonstrating 68Ga-FAPI-04 PET-CT is a promising imaging mode for judging metastasis and monitoring response to treatment [ ]. Currently, radiopharmaceuticals based on the radioactive isotope 177Lu, such as 177Lu-DOTATOC (SA. FAPI)2, are used. 177Lu-DOTAGA.Glu.(FAPI)2 and 177Lu-FAPi-46 are also being investigated for therapeutic purposes [ ]. In addition, systematic reviews have shown that FAPI-PET CT has a more sensitive detection rate for metastatic lesions [ ].

## Prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) and the ligand for PSMA imaging
Prostate-Specific Membrane Antigen (PSMA) is a type II transmembrane glycoprotein receptor that is overexpressed in more than 90 % of prostate cancer cells, serving as a basis for the diagnostic and therapeutic use of radioactive nuclides in prostate cancer. However, PSMA is also present in the angiogenesis of various other tumors, including lung, breast, colorectal, and thyroid cancers. Preclinical studies suggest that PSMA may assist in cancer-related angiogenesis by degrading the extracellular matrix and participating in integrin signal transduction [ ]. When the endothelial cells of malignant tumor neovessels express PSMA at high levels, whereas PSMA is not expressed in normal tissues, the injection of PSMA ligands labeled with radioactive nuclides will specifically bind to PSMA on the endothelial cell surface of the neovessels. Thus, the higher the malignancy, the more active the angiogenesis, and the more obvious the PSMA imaging [ ].
Most clinical studies investigate the use of Ga68-labeled PSMA ligands, such as 68Ga-PSMA-11 or 18F-PSMA-1007, or antibodies through PET imaging. Because ligands exhibit better binding properties and pharmacokinetics—thanks to faster clearance and lower myelotoxicity compared to antibodies—they are currently the main focus for PSMA diagnosis and therapy in prostate cancer patients [ ]. Bychkov et al. recruited 267 patients and they PSMA is overexpressed in the angiogenesis of DTC compared to normal thyroid tissue [ ]. Case series have shown that PSMA-targeting ligands may show uptake in patients with high serum thyroglobulin (Tg), negative 131I scintigraphy, and 18F-FDG-PET [ ]. Compared to standard treatments, there is strong evidence of tumor regression, including a decrease in prostate-specific antigen (PSA), reduction in tumor size, pain relief, and extended progression-free and overall survival as seen in imaging studies [ ]. De Vries et al. evaluated five patients with RAI-refractory thyroid cancer using 68Ga-PSMA PET/CT to screen for those suitable for 177Lu-PSMA-617 targeted therapy. The results showed that 68Ga-PSMA PET/CT detected more lesions than 18FDG PET/CT and successfully screened out some patients. However, only one of the two patients who received treatment achieved a brief partial response. In conclusion, PSMA-targeted technology has advantages in diagnosis and can effectively screen potential treatment subjects, but its corresponding therapeutic effect is still not satisfactory and requires further research and verification [ ]. The possible role of PSMA ligands in future thyroid cancer research may be better defined.
I-131 imaging provides high specificity for detecting metastases in DTC and functions as both a diagnostic and therapeutic tool, making it the preferred method for post-surgery metastasis removal in DTC. However, its effectiveness drops significantly in poorly differentiated or undifferentiated cancers due to dependence on NIS expression. SSTR imaging has gained attention because of its combined diagnostic and therapeutic potential, but its limited indications and uncertain efficacy mean it remains an area for development in thyroid cancer treatment. 18F-FDG PET-CT is useful for evaluating poorly differentiated metastases, especially those with elevated Tg but negative I-131 scans. However, it is prone to false positives and has lower sensitivity for well-differentiated DTC with high NIS expression compared to I-131. PSMA imaging, mainly used for prostate cancer, has unexpectedly shown PSMA expression in thyroid cancer metastases, presenting a new diagnostic option for iodine-refractory DTC and potential targeted therapy in the future. FAPI PET-CT, which targets the tumor microenvironment, is still in the exploratory phase with ongoing clinical data collection.
Compared with the above-mentioned novel radionuclide imaging, conventional technologies such as ultrasound and CT are widely used in the screening of thyroid nodules and preoperative anatomical assessment. However, they have obvious limitations in functional imaging, molecular targeting, and integrated diagnosis and treatment. Ultrasound can display the morphology, boundary, and blood flow of nodules in real time and guide puncture, but it cannot provide metabolic or receptor-level information. CT provides a clear display of the neck structure and is suitable for preoperative staging, but it also lacks molecular specificity and has limited ability to assess micro-metastases. The core advantage of novel radionuclide imaging lies in elevating diagnosis from the anatomical structure to the molecular functional level and achieving integrated diagnosis and treatment. I-131 imaging is a classic representative of integrated diagnosis and treatment, as it can simultaneously complete imaging and radioactive iodine therapy with a single administration. SSTR imaging has both diagnostic and PRRT potential in medullary thyroid carcinoma, allowing the same target to both locate the lesion and achieve internal irradiation therapy. PSMA imaging also has the prospect of achieving theranostic approaches. If the imaging is positive, subsequent ¹⁷⁷Lu-PSMA radioligand therapy can be adopted, opening up a new path for iodine-resistant patients. Although FAPI PET-CT is currently mainly used for diagnosis, its characteristic of targeting the tumor microenvironment is also extending towards treatment, and it is expected to form a new integrated diagnosis and treatment system in the future. Overall, traditional imaging methods provide benefits in anatomical localization and ease of use. However, advanced molecular imaging techniques, which target specific molecules and combine diagnostic and therapeutic functions, offer significant advantages in accurate disease staging, predicting treatment responses, and guiding personalized therapy.
Radioactive iodine, as a mature and widely used radionuclide, has relatively controllable costs for both examination and treatment, and is highly feasible. Currently the American Thyroid Association (ATA) has incorporated postoperative radioactive iodine therapy for high-risk patients into its guidelines. 18F-FDG PET-CT, due to its high price, is only available in large hospitals and cancer centers. Somatostatin receptor imaging, PSMA imaging, and FAPI PET-CT, because of their high costs and poor feasibility, are difficult to be widely adopted. The latter two are still in the research and exploration stage. Overall, thyroid cancer imaging has shifted from an exclusive reliance on I-131 to a multi-target, collaborative approach. Future studies are needed to confirm the clinical utility of new tracers and to develop personalized imaging strategies based on molecular profiles, ultimately enabling risk stratification and precise treatment.

## Optical imaging and fluorescence-guided surgery
PET-CT uses positron-labeled ligands like ⁶⁸Ga-PSMA for detailed molecular imaging of thyroid cancer and employs β-radionuclide internal irradiation (¹⁷⁷Lu) for treatment. However, its intraoperative use is limited by low spatial resolution, the need for radiation safety measures, and delayed therapeutic effects [ ]. In contrast, optical imaging (OI) offers high spatial and temporal resolution, high sensitivity, excellent contrast, rapid processing, non-invasiveness, simple equipment, and cost-effectiveness. We have also observed that molecular imaging-based optical techniques can non-invasively visualize and often quantify cellular and molecular structures and processes, which is vital for early cancer detection [ ]. This technology allows clinicians and researchers to observe and analyze diseases without disturbing or removing the lesion and can be integrated with treatment. Furthermore, real-time imaging provides dynamic visuals that assist in tracking thyroid cancer development and progression in patients. Techniques include fluorescence imaging, optical coherence tomography (OCT), and photoacoustic imaging (PAI), among others [ ].

## Fluorescence imaging
Fluorescence imaging is a vital technique used in biological diagnosis and image-guided surgery, relevant to both research and clinical applications. It involves creating a fluorescent probe by attaching a fluorescent molecule to a biomolecule that specifically targets tumors. When the probe arrives at and accumulates in the tumor, it emits visible light upon excitation by an external source, facilitating tumor detection, imaging, and diagnosis [ ] ( ). To enhance imaging depth, near-infrared (NIR) light is often utilized. Studies show that NIR light within the second optical window (such as NIR-IIb, 1500–1700 nm) is highly effective for imaging living tissues, providing high spatial and temporal resolution because of its ability to penetrate deep into tissue, reduce photon scattering, and lower autofluorescence [ , ].
 While NIR-II provides two-dimensional images of targets, it lacks detailed spatial information, which can lead to misinterpretation. To overcome this, some studies have developed dual- or multimodal platforms that combine molecular and anatomical data for more accurate diagnosis [ ]. In 2015, Ning Chen and colleagues used Cy5.5-conjugated MnO nanoparticles for dual-mode magnetic resonance and near-infrared imaging of glioma. Their in vivo results showed that PEG-Cy5.5-MnO nanoparticles enhanced tumor contrast in MRI, and whole-body NIR imaging revealed their accumulation in glioma regions [ ]. This prompted investigation into whether these probes could be adapted for thyroid cancer. Later, a pH-responsive MR/NIRF nanoprobe (MnO2@BSA-Cy5.5) was developed by biomineralizing manganese dioxide with BSA and attaching Cy5.5. This nanoprobe’s relaxation rate and T1 signal are activated in acidic environments, enabling the targeted imaging of thyroid tumors with excellent MR/NIRF results [ ]. Deborah Fanfone and her team created new probes by attaching galectin-1 targeting peptides to ultrasmall superparamagnetic iron oxide particles (USPIO) or near-infrared dye (CF770). Their experiments demonstrated high sensitivity (75 %) and specificity (100 %) in detecting papillary thyroid carcinoma [ ]. However, due to some commercial considerations and regulatory jurisdiction issues, these studies have only remained at the laboratory or preclinical stage. Moving forward, research focuses on developing smarter, responsive and easily convertible probes to explore personalized treatment options guided by multimodal imaging, advancing clinical diagnosis and therapy for thyroid cancer.

## OCT
OCT is a non-invasive imaging method that produces high-resolution, three-dimensional images of samples ( ). With a resolution of 1 to 20 μm and a detection depth of 2–3 mm, it provides detailed structural and depth information. OCT utilizes low-energy NIR light sources, combined with microscopic lenses, handheld probes, or endoscopes, for routine assessments [ ]. After near-infrared light irradiates tissue, different microscopic structures (such as cell nuclei, collagen fibers, and glands) exhibit distinct scattering and absorption characteristics. The disordered cell arrangement, abnormal cell nuclei, and reactive interstitial hyperplasia in malignant tumors result in distinct gray levels, textures, and morphological features on OCT images [ ]. As early as 2004, Liron Pantanowitz and colleagues compared OCT images with traditional histopathological images, confirming the potential of OCT for thyroid imaging and lesion detection [ ]. Recent studies suggest that OCT can evaluate follicle shapes and visualize growth patterns of thyroid tissue, aiding in the distinction between malignant and benign nodules [ ]. Therefore, OCT may become a valuable tool for differentiating benign and malignant thyroid nodules in the future. However, its limited imaging depth of about 2 mm has restricted widespread clinical application [ ].
 

## Photoacoustic imaging (PAI)
PAI depends on the photoacoustic effect, where light absorption produces sound waves. When brief pulses of light illuninate biologigal tissues, the tissues absorb photons, leading to thermoelastic expansion and a short pressure increase that generates ultrasound waves, called photoacoustic waves [ ]. These waves are detected by ultrasound transducers to visualize light absorption in the tissue. Since PAI combines optical and ultrasound detection, it supports dual-mode imaging ( ). Tumor neovascularization, lower lipid content and reduced blood oxygen saturation generate stronger photoacoustic signals under laser irradiation of specific wavelengths, thus presenting as brighter regions in the images [ ]. Its clinical benefits include: (a) using natural contrast agents for non-invasive metabolic and histological imaging; (b) overcoming the depth constraints of traditional optical methods for deep tissue imaging; (c) integrating with other imaging techniques to form multimodal platforms, enhancing diagnostic accuracy [ ].
 Jeesu Kim et al. conducted in vivo multispectral PAI on thyroid nodules in 52 patients. Statistical analysis demonstrated that multi-parameter analysis of this multispectral PA could differentiate PTC nodules. PTC nodules exhibit lower SO2 levels compared to benign nodules, aligning with tumor-related hypoxia hypotheses. Integrating photoacoustic imaging of PTC with American Thyroid Association guidelines (ATA) led to a new scoring system with a sensitivity of 83 % and a specificity of 93 % [ ]. Overall, PAI offers the ability to visualize thyroid structure and function without ionizing radiation or exogenous contrast agents, with a short examination time (∼5 min), highlighting its significant potential in diagnosing and managing thyroid diseases [ ]. However, large-scale, multi-center clinical trials are still needed to verify its reliability, sensitivity and specificity. Before it becomes a standard diagnostic tool, more evidence-based medical evidence is required. Secondly, this study mainly focused on papillary thyroid carcinoma. The diagnostic efficacy for other less common but more aggressive types of thyroid cancer (such as MTC and ATC) remains unclear. Currently, PAI equipment is still an emerging specialized device and is far from being as widespread as ultrasound. Therefore, at present, PAI should not be regarded as a primary screening tool to replace conventional ultrasound, but rather as a highly promising supplementary diagnostic and precise triage tool. When the results of ultrasound examination are unclear, PAI can provide crucial blood oxygen function information to help doctors determine whether a biopsy is necessary.

## Fluorescence imaging-guided surgery
Fluorescence imaging can assist surgeons during procedures by enabling precise operations [ ]. The thyroid gland is often small, and the glandular tissue in most thyroid cancer cases is delicate and susceptible to bleeding, requiring highly accurate and careful surgical techniques. Because the parathyroid glands are tiny and difficult to differentiate from fat tissue, meticulous surgery sometimes cannot prevent accidental injury, leading to hypoparathyroidism, especially in patients undergoing lymph node dissection. Fluorescence-guided surgery is an emerging method that appears to reduce this risk [ ]. Surgeons typically use probes to locate thyroid cancer and apply fluorescence imaging to monitor the removal of tumors ( ). A study developed a nitroreductase (NTR) fluorescence probe called Ox-NT [ ]. The structure of Oxazine 1 is effectively broken down to produce low background fluorescence, which helps distinguish tumors from normal tissues and reduces false signals. Under low oxygen conditions, the nitro group of Ox-NTR can be reduced to an amine, then breaks down into Oxazine 1, which emits bright red fluorescence. Fibronectin extra domain B (EDB-FN), an extracellular matrix protein, plays essential roles physiologically. It is expressed in fetal tissues and adult cancer tissues but is absent in many non-cancerous tissues. Another study identified a high-affinity EDB-FN targeting peptide called fibronectin extra domain BEDBp (AVRTSAD) and created a probe based on EDBp: Cy5-PEG4-EDBp (Cy5-EDBp) for surgical navigation [ ]. Mouse experiments demonstrated that the EDBp peptide specifically targeted EDB-FN-positive thyroid cancer, and fluorescence imaging with Cy5-EDBp facilitated the complete removal of tumors in mice. A recent study has confirmed that FGS is helpful in identifying positive surgical margins (PSM) in thyroid cancer surgery and promoting the complete resection of the tumor [ ]. Besides, fluorescence-guided surgery (FGS) has become an essential tool for differentiating parathyroid glands from adipose tissue in real-time [ ]. In a meta-analysis, near-infrared autofluorescence demonstrated high accuracy in identifying parathyroid glands during thyroid and parathyroid surgeries [ ]. The practicality and value of this technology seem promising. However, there are still some flaws, such as limited tissue penetration depth, unstable signals, high equipment cost requirements, and the development of probes. Therefore, it still needs to be further confirmed in future work [ ].
 Previous literature has proposed that optical imaging techniques will enhance the visualization capabilities of surgeons both preoperatively and intraoperatively. Based on this, this review elaborates in more detail on the necessity and obvious advantages of adding fluorescence probes during surgery [ ]. In summary, combining fluorescence imaging with surgery shows great potential for the future. Still, most research remains preclinical, with only a few studies in phase 1 clinical trials [ , , ]. Regarding OCT and PAI, clinical positioning challenges and technical barriers mean they have a long development path [ ].

## Nanotechnology-based therapies

## Phototherapy in thyroid cancer
Traditional treatments for thyroid cancer often encounter challenges such as recurrence, metastasis, and side effects. Recently, optical therapy has emerged as a promising minimally invasive alternative. Photothermal therapy (PTT) employs agents that convert light into heat to selectively destroy tumor tissues. In addition to directly targeting cancer cells, PTT can modify the tumor microenvironment and stimulate systemic immune responses [ , ].
Depending on specific needs, different photosensitizers based on nanomaterials can be chosen. Zhang CM et al. developed 131I-labeled BSA-modified copper sulfide (CuS) nanoparticles (131I-BSA@CuS) to treat ATC, demonstrating excellent biocompatibility and low toxicity both in vitro and in vivo. These nanoparticles efficiently convert 808 nm laser light into heat, with a conversion efficiency of 28.07 %. In vivo, combined therapy using 131I-BSA@CuS showed promising results. SPECT/CT imaging indicated radioactive accumulation peaked within 24 h and remained in the tumor for 5 days after intratumoral injection [ ]. PTT can target thyroid carcinoma in situ and lymph nodes. Xueqian Wang et al. identified a tumor-targeting peptide, TMVP1, with a core sequence LARGR that binds specifically to VEGFR-3, which is mainly expressed on new lymphatic vessels in sentinel lymph nodes (SLNs) of adults with metastasis. By incorporating indocyanine green (ICG) into TMVP1-modified nanoparticles, they developed TMVP1-ICG nanoparticles (TMVP1-ICG-NPs). These target in situ tumors and metastatic SLNs by binding VEGFR-3, improve ICG stability, and enhance PTT effects without significant cytotoxicity [ ]. Another study used Janus nanomotors powered by near-infrared (NIR) light, capable of deep tumor penetration, photothermal ablation, and NIR-triggered immunotherapy. Under NIR irradiation, these nanomotors exhibited strong photothermal effects, actively moved within the body, accumulated in tumors, and enabled effective ablation and immunogenic cell death (ICD) [ ]. Overall, combining PTT with nanomaterials offers promising options for multimodal treatment of thyroid cancer. Customizing nanoparticles to target tumor cells and applying PTT opens promising avenues for future therapies. However, more randomized controlled trials are still needed to verify the clinical effects in order to achieve clinical translation.

## Photodynamic therapy (PDT)
PDT is an advanced, minimally invasive treatment that depends on three main components: a photosensitizer (PS), specific wavelengths of visible light, and molecular oxygen. When exposed to light, the PS becomes excited to the singlet state (S1) and then transitions to the triplet state (T1) via intersystem crossing [ ]. The activated PS can be deactivated through fluorescence or phosphorescence, emitting light that helps in tumor imaging. It can also generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as superoxide anion radicals (O2•−), hydroxyl radicals (OH•), or hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) through proton or electron transfer (Type I). Alternatively, the Type II pathway produces singlet oxygen (1O2). These highly reactive ROS can destroy cancer cells through PDT ( ). Several cell death mechanisms—including apoptosis, necroptosis, autophagy, and necrosis—are involved. Evidence indicates that PDT not only kills tumor cells directly but also induces immunogenic cell death (ICD), thereby boosting anti-tumor immune responses [ ].
 Hypericin (HYP), extracted from St. John's wort (Hypericum perforatum L.), is a natural photosensitizer used in clinical PDT. Hyejin Kim et al. investigated the activity of HYP-assisted PDT in ATC cells. The experimental results demonstrated that the mortality rate of ATC FRO cells significantly increased after treatment with a combination of HYP doses and laser power, verifying the effectiveness of HYP in PDT for ATC. In recent years, nanomedicine (NM) has been widely explored and applied in imaging-guided surgery, local thermal, or radiotherapy for tumors [ ]. A recent nanotechnology has deeply integrated NIR-II imaging with CO gas therapy, providing significant technical support and development direction for fluorescence-guided surgery and precise tumor treatment [ ]. Therefore, researchers considered whether PDT could be combined with nanotechnology to provide a treatment plan that minimizes the risk of surgery-related morbidity and offers effective treatment in thyroid cancer. A study evaluated porphyrin-HDL nanoparticles (PLP)-mediated photodynamic therapy as a minimally invasive, tumor-specific treatment for thyroid cancer. A team conducted in vitro experiments using human papillary thyroid cancer cell lines K1 and MDA-T85. The endocytosis of PLP by K1 and MDA-T85 cells, as well as their sensitivity to PLP-PDT, was evaluated. Meanwhile, orthotopic thyroid cancer models were established in mice and rabbits, respectively. The in vitro results indicated that PLP-PDT significantly reduced cell viability, demonstrating its cytotoxicity to tumor cells. The results showed that PLP-PDT is a safe and effective treatment for thyroid cancer, capable of specifically targeting and eliminating tumors without damaging surrounding normal tissues. The minimally invasive nature of this approach makes it a potential alternative for thyroid cancer treatment [ ].

## Combined therapy
In experimental research, relying solely on PTT or PDT often falls short of delivering optimal therapeutic results. This is because PTT suffers from the thermal shock effect, and PDT faces challenges due to the hypoxic tumor microenvironment [ ]. Combining these therapies offers a promising approach: PTT can boost blood flow, increasing oxygen in the tumor and thereby enhancing PDT effectiveness. This synergy facilitates the destruction of heat-resistant tumors during PDT [ ]. Chen et al. developed a platform using hemin-loaded black phosphorus nanosheets, which exhibit high photothermal efficiency (up to 26.8 %), and heme can produce oxygen, boosting singlet oxygen (SO) generation during PDT. This combined platform effectively merges PDT and PTT while also activating immune responses via photothermal and photodynamic mechanisms, ultimately inhibiting tumor growth [ ]. The current research trend is moving towards the development of a multifunctional therapy platform. Nanoplatforms can carry chemotherapy drugs and immune adjuvants, achieving the combination of chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and PTT/PDT therapy, and integrating diagnosis, treatment, and therapeutic effect monitoring into one [ , ]. A study constructed a tumor-targeted multifunctional nanoplatform based on human serum albumin (HSA), achieving imaging-guided dual-mode phototherapy (PTT + PDT) through covalent conjugation of a near-infrared photosensitizer (IR-DBI), and realizing immune synergistic therapy through non-covalent loading of an immunomodulator (R848) [ ].
However, it is worth mentioning that the PTT and PDT involving the new type of nanomaterial platform used for thyroid cancer are still in the preclinical research stage at present [ ]. Thus, in order to successfully translate to clinical application, improvements are still needed at multiple levels. First, the biological safety and long-term toxicity of nanomaterials are the primary concerns. Second, the complexity of platform construction and large-scale industrial production pose a significant challenge. How to ensure that the large-scale synthesis of such integrated multifunctional nanostructures exhibits uniform chemical properties remains an urgent issue. Third, the precise control of therapeutic parameters needs improvement, and more experimental data are needed for reference. Finally, it is necessary to promote more clinically relevant validation and, ultimately, to develop precise treatment strategies that can adapt to the individualized heterogeneity of tumors.

## RNA-based therapeutic strategies
RNA therapy, including small interfering RNA (siRNA), messenger RNA (mRNA), and microRNA (miRNA), has been widely studied as an innovative approach for cancer diagnosis and treatment [ ].

## mRNA
mRNA is a type of single-stranded RNA molecule, essentially serving as a template of genetic information that encodes a specific protein. Therefore, the research on mRNA has drawn much attention. It is usually delivered into cells by lipid nanoparticles (LNP), where it translates into antigen proteins, thereby activating T cells to kill tumor cells [ ]. A landmark phase 2b clinical trial has confirmed that for patients with high-risk melanoma who have undergone surgery, the adjuvant treatment regimen of mRNA individualized neoantigen vaccine mRNA-4157 combined with PD-1 inhibitor pembrolizumab can significantly prolong recurrence-free survival compared with PD-1 monotherapy. This is the first individualized mRNA cancer vaccine to prove effective in a randomized clinical trial and has entered phase III clinical trials [ ]. However, the research on mRNA in the field of thyroid cancer is still in the preclinical stage. Li et al. developed a lipid-peptide-mRNA (LPm) nanoparticle (NP). Both in vivo and in vitro results demonstrated that it could restore iodine affinity and enhance the efficacy of radioactive iodine, providing a promising novel therapeutic approach for ATC [ ].

## miRNA-based approaches
miRNA is a kind of endogenous, short non-coding single-stranded RNA molecule. Mature miRNA inhibits gene expression at the post-transcriptional level by incompletely complementary binding to target mRNA [ ]. A study successfully developed a pH-sensitive BP@PLGA/PLA nanomicelle system, which achieved efficient proliferation inhibition and apoptosis induction in vitro thyroid cancer cells by targeting the regulation of the miR-620/IRF2BP2 axis, which provides a new target (miR-620) for the precise treatment of thyroid cancer [ ]. Another study constructed P@IP-miRNA nanoparticles, which inhibited AKT3 expression by targeted delivery of miR-338–3p and synergized with sonodynamic therapy to provide a new strategy for the integrated precision diagnosis and treatment of papillary thyroid carcinoma [ ].

## siRNA
siRNA is especially promising for cancer therapy because it can target tumors efficiently [ ]. It works by silencing tumor-specific genes through siRNA, causing cancer cells to undergo apoptosis [ ]. Unlike traditional targeted drugs, siRNA acts at the mRNA level based on the principle of base complementary pairing. Once inside the cell, it guides a complex called RISC, which functions like a "gene scissors", precisely locating and cutting the mRNA of the gene that matches its sequence, thereby effectively halting the continuous production of the protein from the source [ ]. Anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (ATC), a rare and aggressive type of thyroid cancer, poses a serious health threat. Patients typically survive only 3–5 months, with nearly 100 % mortality, and the cancer often spreads rapidly beyond the thyroid to nearby and distant sites, classifying it as stage IV [ ]. The BRAF-siRNA therapy in ATC has entered the clinical research stage. Liu et al. created a near-infrared systemic siRNA delivery nanoplatform that successfully delivers siRNA [ ]. This platform effectively silences the BRAF gene in tumors and inhibits growth without significant side effects. Experiments demonstrated that it allows prolonged circulation and substantial accumulation in tumors in xenograft and orthotopic models.
RNA therapy, through programmable molecular design, can rapidly and precisely target sites that are difficult for traditional drugs to act on, achieving comprehensive regulation from gene silencing to protein expression, and providing a flexible technical platform for personalized and precise medical treatment. However, translating this into clinical practice faces challenges due to RNA's biological instability, Low efficiency of cellular uptake and delivery, and Immunogenicity and off-target effects. Through chemical modification, novel delivery systems and optimized sequence design, the new generation of RNA drugs is evolving towards greater stability, better controllability, and higher precision [ ].
Overall, PRRT can selectively target tumor cells expressing somatostatin receptors, reduce damage to healthy tissues, enable personalized treatment, and improve outcomes. Nevertheless, further studies are needed to confirm its efficacy, given strict indications and patient variability. The combination of photothermal and photodynamic therapy can work together to enhance tumor removal by leveraging the strengths of both methods. Fluorescence-guided surgery (FGS) provides real-time imaging to help surgeons distinguish tumors from normal tissues like parathyroid glands, thus improving surgical accuracy. Yet, it requires specialized equipment and training, which increases complexity and cost, limiting its clinical adoption. RNA therapy shows promise for difficult-to-treat cancers such as ATC, but developing precise delivery nanoparticles remains a major challenge.

## Discussion
Currently, with ongoing improvements in medical detection methods and increased public awareness, the detection rate of small PTC (<1 cm) is rising steadily. While surgical resection remains the primary treatment for differentiated thyroid cancer, the increase in small, potentially clinically silent PTC (<1 cm) and concerns about overtreatment have led social guidelines to recommend a more conservative approach [ ]. Consequently, I. Sugitani et al. propose an alternative to surgery—active surveillance—which can effectively reduce unnecessary FNA procedures and prevent postoperative complications. However, this approach is suitable only for specific populations: a) solitary PTC ≤1 or 1.5 cm with well-defined boundaries within the thyroid, b) at least 2 mm of normal thyroid tissue surrounding the tumor, c) no suspicious lymph nodes, and d) older patients [ ]. For MTC and more aggressive, rare ATC, early detection of lesions is crucial for surgical intervention, which greatly improves patient prognosis. Currently, neck CT, contrast-enhanced imaging, or MRI are primarily used for patients with larger tumors, suspected extrathyroidal metastasis, lymph node involvement, or aggressive histology such as MTC and ATC in advanced stages [ ]. To enable early diagnosis and treatment, we suggest employing PET-CT and optical diagnostic methods. 18F-FDG PET is actively used in clinical settings to detect new lesions and confirm existing metastases. Other radionuclides are also under investigation; notably, the therapeutic agent 77Lu-DOTATATE has shown promise in treating metastatic medullary thyroid cancer, highlighting the potential of PRRT for thyroid cancer, especially MTC [ ]. Nonetheless, further clinical studies are needed to confirm its efficacy and safety, aiming to expand treatment options for patients. As an emerging imaging technology, optical imaging—particularly NIR-II—has become important in medical diagnosis and treatment due to its versatility and high sensitivity. NIR-II probes can effectively penetrate biological tissues and provide deeper imaging capabilities, especially in tumor visualization. The clinical application of this technology depends on developing high-performance fluorescent probes that offer real-time feedback during surgery, helping surgeons to more precisely locate tumor tissues [ ]. Research efforts are also ongoing regarding fluorescence imaging for lymph node metastasis [ , ]. A meta-analysis has shown that surgeries guided by indocyanine green fluorescence imaging can result in the removal of more lymph nodes, including metastatic ones, compared to conventional laparoscopic dissection [ ]. PAI combines the strengths of optical and ultrasound imaging, providing high-contrast, high-resolution images well-suited for vascular and tumor imaging. It produces deep tissue images by irradiating tissues with lasers to generate ultrasound signals. This technology has broad clinical potential, especially for early tumor detection and monitoring [ ]. The integration of PTT and PDT with nanomaterials has led to the development of new nanoplatforms and multimodal therapeutic systems, offering increased treatment options for MTC and ATC patients. With continued advances in nanotechnology, these multimodal platforms have promising research prospects [ ]. Additionally, emerging technologies such as RNA therapy for ATC and fluorescence-guided surgery are being developed. These multimodal diagnostic and treatment platforms bring hope for more personalized patient care, although further clinical trial data and collaborative efforts are essential for successful translation into clinical practice.

## Conclusion
This review systematically highlights the pivotal role of multimodal strategies in advancing thyroid cancer diagnosis and treatment: while traditional ultrasound and fine-needle aspiration biopsy (FNAB) remain foundational, their limitations—insufficient sensitivity, suboptimal specificity, and operator dependence—urgently necessitate innovative technological breakthroughs. For diagnosis, multimodal functional imaging (notably PET-CT with radiotracers such as ¹⁸F-FDG, ⁶⁸Ga-DOTATATE, FAPI, and PSMA ligands) enhances accuracy in tumor characterization, staging, and therapeutic response assessment, particularly for radioiodine-refractory thyroid cancer (RAIR-TC) and aggressive subtypes. Complementarily, optical imaging techniques (e.g. near-infrared fluorescence, optical coherence tomography, photoacoustic imaging) strengthen intraoperative real-time navigation and early detection via high spatiotemporal resolution and non-ionizing radiation. In therapy, PRRT serves as a targeted option for MTC; photothermal and photodynamic therapies offer novel minimally invasive approaches through local tumor ablation and immune modulation; nanotechnology-driven platforms synergize in image-guided therapy, RNA therapy, and drug delivery for intractable subtypes, and fluorescence-guided surgery reduces postoperative complications via precise intraoperative tissue identification. Despite substantial progress, clinical validation of novel tracers, standardization of multimodal protocols, and optimization of nanomaterial safety remain translational bottlenecks. Future research should prioritize personalized diagnosis and treatment to maximize efficacy and minimize complications; deep integration of multimodal diagnostic precision and therapeutic innovation will drive a paradigm shift in thyroid cancer management, ultimately improving patient survival and quality of life. , .
