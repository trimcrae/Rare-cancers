## BACKGROUND
Brain metastases are an exceptionally rare and diagnostically challenging manifestation of differentiated thyroid carcinoma (DTC) in pediatric and young adult patients. The authors present the case of a 21-year-old woman with a history of follicular variant papillary thyroid carcinoma who developed multiple intracranial lesions concerning for metastases.

## OBSERVATIONS
The patient had previously undergone total thyroidectomy and radioactive iodine ablation, with normal serum thyroglobulin and no known systemic spread. Brain biopsy was deferred given the location of the lesions and the risk associated with biopsy.

## LESSONS
Despite the rarity of brain metastases in pediatric and young adult populations with DTC, especially in the absence of concurrent extracranial disease, this case highlights the importance of thorough neurological evaluation and consideration of CSF thyroglobulin as a potential marker. This case emphasizes the need for vigilance in surveillance and an alternative to brain biopsy when intracranial metastasis is suspected.

## Illustrative Case
A 21-year-old female, with a history of PTC and Ebstein’s anomaly after a fenestrated Fontan procedure, was admitted to the pediatric hematology-oncology service for evaluation of multiple brain lesions noted on MRI. She had noticed daily headaches for the past month, most noticeable in the morning. She also reported intermittent nausea, generalized fatigue and weakness, left eye irritation, and intermittent tinnitus. She had seen an ophthalmologist outpatient for these concerns, who noted elevated intraocular pressure. When notified of these symptoms, her primary care provider ordered a brain MRI, which showed a solid and cystic mass within the medial posterior left frontal lobe deep to the motor strip ( and ). There were additional punctate enhancing lesions within the left parietal lobe and enhancement of the left frontal lobe sulcus ( C and ). These findings were overall concerning for intracranial metastases with possible left meningeal spread.
 
One year prior to this admission, she had developed progressive dysphagia; a neck ultrasound demonstrated a thyroid nodule ( ). Fine-needle aspiration of the nodule revealed PTC with Bethesda classification VI. She underwent a total thyroidectomy, and surgical pathology showed a 1.9-cm multifocal papillary thyroid cancer, follicular variant, involving the left lobe and isthmus ( and ). Microscopic extrathyroidal extension and lymphovascular invasion were seen ( ), but there was no capsular invasion. Surgical margins were negative but close (< 1 microscopic field). One lymph node had metastatic disease ( ). Postoperatively, she underwent 75-mCi 131-I thyroid remnant ablation and began levothyroxine suppressive therapy. It was noted that her thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels began to rise 2 months after her radioactive iodine ablation with free T4 remaining low. Despite gradually increasing her levothyroxine dose, TSH continued to increase over the next 6 months (maximum > 150 mIU/L). This change was attributed to the initiation of calcium supplementation interfering with levothyroxine absorption. She was instructed to take the levothyroxine and calcium supplements at different times of the day, with subsequent decrease in her TSH 1 month before her admission. She had a negative 131-I scan 2 months prior to admission.
 
 
Following admission, thyroid function labs were collected; these included TSH (0.11 mIU/L), free T4 (1.61 ng/dL), thyroglobulin (0.6 ng/dL), and thyroglobulin antibody (< 1.0 IU/mL). Given the low serum thyroglobulin, the pediatric endocrinology team was less suspicious of thyroid-related disease. Neck ultrasound was obtained due to localized soft tissue swelling; it demonstrated soft tissue changes related to her previous neck dissection, but no new tissue masses or abnormally enlarged lymph nodes. CT imaging of the abdomen and pelvis, CT imaging of the chest, MRI of the spine, and a positron emission tomography (PET) study showed no evidence of other sites of metastasis. A lumbar puncture was performed to evaluate for infectious etiologies; there was no evidence of infection or malignant cells within the CSF (glucose 52 mg/dL, protein 26 mg/dL), and other fungal/viral testing was negative. An echocardiogram did not show evidence of vegetations and was otherwise consistent with post-Fontan anatomy.
Given the multiple intracranial lesions and history of thyroid cancer, metastatic thyroid cancer was suspected, but normal serum thyroglobulin suggested against that diagnosis. The neurosurgical team was consulted, and while stereotactic biopsy of the brain lesion was possible, the location of the lesion increased the risk of biopsy. For this reason, the treating teams conferred and decided to check her CSF thyroglobulin level, which was elevated at 2.8 ng/mL compared with a serum level of 0.6 ng/mL. The treating teams used this information to diagnose metastatic thyroid cancer to the brain.
Next-generation sequencing returned positive for RET fusion driver mutation, and the patient was started on selpercatinib, a RET kinase inhibitor. At the time of publication, there were ongoing discussions regarding the potential for stereotactic radiosurgery.

## Discussion

## Observations
Intracranial metastases from PTC are a rare clinical entity, accounting for less than 2% of all thyroid carcinoma metastases. , When they occur, they typically manifest years after the initial diagnosis, generally occur in the cerebral hemispheres, and are more commonly associated with aggressive histological variants such as tall cell or columnar cell subtypes. Our patient, however, was diagnosed with FVPTC—traditionally associated with more indolent behavior—making this presentation particularly unusual. , 
Most pediatric FVPTC cases are encapsulated and demonstrate a low propensity for extrathyroidal extension or distant spread. , However, there is another subtype of FVPTC currently classified as infiltrative FVPTC by the World Health Organization. Infiltrative forms of FVPTC more commonly exhibit extrathyroidal extension and lymph node metastases compared with other subtypes, , , and rare cases of distant metastasis, including to the brain, have been documented. , One reported case described a 9-year-old girl with FVPTC and parietal brain metastases successfully treated with radioiodine therapy after elevated thyroglobulin levels prompted further imaging. 
Our patient’s persistently normal serum thyroglobulin and reassuring iodine scan complicated surveillance and delayed suspicion of systemic recurrence. Interestingly, her CSF thyroglobulin was elevated (2.8 ng/mL vs serum 0.6 ng/mL), despite the absence of malignant cells in the CSF. The testing of CSF for thyroglobulin is a novel idea. There was a case report of an 81-year-old man with metastatic PTC characterized by a solitary cystic lesion; the aspirated fluid was sent for cytological examination and thyroglobulin levels were > 300 ng/mL. Thyroglobulin measurements have not been validated in CSF, so there is no reference interval. In our patient’s situation, the treating teams used a comparison to low serum thyroglobulin to make the diagnosis, but further validation should be considered.
If surgical intervention had been required to attain diagnostic clarity, a stereotactic needle biopsy would have been considered. However, this procedure would have been associated with risk given the location of the lesion and its proximity to the motor region of the brain. Additionally, the prevalence of hemorrhage in thyroid cancer brain metastases can be as high as 60%, substantially higher than the 3%–14% seen in brain metastases overall. Instead, the elevated CSF thyroglobulin provided valuable clinical insight that allowed us to defer this procedure and its associated risks.
In our patient, the elevated thyroglobulin in CSF compared with serum was felt to be clinically significant as the blood-brain barrier is largely impermeable to large proteins, such as those made by the thyroid. The anionic nature of these hormones requires tightly regulated membrane transports to cross into CSF. A study by Morimoto et al. investigated calcitonin levels in the plasma and CSF of normal subjects and patients with medullary thyroid carcinoma, finding minimal penetration of calcitonin across the blood-brain barrier even with significantly elevated serum calcitonin. 
The absence of extracranial metastatic disease and negative findings on a PET study and whole-body imaging raised the possibility of isolated CNS spread. The mechanisms for such unusual dissemination remain unclear but may involve hematogenous spread or, speculatively, congenital cardiovascular anomalies such as Fontan physiology acting as a bypass to the pulmonary filter. There have been studies looking at rates of brain metastases in other congenital heart defects with indeterminate conclusions. , 
Our patient’s genetic sequencing was positive for RET fusion driver mutation, a receptor tyrosine protein kinase (RET kinase) that is involved in varied cellular mechanisms. This has been demonstrated to be a common oncogenic driver in PTCs. Compared with other common fusion driver mutations such as NTRK3, RET fusion driver mutations are more likely to have lymph node and distant metastases. 

## Patient Perspective
During the evaluation of this patient’s brain lesions, she reported increased anxiety and decline in mood largely due to the uncertainty surrounding her prognosis. She expressed a desire to retain control over her life despite the substantial mental and physical challenges posed by her cancer diagnosis. She continues to follow up with an outpatient psychiatrist to discuss strategies for coping with the challenges that come with cancer treatment and for managing her mental health.

## Lessons
This case highlights an exceedingly rare presentation of brain metastases in a pediatric patient with FVPTC, occurring in the absence of typical high-risk features or extracranial metastasis. Limitations of this report include the inability to obtain histological confirmation of intracranial metastases due to the lesion’s location, the patient’s ongoing treatment status, and the inherent constraints of a single case, which limit broader generalizability. However, it underscores the importance of maintaining a broad differential in patients with neurological symptoms and a history of thyroid carcinoma, even in the context of reassuring biochemical surveillance. CSF thyroglobulin may serve as a useful adjunct in such cases, although further validation is required. Given the evolving molecular landscape of pediatric thyroid cancer, genetic profiling may provide insights into metastatic potential and inform future management strategies.
