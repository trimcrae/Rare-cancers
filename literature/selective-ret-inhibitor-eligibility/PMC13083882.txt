In this review, we synthesize current insights into key aspects of EC, including established risk factors, the stepwise process of tumorigenesis, strategies for early detection and prevention, and emerging therapeutic approaches. Emphasis is particularly placed on the molecular underpinnings of (1) early tumor initiation and malignant progression, with a focus on genetic mutations, epigenetic modifications, and alterations within the tumor microenvironment; (2) the clonal expansion and metabolic reprogramming that play essential roles in shaping the evolutionary trajectory of esophageal cancer, thereby promoting intratumoral heterogeneity; (3) the transition from normal epithelium to invasive carcinoma in both ESCC and EAC driven by sequential disruptions of molecular networks governing epithelial homeostasis; and (4) advances in early screening techniques and minimally invasive interventions that may offer new opportunities for EC prevention and early detection, especially in high-risk populations. By delineating the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying EC initiation and progression, this review aims to inform future directions in early diagnosis, risk stratification, and individualized therapy, ultimately contributing to improved clinical outcomes and precision prevention of EC.

## Introduction
Esophageal cancer (EC) remains one of the most lethal digestive tract malignancies and is the seventh leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. The epidemiology, mortality, and histopathological characteristics of EC demonstrate significant geographical and ethnic variations. Accordingly, EC was responsible for approximately 511,000 new cancer cases and 445,000 deaths globally in 2022. The disease exhibits significant sex-based and regional disparities, with male incidence and mortality rates being approximately 2 to 3 times higher than those in females. EC is mainly categorized into two histological subtypes, squamous-cell carcinoma (SCC) and adenocarcinoma (AC), which differ substantially in their geographical distribution, etiology, and pathogenesis. SCC predominantly affects the middle and upper parts of the esophagus, with the highest incidence in Iran, Japan, Korea, and China. In contrast, EAC typically develops in the lower part of the esophagus and is the predominant subtype of EC in Europe and the United States. , 
China faces the world’s largest EC burden. It was estimated that there were 224,000 new cases and 187,500 deaths from EC in China in 2022, with age-standardized incidence and mortality rates of 8.32 and 6.68 per 100,000, respectively. EC ranks as the fifth leading cause of cancer death in China. ESCC remains the predominant histological subtype, accounting for more than 80% of EC cases in the Chinese population. The prognosis of both ESCC and EAC patients is poor, with 5-year survival rates ranging from only 10 to 30%, largely due to delayed diagnosis and therapeutic ineffectiveness. Owing to the insidious onset of precancerous or early-stage EC, patients do not exhibit obvious symptoms, resulting in a delayed diagnosis, often at an advanced stage. Together with limited treatment options and high recurrence and metastasis rates, these factors contribute to the persistently low 5-year survival rate. 
The major risk factors for EC include lifestyle, dietary habits, and certain environmental exposures. ESCC often arises from chronic mucosal irritation, leading to a multistep sequence from dysplasia to carcinoma (Fig. ). This evolution encompasses a spectrum from normal epithelium through inflammation, hyperplasia, low-grade intraepithelial neoplasia (LGIN), and high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia (HGIN), ultimately to invasive carcinoma. , , In contrast, EAC typically evolves from Barrett’s esophagus (BE), a metaplastic transformation of the epithelium at the lower part of the esophagus induced by chronic gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD). , Obesity, smoking, and male sex are the major risk factors for EAC, whereas ESCC is more strongly linked to alcohol consumption, tobacco smoking, dietary carcinogens such as nitroso compounds, and micronutrient deficiencies. – 
Recent multi-omics studies have revealed key molecular differences and overlap between ESCC and EAC. – While TP53 mutations are frequent in both types of tumors, EAC has a high prevalence of early amplification of the oncogenes ERBB2, CCNE1, and KRAS. Epigenetic mechanisms, including promoter hypermethylation of tumor suppressor genes such as CDKN2A and APC, are also crucial, particularly in early EAC development. , In ESCC, mutations in TP53, NOTCH1, NFE2L2, and KMT2D, as well as widespread epigenomic and transcriptomic reprogramming, are prominent features. 
Screening, early diagnosis, and timely treatment of EC are essential for reducing morbidity and mortality while improving prognosis and survival outcomes. Early detection and intervention significantly improve patient outcomes, particularly at high-grade precancerous lesions. When detected early, most ECs and their precancerous lesions can be removed endoscopically and minimally invasively, enabling patients to receive radical treatment and achieve a clinical cure, with a 5-year survival rate of up to 95%. 
Contemporary management of EC encompasses various therapeutic modalities, with surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy forming the cornerstone of treatment. , Both ESCC and EAC are currently treated with multimodal approaches, and the development of various novel therapeutic agents is flourishing. The emergence of targeted therapies, such as HER2-directed therapy in ERBB2-amplified EAC, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) for both subtypes, has expanded the therapeutic landscape. However, the heterogeneity of response and frequent recurrence after initial treatment underscore the need for personalized therapeutic strategies informed by molecular profiling. , 
In this review, we provide a comprehensive overview of EC, emphasizing recent progress in epidemiology, molecular pathogenesis, and clinical management. We highlight important advances in our understanding of EC carcinogenesis and cell-intrinsic and cell-extrinsic drivers of malignancy that will improve our ability to detect EC in the precancerous state. We discuss risk stratification and prevention strategies and highlight advances in the treatment of EC.

## Epidemiology

## Disease burden
EC is the seventh most common cause of cancer-related mortality globally, as reported in the 2022 Global Cancer Statistics. , , Five-year survival rates for EC remain poor in most countries, typically ranging from 10 to 30% after diagnosis. There are obvious sex differences in the incidence and mortality of EC, with males having nearly 3-fold higher incidence and mortality rates than females (age-standardized incidence rates of 7.6 vs 2.6 per 100,000, and mortality rates of 6.5 vs 2.2 per 100,000, respectively). 
Significant demographic disparities in the incidence of EC have been observed. Asia has the heaviest cancer burden due to its high population density, accounting for 59.2% of the total incidents in the world. Specifically, China alone accounts for 53.7%. Western Africa and Central America exhibit the lowest burdens. – Based on Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results data from the United States, the incidence of ESCC has continued to decline, while EAC has plateaued after a period of increase. 
Marked regional variation exists between EAC and ESCC. EAC shows the highest incidence rates in Northern Europe and North America, while ESCC is most prevalent in Eastern and South-Central Asia and South Africa. , Among them, ESCC is the most common histological type in China, accounting for 85.8% of all cases, followed by EAC (11.0%) and others (3.2%). , The age-standardized incidence and mortality rates were approximately 3-fold higher in males than in females, and nearly double in rural regions compared with urban areas. Among the seven administrative divisions, Central China exhibited the highest rates, with East and Southwest China following closely behind. In recent years, due to improvements in living standards and increased awareness of cancer prevention and control, the incidence and mortality rates of EC in China have shown a declining trend from 2000 to 2016, with the average annual percent changes of −4.3% (95% CI = −4.7 to −3.9%) and −3.8% (95% CI = −6.0 to −1.4%), respectively. 

## Predisposition and susceptibility of EC
Advances in DNA sequencing technology and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have greatly facilitated the identification of susceptibility genes or risk loci for EC development. These findings provide a foundation for understanding the etiology of EC and open possibilities for early prevention and intervention.
Early studies used candidate gene approaches to identify EC susceptibility loci. Genetic variants in the metabolic enzyme cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) may play a role in mediating susceptibility to ESCC. Moreover, the COX pathway has also been implicated in the risk of developing BE, the precursor lesion to EAC. Specifically, 3 genetic variants of MGST1, a gene involved in the COX pathway, have been associated with an increased risk of both BE and EAC. Other genetic risk loci for EC have also been documented. The GSTP1 polymorphic variant (GSTP1b) has been reported to be significantly enriched in BE (70% versus 41%, P < 0.001) and EAC (76% versus 41%, P = 0.005), which was correlated with reduced GST enzyme activity. The 461G>A mutation in the S100A14 locus is associated with susceptibility to ESCC among smokers, with an odds ratio (OR) of 2.01 (95% CI = 1.50–2.69). 
TP53 polymorphisms have been shown to be genetic determinants of the development of ESCC. An increased risk of ESCC has been associated with the TP53 codon 72Arg>Pro (OR = 1.83, 95% CI = 1.43–2.35, P < 0.001). The 49G>A polymorphism in the CTLA-4 leading sequence, which causes an Ala-to-Thr substitution, has been associated with increased susceptibility to multiple cancers, including lung, breast, esophageal, and gastric cardia cancers. A six-nucleotide deletion in the CASP8 promoter has been associated with decreased susceptibility to EC. 
GWAS approach plays an important role in identifying cancer susceptibility loci, especially single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Through large-scale GWAS in multiple cohorts from five regions in China, susceptibility loci of ESCC on chromosomes 3q27, 4q23, 5q11, 6p21, 10q23, 12q24, 16q12.1, 17p13, 17q21, 18p11, 21q22, and 22q12 have been identified. The associated genes include RPL6, PTPN11, PDE4D, RUNX1, and UNC5CL, all of which are cancer-related genes. Variants in SLC39A6 have been associated with reduced survival time in patients with ESCC. Specifically, rs7242481, located in the 5′ untranslated region (UTR) of SLC39A6, disturbs a transcriptional repressor binding site and results in the upregulation of SLC39A6 expression. Moreover, SLC39A6 promotes the proliferation and invasion of ESCC cells. The rs2274223 SNP in PLCE1 has also been reported to be a genetic risk factor for ESCC development. Its expression in ESCC is significantly greater than that in normal esophageal tissues. 
Pooled analysis of three GWASs of ESCC was subsequently conducted, and 3 new risk loci were found and achieved genome-wide significance: rs7447927 in TMEM173, rs1642764 in ATP1B2, and rs35597309 in the HLA class II region. Six susceptibility loci in CCHCR1, TCN2, TNXB, CYP26B1, and FASN have been found in ESCC from an exome-wide interrogation of large Chinese population cohorts. Further analysis demonstrated that the CYP26B1 variant has an increased ability to catabolize serum all-trans retinoic acid, an anticancer nutrient, which may contribute to increased susceptibility to ESCC. Reportedly, susceptible loci for EAC are also implicated in ESCC development. The G allele of rs11789015 at 9q22 has been shown to be associated with a reduced risk of both EAC and ESCC, likely by downregulating BARX1, a gene often overexpressed in ESCC that promotes tumor invasion. The missense mutation of the RHBDF2 gene leads to the occurrence of tylosis (hyperkeratosis palmaris et plantaris), a condition that confers a lifelong high risk for developing ESCC. It has been reported that in families with tylosis, the lifetime risk of developing ESCC by the age of 65 is as high as 95%. Exome sequencing of nonsense mutations in DNAH9 and missense mutations in GKAP1, BAG1, NFX1, FUK, and DDOST has suggested that these mutations are related to the occurrence of ESCC. 
Large GWAS meta-analyses of BE and EAC have identified several novel risk loci, including CFTR (rs17451754, P = 4.8 × 10⁻¹⁰), MSRA (rs17749155, P = 5.2 × 10⁻¹⁰), TPPP and CEP72 (rs9918259, P = 3.2 × 10⁻⁹), TMOD1 (rs7852462, P = 1.5 × 10⁻⁸), FMNL2 (rs12987825, P = 3.88 × 10⁻⁸), SLC66A1L (rs60849513, P = 9.56 × 10⁻11), HTR3C and ABCC5 (rs9823696, P = 1.6 × 10⁻⁸). , Additional SNPs associated with BE and EAC have also been identified: rs10419226 at 19p13 in CRTC1 (P = 3.6 × 10⁻¹⁰), whose aberrant activation is linked to oncogenic activity; rs11789015 at 9q22 in BARX1 (P = 1.0 × 10⁻⁹), a transcription factor essential for esophageal specification; and rs2687201 at 3p14 near FOXP1 (P = 5.5 × 10⁻⁹), a regulator involved in esophageal development. Sex-specific genetic differences have been observed in BE and EAC susceptibility. In males, a variant at 6q11.1 (rs112894788, KHDRBS2-MTRNR2L9) has been shown to be significantly associated with BE and EAC risk, whereas in females, a variant at 8p23.1 (rs13259457, PRSS55-RP1L1) has been reported to have a borderline association with disease risk. Variants rs3072 at 2p24.1 (OR = 1.14, 95% CI = 1.09–1.18, P = 1.8 × 10⁻¹¹) and rs2701108 at 12q24.21 (OR = 0.90, 95% CI = 0.86–0.93, P = 7.5 × 10⁻⁹), located near GDF7 and TBX5, respectively, are suggested to be associated with BE. GDF7 encodes a ligand in the bone morphogenetic protein pathway, and TBX5 encodes a transcription factor that regulates esophageal and cardiac development. Additional variants, such as rs9257809 at 6p21 within the MHC region (P = 4.09 × 10⁻⁹) and rs9936833 at 16q24 near FOXF1 (P = 2.74 × 10⁻¹⁰), have also been linked to BE. In EAC, a rare germline mutation (S631G) in VSIG10L, affecting a highly conserved serine residue, has been identified in affected BE families. Ultrastructural analysis of esophageal squamous mucosa harboring this mutation revealed dilated intercellular spaces and reduced desmosomes. Moreover, the IL-18RAP SNP rs917997C allele has been shown to have a strong protective effect against both BE (OR = 0.59, P = 0.0002) and EAC (OR = 0.46, P = 6 × 10⁻⁷). Germline mutations in MSR1, ASCC1, and CTHRC1 have also been significantly associated with susceptibility to BE and EAC, with the MSR1 nonsense mutation (c.877C>T, p.R293X) being the most frequent in discovery cohorts and potentially contributing to CCND1 overexpression. 
Gene‒environment interactions play a critical role in EC development. For example, rs13429103 at 2p25.1, located near RNF144A-LOC339788, demonstrated a borderline interaction with smoking (P = 2.18 × 10⁻⁷). Notably, ever smokers carrying the rs13429103-AA genotype exhibit an 11.82-fold increased risk of EAC (95% CI = 4.03–34.67). Variants in metabolism-related genes may be pivotal in the onset of EC. For example, ALDH2 and ADH2 are metabolic enzymes that play crucial roles in tobacco- and alcohol-related ESCC susceptibility. The ALDH2 gene encodes mitochondrial aldehyde dehydrogenase-2, and ADH2 encodes alcohol dehydrogenase-2. Studies across Japanese – and Chinese populations have consistently demonstrated that ALDH2 heterozygosity represents a strong risk factor for EC. The risk is further amplified in individuals with both ALDH2 heterozygosity and ADH2 gene type 1 homozygosity, with an OR of 17.9 (P < 0.001). Drinkers carrying ADH1B (GA or AA genotype) and ALDH2 (GA or AA genotype) risk alleles have a 4-fold greater risk for ESCC than do drinkers not carrying these risk alleles. The NQO1 gene encodes NAD(P)H quinone oxidoreductase 1, a cytoplasmic antioxidant enzyme that detoxifies quinones and reduces oxidative stress. The C609T polymorphism in NQO1 has significant protective effects, with the homozygous TT genotype associated with a reduced risk of both BE (OR = 0.22, 95% CI = 0.07–0.76, P = 0.01) and EAC (OR = 0.16, 95% CI = 0.03–0.94, P = 0.04) compared with the TC/CC genotypes, suggesting a potential defensive role against reflux-related carcinogenesis.

## Environmental carcinogens and lifestyle risk factors for EC
Currently, risk factors for EC have been widely identified, including lifestyle and dietary habits, certain environmental exposures, nutritional deficiencies, and socioeconomic status. – Based on current evidence, certain factors, such as tobacco smoking, have been identified as common risk factors for different subtypes of EC. , However, other factors are subtype-specific. Specifically, gastroesophageal reflux, obesity, and tobacco use are independent risk factors for EAC. , Obesity and GERD together contribute to 70.3% of EAC cases (95% CI = 64.4–76.2%). Obesity, defined by increasing body mass index (BMI), is significantly associated with a heightened risk of GERD symptoms and EAC. A pooled analysis revealed a graded association between BMI and EAC risk. The adjusted ORs are 1.52 (95% CI = 1.15–2.01) for individuals with a BMI of 25–30 kg/m² and 2.78 (95% CI = 1.85–4.16) for individuals with a BMI greater than 30 kg/m² compared with those with a normal weight. In addition, obesity in early life is associated with the development of EAC and gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma. , Studies using genetically engineered mouse models have shown that a high-fat diet accelerates EAC progression through gut microbiota-dependent inflammation and stem cell expansion, independent of body weight. Research has shown that the Mediterranean diet and a healthy dietary pattern are negatively correlated with EC risk in humans. 
In addition to these general and lifestyle-related risk factors, BE, defined as intestinal metaplasia in the distal esophagus, is a complication of GERD and a precursor lesion in most cases of EAC. Patients with BE have an approximately 3–5% risk for developing EAC in their lifetime. However, the risk of developing EAC from BE varies among individuals. Longer Barrett’s segments, larger hiatal hernias, and severe acid reflux contribute to progression to EAC, with white males being more susceptible. Several characteristics have been associated with higher rates of progression from BE to EAC. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 20 cohort studies consisting of 74,943 patients with BE reported that older age (OR = 1.027, 95% CI = 1.007–1.046), male sex (OR = 2.16, 95% CI = 1.84–2.53, versus female sex), history of smoking (OR = 1.47, 95% CI = 1.09–1.98, versus never smoking), and low-grade dysplasia (OR = 4.25, 95% CI = 2.58–7.00) were associated with an increased risk of progression to EAC. Hormones may influence EAC risk. In men, higher circulating testosterone levels are inversely associated with the risk of EAC, with those in the highest quartile showing a 56% reduced risk compared with those in the lowest quartile after adjusting for BMI (OR = 0.44, 95% CI = 0.22–0.88, P = 0.02). These findings highlight the importance of lifestyle intervention in EAC prevention. Additionally, further research is needed to optimize and validate tools used for screening BE and EAC. 
Risk factors such as alcohol use, betel nut chewing, consumption of pickled vegetables and hot foods or drinks, and exposure to X- and γ-radiation are believed to contribute to the development of ESCC. , Diets high in processed meats, salt, and smoked foods have also been associated with increased ESCC risk. Tobacco smoking and alcohol consumption have emerged as primary modifiable risk factors for ESCC, with robust epidemiological evidence supporting their relationship. Freedman et al. reported that current smokers are at increased risk for ESCC. Individuals consuming more than three alcoholic beverages per day had a significantly greater risk of ESCC (hazard ratio = 4.93, 95% CI = 2.69–9.03) than those consuming only one drink per day. According to an epidemiological survey conducted in four high-incidence areas of ESCC in China, the relative risk ratio of ESCC is 1.32 (95% CI = 1.15–1.51) for current smokers. Moreover, in a study on EAC, individuals with BE were significantly more likely to be ever smokers than were population-based controls (OR = 1.67, 95% CI = 1.04–2.67) and GERD controls (OR = 1.61, 95% CI = 1.33–1.96), and the risk of BE increased with increasing number of pack-years of smoking. 
GWAS studies have also demonstrated that ESCC susceptibility loci affect tumor risk through gene‒lifestyle interactions, with more pronounced risk enhancement observed in tobacco and alcohol users. , Through genomic aberrations, alcohol and tobacco might increase the ESCC incidence rate, as evidenced by alcohol-related mutational signatures identified in ESCC patients, which are linked to increased mutations in genes, including ZNF750, TP53, and EP300. , Additionally, there are increased numbers of NOTCH1 and PPM1D mutations in smokers and drinkers. Tobacco and alcohol promote ESCC carcinogenesis by inducing DNA damage via the formation of DNA adducts and the induction of crosslinking, respectively. 
Malnutrition, such as a lack of trace elements and vitamins, is also linked to the risk of ESCC. However, the relationship between nutritional intervention and ESCC prevention remains controversial, as evidenced by conflicting findings in clinical trials. A large nutrient intervention trial confirmed that supplementation with beta-carotene, vitamin E, and selenium can reduce the incidence of EC in people under 55 years of age. However, its efficacy in older people remains uncertain. These contradictory findings underscore the complexity of nutritional factors in ESCC development and highlight the need for a more nuanced understanding of diet-related risk factors.
Environmental exposure to certain carcinogens—including dietary nitrosamines from pickled foods, fungal toxins from contaminated grains, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons from charred meats—may contribute to the development of ESCC. – Analysis of the components from drinking water in Cixian County and Shexian County, two regions with high EC prevalence in China, revealed that nitrite, ammonia, and nitrogen concentrations in water were positively correlated with the incidence of EC. , Long-term exposure to fine particulate matter that ≤ 2.5 μm (PM2.5) in the air increases the risk of ESCC. A population-based study reported that for every 10 μg/m3 increase in PM2.5 exposure, the risk of ESCC increased by 16% (HR = 1.16, 95% CI = 1.04–1.30), likely due to chronic inflammation and DNA damage induced by airborne carcinogens. Furthermore, heat stress, another environmental insult, has been shown to promote angiogenesis and ESCC formation by increasing TRPV2 expression. 

## Clinical and pathological features

## Clinical features
Typical clinical manifestations of EC include progressive dysphagia, choking, and sensations of burning or a foreign body. , Patients may feel pain behind the sternum, regurgitation of food or sour liquid, and heartburn. Patients may initially have difficulty consuming solid foods, which gradually progresses to the need for semiliquid or liquid diets. Other symptoms include fever, chest tightness, shortness of breath, vomiting, melena, hoarseness, or coughing while drinking water. In advanced stages, patients often present with weight loss, fatigue, and generalized physical weakness. Early EC usually lacks specific signs. As the disease progresses into the intermediate and advanced stages, various signs may appear due to the invasion of adjacent organs. For instance, enlarged cervical or supraclavicular lymph nodes may suggest lymphatic metastasis; jaundice, hepatomegaly, or liver tenderness may indicate hepatic involvement; and reduced chest wall movement, shallow, rapid breathing, and tracheal deviation toward the unaffected side may be indicative of malignant pleural effusion. 

## Pathological features
The latest WHO histological classification of esophageal malignancies includes five categories: adenocarcinoma, adenoid cystic carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, undifferentiated carcinoma, and neuroendocrine neoplasms. SCC and AC are the two most common types of EC. SCC is most commonly located in the middle third of the esophagus, followed by the lower third. In a large Japanese patient cohort, more than half (55%) of SCCs occurred in the middle esophagus, 32% in the lower esophagus, and 14% in the upper esophagus. The grading of ESCC is based on the degree of cytological atypia, mitotic activity, and presence of keratinization. Grade 1 tumors are well differentiated and feature enlarged cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm and keratin pearl formation, minimal atypia, and low mitotic activity. Grade 2 tumors are moderately differentiated, with increased atypia, disorganized architecture, more frequent mitoses, and occasional partial hardening of surface cells. Grade 3 tumors are poorly differentiated, consisting mainly of basal-like cells forming irregular nests, often with central necrosis and rare keratinization. Moreover, SCC can also be classified into three histologic subtypes on the basis of growth patterns and cytologic features: verrucous, spindle cell, and basaloid SCC. Among these subtypes, basaloid SCC accounts for approximately 5% of all EC cases, showing a solid or nested growth pattern of basaloid cells and a marked male predominance. Currently, no molecular testing is routinely required to support the diagnosis of ESCC.
The second most common pathological type is AC. AC can exhibit gastric, intestinal, or mixed differentiation, reflecting its distinct pathogenesis. Under chronic exposure to acid and bile reflux, stem or progenitor cells located at the basal layer of the gastroesophageal junction epithelium undergo reprogramming toward a columnar epithelial phenotype. This metaplastic epithelium may subsequently undergo either gastric or intestinal differentiation. The presence of goblet cells characterizes intestinal-type metaplasia, which defines BE. The grading of AC is determined by the proportion of glandular structures within the tumor: tumors with more than 95% well-formed glandular structures are classified as grade 1, those with less than 50% are classified as grade 3, and cases falling between these thresholds are classified as grade 2. Immunohistochemical staining can support the diagnosis, typically showing positivity for CK7 and CK19, and negativity for CK20. 
Other uncommon histological types include adenoid cystic carcinoma, neuroendocrine tumors, and neuroendocrine carcinomas. Adenoid cystic carcinoma of the esophagus is a rare malignant tumor composed of epithelial and myoepithelial cells that are typically arranged in gland-like or tubular structures. It constitutes only approximately 0.1% of all EC cases. Neuroendocrine neoplasms are rare epithelial tumors characterized by neuroendocrine differentiation and are exceedingly rare, accounting for only 0.04–0.1% of all gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms. 

## Cell-intrinsic drivers of the EC

## Somatic mutations in EC
Somatic mutations play a critical role in the initiation and progression of EC (Fig. ). Driver genes of different subtypes of EC exhibit heterogeneity. A combined analysis of whole-genome sequencing (WGS) of 704 ESCC samples from multiple cohorts has identified the 20 most common somatic mutations as the driver mutations, including TP53, NOTCH1, MLL2, FAT1, NFE2L2, PIK3CA, EP300, ZNF750, CDKN2A, CREBBP, FBXW7, NOTCH3, PTCH1, RB1, KDM6A, AJUBA, TGFBR2, CUL3, PTEN, and RBPJ1. Specifically, TP53 is mutated in 85% of ESCC cases, while mutations in NOTCH1, MLL2, and FAT1 occur in 10%–17% of cases. , , , , Notably, 93% of ESCC samples harbored at least one somatic mutation in these genes, highlighting their significant role in ESCC carcinogenesis (Table ). For instance, functional studies indicate that silencing CUL3 or RBPJ can significantly promote malignant phenotypes of ESCC cells, such as proliferation, migration, and invasion, while overexpressing these genes inhibits these phenotypes. Alterations in the retinoblastoma gene (RB1) are frequent and exhibit strong interdependence with mutations in TP53 in ESCC. Interestingly, loss of heterozygosity (LOH) of RB1 is significantly more frequent in tumors harboring TP53 mutations, suggesting potential synergistic effects in disrupting both the Rb and TP53 pathways. , 
The most common driver mutations in EAC include TP53, SMAD4, CDKN2A, ARID1A, APC, PIK3CA, KRAS, MYC, CDK6, VEGFA, CCND1, CCNE1, EGFR, ERBB2, SMARCA4, PBRM1, FBXW7, ELMO1, and DOCK2. , , Compared with wild-type ELMO1, mutated ELMO1 significantly promotes EAC invasion. In addition, driver mutations may impact treatment. , 
CDKN2A, KRAS, and SMAD4 mutations act as prognostic biomarkers linked to poor survival in EAC patients. , Specifically, while CDKN2A loss in BE suppresses EAC initiation by counterselecting TP53 mutations, its deletion in EAC drives poor prognosis via cell cycle dysregulation and metabolic–immune reprogramming. Both BE (14.2%) and EAC (11.7%) have activating mutations in the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) gene in a single study, including the well-known sensitizing mutation L858R and in-frame deletion delE746-A750, suggesting that such genetic alterations may occur early in the course of neoplastic transformation and highlighting the potential utility of genotype-directed tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) therapy even in premalignant stages. 
Notably, multiple studies have consistently demonstrated that TP53 is the most frequently mutated gene in ESCC, occurring in approximately 90% of ESCC patients , , – and has a high mutation frequency in EAC patients. The inactivation of TP53 promotes genomic instability and thus has potent carcinogenicity, which has been reported in both subtypes of EC. , 
TP53 mutations have other biological effects. A case in point is the TP53 R172H mutation in a BE mouse model, which drives the expansion of undifferentiated Cck2r+ progenitor cells and impairs their differentiation, ultimately promoting BE progression to EAC. Moreover, biallelic TP53 inactivation seems to be necessary during malignant transformation. Studies have shown that the key distinction between stable BE and BE progressing to cancer lies in the acquisition and expansion of TP53−/− cell populations, which are characterized by complex structural variants and high-level amplifications. , A case‒control genomic study of BE patients with and without subsequent progression to EAC revealed TP53 mutations in 46% of progressors compared with only 5% of nonprogressors. This finding indicates that TP53 mutation is a potential strong predictor of the malignant progression of BE. Interestingly, there may be some stage-specific mutations involved in the development of EAC. A study screening 109 biopsies from benign metaplastic never-dysplastic BE and high-grade dysplasia (HGD) revealed that TP53 was recurrently mutated in both HGD and EAC samples, whereas mutated SMAD4 was found only in EAC. 
Apart from mutations in ESCC or EAC, recent studies have also reported the widespread accumulation of somatic mutations and clonal expansion in normal esophageal tissues. As age increases, normal esophageal epithelium accumulates a large number of somatic mutations, forming numerous mutant clones that can cover a significant proportion of the epithelial surface area. Among these, NOTCH1 and TP53 mutant clones are particularly common in middle-aged and elderly people. Interestingly, the proportion of NOTCH1 mutations in normal esophagus is much higher than in EC tumors. These findings suggest that somatic mutations may be early molecular events in the development of EC, but not all mutant clones progress to cancer, and other key events are required for cancer progression. , , Additionally, by integrating single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), researchers have revealed an association between NOTCH1 mutations and a shift from epithelial differentiation toward a mesenchymal-like phenotype in ESCC, suggesting that NOTCH1 mutations may promote ESCC by impairing epithelial cell differentiation. However, the activation of wild-type Notch signaling may exert protumorigenic effects. Notch signaling is overactivated in ESCC and promotes tumor angiogenesis by upregulating proangiogenic factors, including VEGF, ANGPT2, and CXCL1. 
In addition to mutations in coding regions, ESCC also harbors substantial alterations in noncoding genomic regions, underscoring the potential regulatory roles in tumorigenesis. , Fifteen noncoding regions have mutation frequencies that are significantly higher than background levels in ESCC, including regions involved in regulating the expression of the lncRNA NEAT1 and the 3′ UTRs of the FOXJ3 and CLOCK genes. In parallel, EAC harbors recurrent alterations in noncoding regions of the genome. Several known noncoding driver elements have been discovered, including the enhancer on chromosome 7, which is associated with TP53TG1; the promoter and 5′-untranslated regions of PTDSS1; the 5′-untranslated region of MMP24; and the promoters of two related histone-encoding genes, HIST1H2BO and HIST1H2AM. These findings further reveal the potential roles of noncoding sequence mutations in ESCC carcinogenesis.
Notably, extensive somatic mutations appear in the precancerous stages of ESCC. Dysplastic esophageal squamous epithelium already carries most of the driver mutations that are detected in ESCC. As lesions progress, different types of genomic alterations display dynamic changes: biallelic inactivation of TP53 significantly increases in early precancerous lesions, and copy number variations (CNVs) and APOBEC-mediated mutations markedly accumulate in late-stage lesions. These findings further elucidate the dynamic changes in mutations during the different stages of ESCC development.

## Chromosomal instability in EC
Chromosomal instability is a notable characteristic of most cancers, , including EC. In the early stages of ESCC, genomes begin to exhibit high instability and polyploidy. This genomic instability progressively accumulates throughout ESCC development, with extensive CNVs emerging as early as the LGIN stage. CNVs across different genomic regions can alter the expression of specific genes, thereby disrupting their functions and potentially impairing tumor-suppressive mechanisms, ultimately facilitating malignant transformation. In ESCC, such alterations often involve amplification or deletion of genes associated with cell cycle regulation, DNA repair, and apoptosis. These variations are further exacerbated in the HGIN and ESCC stages, indicating that chromosomal instability worsens with disease progression. For example, certain CNVs arising from the LGIN state include gains in chr3q, chr5p, chr8p, and chr8q. More CNVs become pronounced and evident in HGIN and ESCC stages, including gain of chr1q, chr11q, and loss of chr5p. The genes affected by these CNVs include SOX2, NFE2L2, CDKN2A, CCND1, and CDCA7, which potentially influence ESCC development. , , Reduced copy numbers of genes located in certain regions, such as ERRFI1 and DLC1, lead to downregulated protein expression, which may play a role in ESCC progression by affecting the EGFR and RhoA pathways. Moreover, studies have suggested that long-term chromosomal instability increases the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) within ESCC cells, promoting ESCC development by inhibiting apoptosis through the activation of the JNK signaling pathway. 
Gene amplification and deletion are also frequent in EAC and the precancerous stage. , – In a comprehensive screening, amplification events were found in 57% of EAC cases, with high amplification frequencies for ERBB2 (21.8%), CCNE1 (12.6%), GATA4 (10.3%), and KRAS (10.3%). Moreover, some of these amplifications—such as those of KRAS, CCND1, and ERBB2—have also been detected in HGD, suggesting that they occur early in tumorigenesis. Multiple minimally deleted regions on chromosome 17 have been identified in intestinal metaplasia and intermediate-grade dysplasia of the esophagus, which expand into large deletions in carcinoma. Using fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) assays, early chromosomal alterations have also been detected in BE, particularly hyperploid chromosomes 4 and 8, which are prevalent in nondysplastic metaplastic epithelium (89% and 71%, respectively). These findings not only support the biological importance of gene amplification in EAC but also highlight the potential for early detection strategies and targeted therapies directed at amplified oncogenes. A study that combined WGS and SNP array profiling has shown that genomic catastrophes are frequent in EAC. Frequent LOH at the APC locus has been reported in EAC. The APC gene is located on chromosome 5q21, a position that is thought to play an important role in the development of the great majority of alimentary tract malignancies. However, further chromosomal analysis indicated that APC may not be the primary target of large deletions in 5q. Instead, IRF1, located at 5q31.1, has emerged as a candidate tumor suppressor gene within the commonly deleted region. 
Structural variations (SVs) further complicate chromosomal instability. WGS has shown that these SVs often lead to different mutational mechanisms affecting cancer-related genes, such as CDKN2A and NOTCH1. Additionally, chromothripsis, kataegis, and breakage-fusion bridges (BFBs) have been identified as contributors to localized chromosomal rearrangements in approximately 55% of ESCC cases. These genomic catastrophes result in the amplification of oncogenes through mechanisms such as the formation of double-minute chromosomes (e.g., FGFR1 and LETM2) or BFB cycles (e.g., CCND1, EGFR, ERBB2, MMPs, and MYC). Similar to ESCC, chromothripsis in EAC can reshape the epigenetic landscape by altering topologically associated domains, chromatin accessibility, and histone modifications, leading to coordinated changes in gene expression and chromosome conformation. 
Extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA) is chromosome-independent circular DNA in cancer cells and is recognized as an important manifestation of genomic instability in esophageal carcinogenesis. Longitudinal studies in Barrett’s esophagus and EAC progression cohorts revealed that ecDNA can be detected in premalignant lesions (24% of early-stage EAC cases) and increases in frequency during cancer progression (43% of late-stage EAC cases). Notably, ecDNA-positive premalignant lesions correlate with increased future cancer risk, and ecDNA exhibits progressive copy number amplification and structural complexity, indicating its role in fostering intratumoral heterogeneity and adaptive evolution.

## Epigenomic and epitranscriptomic alterations in EC
Epigenetic regulation refers to mechanisms that control gene expression without altering the DNA sequence. In addition to genomic alterations, the inclusion of epigenomic and epitranscriptomic mechanisms greatly enhances the complexity and flexibility of biological trait regulation. Numerous studies have identified various epigenomic and epitranscriptomic alterations in esophageal carcinogenesis, – including alterations in the expression of epigenetic-related enzymes, changes in noncoding RNA expression, DNA methylation changes leading to gene silencing or activation, and RNA modifications such as adenosine N6-methylation, which affect RNA stability, all of which contribute to EC progression. – 
From a macroscopic perspective, several studies of EC have revealed extensive genome-wide hypomethylation changes in cancer tissues compared with normal tissues. – Hypomethylation in gene promoters and gene bodies is often associated with increased expression, and specific methylation patterns have demonstrated diagnostic and prognostic value. , Epigenetic silencing through promoter hypermethylation represents a pivotal mechanism for tumor suppressor gene inactivation during the neoplastic progression of BE to EAC. , One of the most affected genes is CDKN2A, whose promoter region is frequently hypermethylated at the early stages of BE progression. In a longitudinal study of BE patients, CDKN2A promoter hypermethylation was detected in nearly all individuals who developed dysplasia but was absent in those who remained nondysplastic, suggesting that this epigenetic alteration precedes and potentially facilitates neoplastic transformation. Similarly, another study reported that 82% of EAC tumors and 30% of premalignant lesions, including intestinal metaplasia, harbored CDKN2A promoter hypermethylation, with a strong correlation between methylation and loss of p16 protein expression. Interestingly, LOH at the CDKN2A locus was also observed but was not significantly associated with loss of protein expression, indicating that methylation is the predominant silencing mechanism. 
The tumor suppressor gene APC on chromosome 5q21 is also frequently epigenetically altered in EAC. Although truncating mutations of APC are rare in EAC, hypermethylation of the APC promoter is observed in up to 92% of ACs, compared with only 50% in ESCC and none in normal tissue. Recent studies have validated the clinical utility of epigenetic biomarkers for BE risk stratification. The methylation-based EsopPredict assay demonstrates high sensitivity (81–100%) in predicting EAC progression via spatiotemporal biopsy analysis, effectively addressing surveillance challenges such as tissue heterogeneity. Multicenter validation confirmed robust risk stratification, with high-risk patients having a 15.2-fold greater 5-year progression risk than low-risk individuals did, supporting personalized BE management. Moreover, the level of hypermethylated APC DNA in plasma has been associated with poor survival in EAC patients, suggesting its utility as a noninvasive prognostic biomarker. However, further studies are needed to establish the clinical reliability of this biomarker.
Methylation profile alterations during esophageal carcinogenesis may be driven by two principal factors. One is mutations of genes encoding epigenetic modification proteins, and the other is the stimulation from environmental signals. WGS studies across multiple cohorts have identified frequent mutations in epigenetic regulatory genes, , , , suggesting the critical role of epigenetic dysregulation in driving ESCC formation.
Changes in chromatin structure via histone modifications add another layer of complexity to the epigenetic regulation of ECs. For example, joint analysis of H3K27ac-marked enhancer and transcriptomic data has revealed many enhancer and superenhancer differences between normal and cancerous tissues, supporting the expression of numerous ESCC-related genes. Global chromatin accessibility studies and multi-omics analysis of ESCC cell lines have identified TP63, SOX2, and KLF5 as part of a core gene regulatory network. This network is closely related to ESCC characteristics by regulating chromatin accessibility, epigenetic modifications, and gene expression profiles. Inhibiting histone deacetylases and bromodomain and extraterminal domain proteins, a protein family that can interpret epigenetic codes and play an important role in regulating gene transcription through identifying and binding acetylated histones or nonhistone proteins, can jointly suppress the growth of ESCC xenograft tumors, further highlighting the importance of epigenetic roles in ESCC. 
Aberrant epigenomic and epitranscriptomic regulation may be caused by individual genes or specific noncoding RNAs. Indeed, numerous studies have reported that epigenetic modification enzymes, either aberrantly expressed or frequently mutated, play a role in ESCC formation, including TET2, FTO, TET3, DNMT1 and HDAC3. In addition, several noncoding RNAs, such as miR-34a, miR-445-3p, lncRNA CASC9 and CCAT1, have been shown to play roles in cancer cell transformation in ESCC.
RNA modifications, particularly m6A methylation, represent another crucial dimension of epigenetic regulation in ESCC. Aberrant RNA m6A modification may affect the translation efficiency and half-life of the corresponding RNA. Cui et al. reported that FTO-mediated demethylation of m6A in the LINC00022 transcript stabilizes the RNA, allowing it to bind the p21 protein, leading to its degradation and promoting ESCC progression. Deng et al. reported that RNA m6A modification can regulate DNA methylation through a novel mechanism. They reported that METTL3-mediated RNA m6A modification recruits FXR1 and TET1 to corresponding genomic sites, leading to demethylation of those DNA regions and thus causing overexpression of oncogenes in ESCC cells. 
Owing to the diversity of epigenetic mechanisms and their functions, comprehensively summarizing their roles in EC is challenging. Epigenetic regulation is a widely discussed mechanism used to explain cancer phenotypes that lack clear genetic cues. As research continues to accumulate, the understanding of epigenetic modulation and its roles in ESCC remains in development.

## The origin of EC cells
The esophageal epithelium consists of nonkeratinized squamous epithelia containing the basal layer, the suprabasal layer, and the differentiated layer. Through division, differentiation, and self-renewal, basal layer cells maintain tissue homeostasis and have been hypothesized to be the origin of ESCC cells. Basal cells endure long-term mutation accumulation and clonal selection within histopathologically normal tissue, driven by sophisticated interactions between genetic factors and the microenvironment, which might ultimately generate invasive cancers. 
Driven by the demand for cancer prevention and early diagnosis, studies concerning markers of basal cells and the molecular mechanisms involved in transformation are increasing. Various markers have been proposed to identify and track basal cells, most likely those basal stem cells considered to be the origin of ESCC. Liu et al. reported that the interaction between SOX2 and STAT3 activation in basal layer cells facilitates malignant transformation, whereas SOX2-negative nonbasal layer cells are not subject to this signaling-induced transformation. Giroux et al. reported KRT15+ long-lived basal layer cells in mice and suggested KRT15 as a marker for basal layer stem cells. Yu et al. identified two distinct esophageal epithelial cell populations: p63⁺SOX2⁺ cells, which are responsible for maintaining stemness and proliferation, and p63−SOX2⁺ cells, which are involved in differentiation and damage repair; notably, the latter population is absent in ESCC-derived organoids. Three-dimensional organoid culture of ESCC tissues may help identify their original cells. SOX2/WNT/BMP signaling promotes self-renewal of esophageal basal layer cells, and KRT15⁺ long-lived basal cells in mice are considered putative esophageal epithelial stem cells and potential cells of origin for ESCC. With advances in methodology, more cancer stem cell markers have been proposed, which might be useful for further defining the origin of ESCC. – 
Through mutations or epigenetic alterations, basal layer cells acquire abnormal expression of cancer-related genes to complete malignant transformation. Many studies have highlighted chronic inflammation as a significant promoter of cancer, creating an inductive environment conducive to cancer cell transformation and laying the foundation for cancer development through interactions with precancerous epithelial cells. – For example, Klf4 overexpression leads to an imbalanced cell fate with increased renewal and proliferation due to basal layer inflammation, driving EC in mice. 
CTNND1 deletion-induced chronic inflammation and immune infiltration promote cancer transformation. The interaction between SOX2 and STAT3 in an inflammatory environment promotes cancer transformation. Using immunohistochemical analysis of ESCC tissues, Ye et al. reported CK14+CD98hc+CK6-Ki67- in basal cells, CK14-CD98hc−CK6+Ki67+ in proliferating cells, and CK14+CD98hc+CK6+Ki67+ in both basal and proliferating cells, suggesting that CD98hc serves as a basal-layer cell marker associated with chronic inflammation. Its ectopic expression disrupts the connection between basal layer cells and the basement membrane, damaging the integrity between the stromal layer and the epithelium and thereby inducing carcinogenesis. Moreover, TP53 mutation, the most prominent genomic mutation in ESCC, has been shown to induce aberrant interactions among basal layers in early precancerous lesions. Mechanistically, TP53 mutation caused the overexpression of TP63, which transcriptionally upregulated EFNB1 and EPHB4 in epithelial cells and strengthened their interaction, further triggering EMT and the cell cycle via downstream SRC/ERK/AKT signaling. Carcinogen exposure also triggers chronic inflammation and promotes carcinogenesis. , 
At the cellular level, BE contains a distinct stem cell population that drives regenerative growth and may serve as a cellular origin for malignant transformation. Lineage-tracing studies have revealed that EAC can alternatively arise from nongoblet columnar epithelium, expanding our understanding of BE-related carcinogenesis. When isolated and transformed in vitro, BE-derived stem cells exhibit a wide range of genomic alterations and long-term self-renewal capabilities and are deemed the origin cells of EAC. Importantly, Lgr5+ stem cells within esophageal glands have been functionally implicated in EAC development, indicating the existence of multiple cellular reservoirs for tumor initiation. 
The cancer development process also involves immune escape and immunosuppression of cancer cells, which serve as a significant force in shaping cancer characteristics. , On this basis, we believe that precancerous cells are progenitor cells that accumulate genome damage during replication, fail to achieve normal differentiation, and enter a state characterized by abnormal, mixed differentiation and stemness features. The abnormal regulatory spectrum of esophageal-specific transcription factors characterizes a precancerous epithelial state that precedes the emergence of overt cancer cells. This model provides a foundation for further revealing early changes determined by the internal fate of the epithelium during carcinogenesis. Pan et al. validated this phenomenon through single-cell transcriptional analysis and described the phenomenon of “confused epithelial cell identity” as an early epithelial cancer transformation signal. 

## Clonality of EC
Mutations accumulate as stem cells undergo renewal and proliferation in normal esophageal mucosa, resulting in a chimeric clonal spectrum within the esophageal epithelium, which is considered a possible origin of cancer clones. , Histopathological studies as early as 1992 identified intraepithelial neoplasia adjacent to ESCC lesions, supporting the field cancerization hypothesis. These findings suggest that cancer clones may emerge from a genetically altered epithelial field rather than from a single transformed cell. – Multisite-sampling studies investigating clonal heterogeneity within and between ESCC tumors support the field cancerization theory of polyclonal origins in ESCC and demonstrate that high intratumor heterogeneity is associated with poor prognosis. 
Widespread large clones carrying TP53 mutations have been observed in both pathologically normal aged esophageal epithelium and precancerous dysplasia. , , The survival advantages conferred by certain mutations under environmental selection pressures allow these clones to gradually expand in normal tissue until additional cancer-driving mutations are acquired, ultimately progressing to cancer clones , (Fig. ). For example, functional studies in mice have revealed that Trp53 loss confers a selective advantage and promotes the development of gastric dysplasia in the presence of dietary carcinogens. However, it is important to note that not all expanding clones represent high-risk precancerous lesions. The elimination of precancerous clones through clonal competition may be a key cancer-suppression mechanism. Colom et al. reported that NOTCH1 mutant clones can outcompete and eliminate high-risk TP53 mutant clones, thereby potentially delaying ESCC initiation. , WGS analysis of multi-stage ESCC development reveals the clonal evolutionary dynamics underlying tumorigenesis, with TP53 mutations and LOH serving as early trunk events that drive clonal expansion and initiate malignant progression. The subsequent accumulation of numerous subclone mutations and CNVs further drives cancer progression. As lesions advance to later stages (from HGIN to ESCC), the continued expansion of cancer clones within the tumor can be observed, providing evidence of cancer cell self-renewal and coevolution with the tumor microenvironment (TME). 
Because it is recognized as the precursor lesion to EAC, BE has been extensively studied in terms of its clonal architecture. , – The presence of substantial intralesional clonal heterogeneity underscores the complexity of its neoplastic progression. A recent high-resolution analysis of individual crypts via laser capture microdissection revealed that Barrett’s metaplasia may originate from progenitor cells residing in glandular ducts. This conclusion is built on the findings that a CDKN2A point mutation is observed in the squamous epithelium of an esophageal gland duct and an adjacent metaplastic crypt. The evolutionary dynamics of BE have been elucidated through a longitudinal surveillance study. Analysis via multicolor FISH of brush cytology specimens revealed that genetic diversity predicts progression but could remain in stable dynamic equilibrium over time. Interestingly, clonal diversity indices adapted from ecology and evolutionary biology could predict progression from BE to EAC, even after controlling for established genetic risk factors such as TP53 mutations and aneuploidy, which underscores the possibility that the accumulation of diverse clones, rather than genetic homogenization, may be a key principle of EAC neoplastic evolution. 
Evolutionary analysis at the crypt and biopsy level adds another dimension to our understanding of BE evolution. In a study involving extensive sampling from BE patients with and without progression over 6.1–7.6 years of surveillance, BE segments were shown to be largely clonal. Clonal expansion occurs at an average rate of 1.58 cm2 (95% CI = 0.09–4.06) per year, often involving the CDKN2A locus. The inactivation of CDKN2A by hypermethylation appears to be an early and clonally expansive event, as genetically altered diploid progenitor cells with p16 and TP53 abnormalities have been shown to spread across large mucosal areas before the acquisition of aneuploidy or cancer. This clonal evolution involves subsequent stochastic events such as LOH at multiple chromosomal loci, including 5q, but not in a fixed order, underscoring the complexity of EAC pathogenesis. Understanding clonal expansion dynamics offers valuable insights for risk stratification and early intervention. However, the lack of longitudinal sampling in most studies remains a key obstacle to accurately modeling individual progression trajectories. 

## Metabolic reprogramming of EC
Metabolic reprogramming has emerged as a hallmark of cancer. ESCC exhibits widespread metabolic aberrations that contribute to its pathogenesis. , ESCC cells display glycolytic aberrations characterized by increased TIGAR expression, which reprograms glucose metabolism through AMPK. Penfluridol, which targets AMPK/FOXO3a/BIM, can inhibit glycolysis and suppress tumor progression. Abnormalities in lipid metabolism have also been observed in ECs. , Exome-wide association analysis revealed that genetic variants in the fatty acid biosynthesis enzyme FASN are associated with an increased risk of ESCC. Upregulation of CPT1A, a key enzyme in fatty acid oxidation, protects ESCC cells from apoptosis by maintaining redox homeostasis. Amino acid metabolic abnormalities, such as the overexpression of tryptophan 2,3-dioxygenase 2, upregulate IL-8 and guide M2 macrophage polarization. Aberrations in alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism throughout the course of ESCC evolution have been shown to further contribute to tumor progression. Targeting these pathways may improve patient prognosis.
In EAC, aberrant arachidonic acid (AA) metabolism has been suggested to play an important role in tumor development. For example, COX2 is overexpressed in human EAC, and its high expression level is associated with a poor clinical prognosis. 
Cox2 is also overexpressed during rat esophageal adenocarcinogenesis. Targeting COX2 with specific inhibitors can induce apoptosis in human EAC cell lines and primary esophageal epithelial cells from biopsy samples, suggesting its potential antitumor efficacy. The 5-lipoxygenase (5-Lox) pathway also plays a key role in esophageal carcinogenesis by generating AA metabolites and activating inflammatory cells. Inhibiting the 5-Lox and COX2 pathways with zileuton and celecoxib significantly reduces EAC incidence, supporting their potential as chemopreventive targets. When the AA pathway is dysregulated, the EAC also exhibits reprogramming of de novo lipogenesis, which leads to phospholipidome alterations. This process is driven by the overexpression of ATP-citrate lyase (ACLY), a key enzyme that provides acetyl-CoA building blocks for new fatty acid synthesis. The increased production of these fatty acids results in a remodeled cancer cell membrane characterized by an enrichment of phospholipids with longer and more polyunsaturated acyl chains. Silencing ACLY in EAC cells was shown to reverse these structural changes, directly linking this enzyme to the altered phospholipid profile of EAC. 
Metabolic reprogramming of cancer cells often results from mutations in oncogenes, tumor suppressor genes, or genes encoding metabolic enzymes themselves. Lipidomic profiling of transformed gastroesophageal junction organoids with dual knockout of TP53 and CDKN2A revealed marked upregulation of platelet-activating factors (PTAFs), a distinct class of lipids. Mechanistically, the loss of TP53 and CDKN2A leads to increased expression of the transcription factor FOXM1, which has been shown to transcriptionally activate the platelet-activating factor receptor (PTAFR). This activation amplifies the PTAF–PTAFR signaling loop, thereby promoting tumor progression. 

## Cell-extrinsic drivers of the EC

## Tumor microenvironment
The TME of EC is a complex and dynamic ecosystem composed of various stromal cells, immune cells, vascular endothelial cells, and the extracellular matrix (ECM) (Fig. ), which play indispensable roles in tumor development, progression, invasion, metastasis, and treatment resistance. , Among these stromal components, fibroblasts are among the most important cell types. Normal fibroblasts undergo phenotypic transformation under various stimuli, especially soluble factors secreted by tumor cells (such as TGF-β and CXCL2), extracellular vesicles (such as exosomes), and inflammatory factors (such as IL-1 and IL-6), which form cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs). , , The CAF population is highly heterogeneous, and their diverse subtypes and functions have been gradually revealed. – In the early stages of ESCC, epithelial cells and fibroblasts undergo coevolution, leading to the development of malignant phenotypes. This process is triggered by the downregulation of the transcription factor KLF4, which regulates the expression of the epithelial homeostasis regulatory molecule ANXA1 in epithelial cells. Consequently, the reduction in ANXA1 expression, combined with the increased presence of TGF-β, leads to the transformation of normal fibroblasts into CAFs. The resulting CAFs subsequently contribute to the occurrence of ESCC. CAFs play a core driving role in the critical stage from HGIN to invasive cancer. A significant structural change—the formation of the “CAF-Epi niche”—occurs mainly during the HGIN stage and plays a key role in promoting tumor progression and immune evasion. This is formed at the invasive front of the tumor by invasive epithelial cells that effectively recruit and “educate” normal fibroblasts to transform into CAFs by overexpressing JAG1 and activating the NOTCH1 signaling pathway in adjacent fibroblasts. Research suggests that activated fibroblasts play an early and critical role in the development of EAC. Single-cell sequencing analysis of BE intestinal metaplasia revealed that these lesions are very early, accompanied by a shift toward a protumor, activated fibroblast microenvironment characterized by the expansion of S2/S3 fibroblast subgroups with ECM deposition and contractile properties. These findings suggest that fibroblast activation may be an early event in the development of both ESCC and EAC.
Despite differences in the driving mechanisms and timing of key events, CAFs play important roles in promoting tumor growth, invasion, metastasis, immune evasion, and treatment resistance in both ESCC and EAC. , For example, PDE5 inhibitors can effectively target CAF-mediated chemotherapy resistance in EAC by blocking fibroblast activation and their tumor-promoting functions. By binding to the CD147 receptor on the surface of CAFs, ESCC-derived S100A8 can activate the intracellular RhoA‒ROCK‒MLC2‒MRTF‒A signaling pathway, thereby polarizing CAFs toward a procancer phenotype and conferring resistance to chemotherapeutic drugs to tumor cells. In radiotherapy-resistant ESCC, a positive feedback loop between collagen type 1 derived from CAFs and CXCL1 secreted by tumor cells has been identified; this feedback loop synergistically promotes the DNA repair capacity of cancer cells and augments CAF activation. A recent study reported a novel subpopulation of senescent fibroblasts, termed hypoxia-induced senescent cancer-associated fibroblasts (hsCAFs), in ESCC. hsCAFs are induced by the synergistic action of hypoxia and cancer cells and are characterized by high levels of IGF1 secretion. A previous study revealed that by secreting IGF1 to suppress AMPK activity in cancer cells, hsCAFs promote cancer stemness and chemoresistance. 
Immune cells are another important component of the TME of EC. Like many other solid tumors, ECs exhibit an immunosuppressive microenvironment characterized by the suppression of antitumor cytotoxic cells (such as CD8+ T cells and NK cells), the occurrence of immunosuppressive T cells (regulatory T cells (Tregs)), myeloid cells (such as tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and tumor-associated neutrophils), and the infiltration of protumor inflammatory cells. During the progression from normal epithelium through precancerous lesions to invasive cancer, the composition of immune cell infiltration undergoes dynamic evolution, changing from an initial protective or proinflammatory state to a strong immunosuppressive environment, providing the conditions for malignant tumor growth. , The dynamic balance between protumor and antitumor factors in the esophageal TME ultimately determines whether nascent tumor cells are eliminated or progress to malignancy. For ESCC, spatial transcriptomic analysis revealed that there may be signs of immune activation at the LGIN stage, such as increased T-cell density; however, as the disease progresses to the HGIN and ESCC stages, the immunosuppressive state gradually becomes predominant. Especially in areas where the CAF-Epi niche forms, there is often more significant immunosuppression, characterized by a reduced density or exclusion of cytotoxic T cells (such as CD8+ Teff cells), forming “immune desert” or “immune exclusion” areas, whereas protumor SPP1+ TAMs and Tregs tend to be enriched. This “immune isolation” microenvironment mediated by the CAF-Epi niche effectively prevents immune cells from killing tumor cells by reshaping the ECM to form physical barriers and producing immunosuppressive factors, thereby promoting tumor immune evasion and clonal evolution and allowing tumor cells with immune evasion ability (such as cells with HLA LOH) to survive and expand. 
In the development sequence of EAC, the immune environment undergoes a complex transition from proinflammatory to immunosuppressive. Early reflux esophagitis may be dominated by Th1-type inflammation, whereas at the BE stage, the Th2-type immune response predominates, characterized by increased IL-4-producing cells and elevated IL-6 and IL-10 levels. The soluble factors secreted by BE can even inhibit the production of IFN-γ and TNF-α by peripheral T cells. As lesions progress to HGD and EAC, immunosuppressive features further intensify. Multiplexed immunohistochemistry and gene expression deconvolution analysis revealed that the numbers of FOXP3+ Tregs and CD163+ M2-type TAMs tend to increase from BE to EAC. , These cells significantly accumulate at the HGD stage, but Treg numbers decrease at the EAC stage. Moreover, a significant decrease in the proportion and infiltration of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, especially within the tumor area of EAC, has been observed. , In addition, a significant reduction in eosinophils and increase in pro-B cells, as well as an increase in a new CSF1R+CD1C+ myeloid cell subgroup, were observed during EAC progression. Despite different evolutionary details, both ESCC and EAC eventually form an immunosuppressive microenvironment conducive to tumor growth and escape. Therefore, precise characterization of the immune microenvironment is critical for formulating clinical treatment strategies, as patients exhibit heterogeneous immune microenvironment features closely related to their sensitivity to immunotherapy. For example, the presence of a subpopulation of CD8+ T cells expressing SPRY1 can enhance the activation of the immuno-tumor microenvironment and indicate responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitors. 
Crosstalk between tumor cells and various cellular components within the TME can occur through various molecular pathways, including those involving cytokines and chemokines. , These signaling molecules not only directly act on tumor cells to regulate cancer gene expression and cellular behavior by activating signaling pathways such as the JAK/STAT and NF-κB pathways but also broadly influence other components of the TME, including regulating immune cell recruitment and differentiation, affecting angiogenesis, and shaping a favorable microenvironment for tumor metastasis, thus playing a role in various stages of tumor initiation, progression, invasion, and metastasis. , , In the developmental sequence of EAC, specific cytokine changes are particularly prominent. Studies have shown that the expression of IL-6 and CXCL8 significantly increases during the progression from BE to HGD and EAC, which may be closely related to the observed dynamic changes in immune cells. In addition, TNF-α can increase IL-8 expression in a dose-dependent manner. Moreover, various proinflammatory (such as IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-2) and anti-inflammatory/immunosuppressive (such as IL-10 and IL-6) cytokines can be detected in EAC tissues, forming a complex mixed cytokine spectrum. In ESCC, in addition to the general upregulation of protumor and immunosuppressive cytokine/chemokine pathways, – specific signaling axes are more clearly defined. For example, invasive epithelial cells drive the expression of downstream chemokines (such as CXCL1 and CXCL8) through the JAG1-NOTCH1 signaling pathway, which not only mediates the formation of the CAF-Epi niche but also further promotes the recruitment and activation of CAFs, constituting a key mechanism by which interactions between tumor cells and stromal cells drive tumor progression. Additionally, the intrinsic genetic characteristics of tumor cells can directly regulate their secreted cytokine spectrum, thereby shaping the immune microenvironment. For example, CDKN2A inactivating mutations common in ESCC have been shown to mediate immune evasion by upregulating CCL2, recruiting exhausted T cells and M2-type TAMs via the CCL2‒CCR2 axis. An in-depth understanding of these multifaceted and spatially specific interactions within the TME of EC, revealing the precise molecular mechanisms regulating these complex cellular communication networks, represents a critical frontier in EC research and is expected to provide a theoretical basis and targets for developing more effective treatment strategies for different subtypes of EC.

## Microbiota
The complex ecosystem of microorganisms that coexist with human cells has emerged as a critical factor in cancer biology, influencing initiation, progression, and treatment responses. – Tissue biopsy cultivation and high-throughput sequencing technologies have revealed the existence of complex microbial communities within the esophagus. Microbial dysbiosis, an imbalance in the composition and function of the microbiota, plays a key role in the development and progression of EC. Different subtypes of EC are associated with specific microbial characteristics. For example, the esophageal microbiota in ESCC patients typically exhibits reduced diversity and enrichment of oral anaerobes and pathogens. – In particular, the periodontal pathogen Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) is significantly associated with increased ESCC risk and poor prognosis. Similarly, Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum), which plays an important role in colorectal cancer, is also enriched in ESCC tumor tissues and is associated with tumor invasiveness and shorter patient survival time. , In EAC and its precancerous lesions (such as BE), significant changes in microbial composition are observed. The BE stage typically shows a shift from predominantly Gram-positive bacteria to Gram-negative bacteria (such as Veillonella and Prevotella). A special phenomenon is that Helicobacter pylori infection in the stomach has a protective effect against EAC, and its eradication may indirectly promote the occurrence of EAC. – 
The mechanisms by which the microbiome drives EC development involve multiple levels. First, an imbalanced microbiome, especially enriched Gram-negative bacteria, can activate the TLR4/NF-κB pathway by releasing lipopolysaccharide, inducing and maintaining chronic inflammation, thus promoting the progression from inflammation to BE and then to EAC. Second, specific bacteria can modulate the immune microenvironment to favor tumor cell survival. , For example, P. gingivalis can induce IL-6 production, recruit myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and inhibit antitumor immunity ; F. nucleatum can upregulate PD-L1 expression on tumor cells, helping tumors evade immune surveillance. Additionally, microbial metabolites or effector molecules can directly affect cancer cell behaviors. 
F. nucleatum can produce putrescine in ESCC cells, promoting cell proliferation ; P. gingivalis can confer survival advantages and proliferative capacity to cells by activating the JAK/STAT3 pathway, inhibiting apoptotic signals, – and even inducing atypical hyperplasia in vitro in normal esophageal epithelium. Furthermore, F. nucleatum can induce resistance to chemotherapeutic drugs in ESCC cells by inducing autophagy. 
The impact of the microbiota on EC is not limited to the local esophageal environment. Changes in the oral microbiome, especially in bacteria associated with periodontal disease, such as P. gingivalis, have been shown to be related to an increased risk of EC, suggesting the existence of an “oral‒esophageal axis” interaction. , Epidemiological studies have linked poor oral hygiene habits with a significant increase in ESCC risk. Comparative analysis revealed that the microbiomes of paired saliva and tumor samples from some ESCC patients are very similar, with specific enrichment of Fusobacterium and Prevotella observed particularly in tumors. This finding supports the model of oral bacteria directly colonizing esophageal tumors or their metabolic products affecting distant organs. Therefore, the detection of specific microbes (such as P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum) or microbial community patterns in oral tissues may serve as biomarkers for early diagnosis, risk assessment, and prognostic evaluation. The overall composition of the gut microbiome is also closely related to EC risk. , Mendelian randomization studies have suggested causal associations between specific intestinal genera and EC risk, with Gordonibacter and Oxalobacter increasing risk and Turicibacter and Romboutsia decreasing risk, suggesting that the gut microbiota can serve as potential risk biomarkers. Gut microbiota dysbiosis may also indirectly participate in the pathological process of EC by affecting systemic inflammation levels and immune status. Additionally, lifestyle and environmental factors, such as dietary habits, alcohol consumption, smoking, and medication use, can influence EC risk by modulating the microbial composition. – The composition of the microbiome also affects EC patients’ response to treatments such as chemotherapy and immunotherapy. , , For example, a high abundance of F. nucleatum in tumors is associated with a poor response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in ESCC, while the diversity of the gut microbiota and changes in the abundance of specific species are also related to treatment efficacy and patient survival time. , These findings collectively point to the potential of targeting the microbiome through dietary interventions, probiotics or prebiotics, and fecal microbiota transplantation as a new strategy for EC prevention and adjuvant therapy.

## Prevention of the EC

## Reducing exposure to risk factors
Most cancers are preventable, and prevention strategies can be categorized into primary, secondary, and tertiary prevention strategies. Prevention of EC begins with reducing exposure to known risk factors. Risk factors differ between ESCC and EAC. The key risk factors for ESCC include tobacco smoking and alcohol consumption. Population-based studies provide evidence for the effectiveness of these preventive measures, highlighting the impact of lifestyle changes. The effect of tobacco smoking cessation on the risk of EC has been extensively studied. A comprehensive meta-analysis covering population-based studies revealed that smoking cessation is associated with a decrease in the risk of ESCC over time. Compared with current smokers (relative risk [RR] = 4.18, 95% CI = 3.42–5.12), former smokers had a lower risk (RR = 2.05, 95% CI = 1.71–2.45), and this protective effect became more pronounced with longer time since quitting. A significant risk reduction is observed after 5 years of quitting, with risk continuing to decline and approaching that of never smokers (RR = 0.22, 95% CI = 0.18–0.28) after 20 years or more (RR = 0.34, 95% CI = 0.25–0.47). However, the impact of smoking cessation on the risk of EAC is limited. With nonsmokers as a reference, the risk of EAC for former smokers (RR = 1.66, 95% CI = 1.48–1.85) was only slightly lower than that for current smokers (RR = 2.34, 95% CI = 2.04–2.69). The risk of EAC did not significantly decrease over time after smoking cessation. Even after a long period of smoking cessation, compared with that of individuals with ESCC, where the risk ratio decreases to 0.34 after 20 years of smoking cessation, the risk ratio for EAC only decreases to 0.72 after 20 years of smoking cessation. Furthermore, a large population-based case‒control study in China revealed a positive correlation between environmental tobacco exposure and the risk of EC among never smokers. The study reported overall ORs of 1.44 (95% CI = 1.31–1.58) and 1.56 (95% CI = 1.35–1.82) among nonsmokers. This finding emphasizes the importance of implementing comprehensive smoke-free policies in public and private spaces to protect nonsmokers from the harmful effects of passive smoking.
A comprehensive review of evidence recently conducted by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) concluded that reducing or completely stopping alcohol consumption does indeed lower the risk of EC. A case‒control study from Latin America revealed that, compared with current drinkers, individuals who had quit drinking for 20 or more years had a 54% lower risk of EC (OR = 0.46, 95% CI = 0.19–1.16). These findings highlight the clear benefit of alcohol cessation. However, unlike ESCC, higher alcohol intake is not associated with an increased risk of EAC. A pooled analysis from the BEACON Consortium revealed that even at the highest alcohol intake level (≥7 drinks per day), no increased risk of EAC was observed compared with that of nondrinkers (OR = 0.97, 95% CI = 0.68–1.36). 
BE-associated chronic GERD and obesity are the main risk factors for EAC. Given this strong association, the management of these conditions is critical. Management typically involves lifestyle and dietary changes, such as weight loss for overweight individuals, avoiding trigger foods, advising against lying down immediately after meals, and encouraging elevation of the head during sleep, , which can indirectly impact EAC risk by reducing reflux symptoms.
Medications such as proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) are also commonly used to reduce stomach acidity and are the primary medical treatment for GERD. Studies specifically investigating PPI use in BE patients suggest a protective effect against neoplastic progression. A meta-analysis of seven observational studies revealed that PPI use was associated with a 71% reduction in the risk of EAC or HGD in patients with BE (adjusted OR = 0.29, 95% CI = 0.12 − 0.79). This finding is supported by a large prospective cohort study that reported that PPI use during a median 5.2-year follow-up was associated with a 79% reduced risk of HGD or EAC (time-dependent HR = 0.21, 95% CI = 0.07–0.66). In some cases, surgery may be an option for controlling reflux. Additionally, obesity, especially central obesity, is associated with an increased risk of EAC, often in combination with GERD, and maintaining a healthy weight may help limit this risk. – 
Dietary improvement is another avenue for prevention. In areas where drinking extremely hot beverages and consuming pickled vegetables are common, promotional measures should be considered to mitigate the potential increased risk of ESCC associated with these habits. Additionally, a population-based absolute risk model developed in China identified smoking and the consumption of onion and garlic as important modifiable lifestyle-related risk factors for EC. Moreover, interventions have shown modest benefits in malnourished populations with high ESCC incidence. A trial in Linxian County, Henan Province, China, tested vitamin and mineral supplements in approximately 30,000 adults. Supplementation with β-carotene, vitamin E, and selenium led to significant reductions in EC mortality and incidence. After 5 years, those receiving this combination had a 42% lower prevalence of EC according to endoscopic surveys, and survival rates also improved. While vitamin supplements are not a panacea, this trial suggests that correcting micronutrient deficiencies in high-risk areas may be helpful.
Furthermore, medication-based preventive measures are being explored, with a meta-analysis suggesting a protective effect of the use of aspirin and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) for ESCC (OR = 0.58, 95% CI = 0.43–0.78). The chronic inflammatory environment in BE provides a theoretical basis for the use of drugs with anti-inflammatory properties. A meta-analysis covering more than 5000 patients with BE revealed that the use of COX inhibitors was associated with a significant 36% reduction in the combined risk of HGD or EAC (RR = 0.64, 95% CI = 0.53–0.77). The AspECT randomized trial has provided important prospective evidence for evaluating the chemopreventive effects of proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) and aspirin in BE patients. This trial (median follow-up of 8.9 years) revealed that high-dose PPI (80 mg daily) significantly delayed the time to progression to the composite endpoint (all-cause mortality, EAC, or HGD) compared with low-dose PPI (20 mg daily) (P = 0.038). For aspirin, the main effect did not reach statistical significance in the overall analysis (P = 0.068) but showed a protective effect after excluding patients with early use of NSAIDs (p = 0.043). Importantly, the combination of high-dose PPI and aspirin had the strongest protective effect, particularly compared with the control group, which received low-dose PPI without aspirin (P = 0.0068). Compared with high-dose PPI alone, the addition of aspirin tended to further reduce the risk of the composite endpoint (event rate approximately 9% versus 13%), although this specific comparison did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.068). These results suggest that high-dose PPI use combined with aspirin may be beneficial for BE patients, but the potential adverse effects of long-term aspirin or NSAID use (such as bleeding risk, although serious events were rare in this trial) require careful risk‒benefit assessment before making recommendations at a broad population level. In addition to behavioral modifications such as smoking cessation and dietary changes, adopting WHO-recommended physical activity (at least 150 min of moderate-intensity exercise per week) has been demonstrated to play a pivotal role in cancer prevention and management. 
In conclusion, the cornerstone of EC prevention remains reducing exposure to established lifestyle risk factors. Tobacco cessation, reducing or completely stopping alcohol consumption, and dietary improvements have shown practical effects. These measures, combined with general cancer prevention strategies, constitute the first line of defense against ESCC and EAC.

## Early detection and diagnosis of EC
Early detection of EC is crucial for improving patient prognosis, as typical symptoms such as difficulty swallowing, weight loss, and chest pain usually appear only when the disease has progressed to later stages. For both EAC and ESCC, the multistep nature of EC development provides opportunities for secondary prevention, including early detection and intervention (Fig. , Table ). Endoscopic screening is currently the gold standard for detecting esophageal precancerous lesions or early tumors, and some high-incidence countries have implemented endoscopic examinations in population-based screening programs. Evidence from these programs demonstrates the life-saving potential of screening. For instance, a large multicenter population cohort study based on six high-risk regions in China reported that individuals receiving one-time endoscopic screening had not only a significantly reduced risk of EC incidence (RR = 0.74, 95% CI = 0.69–0.79) compared with controls but also a substantially reduced mortality risk of 60% (RR = 0.40, 95% CI = 0.36–0.45). However, a 9-year follow-up report from a large-scale, community-based randomized controlled trial (ESECC study) conducted in high-risk areas of northern China provided a different perspective. This trial evaluated the effectiveness of Lugol’s iodine chromoendoscopy screening. In the primary intention-to-treat analysis, compared with the control group, the screening group presented a 19% reduction in the incidence of EC (adjusted HR = 0.81, 95% CI = 0.60–1.09) and an 18% reduction in mortality (adjusted HR = 0.82, 95% CI = 0.53–1.26), but these reductions were limited and did not reach statistical significance. The researchers noted that insufficient screening compliance and delayed treatment for those who screened positive might have weakened the overall effectiveness of screening. Despite differences in design and results across studies, this evidence from China collectively emphasizes the tremendous potential of endoscopic screening in reducing the burden of EC while also highlighting the importance of optimizing screening strategies and ensuring appropriate follow-up care. 
In Western countries, routine EC screening is not currently recommended for the general population because of relatively low incidence rates and a lack of evidence that screening reduces mortality. Therefore, screening strategies focus primarily on high-risk populations, especially BE patients. Clinical guidelines recommend screening endoscopy for BE in individuals with chronic GERD and additional risk factors (such as age >50, male sex, white race, long-term GERD, obesity, or family history), reflecting the concept of precision screening aimed at identifying populations most likely to benefit from early detection. One-time endoscopic examination in this high-risk group can identify Barrett’s metaplasia, a precursor to adenocarcinoma. If BE is detected, patients enter a monitoring program with regular endoscopic examinations and biopsies to detect progression to dysplasia or early cancer. Risk-stratified monitoring strategies ensure that individuals at high risk of progression receive closer follow-up through regular endoscopic examinations and biopsies. The monitoring frequency is adjusted according to the grade of dysplasia, with ACG guidelines indicating that BE patients without dysplasia may be monitored every 3–5 years, whereas patients with LGIN require more frequent monitoring (initially every 6–12 months, then annually). Similarly, research on high-risk populations for ESCC in China also emphasizes the value of surveillance. Specifically, a large, multicenter prospective cohort study followed 3258 patients diagnosed with LGIN through endoscopic screening in rural Chinese areas for a median follow-up of 7.96 years. The study revealed that patients who received at least one surveillance endoscopy had a significantly lower risk of developing ESCC by 31% (adjusted HR = 0.69, 95% CI = 0.50–0.95) than those who received no surveillance endoscopy. This further supports the value of endoscopic surveillance as a secondary prevention measure, especially in populations at greater risk for specific EC subtypes.
Advances in endoscopic technology have significantly enhanced the detection capabilities for early EC and its precancerous lesions. High-definition white light endoscopy combined with advanced imaging technologies, such as chromoendoscopy (spraying dyes such as Lugol’s iodine or acetic acid to highlight mucosal abnormalities), narrow-band imaging (NBI, using specific wavelengths of light to enhance mucosal details), and magnifying endoscopy (providing magnified views of the esophageal surface), enable endoscopists to more clearly identify subtle mucosal changes that may represent early tumors. – For example, NBI has shown marked superiority over traditional white light imaging in detecting superficial lesions, achieving significantly higher sensitivity (97% versus 55%, P < 0.001). 
To improve screening accessibility and acceptance, researchers are actively exploring various new, less invasive screening tools as alternatives or supplements to traditional endoscopy. The development of these nonendoscopic screening technologies promises to increase screening participation rates, especially among populations reluctant to undergo endoscopic examinations, and may be more cost-effective in resource-limited settings. For example, the Cytosponge-TFF3 test combines swallowable sponge sampling with TFF3 biomarker detection. In a large randomized controlled trial (BEST3) in the UK, patients with gastroesophageal reflux symptoms who were taking long-term acid suppressants and who were offered the Cytosponge-TFF3 test (intervention group, n = 6834) had significantly higher detection rates of BE than did those in the usual care group (n = 6388). Specifically, the detection rate was 20.2 cases per 1000 person-years in the intervention group versus only 2.0 cases per 1000 person-years in the usual care group, increasing BE detection more than tenfold (adjusted RR = 10.6, 95% CI = 6.0–18.8, P < 0.0001). Another large-scale, multicenter prospective study in China developed and validated a new machine learning-based, fully automated sponge cytology screening method. Using a minimally invasive sponge device to collect cell samples, artificial intelligence (AI) was integrated for the automatic analysis of numerous cytological features and epidemiological risk factors to construct a prediction model. The results showed that this fully automated model demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance (test set AUROC 0.960) in a large cohort of over 17,000 people. This method, through risk stratification, could prevent approximately 90% of unnecessary endoscopies, providing an efficient, objective new screening option for high-risk populations. 
Additionally, AI-assisted image recognition technology is emerging as an auxiliary tool to help endoscopists more accurately and efficiently identify minute endoscopic features of early esophageal malignancies. A large randomized trial in China evaluated an AI-based computer-aided detection (CAD) system during ESCC endoscopic screening. This system is connected to endoscopic equipment, analyzes NBI video streams in real time on a separate monitor, and highlights suspected lesion areas through visual markers, providing an immediate reference for operators. The results showed that the CAD system significantly improved the detection rate of esophageal neoplastic lesions (RR = 1.96); for junior endoscopists, the detection rate more than doubled (RR = 3.58), mainly due to improved recognition of precancerous lesions (such as LGIN and HGIN). The CAD system significantly reduced the miss rate—the false negative rate decreased from approximately 52% in conventional endoscopy to only approximately 9% in AI-guided endoscopy. AI captures lesions missed by less experienced endoscopists, helping to improve diagnostic levels and reduce experience-based disparities. Moreover, Pisula et al. presented an attention-based convolutional neural network that accurately detects HER2 overexpression in gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma, offering a reliable and automated alternative to manual assessment and paving the way for personalized therapy. The integration of these technologies significantly enhances the ability to detect ECs at earlier, more treatable stages.
Moreover, research on biomarker-based EC screening is advancing. Scientists are investigating markers in blood or biological samples obtained through nonendoscopic methods (circulating tumor DNA, methylated DNA fragments, and extracellular vesicles) to achieve early cancer detection without requiring endoscopy. For example, frequent tumor burden monitoring via tumor-specific ctDNA analysis via digital polymerase chain reaction may provide opportunities for early intervention and the selection of alternative, more effective treatments for relapse. Similarly, microRNAs collected from saliva were reported to successfully serve as noninvasive biomarkers for the early detection and risk stratification of ESCC. Specifically, Li et al. developed a 6-miRNA signature from salivary extracellular vesicles that demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance, with an AUROC of 0.969 for detecting early-stage ESCC. Bian et al. conducted a large, multicenter, prospective, double-blind study evaluating a detection method called “IEsohunter,” which is based on detecting the methylation of the OTOP2 and KCNA3 genes in plasma circulating free DNA (cfDNA). The results showed that this blood test demonstrated high accuracy in diagnosing EC (including stage I) and HGIN, with an overall sensitivity of 87.4% and specificity of 93.3%. Notably, the test showed 78.5% sensitivity in stage I EC patients, and the test results were negative in postoperative patients, suggesting its potential for monitoring treatment efficacy or recurrence. Similarly, Zhang et al. conducted a multicenter prospective clinical trial that developed and validated a diagnostic model based on three cfDNA methylation markers (Epo, MT-1A, and Septin9). This model achieved an overall sensitivity of 85.5% and specificity of 95.3% in distinguishing EC patients from non-EC controls in a multicenter prospective cohort of 1429 participants. Importantly, the model showed high sensitivity in diagnosing early tumors (56% for stage 0, 77% for stage I). The results of these studies indicate that the assessment of tumor-related methylation status in blood samples contributes to noninvasive, reliable diagnosis of early EC. Similarly, profiling proteins from small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) via a microfluidic biochip and machine learning has recently emerged as a powerful tool, achieving high accuracy (>90%) for early ESCC diagnosis. In the coming years, we expect AI-assisted endoscopy and improved biomarkers to complement traditional screening methods, enabling earlier diagnosis of EC in both high-risk and general populations.

## Early intervention in EC
When EC or its precancerous lesions are detected at an early stage, a series of minimally invasive interventional treatments can be employed to eradicate local lesions, prevent disease progression to later, more difficult-to-treat stages, and avoid complications associated with late-stage treatment. Endoscopic resection (ER) techniques, such as endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), have become key methods for early intervention, effectively replacing esophagectomy in many cases. ER techniques allow doctors to remove focal lesions through endoscopy. For example, ESD removes larger and potentially deeper lesions in one piece by dissecting the submucosal layer beneath the lesion, thereby avoiding open surgery. , For early ESCC, a large retrospective cohort study from China compared patients with T1a-m2/m3 or T1b stage disease who received either ESD (n = 322) or esophagectomy (n = 274). The results showed no significant differences in overall survival (OS) or cancer recurrence/metastasis rates between the two treatment methods after a median follow-up of 21 months. Notably, the disease-specific mortality rate was significantly lower in the ESD group (3.4% versus 7.4%, P = 0.049), and the rate of serious perioperative non-fatal adverse events was also significantly lower than in the esophagectomy group (15.2% versus 27.7%, P < 0.001). These findings suggest that organ-preserving ESD for specific early ESCC patients is comparable to surgery while offering superior safety. Similarly, endoscopy has shown encouraging long-term efficacy in treating early EAC. A large study of ER in 1000 patients with intramucosal AC reported after an average follow-up of nearly 5 years. In this study, patients achieved a 93.8% long-term complete remission rate, with a calculated 10-year survival rate of 75%. More importantly, this treatment approach was safe, with an extremely low rate of major complications (1.5%) and almost zero treatment-related mortality (tumor-related mortality of only 0.2%). In summary, these studies collectively confirm that ER can provide tumor control effects and long-term survival comparable to esophagectomy for carefully selected early EC patients, while significantly reducing perioperative risks and complications. This makes endoscopic treatment an efficient and safe curative method for early EC, which has become the standard treatment option in many cases as an alternative to surgery. A meta-analysis covering 15 studies with a total of 4731 early EC patients further confirmed that compared with esophagectomy, ER significantly reduced the risk of major adverse events (RR = 0.46) and surgery-related mortality (RR = 0.27). However, long-term monitoring after ER is crucial for monitoring any signs of recurrence. The meta-analysis also revealed that ER may be associated with higher tumor recurrence rates (RR = 1.69, 95% CI = 0.99–2.89) and lower R0 resection rates (RR = 0.92), and in terms of long-term OS, esophagectomy may have a slight advantage (HR = 1.21, 95% CI = 1.02–1.43). 
Additionally, endoscopic ablation techniques, especially radiofrequency ablation (RFA), have become the main approach for treating BE with dysplasia, particularly for flat lesions. RFA uses controlled heat produced by radiofrequency energy to destroy abnormal Barrett’s mucosa, aiming to eliminate dysplasia and intestinal metaplasia, thereby preventing progression to EAC. Its effectiveness has been confirmed in several key clinical trials. A multicenter, sham-controlled randomized trial included 127 BE patients with low-grade dysplasia (LGD) or HGD. The results showed that at 12 months, patients in the RFA group had significantly higher rates of complete dysplasia clearance than did those in the sham group (LGD: 90.5% versus 22.7%; HGD: 81.0% versus 19.0%; P < 0.001) and significantly higher overall complete intestinal metaplasia clearance rates (77.4% versus 2.3%, P < 0.001). More importantly, RFA significantly reduced the risk of disease progression (3.6% versus 16.3%, P = 0.03) and cancer incidence (1.2% versus 9.3%; P = 0.045). For the specific and common subgroup of LGD, a European multicenter randomized trial (SURF trial) directly compared RFA with endoscopic surveillance. , This study was terminated early owing to the clear superiority of RFA, as over 3 years of follow-up, RFA significantly reduced the risk of progression to HGD or adenocarcinoma from 26.5% in the surveillance group to 1.5% (absolute risk reduction 25.0%, P < 0.001) and reduced the risk of progression to adenocarcinoma from 8.8 to 1.5% (absolute risk reduction 7.4%, P = 0.03). The RFA group achieved a complete dysplasia clearance rate of 92.6% and a complete intestinal metaplasia clearance rate of 88.2%. A meta-analysis including 3802 patients further consolidated these findings, reporting pooled complete clearance rates of approximately 91% for dysplasia and 78% for intestinal metaplasia after RFA. However, the meta-analysis also revealed that even after successful clearance, the recurrence rate of intestinal metaplasia was approximately 13%, emphasizing the necessity of continued monitoring after treatment. In terms of safety, the main adverse event of RFA is esophageal stricture, with an incidence of approximately 5% reported in the meta-analysis and 11.8% reported in the SURF trial, but this can usually be resolved through endoscopic dilation. Overall, these high-quality studies collectively confirm that RFA is an effective means of treating BE with dysplasia and is capable of significantly reducing the risk of cancer progression with acceptable safety.

## Precise treatment of the EC

## Targeted therapy
Targeted therapy interferes with specific molecular pathways that drive cancer cell growth and survival, and its application is guided primarily by specific biomarker detection results. EAC shares similar molecular characteristics with gastric cancer (GC), while ESCC exhibits different features. In EAC, HER2 overexpression represents an important therapeutic target, but is less common in ESCC. , Trastuzumab combined with fluoropyrimidine and platinum-based chemotherapy has become the first-line standard treatment for HER2-positive advanced EAC. Compared with chemotherapy alone, the phase III ToGA clinical trial demonstrated that adding trastuzumab to standard fluoropyrimidine plus platinum-based chemotherapy significantly improved OS (median OS: 13.8 months versus 11.1 months, HR = 0.74, P = 0.0046). Another key target is VEGFR-2. The REGARD trial revealed that, compared with placebo, ramucirumab monotherapy improved OS in advanced gastric or esophagogastric junction (EGJ) adenocarcinoma patients. The RAINBOW trial confirmed that ramucirumab plus paclitaxel significantly prolonged OS and progression-free survival (PFS) compared with paclitaxel alone. An emerging target is claudin 18.2, which is highly expressed in a subset of EACs. Zolbetuximab, the first monoclonal antibody targeting CLDN18.2, significantly improved PFS and OS compared with chemotherapy alone in two phase III trials. For HER2-negative, CLDN18.2-positive advanced unresectable or metastatic gastric/EGJ adenocarcinoma, zolbetuximab combined with the CAPOX or FOLFOX regimens has been approved as a first-line treatment option. , 
The role of EGFR-targeted therapies in EC has been investigated, with various outcomes reflecting different therapeutic strategies and patient populations. For resectable EC (both EAC and ESCC), the SAKK 75/08 phase III trial evaluated the addition of the monoclonal antibody cetuximab to neoadjuvant chemoradiation and surgery. While cetuximab significantly improved locoregional control, it only led to a clinically relevant but nonsignificant trend toward improved OS (HR = 0.73, 95% CI = 0.52–1.01, P = 0.055). In contrast, for patients with nonresectable, advanced, or metastatic ESCC, the POWER trial, a phase III study of cisplatin and 5-fluorouracil with or without the monoclonal antibody panitumumab, was stopped early owing to a lack of efficacy in the panitumumab arm, indicating that EGFR inhibition with panitumumab did not improve survival in this unselected advanced ESCC population. More recently, the efficacy of EGFR TKIs has been explored specifically in patients with EGFR-overexpressing ESCC. A phase II proof-of-concept trial investigated afatinib in pretreated metastatic ESCC patients with confirmed EGFR overexpression. This study met its primary endpoint, demonstrating a confirmed objective response rate of 39% and a median OS of 7.8 months, with a manageable toxicity profile. These findings highlight the potential of TKIs in selected ESCC populations.
For tumors with specific genetic alterations, targeted therapy options exist regardless of the histological type. For patients with NTRK gene fusions, larotrectinib and entrectinib are FDA-approved treatment options. , For patients carrying RET gene fusions, selpercatinib is an approved drug. For patients with BRAF V600E mutations, dabrafenib combined with trametinib is an approved regimen. These medications are available for advanced EC patients with corresponding gene alterations who have failed previous treatments on the basis of their tissue/site-agnostic indications. In addition to established targets, several novel targets are under active investigation for EC treatment. The c-MET pathway is a key player in tumor progression and is typically activated by its ligand, hepatocyte growth factor (HGF). While early attempts to target HGF with antibodies such as rilotumumab yielded disappointing results in broader MET-positive populations in the RILOMET-1 trial (HR = 1.34, P = 0.003), selective MET TKIs have shown promise, particularly in tumors with MET gene amplification. For example, early clinical data with AMG 337 demonstrated an objective response rate of 29.6% in patients with MET-amplified solid tumors, including EC. The PI3K/AKT/mTOR cascade is frequently dysregulated in ESCC. Inhibitors targeting different nodes of this pathway are being explored. , The NOTCH signaling pathway is also a complex but compelling target in ESCC. The NOTCH pathway, known for its role in cancer stemness and epithelial‒mesenchymal transition, is another promising target, with gamma-secretase inhibitors (e.g., nirogacestat) currently in early-phase trials. , The development of these targeted therapies is increasingly guided by precise biomarker identification to select patient populations most likely to benefit.

## Immunotherapy
The advent of ICIs, such as anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-L1 agents, has significantly reshaped the therapeutic landscape for advanced EC (Table ). , The combination of an anti-PD-1 antibody with platinum-based chemotherapy has now superseded chemotherapy alone as the standard first-line treatment for most patients with unresectable locally advanced, recurrent, or metastatic disease, as supported by several pivotal phase 3 clinical trials. Two key global trials established the benefit of this combination approach across different histological subtypes and patient populations. The KEYNOTE-590 study demonstrated that adding pembrolizumab to cisplatin and 5-fluorouracil chemotherapy significantly improved OS and PFS compared with chemotherapy plus placebo. Notably, in patients with ESCC and a PD-L1 combined positive score (CPS) of 10 or greater, the median OS was significantly extended (13.9 versus 8.8 months, HR = 0.57, 95% CI = 0.43–0.75, P < 0.0001), with benefits also observed in the overall ESCC population (OS HR 0.72, 95% CI = 0.60–0.88, P = 0.0006), the total CPS ≥ 10 population (OS HR 0.62, 95% CI = 0.49–0.78, P < 0.0001), and the overall study population irrespective of histology or PD-L1 status (OS HR 0.73, 95% CI = 0.62–0.86, P < 0.0001). Similarly, PFS improved across these groups. Complementing this, the CheckMate 649 trial focused primarily on HER2-negative gastric, gastroesophageal junction, and esophageal adenocarcinomas. Nivolumab plus chemotherapy significantly improved OS and PFS compared with chemotherapy alone in patients with a PD-L1 CPS ≥ 5 (OS HR 0.71, 98.4% CI = 0.59–0.86, P < 0.0001 at primary analysis; 3-year follow-up OS HR 0.70, 95% CI = 0.61–0.81). While both trials confirmed the first-line ICI-chemotherapy benefit globally, they differed in primary patient populations (KEYNOTE-590 included substantial ESCC, CheckMate 649 focused on EAC/EGJ adenocarcinoma) and chemotherapy backbones. 
Further evidence supporting first-line ICI combinations. The RATIONALE-306 trial demonstrated superior OS with tislelizumab plus investigator-chosen chemotherapy (platinum plus either fluoropyrimidine or paclitaxel) versus placebo plus chemotherapy in advanced/metastatic ESCC patients (HR = 0.66, 95% CI = 0.54–0.80, P < 0.0001). Similarly, the ESCORT-1st trial, conducted in China, revealed that camrelizumab combined with cisplatin plus paclitaxel significantly improved OS (HR = 0.70, 95% CI = 0.56–0.88, P = 0.001) and PFS (HR = 0.56, 95% CI = 0.46–0.68, P < 0.001) in advanced/metastatic ESCC patients. The ORIENT-15 trial, primarily in China, also reported significant improvements in OS (HR = 0.63, 95% CI = 0.51–0.78, P < 0.001) and PFS (HR = 0.56, 95% CI = 0.46–0.68, P < 0.001) with sintilimab plus chemotherapy (cisplatin plus paclitaxel or 5-FU) in the same setting. In contrast, the ATTRACTION-4 trial, which investigated nivolumab plus SOX or CAPOX in gastric/EGJ cancer, met its PFS endpoint (HR = 0.68, P = 0.0007) but did not demonstrate a statistically significant improvement in OS (HR = 0.90, P = 0.26), potentially influenced by high rates of subsequent therapy, including ICIs, in the control arm. Additionally, studies of other PD-1 inhibitors combined with chemotherapy have provided further support for this strategy. For example, the JUPITER-06 trial demonstrated that toripalimab plus chemotherapy significantly improved both PFS (HR = 0.58, 95% CI = 0.46–0.74, P < 0.0001) and OS (HR = 0.58, 95% CI = 0.43–0.78, P = 0.0004) compared with chemotherapy alone. Likewise, serplulimab, a fully humanized anti-PD-1 antibody, combined with chemotherapy in the ASTRUM-007 trial significantly improved OS (HR = 0.68, 95% CI = 0.53–0.87, P = 0.002) and PFS (HR = 0.60, 95% CI = 0.48–0.75, P < 0.0001) compared with chemotherapy alone. Collectively, these studies underscore the efficacy of various PD-1 inhibitors combined with different platinum doublets as first-line therapies.
ICIs also play a role as adjuvant treatments. The CheckMate 577 trial investigated nivolumab versus placebo in patients with resected (R0) esophageal or EGJ cancer (both SCC and AC) who had received neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy but had residual pathological disease. Nivolumab significantly improved disease-free survival (DFS) compared with placebo (median DFS 22.4 versus 11.0 months; HR = 0.69, 96.4% CI = 0.56–0.86, P < 0.001), establishing adjuvant nivolumab as a standard-of-care option for this high-risk patient population. Neoadjuvant immunotherapy, alone or combined with chemotherapy, is under active investigation for resectable EC. The goal is often to increase pathological response rates, potentially improving long-term survival. The prospective, single-arm phase II Keystone-001 trial evaluated three cycles of neoadjuvant pembrolizumab plus paclitaxel and cisplatin, specifically for resectable stage III ESCC. This study reported encouraging efficacy, with a high major pathological response rate of 72% and a pathological complete response (pCR) rate of 41%. After a median follow-up of 27.2 months, the 2-year OS and DFS rates were promising at 91% and 89%, respectively. To further support neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, the phase III ESCORT-NEO/NCCES01 trial evaluated neoadjuvant camrelizumab plus chemotherapy (either Nab-paclitaxel and cisplatin or paclitaxel and cisplatin) in patients with resectable thoracic LA-ESCC. This trial revealed significantly higher pCR rates for both camrelizumab-containing arms than for chemotherapy alone (cam + nab-TP: 28.0% versus TP: 4.7%, P < 0.0001; cam + TP: 15.4% versus TP: 4.7%, P = 0.0034), meeting the primary endpoint for pCR, although event-free survival data are not yet mature. The concept of neoadjuvant ICI monotherapy has also been explored. For example, a phase 1b trial of neoadjuvant adebrelimab monotherapy in patients with resectable ESCC (NATION-1907) demonstrated safety and feasibility, with 24% of patients achieving a major pathological response and a pCR rate of 8%, with a 2-year OS of 92%. Larger phase III trials are ongoing to definitively establish the role and optimal strategy for neoadjuvant ICI therapy.
The selection of patients most likely to benefit from ICIs remains to be investigated. , The PD-L1 expression level, typically assessed by the combined positive score (CPS), is the most widely used biomarker, with higher CPSs (e.g., ≥5 or ≥10) generally correlating with greater benefit from ICI combinations. Although rare in patients with EC, a microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) or deficient mismatch repair (dMMR) status strongly predicts the benefit of ICIs. , The tumor mutation burden (TMB) is also being explored. Research into novel biomarkers and predictive signatures continues. For example, a high APOBEC mutation signature has been shown to be associated with poor prognosis in ESCC patients, and inhibiting APOBEC mutagenic activity can potentially increase the efficacy of immunotherapeutic interventions. Adenosine deaminase acting on RNA 1 (ADAR1)-mediated A-to-I RNA editing is a distinct epigenetic characteristic in ESCC that has the potential to serve as a promising biomarker for predicting immunotherapy response. In addition to PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4, other immune checkpoints, such as TIGIT and LAG-3, are being explored as targets for novel immunotherapies. Cellular therapies, such as chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy and T-cell receptor (TCR) T-cell therapy, are in the early stages of development to overcome resistance. – 

## Multimodal therapy
The curative treatment of EC requires a multimodal approach, reflecting the complex biological characteristics and therapeutic challenges of this disease. The current standard treatment for locally advanced EC is a combination of chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy plus surgery. Both neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (CROSS protocol ) and perioperative chemotherapy (FLOT regimen ) have demonstrated superior efficacy compared with surgery alone, significantly improving both OS and PFS. Nevertheless, comparative efficacy data remain inconclusive. The Neo-AEGIS trial reported comparable clinical outcomes between the two approaches in terms of survival benefit and quality-of-life metrics. In contrast, the ESOPEC trial demonstrated a clinically meaningful survival advantage for the FLOT regimen, with a median OS of 66 months versus 37 months for CROSS. These conflicting results underscore the necessity for further large-scale, prospective randomized controlled trials and robust real-world evidence to optimize treatment selection and establish evidence-based clinical guidelines. This approach integrates various treatment modalities, including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, which are combined in different sequences on the basis of specific tumor characteristics. Treatment strategy selection is highly individualized and is primarily based on two key factors, e.g., histological type (ESCC versus EAC) and tumor anatomical location (cervical, thoracic, or distal and gastroesophageal junction). , EC cells exhibit remarkable adaptability and are capable of developing resistance through multiple mechanisms, thereby undermining treatment efficacy. Drug resistance can be broadly categorized into 10 distinct yet interrelated features (Fig. ). – These features often manifest concurrently, further challenging efforts to overcome them. Therefore, effective therapeutic strategies must address these complex resistance mechanisms. With the advancement of precision medicine, EC treatment is moving toward more personalized and comprehensive approaches. , Molecular analysis of tumors plays an increasingly important role in treatment decision-making, helping to identify patient populations most likely to benefit from specific treatment combinations. Numerous targeted therapeutic agents have been developed against esophagus-related oncogenic signaling pathways, including those involving TP53, NOTCH, NRF2, HIPPO, and WNT; however, their clinical efficacy remains limited (Fig. ). Modern multimodality therapy for EC includes not only traditional surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy but also the integration of targeted therapies and immunotherapies. Optimizing the sequence and combination of these treatment modalities is the key to improving patient prognosis. 
The future development of precision treatment for EC requires multidisciplinary collaboration and relies on the combined application of early detection methods, comprehensive multi-omics data, and other advanced diagnostic technologies (Fig. ). , Ongoing clinical trials are rapidly advancing in this field, striving to achieve precise matching of treatment with the biological characteristics of each tumor (including tissue subtypes and molecular features) and individual patient conditions. This integrated approach represents the true realization of precision medicine for treating EC, with the potential to significantly improve treatment outcomes for this disease in the future.

## Conclusion and future perspectives
Significant advances in EC research and management have emerged across multiple fronts, from elucidating the pathogenesis to innovating therapeutic approaches for locally advanced and metastatic diseases. In particular, improvements in the identification of premalignant lesions, early diagnosis, and the advent of ICI-based immunotherapy have improved the survival outcomes of patients. However, challenges persist, and further research is necessary to address unmet needs in early detection and screening, develop more effective and personalized therapeutic strategies, and further elucidate the molecular underpinnings of EC.
A fundamental challenge in managing EC lies in the current limitations of early detection and diagnosis. Owing to insidious symptoms and the lack of highly sensitive and specific biomarkers, the diagnosis of precancerous or cancerous esophageal lesions is primarily based on endoscope-based pathologic changes, which are expensive, painful, and often cause late diagnosis. Even when precancerous lesions are detected, when and how they develop into invasive cancer are unknown, which presents challenges for patient management. In fact, clinical observations have shown that some precancerous lesions might not progress to cancer. This highlights an important aspect of precancerous lesions: they are heterogeneous, and the factors that determine whether they will evolve into cancer remain elusive. To interrupt malignant progression and identify people who may have progressed to cancer, deeper insight into the mechanisms that drive the transition from premalignant lesions to malignant progression is needed.
The current understanding of carcinogenesis emphasizes the interaction between cell autonomous and non-cell autonomous factors, referring to genetic and epigenetic changes, environmental carcinogen exposure, tissue microenvironment imbalance, and immune dysfunction. These factors can have either promoting or inhibitory effects on carcinogenesis, depending on the context. For example, NOTCH1 mutation may help preserve tissue homeostasis by competing with other malignant clones in ESCC. These alterations may be detectable in precancerous lesions; however, they are not the primary and essential fundamental drivers of cancer initiation. Only precancerous lesions that possess a special set of characteristics can progress to cancer. Future research must prioritize the identification of these core genetic and environmental signatures and elucidate how they contribute to the transition from precancerous conditions to invasive cancer. These factors can serve as early intervention endpoints in clinical trials and targets for preventing cancer progression. By elucidating how these factors contribute to the transition from precancerous conditions to invasive cancer, we may develop more effective and personalized therapeutic strategies.
Recent advancements in single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, along with proteomics and bioinformatics, have highlighted the intercellular communication between malignant epithelial cells and stromal cells, which is of particular interest for future research (Table ). Identifying additional biomarkers that robustly correlate with esophageal carcinogenesis, progression, and treatment response, as well as characterizing neoantigens and other mechanisms to induce or maintain antitumor immune responses, is essential for improving EC management. 
The path forward in EC research and treatment still requires a coordinated effort across multiple disciplines, from basic science to clinical practice. By addressing these critical areas, we can work toward the final goal of reducing the burden of EC through improved prevention, early detection, and more effective therapeutic strategies.
