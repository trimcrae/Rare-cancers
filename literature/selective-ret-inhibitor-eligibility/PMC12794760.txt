## ABSTRACT
The hormone receptor (HR)‐positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)‐negative breast cancer subtype accounts for most early and metastatic breast cancer (MBC) cases. HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC is characterized by a relatively prolonged, although variable, disease course and substantial intertumoral and intratumoral heterogeneity. Although endocrine‐based therapies remain the cornerstone of treatment, nearly all patients eventually develop resistance, which is increasingly addressed with biologically targeted agents and newer‐generation cytotoxic drugs. This review summarizes the current understanding of HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC biology, highlighting mechanisms of intrinsic and acquired resistance, including driver genomic alterations that, along with clinical factors, help guide therapeutic choices and sequencing. The authors specify and discuss how genomic and transcriptomic profiling inform treatment selection and how side effects and overall patient experience are considered in decision making. Advances in targeted therapies, such as CDK4/6 (cyclin‐dependent kinase 4/6), PI3K (phosphatidylinositol 3‐kinase), and AKT (protein kinase B) inhibitors, and next‐generation estrogen receptor degraders are reviewed along with the expanding role of antibody–drug conjugates and biomarker‐guided tumor‐agnostic biologic therapies. The authors also explore evolving biologic concepts, including the impact of the tumor microenvironment on resistance and disease progression, and consider the distinct clinical behavior of invasive lobular carcinoma and current approaches. Emerging data from contemporary clinical trials promise to refine clinical and biomarker‐driven algorithms and improve outcomes for patients with HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC. Ongoing multi‐omic research and adaptive clinical strategies will be essential to further the application of precision oncology in this most common subtype of MBC. Importantly, patient‐reported outcomes, shared decision making, and real‐world evidence are increasingly useful in formulating comprehensive care plans, guidelines, and policy. The modern treatment landscape features a personalized approach that integrates clinical features, biomarkers, and patient preferences across the disease continuum.
Keywords: antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs), cyclin‐dependent kinase 4/6 inhibitors (CDK4/6is), endocrine resistance, hormone receptor (HR)‐positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)‐negative metastatic breast cancer, patient‐related outcomes (PROs), selective estrogen receptor degraders (SERDs), signal transduction phosphatidylinositol 3‐kinase/protein kinase B/mammalian target of rapamycin (PI3K/AKT/mTOR) inhibitors

## INTRODUCTION
Metastatic breast cancer (MBC) is one of the leading causes of cancer‐related death among women in the United States and globally and accounts for most breast cancer deaths. Despite notable therapeutic advances, the prognosis for patients with MBC remains poor, with a 5‐year overall survival (OS) rate of about 32%, although survival varies significantly depending on numerous clinical and biologic factors. According to the American Cancer Society, about 30% of patients diagnosed with early stage breast cancer will eventually experience metastasis, and 5%–8% of patients present with de novo MBC at initial diagnosis. , The United States has more than 200,000 people living with MBC at any given time. , About 60%–70% of MBCs are hormone receptor (HR)‐positive, characterized by the presence of estrogen receptor (ER) and/or progesterone receptor (PR) in tumor cells. As specified in the classification criteria for these biomarkers from the American Society of Clinical Oncology and the College of American Pathologists, breast cancers are classified as ER‐positive/PR‐positive when 1%–100% of tumor cell nuclei exhibit ER/PR positivity in immunohistochemical analysis. Tumors in which 1%–10% of nuclei exhibit ER and/or PR positivity are categorized as ER/PR‐low and may have outcomes closer to those of ER‐negative/PR‐negative disease. Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)‐negative tumor cells do not exhibit overexpression of HER2 or amplification of its encoding gene, ERBB2. Estrogen exposure is considered as a key driver of HR‐positive breast cancer; hence, targeting ER and its associated pathways is a fundamental component of treatment for this subtype. Over the past decade, several new therapeutic agents coupled with powerful tumor cell‐based and genomic analytic techniques have generated significant gains in disease‐free survival and OS accompanied by improvements in patient‐reported outcomes (PROs) and health‐related quality of life. 

This narrative review focuses specifically on HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC, summarizing advancements in the understanding and evolution of treatment paradigms and emphasizing the shift toward endocrine therapies and their combinations with novel targeted agents. Additional goals are to equip the reader with analytic and decision‐making pathways for the treatment of HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC that can be applied across the disease/time continuum. Common patient concerns are included throughout the review to enhance optimal communication of this material to patients and family and their inclusion in decision making. We partnered with a breast cancer researcher and survivor who is also a patient advocate during the concept development and drafting, integrating her perspective and scientific insight. We included data on PROs and treatment burden to ensure that the patient experience guided our evidence synthesis.

## ESTABLISHING THE DIAGNOSIS OF MBC
The evaluation of MBC involves a multimodal approach designed to define the location, extent, and tumor cell characterization of disease dissemination. Patients with a history of treated early breast cancer and those with de novo MBC can present with either acute and prominent symptoms or very subtle and progressive signs and symptoms of disseminated disease that may be quite variable and include focused or migratory pain; fatigue; shortness of breath; gastrointestinal symptoms (sometimes vague); a new lump/mass in the breast, a lymph node, or the skin; and headaches, dizziness, or visual changes. Systemic imaging techniques, including computed tomography (CT), positron emission tomography (PET), and nuclear medicine bone scintigraphy, are commonly used to identify and characterize metastatic sites in the lungs, liver, bones, and other locations. Contrast‐enhanced magnetic resonance imaging can be particularly useful for evaluating central nervous system involvement and suspicion of spinal cord compression. Evidence of locoregional disease recurrence should always prompt investigations for distant metastases. Laboratory investigations should include complete blood counts and liver and renal function tests. Detection of tumor markers, such as cancer antigens CA 15‐3 and CA 27‐29, can also be included; although these markers are not diagnostic on their own, they can be used to track tumor activity and response over time. Any suspicious mass or lesion detected based on symptoms or incidentally upon routine examination or imaging should be subjected to a biopsy. Soft tissue and visceral masses may yield more tumor cellularity than bone and pleural effusions, but the latter can also yield interpretable results, albeit with a higher risk of diagnostic uncertainty or failure. A core‐needle biopsy (14–18 gauge, with a larger needle being best if safe and feasible) is preferable over fine‐needle aspiration because the larger needle maintains the tissue architecture, optimizing interpretation of immunohistochemical stains and yielding a sufficient amount of material for comprehensive genomic sequencing. The ER, PR, and HER2 status and the programmed death ligand‐1 (PD‐L1) status (for triple‐negative MBC) in the biopsy specimen are critical to guiding selection of the initial systemic treatment options. In one large series using pooled published data on nearly 10,000 patients, the ER, PR, and HER2 status discordance rates for primary and metastatic lesions were 20%, 33%, and 8%, respectively. Next‐generation sequencing may be helpful at the time of diagnosis of metastatic disease for a select subset of patients, particularly those with evidence of endocrine resistance and eligibility for targeted agents such as inavolisib (see Therapy targeting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, below). For most patients, however, comprehensive genomic testing is most informative at the time of disease progression, when preexisting or newly arising mutations (e.g., ESR1) may inform subsequent therapy selection.
The Division of Cancer Control and Population Sciences at the National Cancer Institute identified five key gaps in advanced cancer (e.g., MBC) research: epidemiology and surveillance, symptom management, psychosocial research, health care delivery, and health behaviors. Education and support planning must be instituted at the outset because goals‐of‐care discussions and advance care planning are necessary components of overall treatment planning. Evaluation for special clinical situations, such as advanced age, frailty, comorbidities, and emotional/psychological/psychiatric needs, should be conducted in a systematic manner using available tools and appropriate referrals as needed. 

## EVOLUTION OF THE MANAGEMENT OF ER‐POSITIVE MBC: A HISTORICAL PERSPECTIVE
In 1896, Sir George Beatson wrote a case report on a woman who underwent a bilateral oophorectomy which was followed by regression of a locally advanced breast cancer. At the time of nursing her first child, she had noticed a small breast lump, which later regressed but then grew rapidly 2 years later during her next pregnancy. Beatson postulated that the action of the ovaries is linked with breast tumors, having observed both benign and malignant breast growth associated with menstrual cycling. He also cited the known inverse relation between lactation and estrus in several mammals and the observed conversion of proliferative breast epithelial tissue to fatty tissue in rabbits after oophorectomy. He subsequently reported on an expanded series of 99 women with advanced breast cancer, demonstrating clinical improvement in 24 patients who underwent surgical ovarian ablation but highlighting that not all breast cancers were sensitive to this therapy. Although oophorectomy has become an accepted therapy for breast cancer (later replaced by ovarian irradiation, then medical suppression with gonadotropin‐releasing hormone analogs), it was not until estrogen was identified and its receptor isolated and quantified in the 1950s that the scientific basis for endocrine therapy for breast cancer became more accepted and endocrine therapy became widely used in clinical practice. , Subsequent understanding of the pathways of estrogen biosynthesis led to the use of sequential surgical endocrine maneuvers, such as adrenalectomy and hypophysectomy, after the development of clinical resistance to therapy. 

As chemotherapy emerged as an effective modality for highly aggressive cancers, such as lymphoma and other hematologic malignancies, especially when used in non–cross‐resistant combinations, it also became the preferred treatment for MBC. This shift was motivated by successes in the adjuvant setting and the rapid responses of MBC to first‐line therapy using the so‐called Cooper regimen (cyclophosphamide, methotrexate, fluorouracil, vincristine, and prednisone), with the later substitution of doxorubicin. However, the use of chemotherapy was constrained by significant toxicity and limited long‐term survival benefits. , Agents such as alkylators, anthracyclines, and taxanes became prominent as clinicians sought to deliver aggressive treatment, ultimately escalating dose intensity into ranges requiring autologous bone marrow and stem cell support. Such aggressive chemotherapy regimens improved disease‐free survival but not OS; hence, autologous bone marrow and stem cell support is not used in contemporary treatment of breast cancer. 

The introduction of selective ER modulators, particularly tamoxifen, which was repurposed from its intended use as a contraceptive agent, revolutionized the treatment of HR‐positive breast cancer. Later lines of endocrine therapy, such as aminoglutethimide, which suppresses aromatase and the adrenal axis, and the androgen fluoxymesterone, had excessive toxicity and were eventually replaced by more targeted agents like aromatase inhibitors (AIs; e.g., anastrozole) and ER degraders (e.g., fulvestrant). Subsequent studies explored the use of sequential therapies upon the emergence of clinical resistance. , Randomized clinical trials have demonstrated that these targeted endocrine agents not only provide therapeutic benefits but also enhance patients' quality of life by minimizing the burden of chemotherapy‐related adverse effects, perhaps even more so in the earlier course of treatment. 

Numerous studies have indicated that AIs are preferable to tamoxifen and other older hormonal therapies in postmenopausal women because of their better outcomes and side‐effect profiles, along with the absence of thrombotic events and uterine hyperplasia/carcinoma risks associated with tamoxifen. , , Fulvestrant was shown to be superior to AI's as initial treatment in terms of progression‐free survival (PFS), but not OS, specifically in patients previously unexposed to endocrine therapy. However, given the inconvenience of monthly fulvestrant injections, it is typically used in second or later lines of therapy. , 

With the widespread use of endocrine therapies in clinical practice, distinguishing between endocrine therapy‐sensitive and endocrine therapy‐resistant breast cancers helps to tailor effective treatment strategies. Several clinical and biologic factors are linked with the degree and length of response to endocrine therapy, although none can be used in isolation because multiple drivers of resistance likely act in concert. Greater sensitivity to endocrine therapy has been observed in patients with de novo HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC (likely because of a lack of prior endocrine therapy exposure) and those with recurrent disease occurring >2 years after completing adjuvant endocrine therapy. 

For more than a decade, physicians have made a concerted effort to recommend endocrine therapy as the preferred initial treatment for newly diagnosed, HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC, whether recurrent or de novo, with the addition of cyclin‐dependent kinase 4 inhibitors (CDKis) as a significant advance, as discussed elsewhere. This recommendation has been increasingly supported by literature reviews, overview analyses, and randomized trials of combined endocrine and biologic therapies demonstrating equivalent PFS and better quality of life than with endocrine‐based therapy alone, even in patients considered to have visceral crisis. , , Exceptions may include patients with low ER expression (<10%) and those with the endocrine‐resistant basal intrinsic subtype according to molecular (RNA) profiling, which is typically seen in triple‐negative breast cancer. However, the latter situation is uncommon, and prospective controlled trials have yet to be conducted to establish the superiority of chemotherapy.

## BIOLOGY AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF HR‐POSITIVE BREAST CANCER
ER signaling is involved in normal development and maintenance of several cell tissue types and also plays a central role in HR‐positive/HER2‐negative breast carcinogenesis and metastasis through classical and nonclassical mechanisms. In the classical pathway, the ER, upon estrogen binding, translocates to the nucleus, associates with coactivators and repressors, and binds to estrogen response elements (defined, short, specific DNA sequences) on target genes, leading to their transcription. These genes include CCND1 (cyclin D1), which promotes the transition from G1 phase to S phase of the cell cycle; BCL2, an antiapoptotic protein; and other genes that promote cell growth, division, and invasion. This classical pathway can be therapeutically targeted using selective ER modulators such as tamoxifen, estrogen deprivation with ovarian suppression and AIs, and with selective ER degraders (SERDs) like fulvestrant, elacestrant, and imlunestrant. 

In contrast, nonclassical ER signaling involves membrane‐initiated (nongenomic) pathways that activate kinase cascades, which contribute to tumorigenesis even in the absence of estrogen (Figure  ), such as the phosphatidylinositol 3‐kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT), mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK; PI3K/AKT/MAPK) pathways. , Common somatic mutations include activating alterations in PI3K catalytic subunit alpha (PIK3CA), which encodes the alpha catalytic subunit of the PI3K enzyme involved in cell growth signaling. These mutations can confer resistance to endocrine therapies. Certain mutations of AKT1 (in particular, the hotspot E17K) can also activate PI3K signaling. 
PIK3CA mutations tend to be truncal in the sense that they are usually present in the primary tumor and continue to be seen upon disease progression; additional mutations can evolve over time. Another mechanism leading to uncontrolled PI3K pathway activation and, consequently, enhanced tumor growth and survival is loss of the phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) tumor‐suppressor gene that encodes a phosphatase and negative regulator of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. In addition, ER signaling can inhibit proapoptotic factors, allowing cancer cells to evade programmed cell death. ER signaling has roles in tumor development through several other cascade effects, including stimulating angiogenesis, promoting immune evasion, and enhancing mesenchymal characteristics that facilitate metastasis and promote drug resistance.
 

## RESISTANCE TO ENDOCRINE THERAPY
The biology underlying resistance to endocrine and other therapies is not fully understood. The known drivers of resistance and relevant modulators of disease behavior are discussed below. Clinically, endocrine therapy resistance can be classified as primary (intrinsic) or secondary (acquired). Although resistance likely exists on a continuum, tumors with primary resistance are defined as those that relapse within the first 2 years of adjuvant endocrine therapy or disease progression during the first 6 months of first‐line endocrine therapy for MBC. Tumors with secondary resistance generally are those that relapse after 2 years of adjuvant therapy, recur within 12 months of completing adjuvant endocrine therapy, or progress ≥6 months after initiating endocrine therapy for MBC. , A longer time to relapse after initial treatment portends better outcomes, with the caveat that de novo cases are also associated with better prognosis. 

The advent of next‐generation DNA sequencing has revolutionized the detection of several key genomic alterations that influence tumor behavior and treatment response, providing deeper insights into the tumor biology and enabling the integration of this information into clinical care in real time. These alterations can generally be classified as gain of function (oncogenic mutations/amplifications/fusions) or loss of function (deletions/loss, frame shift mutations, and epigenetic [gene‐silencing] alterations), as shown in Table  . Chromosomal instability is linked with poor treatment outcomes, in part because it facilitates oncogenic genomic aberrations. Common copy number variations, such as gains on 1q and 8q and deletions on 16q and 17p, can lead to aberrant or high levels of expression of growth‐ and resistance‐promoting receptors. Thus, these variations offer additional prognostic information. Intratumoral heterogeneity and clonal selection drive tumors to evolve toward more aggressive phenotypes and drug resistance, posing ongoing challenges and emphasizing the need for tailored therapeutic approaches over the disease course. 

 Intrinsically endocrine‐resistant tumor cells can evade endocrine therapy from the outset, leading to poor initial treatment responses. Lower levels of ER expression and the absence of PR (an ER‐dependent gene) expression are common factors associated with shortened times to recurrence. Although most cases of intrinsic resistance may not have an identifiable driver, molecular alterations involving the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, which activates estrogen‐independent (nonclassical pathway) alternative survival signaling, can mediate resistance. In addition, tumors can activate alternative (nonclassical) signaling pathways through cross‐talk mediated by receptor tyrosine kinases, such as HER2, fibroblast growth factor (FGF) receptor 1 (FGFR1), and insulin‐like growth‐1 receptor (IGF1R) or through PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway alterations, especially in luminal B and HER2‐positive tumors, resulting in relative resistance to endocrine therapy. , 

Neurofibromin 1, which is encoded by the NF1 gene, negatively regulates the RAS/MAPK pathway, and its loss, which is enriched in endocrine‐resistant tumors and in tumors that progress after treatment with CDK4/6is, has been shown to contribute to endocrine therapy resistance. In addition, variations in ER expression or the presence of different ER splice variants can influence therapy responsiveness. Although somatic mutations of TP53 are more common in ER‐negative breast cancer than in ER‐positive disease, their presence in patients with ER‐positive disease has been associated with worsened prognosis, potentially because of impaired cell‐cycle control and apoptosis regulation. 

Transcriptomic (i.e., RNA) analyses, including gene expression arrays, have been used primarily to inform adjuvant therapies for early stage breast cancer, but some data on the use of these assays to predict outcomes in MBC could inform future practice. For example, intrinsic subtype RNA profiling data (PAM50 assay) for 580 patients who had HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC treated with first‐line ribociclib and endocrine therapy were included in a pooled analysis of the three MONALEESA trials. That analysis demonstrated a median OS duration of 68 months for the luminal A subtype, 59 months for the luminal B subtype, 40 months for HER2‐enriched MBC, and 19 months for the rare basal‐like subtype, which is typically associated with triple‐negative receptor status and seen in only 2.4% of all patients. Worse outcomes were also noted in the control group, consistent with the expected lack of benefit from endocrine therapy in the basal‐like subtype. Transcriptomic analysis has also been used to predict sensitivity to endocrine therapy. The SETER/PR index, an 18‐gene expression signature, was trained and validated to predict sensitivity to endocrine therapy in patients with HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC in a study by Sinn et al., in which higher biopsy‐based SETER/PR index scores were associated with significantly longer PFS and OS among 140 patients with MBC. This assay is being tested further as a potential predictor of endocrine sensitivity but can only be used in patients who have wild‐type ESR1 because activating ESR1 mutations generate artifactually high scores, as expected.
Acquired resistance to endocrine therapy, conversely, develops over time in tumors that initially respond to it but later become unresponsive. Mutations of the ESR1 gene, which encodes ER, tend to be detected after exposure to estrogen‐deprivation therapy with AIs or fulvestrant. These mutations typically occur in the ligand‐binding domain, particularly Y537S and D538G, which confer ligand‐independent ER activity that leads to endocrine therapy resistance and increased tumor aggressiveness, necessitating a switch to alternative strategies to overcome resistance (as described further below). Tandem mutations (multiple, concurrent ESR1 mutations) further enhance constitutive ER activity and resistance to endocrine therapy.
Mutations in the RB1 tumor‐suppressor gene can also contribute to therapy resistance. RB1 plays a central role in regulating the G1‐S transition of the cell cycle by inhibiting E2F transcription factors that govern entry into the cell cycle. CDK4/6is like palbociclib, ribociclib, and abemaciclib function by maintaining RB1 in an active (hypophosphorylated) state and keeping it bound to E2F transcription factors, thereby keeping them inactive and preventing uncontrolled cell proliferation. However, RB1 loss caused by inactivating mutations or gene silencing results in an uncontrolled G1‐S transition independent of CDK4/6 regulation, rendering these inhibitors (as well as endocrine therapy alone) ineffective. A retrospective analysis of patient tumor samples from clinical trials demonstrated that patients who had RB1‐deficient, HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC at baseline exhibited significantly shorter PFS after treatment with CDK4/6is than patients who had RB1 wild‐type tumors. The median PFS duration for patients with RB1‐mutated tumors was about 3.7 months, whereas that for patients who had RB1 wild‐type tumors was 9–12 months after treatment with palbociclib or ribociclib. RB1 loss was observed at baseline, indicating de novo resistance, and postprogression in those who had lost RB1 after it was initially present at baseline, suggesting acquired resistance. These findings establish RB1 loss as a predictive biomarker for both primary and acquired resistance to CDK4/6is. Emerging data suggest that patients with tumoral RB1 loss exhibit resistance to endocrine/biologic therapies in general and thus may benefit more and sooner from chemotherapy options, including the antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs). 

Understanding both intrinsic and acquired resistance mechanisms will enable the development of optimized treatment algorithms, although these phenotypes are not always explained by specific genomic alterations. , Importantly, resistance mechanisms in metastatic disease result not only from genomic alterations but also from less well understood phenomena such as epigenetic alterations, including changes in chromatin structure and other mechanisms of gene silencing. Although epigenetic therapies have yet to be translated into the clinical setting, this approach is very promising for personalized medicine that targets chromatin biology and other nongenomic alterations. Clonal evolution likely proceeds as early as initial exposure to adjuvant therapy and continues with multiple evolutionary trajectories under the selective pressures of the subsequent courses of therapy. This fertile area of research is likely to yield novel, rationally designed treatments. 

Thus, advances in understanding the fundamental biology of resistance are needed to enable rational drug discovery. Breakthroughs will require the collection and multi‐omic analysis of tumor tissue—ideally, matched patient samples obtained before therapy and after response and/or progression during therapy. Such tissue collection is increasingly being built into large‐scale clinical trials testing new therapies and requires engagement of key stakeholders, including patients, patient advocates, clinical investigators, regulatory specialists, basic scientists, and experts in bioinformatics, medicinal chemistry, and pharmacology. The complexity of diagnostic tests and the waiting time for results can be difficult for patients, and we have observed that it is helpful to explain to patients that the goal is to acquire basic biologic information that will enable the formulation of personalized treatments. This also helps involve the patient in treatment decisions and improve understanding of the overall strategy.

## IMPACT OF THE TUMOR MICROENVIRONMENT ON HR‐POSITIVE BREAST CANCER BIOLOGY
The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays an important role in the progression and therapeutic response of HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC. The TME consists of the extracellular matrix and various cellular compartments comprising an array of specialized, immunophenotypically and functionally distinct cells, including cancer, stromal, and immune cells. These components interact dynamically to influence tumor behavior. Cytokines and other soluble factors, many of which are produced by TME cells, modulate immune and inflammatory responses, have direct effects on tumor cells, and are considered components of the TME.
The TME affects the biology of HR‐positive breast cancer through several mechanisms. First, it provides a supportive niche for tumor cells that enhances their survival, proliferation, and metastatic potential. Second, interactions between cancer cells and the TME can trigger epithelial–mesenchymal transition, a multigene process by which cancer cells acquire invasive capability and undergo other developmental changes, including immune downregulation and loss of epithelial proteins and cellular functions, that contribute to tumor aggressiveness and therapeutic resistance. Third, factors secreted by stromal cells and immune components can promote tumor growth through growth factor signaling and metabolic adaptation. Fourth, regions within tumors may become hypoxic, leading to altered metabolism in cancer cells. These metabolic changes, driven by nutrient competition, hypoxic stress, and stromal and immune‐derived factors, further reinforce tumor aggressiveness, immune suppression, and therapeutic resistance within the TME. , 

The interaction between cancer cells and stromal cells in the TME produces several effects that drive tumor progression. Stromal components—including tumor‐associated fibroblasts, adipocytes, and endothelial cells—secrete cytokines, growth factors, and extracellular matrix proteins that create a tumor‐supportive microenvironment. These interactions can enhance tumor‐cell invasiveness and metastatic potential. For instance, interleukin‐6 produced by stromal cells can activate signaling pathways (e.g., JAK/STAT, PI3K, RAS, and NF‐κB) that promote tumor cell proliferation, survival, and drug resistance. 

In turn, tumor cells actively manipulate the surrounding stroma to facilitate their growth and survival. They can alter the composition and structure of the extracellular matrix, promoting cell migration and invasion. Enzymes like matrix metalloproteinases degrade extracellular matrix components, allowing tumors to infiltrate surrounding tissues. In addition, tumor cells secrete molecules that recruit stromal cells to the TME, further strengthening stromal support. These interactions may also influence drug metabolism or enhance drug efflux, making cancer cells more resistant to treatment. 

The TME is often enriched with immunosuppressive immune cells, such as regulatory T cells and myeloid‐derived suppressor cells. These cells further weaken antitumor immunity by suppressing the activity of cytotoxic T cells and promoting an immune‐tolerant environment, ultimately facilitating tumor progression. One key mechanism underlying the immune‐suppressive TME involves immune checkpoints. For example, PD‐L1 expressed on tumor cells binds to programmed cell death protein 1 (PD‐1) on T cells, suppressing T‐cell activation and function, dampening the immune response, and allowing cancer cells to persist unchecked. 

Immune resistance varies by breast cancer subtype. Whereas immune evasion is most pronounced in triple‐negative breast cancer, HR‐positive/HER2‐negative disease also exhibits this capacity, particularly in luminal B and high‐grade tumors. HR‐positive cancers generally have a lower tumor mutational burden (TMB) and reduced immune infiltration, and single‐cell sequencing has demonstrated the emergence of an immunosuppressive microenvironment after CDK4/6i therapy. These findings suggest that immune resistance may play a more important role in HR‐positive disease than previously recognized, although clinical translation of this insight remains limited. There are focused trials of immunotherapy in select patients with HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC. In the PACE trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT03147287), adding the immune checkpoint inhibitor avelumab to fulvestrant plus palbociclib improved median PFS (8.1 vs. 4.8 months with fulvestrant alone), but the difference was not statistically significant. The benefit appeared greater in tumors with ESR1 or PIK3CA mutations. These results are exploratory and need confirmation in larger, biomarker‐driven studies.

## LOBULAR MBC
In the metastatic setting, infiltrating lobular breast cancer (ILC), which constitutes 10%–15% of all breast cancer cases, is predominantly of the HR‐positive/HER2‐negative subset, making it generally responsive to endocrine therapies. Whereas ILC is approached diagnostically and therapeutically in a manner similar to infiltrating ductal cancer, the unique biology may result in variations in management. These tumors very commonly exhibit loss of CDH1 (E‐cadherin) because of either genomic loss‐of‐function mutations or epigenetic silencing; CDH1 loss contributes to increased cell migration and invasion, a potential link to more aggressive disease. , The ILC tumor architecture differs from that of infiltrating ductal cancer in its lack of cellular adhesion and appears microscopically as tumor cells that line up single‐file rather than forming discrete tumor masses. Because this is also reflected at the macroscopic level, ILC tumors are more difficult to image and follow clinically and radiographically for response or progression. Molecular features of ILC are not substantially different from those of infiltrating ductal cancer, with a few exceptions, such as a greater tendency to exhibit clonal selection for constitutively activating mutations of ERBB2 (the gene encoding the HER2 receptor; see Table  ). Patients with pretreated ILC can also exhibit acquired apolipoprotein B messenger RNA editing catalytic (ApoBEC) genomic signatures because of cytidine deaminases, which cause hypermutability in several cancer types, most notably ILC. This can result in a very high TMB, and it can predict resistance to endocrine and CDK4/6i‐based therapy and potential sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibitors (see Table  ). , However, ILC tends to show resistance to cytotoxic chemotherapy, possibly because of its lower proliferation rate. Prospective data on the efficacy of novel agents—such as oral SERDs, ADCs, or immunotherapies—in ILC remain limited because these patients are underrepresented in most clinical trials. Dedicated studies are needed to define optimal treatment approaches for this distinct histologic subtype.

## MOLECULAR SURVEILLANCE OF MBC
Clinical assessments of tumor samples before and after treatment provide further insights into the biologic adaptations driving resistance, guiding the development of novel approaches to overcoming endocrine therapy failure. Over time, selective pressure from endocrine therapy and targeted agents (e.g., CDK4/6is or PI3Kis) leads to clonal selection and tumor adaptation. Given the spatial and temporal heterogeneity of MBC, sequential biopsies from repeat tumor tissue sampling and/or liquid biopsy have the potential to characterize tumor evolution, identify emerging resistance mechanisms, and tailor treatment strategies. However, the appropriate timing and frequency of sequential biopsies have yet to be established and must be tailored to the clinical situation. 

Rapid improvements in the performance and cost of the less invasive liquid biopsy approach to analyzing circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) has allowed for more sensitive detection of actionable mutations, including subclonal mutations like ESR1, and for monitoring of tumor evolution at an earlier time point. Integrating ctDNA monitoring with clinical evaluations may offer a more comprehensive approach to managing MBC, ensuring that treatment strategies remain aligned with the tumor's latest molecular characteristics. The levels of ctDNA in circulation tend to correlate with tumor burden and response to therapy: a decrease in total ctDNA levels within a treatment cycle correlates with response, whereas stable or increasing levels may indicate treatment resistance or disease progression. Currently, scan results and clinical evaluations are required to confirm disease status. Trials testing molecular monitoring for the timing of treatment change and informing treatment choices are both ongoing and planned. Pre‐analytical variables, such as tumor DNA shedding rates as well as assay sensitivity limitations for detection of low mutant allele fractions, are potential constraints.
The PADA‐1 trial highlighted the potential clinical value of molecular surveillance coupled with an adaptive systemic therapy approach (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT03079011). Patients on palbociclib and AI therapy with emergent mutations in ESR1 in ctDNA but without clinical progression were randomized to switch from AI to fulvestrant versus continuing on AI, both given with continuation of palbociclib. Compared with continuation on AI, patients who switched to fulvestrant had significantly better PFS (11.9 vs. 5.7 months; hazard ratio, 0.61; p = .004); however, no difference in OS between the two groups has yet been reported. Similarly, the phase 3 SERENA‐6 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT04964934) demonstrated that patients receiving AI in combination with a CDK4/6i in whom emergence of ESR1 mutations was detected by serial ctDNA assessments without clinical progression had a significantly longer PFS duration of 16.0 months (95% confidence interval [CI], 12.7–18.2 months) when they switched to camizestrant, an oral SERD, than those who remained on AI (PFS, 9.2 months; 95% CI, 7.2–9.5 months). No difference in OS benefit was observed from switching drugs, and the time to second progression on subsequent therapy was not significantly different but with immature data. The time to second progression on subsequent therapy may be the best end point to use to prove utility; however, because the trial was asymmetric in its adaptive design, with the control group not receiving camizestrant upon clinical progression, it is difficult to distinguish the contribution of molecular monitoring to trigger an early therapy change versus a benefit due to camizestrant. Future trials using molecular triaging to one of two or more options may accelerate drug development and optimize the timing of therapy change.
From the patient's viewpoint, ongoing monitoring of clinical status, including response to treatment, involves frequent scans and examinations. Re‐biopsy of a tumor at the time of progression may be necessary, as the status of HR and HER2 can change because of the selective pressures of drug exposure and invasion/metastasis. An important step is to inform patients and their families and/or caregivers of the reasons for these procedures and give them a road map that includes key milestones, such as repeat biopsies at progression, ctDNA assessments, imaging scans, and potential therapy changes.
A practical limitation of ctDNA analysis is the presence of clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP), in which age‐related hematopoietic mutations may be detected and misinterpreted as tumor‐derived alterations. From the patient’s perspective, it is important to explain that not every mutation identified in blood necessarily originates from the cancer, and distinguishing CHIP from tumor‐derived mutations is critical for avoiding inappropriate therapy choices. There is no standard treatment for CHIP, although it does appear to portend a higher risk of developing hematologic malignancies and nonmalignant (cardiac, neurovascular, gastrointestinal, renal, and autoimmune) diseases, and it is reasonable to refer patients for surveillance and clinical trial opportunities being conducted at several academic and other centers. Attentiveness to patients’ preferences and limitations and expanded discussions of options at key points in the course of their treatment may be particularly important given the complexity and multiphasic nature of this disease.

## TREATMENT PATHWAYS FOR HR‐POSITIVE/HER2‐NEGATIVE MBC
In HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC management, decision support is critical to optimize treatment selection and improve patient outcomes. This involves integrating clinical guidelines, genomic profiling data, and individual patient factors, such as previous endocrine therapy responses, comorbidities, and performance status. Multidisciplinary tumor boards and advanced decision‐support systems help clinicians navigate complex treatment algorithms by synthesizing data from genomic sequencing, PROs, and evolving clinical trial information. Although artificial intelligence is increasingly being explored in oncology research and drug discovery, its performance in clinical management and multidisciplinary tumor boards has been limited to date. One evaluation demonstrated that artificial intelligence was not consistently aligned with expert consensus in multidisciplinary tumor board decisions, highlighting the need for cautious integration and rigorous validation of these tools before routine use. In this section, we provide an expanded discussion of National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN)‐recommended therapy for HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC, along with supporting evidence (summarized in Figure  ), and outline novel approaches to newer therapies that are being tested in this highly fluid field.
 

## CDK4/6 inhibitors in first‐line and second‐line therapy for MBC
Despite the successes of endocrine therapy, resistance develops in virtually all patients and remains a pressing challenge. The recognition of growth factor receptor pathway activation as a key driver of endocrine therapy resistance, as illustrated in Figure . Targeting dysregulation of the cell cycle—long recognized as a hallmark of cancer—was enabled by the development of selective CDK4/6is that specifically target the G1‐ to S‐phase transition. In the first‐line setting, integrating CDK4/6is with endocrine therapy has revolutionized treatment for HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC and represents the standard of care in the absence of contraindications to therapy. For patients who have progression within 12 months of or during adjuvant endocrine therapy, which is associated with a significantly shorter response to first‐line CDK4/6i‐based therapy, the use of inavolisib, a mutant‐selective PI3Ki, is recommended in the presence of PIK3CA mutation, as further discussed below (see Therapy targeting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway). The pivotal trials leading to approvals of the three CDK4/6is approved in the United States are listed in Table  . For example, in the randomized phase 3 PALOMA‐2 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT01740427), , which enrolled postmenopausal women with HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC, the addition of palbociclib to letrozole extended the median PFS duration to 24.8 months versus 14.5 months with letrozole alone, translating to a 42% reduction in the risk of disease progression (hazard ratio, 0.58; 95% CI, 0.46–0.72), although differences in OS were not significant. Grade 3/4 neutropenia was the most common toxic effect.
 Similarly, the MONALEESA‐2 phase 3 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT01958021) , demonstrated the benefit of ribociclib in combination with letrozole versus letrozole alone. Patients receiving ribociclib had a median PFS duration of 25.3 months versus 16.0 months in the control group (hazard ratio, 0.57; 95% CI, 0.46–0.70; p < .001) and significantly longer median OS of 63.9 months versus 51.4 months (hazard ratio, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.63–0.93; p = .004), with neutropenia and QT interval prolongation the major toxic effects. The MONALEESA‐7 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02278120) demonstrated that premenopausal women derived similar benefits from the addition of ribociclib to an AI and ovarian suppression, with a median PFS duration of 20.5 months (hazard ratio, 0.59; 95% CI, 0.48–0.73; p < .001). Ribociclib also significantly improved OS duration, at 42 months (70.2% vs. 46.0%) with the combination of ribociclib, an AI, and ovarian suppression (hazard ratio, 0.71; 95% CI, 0.54–0.95; p = .00973). 

In the MONARCH‐3 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02422615), postmenopausal women who received abemaciclib paired with an AI had a median PFS duration of 28.2 months, significantly outperforming the duration of 14.8 months in the placebo group (hazard ratio, 0.54; 95% CI, 0.41–0.72; p < .001). However, the effect on OS was not significant (hazard ratio, 0.804; 95% CI, 0.637–1.015; p = .0664). Gastrointestinal side effects like diarrhea were the major adverse events.
Treatment with palbociclib significantly improved PFS in the second‐line setting when combined with fulvestrant in the PALOMA‐3 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT01942135). The median PFS duration was 9.5 months with palbociclib plus fulvestrant versus 4.6 months with a placebo plus fulvestrant (hazard ratio, 0.46; 95% CI, 0.36–0.59; p < .0001), and the median OS duration was 34.9 months for the palbociclib group versus 28.0 months for the placebo group (hazard ratio, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.64–1.03; p = .043). , 

In addition, in the MONALEESA‐3 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02278120), , investigators extended the application of ribociclib by combining it with fulvestrant in both the first‐line and second‐line settings in postmenopausal women. The study showed a longer median PFS duration of 20.5 months in the group receiving ribociclib compared with 12.8 months in the group receiving fulvestrant alone (hazard ratio, 0.59; 95% CI, 0.48–0.73; p < .001). OS was significantly better in the ribociclib/fulvestrant combination group than in the fulvestrant monotherapy group, with a median OS duration of 53.7 months versus 41.5 months, respectively (hazard ratio, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.59–0.90; p = .004). Neutropenia, fatigue, and elevated liver enzymes were the major toxic effects.
In the MONARCH‐2 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02107703), , abemaciclib plus fulvestrant significantly improved PFS (16.4 vs. 9.3 months; hazard ratio, 0.553; 95% CI, 0.449–0.681; p < .001) and OS (46.7 vs. 37.3 months; hazard ratio, 0.784; 95% CI, 0.606–0.945; p = .01) compared with fulvestrant alone. Diarrhea was the most common adverse event, and the rate of severe neutropenia was lower than that for other CDK4/6is.
One of the key questions arising from the pivotal CDK4/6i trials is whether these therapies extend survival. PFS was the primary end point for these studies, and they were not independently powered to detect OS benefits. However, ad hoc analyses have demonstrated better OS versus single‐agent endocrine therapy, with nonoverlapping 95% CIs for ribociclib in the first‐line and second‐line settings and abemaciclib in the second‐line setting, but not for palbociclib. , , It is important to note that survival benefits are difficult to compare among the different agents because this outcome was not the primary end point, and the studies enrolled different populations, particularly with various prior treatments in the metastatic setting. Real‐world analyses using propensity score matching and other tools to address patient heterogeneity and treatment selection bias have yielded conflicting results. , The largest population‐based study demonstrated similar survival durations with the three approved CDK4/6is; in three‐way comparisons, there was a slight OS advantage for abemaciclib over palbociclib, with OS hazard ratios not crossing unity. 

Although differences in survival data may influence clinicians' selection of the specific CDK4/6i drug used, the toxicity profile may be the most important consideration in choosing initial and subsequent therapy. Ribociclib and palbociclib are associated with higher rates of neutropenia and other cytopenias, whereas abemaciclib causes more gastrointestinal side effects, particularly diarrhea. Furthermore, ribociclib is associated with uncommon hepatotoxic effects and QT interval prolongation. Notably, these observations are based on cross‐trial comparisons and should be interpreted with caution. All three of the inhibitors can increase the risk of thrombotic events and interstitial lung disease/pneumonitis. For patients who are felt to have contraindications to CDK4/6i therapy, endocrine therapy alone is recommended.
Beyond these practice‐changing studies, trials like RIGHT Choice and ABIGAIL have further increased our understanding of treatment strategies for patients who have MBC with a high disease burden and visceral crisis. The RIGHT Choice trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT03839823) demonstrated that first‐line ribociclib plus endocrine therapy in premenopausal women resulted in a median PFS duration of 21.8 months (95% CI, 17.4–26.7 months) compared with 12.8 months (95% CI, 10.1–18.4 months) for combination chemotherapy. The overall response rate (ORR) was 66.1% for ribociclib plus endocrine therapy and 61.8% for combination chemotherapy, with a median time to response of 4.9 versus 3.2 months, respectively. Ribociclib was associated with neutropenia, hepatotoxicity, and QT interval prolongation, whereas chemotherapy was associated with myelosuppression, neuropathy, and alopecia. Similarly, the ABIGAIL trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT04603183) has produced promising evidence of benefit from abemaciclib combined with AIs in patients with aggressive breast cancer. That study demonstrated improved 12‐week response rates compared with paclitaxel. Toxic effects associated with abemaciclib include diarrhea, neutropenia, and fatigue, whereas those associated with paclitaxel include neuropathy, alopecia, and myelosuppression.
Several trials have directly compared CDK4/6i therapy with chemotherapy in patients who received prior endocrine therapy for HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC. In the PEARL trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02028507), palbociclib combined with endocrine therapy (exemestane or fulvestrant) did not improve PFS over capecitabine in patients who had progressed on a prior AI, either in the overall cohort (median PFS, 7.5 vs. 10.0 months; adjusted hazard ratio, 1.13; 95% CI, 0.85–1.50) or in patients with wild‐type ESR1 (median PFS, 8.0 vs. 10.6 months; adjusted hazard ratio, 1.11; 95% CI, 0.87–1.41). Although the median OS duration was 31.1 months for the palbociclib plus endocrine therapy group compared with 32.8 months for the capecitabine group (adjusted hazard ratio, 1.10; 95% CI, 0.81–1.50; p = .550), palbociclib plus endocrine therapy was better tolerated and was associated with improved quality of life. Conversely, in the Young‐PEARL trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02592746), which enrolled premenopausal patients who experienced disease progression during or after previous tamoxifen treatment and had received no previous AI treatment, palbociclib plus endocrine therapy produced a significantly longer PFS than capecitabine (median, 19.5 vs. 14.0 months; hazard ratio, 0.74; p = .036), although OS was similar in the two groups (median, 54.8 vs. 57.8 months, respectively; hazard ratio, 1.02; p = .92). Neutropenia and fatigue were commonly observed with palbociclib, whereas hand–foot syndrome, diarrhea, and mucositis were associated with capecitabine treatment.
Collectively, based on consistent gains in PFS and overall improved quality of life, these findings underscore the transformative role of CDK4/6is in improving clinical outcomes in patients with MBC.

## Timing of treatment with CDK4/6 inhibitors
In the evolving landscape of MBC treatment, the optimal timing for initiating CDK4/6i administration remains an important question. In the SONIA trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT03425838), investigators examined whether beginning treatment with CDK4/6is alongside endocrine therapy in the first‐line setting is essential or whether their use can be delayed until the second line without compromising patient outcomes. Notably, the trial's primary end point—the time to second disease progression—did not differ markedly between early and delayed initiation. Specifically, the median time to second disease progression was 31.0 months for a first‐line CDK4/6i plus an AI and 26.8 months for a first‐line AI followed by a CDK4/6i (hazard ratio, 0.87; p = .10). Furthermore, the median OS duration was 45.9 months for the first‐line CDK4/6i group and 53.7 months for the second‐line CDK4/6i group (hazard ratio, 0.98; p = .83). Although the efficacy of the two approaches did not differ significantly, patients experienced considerably less overall toxicity with less palbociclib exposure, and the cost over the two treatment lines was significantly lower with delayed palbociclib use.
The SONIA trial thus supports a more individualized, value‐based approach based on shared decision making. For patients with low‐burden, indolent disease and strong concerns about cumulative toxicity, quality of life, or cost, deferring CDK4/6i to the second line is a reasonable option. Conversely, for patients with high tumor burden, visceral crisis, symptomatic disease, or features suggesting a higher risk of rapid progression, first‐line combination therapy remains important given the response rates and PFS benefits seen in earlier trials and the absence of a definitive predictive biomarker to select patients who will derive long‐term survival benefit from early CDK4/6i. Uncertainty remains in selecting which patients are expected to do well with endocrine therapy alone and which might benefit from a combination with CDK4/6i. Future work should entail biomarker discovery, comparative‐effectiveness studies across different CDK4/6is, and sequencing studies that reflect contemporary second‐line and later‐line options.

## Biomarker‐driven treatment after CDK4/6 inhibitor‐based therapy
After disease progression during CDK4/6i‐based therapy, several second‐line systemic therapy options can be considered while weighing various factors to assess the likelihood of response to endocrine‐based therapy or the need to move to chemotherapy‐based treatment. Decision making should integrate clinical factors, such as the duration of response to first‐line endocrine therapy and the overall burden of disease, with tumor biomarker data.
Because CDK4/6i‐based therapy is increasingly used in the first‐line setting, the choice of second‐line therapy should be informed by prior therapy and genomic analysis. Acquisition of tumor tissue can be considered after disease progression unless the interval from the previous biopsy is short (although the optimal timing is not established) to ascertain the receptor status (ER, PR, and HER2) and to perform genomic analysis. Tissue testing for receptor biomarkers should follow established American Society of Clinical Oncology/College of American Pathologists guidelines. , Alternatively, liquid biopsy can be performed if a tumor biopsy is not feasible. Comprehensive testing—whether using tumor tissue or ctDNA—for alterations like PIK3CA and ESR1 mutations and for less common but actionable aberrations like NTRK fusions, microsatellite instability, high TMB, and RET fusions is recommended. Liquid biopsy may be better for detecting ESR1 mutations, which tend to be subclonal and may not be uniformly detectable in a given tumor sample. As liquid biopsy becomes more sensitive, comprehensive, and less expensive overall, the preference may shift to this assay. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) is increasingly requiring the co‐development of qualifying biomarkers that predict a favorable risk/benefit ratio for targeted therapies. Genomic sequencing results should be interpreted using gene annotation, which provides additional background for each genomic aberration, including gene function, type of aberration and its consequence, and recommended therapies, as depicted in Table  .

## Therapy targeting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway
In the phase 3 SOLAR‐1 study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02437318), investigators evaluated the addition of alpelisib to fulvestrant in patients with PIK3CA‐mutant, HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC whose disease progressed on prior endocrine therapy. The addition of alpelisib to fulvestrant improved the median PFS duration to 11.0 months versus 5.7 months with placebo plus fulvestrant (hazard ratio, 0.65; 95% CI, 0.50–0.85). The median OS duration was 39.3 months for the alpelisib group compared with 31.4 months for the placebo group (hazard ratio, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.64–1.15; p = .15), leading to the approval of alpelisib. Subsequently, the BYLieve study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT03056755) confirmed the clinical activity of alpelisib after progression on CDK4/6i.
Similarly, the CAPItello‐291 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT04305496) demonstrated that treatment with the AKT inhibitor capivasertib paired with fulvestrant improved the median PFS duration to 7.3 months versus 3.1 months with fulvestrant plus placebo (hazard ratio, 0.50; 95% CI, 0.38–0.65) in patients who had genomic alterations in PI3K, AKT, or PTEN.
In the pre‐CDK4/6i era, the BOLERO‐2 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT00863655) demonstrated that treatment with exemestane plus everolimus led to median PFS durations of 10.6 months versus 4.1 months with a placebo (hazard ratio, 0.36; 95% CI, 0.31–0.48; p < .001), although no OS benefit was observed (31.0 vs. 26.7 months for everolimus plus exemestane vs. placebo plus exemestane, respectively; hazard ratio, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.75–1.05; p = .17). Also, the PrE0102 study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT01797120) demonstrated that the combination of fulvestrant and everolimus improved the median PFS duration to 10.4 months from 5.1 months with fulvestrant alone (hazard ratio, 0.61; 95% CI, 0.40–0.92). The median OS duration was 28.3 months for the everolimus group and 31.4 months for the placebo group (hazard ratio, 1.31; p = .37). Everolimus has also been shown to improve PFS when added to tamoxifen. , Although combinations of everolimus with fulvestrant or tamoxifen are not approved by the FDA, they are recommended in NCCN guidelines and can be used if AI therapy was used previously and was not tolerated. Everolimus‐containing therapy may be an option in later lines of therapy, in the absence of qualifying mutations for other targeted therapies, or if other options are not tolerated.
In the INAVO120 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT04191499), investigators tested the use of the mutant‐selective PI3Ki inavolisib added to standard palbociclib and fulvestrant as first‐line therapy for patients who have MBC with PIK3CA mutations and endocrine therapy‐resistant disease, defined as disease progression during or within 12 months of completing adjuvant endocrine therapy. PFS was significantly longer in this high‐risk cohort (15.0 vs. 7.3 months; hazard ratio, 0.43; 95% CI, 0.32–0.59; p < .001), with a significantly longer median OS duration of 34.0 months in the treatment group (95% CI, 28.4–44.8 months) compared with the placebo group (27.0 months; 95% CI, 22.8–38.7 months) and a hazard ratio of 0.67 (95% CI, 0.48–0.94; p = .02). This trial led to the approval of inavolisib for this select patient group, thereby emphasizing the benefit of identifying and targeting specific molecular alterations early in the course of disease.
Across this class of drugs, common adverse events include hyperglycemia, rash, stomatitis, diarrhea, and other gastrointestinal effects. Hyperglycemia (caused by the insulin receptor also signaling through the PI3K pathway) is particularly notable with alpelisib, requiring careful monitoring and even prophylactic therapy and, when necessary, collaboration with endocrinology for hyperglycemia management. Capivasertib is more commonly associated with diarrhea and, to a lesser degree, with hyperglycemia, whereas everolimus carries risks of stomatitis, hyperglycemia, and pneumonitis. Inavolisib, when evaluated under stricter eligibility criteria for baseline diabetes compared with alpelisib in SOLAR‐1, demonstrated a lower incidence of hyperglycemia, rash, and gastrointestinal effects, although neutropenia was more frequent because of its combination with palbociclib. These safety profiles should be considered when selecting therapy, alongside efficacy data and patient comorbidities.

## Newer endocrine therapies and ESR1 mutations
Endocrine therapies refer to those that directly target ER. ESR1 mutations are common after estrogen‐deprivation therapy and, as described above, SERDs have relevant activity in the presence of these mutations. The EMERALD trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT03778931) demonstrated that elacestrant‐based monotherapy significantly extended median PFS over standard endocrine therapy (2.8 vs. 1.9 months in the overall cohort; 3.8 vs. 1.9 months in patients with ESR1 mutations [hazard ratio, 0.55; 95% CI, 0.39–0.77]; and 8.6 vs. 1.9 months in patients with prior CDK4/6i use for at least 12 months). However, the difference in OS did not meet the predefined significance level. Gastrointestinal toxicity (primarily nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and decreased appetite) and fatigue were the main adverse events. Elacestrant is now approved for patients with activating ESR1 mutations. Combinations of elacestrant with CDK4/6is and PI3Kis are being explored.
The phase 3 EMBER‐3 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT04975308) compared the SERD imlunestrant as monotherapy or in combination with abemaciclib versus standard endocrine therapy in patients with HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC who had disease recurrence or progression during or after AI therapy, administered either alone or with a CDK4/6i. Among the cohort with ESR1 mutations, the median PFS was 5.5 months versus 3.8 months with standard‐of‐care fulvestrant or exemestane (hazard ratio, 0.62; 95% CI, 0.46–0.82; p = .008), which is now FDA‐approved as a single agent after one or more line(s) of endocrine therapy; however, no significant difference was observed in the cohort with wild‐type ESR1. PFS was longer in patients who received the combination of imlunestrant with abemaciclib than in those who received imlunestrant alone, regardless of ESR1 mutation status (9.4 vs. 5.5 months; hazard ratio, 0.57; 95% CI, 0.44–0.73; p < .001). The trial also demonstrated consistent benefit in the imlunestrant–abemaciclib group previously treated with a CDK4/6i, with a median PFS of 9.1 months versus 3.7 months (hazard ratio, 0.51; 95% CI, 0.38–0.68). Gastrointestinal disturbances and fatigue were the major toxicities.
Additional novel SERDs are in earlier phases of investigation. For instance, the randomized phase 2 SERENA‐2 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT04214288) demonstrated that treatment with camizestrant led to a median PFS duration of 7.2 months versus 3.7 months with fulvestrant treatment (hazard ratio, 0.42; 95% CI, 0.31–0.56), with hot flashes and gastrointestinal side effects as the major adverse effects in patients with ESR1‐mutant or wild‐type ESR1 disease.

## Proteolysis‐targeting chimera estrogen receptor degraders

## Vepdegestrant (ARV‐471)
Vepdegestrant is a proteolysis‐targeting chimera that directs rapid ER degradation through the cell's proteasomal machinery. In the phase 2 VERITAC trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT04072952), the clinical benefit rate (CBR) was 38% overall and 51% in ESR1‐mutant tumors, with median PFS durations of 3.5 and 5.5 months, respectively. The phase 3 VERITAC‐2 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05654623) demonstrated superior PFS with vepdegestrant compared with fulvestrant in patients with ESR1‐mutant tumors (5.0 vs. 2.1 months; hazard ratio, 0.57; p<.001). Combination studies have shown encouraging activity: with palbociclib, the ORR was 42% and the median PFS was 11.1 months regardless of ESR1 status (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05501769). With abemaciclib, the CBR was 62.5% and the ORR was 26.7% (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05548127). Most toxicities were grade 1–2, with no dose reductions required, and safety was consistent with CDK4/6i profiles. This agent was not approved by the FDA at time of this publication. Ultimately, novel endocrine agents are likely to be most effective in combination with proven biologic combinations, which are in current testing.

## Second‐line therapy and beyond for biomarker‐negative MBC
In patients who have tumors for which biomarkers do not directly inform the use of targeted therapy, several effective treatment options remain. Endocrine therapy combined with everolimus is an option regardless of mutations in PIK3CA, AKT, or PTEN, as discussed above (see Therapy targeting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway).
In addition, several trials have tested the effects of continuing CDK4/6 inhibition upon MBC progression, particularly in patients who are not candidates for other biologically targeted therapies and remain asymptomatic or have only minimal symptoms and in those who had a longer duration of prior response to CDK4/6i. Findings suggest that sequential use of a CDK4/6i may be more beneficial than nonsequential approaches. 

The phase 3 postMONARCH randomized trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05169567) demonstrated improved outcomes using fulvestrant combined with abemaciclib versus a placebo in patients who previously received a CDK4/6i (median PFS, 6.0 vs. 5.3 months; hazard ratio, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.57–0.95), particularly when the prior treatment duration exceeded 12 months. Consistent benefits were observed in both the ESR1‐mutated and the wild‐type ESR1 subgroups. In addition, the phase 2 MAINTAIN randomized trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05207709) evaluated patients with HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC that had progressed on prior CDK4/6i therapy. After progression, patients were randomized to receive either ribociclib combined with a switched endocrine therapy (fulvestrant or exemestane) or placebo with the same switched endocrine therapy. The trial demonstrated a significant improvement in median PFS for the ribociclib group (5.3 vs. 2.8 months in the placebo group; hazard ratio, 0.57; 95% CI, 0.39–0.83; p = .006). In contrast, rechallenge with palbociclib did not prove beneficial in the phase 2 PACE randomized trial or the phase 2 PALMIRA randomized trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT03809988) compared with endocrine therapy alone in patients who previously experienced progression while receiving palbociclib (in the adjuvant or first‐line setting for MBC after a response or stable disease ≥24 weeks). The median PFS duration was 4.2 months in the palbociclib plus endocrine therapy arm versus 3.6 months in the endocrine therapy alone arm, with a hazard ratio of 0.84. Taken together, the best evidence for use of a second CDK4/6i after MBC progression is for abemaciclib based on phase 3 trial data with adequate power. Furthermore, abemaciclib is already approved as a single agent for use in later lines of therapy. More selective targeting of CDK4 alone may be more effective and less toxic than CDK4/6 blockade; this is currently being explored in a controlled trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT06105632). 

The use of poly(ADP‐ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors for BRCA‐deficient cancers is well established and has been integrated into the management of HER2‐negative, BRCA‐mutated MBC (Table  ). The OlympiAD trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02000622) demonstrated that treatment with the PARP inhibitor olaparib significantly improved PFS (median, 7.0 vs. 4.2 months with chemotherapy; hazard ratio, 0.58; 95% CI, 0.43–0.80; p < .001), whereas OS did not differ significantly between the two groups (median OS, 19.3 vs. 17.1 months, respectively; hazard ratio, 0.90; 95% CI, 0.66–1.23; p = .513). Anemia, nausea, and fatigue were the main toxicities. In the EMBRACA trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT01945775), talazoparib treatment of patients with HER2‐negative MBC who had germline BRCA mutations also improved median PFS duration (8.6 vs. 5.3 months with chemotherapy), whereas the median OS duration did not significantly differ (19.3 vs. 19.5 months, respectively; hazard ratio, 0.848; 95% CI, 0.690–1.040; p = .17). Myelosuppression, fatigue, and gastrointestinal adverse events were the major toxic effects. Olaparib also was active in patients with MBC who had somatic BRCA1/2 mutations (ORR, 50%; median PFS, 6.3 months) or germline PALB2 mutations (ORR, 82%; median PFS, 13.3 months). 

 

## Endocrine therapy‐refractory disease
For patients with endocrine therapy–refractory disease, chemotherapy options like capecitabine, taxanes, liposomal doxorubicin, eribulin, vinorelbine, and gemcitabine have been the cornerstones of treatment. Major toxic effects include myelosuppression, neuropathy (taxanes), hand–foot syndrome (capecitabine), alopecia, and fatigue. Potent ADCs have exhibited greater activity than chemotherapy. ADCs are internalized into tumor cells through specific receptors that are targeted by antibodies bound to potent chemotherapeutic agents (payloads). The toxic moiety is then released into the tumor cells, resulting in cell killing and release of the drug into the TME.
Trastuzumab deruxtecan (T‐DXd), an ADC consisting of a humanized anti‐HER2 monoclonal antibody linked to a topoisomerase I inhibitor payload, was compared with chemotherapy of the physician's choice in the DESTINY‐Breast04 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05048797) for HER2‐low MBC (determined by immunohistochemistry expression from 1 to 2+, with fluorescence in situ hybridization–negative tumors). In the HR‐positive subgroup, the median PFS duration with T‐DXd was 9.6 months versus 4.2 months with chemotherapy (hazard ratio, 0.37), and the median OS duration was 23.9 months with T‐DXd versus 17.6 months with chemotherapy (hazard ratio, 0.69). In addition, the DESTINY‐Breast06 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT04494425) evaluated the activity of T‐DXd in patients with HER2‐low or HER2‐ultralow tumors (an immunohistochemistry score of 0 with incomplete/faint membrane staining in ≤10% of cells) who had received one or more lines of endocrine therapy but no previous chemotherapy for MBC. The median PFS duration was 13.2 months with T‐DXd versus 8.1 months with chemotherapy (hazard ratio, 0.63; 95% CI, 0.53–0.75; p < .0001). OS data were immature at the time of reporting (approximately 40% maturity) but suggested a trend toward improved OS with T‐DXd. Notably, T‐DXd is associated with a risk of specific adverse events, including pneumonitis, although these are rarely fatal. Therefore, the toxicity of the treatment should be balanced with its potential clinical benefits.
The TROPiCS‐02 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT03901339) demonstrated that treatment with sacituzumab govitecan, a TROP2‐targeted ADC, significantly extended PFS (median, 5.5 vs. 4.0 months for standard chemotherapy) and OS (median, 14.4 vs. 11.2 months for chemotherapy) in patients with HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC, with the major toxic effects being neutropenia, diarrhea, and nausea. In the TROPION‐Breast01 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05104866), treatment with the TROP2‐targeted ADC datopotamab deruxtecan resulted in a median PFS duration of 6.9 months compared with 4.9 months for chemotherapy but showed no significant improvement in OS (hazard ratio, 1.01; 95% CI, 0.83–1.22; p = .94). Mucositis, pneumonitis, and cytopenias were the major toxic effects.
ADCs are typically used as a second‐line, third‐line, or later treatment after progression on endocrine therapy and CDK4/6i in patients with HER2‐low disease. This approach may be considered in patients who have rapid disease progression (e.g., visceral crisis), high symptom burden, primary or secondary endocrine resistance, or genomic features associated with lack of response (e.g., RB1 loss conferring CDK4/6i resistance; FAT1 loss). Sequential use of ADCs should consider limited efficacy and potential toxicities, such as pneumonitis. Shared decision making is critical when considering sequencing of ADCs in this context.

## Tumor‐agnostic treatment approaches applicable to HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC
Although rare in breast cancer, NTRK and RET fusions and mutations represent emerging therapeutic opportunities. The NTRK inhibitors larotrectinib, investigated in the SCOUT trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02637687), and entrectinib, investigated in the ALKA‐372‐001 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02568267), have shown good ORRs (75%–80%) as treatment for advanced cancers harboring NTRK gene fusions. Also, the RET‐selective inhibitor selpercatinib has been approved for treatment of RET fusion–driven solid tumors. , 
BRAF V600 mutations are very rare in breast cancer—occurring in <1% of patients, in whom BRAF/MEK inhibition with dabrafenib and trametinib achieved a 38% response rate across diverse BRAF V600‐mutated cancers. 

A subset of patients with HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBCs (n = 28) may exhibit a TMB >10 mutations per megabase, an indicator for response to immune checkpoint inhibitors, thus representing a tumor‐agnostic indication for therapy with single‐agent pembrolizumab. High microsatellite instability is rare in MBC (approximately 0.6%) but, when present, is linked to a higher mutational burden and may identify a small subset of patients who could benefit from immunotherapy. 

## Newer agents in development for HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC
Multiple novel oral SERDs, selective ER modulators, proteolysis‐targeting chimeras, and complete ER antagonists are in clinical development.

## Palazestrant (OP‐1250)
Palazestrant is a complete ER antagonist with SERD‐like activity. In phase 1/2 trials, it demonstrated meaningful single‐agent activity, with a median PFS of 4.6 months and a CBR of 40%. In patients with ESR1 mutations, the median PFS was 5.6 months and the CBR was 52%. Most adverse events were low‐grade, although grade 4 neutropenia occurred in six patients. In combination with ribociclib, palazestrant achieved an ORR of 27% and a CBR of 76%, underscoring potential synergies between SERDs and CDK4/6is (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05126465). Further development with CDK4/6is is ongoing.

## Lasofoxifene
Lasofoxifene is a next‐generation selective ER modulator developed for ESR1‐mutant tumors. In the phase 2 ELAINE1 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT03781063), lasofoxifene did not significantly improve PFS over fulvestrant (5.6 vs. 3.7 months) but showed some benefit. In the single‐arm phase 2 ELAINE2 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT04432454), lasofoxifene combined with abemaciclib yielded an ORR of 55% and a median PFS of 13 months, with favorable tolerability. A phase 3 trial (ELAINE3; ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05696626) comparing lasofoxifene plus abemaciclib versus fulvestrant plus abemaciclib is in progress.

## Giredestrant
In the phase 3 evERA trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05306340), giredestrant, an oral SERD, with everolimus significantly improved PFS compared with standard endocrine therapy plus everolimus in patients previously treated with a CDK4/6i and endocrine therapy. Among patients with ESR1‐mutant disease, giredestrant plus everolimus achieved a 62% reduction in the risk of progression or death (hazard ratio, 0.38; median PFS, 9.99 vs. 5.45 months), whereas in the overall intention‐to‐treat population, a 44% risk reduction was observed (hazard ratio, 0.56; median PFS, 8.77 vs 5.49 months). Although OS has yet to be reported, these data support giredestrant plus everolimus as a potentially new, all‐oral option in the post‐CDK4/6i setting. 

## 
CDK2 and CDK4 inhibitors
CDK2 activation is an escape pathway associated with resistance to CDK4/6i. Novel CDK2is, including PF‐07104091 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT04553133) and BLU‐222 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05252416), are under clinical investigation both as monotherapy and in combination with endocrine therapy. Atirmociclib (PF‐07220060) is a highly selective CDK4i associated with less neutropenia than current CDK4/6is and is being studied in phase 3 trials. Early results have demonstrated promising antitumor activity with manageable toxicity.

## Fibroblast growth factor receptor inhibitors
Deregulated FGF and FGFR alterations, particularly FGFR1 amplification (approximately 10% of HR‐positive/HER2‐negative tumors), drive resistance to endocrine therapy and CDK4/6is. In the phase 2 FOENIX‐MBC2 study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT04024436), futibatinib (an irreversible FGFR1–FGFR4 inhibitor) plus fulvestrant produced a median PFS of 7.2 months and an ORR of 18% in patients with high‐level FGFR1 amplification post‐CDK4/6i treatment. Adverse events included hyperphosphatemia, alopecia, constipation, and dry mouth. Although activity was encouraging, patient selection remains a challenge.

## 
HER2 mutations

HER2 mutations are associated with resistance to endocrine therapies but are actionable when combined with irreversible HER2 tyrosine kinase inhibitors like neratinib. The SUMMIT trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT01953926) demonstrated the clinical benefit of neratinib (particularly when combined with fulvestrant and HER2‐antibody therapy) in patients with HER2‐mutated/HR‐positive MBC, although this treatment is not yet approved.

## PI3K pathway inhibitors
Gedatolisib, a dual PI3Ki/mTORi, has demonstrated robust clinical activity in combination with palbociclib and endocrine therapy. In a phase 1b study, the ORR was 85%, with a median PFS of 48.4 months in treatment‐naive patients. Among patients pretreated with endocrine therapy but not a CDK4/6i, the ORR was 61.5%, with a median PFS of 12.9 months, but prior CDK4/6i exposure reduced this benefit (ORR, 25.0%–55.6%; PFS, 5.1–12.9 months). Clinical activity was observed irrespective of PIK3CA mutation status. The most common grade 3 and 4 adverse events were neutropenia (63%), stomatitis (27%), and rash (20%); hyperglycemia was less frequent (6%). In the three‐arm phase 3 clinical trial VIKTORIA‐1 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05501886), the gedatolisib‐based triplet regimen (gedatolisib + fulvestrant + palbociclib) achieved a median PFS of 9.3 months versus 2.0 months with fulvestrant alone (hazard ratio, 0.24; 95% CI, 0.17–0.35); the gedatolisib plus fulvestrant doublet demonstrated a median PFS of 7.4 months versus the fulvestrant‐alone arm (hazard ratio, 0.33; 95% CI, 0.24–0.48). Treatment benefit was consistent regardless of which prior CDK4/6i was used. Both regimens containing gedatolisib were well tolerated, and most adverse events were grade 1 or 2. There were notably low rates of hyperglycemia (9%–11%) and diarrhea (12%–17%), which are atypical for PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway–targeting agents. Although OS was not reported, the encouraging findings validate the dual targeting of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in patients with wild‐type PIK3CA disease and support gedatolisib plus fulvestrant, with or without palbociclib, as a potential new standard of care in the post‐CDK4/6i setting. One caveat is that this trial did not formally compare dual mTOR/PI3K blockade versus singly blocking either PI3K or mTOR in the background of endocrine therapy. The ongoing VIKTORIA‐2 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT06757634) is comparing first‐line fulvestrant plus a CDKi with or without gedatolisib, and the results are eagerly anticipated. Mutant‐selective agents, including RLY‐2608, OKI‐219, TOS‐358, and STX 478, are also being explored as potentially more effective and less toxic PI3Kis, along with ALTA2618, which is an AKT1 E17K mutant‐specific inhibitor.

## Immunotherapy
Additional evidence suggests that immunotherapy‐containing regimens hold promise for a subset of HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBCs, but features of immunoreactivity, such as immune cell infiltration, PD‐L1 expression, and high‐risk gene expression profiles, have not yet been established as predictors of benefit. , , 

## Antibody−drug conjugates
Newer ADCs used with other chemotherapy payloads, such as antimicrotubule agents and even targeted therapies as payloads, are undergoing testing. The phase 2 ICARUS‐BREAST01 study (NCT04965766) demonstrated that patritumab deruxtecan (HER3‐DXd) has promising efficacy and manageable safety in patients who have HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC and previously received CDK4/6i treatment, with exploratory analyses suggesting that HER3 expression, spatial distribution, and ESR1 mutation status may influence response and resistance.

## Management of HR‐positive/HER2‐negative brain metastases
Brain metastases in patients with HR‐positive/HER2‐negative breast cancer are a challenging clinical scenario, with an incidence ranging from 5% to 15% over several years, a lower rate than that observed in HER2‐positive or triple‐negative subtypes. Brain metastases from HR‐positive/HER2‐negative breast cancer traditionally have been managed with local therapies such as surgical resection and irradiation (including stereotactic or whole‐brain radiation therapy) and continuation of appropriate systemic therapy. However, clinical trials are exploring the use of systemic therapies that have demonstrated intracranial activity before local interventions, a trend that is partially driven by the more favorable side‐effect profiles of systemic agents. Notably, ADCs like T‐DXd have shown meaningful intracranial activity. In one trial, patients who had HER2‐low breast cancer with leptomeningeal disease treated with T‐DXd achieved a median OS of 13.3 months and PFS of 8.9 months. It is likely that these results were facilitated by blood–brain barrier disruption in macroscopic metastases and by a bystander payload effect. , Among systemic therapies, abemaciclib is the only CDK4/6i with prospective intracranial clinical data in HR‐positive disease. In addition, elacestrant has been shown to achieve meaningful cerebrospinal fluid concentration, suggesting blood–brain barrier penetration. 

## Patient‐reported outcomes
Incorporating the consideration of side effects, frequency of clinic visits and blood work, and physical and emotional well‐being through the use of PROs or symptom inventories ensures that therapeutic strategies align not only with tumor biology but also with individual patient values, treatment goals, and tolerability thresholds. PROs are now routinely incorporated into randomized clinical trials to assess the impact of treatments on symptoms, emotional well‐being, and daily functioning. In one study, Basch et al. evaluated whether routine symptom monitoring using PROs during cancer treatment could affect OS. They observed that patients in the PRO intervention arm experienced a significant survival benefit, with a median OS duration of 31.2 months compared with 26.0 months in the usual‐care group. This corresponded to a hazard ratio of 0.83 (p = .04), signifying a 17% reduction in the risk of death and suggesting that the PRO assessment process, PROs themselves, and interventions resulting from patient reports can improve clinical outcomes.
Although PROs are being integrated across the continuum of care, they may be even more important when patients undergo later lines of therapy, when benefits and harms must be more closely balanced, than in earlier lines of therapy. Patients with MBC often benefit from a holistic palliative approach focusing on physical, emotional, and, frequently, spiritual needs alongside conventional treatment in the clinical trial setting. Incorporating mindfulness, self‐care, and open communication with the management provided by the health care team improves quality of life and well‐being.
Importantly, key trials and correlative studies have informed decision making and have emphasized the personalization of therapy for MBC based on tumor biology, biomarker status, and patient‐specific factors. Patients are encouraged to take advantage of the many comprehensive resources provided by advocates to help them expand their knowledge about the disease and become empowered to make their own treatment decisions. Patients with newly diagnosed breast cancer can connect with patient advocates and organizations both locally and nationally, as detailed in Table  .
 The patient's experience and point of view are increasingly being incorporated into and affecting clinical care, particularly for MBC. Patients strive for a successful partnership with their breast oncology care teams. Patients with breast cancer have their own personal risk‐management priorities and want to be involved in open conversations with health care providers and other patients. Many patients with MBC encounter barriers in their treatment journey, having limited information and understanding of breast cancer biology and treatment, which hinders their ability to make informed decisions. The aphorism “knowledge is power,” attributed to Sir Francis Bacon, represents knowledge as the cornerstone of progress and a guide for the future. One source of empowering knowledge is a breast cancer navigator who can facilitate a patient’s self‐education process and work with the oncology team to ensure the patient is well informed about their cancer, treatment options and potential outcomes and is equipped with a plan for future therapeutic options if the need arises. Navigators can also guide patients in adopting healthy lifestyle choices, such as a balanced diet, physical activity, and stress management, which can help lower the risk of recurrence and improve survival. , 

## SUMMARY RECOMMENDATIONS REGARDING SURVEILLANCE AND WORKUP FOR HR‐POSITIVE/HER2‐NEGATIVE MBC
Figure  illustrates the recommended clinical pathway for newly recurrent or de novo HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC.
The following bullet points synthesize the key recommendations for newly recurrent or de novo, HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC, integrating standard‐of‐care options (endocrine therapy with or without targeted agents) and highlighting the importance of biomarker‐driven decisions, regular surveillance, and multidisciplinary management.

## Workup for newly diagnosed MBC

For patients with a suspicion of any metastatic involvement, obtain comprehensive staging scans (e.g., CT, bone scan, or PET/CT, as appropriate).
Perform biopsy analysis of a suspected metastatic lesion (preferably the most safely accessible lesion; nonbone site preferred) to confirm diagnosis and re‐evaluate receptor status (ER, PR, HER2).
For confirmed MBC, consider genomic/molecular tests (e.g., next‐generation DNA/RNA sequencing) based on clinical appropriateness and actionability. Testing that will not affect the selection of first‐line therapy can be deferred until progression.

## First‐line therapy (HR‐positive/HER2‐negative)

Consider tumor biology and burden.
➢High visceral disease burden may influence decisions toward more aggressive or combination approaches.
We recommend endocrine therapy plus a CDK4/6i. This is the preferred first‐line therapy approach for HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC according to American Society of Clinical Oncology/NCCN guidelines, including patients with a high visceral burden of disease.
Fulvestrant, palbociclib, and inavolisib are all options for endocrine‐resistant cancers harboring PIK3CA mutations.

## Subsequent lines of therapy

Check for biomarkers (e.g., ESR1, PIK3CA, and BRCA status if not previously done). Molecular profiling may identify other, less common actionable targets, including tumor‐agnostic targets (e.g., NTRK or RET fusions, high TMB, microsatellite instability) for targeted therapies or immunotherapies.
For patients with germline BRCA1/2 mutations, recommend olaparib or talazoparib.
For patients with PIK3CA mutations, recommend fulvestrant in combination with capivasertib or alpelisib.
For patients with PTEN loss/AKT1 mutations, recommend fulvestrant in combination with capivasertib.
For patients with ESR1 mutations, recommend discontinuation of AI and initiation of elacestrant or imlunestrant.

## Endocrine insensitivity or high tumor burden

For tumors with HER2 expression (1+), suggest T‐DXd, particularly if there is brain or leptomeningeal involvement
Transition to sacituzumab govitecan or single‐agent chemotherapy if there is no targetable mutation or target.

## Monitoring response and follow‐up

Imaging frequency
➢Typically conducted every 3–4 months to assess treatment response.
➢The same imaging modality should be used for consistency (e.g., CT or PET/CT).
Clinical assessments
➢Evaluate performance status, organ function, and symptom burden regularly.
➢Modify treatment or supportive care strategies as needed.
Long‐term follow‐up
➢Continue periodic scans and clinical evaluations; intervals can be extended if disease remains stable.
➢Manage comorbidities and treatment‐related side effects proactively.

## CONCLUSIONS
The management of HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC has evolved significantly, reflecting a paradigm shift from traditional chemotherapy to more targeted endocrine therapies and the use of innovative biologic agents. Patient‐centered perspectives on HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC are increasingly shaping treatment decisions, with quality of life often taking precedence over modest gains in survival. Patients living with MBC emphasize the importance of maintaining functional independence, minimizing treatment‐related side effects, and preserving day‐to‐day quality of life. In real‐world practice, some patients discontinue therapy early because of side effects, underscoring the gap between efficacy in clinical trials and usability in routine care.
Although substantial progress has been made, the future of therapy for HR‐positive/HER2‐negative MBC lies in a more personalized approach. Biomarker identification, genomic profiling, and the use of emerging combination strategies, such as pairing endocrine therapy with immunotherapy or other novel treatments, will likely be crucial to overcoming treatment resistance and optimizing therapeutic strategies.
In addition, integrating machine learning and artificial intelligence to analyze complex multi‐omic data promises to further personalize and optimize treatment decisions. Future studies will undoubtedly enhance our understanding of this complex disease and improve patient outcomes.
Adapting therapy to address evolving mechanisms of resistance, as shown in the PADA‐1 and SERENA‐6 trials, represents an innovative improvement over the standard of empirical sequences of therapies. We anticipate a new generation of trials using multi‐omic technologies and sophisticated bioinformatic tools to more effectively guide individualized treatment. We can easily envision patients asking that first‐line and second‐line MBC therapies be moved earlier in their treatment plans based on genomic evolution. Both scientists and patients have great hope and strong optimism regarding the future of personalized treatment for MBC.
