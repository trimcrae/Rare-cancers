## Abstract
There has been significant progress in understanding the molecular landscape of pediatric differentiated thyroid carcinoma (DTC) over the past 2 decades. Classification of pediatric DFC into 3 tiers, RAS-like mutant, BRAF mutant, and kinase fusions, accurately reflects an increasing risk for invasive behavior, including regional and distant metastasis. In clinical practice, somatic oncogene testing for nodules with indeterminate cytology per the Bethesda System for Reporting Thyroid Cytopathology provides objective data to optimize surgical planning. In addition, knowledge of the somatic oncogene for widely invasive carcinomas allows for incorporation of oncogene-specific inhibitory therapy both in the adjuvant and neoadjuvant setting. In the present review, we review the risk factors, clinical presentation, and evaluation of pediatric DTC, highlighting the correlation among ultrasound features, cytology, and oncogenic driver of the tumor. We subsequently propose an integrated, multimodal approach that can be used to improve diagnostic accuracy and reliability for preoperative planning as well as identify and discuss which pediatric patients may benefit from systemic oral targeted therapy.
Keywords: pediatric differentiated thyroid carcinoma, molecular therapy, malignancy risk assessment

## Pediatric Thyroid Carcinoma: An Overview

## Introduction
Thyroid carcinoma is the most common endocrine tumor in children and adults. Over the past 2 decades, significant progress in understanding the molecular landscape of thyroid neoplasms has been achieved. This review summarizes key risk factors associated with pediatric thyroid carcinoma and common oncogenic drivers. By understanding these factors, we can develop an integrated, multimodal approach to the evaluation of pediatric thyroid nodules for improved preoperative diagnostic accuracy and reliability. Increasingly, there are also opportunities to incorporate oncogene-specific inhibitory therapy to maximize the benefit from surgical and/or radioactive iodine (RAI) interventions.

## Prevalence
Thyroid carcinoma is the eighth most common malignant tumor in children and the second most common carcinoma in adolescent girls in the United States ( , ). The incidence of pediatric thyroid carcinoma is 1.1 per 100 000 in the United States ( ). Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) is the most common (global: 86%; United States 83%) thyroid carcinoma in children, followed by follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC) (global: 9%; United States 10%), and medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) (global 4%; United State 5%). Oncocytic carcinoma of the thyroid (OCA) and poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma (PDTC) account for <1% (global and United States) of cases ( , ) ( ).
 Mirroring the epidemiological landscape of thyroid carcinoma in adults, there has been a substantial increase in the incidence of pediatric thyroid carcinoma worldwide since the 1990s. However, 30-year disease-specific survival remains >98% for children and adolescents ( ). The detection of subclinical thyroid carcinoma contributes to the increased incidence ( ), highlighting the need to (1) limit active surveillance to patients with known predisposition and (2) develop a multimodal system that accurately differentiates nodules for surveillance from nodules that should undergo further evaluation and treatment ( ).

## Thyroid Carcinoma Types
Approximately 95% of thyroid carcinomas are derived from follicular cells, specifically thyrocytes. The remaining 5% of thyroid carcinomas are derived from the parafollicular C-cells, which secrete the hormone calcitonin. These types of thyroid carcinoma are known as MTC ( ). Follicular cell derived thyroid carcinoma (also termed nonmedullary thyroid carcinoma) is further categorized into differentiated thyroid carcinoma (DTC), including PTC, FTC, and OCA, and the dedifferentiated types, PDTC, and anaplastic thyroid carcinoma ( ).

## Papillary thyroid carcinoma
There are approximately 13 subtypes of PTC featuring differing oncogenic drivers and clinical behavior ( ). Within pediatric PTC, 50% are classic (C-PTC), 5% to 25% are the infiltrative follicular variant, 10% are diffuse sclerosing PTC (DS-PTC), 1% to 15% are tall cell (TC-PTC), and 1% to 2% are solid/trabecular subtype (ST-PTC) ( ). Locally, PTCs metastasize via the lymphatic system and distally; PTCs metastasize hematogenously, most commonly to the lungs. The risk of regional lymph node metastasis in encapsulated classical PTC is similar to unencapsulated PTC ( ). Compared with adults, PTCs in children are more likely to have larger tumor size ( ) and extrathyroidal disease (17-24% in children; 9-16% in adults) ( , ), regional lymph node metastasis (40-75% in children; 40% in adults) ( , , , ), and distant metastasis (5-20% in children; 2% in adults) ( , , ).

## Follicular thyroid carcinoma
FTC comprises 5% to 10% of pediatric DTC. It is typically a single tumor enclosed within a capsule and is often larger than 2 cm. The risk of regional lymph node metastasis in FTC, diagnosed by employing strict pathologic criteria, is rare ( ). The diagnosis of FTC is based on the histologic identification of capsular and/or vascular invasion; fine needle aspiration (FNA) alone is insufficient for diagnosis and is commonly diagnosed as either “atypia of undetermined significance” (TBSRTC [The Bethesda System for Reporting Thyroid Cytopathology] category III) or “follicular neoplasm” (TBSRTC category IV) ( ). The World Health Organization classifies FTC into 3 groups. The most common group is minimally invasive FTC, where the invasion is limited to the tumor capsule. The second group is encapsulated angioinvasive FTC, which invades into blood vessels within the tumor capsule. The third group is widely invasive FTC, where invasion extends beyond the capsule ( ). FTC with invasion into 4 or more vessels within the tumor capsule or beyond is associated with an increased risk of distant metastasis to the bone and lung. These cases generally have a less favorable prognosis than PTC with distant metastasis ( , , ).

## Risk Factors

## Age and sex
In pediatric patients, the incidence of thyroid carcinoma increases with age and is most commonly diagnosed between 15 and 19 years of age. In prepubertal patients (<10 years old), there is no difference in incidence based on sex ( ). Conversely, the incidence increases in postpubertal females (aged 15-19 years of age) with a female:male ratio of 3:1 ( ). The increased incidence of thyroid carcinoma among postpubertal females may be due to sex hormones, genetics, and/or the immune system impacting both the risk for thyroid carcinoma and its progression in a sex-specific manner ( , ). Nonetheless, the invasive behavior and disease-specific survival of thyroid carcinoma are similar in both sexes across all age groups with some but not all studies showing a higher recurrence in male ( ).

## Ionizing radiation
Exposure to ionizing radiation is an extensively studied risk factor for the development of PTC ( , ). Radiation exposure could originate from different sources, including environmental or secondary to radiation therapy for nonthyroid malignancies ( ). Among survivors of other pediatric cancers, 10% develop thyroid carcinoma as a secondary malignancy ( ). Additionally, among all children diagnosed with thyroid carcinoma, 5% to 10% have a history of radiotherapy for a nonthyroid primary tumor ( ). Even lower-level radiation exposures of 0.05 to 0.1 Gy are associated with increased risk of thyroid malignancies ( , ). Patient age at the time of radiation exposure plays a significant role in the risk and latency of developing PTC. The younger the age at the time of radiation exposure, the higher the risk and shorter the latency for developing PTC ( ).
At the molecular level, radiation-induced PTCs are enriched for gene fusion events ( , ). This is likely due to radiation induced DNA double stranded breaks resulting in chromosomal recombination ( , ) ( ). These fusion-driven PTCs are further described in “High Risk for Invasive Disease: Kinase Fusions.” The association between radiation exposure and an increased risk for developing FTC is less clear ( ).
Long-term surveillance is recommended for pediatric cancer survivors at risk of PTC. Starting 5 years after treatment, patients are recommended to have neck palpation every 1 to 2 years, or thyroid ultrasound every 3 to 5 years. Patients 10 years or younger at the time of radiation exposure have the greatest risk of developing thyroid carcinoma. Ultrasound surveillance in these individuals can help detect PTC earlier, before regional and distant metastases occur ( ).

## Autoimmune thyroiditis
Autoimmune thyroiditis (AIT) is a common thyroid disorder in children ( ). The reported incidence of PTC among children and adolescents with Hashimoto thyroiditis is 0.6% to 3%. Hashimoto thyroiditis was found in 6.3% to 40% of those with PTC ( ). In Graves disease, the presence of a thyroid nodule is associated with an increased risk of PTC, with mixed data regarding the risk for extrathyroidal extension and metastases ( ). Possible explanations for thyroid carcinoma development in the setting of AIT include (1) persistent chronic inflammation creating a favorable environment for malignant transformation, with infiltrating immune cells promoting abnormal repair of DNA, and (2) persistent elevated thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) or thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulin causing follicular epithelial hyperplasia and promoting tumorigenesis ( ). In pediatric patients, no clear relationship between thyroid carcinoma outcome and AIT has been described ( ).
Thyroid nodules in the setting of AIT is an independent risk factor for thyroid carcinoma ( , ). The current guidelines recommend that ultrasound should be performed in children with AIT when there is a palpable nodule, gland asymmetry, or cervical lymphadenopathy ( ). Ultrasound should also be considered when deciding between surgery and radioiodine ablation for pediatric patients with Graves pursuing definitive treatment ( ).

## Diagnosis

## Presentation
Most children with thyroid carcinoma present with an asymptomatic neck mass: approximately 40% are discovered by the patient or their family, 40% are found by medical providers, and 20% are incidentally detected during imaging ( ). Nonpalpable thyroid nodules identified by imaging have the same risk for DTC as palpable lesions of the same size, although screening imaging can identify smaller tumors ( ). It is recommended for all thyroid nodules with suspicious sonographic features in children be further investigated; the likelihood of DTC in a thyroid nodule is higher in children (19-22%) than in adults (12-14%) ( , ).

## Ultrasound
Ultrasound should be performed on all children with a suspected or confirmed thyroid nodule ( ). Solid composition, hypoechogenicity, irregular margin, and punctate echogenic foci consistent with microcalcifications are the sonographic characteristics associated with an increased risk for malignancy ( ) ( ). For patients with a suspicious thyroid nodule, ultrasound of the central and lateral neck for lymphadenopathy should be performed to guide locations to be sampled by FNA to optimize surgical management ( , ). Ultrasound features of metastatic lymph nodes include round shape with increased echogenicity, cystic areas, punctate echogenic foci, and peripheral blood flow on Doppler imaging ( ) ( ).
 The diagnostic performance of the adult-based American College of Radiology—Thyroid Imaging and Reporting Data System (ACR-TIRADS) ultrasound stratification system in evaluating thyroid nodules in pediatric patients is influenced by institutional practice patterns and operators performing and interpretating ultrasound ( , ).

## Fine needle aspiration
FNA specimens are classified according to the Bethesda System for Reporting Thyroid Cytopathology (TBSRTC) into 1 of 6 diagnostic categories with a range of malignancy risk: I, nondiagnostic (mean: 14%, range: 0-33); II, benign (6%; 0-27); III, atypia of undetermined significance (28%; 11-54); IV, follicular neoplasm (50%; 28-100); V, suspicious for malignancy (81%; 40-100); and VI, malignancy (98%; 86-100) ( ). FNA of suspicious lymph nodes in the central and lateral neck compartment should be performed during assessment of the intrathyroidal lesion to determine the need and the extent of compartment-based lymph node dissection ( , , ).
Interobserver variability may occur in the diagnosis and classification of thyroid FNA specimens per TBSRTC classification, especially for pediatric follicular patterned lesions. This can impact precise assessment of malignant potential and surgical planning ( ), as seen in the differing risks of malignancy in TBSRTC category III to IV nodules in adult vs pediatric patients: 13% to 30% vs 11% to 54% in TBSRTC III, and 23% to 34% vs 28% to 100% in TBSRTC IV, respectively ( ).

## Management of Differentiated Thyroid Carcinoma

## Surgery
The recommendation to pursue surgical intervention and its extent (nonsurgical ablative vs lobectomy vs total thyroidectomy with compartmental lymph node dissection) is based on an individual patient's risk of thyroid carcinoma (including sonographic features, cytology, and oncogene testing), the risk of surgery, and the tolerance of uncertainty in the family. Somatic oncogene testing utilizing a thyroid next-generation sequencing panel should be considered for all nodules with indeterminate cytology: atypia of undetermined significance (TBSRTC III), follicular neoplasm (TBSRTC IV), and suspicious for malignancy (TBSRTC V) ( ).
Thyroid nodules associated with benign to minimally invasive intrathyroidal DTC have smooth margins and no punctate echogenic foci on ultrasound ( ) with TBSRTC category III atypia of undetermined significance or IV follicular adenoma cytology ( , ). Initial thyroid lobectomy is generally recommended. The most common DTC seen on pathology are invasive encapsulated follicular variant PTC (IEFVPTC) and FTC. If pathology reveals >2 capsular vessels with tumor invasion or high-grade features (solid, trabecular, or insular growth pattern, mitotic index ≥3 per 10 high power fields, necrosis, convoluted nuclei), completion thyroidectomy should be pursued ( ).
Nodules associated with a high likelihood of PTC with bilateral disease as well as extrathyroidal extension have irregular, infiltrative margins and punctate echogenic foci ( ) on ultrasound with TBSRTC category V suspicious for malignancy or VI malignant cytology. Total thyroidectomy and central compartment lymph node dissection are generally recommended for patients with these features, although some authors have suggested that lobectomy may be considered for lesions ≤ 2 cm without preoperative evidence of regional metastases ( , ).

## Radioactive iodine treatment
The American Thyroid Association (ATA) recommends RAI treatment for pediatric intermediate and high-risk DTC with RAI-avid residual disease not amendable to surgery, and/or distant metastatic disease ( ). RAI is not recommended routinely for children with ATA pediatric low-risk DTC ( ). The American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) tumor–node–metastasis (TNM) nomenclature is used to stratify children into low, intermediate, and high risk for persistent postsurgical disease based on AJCC TNM ( , ).

## Advanced/progressive distant metastatic disease
The most common site for distant PTC metastasis is the lung. Risk factors for pulmonary metastasis include extrathyroidal extension, lateral neck lymph node metastasis, and high postoperative thyrotropin stimulated thyroglobulin ( , ). Despite treatment with RAI, most children with DTC and lung metastasis do not achieve complete remission; incomplete response to treatment. with years of stable residual disease, is common. A small percentage may experience progressive disease ( , , , ). Although >98% of pediatric patients with DTC survive, the disease-specific mortality rate for pediatric patients with pulmonary metastasis may be as high as 10% ( , , , ). In individuals who have RAI-refractory or nonavid disease, or when surgical resection of the primary tumor carries a high risk of morbidity, molecularly targeted therapies should be considered (detailed in “Therapeutic Opportunities in the Management of Pediatric DTC”).

## Genetics of Pediatric Differentiated Thyroid Carcinoma
The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) was instrumental in identifying and characterizing the genetic and molecular changes associated with adult PTC. Adult PTCs were categorized into RAS-like and BRAF-like subtypes utilizing associated gene expression signatures. RAS-like tumors are associated with a lower risk for invasive disease. BRAF-like tumors are associated with increased risk of invasive and metastatic disease ( ). Because this study was composed of primarily adult PTCs (99%) with only 3 patients under 18 years old, this 2-tier molecular classification is not considered sufficient to accurately stratify pediatric PTCs. In addition, the cohort in the TCGA only had 8 patients with distantly metastatic PTC.
Kinase fusions in pediatric thyroid carcinoma are associated with an increased risk for extrathyroidal invasion and distant metastasis when compared with RAS-like or BRAF-mutant fusions. The higher prevalence of kinase fusions in pediatric thyroid carcinoma led to the proposal of a 3-tiered molecular classification for pediatric DTC and its associated risk of invasive behavior: RAS-like mutant (low risk), BRAF-mutant (intermediate risk), or kinase fusion (high risk) ( ) ( ).
 

## Papillary Thyroid Carcinoma

## Lower risk for invasive disease: RAS-like mutations
The RAS-like subgroup of adult PTCs is mainly composed of PTCs harboring NRAS, HRAS, and KRAS mutations (13%), PAX8::PPARG fusions (1%), and non-V600E BRAF mutations (1%). These tumors are enriched for follicular growth pattern on histology, have lower mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) transcriptional output, and are highly differentiated, reflecting their typically indolent, minimally invasive behavior. DTC associated with RAS-like mutations are solid, often >2 cm, with smooth margins and no punctate echogenic foci on ultrasound. RAS-like DTC have a lower-risk for regional lymph node metastasis than BRAF V600E PTC ( ).
In contrast, pediatric follicular patterned PTCs are less likely to feature HRAS, KRAS, or NRAS alterations (0-5%) ( , ). The most common RAS-like mutation in pediatric thyroid tumors are mutations in DICER1 ( ) ( ) ( , ). DICER1 mutations are comparatively enriched in the pediatric population (∼10%) compared with adult PTCs (<1%) ( , , ). Pediatric DTCs harboring DICER1 RNase IIIb hotspot mutations have MAPK signaling output and differentiation levels similar to the RAS-like subgroup of adult PTCs ( ). Similar to adults, these genetic changes are associated with a low risk for invasive disease ( ).
 In contrast to the high specificity for PTC associated with identification of a BRAF V600E or fusion oncogenes, many genetic alterations associated with RAS-like cancers are found across the spectrum of thyroid lesions, including both benign and malignant tumors with low risk for invasive behavior. This includes mutations in RAS, DICER1, PTEN, non-V600E BRAF, and PAX8::PPARG fusions ( , ). Notably, both DICER1 and PAX8::PPARG fusions have been identified in PDTC as well as thyroblastoma (DICER1), including pediatric patients ( , , ) ( ).

## Intermediate risk for invasive disease: BRAF V600E mutation
Mutations in the BRAF proto-oncogene are the most frequent genetic alteration in adult PTCs, accounting for approximately 60% of cases ( ). The valine to glutamate substitution at residue 600 (p.V600E) is the most common BRAF mutation (>95%), which leads to constitutive activation of the BRAF kinase domain and induces MAPK signaling ( ). In adult PTCs, BRAF p.V600E mutations are associated with papillary architecture, readily identifiable nuclear features of PTC, invasive behavior, and RAI refractory disease ( ). In pediatric PTCs, BRAF p.V600E mutation is seen in 20% to 40% of cases and is associated with an increased risk for regional metastases but a low risk for distant metastasis or RAI refractory disease ( , ). Co-occurrence of a TERT or PIK3CA mutation with BRAF p.V600E is rare in pediatric PTC ( ). This may explain maintenance of differentiation (including expression of the sodium iodide symporter) and a lower risk for RAI refractory disease compared to adults ( ) ( ). DTC associated with BRAF-like mutations (and some fusion oncogenes) are typically solid, with irregular, lobulated margins and punctate echogenic foci on ultrasound ( , , ).

## High risk for invasive disease: kinase fusions
Kinase fusions occur with a higher frequency (∼50%) in pediatric DTCs than in adults (15%) ( , , , , ). The prevalence of gene fusion events is enriched in radiation-exposed pediatric patients (60-86%) ( , , ). Kinase fusions are primarily associated with PTCs with increased risk for regional and distant metastasis, including DS-PTC and infiltrative follicular variant papillary thyroid carcinoma ( , , ). Kinase fusions are more common than BRAF mutations in patients <10 years and in pediatric PTC with distant metastasis, including both prepubertal and pubertal patients ( ).
Fusions of the proto-oncogene RET and NTRK are the most common; other kinase fusions may involve ALK, BRAF, and MET ( , , ). Receptor tyrosine kinase fusions can activate both MAPK and phosphoinotide 3-kinase to promote cell proliferation, survival, and migration/metastasis ( , ) ( ). Kinase fusions in thyroid carcinoma are associated with younger age of onset, with fusions identified in the vast majority of tumors in children less than 10 years old ( ). Recent studies in pediatric thyroid carcinomas suggest that tumors harboring fusions have increased MAPK signaling output compared with BRAF-mutant tumors ( , ).
 

## Somatic Alterations in Follicular Thyroid Carcinoma
FTC accounts for approximately 5% to 10% of DTC in the pediatric population ( ). Most of the current knowledge on FTC genetics is derived from studies involving tumors from adult patients. Adult FTCs are driven by mutually exclusive mutations of NRAS, HRAS, KRAS (10-57%), PAX8::PPARG fusions (up to 35-50%), and, less frequently, mutations of DICER1, PTEN, and STK11 ( , , ). Mutations in the TERT promoter, EIF1AX, and PIK3CA are also reported and may be associated with poor clinical outcomes in FTC, particularly when co-occurring with RAS mutations ( ). Thyroid tumors with RAS, DICER1, and PTEN are most frequently associated with a low risk for invasive behavior. However, these thyroid oncogenes can be encountered in a spectrum of thyroid tumors, from benign follicular adenomas to follicular patterned DTC (IEFVPTC and FTC) as well as PDTC ( , ). Ultrasound features of FTC include solid composition, > 2 cm, smooth margins, and no punctate echogenic foci. Cytology is indeterminate and the diagnosis of FTC is made on pathology evaluation, most commonly following lobectomy.
The few studies exploring the genetics of FTC in children and adolescents reveal genetic drivers similar to adults but at different frequency. Pediatric FTC has a higher prevalence of DICER1 hotspot mutations (25-53%) and fewer RAS mutations (0-33%) and PAX8::PPARG fusions (0-11%) ( , ).

## Germline Alterations and Genetic Predisposition
There is a 4-fold increased risk for DTC among first-degree relatives of those with DTC, which increases to 7-fold in those with affected siblings ( ). Familial nonmedullary thyroid carcinoma (FNMTC) is defined as more than 2 first-degree relatives with history of PTC or FTC ( , ), and accounts for 3% to 10% of cases. Genetic anticipation may be seen in FNMTC. The affected second FNMTC generation presents at a younger age with more advanced disease ( ). FNMTC can be further categorized into syndromic (5%) and nonsyndromic (95%) ( ).

## Syndromic familial nonmedullary thyroid carcinoma
Syndromic FNMTC is associated with a germline mutation in a tumor predisposition gene resulting in increased risk of both thyroid and nonthyroidal carcinoma ( , ). The most common cancer predisposition syndromes associated with pediatric DTC are PTEN (phosphatase and tensin homolog) hamartoma tumor syndrome (35%), DICER1 syndrome (16%), and familial adenomatous polyposis (12%) ( , ). While DTCs in syndromic FNMTC are generally associated with a lower risk for invasive disease, DICER1 syndrome may be associated with PDTC ( , ). Diagnostic considerations and surveillance guidelines tailored to the specific cancer predisposition syndrome have been established ( ) with updated versions of the American Association for Cancer Research Childhood Cancer Predisposition guidelines pending publication this year.

## Nonsyndromic familial nonmedullary thyroid carcinoma
In nonsyndromic FNMTC, a unifying susceptibility gene or locus has not been found. However, the transmission of the disorder follows an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance with variable penetrance ( , ) The clinical presentation and outcome of nonsyndromic FNMTC remain controversial. Compared to sporadic DTC, some investigators report nonsyndromic FNMTC demonstrate earlier age of onset, more aggressive features such as higher incidence of multifocality and lymph node metastasis, and poor outcome ( , ).
Ultrasound surveillance of siblings of individuals with nonsyndromic FNMTC is associated with the detection of DTC of smaller size, and lower incidence of nodal metastasis and extrathyroidal extension, suggesting that ultrasound surveillance of at risk individuals in kindreds with nonsyndromic FNMTC may be beneficial ( ). However, ultrasound surveillance of individuals with only 1 affected family member carries a greater likelihood of identifying benign nodules and a low probability of carcinoma ( ). Close surveillance may be most beneficial for patients with 3 or more affected family members ( ).

## Incorporating Molecular Data to Improve Diagnosis
TCGA demonstrated the benefit of using somatic oncogene data to identify the risk for invasive behavior and provide management guidance ( ). Oncogenic alterations may have correlative features on ultrasound and cytology; oncogenes associated with a low risk for invasive behavior are typically encapsulated tumors with indeterminate cytology, and oncogenes associated with an increased risk for invasive behavior may exhibit irregular, infiltrative margins with multiple punctate echogenic foci and malignant cytology ( , , ). Thus, integrating ultrasound, cytology, pathology, and molecular features can increase the accuracy and reliability of the preoperative invasiveness/malignancy risk assessment and inform the surgical plan ( ). Somatic oncogene data provides objective information to substantiate the subjective sonographic and cytologic evaluations.
The following cases highlight the association between the somatic oncogenic driver with the sonographic appearance, cytology, and histopathology. Integrating clinical findings with the 3-tiered oncogene levels provides valuable insight into increasing risk of extrathyroidal, invasive disease. The application of an integrated approach incorporating the 3 modalities available prior to surgery (ultrasound, cytology, and somatic oncogene) can optimize surgical and medical management across the spectrum of disease, from low to high risk for invasive, extrathyroidal metastatic disease ( ).
 

## Oncogenes Associated With Low Risk for Invasive Behavior

## Case 1
A 10-year-old male presented to his pediatrician for a persistent right neck mass. Ultrasound demonstrated a 2.5 cm solid, isoechoic lesion, not taller than wide and with smooth margins, in the right thyroid lobe. There were no peripheral calcifications or punctate echogenic foci in the nodule. Regional (central and lateral neck) adenopathy was not identified. FNA was consistent with a TBSRTC category IV follicular neoplasm. Molecular testing revealed a somatic, pathogenic PTEN mutation. The patient underwent a right thyroid lobectomy. The histopathologic finding was consistent with an encapsulated angioinvasive FTC, but with only 1 pericapsular blood vessel involved the tumor was considered low risk for distant metastasis and completion thyroidectomy was not performed ( ). The patient has been followed for 4 years without evidence of recurrent disease.
 

## Case 2
A 7-year-old previously healthy female was referred to endocrinology for a right neck mass. Thyroid ultrasound demonstrated a 4.3 cm solid lesion, not taller than wide, with mixed echogenicity, and a smooth margin in the right thyroid lobe. There were no punctate echogenic foci or peripheral calcification. No cervical adenopathy was identified. FNA was consistent with TBSRTC category III atypia of undetermined significance. Molecular testing revealed a somatic DICER1 pathogenic hot-spot mutation. The patient underwent right thyroid lobectomy. Histopathology showed IEFVPTC with focal transformation to PDTC. The posterior margin was involved and angioinvasion was present in 3 capsular vessels. In view of high-grade histopathologic features (focal transformation to PDTC) and the presence of angioinvasion, the patient underwent completion thyroidectomy. AJCC TNM staging was T3N0M0 ( ).
 Oncogenes that are associated with a low risk for invasive behavior include RAS, DICER1, PTEN, and PAX8::PPARG rearrangements. On ultrasound, the lesions are typically unifocal, solid, isoechoic/hypoechoic in echogenicity, with smooth margins and without punctate echogenic foci or regional lymphadenopathy. FNA is most commonly indeterminate, either a TBSRTC category III atypia of undetermined significance or IV follicular neoplasm. These tumors are of follicular pattern, which could be benign (lack of capsular or vascular invasion) such as follicular adenoma and noninvasive follicular thyroid neoplasm with papillary-like nuclear features, or malignant such as IEFVPTC and minimally invasive or encapsulated angioinvasive FTC. Histopathologic findings show encapsulation and lack of lymphatic invasion. A small percent of tumors will have capsular angioinvasion or high-grade features. Regional lymph node and distant metastasis are uncommon. Thyroid lobectomy is usually adequate to achieve remission. However, for lesions with high-grade histologic findings (solid, trabecular, or insular growth pattern, mitotic index ≥3 per 10 high-power fields, necrosis, convoluted nuclei) or vascular invasion (>2 vessels) completion thyroidectomy should be considered. Germline mutational analysis should also be performed in individuals with lesions harboring somatic mutations in DICER1 and PTEN based on family history of cancer predisposition as well as physical findings, including macrocephaly in both DICER1 syndrome and PTEN hamartoma tumor syndrome.

## Oncogenes Associated With Intermediate Risk for Invasive Behavior

## Case 3
A 15-year-old previously healthy female was found to have left neck swelling during routine physical examination. Thyroid ultrasound demonstrated a 3.5 cm solid, hypoechoic nodule, wider than tall, with an irregular margin, no extrathyroidal extension, but with multiple punctate echogenic foci. Several enlarged, round hypoechoic cervical lymph node with peripheral blood flow were identified on preoperative ultrasound in left level 6 (central neck, below the thyroid). FNA of the thyroid nodule was compatible with a TBSRTC category VI malignant lesion. The patient proceeded to thyroidectomy with ipsilateral central neck dissection. Molecular testing revealed a BRAF V600E mutation. Pathology demonstrated a 3.4 cm C-PTC without extrathyroidal extension but with lymphatic invasion. Tumor was present in 8/22 central lymph node. AJCC TNM staging was pT2N1aM0. The lesion was of ATA intermediate risk; the patient received RAI, with excellent response ( ).
 
BRAF V600E is associated with intermediate risk for invasive behavior. Sonographic features include solid composition, hypoechoic echogenicity, irregular margin, and/or the presence of extrathyroidal extension, and taller than wide shape. Punctate echogenic foci and/or peripheral calcification are typically present. Central and lateral neck lymphadenopathy are common. However, distant metastases are uncommon in pediatrics. FNA commonly reveals a TBSRTC category V suspicious for malignancy or VI malignant. The BRAF V600E somatic mutation is associated with C-PTC as well as TC-PTC. Invasive histologic findings including multifocality, extrathyroidal extension, lymphatic/ angioinvasion. In the presence of lateral neck lymph node metastasis, total thyroidectomy with therapeutic central and lateral neck dissection should be performed. Lobectomy with prophylactic central neck dissection may be sufficient to achieve remission in individuals without evidence of central or lateral neck lymph node metastasis; completion thyroidectomy should be considered if multifocality or ≥5 regional lymph node metastases are identified on prophylactic central neck lymph node dissection ( ).

## Oncogenes Associated With High Risk for Invasive Behavior

## Case 4
A 12-year-old previously healthy male was referred to oncology clinic for a neck mass. Neck ultrasound showed a 2.3 cm solid, very hypoechoic nodule, taller than wide, with irregular margins and diffuse punctate echogenic foci. Multiple enlarged, round echogenic lymph nodes with punctate echogenic foci and peripheral blood flow on Doppler imaging were present in the bilateral lateral neck (level 2-4). FNA of the thyroid lesion and bilateral level 2 and 4 lymph nodes (lateral neck) were compatible with TBSRTC category VI malignant cytology. There was no evidence of pulmonary metastasis on preoperative chest computed tomography (CT). The patient underwent total thyroidectomy, bilateral central and lateral neck dissection. Histopathologic findings were consistent with high grade DS-PTC involving both thyroid lobes and the isthmus as well as 26 of 98 lymph nodes. Positive extrathyroidal and extranodal extension was present as well as extensive angioinvasion in the tumor. AJCC TNM staging was pT4aN1bM0. The lesion was of ATA high risk. Somatic oncogene molecular testing revealed a NCOA4::RET fusion ( ).
 Kinase fusions involving RET, NTRK, ALK, and BRAF are associated with a high risk for invasive behavior. The sonographic features are similar to BRAF V600E; however, there is increased risk for regional and distant metastasis in children. FNA typically reveals a TBSRTC category V suspicious for malignancy or VI malignant. Total thyroidectomy with therapeutic central and lateral neck dissection is indicated in patients with confirmed lateral neck metastasis on preoperative evaluation. For patients with lateral neck lymph node metastasis (N1b), up to 25% to 30% of patients will have distant metastasis, most commonly to the lungs.

## Therapeutic Opportunities in the Management of Pediatric DTC
Recent advances in molecular profiling and clinical use of next-generation sequencing has led to increasing recognition of targetable oncogenic drivers in pediatric thyroid carcinoma. In parallel, multiple molecularly targeted therapies for the treatment of thyroid carcinoma have been studied, primarily in adult patients, and received United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval—with approval for some of these agents now extending into the pediatric age range ( and ) ( ). With molecular therapy for pediatric thyroid carcinoma in its nascency, questions remain around the timing for initiation of these agents (neoadjuvant, adjuvant), indications (disease not amenable to surgery/radiation, systemic disease, differentiation therapy for increasing RAI efficacy), durability of response and duration of treatment, as well as combination with other agents or modalities (especially RAI and surgery).
 Most pediatric patients with DTC achieve remission without the use of systemic therapy, with surgery with (ATA pediatric intermediate and high risk) or without RAI therapy (ATA pediatric low risk) ( ). Exceptions include pediatric patients with moderate to high burden distant metastasis (most commonly pulmonary) that is progressive or unresponsive to RAI. Prospective, multicenter clinical trials are needed to better define the use of systemic therapy in pediatrics, including patients with RAI refractory disease as well as for patients that present with morbidly invasive regional disease ( ).
 

## Antiangiogenic Multikinase Inhibitors
Antiangiogenic multi–tyrosine kinase inhibitors (multi-TKIs) were the first targeted therapies developed for thyroid carcinomas and are FDA approved for adult patients with DTC and used off label in pediatric patients. Oncogene specific targeted therapies have demonstrated superiority to multi-TKIs in adult patients with RET-mutant MTC, but comparative studies have not been conducted among patients with kinase fusion–driven PTC. In pediatrics, oncogene-specific therapies are often prescribed instead of TKIs given their more favorable side effect profile and pediatric labeling. The exception is the use of multi-TKIs for tumors without a targetable oncogenic driver, including OCA and PDTC. The following is a review of the most commonly prescribed systemic therapy in pediatric DTC.

## Lenvatinib
Lenvatinib is a multi-TKI of RET, PDGFRa, KIT, VEGFR1-3, and FGFR1-4, as well as other kinases associated with pathogenic angiogenesis and cancer progression ( , ). In the adult clinical trial SELECT, including patients with locally recurrent or metastatic RAI refractory DTC, lenvatinib-treated patients had significant improvement in progression-free survival compared with placebo (18.3 months vs 3.6 months, respectively) with an objective response rate of 65% (2% in placebo) ( ). Lenvatinib is taken orally daily, and is FDA approved for adults with RAI-refractory DTC. There is no FDA-approved pediatric dose, although studies support prescribing children with an equivalent body surface area–adjusted adult dose ( ). Case reports show promise of durable response in pediatric patients with RAI refractory DTC and when used as neoadjuvant therapy to optimize clinical status prior to tumor resection ( , , ).

## Cabozantinib
Cabozantinib is a multi-TKI with activity against RET, VEGF, MET, ROS1, TYRO3, MER, KIT, TRKB, FLT-3, TIE-2, and AXL. It is FDA-approved for patients 12 years and older with DTC that is RAI refractory and has progressed following prior VEGFR-targeted therapy. There is body surface area and age-based dosing for pediatric patients ( ). In a phase 1 trial including pediatric patients with MTC, 2 of 5 (40%) of pediatric patients with MTC demonstrated partial response ( ). In the COSMIC-311 trial including patients 12 years and older with advanced/metastatic RAI-refractory DTC that progressed after prior VEGFR targeting inhibitor treatment, patients treated with cabozantinib had improved progression-free survival compared with placebo (11 months vs 1.9 months); however, the endpoint for determining objective response rate was not met ( ).

## Treatment-related adverse event
As a class, on-target off-tumor adverse effects from antiangiogenic multi-TKIs relate to their impact on VEGFR and PDGFR inhibition, and in particular vascular and neurologic manifestations. VEGFR inhibition leads to decreased endothelial synthase activation, increased vascular tone, and hypertension. PDGFR inhibition is believed to decrease pericytes leading to dysregulated microcirculation, increasing risk for coronary artery spasm and heart failure—these are exceptionally rare events in children. Clinically, the vascular-related adverse effects include hypertension, stroke, hemorrhage, gastrointestinal perforation, and fistula formation. Regression of thyroid capillaries may cause primary hypothyroidism ( ). In children, more common effects include palmar–plantar erythrodyesthesia, diarrhea, and proteinuria; cases of physeal widening were reported in patients with open growth plates ( , , , ).
In small case reports/series, pediatric treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) from lenvatinib also included hand–foot syndrome and proteinuria requiring dose modification ( , ). Common TRAEs for cabozantinib were reported to be similar to adult studies; Grade ≥3 TRAEs included hypertension, palmar–plantar erythrodysesthesia, diarrhea, increased liver enzymes, proteinuria, fatigue, and weight loss. Similar to other RET inhibitors, physeal widening was appreciated in 5 of 23 patients with open growth plates, though longitudinal growth continued in all patients ( ).

## Kinase Fusion Targets

## Rearranged during transfection
In PTC, the most common kinase fusions result from chromosomal rearrangements involving RET. Two RET inhibitors are FDA approved for the treatment of pediatric thyroid carcinoma and several clinical trials are recruiting pediatric patients for RET targeted therapy.

## Selpercatinib
Selpercatinib is an ATP-competitive, selective RET inhibitor of the RET receptor tyrosine kinase, with demonstrated efficacy across many RET fusion partners ( ). In addition to RET, there is some off target inhibition of VEGFR1 and VEGFR3 ( ). Selpercatinib is approved for patients ≥2 years of age, with weight-based twice-daily dosing ( ). In a recent clinical trial, pediatric patients with PTC (n = 10) demonstrated an overall response rate of 100% (5/5) ( ).

## Pralsetinib
Pralsetinib is a selective RET kinase inhibitor targeting wild-type RET, kinase-activating RET mutations, and RET fusions. At higher concentrations, pralsetinib also demonstrates in vitro kinase inhibition of DDR1, TRKC, FLT3, JAK1-2, TRKA, VEGFR2, PDGFRb, and FGFR1 ( , ). In adult patients with previously treated RET fusion–positive DTC enrolled on the ARROW trial, the overall response rate was 86% ( ). While there is no published pediatric data on the use of pralsetinib to our knowledge, it is FDA approved for patients 12 years of age and older with RET fusion–positive thyroid carcinoma based on extrapolation from adult data ( ).

## RET inhibitor TRAEs
TRAEs associated with RET inhibitors include diarrhea, nausea, fatigue, rash/skin lesions, and hypertension ( ). In pooled analyses of selpercatinib clinical trials, the most common adverse reactions (≥25%) include edema, diarrhea, fatigue, dry mouth, abdominal pain, rash, and headache ( ). Grade ≥3 TRAEs included hypertension (18-20%), elevation in liver enzymes (8-12%), and hemorrhage (2.3-2.6%) ( , ). Selpercatinib can cause concentration-dependent QT prolongation; QTcF interval >500 ms was measured in 5% to 6% of patients ( , ). Most TRAEs were Grade 1 to 2; dose reduction due to TRAEs occurred in 31% to 41%, and TRAE-related treatment discontinuation occurred in 3% to 8% of cases ( , , ). Selpercatinib's FDA label includes recommendations for monitoring growth plates in adolescent patients with open growth plates, and consideration of therapy interruption or discontinuation based on the severity of any appreciated abnormalities and individual risk/benefit ( ). Similarly, for pralsetinib animal dose toxicology studies identified physeal dysplasia and tooth abnormalities at doses relevant to pediatric and adolescent patients ( ). Selpercatinib also inhibits type 2 deiodinase activity and patients may benefit from addition of liothyronine while on therapy if their serum T3 level decreases with associated increase in TSH ( ).

## RET inhibitor resistance
Resistance appears rare to date in pediatric patients with thyroid carcinoma treated with selective RET inhibitors; no resistance mutations were reported among the 24 pediatric patients enrolled to LIBRETTO-121 with a 24-month duration of response rate of 100% ( ). In contrast, adult studies have shown prolonged exposure to RET inhibitors can lead to an accumulation of additional oncogenic driver mutations in BRAF, KRAS, and NRAS, as well as on-target or target bypass resistance mechanisms resulting in disease progression ( , ). Additional data and longer follow-up of pediatric patients are necessary to understand the prevalence of evolution of resistance in pediatric patients.

## Neurotrophin receptor tyrosine kinase
Tropomyosin receptor kinase (TRK) inhibitors are FDA approved from newborns through adults in a histology-agnostic fashion for cancers with NTRK gene fusions, including PTC ( , , ).

## Larotrectinib
Larotrectinib (first-generation TRK inhibitor) is a highly selective competitive inhibitor of the ATP binding sites of the 3 TRK kinases ( ). Larotrectinib is FDA approved for patients of any age with locally advanced or metastatic NTRK gene fusion–positive solid tumors ( ). Across histologies, larotrectinib has demonstrated a 79% objective response rate, including an 84% response rate in pediatric patients ( , ). In patients with NTRK fusion–positive thyroid carcinoma, a pooled analyses across 3 trials (NCT02576431, NCT02122913, and NCT02637687) reported 19 of 24 (79%) of patients had an objective response to larotrectinib therapy ( ). In the pediatric Phase II study, 2/2 pediatric patients with DTC responded to treatment, including 1 complete response over >30 months of therapy ( , ). Additional pediatric PTC cases with progressive or initially RAI low-avidity or refractory disease have been reported with favorable response, including increased RAI avidity ( , ).

## Entrectinib
Entrectinib (first-generation TRK inhibitor) is a multikinase inhibitor with high potency against TRKA/B/C, ALK, and ROS1 ( ). Entrectinib is approved for patients >1 month of age with advanced or metastatic NTRK gene fusion–positive solid tumors ( ). Across histologies, the objective response rate to entrectinib in NTRK fusion–positive solid tumors was 57%, and 54% in adults with NTRK fusion–positive thyroid carcinoma ( , ). Entrectinib use in pediatric patients with thyroid carcinoma has not been reported to our knowledge.

## Repotrectinib
Repotrectinib is a second-generation TRK inhibitor with additional activity against ALK and ROS1; importantly, it demonstrates both in vitro and in vivo activity against solvent-front mutations ( , ). It is approved for patients 12 years and older with advanced or metastatic NTRK gene fusion positive solid tumors demonstrating a 58% objective response rate in TKI-naïve and 50% objective response rate in TKI-pretreated adult patients. This study included 5 patients with TKI-naïve thyroid carcinoma and 4 with TKI-pretreated cancers, with objective responses observed in all TKI-naïve patients and 2 of 4 TKI-pretreated patients ( ). There currently are no published reports of its usage in pediatric thyroid carcinoma. Trial NCT03093116 is currently recruiting for pediatric patients with NTRK1-3 rearranged solid tumors, including thyroid carcinoma ( ).

## TRK inhibitor adverse events
Potential off-tumor, on-target side effects include pain upon TRK inhibitor discontinuation or with late administration of a scheduled dose, peripheral neuropathy, dizziness/ataxia/orthostatic hypotension without hypovolemia, weight gain, and cytopenias ( , , ). Fractures were also reported in 23% of pediatric patients enrolled in entrectinib trials; however, this finding was compounded by many of the enrolled patients being on prolonged steroid courses for brain tumors ( ). Subsequently, investigators identified a fracture incidence of 7.7% in pediatric patients treated with larotrectinib (n = 9 of 117) with the majority of fractures being trauma or tumor related ( , , ).
TRAE reported in pooled analyses of larotrectinib clinical trials (NCT02576431, NCT02122913, and NCT02637687), were mainly Grade 1 to 2. Among pediatric patients, the most common/notable reported effects included pyrexia (45%), increase in liver transaminases (42-63%), decreased neutrophils (21-60%), vomiting (21-42%), diarrhea (35%), rash (28%), and weight gain (4%) ( , , , ). Grade ≥3 events included nausea, ALT increase, and neutrophil decrease (4% each) ( , , ).
The most common TRAE reported in pooled analyses of pediatric patients with varying oncologic diagnoses in entrectinib clinical trials (ALKA, STARTRK-1, STARTRK-2, and STARTRK-NG) include decreased neutrophils (all grades: 27%, Grade ≥3: 2%), decreased platelets (all grades: 10%, Grade ≥3: 0%), and weight gain (all grades: 20%, Grade ≥3: 7%). Three of 30 pediatric patients discontinued use due to significant adverse reactions (Grade 4 pulmonary edema, Grade 3 dyspnea, and Grade 4 pancreatitis) ( , , ).

## TRK inhibitor resistance
Similar to RET inhibitors, resistance to first-generation TRK inhibitors can develop by the evolution of TRK kinase domain mutations or activating mutations that bypass the TRK kinase; more data is needed on the frequency with which these occur in pediatric thyroid carcinoma. To our knowledge, these have not been reported in children with thyroid carcinoma, although the number of patients reported in the literature is very small. The second-generation TRK inhibitor repotrectinib has been reported to overcome some of the on-target resistance mutations ( , , ).

## Other kinase inhibitors
There are limited data on the efficacy of other molecularly targeted inhibitors for the treatment of PTC harboring ALK or other kinase fusions. While the majority of kinase fusion targeted therapies have demonstrated broad, potentially histology agnostic activity, additional study of these agents is needed to understand their activity and potential use in pediatric thyroid carcinoma ( , ).

## Nonfusion Oncogene Targets

## BRAF

## Dabrafenib with or without trametinib
Dabrafenib is a kinase inhibitor that binds to mutant BRAF monomers, resulting in decreased activation of the MAPK pathway in the setting of BRAF V600E mutations ( ). Combination with the MEK inhibitor trametinib was investigated because durability of BRAF monotherapy response is limited ( ). Further, first-generation BRAF V600E inhibitors result in paradoxical activation of the MAPK pathway in normal cells. Dabrafenib with trametinib is FDA-approved for BRAF V600E positive, progressive unresectable, or metastatic solid tumors, including patients 6 years of age or older ( ). In clinical trials, adults with progressive, unresectable, or metastatic anaplastic thyroid carcinoma treated with dabrafenib and trametinib demonstrated an overall response rate of 56% and median overall survival of 14.5 months; in contrast, prior approaches with systemic therapy for anaplastic thyroid carcinoma yielded response rates around 15% ( , ). One pediatric patient with PTC received dabrafenib/trametinib in a Phase I/IIa open-label study (NCT01677741); while this combination therapy was reported to be well-tolerated, objective response data is not yet available ( ).

## BRAF/MEK inhibitor adverse events
The most common Grade ≥3 TRAE in pediatric patients are fatigue, vomiting/diarrhea, abdominal pain, and rash. Serious adverse reactions in >5% of pediatric patients include pyrexia and decreased cardiac ejection fraction; TRAE-related treatment discontinuation occurred in 21% ( ).

## Other

## RAS, PTEN, DICER1
There are no available data on effective molecularly targeted agents in pediatric patients with thyroid carcinoma primarily driven by RAS, PTEN, or DICER1 mutations. Currently, there are no active pediatric thyroid carcinoma clinical trials targeting these genetic alterations.

## When to Consider Oncogene Targeted Therapy in Pediatric Differentiated Thyroid Carcinoma
In both adult and pediatric case reports, adjuvant systemic molecular therapy has been shown to reduce tumor burden and increase RAI avidity in patients with RAI refractory disease ( , , , , ). The neoadjuvant use of targeted inhibitors may be considered if the patient presents with regional disease that is rapidly progressive and/or not amenable to local control, threatening the airway or major anatomic structures, or distant metastatic disease the demonstrates low RAI avidity on whole body scan (WBS) with confirmation of disease progression on anatomic imaging (CT or magnetic resonance imaging) ( ).
The following case demonstrates the potential utility of incorporating oncogene-specific inhibitory therapy in a patient with PTC associated RAI refractory pulmonary metastasis.

## Case 5
A 7-year-old female with a history of congenital cardiac malformation, now postsurgical repair, presented with neck lymphadenopathy. Ultrasound revealed an infiltrative lesion occupying both lobes with bilateral, lateral neck lymph node metastases. FNA was positive for malignancy (TBSRTC category VI). Total thyroidectomy with central and bilateral lateral dissection as well as sternotomy demonstrated DS-PTC with positive margins, extensive angioinvasion, and lymphatic invasion with >300 positive lymph nodes. A therapeutic dose of RAI was administered with the post-treatment WBS showing uptake in the neck, lungs, and skull (1 lesion). Thyrotropin stimulated thyroglobulin at the time was 3850 ng/mL with TSH 161 mIU/L. Serial chest CT showed mild structural progression of diffuse micronodular metastasis with review of the initial post-RAI WBS showing patchy pulmonary activity. Somatic oncogene testing identified a NCOA4::RET alteration (RET/PTC3). Based on these findings, selpercatinib was initiated, 92 mg/m2/day. Subsequent chest CT showed near-complete structural response after 1 year on molecular therapy with no evidence of bone metastasis on magnetic resonance imaging. Repeat diagnostic RAI was administered, demonstrating diffuse pulmonary activity and 2 lesion in the neck ( ). One year after stopping selpercatinib and the second therapeutic RAI, chest CT revealed durable near-complete structural response in the lungs with no identified pulmonary or neck metastasis ( ).
 

## Current State of the Art
Recent discoveries in the genetics of pediatric thyroid carcinoma have facilitated advances in the diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment of the disease. The enrichment of fusions in pediatric PTCs associated with an increased risk for distant metastasis favors a 3-tiered classification regarding metastatic potential: RAS-like mutation vs BRAF-mutant vs kinase fusions rather than the 2-tiered classification used for thyroid cancer per TCGA for adult PTCs. The detection of genetic alterations associated with a low risk for invasive disease (RAS, DICER1, PTEN, non-V600E BRAF, and PAX8::PPARG) in nodules with indeterminate cytology has proven to be informative in limiting the surgical extent and lymph node dissection. Patients with genetic drivers associated with an intermediate (BRAF V600E) or high risk for invasive disease (fusions involving RET, NTRK3, and ALK) must undergo a complete preoperative sonographic assessment of cervical lymph nodes to optimize surgical planning ( , ). The subjective nature of ultrasound and cytology interpretation is associated with a wide variability of malignancy risk assessment in pediatrics. In contrast, identification of the somatic oncogenic driver is objective with great potential to improve diagnostic accuracy and reliability of the preoperative assessment.
Utilization of selective inhibitors targeting RET, NTRK3, and ALK could help restore RAI uptake and increase efficacy of RAI therapy. These agents have shown promising results in preclinical studies and case reports by inducing redifferentiation, and RAI uptake in tumors harboring RET, NTRK3, and ALK fusions ( , , , , ). These drugs could also be employed in neoadjuvant therapy, aiming to induce tumor regression prior to resection of widely invasive tumors ( ).
Despite all the recent advances in the genomics of pediatric thyroid carcinoma, much remains to be explored. Most mutational studies to date have used targeted panels. Moreover, genome-wide analysis of miRNA expression and DNA methylation are largely underexplored in the pediatric population. Given the rarity of these tumors, it is particularly challenging to define associations between molecular alterations and clinicopathological features. Continuous efforts are on the way to build and expand the Child and Adolescent Thyroid Consortium, a multi-institutional collaborative research program dedicated to studying pediatric thyroid carcinoma. One of the ongoing aims is the execution of a multi-omics project to define the genomic and transcriptomic landscape across several pediatric tumors. Our efforts aim to fill the gaps in the current molecular knowledge, providing important information towards improving patient care.
