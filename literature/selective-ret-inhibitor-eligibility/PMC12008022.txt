## ABSTRACT
Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) can treat various cancers, primarily through their antiangiogenic effects. However, as angiogenesis is crucial for successful wound healing, TKIs may adversely impact wound repair. This review analysed all 63 FDA‐approved TKIs and identified evidence for wound healing and repair implications in 24 agents. The primary mechanism contributing to impaired wound healing appears to be the inhibition of vascular endothelial growth factor receptors, with secondary targets, such as epidermal growth factor receptors and platelet‐derived growth factor receptors, potentially playing a role. Information from safety package inserts, preclinical studies, case reports and clinical trials suggests that these TKIs can cause delayed or impaired wound healing. The safety information generally recommends discontinuing treatment for at least one to 2 weeks before elective surgery and resuming treatment only after adequate wound healing has occurred. Neoadjuvant therapy with TKIs may be feasible if sufficient time is allowed between the cessation of the TKI and the onset of surgery. As the use of TKIs continues to increase, healthcare professionals should be aware of their potential impact on wound healing and take appropriate precautions to minimise the risk of wound‐related complications.

## Summary

Successful wound healing depends on tightly regulated angiogenesis, which can be disrupted by TKIs used in cancer therapy.
This review examined the evidence for the impact of 24 FDA‐approved TKIs on wound healing and repair, focusing on their mechanisms of action, indications, and reported adverse effects on wound healing processes.
Results indicate that TKIs, particularly those targeting VEGF signalling pathways, with secondary targets such as epidermal growth factor receptor and platelet‐derived growth factor receptor, can significantly impair wound healing.
The review revealed a spectrum of wound healing complications associated with different TKIs, with specific agents demonstrating more significant impacts than others.
Current recommendations for the timing of TKI discontinuation before surgery or the facilitation of wound healing largely suggest a cessation period of 3–5 half‐lives of the respective medication.

## Introduction
Protein kinase pathways have emerged this century as a critical focus in disease research, with their dysregulation implicated in numerous pathological conditions [ , , ]. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved many small‐molecule protein kinase inhibitors (PKIs). At the end of 2023, there were 80 FDA‐approved therapeutic small molecule PKIs that target protein‐serine/threonine kinases (STKs), mitogen‐activated protein kinase kinase 1/2 (MEK1/2), and tyrosine kinases [ ]. Of these 80 PKI agents, 63 target protein‐tyrosine kinases.
The protein‐tyrosine kinases are subdivided into cytoplasmic non‐receptor tyrosine kinases and transmembrane receptor tyrosine kinases [ ]. The non‐receptor protein‐tyrosine kinases are subdivided into the following kinase families: Abelson tyrosine kinase (ABL), activated Cdc42 kinase (ACK), c‐Src tyrosine kinase (SRC), C‐terminal SRC kinase (CSK), focal adhesion kinase (FAK), feline sarcoma (FES), FYN‐related kinase (FRK), Janus Kinase (JAK), tyrosine kinase expressed in hepatocellular carcinoma (TEC) and spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK) [ , , , ]. The receptor protein‐tyrosine kinases are subdivided into the following: anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), colony‐stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF1R), cholecystokinin receptor (CCKR), discoidin domain‐containing receptor (DDR), epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), erythropoietin‐producing hepatocellular carcinoma receptor (EPHR), fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR), Feline McDonough Sarcoma (c‐FMS)‐like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3), KIT protein (KIT), mesenchymal‐epithelial transition factor (MET), platelet‐derived growth factor (PDGFR), vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR), rearrangement during transfection (RET), ROS, tyrosine kinase receptor with immunoglobulin and EGF homology (TIE), Tyro3/Axl/Mer (TAM) receptors and tropomyosin receptor kinase (TRK) [ , , , , ].
Receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) play a crucial role in regulating cellular processes and have been linked to the development of various diseases, particularly cancers. These signalling proteins play a role in regulating cancer cell growth and metastasis [ ]. The 20 subfamilies of known human RTKs all share a similar molecular structure [ ]. Judah Folkman's hypothesis that tumour growth depends on angiogenesis and antiangiogenesis could help prevent such growth [ ], has spurred extensive research into the role of angiogenesis in tumour formation [ , , , ]. Multiple kinases are involved in angiogenesis, including RTKs such as VEGFRs [ ].
Small molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), including non‐receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (NRTKIs) and receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (RTKIs), represent a significant advance in treating many cancers, primarily through their antiangiogenic effects. Angiogenic tyrosine kinase inhibition has emerged as a systemic therapy for cancers such as hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), colorectal carcinoma and renal cell carcinoma (RCC) [ ]. However, angiogenesis is also a fundamental component of successful wound healing, and disruptions in these pathways could compromise wound repair [ ]. Although several approved TKIs have been reported to impair wound repair, no prospective randomised clinical studies have been specifically designed to evaluate wound healing as a primary endpoint with these TKIs [ ].
Despite this, the use of these TKIs will be on the rise as they provide extensive advantages over chemotherapeutic agents [ ]. Moreover, physicians managing acute and chronic wounds will increasingly encounter patients undergoing treatment with these drugs. They will need to be aware of potential issues these drugs may have on wound healing and repair. In this review, we highlighted the evidence for the impact of the following 24 RTKIs (axitinib, cabozantinib, erlotinib, fruquintinib, infigratinib, lenvatinib, nintedanib, pazopanib, pemigatinib, pralsetinib, regorafenib, ripretinib, selpercatinib, sorafenib, sunitinib, tivozanib, vandetanib) and NRTKIs (bosutinib, dasatinib, ibrutinib, imatinib, nilotinib, ponatinib, zanubrutinib) on wound healing and repair. These TKIs were the only ones with evidence of impaired wound healing and repair. Moreover, we reviewed the half‐lives, mechanisms of action, indications, impact on wound healing and cessation protocols of these TKIs (Table  ).

## Materials and Methods
A comprehensive literature review was conducted to identify and evaluate the available evidence on the impact of all 63 FDA‐approved TKIs (as of January 1st, 2024) on wound healing and repair [ ]. The safety prescribing information for each TKI was screened for wound healing issues. Moreover, the following electronic databases were searched: PubMed, Google Scholar and Web of Science. The search terms used included combinations of ‘tyrosine kinase inhibitor’, ‘wound healing,’ ‘wound repair’, ‘surgical complications’ and the names of specific TKIs (e.g., ‘axitinib’). The search was limited to articles published in English. The inclusion criteria for the review were: (1) studies investigating the impact of TKIs on wound healing or repair in vitro, animal, or human studies and (2) case reports, case series, observational studies, clinical trials or review articles. The exclusion criteria were: (1) studies not related to TKIs and wound healing or repair, (2) studies published in languages other than English and (3) duplicate publications. The titles and abstracts of the retrieved articles were screened for relevance. Full‐text articles of potentially eligible studies were then reviewed to determine their inclusion in the review. The reference lists of the included articles were also manually searched to identify any additional relevant studies. Data extracted from the included studies and safety information comprised the TKI investigated, its mechanism of action, FDA‐approved indications, evidence of impact on wound healing or repair, and the drug's half‐life.
Evidence for wound healing complications, or lack thereof, was categorised into three levels. Moderate clinical evidence was defined by documentation in clinical studies and/or well‐documented case series or reports demonstrating consistent wound healing complications. Limited clinical evidence was characterised by isolated case reports or studies with inconsistent clinical findings where wound healing complications were noted but patterns were not clearly established. ‘Theoretical risk’ was defined as having no clinical evidence of wound healing complications, with risk assessment based solely on pre‐clinical evidence, prescribing information warnings or mechanism‐based theoretical concerns.

## Review
3.1

## Receptor Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors
3.1.1

## Moderate Clinical Evidence
3.1.1.1

## Cabozantinib
Cabozantinib is an RTKI that strongly inhibits VEGFR2, MET, AXL, RET, FLT3, KIT and TIE2, with secondary targets including VEGFR1, VEGFR3, MERTK, TYRO3, FMS and tropomyosin receptor kinase B [ , ]. It has been approved for treating advanced metastatic medullary thyroid cancer (MTC) and RCC [ , ]. Additionally, cabozantinib has shown efficacy against various other cancers, such as breast, prostate, liver, lung and bladder [ , , , , , , , , , , ]. Cabozantinib has a half‐life of 55 h and an extended terminal plasma half‐life of 99–120 h and demonstrates an approximate five‐fold accumulation by the 15th day of daily administration [ , , ].
The safety information for cabozantinib (Cabometyx) states it may cause issues with wound healing, advising patients on cabozantinib to inform their healthcare provider of any wound healing problems. It is recommended that cabozantinib be discontinued at least 3 weeks before any planned surgery, with resumption left to the provider's discretion [ , , , ].
Other sources recommend cabozantinib discontinuation 28 days pre‐surgery due to potential impaired wound healing. Surgery sites should be fully healed before resuming treatment, and patients should report signs of infection (redness, swelling, warmth) [ , ]. The extended 28‐day discontinuation period likely relates to cabozantinib's longer half‐life [ , ]. For neoadjuvant cabozantinib, safe administration requires pre‐surgical discontinuation based on its 5‐day half‐life, balanced against preventing tumour regrowth [ ].
In vitro studies showed cabozantinib inhibits CE81T cell migration in DMEM/F‐12 medium with 2% FBS [ ]. Beyond VEGF inhibition, cabozantinib's wound healing impairment likely occurs through the MET/hepatocyte growth‐factor signalling pathway targeting, which interfaces with pathways like EGFR [ ].
A case reported dermatitis and ulceration at prior radiation sites during cabozantinib treatment [ ]. In urothelial carcinoma patients, cabozantinib induced cutaneous toxicities, including scrotal erythema/ulceration, nail splinter haemorrhages, hair depigmentation and hand‐foot skin reactions [ ]. In advanced RCC, cabozantinib‐associated adverse events include healing‐related complications: gastrointestinal perforations, fistulas, jaw osteonecrosis and haemorrhaging [ , ]. Gastrointestinal perforation has also been reported with cabozantinib in thyroid cancer treatment [ ].
A phase II single‐site study of recurrent/metastatic salivary gland cancers revealed significant cabozantinib‐associated wound complications. Six of 25 patients (24%) developed grade 3–5 complications at a median of 7.1 months on treatment, with four cases occurring in previously irradiated areas [ ]. Another phase II trial of cabozantinib in advanced soft‐tissue sarcomas reported one wound dehiscence (1.8%) at a previous skin graft site attributed to the drug [ ]. In a phase III clear‐cell RCC study, wound complications occurred in 2.4% of cabozantinib‐treated patients [ ].

3.1.1.2

## Pazopanib
Pazopanib, an RTKI targeting VEGFR1/2/3, PDGFR, FGFR1/3, KIT, ITK, LCK and c‐FMS, is approved for RCC and soft‐tissue sarcomas [ , , , ]. The drug shows clinical activity against breast, lung, cervical, and thyroid cancers, HCC, prostate, colorectal, multiple myeloma, melanoma and glioblastoma [ , , , , , , ]. The mean half‐life of pazopanib is approximately 31 h (20.3–52.3 h) [ , ].
The safety prescribing information of pazopanib (VOTRIENT) indicates that VEGF pathway inhibition can impair wound healing [ , , , , , , , , , , ]. It recommends stopping pazopanib 1 week before elective surgery and waiting ≥ 2 weeks post‐major surgery or until adequate wound healing before resuming. The safety of pazopanib reinitiation following wound healing complications remains unestablished [ , , ].
In glaucoma filtration surgery, topical pazopanib prevented fibroblast migration and reduced scarring, likely through PDGF inhibition [ , ]. A patient with metastatic RCC taking pazopanib and later axitinib showed impaired healing of a gluteal abscess [ ]. Another RCC patient with lung metastases experienced poor post‐Mohs wound healing while on pazopanib. The defect improved after drug discontinuation but expanded with necrotic debris upon pazopanib resumption due to enlarging lung nodules [ ]. In a phase II study of neoadjuvant pazopanib (800 mg daily for 12–14 weeks) before cytoreductive nephrectomy in advanced RCC patients, surgical outcomes showed low morbidity, minimal complications, and 2% surgical‐related deaths. Surgery occurred ≥ 48 h after the last pazopanib dose, with treatment paused for 14 days post‐surgery before 6 weeks of resumption [ ].

3.1.1.3

## Sorafenib
Sorafenib, a multikinase RTKI, targets VEGFR2/3, PDGFR, RAF kinases (CRAF, BRAF), MEK, extracellular signal‐regulated kinase (ERK), KIT, FLT3 and RET. FDA approval includes unresectable HCC, advanced RCC, and differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) [ , , , , ]. Clinical trials for melanoma, ovarian, breast, non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and pancreatic cancers showed no statistically significant benefit or harm at prespecified thresholds [ ]. The mean elimination half‐life of sorafenib is approximately 25–48 h [ ].
Sorafenib's (NEXAVAR) package insert indicates that no formal wound healing studies have been conducted. For major surgeries, temporary discontinuation is advised, with limited clinical data on optimal resumption timing. Restart decisions should be based on clinical assessment of satisfactory wound healing [ ].
Keloids present as solid, rubbery lesions or lustrous, fibrous nodules due to aberrant healing [ ]. During dermal repair, injury‐induced growth factors trigger pathway dysregulation, activating keloid fibroblasts (KFs) and promoting extracellular matrix accumulation [ ]. An in vitro study reported that sorafenib inhibits multiple pathways critical in keloid development, functioning as an antagonist of MAPK/ERK and TGF‐β/Smad signalling in primary KFs. Sorafenib inhibited KF migration, proliferation, invasion and collagen production. Ex vivo studies demonstrated sorafenib's ability to halt KF migration, suppress angiogenesis, and reduce collagen accumulation [ ].
A mouse study evaluated sorafenib's impact on liver regeneration using oral administration (30 mg/kg/day for 14 days vs. vehicle), with treatment stopped 1 day pre‐hepatectomy or started 1 day post‐surgery. Liver regeneration was measured as the percentage of initial liver weight [ ]. Pre‐surgical sorafenib discontinuation did not affect hepatic regeneration, while continuous administration and post‐surgical initiation significantly altered liver regeneration at 120 h compared with controls. Post‐surgical sorafenib groups showed reduced bromodeoxyuridine‐positive nuclei, decreased phospho‐ERK levels, elevated VEGF‐A levels and wound healing complications. The study concluded that pre‐surgical sorafenib cessation preserves normal hepatic regeneration, while post‐hepatectomy administration affects late‐stage regeneration [ ].
A review noted wound healing as a potential sorafenib adverse event [ ]. A chemotherapy manual suggests 10‐day pre‐surgical discontinuation, though without specified rationale [ ].
A case report describes worsening left lower extremity ulceration in a 67‐year‐old patient receiving sorafenib 200 mg BID for 4 months for Child‐Pugh Class B HCC. The wound showed mixed slough and eschar, smooth erythematous tissue, irregular margins, hyperpigmented surrounding skin and moderate purulent exudate [ ]. In another case, a 36‐year‐old man with metastatic RCC on sorafenib 800 mg daily experienced reversible delayed ileostomy healing upon drug discontinuation [ ]. Conversely, in a pre‐liver transplant setting, a patient with recurrent HCC post‐hepatic resection received neoadjuvant sorafenib without experiencing wound healing complications, hepatic decompensation or bleeding [ , , , , , , ].
No significant wound healing complications were mentioned in a neoadjuvant prospective trial evaluating the safety and feasibility of sorafenib 400 mg BID for stage 2/advanced RCC (n = 30). Patients received sorafenib for a median of 33 days (range: 8–59), with a discontinuation median of 3 days pre‐surgery (range: 2–14). Surgeries included laparoscopic (n = 16) and open (n = 14) nephrectomies, plus additional procedures (10 caval thrombectomies, 5 adrenalectomies). One patient experienced conservatively managed superficial wound breakdown on postoperative day 8. Metastatic patients resumed systemic therapy at 4–6 weeks post‐surgery after confirmed secure wound closure [ , ].

3.1.1.4

## Sunitinib
In biochemical and cellular studies, sunitinib, an RTKI, potently inhibits VEGFR1/2, FLT3, KIT, PDGFRα/β, RET and CSF1R [ , , , ]. It is approved for GISTs, pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours and RCC [ ] and shows activity against HCC, SCLC, familial adenomatous polyposis, breast cancer, multiple endocrine neoplasia types 2A and 2B, AML, melanoma and glioma [ , , , , ]. In humans, sunitinib's terminal half‐life is ~51 h (40–60 h) [ , ].
The sunitinib (SUTENT) package insert documents cases of impaired wound healing during therapy. It recommends temporary discontinuation before major surgery. The optimal timing for post‐surgical resumption remains undefined due to limited clinical experience, with reinitiation decisions based on clinical assessment of surgical recovery [ ]. A systematic review, meta‐analysis and additional studies recommend discontinuing sunitinib ≥ 1 week pre‐surgery (approximately four half‐lives) for planned/elective procedures, with resumption after wound healing at least 1 week post‐surgery [ , , ]. For advanced RCC and GIST management, Desai et al. specifically advocate discontinuation ≥ 1 week before surgery and resumption no earlier than one week after surgery [ ].
In mice, sunitinib at 40 mg/kg/day showed wound tensile strength equivalent to controls at all time points. At 80 mg/kg/day, tensile strength decreased by 40% by day 20, normalising by day 34. Dexamethasone treatment reduced tensile strength by 26% by day 34. Histologically, 40 mg/kg/day of sunitinib showed no healing variations, while 80 mg/kg/day caused mild, temporary delays in granulation tissue formation, remodelling and contraction at days 13, 20 and 34. Sunitinib exposure correlated directly with dose [ , , , , , , , , , , , , ].
In a small case series, three patients underwent nephrectomy while on sunitinib, discontinued 3 weeks, 1 week and 2 days pre‐surgery, with no reported wound healing or haemostasis complications [ ]. Conversely, a case report documented severe complications (tissue necrosis, toxic epidermal necrolysis, impaired wound healing) leading to forefoot amputation after 6 days of sunitinib (50 mg daily). The patient developed right foot erythema, swelling and blisters, progressing to necrosis requiring amputation [ ].
A phase II trial evaluating the safety and efficacy of preoperative sunitinib in metastatic RCC patients found that 13% of those with clinical benefit experienced delayed wound healing. Comparing 1‐day versus 14‐day pre‐surgical drug discontinuation showed no significant differences in complications. The median time to restart sunitinib post‐nephrectomy was 21 days (range 14–82 days), including a pretreatment break. Median sunitinib therapy duration was 28 days (range 22–96 days) [ , ].
An prospective open‐label study of presurgical sunitinib in T1b–T3 clear cell RCC discontinued therapy 5 days pre‐surgery for the first five patients and 24 h pre‐surgery for the remaining patients, reporting no adverse perioperative effects [ , ]. A retrospective study evaluating presurgical sunitinib for tumour size reduction before partial nephrectomy showed an 86% surgical intervention rate and 29% overall complications, including five grade 3 or higher. The authors determined these complications were not directly attributable to presurgical sunitinib [ , , , , , ].
A retrospective study compared healing complications in imatinib‐resistant metastatic GIST patients treated with sunitinib (26 patients, 28 procedures) versus imatinib (46 patients, 53 procedures). Sunitinib was stopped preoperatively (median 5 days, range 0–26) and resumed postoperatively (median 33 days, range 12–183) and post‐discharge (median 20 days, range 7–178) at the first clinic visit [ ]. The sunitinib group experienced 17 complications in 14 procedures (50%), compared with 23 complications in 20 procedures (38%) for imatinib. Postoperative fistulas, leaks or dehiscence occurred in 2 (7%) procedures from the sunitinib group and 4 (8%) procedures from the imatinib group. No wound healing complications were observed in 18 patients who discontinued sunitinib < 9 days pre‐surgery. Despite sunitinib's broader tyrosine kinase inhibition, the study concluded that wound healing complications were not increased compared with imatinib after extensive resections [ ].
A prospective cohort study (n = 52) evaluating the post‐operative complication rate of patients with metastatic RCC being treated with sunitinib recommended suspending sunitinib 10 days pre‐surgery and resuming 10 days post‐surgery. Nine patients underwent 11 surgeries within 90 days of sunitinib initiation, with four experiencing pre‐surgical grade 2 cutaneous toxicity [ ]. The actual mean discontinuation was 7.8 days pre‐surgery and 19.8 days post‐surgery. Two of the 11 procedures resulted in surgical site wound necrosis, both in patients with prior sunitinib‐related skin toxicity. While perioperative sunitinib toxicity was low overall, the authors suggested patients with pre‐surgical cutaneous toxicity may have an increased risk of delayed wound healing [ , ].

3.1.1.5

## Lenvatinib
Lenvatinib, an oral multitargeted RTKI, inhibits VEGFR1/2/3, FGFR1/2/3/4, PDGFR, RET and KIT [ ]. FDA approvals include RAI‐refractory DTC, advanced RCC, unresectable or advanced HCC [ ] and combination use with pembrolizumab for advanced endometrial carcinoma [ ]. Hypertension is its most common side effect, alongside wound healing complications [ , ]. The drug's terminal half‐life is ~28 h [ ].
The safety information for lenvatinib (LENVIMA) indicates impaired wound healing during treatment [ , , , , , ]. The manufacturer recommends withholding lenvatinib ≥ 1 week before elective surgery and resuming ≥ 2 weeks post‐major surgery only after adequate wound healing. The safety of post‐healing treatment resumption remains unestablished [ ]. An expert consensus guideline on lenvatinib‐related adverse events in HCC patients aligns with safety prescribing information regarding wound healing [ , ]. For RAI‐refractory DTC patients on lenvatinib, the guidance recommends pre‐ and post‐operative drug discontinuation until adequate wound healing occurs. While optimal pre‐surgical withdrawal duration remains unclear, authors suggest 3–5 half‐lives, adjusted for surgical complexity [ ].
A case report describes a 53‐year‐old woman's recovery from a lenvatinib‐induced subclavicular ulcer in RAI‐refractory DTC. After total thyroidectomy, multiple neck dissections, and RAI therapy failed to control locoregional recurrences and metastases (lung, bone), sorafenib was replaced by lenvatinib, which rapidly reduced tumour size. The resulting subclavicular ulcer, revealing subclavicular artery pulsation, was treated with a pectoralis major myocutaneous flap. Lenvatinib was stopped 8 days pre‐ and 12 days post‐surgery, with uneventful recovery [ ]. A second case report details lenvatinib use before conversion surgery in a 71‐year‐old woman with ATC [ ]. Lenvatinib induced partial response, reducing primary tumour and lymph node metastases to achieve resectability. Surgery occurred 7 days post‐lenvatinib discontinuation without complications. Histopathology showed granulation tissue, papillary thyroid carcinoma, and squamoid ATC cells (50% ATC), demonstrating lenvatinib's potential to improve resectability in previously unresectable ATC [ ].
An FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) analysis (February 13, 2015—February 15, 2017) identified nine unpublished lenvatinib‐associated wound healing complications. Seven cases showed postoperative complications with impaired healing or wound dehiscence. Detection of delayed wound healing ranged from 4 to 58 days post‐injury/surgery. Variable timing of initial lenvatinib exposure relative to tissue injury may have influenced healing impairment detection and development. The case‐level evidence suggests a potential lenvatinib‐wound healing complication association based on temporality and biological plausibility [ ].
A Kanagawa Cancer Center study of 94 lenvatinib‐treated thyroid cancer patients evaluated surgical safety. Of 14 invasive procedures in 11 patients (8 local, 6 general anaesthesia, including 3 emergency cases), only one wound complication occurred: delayed healing following thoracic drain insertion for acute pneumothorax. This patient, with possible lenvatinib‐related hypoxemia from lung metastases, required 14 weeks for healing after lenvatinib resumption at day 17. The study found invasive procedures generally safe but recommended 1–2 weeks pre‐surgical lenvatinib discontinuation and post‐surgical resumption after confirmed wound healing while monitoring for disease progression during extended drug holidays [ ].

## Limited Clinical Evidence
3.1.2.1

## Axitinib
Axitinib, an RTKI that potently inhibits VEGFR1/2/3, PDGFRβ and KIT [ , ], is approved for advanced RCC [ ] and shows efficacy against squamous cell carcinoma of the head and neck, melanoma and advanced thyroid cancers [ , , , ]. Its half‐life ranges from 2.5 to 6.1 h [ ].
The safety information for axitinib (Inlyta) states it may impair wound healing. Treatment should be stopped ≥ 2 days before planned surgery. After major surgery, axitinib should not be resumed for ≥ 2 weeks or until complete wound healing. The safety information indicates dose reduction or discontinuation may be needed, and optimal timing for restart after wound‐healing complications is undefined [ ].
A case report of neoadjuvant axitinib in a Japanese RCC patient showed no surgical complications post‐discontinuation [ ]. Another case reported poor healing of a gluteal cleft ulcerated abscess in a metastatic RCC patient taking pazopanib followed by axitinib [ ]. A phase II single‐site, single‐arm study of patients with locally advanced non‐metastatic clear cell RCC reported three patients with grade 3 complications (chylous ascites and post‐operative bleeding) and one patient with superficial wound dehiscence among 24 nephrectomy patients receiving 12 weeks of neoadjuvant axitinib [ ]. These complications were evaluated after axitinib discontinuation [ , ]. No prospective studies have specifically investigated axitinib's effect on wound repair.

3.1.2.2

## Nintedanib
Nintedanib, an oral RTKI, targets VEGFR1/2/3, FGFR1/2/3 and PDGFRα/β [ ]. Originally approved for NSCLC in the European Union (EU) and studied for advanced ovarian cancer, its fibrosis‐inhibiting properties led to expanded use in benign diseases [ , , ]. FDA approvals include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), chronic fibrosing interstitial lung disease and systemic sclerosis‐associated interstitial lung disease [ , ]. Nintedanib has a 9.5‐h effective half‐life (gCV 31.9%) in IPF patients [ ].
The nintedanib (OFEV) prescribing insert notes potential wound healing impairment based on the mechanism of action, though clinical trials showed no increased incidence. Clinical judgement should guide treatment initiation or resumption for perioperative interruption to ensure adequate wound healing [ ].
In a rat tracheal stenosis trauma model, nintedanib (10 or 20 mg/kg/d) significantly reduced stenotic lesions versus saline controls by decreasing collagen deposition, fibrotic markers, and CD4+ T‐lymphocyte infiltration. Nintedanib dose‐dependently inhibited TGF‐β1‐induced HFL‐1 cell proliferation, migration, differentiation and profibrotic gene expression in vitro while blocking TGF‐β1‐activated Smad2/3 and ERK1/2 phosphorylation. These dual anti‐fibrotic and anti‐inflammatory effects prevented tracheal stenosis [ ].
A 72‐year‐old IPF patient on nintedanib (300 mg/day BID, 2‐year duration) developed oropharyngeal carcinoma. Surgery proceeded without nintedanib interruption, followed by RT (66 Gy/33 fractions). After 20 Gy, acute pharyngeal mucositis developed, with delayed healing extending beyond 2 months post‐RT. Nintedanib was reduced to 200 mg/day at day 110 post‐RT but maintained due to risk factors and limited alternatives. At discharge (day 119 post‐RT), mucosal healing remained poor. At one‐year follow‐up, incomplete mucosal normalisation persisted, requiring acetaminophen and oxycodone for pain. The case highlights an unusual presentation of chronic mucositis following standard‐dose RT, with prolonged opioid dependence despite cancer remission. The authors suggest careful nintedanib monitoring pre‐RT in IPF patients [ ].
A 67‐year‐old male with non‐ST elevation myocardial infarction and stable IPF underwent coronary bypass surgery while on nintedanib (150 mg twice daily for 5 months). The drug continued preoperatively and was resumed on postoperative day 2. At 4 weeks post‐surgery, serous discharge appeared from the left radial artery harvest and sternotomy wounds. Surgery revealed complete soft‐tissue dehiscence and absent healing fibrosis in both wounds. Nintedanib discontinuation led to satisfactory wound healing within 6 weeks. At 7 months, the wounds remained stable and nintedanib remained discontinued due to stable pulmonary status. The occurrence of complete wound dehiscence of the forearm suggested nintedanib as the systemic cause of impaired healing, leading investigators to recommend perioperative discontinuation, consistent with manufacturer guidelines [ ].

3.1.2.3

## Pemigatinib
Pemigatinib, an RTKI, targets FGFR1, FGFR2 and FGFR3 [ ]. FDA approval covers unresectable locally advanced or metastatic cholangiocarcinoma with FGFR2 fusion/rearrangement in previously treated patients and relapsed/refractory myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms (MLNs) in adults with FGFR1 rearrangement [ ]. The drug (PEMAZYRE) has a geometric mean elimination half‐life of 15.4 h (CV: 51.6%), with no reported wound healing complications in safety information [ ].
Pemigatinib wound healing complications are sparsely documented. One case report describes an 82‐year‐old female with advanced metastatic cholangiocarcinoma developing severe bilateral lower extremity pain, progressing to retiform purpura and necrotic ulceration after 3 months of treatment. Biopsy confirmed calciphylaxis cutis, which improved with pemigatinib discontinuation, antibiotics, wound care and sodium thiosulfate infusions [ ]. The authors identified a rare link between non‐uremic calciphylaxis cutis and FGFR inhibitors in patients with normal renal function. Proposed mechanisms include FGFR inhibitor‐induced FGF‐23 elevation promoting vascular calcification or direct tissue signalling effects [ ]. Similar non‐uremic calciphylaxis cases with preserved renal function were reported with erdafitinib (two cases) and an unspecified FGFR inhibitor (one case) [ , , ].

3.1.2.4

## Vandetanib
Vandetanib is an RTKI targeting RET, EGFR, VEGFR1/2/3 (particularly VEGFR2), FGFR1/2/3/4, PDGFRα and KIT [ , , , , , ]. Its FDA‐approved efficacy in advanced or MTC stems from the dual inhibition of RET and VEGF pathways [ ]. Proposed applications include oestrogen receptor‐negative breast cancer, multiple endocrine neoplasia Type 2a and 2b, NSCLC, anaplastic astrocytoma and HCC [ , , , , , ]. Vandetanib has a median half‐life of 19 days [ ].
Vandetanib (CAPRELSA) safety information reports preclinical mouse studies showed reduced skin tensile strength versus controls, suggesting delayed but not completely inhibited wound healing. The optimal pre‐elective surgery discontinuation timing to avoid wound healing impairment remains undetermined [ ].
In a wound healing assay utilising HUVECs to evaluate angiogenesis, vandetanib inhibited wound healing, invasion, and tubular formation under both VEGF‐treated and untreated conditions versus controls (p < 0.05). Results were replicated in MDA‐MB‐231 and MCF‐7 cells, with MCF‐7‐derived tumour‐conditioned medium used for subsequent tests. VEGF enhanced wound healing, invasion, and tubular formation in HUVECs, while vandetanib blocked these angiogenic effects [ ].
Ko et al. investigated vandetanib's effects on wound healing using Balb/C mice with full‐thickness sutured incisions. Breaking strength, laser Doppler blood flow, and microvessel density were assessed at days 7 and 28 post‐wounding. Vandetanib‐treated mice showed dose‐dependent decreases in breaking strength versus controls at day 7 (p < 0.001) and day 28 (p < 0.005). Histology showed reduced fibrosis and epithelial proliferation in treated mice, with no differences between dosage groups. Microvessel density remained similar across groups. While not preventing healing, vandetanib reduced skin‐breaking strength versus controls [ ].
A study investigating ATP's protective effects against vandetanib‐induced skin damage used 18 rats in three groups: healthy, vandetanib‐only, and vandetanib plus ATP [ ]. After 4 weeks, skin analysis revealed higher MDA and total oxidant status (TOS) levels but lower total glutathione (tGSH) and total antioxidant status (TAS) in the vandetanib group versus others. Histopathological examination showed significant skin damage in the vandetanib group (thinned epithelium, damaged hair follicles, abnormal keratin accumulation, dermal edema), which improved significantly with ATP co‐administration [ ].
Brief mentions of delayed wound healing were seen in a report of cases of patients treated with vandetanib who experienced cutaneous pigmentation and photosensitivity [ ].

3.1.3

## Theoretical Risk
3.1.3.1

## Erlotinib
Erlotinib is an RTKI and a first‐generation, quinazoline‐based small molecule that reversibly inhibits EGFR/HER1 [ ]. HER1 is also known as Erb1 or EGFR [ ]. The FDA has approved erlotinib for treating NSCLC and pancreatic cancer [ ]. The prescribing insert for erlotinib (TARCEVA) reports a median half‐life of 36.2 h. It does not address wound or repair healing issues, nor does it provide guidance on the timing of erlotinib administration in relation to surgery [ ].
Sun et al. (2022) investigated the effects of erlotinib on wound healing in vitro and in vivo. Epidermal stem cells incubated with 2 μM erlotinib exhibited significant delays in migration and scratch closure at 24‐ and 36‐h post‐injury. In vivo, erlotinib‐treated rats showed a significantly decreased wound healing rate post‐injury from day 7 to day 14 [ ]. Kaftan et al. (2008) examined topical erlotinib's impact on healing in induced bilateral tympanic membrane perforations in a rat model. The mean healing period was 12.1 days in the erlotinib group compared to 6.4 days in the control group [ ]. In a similar experiment, Kaftan et al. applied erlotinib to the tympanic membrane of rats daily for 12 consecutive days. They found that erlotinib prolonged healing to 11.8 days compared to a reference value of 7 days. Both studies observed corresponding differences in histological parameters between the erlotinib‐treated and control groups [ ].

3.1.3.2

## Fruquintinib
Fruquintinib, a second‐generation RTKI, is a highly selective and potent small‐molecule inhibitor targeting VEGFR1/2/3 [ , , ] with weak inhibition of RET, FGFR‐1 and KIT [ ]. Preclinical investigations demonstrate that the drug minimally interacts with CYP450 systems, reducing pharmacokinetic drug–drug interaction risks [ ]. The FDA approved fruquintinib in 2023 for adult metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) as third‐line or later therapy [ , ]. Clinical trials show promising efficacy in advanced non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and advanced gastric cancer [ , ]. The drug has a 42‐h elimination half‐life with continuous administration [ ].
The prescribing information for fruquintinib (FRUZAQLA) indicates that VEGF signalling pathway inhibitors may impair wound healing [ ]. It mentions a study of 911 patients with metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC), and only one Grade 2 wound dehiscence case was reported [ ]. Recommendations include discontinuing fruquintinib ≥ 2 weeks pre‐major surgery and resuming ≥ 2 weeks post‐surgery after adequate wound healing. The safety of resumption following wound healing complications remains unestablished. Patients should be informed of potential wound healing impairment and advised to report planned surgical procedures [ ].
Reports of fruquintinib‐induced wound complications are poorly documented. In a retrospective study of 124 refractory sarcoma patients receiving fruquintinib after multiple TKI resistance, three cases of ‘wound‐healing problems’ were identified but not further detailed, limiting understanding of their nature and severity [ ].

3.1.3.3

## Infigratinib
Infigratinib, an RTKI, inhibits FGFR1, FGFR2 and FGFR3 with low nanomolar IC50 values, showing reduced FGFR4 activity. The drug (TRUSELTIQ) has a steady‐state geometric mean terminal half‐life of 33.5 (39% CV) hours [ ]. Its primary human metabolites, BHS697 and CQM157, maintain comparable FGFR1‐3 binding affinities to the parent compound. The prescribing insert reports no wound repair concerns for TRUSELTIQ [ ]. The FDA granted accelerated approval for previously treated, unresectable locally advanced or metastatic cholangiocarcinoma with FGFR2 fusion/rearrangements, but this indication was later withdrawn in the U.S. due to confirmatory trial recruitment challenges [ ].
Limited evidence exists regarding infigratinib's wound healing complications due to insufficient indications. In a rat oesophageal model study, infigratinib (5 mg/kg or 10 mg/kg) significantly reduced stent‐induced tissue hyperplasia markers: granulation tissue area percentage, submucosal fibrosis thickness, epithelial layer count, inflammatory cell infiltration degree, collagen deposition degree, FGFR1‐expressing myofibroblast count and proliferating myofibroblast count versus control [ ].

3.1.3.4

## Pralsetinib
Pralsetinib is an RTKI targeting wild‐type, oncogenic fusions and oncogenic mutations of RET proteins [ ]. At high clinical concentrations in purified enzyme assays, it demonstrates broad‐spectrum kinase inhibition, including DDR1, FGFR1, FLT3, JAK1/2, TRKA, TRKC, VEGFR2 and PDGFR. FDA approval covers metastatic non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) with RET gene rearrangements and advanced/metastatic RET‐fusion positive thyroid cancer in adults who have exhausted radioactive iodine (RAI) therapy and need systemic treatment [ , ].
According to the prescribing information, pralsetinib (GAVRETO) has a mean plasma elimination half‐life of 15.7 h (±9.8) for single doses and 20 h (±11.7) for multiple doses [ ]. The drug may impair wound healing through VEGF signalling pathway inhibition, necessitating discontinuation ≥ 5 days before elective surgery and ≥ 2 weeks after major surgery until adequate healing occurs. The safety of resumption post‐healing complications is unknown [ ]. No clinical cases of wound repair impairment have been reported in the literature.

3.1.3.5

## Regorafenib
Regorafenib, an oral multikinase RTKI, targets diverse oncogenic kinases and the angiogenic tumour microenvironment, including VEGFR1/2/3, PDGFR, FGFR1/2, TIE2, DDR2, TrkA, Eph2A, RAF1, KIT, RET, BRAF, BRAFV600E, osmotic stress/ABA‐activated protein kinase 2 (SAPK2), FRK, ABL and CSF1R [ , , , ]. Structurally similar to sorafenib, it differs by a fluorine on the phenyl ring, enhancing inhibition of angiogenesis‐related receptors VEGFR2 and FGFR1 [ ].
The FDA approved regorafenib for mCRC in patients unresponsive to oxaliplatin, fluoropyrimidine and irinotecan‐based chemotherapy, and for Kirsten rat sarcoma virus (KRAS) wild‐type tumours after anti‐EGFR therapy failure [ ]. FDA approval also covers metastatic GISTs following progression on standard therapies, based on improved progression‐free survival versus placebo [ ]. Additionally, regorafenib received FDA approval for HCC post‐sorafenib treatment, becoming the first FDA‐approved drug for this indication and anticipated standard of care [ ]. Single 160 mg dose geometric mean elimination half‐lives of regorafenib are: 28 h (14–58), M‐2 25 h (14–32) and M5 51 h (32–70) [ ].
The regorafenib (STIVARGA) prescribing insert notes that no formal wound healing studies exist, but it emphasises that VEGFR inhibitors like regorafenib may impair wound healing. The insert recommends that treatment should be stopped ≥ 2 weeks before scheduled surgery, with resumption based on clinical assessment of wound healing adequacy. Regorafenib should be discontinued if wound dehiscence occurs [ ].
Krishnamoorthy et al. reviewed regorafenib toxicity management and posited that the drug's multi‐pathway targeting, particularly VEGF‐related pathways, may impair wound healing. Recommendations include stopping regorafenib ≥ 4 weeks before and after elective surgery, with resumption only after complete healing. For minor procedures (biopsy, port placement, tooth extraction), the drug should be discontinued until full wound healing. Treatment should be stopped if wound healing fails to occur [ ].

3.1.3.6

## Ripretinib
Ripretinib, a receptor type‐II tyrosine switch control inhibitor, targets wild‐type and mutant KIT and PDGFR, plus other kinases, including PDGFR, TIE2, VEGFR2 and BRAF proto‐oncogene. FDA approval covers adults with advanced GISTs after ≥ 3 lines of TKI therapy. The drug is under investigation for systemic mastocytosis and gliomas [ ].
According to the prescribing information, ripretinib (QINLOCK) has a mean half‐life of 14.8 h (CV% 30) after a single 150 mg oral dose [ ]. It recommends withholding the drug ≥ 1 week before elective surgery and ≥ 2 weeks post‐major surgery or until adequate wound healing. Safety data for resumption after wound healing complications is undefined [ ]. Current literature shows no documented clinical evidence of ripretinib‐associated wound healing complications.

3.1.3.7

## Selpercatinib
Selpercatinib, an RTKI and selective RET inhibitor, shows multikinase inhibition at clinical doses, targeting VEGFR1/2 and FGFR1/2/3 [ ]. FDA approval covers metastatic RET fusion‐positive NSCLC, advanced or metastatic RET‐mutant MTC requiring systemic therapy, advanced RET fusion‐positive thyroid cancer resistant to RAI, and locally advanced or metastatic solid tumours with RET gene fusions [ ].
Selpercatinib (RETEVMO) has a 32‐h half‐life after oral administration [ ]. The drug may impair wound healing through VEGF pathway inhibition. Prescribing information recommends withholding treatment ≥ 7 days before elective surgery and ≥ 2 weeks post‐major surgery or until adequate healing occurs. The safety profile for resumption after wound healing complications remains undefined, necessitating careful clinical management [ ]. Literature review shows no documented clinical evidence of wound repair complications with selpercatinib.

3.1.3.8

## Tivozanib
Tivozanib is an oral small‐molecule RTKI targeting VEGFR1/2/3, along with KIT and PDGFRβ. It has FDA approval for advanced RCC after ≥ 2 prior systemic therapies [ ].
Tivozanib (FOTIVDA) has a 111‐h half‐life. According to the prescribing information, as a VEGF signalling pathway inhibitor, it should be withheld ≥ 24 days before elective surgery. Post‐surgical administration requires ≥ 2 weeks delay or adequate wound healing. The safety of resumption after wound healing complications is unknown [ ]. No wound repair risks are documented in the literature.

3.2

## Non‐Receptor Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors
3.2.1

## Moderate Clinical Evidence
3.2.1.1

## Ibrutinib
Ibrutinib, an NRTKI, irreversibly inhibits Bruton's tyrosine kinase (BTK) by covalently binding to cysteine 481 in its ATP‐binding pocket. It also targets nine other kinases with cysteine residues: four Tec family kinase (TFK) members [inter‐leukin‐2 inducible T cell kinase (ITK), TEC, bone marrow tyrosine kinase on chromosome X (BMX) and receptor‐like kinase (RLK)/tyrosine‐protein kinase (TXK)], three EGFR family members (EGFR, ErbB2/HER2 and ErbB4/HER4), B lymphocyte kinase (BLK) and JAK3 [ ]. FDA approval covers CLL or SLL with 17p deletion, Waldenström Macroglobulinaemia (alone or with rituximab), and chronic graft versus host disease (cGvHD) in adults after failing ≥ 1 systemic therapy [ ]. According to the prescribing information, ibrutinib (IMBRUVICA) has a 4–6 h half‐life, with no reported wound repair complications [ ].
Perioperative ibrutinib management involves bleeding risks, requiring 3–7 days of drug withdrawal pre‐ and post‐surgery, based on procedure type and bleeding risk [ ]. While not directly addressing wound repair, perioperative bleeding can impact healing, as shown by Aghazadeh et al.'s retrospective dermatologic surgery study. The study classified bleeding complications as mild (crusting, ecchymosis, transient serous oozing < 24 h, or minor bleeding < 1 h resolving with pressure), medium (serous exudation ≥ 24 h, dehiscence < 2 mm or superficial tissue loss in flaps/grafts) or severe (substantial intra/post‐operative blood loss, persistent bleeding > 1 h refractory to pressure, acute hematoma, flap/graft necrosis, or dehiscence > 2 mm). Ibrutinib users showed 8% bleeding incidence versus 1% in controls, with six complications among 75 procedures (two severe, four mild). When feasible, the authors recommend discontinuing ibrutinib 5 days pre‐surgery and 3–5 days post‐dermatologic surgery [ ].

3.2.1.2

## Imatinib
Imatinib mesylate (Imatinib) is an NRTKI primarily targeting PDGFRα/β [ ]. It also inhibits KIT (associated with gastrointestinal stromal tumours [GISTs]), BCR‐ABL fusion protein (related to philadelphia chromosome‐positive chronic myeloid leukaemia [Ph + CML]), ABL‐related gene (ARG), DDR1/2, lymphocyte cell‐specific protein‐tyrosine kinase (LCK) and CSF1R [ , , , , , , ].
Imatinib initially gained FDA approval for CML [ ]. Its approved indications include: adult and paediatric Ph + CML in chronic phase (CP), Ph + CML in blast crisis (BC), Ph + CML in accelerated phase (AP), Ph + CML in CP post interferon‐failure, adult relapsed/refractory Ph + ALL, first‐line paediatric Ph + ALL with chemotherapy, adult myelodysplastic (MDS)/myeloproliferative (MPD) diseases with PDGFR gene re‐arrangements, adult aggressive systemic mastocytosis (ASM) without D816V KIT mutations, chronic eosinophilic leukaemia (CEL) or adult hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES) with factor interacting with PAP (FIP)1 L1‐PDGFR fusion kinase, adult unresectable, recurrent and/or metastatic dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans, KIT positive unresectable and/or metastatic malignant GISTs, and adult adjuvant treatment post‐resection of KIT positive GIST [ , , , , , , , ]. Imatinib's elimination half‐life is approximately 18 h [ ].
The prescribing information for imatinib mesylate (Gleevec) does not mention wound healing issues [ ]. A pre‐clinical study of collagen type 1 transgenic reporter mice showed imatinib delayed wound closure with myofibroblast frequency, fibronectin ED‐A expression, and collagen type I expression [ ].
However, case reports document impaired wound healing [ , , ]. One report describes a 56‐year‐old male with multiple lower leg ulcers and a recurring chronic posttraumatic foot wound. His history included allogenic stem cell transplantation for acute myeloid leukaemia (AML), cGvHD, and factor V Leiden thrombophilia. While on phenprocoumon and 5 years of oral imatinib for a gastrointestinal tumour, topical steroids and urea‐containing ointments improved his eczematous peri‐wound, but the wounds resisted various therapies and new ulcers developed. Upon tumour stabilisation and imatinib discontinuation, significant wound improvement occurred within 6 weeks despite unchanged wound care and medications. Complete healing was achieved in 5 months, with no recurrence at 12‐month follow‐up [ ].
A case report describes a 66‐year‐old male with GIST whose surgical wound from wide local excision healed primarily. Imatinib initiated 33 days post‐surgery led to wound discharge and ulceration after 10 days. There were negative microbial cultures and no evidence of immune suppression. Imatinib was stopped due to neutropenia concerns and persistent discharge. While the wound healed with healthy granulation within a week, discharge recurred upon imatinib restart [ ]. In another case, a 58‐year‐old man with a history of a GIST on imatinib (400 mg daily) underwent ridge extraction, tooth extraction (no. 19), and planned implant placement. He experienced delayed soft tissue healing and bone graft loss, recurring after revision surgery, but resolved after discontinuing imatinib. This represented the first reported case of imatinib‐induced prolonged gingival healing post‐oral surgery [ ].
As mentioned before, a retrospective study of 188 patients with imatinib‐resistant metastatic GISTs revealed no increased wound healing complications with sunitinib versus imatinib after extensive resections, despite sunitinib's broader tyrosine kinase inhibition [ ].

3.2.1.3

## Ponatinib
Ponatinib, an NRTKI, targets BCR‐ABL (particularly T315I mutation), FGFR1/2/3/4, PDGFR, VEGFR2, SRC, KIT, RET, TIE2, FLT1/3, RIPK1/3, AKT, EphR, ERK1/2 and MEKK2 (MEK/ERK Kinase 2) [ , , , ]. This third‐generation TKI is approved for TKI‐resistant or intolerant chronic, accelerated or blast phase CML and Ph + ALL [ , ]. The drug inhibits cell proliferation in AML, chronic eosinophilic leukaemia, NSCLCs and endometrial cancers [ , , , ]. FDA reports show increased blood clot formation and vascular constriction in clinical trials [ ]. The drug has an elimination half‐life of 27.4 h and a mean terminal half‐life of ~24 h (range 12–66 h) [ , ].
The package insert for ponatinib (ICLUSIG) reports impaired wound healing. They advise withholding treatment ≥ 1 week before elective surgery and ≥ 2 weeks post‐major surgery until adequate healing. Safety data for resumption after wound healing complications is insufficient. Gastrointestinal perforations/fistulas have been reported, requiring permanent discontinuation [ ].
In vitro studies showed ponatinib decreased both basal and VEGF‐stimulated HUVEC migration at 24 h by 30%, 26% and 9% at 1, 3, and 10 μM, respectively. Transwell migration assays measuring HUVEC migration over 24 h demonstrated ponatinib reduced VEGF‐induced cell migration by 68%, 30% and 12% at 1, 3 and 10 μM, respectively [ ].
The phase II PACE trial identified impaired wound healing as a less common but significant safety concern in Ph + ALL patients receiving ponatinib [ , ]. Despite its mechanism suggesting potential wound healing effects, no direct formal studies exist [ , ].

3.2.2

## Limited Clinical Evidence
3.2.2.1

## Dasatinib
Dasatinib, an NRTKI, is a second‐generation multitarget kinase inhibitor showing superior in vitro activity against unmutated BRC‐ABL kinase compared with imatinib and most imatinib‐resistant BCR‐ABL mutants [ , ]. Originally targeting SRC family kinases (SRC, FYN, FRK, LCK/YES novel [LYN] YES), its inhibition extends to TEC kinases (BTK, TEC), DDR1, ABL2, salt‐inducible kinase 2 (SIK2), receptor‐interacting serine/threonine kinase 2 (RIPK2), erythropoietin‐producing hepatocyte receptor A2/B2 (EphA2/B2), PDGFRβ and KIT [ , , , , ]. It is approved for newly diagnosed Ph + CML in chronic phase, chronic accelerated phase, myeloid or lymphoid blast crisis Ph + CML with resistance to prior therapy, and Ph + acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL) with resistance to prior therapy [ ]. Dasatinib's average terminal half‐life is 3–5 h [ , ].
While dasatinib's (Sprycel) safety information notes the possibility of Stevens‐Johnson syndrome, it does not mention wound healing effects [ ]. However, preclinical studies suggest a potential wound healing benefit [ , , ]. Wound healing involves overlapping phases and coordinated responses from non‐immune cells, immune cells and biomolecules. Platelets, clotting factors, and immunoreceptor tyrosine‐based activation motif (ITAM)‐associated receptors, including Glycoprotein VI (GPVI) and C‐type lectin‐like receptor 2 (CLEC‐2), are crucial [ , ]. Mouse studies suggest that dasatinib promotes wound healing by blocking GPVI and CLEC‐2 in platelets [ ]. Research explored dasatinib's potential to enhance skin wound healing by disrupting vascular integrity during inflammation in mice [ ]. The study shows dasatinib inhibits ITAM receptor‐mediated platelet aggregation and increases vascular permeability during inflammatory wound healing in mice. Researchers suggest this vascular integrity loss benefits cutaneous wound healing, attributing the healing promotion to GPVI and CLEC‐2 platelet function inhibition, which increases vascular permeability and causes transient intratissue haemorrhage [ ]. This mechanism enables plasma‐derived molecules (fibrinogen, growth factors, cytokines) to enter wounds, enhancing tissue repair. The early‐phase self‐limited vascular leakage differs from prolonged intratissue haemorrhage, which may impair healing [ ].
Though dasatinib is not generally reported to impair wound healing, a case study suggests potential interference through off‐target effects. A 59‐year‐old woman with Ph + ALL, morbid obesity, and diet‐controlled diabetes experienced surgical wound dehiscence unresponsive to wound care and antibiotics while on dasatinib. Temporary dose reductions led to incremental wound healing improvements [ ]. The case suggests dasatinib may alter the wound healing environment.

3.2.2.2

## Zanubrutinib
Zanubrutinib is a next‐generation NRTKI and irreversible BTK inhibitor with minimal off‐target inhibition of TEC and EGFR kinases [ ]. FDA approval includes mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) in previously treated patients, Waldenström Macroglobulinaemia, relapsed or refractory marginal zone lymphoma (MZL) in adults after ≥ 1 anti‐CD20‐based regimen, CLL, SLL and relapsed or refractory follicular lymphoma (FL) in adults with obinutuzumab after ≥ 2 systemic therapies [ ]. The drug's mean elimination half‐life is 2–4 h following single oral doses of 160 or 320 mg [ ], with no wound healing complications addressed in current prescribing information [ ].
Wang et al. reported three cases of zanubrutinib‐related dermatological toxicities. In one case, a 69‐year‐old male with relapsed CLL developed left lower extremity skin lesions after 1 month of zanubrutinib maintenance. The lesions progressed from papules to pustules, cellulitis and necrosis over 6 months, resolving after zanubrutinib discontinuation and > 6 months of treatment/follow‐up. Despite high BTK selectivity, the authors suggest these effects may stem from off‐target EGFR inhibition, affecting cell cycle progression and increasing apoptosis, or from KIT and PDGFR inhibition [ ].

3.2.3

## Theoretical Risk
3.2.3.1

## Bosutinib
Bosutinib is an NRTKI that targets Src and Abl kinases as a third‐generation BCR‐ABL inhibitor [ ]. FDA approval covers Ph + CML for newly diagnosed chronic phase patients as first‐line therapy and for chronic, accelerated or blast phase patients with resistance or intolerance to other therapies, including imatinib [ ]. According to the prescribing insert, bosutinib (BOSULIF) has a mean terminal phase elimination half‐life of 22.5 h, with no explicit mention of wound‐healing complications [ ].
In vitro research using HUVECs found bosutinib inhibited scratch closure at high supratherapeutic concentrations, though clinical evidence on wound repair remains limited [ ].

3.2.3.2

## Nilotinib
Nilotinib is an oral second‐generation NRTKI that inhibits the Abl kinase domain through ATP competition, effective against most imatinib‐resistant BCR‐ABL mutations. It also inhibits KIT, PDGFR, DDR1, and non‐tyrosine kinase NQO2 (NAD(P)H:quinone oxidoreductase) [ ]. FDA approval covers first‐line treatment of newly diagnosed CP and second‐line treatment for chronic and accelerated phase Ph + CML in patients resistant or intolerant to imatinib [ ]. Nilotinib (Tasigna) has an elimination half‐life of ~17 h, with no wound repair complications noted in the prescribing information [ ].
Research on nilotinib's arterial thrombosis effects has revealed its impact on endothelial function and wound healing. Hadzijusufovic et al. demonstrated nilotinib significantly impaired HUVEC migration and sprouting in a scratch wound healing assay [ ]. Travers et al. found nilotinib and ponatinib reduced HUVEC wound healing more than imatinib and asciminib in vitro, with nilotinib also decreasing endothelial cell wound healing in a mouse carotid artery wire injury model [ ]. Using human induced pluripotent stem cell‐derived endothelial cells, Pinheiro et al. showed nilotinib decreased migration in wound healing scratch assays [ ].

## Discussion
Of the 63 FDA‐approved TKIs reviewed, 24 (17 RTKIs, 7 NRTKIs) demonstrated adverse effects on wound healing and repair. VEGFR inhibition is the primary mechanism underlying this impairment, with EGFR and PDGFR inhibition as secondary mechanisms. Evidence quality varies considerably among these TKIs, ranging from direct wound healing studies to clinical trial adverse events, case reports, prescribing warnings and in vitro studies, complicating the assessment of clinical significance.
Fifteen agents show anti‐VEGFR activity: axitinib, cabozantinib, fruquintinib, lenvatinib, nintedanib, pazopanib, ponatinib, pralsetinib, regorafenib, ripretinib, selpercatinib, sorafenib, sunitinib, tivozanib and vandetanib [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ]. Fifteen agents target PDGFR: axitinib, dasatinib, imatinib, lenvatinib, nilotinib, nintedanib, pazopanib, ponatinib, pralsetinib, regorafenib, ripretinib, sorafenib, sunitinib, tivozanib and vandetanib [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ]. Four agents target EGFR: erlotinib, ibrutinib, vandetanib and zanubrutinib [ , , , , , , , , ]. Vandetanib uniquely inhibits all three receptors (EGFR, PDGFR, VEGFR). Eleven agents show dual VEGFR/PDGFR inhibition: axitinib, lenvatinib, nintedanib, pazopanib, ponatinib, pralsetinib, regorafenib, ripretinib, sorafenib, sunitinib and tivozanib. Three agents (cabozantinib, fruquintinib, selpercatinib) target VEGFR only, three (dasatinib, imatinib, nilotinib) target PDGFR only, and three (erlotinib, ibrutinib, zanubrutinib) target EGFR only.
The inhibition of VEGFR and, consequently, angiogenesis by TKIs plays the most prominent role in their capacity to impede wound healing and repair. VEGFRs, an RTK subclass, include VEGFR2 (KDR), VEGFR1 (FLT1) and VEGFR3 (FLT4), which trigger cell survival, proliferation and angiogenesis upon ligand binding [ ]. The VEGF family, essential for vascular growth and angiogenesis, consists of VEGF‐A, VEGF‐B, VEGF‐C and VEGF‐D [ , ], along with VEGF‐E and PLGF [ ]. VEGFR1 binds PLGF, VEGF‐B and VEGF‐A [ ], and regulates excessive vascular formation, serum VEGF‐A levels, and VEGF‐A/VEGFR2 activation through competitive inhibition [ ]. VEGF‐A promotes new blood vessel formation in pathological and physiological conditions [ , ], while VEGF‐B promotes cardiovascular growth and neuronal survival through angiogenesis [ ]. PLGF exhibits dual anti‐ and pro‐angiogenic effects, stimulating stromal cell proliferation and activation [ ]. VEGFR2 activation by VEGF‐A triggers angiogenic pathways, endothelial cell survival/growth and stimulates their morphogenesis, chemotaxis and mitosis [ ]. VEGFR3 mediates lymphangiogenesis through VEGF‐D and VEGF‐C activation [ ].
Injury‐induced cellular responses release VEGF, affecting wound healing through collagen synthesis and epithelialization [ ]. VEGF signalling inhibition through medications such as bevacizumab, a recombinant humanised monoclonal IgG1 antibody, can impair wound healing [ , ]. Documented complications of bevacizumab include wound dehiscence, ecchymosis, bleeding and infection [ ]. Multiple studies report wound healing and bleeding complications with bevacizumab [ , , ]. Multivariate analysis identified presurgical targeted therapy as a significant predictor of wound complications (odds ratio 4.14, p = 0.03) [ ]. A Phase II study with 101 patients demonstrated increased delayed superficial wound healing with preoperative bevacizumab [ ], while another study found high wound complication rates in post‐surgery bevacizumab‐treated ovarian cancer patients [ ]. Despite logical expectations of similar complications with VEGFR inhibition, inconsistent evidence for VEGFR‐blocking TKIs prevents confirmation. Questions remain about whether multi‐targeted TKIs versus selective VEGFR inhibitors cause more severe complications or if multiple versus single VEGFR type inhibition has a greater impact.
EGFR is a secondary TKI target, less significant than VEGFR, potentially contributing to poor wound healing [ ]. EGFR includes four proteins: HER4 (ErbB4), HER3 (ErbB3), HER2 (ErbB2) and EGFR (ErbB1) [ , ]. EGFR‐mediated EGF signalling promotes tumour angiogenesis, apoptosis, adhesion, migration, differentiation, proliferation, cell growth and endothelial cell differentiation/proliferation while upregulating angiogenic factors like VEGF for indirect tumour angiogenesis promotion [ , ]. EGFR pathway inhibition reduces angiogenesis, tumour growth, and metastasis in vitro and animal studies [ ]. EGF system inhibition can impair wound healing through defective cellular differentiation and proliferation [ , ]. However, EGF/EGFR‐specific monoclonal antibodies cetuximab and panitumumab show no wound healing complications [ , , ].
PDGFRβ is essential for fibroblast and pericyte recruitment in cutaneous wound repair [ ]. The PDGF/PDGFRβ axis promotes endothelial cell growth, reproduction, angiogenesis and vascular maturation [ , ]. PDGFRα signalling enhances fibroblast progenitor proliferation in early wound healing, while subsequent downregulation promotes fibroblast‐to‐myofibroblast differentiation [ ]. PDGF‐AA drives angiogenesis, invasion, metastasis, differentiation and cell proliferation [ ], and its topical application enhances delayed wound closure in senescence‐free murine wounds [ ]. Supramolecular hydrogels containing PDGF‐BB homodimers have been shown in studies to enhance cutaneous wound repair [ ].
Paradoxically, some RTKIs show unexpected wound healing benefits. Vemurafenib, which selectively targets BRAF V600E‐mutated kinase via ATP‐binding site interaction while preserving wild‐type BRAF [ ], accelerates skin wound healing, demonstrated through in vitro keratinocyte assays and in vivo murine models. Escuin‐Ordinas et al. showed vemurafenib enhances hair follicle regeneration and accelerates impaired wound repair in diabetic mice [ ]. JAK 1 and 2 inhibitors ruxolitinib and baricitinib effectively treat pyoderma gangrenosum ulcerations [ , , , ]. These findings indicate that targeted kinase modulation's impact depends on patient context, beyond wound pathway complexity, necessitating individualised clinical approaches.
Neoadjuvant treatment with wound‐impairing TKIs can be appropriate when allowing sufficient time between drug cessation and surgery [ , , , , , ]. This approach requires evaluating patient‐specific factors (surgical procedure, age, comorbidities, concurrent medications) to balance tumour shrinkage benefits against healing risks. Recommendations for preoperative cessation demonstrate significant variability. While a clear pattern based on half‐life is not completely consistent, there is a general trend: TKIs with shorter half‐lives (< 24 h) typically require cessation periods of at least 2–7 days, those with moderate half‐lives (24–72 h) often recommend at least 1–2 week cessation periods, and agents with longer half‐lives (> 72 h) like cabozantinib and tivozanib require extended cessation periods of at least 2–3 weeks or more [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ]. Current recommendations typically suggest 3–5 half‐lives for preoperative cessation based on pharmacokinetics, though few recommendations account for surgery type, patient factors, or wound healing status [ , , , , ]. While most TKI prescribing information recommends resumption after ‘adequate wound healing’, specific guidance varies considerably between agents. Some TKIs leave the decision entirely to clinical judgement, while others specify minimum post‐surgical waiting periods, typically 1–2 weeks [ , , , ]. For agents with longer half‐lives, longer delays may be warranted.
The absence of prospective randomised clinical trials specifically designed to assess wound healing outcomes with TKIs makes it challenging to establish definitive causation and optimal management strategies. Several factors complicate TKI management decisions, including the lack of standardised criteria for ‘adequate healing’, the need to balance wound healing against cancer progression risks, variable healing timeframes among patients, and limited data on early versus delayed resumption. Long‐term TKI therapy may increase risks of poor healing in minor injuries, potentially leading to complications. Future research should address these knowledge gaps through prospective trials examining optimal cessation and restart timing, standardised assessment criteria and patient‐specific risk stratification tools. Additionally, investigators should explore protective strategies and adjunctive therapies that could allow continued TKI treatment while minimising wound‐related complications. Long‐term follow‐up studies are crucial to assess the impact of chronic TKI use on tissue repair and regeneration, ultimately leading to evidence‐based guidelines that balance oncologic and surgical outcomes.
The impact of TKIs on wound healing necessitates a coordinated interdisciplinary approach to perioperative care. Oncologists, surgeons and wound care specialists should collaborate to develop individualised management plans that optimise both cancer treatment and surgical outcomes. Early involvement of wound care specialists may help identify high‐risk patients and implement preventive measures. When TKI interruption is necessary, teams should consider potential bridging strategies such as short‐acting agents or alternative therapeutic approaches during the perioperative period [ ]. Promising adjunctive therapies to support wound healing include topical growth factors, platelet‐rich plasma and specialised dressing protocols, though their efficacy specifically in TKI‐treated patients requires further study [ , ]. Additionally, careful monitoring of nutritional status, glycemic control and other modifiable risk factors may help optimise wound healing conditions [ ]. For emergency surgeries where TKI discontinuation is not feasible, enhanced post‐operative monitoring and aggressive wound care measures may be warranted.
Several limitations exist in this review. Most evidence comes from preclinical studies or case reports, lacking large‐scale prospective clinical trials focused on wound healing outcomes. Direct comparisons are difficult due to heterogeneous study designs, outcome measures and TKI dosing regimens. The long‐term impact of TKIs on tissue repair and remodelling remains unclear. However, the review's strengths include a comprehensive analysis of 24 FDA‐approved TKIs and their wound healing effects, incorporating diverse sources (safety package inserts, preclinical studies, case reports and clinical trials). This broad approach enables a nuanced understanding of evidence levels for each TKI. The identification of common themes and mechanisms across TKIs provides insights into tyrosine kinase inhibition and wound healing, informing future research and clinical practice.
Given the expanding use of TKIs in cancer therapy and other diseases, the incidence of wound healing complications is likely to increase. This underscores the need for heightened awareness among healthcare providers and the development of evidence‐based management strategies. While TKIs have advanced the treatment of many cancers, their adverse impact on wound healing presents a clinical challenge. A nuanced understanding of the mechanisms involved, careful patient selection and monitoring and individualised management approaches are essential to optimise outcomes.
