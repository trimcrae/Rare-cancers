## Abstract
Medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) is a rare neuroendocrine neoplasm derived from parafollicular C cells of the thyroid gland, primarily driven by alterations in the rearranged during transfection (RET) oncogene. This review focuses on the current advances in treatment modalities for MTCs, encompassing surgical interventions, targeted therapy, postoperative surveillance, and future challenges. Total thyroidectomy is the primary curative approach for MTCs. However, the extent of lymph node dissection, particularly lateral neck dissection, remains controversial. Postoperative surveillance involves monitoring serum calcitonin and carcinoembryonic antigen levels, imaging assessments, and dynamic risk stratification to detect recurrence. Drug therapy offers an alternative approach in cases of challenging surgical scenarios or advanced MTCs. Multi-kinase inhibitors (e.g. vandetanib and cabozantinib) have shown increased survival outcomes for metastatic MTCs and are approved as effective treatment options. More recently, selective rearranged during transfection inhibitors like selpercatinib and pralsetinib have demonstrated higher efficacy and better tolerability. Despite these advances, challenges persist in managing MTCs, including addressing biochemical recurrence, determining surgical scope, considering immunotherapy, and treating advanced cases. Personalized medicine approaches, incorporating genetic screening, and innovative therapies, are essential for improving survival and quality of life in MTC patients.
Keywords: biochemical recurrence, calcitonin monitoring, follow-up monitoring, lymph node dissection, medullary thyroid carcinoma, precision medicine, RET inhibitors

## Introduction
Medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) is a rare neuroendocrine malignancy, accounting for 2%–4% of all thyroid cancers[ , ]. In the United States, approximately 44 000 new cases of thyroid cancer are diagnosed per year, with a 5-year survival of 98.5%[ ]. A subset of patients may present an aggressive clinical course, leading to tumor recurrence or death in the subsequent 5 years. MTC is associated with higher mortality, accounting for 8.6% of thyroid cancer-related deaths, and the 10-year survival rate was 10%–40% following the first diagnosed metastasis[ ]. MTC originates from parafollicular C cells in the thyroid gland, which releases calcitonin (Ctn) and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA)[ ] (Fig. ). MTCs are classified into hereditary and sporadic types, with the latter constituting 75% of cases[ ]. Hereditary MTC is predominantly related to germline mutations in the rearranged during transfection (RET) proto-oncogene, a receptor tyrosine kinase critical for cell proliferation and differentiation[ – ]. These mutations are central to multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 (MEN2) syndromes, classified into MEN2A and MEN2B. MEN2A, the more prevalent subtype, is commonly linked to mutations in cysteine residues of RET exons 10 and 11 (e.g. C609, C611, C618). In contrast, MEN2B is a more aggressive and early onset form that is primarily associated with M918T mutation in exon 16[ , ]. These genotype-phenotype correlations have informed risk stratification and management strategies. The American Thyroid Association (ATA) categorizes RET variants into three risk levels: “highest risk” (HST), “high risk” (H), and “moderate risk” (MOD), guiding the timing of prophylactic thyroidectomy and surveillance protocols[ ]. Sporadic MTC cases often harbor somatic mutations in RET (e.g. M918T variant) that are associated with a more aggressive phenotype. Approximately 50%–80% of sporadic MTCs exhibit RET mutation, while RAS mutation occurs in about 30% of RET-negative cases[ , , ]. Notably, RET and RAS mutation are typically mutually exclusive, underscoring distinct molecular pathways in MTC pathogenesis[ , ].

## HIGHLIGHTS

Total thyroidectomy with lymph node dissection is primary for medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC); lateral lymph node dissection debated.
Post-op monitoring via serum calcitonin/carcinoembryonic antigen, imaging, and dynamic risk stratification.
Rearranged during transfection-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) and next-gen selective inhibitors enhance advanced MTC survival.
Challenges include biochemical recurrence, surgical scope decisions, and TKI resistance.

 Surgical resection, specifically total thyroidectomy with appropriate lymph node dissection, remains the cornerstone of MTC treatment. However, the high rate of recurrence and the presence of metastatic disease in some patients necessitate additional therapeutic approaches. Targeted systemic therapies have emerged as effective interventions, particularly in patients with progressive MTC unsuitable for surgery. Multi-kinase inhibitors (MKIs, e.g. cabozantinib and vandetanib) have demonstrated efficacy in treating advanced MTCs by targeting multiple tyrosine kinases, including RET[ , ]. However, their use is often limited by off-target effects and associated toxicities. The advent of highly selective RET inhibitors (e.g. pralsetinib and selpercatinib) has revolutionized MTC management. For instance, selpercatinib has received FDA approval for treating advanced RET-positive MTCs, reflecting its therapeutic potential. Despite these advancements, challenges persist in the MTC, including the optimal approach to biochemical recurrence, determination of surgical scope, consideration of immunotherapy, and treatment of advanced cases. Various guidelines, including the ATA[ ], the Chinese Association of Thyroid Surgery[ , ], the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO)[ , ], and Japan Associations of Endocrine Surgeons guidelines[ ] recommend and provide updates on the management, with a focus on diagnosis, surgical, adjuvant therapy, follow-up monitoring, prognosis, and therapeutic challenges. ## Diagnosis
Early diagnosis of MTC is critical (preferably before surgery), as it significantly improves prognosis and enables timely prophylactic interventions. Current guidelines (ATA 2015 and ESMO 2019) emphasize a multimodal diagnostic approach, incorporating neck ultrasound (US), fine-needle aspiration (FNA) biopsy, serum Ctn, and CEA measurements, and RET mutation analysis[ ] (Fig. ).

 Neck US remains the primary imaging modality for its accessibility and cost-effectiveness. However, the US risk stratification systems identify fewer than 50% of MTC cases, underscoring the need for FNA biopsy even when US findings are inconclusive[ ]. Histologically, MTC is characterized by salt-and-pepper chromatin, multi-nucleation, and plasmacytoid or spindled cells within a fibrous stroma. Cytological features in FNA samples can vary, and low cellularity may limit its diagnostic accuracy, with sensitivity up to 86%[ ].
Serum Ctn measurement is a critical component in diagnosing sporadic MTC. Basal calcitonin (bCtn) levels correlate with tumor size, with values of 60–100 pg/mL suggesting C-cell proliferation, and levels above 500 pg/mL indicating potential metastases[ ]. However, false positives can occur due to ectopic Ctn production or proton pump inhibitor use[ ]. Calcium-stimulated calcitonin testing can mitigate these issues, though standardized diagnostic cutoffs are lacking. Discrepancies exist between ATA and ETA guidelines regarding bCtn testing. ETA recommends routine measurement, while ATA advises against it for cost-effectiveness reasons.
CEA, another marker produced by C cells, is useful for monitoring disease progression, as its levels can also be seen in smokers or individuals with gastrointestinal or lung diseases. While not suitable for initial screening, CEA measurement complements Ctn testing in the diagnostic process its levels increase with tumor mass and metastasis[ ]. Combining US with serum Ctn and CEA measurements significantly enhances diagnostic sensitivity (95% vs. 77% with US alone)[ ].
FNA washout fluid (FNA-Ctn) offers a more sensitive diagnostic approach, particularly when cytology is inconclusive[ ]. Measuring Ctn in the washout fluid enhances diagnostic accuracy and is recommended by the ATA in such cases. Emerging techniques like liquid biopsy for circulating tumor DNA show promise for monitoring MTC but remain investigational.
Genetic counseling and RET mutation sequencing are recommended for all suspected cases, regardless of clinical presentation. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines recommend that all patients who are RET germline unknown or RET-negative should undergo somatic RET testing[ ]. Identifying a MEN2-specific RET mutation allows for early detection and monitoring of at-risk individuals, reducing the need for extensive follow-up. Germline RET mutation testing cannot diagnose sporadic MTC, which is confirmed only in the absence of MEN2-specific RET mutations.
Traditional computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are not routinely used for MTC diagnosis, but they may be helpful in detecting metastatic lesions in the lungs, mediastinal lymph nodes, or liver[ ].

## Surgical therapies
Total thyroidectomy is the only curative option for MTC, and extensive surgery (e.g. lateral neck dissection) mainly depends on the diagnostic markers and preoperative imaging. Preoperative high-resolution neck US is the first-line imaging choice to evaluate the lymph node. For patients with large tumors, or aggressive histologies, CT/MRI and positron emission tomography (PET)/CT with tracers (mainly 18F-DOPA and gallium-68 (68 Ga)-DOTATATE) are utilized to enhance diagnostic accuracy. Serum Ctn and CEA are pivotal markers for diagnosis and postoperative surveillance.

## Prophylactic surgery
The ATA provides stratified recommendations based on the specific RET mutation and its associated risk profile. Children with MEN2B syndrome, especially those harboring the M918T mutation, are categorized as ATA-HST. These populations are advised to undergo total thyroidectomy within the first year, potentially even in the initial few months, to prevent early-onset MTC. For individuals with MEN2A syndrome and high-risk mutation (such as codon 634), prophylactic thyroidectomy is recommended before the age of five. The timing of surgery is often guided by serum Ctn levels, with elevated levels prompting earlier intervention[ ]. Moderate-risk (ATA-MOD) mutation carriers may be managed with regular monitoring, including annual physical examinations, cervical ultrasonography, and serum Ctn measurements starting from age three[ ]. Surgical intervention in these patients is typically considered when there is a significant rise in Ctn levels or based on family history and parental preference.
The efficacy of early prophylactic thyroidectomy is supported by clinical studies demonstrating reduced recurrence rates and improved outcomes. A global multicenter retrospective study involving 345 patients with MEN2B indicated that thyroidectomy performed before the age of one significantly decreased the risk of MTC recurrence[ ]. These findings underscore the importance of timely surgical intervention in high-risk populations.

## Management of the primary tumor
Total thyroidectomy and central node dissection are recommended for MTCs with clinically apparent disease[ , ]. In hereditary MTC cases, commonly associated with RET proto-oncogene mutations, multifocal or bilateral lesions are often present, necessitating total thyroidectomy[ , ]. Genetic screening for RET mutations is crucial, as approximately 4%–10% of individuals with apparently sporadic MTCs harbor germline mutation.
In sporadic MTC, multifocal or bilateral lesions occur in approximately 6%–30%, and most researchers recommend a total thyroidectomy as the initial surgical treatment option[ , ]. However, it has been observed that sporadic MTCs without RET mutations have the likelihood of bilobar thyroid lesions near 0%. Therefore, some studies recommend lobectomy of the affected gland, rather than total thyroidectomy for sporadic MTC[ ]. However, the latest international guidelines, such as ESMO and ATA, still recommend total thyroidectomy for sporadic MTC[ , – ]. Nevertheless, total thyroidectomy remains the standard recommendation, especially in cases with lateral cervical lymph node metastases or when preoperative imaging indicates bilateral involvement[ ].

## Extent of lymph node dissection

## Central lymph node dissection
The debate surrounding central lymph node removal for MTC revolves around whether the central lymph nodes should be prophylactically removed, along with total thyroidectomy. In cases of MTC with preoperatively confirmed central lymph node metastasis (cN1a), the removal of central lymph nodes has been reported[ , , , , ]. However, because of the location of the central regional lymph nodes, preoperative imaging tools, especially the commonly used US, are not ideal for evaluating the central lymph nodes within the group[ ] (Fig. ). MTC exhibits a high rate of lymph node metastasis in the central zone (50%–75%)[ , , ]. Prophylactic lymph node dissection in the central zone for MTC has been shown to evaluate lymph node metastases in the central zone in postoperative patients[ – ]. Consequently, most current guidelines recommend prophylactic lymph node dissection in the central group after total thyroidectomy[ , ]. However, a retrospective analysis of 300 MTC patients revealed a positive correlation between serum Ctn levels and the risk of lymph node metastasis. The risk of central lymph node metastasis was 69% when the serum Ctn levels ranged from 2000 to 10 000, and 34% when the Ctn levels ranged from 200 to 500. Notably, no central lymph node metastasis occurred when the serum Ctn levels were <20 pg/mL. Therefore, whether to perform central lymph node dissection in patients with MTC remains controversial[ , ].

 

## Lateral cervical lymph node dissection
While the therapeutic lateral cervical lymph node dissection (LCLND) is standard for patients with clinically evident cN1b[ , , , , ], the role of prophylactic LCLND in MTC patients without radiographic evidence (cN0) remains controversial.
Current guidelines offer varying recommendations. The ATA suggests considering LCLND based on preoperative serum Ctn levels, recommending ipsilateral central and lateral neck dissection for Ctn >200 pg/mL, and central neck dissection alone for Ctn >20 pg/mL[ ]. The ESMO provides similar guidance, advocating for prophylactic LCLND in patients with Ctn levels exceeding 200 pg/mL[ ]. Conversely, the Japanese Society of Endocrine Surgeons neither supports nor opposes prophylactic LCLND, emphasizing individualized decision-making[ ]. Chinese expert consensus suggests that prophylactic LCLND is not necessary for most MTC patients with cN0, but should be considered in conjunction with central lymph node metastasis, serum Ctn levels, and primary focal burden[ ].
Despite these recommendations, the efficacy of prophylactic LCLND in improving survival outcomes is debated. A retrospective study involving 316 MTC patients found no significant difference in 10-year postoperative recurrence rate or overall survival between cN0 patients who underwent prophylactic LCLND and those who did not[ ]. Similarly, other researchers also reported that prophylactic lateral neck dissection does not affect the risk of recurrence or survival in sporadic MTC patients[ , ]. However, these findings are based on a small sample size. A meta-analysis of 18 publications involving 1696 MTC patients revealed that prophylactic central neck dissection significantly reduced local recurrence, while ipsilateral lateral neck dissection was negatively correlated with overall mortality[ ].
Given these findings reliance solely on serum Ctn levels to guide prophylactic LCLND may be insufficient[ ]. An alternative biomarker, proliferative desmoplastic stromal reaction (DSR), has been proposed for this purpose. It is a histopathological feature characterized by fibrous tissue formation around tumor cells. In a study of 360 MTC patients, none with DSR-negative tumors had lateral lymph node or distant metastases, whereas 31.4% of DSR-positive patients exhibited lymph node metastases[ ]. Another study analyzing 139 sporadic unifocal MTC cases identified DSR positivity and basal Ctn >113 pg/mL as independent risk factors for nodal metastases[ ]. These findings support a tailored surgical concept: patients with DSR-negative tumors may avoid unnecessary lateral neck dissection, reducing the risk of complications, such as lymphatic drainage issues, phrenic nerve injury, parasympathetic nerve injury, and Horner’s syndrome due to cervical sympathetic trunk injury (Fig. ). By systematically integrating germline/somatic mutation profiles, preoperative Ctn thresholds, and intraoperative frozen section-validated DSR status, we developed a molecularly guided surgical decision matrix (Fig. ) that optimizes resection extent based on real-time biologic aggressiveness.

 

## Surgical outcomes

## Postoperative radioiodine treatment
Parafollicular cells are unable to take up iodine; hence, most scientists believe that the I131 treatment is ineffective in MTC[ ]. However, some studies have indicated that the removal of thyroid remnants by I131 after total thyroidectomy for MTC leads to a reduction in serum Ctn levels[ ]. Nonetheless, there is no reliable clinical evidence regarding the effect of I131 on MTC recurrence and patient survival (Fig. ).

## Postoperative hormone replacement therapy
After total thyroidectomy, MTC patients regularly receive thyroxine replacement therapy to maintain normal thyroid hormone level. As the MTC originates from parafollicular C cells that lack express the thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) receptor, it does not respond to TSH suppression (Fig. ). Unnecessary TSH suppression may expose patients to potential adverse effects, including cardiovascular complications and decreased bone mineral density[ ].

## Follow-up monitoring
Postoperative follow-up of MTC mainly involves regular monitoring of serum Ctn and CEA levels, as well as neck US and other imaging studies. Additionally, dynamic postoperative risk stratification for recurrence (RSS) is important for formulating a follow-up plan for subsequent treatment[ ].

## Dynamic monitoring of serologic markers

## Ctn levels
Serum Ctn values are closely linked to TNM stage, lymph node or distant metastasis, and recurrence[ , – ]. Ctn is a crucial biomarker in thyroid cancer, particularly for the diagnosis and monitoring of MTC. Its primary physiological role is to maintain calcium homeostasis by inhibiting osteoclast activity in bones, which reduces calcium release into the circulation[ ]. In a single-center analysis of 263 patients with MEN2 type MTC, Raue et al[ ] found that the positive predictive rates for diagnosing MTC were 8.3%, 25.0%, and 100% in patients with serum Ctn levels of 20–50 ng/L, >50–100 ng/L, and >100 ng/L, respectively.
Patients with undetectable Ctn levels after surgery could be defined as a biochemical cure[ ]. Persistent postoperative serum Ctn levels >10 ng/L indicate the residual disease occurrence, while serum Ctn levels >150 ng/L refer to the possibility of distant metastases[ , , ]. The doubling time (DT) of the Ctn level may reflect the prognosis. A study reported that MTC patients with serum Ctn level DT <6 months had 5- and 10-year survival rates of 80% and 25% in those with total thyroidectomy and bilateral neck dissection while those with DT between 6 and 24 months had 5- and 10-year survival rates of 92% and 37%[ ]. It is important to note that several factors can increase serum Ctn levels. Hyperparathyroidism, non-MTC thyroid cancer, and autoimmune thyroiditis may elevate serum Ctn levels. Physiological C-cell hyperplasia, small-cell lung cancer, prostate, and neuroendocrine malignancy may increase serum Ctn levels. The high frequency of C-cell hyperplasia (CCH 30%–75%) is one of the most important confounding factors limiting the diagnostic efficacy of serum Ctn levels for MTC. Costante et al[ ] reported that CCH caused a false positive rate of 25% and 10.5% while preoperative detection of serum Ctn of 50–100 pg/mL and ≥20 ≤ 50 pg/mL.

## Optimal time for postoperative serum Ctn measurements
The optimal timing for postoperative serum Ctn levels is crucial to predict MTC outcomes[ , ]. The normalization of serum Ctn values after thyroid surgery is influenced by factors, such as the half-life of Ctn, lymph node metastasis, residual glandular tissue, and recurrence. In a follow-up trial of 295 MTCs, Machens et al[ ] observed that the recovery time in MTCs without lymph node metastasis was dependent on preoperative serum Ctn levels. For the metastatic cohorts, the recovery time was dependent on the number of lymph node metastases and they had a mean recovery time of 36.9 days. Additionally, Andrade et al[ ] found that 97% of MTC patients without postoperative recurrence had serum Ctn levels below the limit of detection within 1 month, but the normalization of serum Ctn levels could take up to 6 months in cases of biochemical or structural recurrence[ ].

## Carcinoembryonic antigen
The levels of serum CEA are positively associated with the aggressiveness of MTC and are an important biomarker for assessing MTC prognosis. The combination of serum Ctn, and CEA can help evaluate the risk of tumor recurrence[ , ]. Preoperative serum CEA levels were positively correlated with tumor burden, as indicated by the maximum tumor diameter. As serum CEA levels gradually increased, the maximum tumor diameter also progressively increased. When serum CEA levels exceeded 10 ng/mL, there was significant tumor enlargement, with a mean 19.3 mm in one diameter. Furthermore, the metastatic lymph node number displayed a linear positive correlation with a gradual elevation of both serum Ctn and CEA values in MTC patients. The serum CEA levels of >10 ng/mL and Ctn >500 pg/mL may reflect 7.5 lymph nodes invaded by MTC. Similarly, MTC patients with metastatic lymph nodes >20 could have serum CEA levels >50 ng/mL[ ]. It is notable that although serum CEA levels are generally elevated in most MTCs, they are usually not as high as those with gastrointestinal malignancies.

## Imaging dynamics monitoring
Postoperative surveillance of MTC necessitates precise imaging to detect residual or recurrent lesions, particularly in patients exhibiting elevated serum Ctn and CEA levels. Initial investigations include cervical ultrasonography, CT, and MRI. However, these techniques may fail to localize lesions in a significant subset of patients, prompting the need for advanced molecular imaging. PET/CT using 68 Ga-labeled somatostatin analogs (SSAs), such as 68 Ga-DOTATATE, has demonstrated efficacy in detecting neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) due to their affinity for somatostatin receptors (SSTRs)[ – ]. The diagnostic performance of these agents can vary based on the level of SSTR expression in individual tumors. Recent studies have explored the use of 68 Ga-labeled SSTR antagonists, which may offer higher tumor uptake and lower background activity compared to agonists, potentially enhancing lesion detectability. For instance, Liu et al[ ] evaluated the diagnostic performance of four 68 Ga-labeled SSTR antagonists in 549 NEN patients, finding that [68 Ga]-NODAGA-LM3 exhibiting the most favorable imaging profile, with higher tumor uptake and tumor-to-liver ratios compared to SSTR agonists. Nevertheless, the accessibility and sensitivity of 68 Ga-SSAs for detecting MTC require further improvement.
An emerging imaging modality involves targeting the fibroblast activation protein (FAP), which is overexpressed in cancer-associated fibroblasts. 68 Ga-FAP inhibitors (FAPIs) have shown higher and selective tumor uptake across various cancer types, including MTC. In MTC, FAP expression is observed in approximately 79% of tumor cells and 100% of tumor stroma, with expression levels correlating positively with lymph node metastasis[ ]. Ballal et al[ ] reported that [68 Ga] Ga-FAPI PET/CT outperformed [68 Ga] Ga-SSA PET/CT in identifying multiple organ metastases of MTC. Moreover, a recent prospective study compared [68 Ga] Ga-CTR-FAPI PET/CT with [18 F] FDG-PET/CT in 50 MTC patients[ ]. The [68 Ga] Ga-CTR-FAPI PET/CT demonstrated a higher patient-based detection rate (up to 98%) and superior diagnostic accuracy. Notably, this improved detection led to changes in clinical management for 32% of patients and alterations in surgical plans. Further advancements include the development of covalently targeted radioligand-engineered FAPIs, which exhibit greater tumor uptake and retention compared to original FAPIs[ ]. These novel agents have identified more tumor lesions in patients than previous methods, indicating their potential to enhance imaging sensitivity and influence treatment strategies. However, additional studies are necessary to validate these findings and determine their clinical applicability.

## Risk of recurrence stratification
RSS is an important tool to assess the possibility of recurrence after surgery for MTC patients. This stratification system can help doctors to develop individualized treatment plans and follow-up strategies. In the 2009 version of the ATA guidelines, hereditary MTC was classified into groups A, B, C, and D based on the risk of recurrence[ ]. In 2015, the updated ATA MTC guidelines replaced the risk categories A, B, C, and D with moderate (MOD) for the original A and B, high (H) for original C, and highest risk (HST) for the original D category, respectively[ ]. The risk designation for sporadic MTC is based on the AJCC tumor (T), node (N), and metastasis (M) categories (Fig. )[ ].

 

## Dynamic risk stratification
Dynamic risk stratification (DRS) is a method used to dynamically adjust the recurrence risk stratification for MTC patients after surgery. DRS improves prognostic outcomes by assessing responses to initial treatment and diagnostic staging. This method is particularly important for stage III or IV MTCs, who are initially treated for the same stages[ ]. The ESMO guidelines recommend DRS[ , ]. Because of its clinical significance in DTC, DRS for MTC has been developed to predict risk based on treatment response and biological tumor behavior, including changes in serum Ctn and CEA levels[ , , ]. Unlike TNM grading, DRS combines biochemical and structural responses to the initial treatment for better predicting disease-free tumor survival and recurrence risk[ ]. It provides more useful prognostic information in real time than the original TNM staging system. In 2013, Tuttle and Ganly proposed a DRS system for recurrent MTC to classify post-initial surgery patients into four categories[ ]: (a) Biochemical cure: the tumor was completely resected, and Ctn levels dropped below detectable levels; (b) anatomic cure: elevated tumor markers (Ctn and CEA) without visible lesions on imaging; (c) anatomic residual: persistent anatomic residual or distant metastasis; (d) uncertain disease status: non-specific imaging abnormalities, biochemical abnormalities, or undetectable anatomical residuals. Multivariate analysis of 71 MTCs from 39 families showed that the probability of having stage III or IV at diagnosis tripled with an increased risk from grade 1 to grade 3[ ]. A population-based dataset from China indicated that patients in the high-risk group were 7.8 times more likely to have stage III or IV at diagnosis than patients in the intermediate-risk group[ ]. In the ATA and NCCN guidelines, postoperative disease surveillance depends on serum Ctn and CEA levels.
DRS system has demonstrated significant clinical utility in managing MTC. Kwon et al[ ] showed that DRS based on the best response to initial therapy, offers superior prognostic information compared to traditional TNM staging, with a higher proportion of variance explained in predicting no evidence of disease (NED) status. Similarly, Other researchers also reported that DRS provides more accurate predictions of disease-specific mortality and recurrence in MTC patients[ , ]. These studies underscore the enhanced prognostic accuracy of DRS over static staging systems, supporting its integration into clinical practice for personalized patient management.
Long-term follow-up data from our institution further validate the clinical utility of DRS. In two representative cases managed at our institution, Ctn surveillance over 7–12 years revealed delayed recurrences despite initial biochemical remission. A 29-year-old male underwent a right lobectomy with central dissection in 2014. Postoperatively, Ctn stabilized for 3 years before rising again at Year 4, necessitating reclassification from “biochemical cure” to “anatomic residual” (Fig. A). Similarly, a 50-year-old male treated in 2009 exhibited low but detectable Ctn (13.6–38.3 pg/mL) for 9 years postoperatively. A gradual rise at Year 10 preceded ultrasonographically confirmed recurrence at Year 12, triggering therapeutic reintervention (Fig. B). These cases highlight that continuous DRS re-evaluation is critical for lifelong surveillance. We incorporated biochemical markers (Ctn/CEA kinetics), molecular profiling (RET/RAS status), and histopathologic features (DSR) into a unified stratification tool – Integrated Dynamic Risk Stratification (Fig. ). This approach may provide clinicians with practical tools for personalized MTC management, moving beyond a mere summary of existing knowledge.

 

## Targeted therapies
Current TKIs for RET mutations encompass both MKIs (e.g. vandetanib, cabozantinib, lenvatinib, anlotinib, and sorafenib)[ , – ] and RET-selective TKIs (selpercatinib and pralsetinib).

## Multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs)
Vandetanib and cabozantinib are multi-kinase TKIs primarily targeting RET and VEGFRs. Both have been approved by the FDA and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) for the advanced MTC treatment[ , , ]. Vandetanib demonstrated a median PFS of 30.5 months compared to 19.3 months with placebo in a phase III trial involving 331 patients[ ]. Common adverse events included diarrhea, nausea, rash, and hypertension. Cabozantinib, enrolled 330 MTC patients, showed a median PFS of 11.2 versus 4.0 months for placebo, with side effects like diarrhea, palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia, weight loss, and fatigue[ ]. Despite their efficacy, these MKIs have limitations due to off-targeted toxicities and resistance mutations (e.g. RET V804M/L)[ – ].
Other TKIs, such as sorafenib, lenvatinib, and anlotinib have been explored in phase II trials for advanced MTC[ , , ]. Lenvatinib, for instance, showed an ORR of 36% in a study of 59 patients, regardless of RET mutation status (NCT00784303)[ ]. However, these agents often present with notable toxicities and are generally considered for salvage therapy in patients who have progressed on other treatments.

## Highly selective RET inhibitors
Highly selective RET inhibitors (e.g. selpercatinib and pralsetinib) have been developed to achieve higher potency and decreased toxicity[ , ]. Selpercatinib exhibited an ORR of 69% in patients pre-treated with MKIs and 73% in treatment-naïve cases in the LIBRETTO-001 trial[ ]. The LIBRETTO-531 phase III trial (NCT04211337) further demonstrated its superiority over vandetanib or cabozantinib, with a 12-month PFS of 86.8% compared to 65.7% in the control group[ , ]. Pralsetinib, evaluated in the ARROW trial (NCT03037385), showed an ORR of 60% in pre-treated patients and 71% in treatment-naïve individuals[ ]. However, its development faced strategic setbacks, leading to the withdrawal of its indication for advanced RET-positive MTC in the U.S.
Emerging selective RET inhibitors, such as SY-5007, also showed promising results in early-phase trial[ ]. Clinically, SY-5007 achieved an ORR of 52.2% in MTC and exhibited superior tolerability compared to selpercatinib and pralsetinib. Ongoing clinical trials are also evaluating other RET inhibitors, including TPX-0046 (NCT04161391), TAS0953/HM06 (NCT04683250), and BOS-172738 (NCT03780517).

## Resistance to RET-target therapy
On-target resistance mechanisms involve the secondary mutations in the RET kinase domain, such as solvent front mutations at G810 the residue (e.g. G810S, G810R), which hinders compound binding[ – ]. Off-target resistance can occur through activating downstream signaling pathways, including MET amplification or mutation in downstream effectors like KRAS and BRAF[ , ]. Understanding resistance mechanisms is essential for developing next-generation inhibitors and combination therapies.

## MKI in non-RET mutation
A subset of MTC patients lacks RET mutations, necessitating alternative therapeutic approaches. MKIs have shown limited efficacy in this population[ ]. Vandetanib achieved an ORR of 9.5% in RET-negative patients compared to 39.7% in RET-positive patients in the phase III OBS14778 trial (NCT01945762). Cabozantinib demonstrated similar ORRs in both RET-positive and RET-negative subgroups (32% vs. 25%), with a significant PFS benefit over placebo[ ]. Recent molecular analyses have identified mutations in the MAPK pathway in RET-negative MTC, suggesting potential targets for therapy[ ]. Additionally, these tumors may exhibit higher expression of immune-related markers, indicating a possible role for immunotherapy. Further research is needed to verify these findings and develop effective treatments for this patient population.

## Other approaches
Immune profiling has revealed overexpression of immune checkpoint molecules in MTC. Notably, CD276 (B7-H3) expression is significantly higher in MTC cells compared to normal tissues, indicating its potential as a therapeutic target[ ]. Enoblituzumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting CD276, has shown promise in preclinical studies and is under investigation for its efficacy in MTC treatment[ ]. Furthermore, co-expression of immune co-inhibitory receptors has been observed in MTC tissues. A study involving 200 MTC patients demonstrated that expression of these receptors correlates with an increased risk of tumor recurrence, suggesting that therapies targeting these checkpoints may be beneficial[ ]. However, clinical trials are necessary to validate the efficacy and safety of these immunotherapeutic approaches in MTC patients.
External beam radiotherapy (EBRT) has been considered for patients with advanced MTC, especially when surgical options are limited. A retrospective analysis of 2046 MTC patients from the SEER database found that EBRT did not significantly improve overall or disease-specific survival rates[ ]. Therefore, the decision to employ EBRT should be individualized, weighing potential benefits against the risks and considering patient-specific factors.
Chemotherapy in MTC remains limited. Traditional agents, such as 5-fluorouracil and dacarbazine, have been used with modest success. A clinical study on the treatment of 20 MTCs with the combination of adriamycin, 5-fluorouracil, and dacarbazine achieved partial remission in 3 cases and stabilization in 10 cases[ ]. However, the overall efficacy of chemotherapeutic agents in MTC is limited, and their use is generally reserved for cases where other treatments are ineffective or unavailable[ ].

## Challenges

## Challenge 1 management of biochemical relapses
Elevated serum Ctn levels occur postoperatively in some MTC patients without imaging abnormalities, a condition known as biochemical recurrence. There is no reliable threshold for serum Ctn levels to diagnose MTC recurrence due to variations in testing methods across different laboratories and medical facilities. If the post-operative serum Ctn is consistently >10 ng/L, a residual lesion is suspected[ , , ]. Unfortunately, imaging examinations usually do not reveal any abnormalities for this subgroup of patients, and there is no reliable clinical evidence to determine whether continual observation is safe and effective. Further investigation is warranted to identify potential recurrence foci by genetic screening and nuclear medicine for MTC patients experiencing biochemical recurrence.

## Challenge 2 indications and scope of cervical lymph node dissection
The extent and indications for LCLND are controversial. The approach to LCLND can differ between sporadic and familial MTC. Although prophylactic LCLND can eradicate underlying lesions, it also increases costs and complications for patients[ , ]. Therefore, further randomized trials are urgently needed to demonstrate the importance of prophylactic cervical lymph node dissection in the treatment of MTC.

## Challenge 3 alternatives for refractory MTCs
Although various targeted therapies, such as TKI inhibitors and RET inhibitors, are now available for treating advanced MTCs, clinical applications have shown that some patients do not respond to these agents[ , ]. For this group, combination therapies targeting immunosuppressive pathways have shown some efficacy in other tumor types but require validation for MTC[ ]. For the non-RET mutation advanced MTCs, current MKIs have shown limited clinical efficacy and many significant adverse effects remain exist due to off-target effects, leading to poor quality of life. Thus, there are still unmet needs for these cohorts. Additionally, the potential of other neoadjuvant therapeutic modalities, such as immunotherapy, cancer vaccines, gene therapy, inhibitory factors, and targeted radioimmunotherapy should be investigated in more clinical trials for MTC.

## Conclusion
MTC remains a formidable challenge in the field of thyroid cancer due to its rarity, aggressive nature, and the complexity of its management. Early diagnosis and prompt options are critical to improve patient outcomes. This article reviews recent advancements in treatment modalities, including surgical intervention, targeted therapy, postoperative surveillance, and future challenges. While total thyroidectomy is the cornerstone of MTC treatment, the extent of prophylactic LCLND remains debated. Postoperative follow-up for MTC primarily involves regularly monitoring serum Ctn /CEA, alongside imaging modalities to detect recurrence (Fig. ).

 RET inhibitor-related acquired resistance was a significant challenge, necessitating the exploration of novel agents and combination treatments to improve efficacy and limit the off-target toxicities. Persist challenges in MTC treatment include accurately predicting recurrence, determining the appropriate extent of surgery, exploring immunotherapy options, and managing locally advanced unresectable or metastatic MTC. These challenges underscore the significant gaps in the understanding and management of MTC.
