## Abstract

## PURPOSE
We analyzed the oncogenic potential of RET Δ898-901 mutant and its response to selpercatinib, vandetanib, and cabozantinib in vitro and in a clinical case.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS
A 35-year-old man with a medullary thyroid cancer (MTC) harboring a somatic D898_E901 RET deletion was sequentially treated with vandetanib, selpercatinib, cabozantinib, and fluorouracil (5-FU)-dacarbazine. Functional study of RET Δ898-901 mutant was performed in HEK-293T, NIH-3T3, and Ba/F3 cells. RET C634R and wild-type cells served as positive and negative controls, respectively.

## RESULTS
The patient showed primary resistance to vandetanib and secondary resistance to selpercatinib after 12 months. Comprehensive next-generation sequencing of a progressing lesion during selpercatinib showed no additional RET mutation but an acquired complete genetic loss of CDKN2A, CDKN2B, and MTAP genes. Subsequent treatment with cabozantinib and 5-FU-dacarbazine had poor efficacy. In vitro, RET Δ898-901 showed higher ligand-independent RET autophosphorylation compared with RET C634R and similar proliferation rates in cell models. Subcutaneous injection of Δ898-901 NIH 3T3 cells in nude mice produced tumors of around 500 mm3 in 2 weeks, similarly to RET C634R cells. Selpercatinib inhibited cell growth of Ba/F3 RET Δ898-901 and RET C634R with a similar half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of approximately 3 nM. Vandetanib was five-fold less effective at inhibiting cell growth promoted by RET Δ898-901 mutant (IC50, 564 nM) compared with RET C634R one (IC50, 91 nM). Cabozantinib efficiently inhibited Ba/F3 RET C634 proliferation (IC50, 25.9 nM), but was scarcely active in Ba/F3 RET 898-901 (IC50 > 1,350 nM).

## CONCLUSION
D898_E901 RET deletion is a gain-of-function mutation and responds to tyrosine kinase inhibitors in MTC. RET Δ898-901 mutant is sensitive to selpercatinib and vandetanib, and acquired resistance to selpercatinib may develop via RET-independent mechanisms.

RET 898_901 indel in medullary thyroid cancer is oncogenic and responds to selpercatinib, but resistance may develop RET-independently.

## INTRODUCTION
Medullary thyroid cancer (MTC) derives from parafollicular thyroid cells and accounts for 1%-2% of all thyroid cancers. MTC is sporadic in approximately 75% of patients, whereas the rest of cases occurs within the multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 (MEN2) A and B hereditary syndromes. The development of MTCs is strongly related to alterations in RET proto-oncogene, a somatic mutation being found in up to 50% of patients with sporadic MTC and a germline mutation in almost all patients with MEN2. 

## CONTEXT

Key Objective

Medullary thyroid cancer (MTC) is a rare aggressive thyroid cancer driven by RET mutations in about 50% of sporadic cases. In this study, we aimed to assess the oncogenic potential of the RET Δ898-901 mutant and its response to selpercatinib, vandetanib, and cabozantinib.

Knowledge Generated

D898_E901 RET in-frame deletion is a gain-of-function mutation that is associated with a clinical aggressive behavior of MTC. RET Δ898-901 mutant is highly sensitive to RET-selective tyrosine kinase inhibitors. Acquired resistance to selpercatinib may develop via RET-independent mechanisms.

Relevance

The characterization of the oncogenic potential of RET Δ898-901 mutant and its different response to selective tyrosine kinase inhibitors allows to customize the optimal treatment strategy for affected patients.

About 90% of RET mutations in MTC are missense substitutions. Among these, the most frequent are the M918T, found in 50%-80% of sporadic RET-altered MTC and in >95% of MEN2B, and the C634R, found in up to 40% of MEN2A. The oncogenic potential of most MTC-associated RET mutations has been characterized and a genotype-phenotype correlation has been observed. On the contrary, in about 10%-15% of cases, MTC harbors rarer gene alterations, for many of which there is limited knowledge about their oncogenic potential and clinical significance. Moreover, despite most RET mutations are sensitive to multikinase inhibitors (MKI) such as vandetanib and cabozantinib, some mutations are resistant to MKI (eg, at V804 residue) but respond to RET-selective inhibitors such as selpercatinib and pralsetinib. 
In this study, we functionally analyzed the oncogenic potential of the D898_E901 in-frame RET deletion, a rare gene mutation first identified by Uchino et al. We tested the response of the RET Δ898-901 mutant to vandetanib, selpercatinib, and cabozantinib in vitro, and reported the case of a 35-year-old man with a locally advanced sporadic MTC harboring a D898_E901 RET deletion. The patient was treated sequentially with vandetanib, selpercatinib, and cabozantinib. The analysis of the tumor for the presence of additional mutations during progression under selpercatinib revealed no further mutation in RET, but new homozygous loss of CDKN2A, CDKN2B, and MTAP genes.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## RET Gene Sequencing
Somatic RET testing in the primary tumor was accomplished by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification and direct sequencing of the entire coding sequence of exons 5, 8, 10, 11, and 13-16, including the exon-intron junctions (reference sequence: LRG_518t1), using DNA extracted from a paraffin-embedded tumor tissue sample.
The FoundationOne assay was performed on paraffin-embedded samples from primary tumor and a progressing lesion during selpercatinib treatment. In brief, FoundationOne is a next-generation sequencing (NGS) analysis that identifies substitutions, insertions, deletions, and copy-number alterations in 324 cancer-related genes, as well as introns of 36 genes involved in rearrangements, microsatellite instability, and tumor mutational burden.

## Compounds
Vandetanib, selpercatinib, and cabozantinib were purchased from Selleckchem (Houston, TX). For cell treatment, compounds were dissolved at a concentration of 50 mM in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and stored at −80°C. Final dosing solution was freshly prepared for each experiment from the stock solution; the equivalent amount of vehicle (DMSO) was used as control.

## Cell Cultures
Human HEK 293T cells were from American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA) and were grown in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; GIBCO; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Transient transfections of wild-type RET, RET C634R, and RET Δ898-901 vectors (all coding the short RET-9 isoform) were carried out with the FuGENE reagent according to manufacturer's instructions (Promega, Madison, WI). Ba/F3 murine pro-B cells stably expressing RET C634R and RET Δ898-901–mutant proteins (in the long RET-51 isoform) were generated by electroporation. Parental and transformed Ba/F3 cells were cultured in RPMI supplemented with 10% FBS (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA), supplemented with 10 ng/mL interleukin-3 (IL-3) in the case of parental cells (Peprotech, London, UK). Mouse NIH3T3 fibroblasts stably transfected with RET C634R– or RET Δ898-901–expressing vector were generated by transfection with electroporation and were grown in DMEM supplemented with 5% calf serum (CS) (GIBCO).

## Growth Curves and Cell Viability Assay
RET C634R– and RET Δ898-901–transformed NIH3T3 cells were seeded in 6-well plates (10,000/well). NIH3T3 transfectants were grown in 2.5% CS. Cells were counted in triplicate every 2 days. RET C634R– and RET Δ898-901–transformed Ba/F3 were seeded in 6-well plates (300,000/well). The day after plating, cells were counted, and various concentrations of drug or vehicle were added to the medium. Cells were counted in triplicate daily for 4 consecutive days and the number of cells relative to the last day of the experiment was used to calculate growth inhibition. Half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) doses for cell growth were calculated through a curve fitting analysis from last day of growth curves using the Prism software (GraphPad Software Inc, Boston, MA).

## Plasmid Generation
Plasmid RET C634R was previously described. To generate the RET Δ898-901–expressing plasmid, we used recombinant PCR to delete 12 nucleotides (TGTTTATGAAGA) into the RET9 coding sequence. Briefly, the two PCR products, CAA​ATT​GTA​TGG​GGC​CTG​CA/ACG​TAG​GAA​TCC​TCT​CGG​GAC​AAG​CCG and CTT​GTC​CCG​AGA​GGA​TTC​CTA​CGT/GGC​TTG​TAC​TCG​GTC​ATG​GT, were combined by PCR using CAA​ATT​GTA​TGG​GGC​CTG​CA/GGC​TTG​TAC​TCG​GTC​ATG​GT as outside oligonucleotides. The final PCR product was inserted into BglII/SfiI restriction sites of the pBABE-RET9 expression vector. Successful mutation introduction was verified by Sanger sequencing.

## Protein Studies
Anti-RET (14698), anti–phospho RET Y905 (3221), anti–phospho MAPK (Thr202/Tyr204; 4370), anti–p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2; 4695), anti-MEK (9122), and anti–phospho MEK (9121) antibodies were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA). Anti–phospho RET Y1062 (ab51103) and anti–phospho RET Y1015 (ab74154) were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Anti–phospho tyrosine, clone 4G10 (05-321) was from Merck Millipore (Burlington, MA). Secondary antibodies coupled to horseradish peroxidase were purchased from Bio-Rad (Hercules, CA).

## Mouse Experiments
Animal studies were conducted at the Dipartimento di Medicina Molecolare e Biotecnologie Mediche in accordance with Italian regulations for experimentation on animals. The study was approved by the Italian Ministry of Health (authorization no. 1023/2015-PR). Female BALB/c nu/nu mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratories (Wilmington, MA). All manipulations were performed while the animals were under isoflurane gas anesthesia. No mouse showed signs of wasting or other signs of toxicity. Four mice were inoculated subcutaneously into both flanks with 2 × 105 RET C634R– or RET Δ898-901–transformed NIH3T3 (two mice for each cell line). After 14 days, tumors were measured by calypter. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation; tumors were excised and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Frozen tumors were then homogenized in lysis buffer by using the Mixer Mill MM300 (Qiagen, Hilden, DE). Samples were clarified twice by centrifugation at 10,000×g. Protein phosphorylation was assayed according to standard Western blot procedures.

## RESULTS

## Case Presentation
In February 2018, a 35-year-old man presented to our University Hospital with a locally advanced, sporadic MTC. A neck ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration revealed a MTC with multiple lymph node metastases. The patient discovered the disease by self-examination of cervical lymph nodes. He had no constitutional symptoms, serum calcitonin (Ct) was 1,570 pg/mL, and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) was 28.2 ng/mL. A computed tomography (CT) scan with contrast did not disclose distant metastases in brain, lungs, liver, or bones. He had no family history, and no germline RET mutation was found. In May and July 2018, the patient was submitted to a two-step neck surgery: the removal of a 5-cm MTC, extensively angioinvasive, was macroscopically incomplete; a central and bilateral lymph node dissection removed 26 metastatic lymph nodes (the largest of 4 cm in diameter) of 79. The disease was classified T4N1bM0. Somatic RET testing in the primary tumor tissue revealed the presence of the D898_E901 in-frame RET deletion (RET Δ898-901).
One month later, a postsurgery CT scan revealed large bilateral MTC remnants in the thyroid bed and multiple neck and mediastinal large lymph node metastases. At that time, serum Ct and CEA were 995 pg/mL and 28.7 ng/mL, respectively. Given the burden of disease, in October 2018, the patient started a systemic treatment with vandetanib 300 mg once daily. Grade 1-2 adverse events occurred (nausea, fatigue, and folliculitis). A CT scan performed 2 months later showed progressive disease with a 23% increase according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors v. 1.1 compared with baseline. Vandetanib was suspended, and the patient received external-beam radiotherapy to bilateral neck and upper mediastinum for a total dose of 66 Gy.
The patient was then followed up with serial CT scans until September 2020, when the disease progressed in the neck and three metastases were discovered in the liver (Figs A and B). Serum Ct was 581 pg/mL and CEA 31.2 ng/mL. The patient was treated with selpercatinib 160 mg twice daily. Two weeks later, the serum Ct and CEA dropped to 4.8 pg/mL and 19.4 ng/mL, respectively. The best tumor response was reached after 4 months, with a 61% reduction in the sum of tumor diameters of target lesions (Figs C and D). The only adverse event was a G2 hypertension. Unfortunately, 1 year after initiation of selpercatinib treatment, the tumor rapidly progressed with a 62% increase in the sum of tumor diameters compared with the best tumor response (Fig ). The patient was treated with cabozantinib 140 mg once daily with a stable disease for about 4 months, but then the tumor rapidly progressed. A cytotoxic chemotherapy regimen (fluorouracil-dacarbazine) was given but the disease continued to progress, and the patient died 6 months later in September 2022.
 To investigate the comprehensive genomic profile of the MTC in this patient, we performed an NGS analysis of the primary tumor on the basis of the FoundationOne assay. The only gene alteration reported was the D989_E901 in-frame deletion in exon 15 of the RET gene. Microsatellite status was stable, and the tumor mutational burden was of five mutations-per-megabase. We repeated the analysis on a progressing neck tumor focus during selpercatinib treatment. This second NGS analysis showed three new gene alterations, namely, homozygous loss of CDKN2A, homozygous loss of CDKN2B, and homozygous loss of MTAP, without any further mutation in the RET gene. Microsatellite status was stable, and the tumor mutational burden was of 6.30 mutations-per-megabase.

## Functional Characterization of RET Δ898-901–Mutant Protein
The oncogenic potential of the identified Δ898-901 RET deletion was only partially known. To explore whether Δ898-901 deletion was able to activate RET autophosphorylation in a ligand-independent manner, we transiently expressed RET Δ898-901–mutant protein in HEK 293T cells. As shown in Figure A, phosphorylation on canonical RET autophosphorylation sites, Y1062, Y1015, and Y905, as well as on total tyrosine residues was strongly enhanced in RET Δ898-901 compared with wild-type RET. Interestingly, phosphorylation of RET Δ898-901 was increased also with respect to the classical MEN2-associated RET C634R mutant. Consistently, stable expression of RET Δ898-901 mutant was able to promote NIH 3T3 murine fibroblast proliferation in low serum (Fig B) and IL-3–indedendent proliferation of Ba/F3 pro-B lymphocytes (Figs A and B). In addition, when injected subcutaneously in immunodeficient mice, NIH 3T3 cells expressing RET Δ898-901 were able to form tumors of around 500 mm3 in 2 weeks, similarly to fibroblasts expressing RET C634R oncogenic mutant, and these tumors expressed phosphorylated RET (Figs C and D).
 

## Sensitivity of RET Δ898-901–Mutant Protein to Vandetanib, Selpercatinib, and Cabozantinib
To test RET Δ898-901 sensitivity to the RET inhibitors selpercatinib and vandetanib, we measured drug IC50 doses in Ba/F3 RET Δ898-901 and Ba/F3 RET C634R. As shown in Figure A, selpercatinib was able to inhibit cell proliferation of both Ba/F3 RET Δ898-901 and Ba/F3 RET C634R cells very efficiently, with an IC50 dose of around 3 nM for both cell lines. Thus, RET Δ898-901 and RET C634R phosphorylation were equally inhibited by selpercatinib in these cells (Fig B). Vandetanib was around five-fold less effective at inhibiting cell growth promoted by RET Δ898-901 mutant (IC50, 564 nM) compared with RET C634R one (IC50, 91 nM; Fig C). Consistently, vandetanib was slightly less effective at inhibiting RET Δ898-901 protein phosphorylation compared with RET C634R one (Fig D). Similarly, we also tested RET Δ898-901 sensitivity to cabozantinib. As shown in Figure E, although cabozantinib was able to efficiently inhibit Ba/F3 RET C634 proliferation (IC50, 25.9 nM), it was scarcely active in Ba/F3 RET Δ898-901 (IC50 > 1,350 nM). Consistently, cabozantinib was able to effectively reduce RET C634 phosphorylation on Y1062 at 30 nM, while it did not have any effect on RET Δ898-901 phosphorylation to 1,000 nM (Fig F).

## DISCUSSION
The D898_E901 in-frame 12-bp deletion in exon 15 of RET is an uncommon genetic alteration in MTC. This deletion, that has been first identified by Uchino et al, lies within the catalytic loop of the RET protein. It was then described in one case report, in two of 69 RET-positive MTC patients from an Italian multicenter study, in two of 20 RET-positive patients from the University Hospital of Padua, and in eight of 178 RET-positive patients from the University Hospital of Pisa series. All these cases were somatic alterations. One patient with a MTC harboring the D898_E901 RET deletion was treated in phase I trial of cabozantinib and achieved a stable disease. Finally, three patients were treated in phase I-II trial of selpercatinib (in two of 143 cases) and pralsetinib (in one of 84 cases). However, data on the efficacy of selpercatinib and pralsetinib in these few patients are not available, and there are no clinical data in terms of aggressiveness and outcome of such deletion.
Here, we demonstrated that D898_E901 RET deletion is a gain-of-function mutation able to drive the development of tumors in vivo. RET D898-E901 autophosphorylation in cells was stronger than RET C634R one, suggesting a potent activation of the kinase activity for the deletion mutant. Moreover, we showed that RET Δ898-901–mutant protein responds to the kinase inhibitors vandetanib and selpercatinib. In our patient, MTC presented with an aggressive behavior at diagnosis and during the course of the disease showed global resistance to treatment with vandetanib. However, our data from preclinical models indicated that RET D898-E901 protein is still sensitive to vandetanib, even if slightly less than RET C634R mutant. Thereby, the resistance to vandetanib observed in the patient can be attributed to multiple reasons, such as a stronger kinase activity compared with common RET mutations such as RET C634R, a partial reduced sensitivity of this mutant to the drug, but also patient-specific pharmacokinetics and drug distribution. Albeit for a short amount of time, our patient achieved a stable disease with cabozantinib, as occurred to the single patient with RET D898_E901–mutated MTC treated within the cabozantinib phase I trial. In view of our results showing very limited sensitivity of RET D898_E901 to cabozantinib in vitro (Figs E and F), the tumor response of the presented patient to cabozantinib might indicate that cabozantinib acted via a RET-independent mechanism, possibly through its antiangiogenic activity.
Significantly, NGS analysis showed no mutation other than the RET D898_E901 deletion in the primary tumor, when primary resistance to vandetanib occurred. Selpercatinib showed instead a powerful inhibitory effect in the clinical context albeit for only 12 months, after which the tumor rapidly progressed with a marked increase in serum Ct (from 22 to 402 pg/mL in 3 weeks) and in tumor size. Mechanisms of resistance to selective RET inhibitors such as selpercatinib include RET G810 solvent front and Y806C/N hinge mutations, RET V738A mutation at the β2 strand, MET amplifications, and non–RET-driven MAPK reactivation. In our case, the tumor secondary resistance to selpercatinib was not due to an acquired mutation in RET, as excluded by a comprehensive NGS sequencing (including all RET exons) of a progressing neck lesion. Notably, this NGS analysis reported acquired homozygous loss of CDKN2A, CDKN2B, and MTAP, which were intact in the naïve primary tumor tissue. CDKN2A encodes the p16 and p14 tumor suppressor proteins that by blocking the activity of CDK4/6 and MDM2, respectively, can enhance pRB and p53 function. Similarly, CDKN2B encodes the p15 tumor suppressor that also inhibits CDK4/6 and promotes block of cell proliferation. CDKN2A lies adjacent to CDKN2B genes on chromosome 9p21.3, in a region that is frequently deleted in tumors. Several evidence in the literature demonstrate that inactivation of pRB and p53 pathways in living animals promote MTC formation. , Therefore, simultaneous inactivation of RB and p53 pathways is likely to contribute to cancer progression and may render RET inhibition less effective in restricting cancer growth. In this context, CDKN2A loss was also reported in four cases of RET fusion-positive non–small-cell lung cancer with acquired resistance to RET-selective inhibitors in combination with other oncogenic mutations. Interestingly, CDKN2A and CDKN2B deletion is the only genetic additional change found in this specific patient upon progression on selpercatinib. Overall, these data may reinforce the notion that acquired resistance to RET-selective inhibitors can occur via RET-independent mechanisms, which require alternative clinical treatments not targeting the RET protein. Chromosome 9p21.3 deletion involves also the MTAP gene but there is no evidence that MTAP acts as a cancer driver.
In conclusion, we demonstrated that the D898_E901 RET deletion in MTC is a gain-of-function mutation that confers a clinical aggressive behavior to MTC and responds to tyrosine kinase inhibitors. Our study showed that RET Δ898-901 mutant brilliantly responded to selpercatinib, albeit a RET-independent acquired treatment resistance appeared 1 year after treatment initiation.
