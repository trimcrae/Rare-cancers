## Abstract
Patient‐reported symptoms and functioning are valuable to understanding the tolerability of cancer treatments in clinical trials. Assessment of key side effects, physical and role functioning, and overall side effect bother in trials may be helpful for benefit–risk evaluation and useful to communicate in product labeling. Inclusion of these assessments has been supported in the literature and Food and Drug Administration guidance documents. In this article, the authors provide their perspective on the evolution of how this data has been considered in oncologic therapeutic evaluation and provide context for the research being presented by the National Cancer Institute Tolerability Consortium.
Keywords: tolerability, patient‐reported outcomes, cancer treatment, clinical outcome assessments, side effects

## Short abstract
Patient‐reported symptoms and functioning are valuable to understanding the tolerability of cancer treatments in clinical trials. The National Cancer Institute Tolerability Consortium’s work has advanced methods to collect and analyze this information.

In addition to experiencing the symptomatic and functional impairments of living with cancer, patients are faced with the side effects of cancer treatment. As the efficacy of cancer treatments improves, , patients still face the prospect of significant side effects. The past decade has seen growth of novel cancer treatments with unique mechanisms of action, including targeted therapies, immune checkpoint inhibitors, cell‐based therapies, and antibody drug conjugates, each with their own unique side effect profiles. The paradigm of administering intravenous cytotoxic chemotherapy for a fixed duration has given way to extended treatment with oral or subcutaneously administered agents, usually as an outpatient with varying direct interaction with clinic staff. With more treatment regimens available, patients are usually offered many sequential lines of therapy over the course of cancer treatment, and long‐term toxicities from prior lines may persist for years.
Over the past decade, the definition of tolerability in the context of cancer treatment has evolved. In 2018, a multistakeholder group proposed a revised definition of tolerability as “the degree to which symptomatic and non‐symptomatic adverse events associated with the product’s administration affect the ability or desire of the patient to adhere to the dose or intensity of therapy. A complete understanding of tolerability should include direct measurement from the patient on how they are feeling and functioning while on treatment.” Along with this revised definition, multiple groups, including the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and National Cancer Institute (NCI), have advocated for a concerted effort to measure symptoms and function by patient self‐report in cancer clinical trials and to investigate methods on how these data can be informative to both patients and healthcare providers. In addition to understanding the onset, trajectory, and impact of side effects on patients’ ability to perform day‐to‐day activities, thorough assessment of tolerability provides valuable information that may affect patient outcomes. For example, diminished treatment tolerability may preclude patients’ ability to continue with an effective cancer treatment. This, in turn, could impact outcomes such as progression‐free survival and overall survival in addition to worsening health related quality of life. , 

Since inception, the FDA’s Oncology Center of Excellence (OCE) has made significant strides in encouraging rigorous assessment of patient‐reported symptom and functional status data to inform our understanding of treatment tolerability in cancer clinical trials. OCE has provided guidance on how to assess these types of data, including thoughtful integration of patient‐reported outcome (PRO) endpoints in cancer clinical trials. This includes the measurement of anticipated symptomatic adverse events using a parsimonious selection of items from well‐established item libraries, as well as a global measure of overall side effect impact or side effect bother. Assessing overall side effect impact/bother captures two important concepts that self‐reporting of individual symptom severity or interference may not: the cumulative effects of multiple low‐grade symptoms and the impact of unforeseen side effects that were not captured by clinician or patient reporting. This thinking is outlined in the FDA’s Core Patient‐Reported Outcomes in Cancer Clinical Trials guidance, finalized in October 2024, and serves as an oncology‐specific complement to the FDA Patient‐Focused Drug Development guidance series. 

FDA has hosted many workshops focusing on the tolerability of cancer therapy. In 2017, OCE held the Second Annual Clinical Outcomes Assessment in Cancer Clinical Trials (COA‐CCT) Workshop with the Critical Path Institute to specifically discuss ways to effectively collect PROs. This meeting discussed early experiences with the NCI’s Patient‐Reported Outcomes version of the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (PRO‐CTCAE) Item Library soon after its public release in 2015. Conclusions from this workshop include the value of collecting PROs in cancer clinical trials to complement clinician reporting of adverse events. In 2023, the COA‐CCT workshop focused on overall side effect impact as a core PRO, including discussion of how comparative tolerability endpoints could be included in cancer clinical trials. In the 2024 COA‐CCT workshop, FDA and NCI moderators revisited granular measurement of patient‐reported side effects. Despite improvements in the PRO‐CTCAE to expand symptom lists and language availability, deployment of the measure and selection of individual symptoms remain inconsistent. This workshop highlighted ways to select symptoms using information about the mechanism of action and potential symptomatic adverse events that are of high importance to patients and health care providers. Many of the speakers and panelists at COA‐CCT workshops are multidisciplinary researchers who have participated in the NCI Tolerability Consortium, and publicly available workshop materials include results from NCI‐funded Tolerability Consortium research.
High‐quality patient‐reported tolerability data has directly impacted the FDA’s benefit–risk evaluation for drug approvals in oncology, and PRO data are increasingly included in oncology product labels. For example, FDA approval of belzutifan for the treatment of renal cell carcinoma was based on the results of the LITESPARK‐005 trial. More hypoxia was observed in patients treated with belzutifan; however, patient‐reported dyspnea was less than for those receiving everolimus, providing more information than clinician‐reported safety data alone. Another recent example is the approval of selpercatinib for patients with advanced or metastatic RET‐mutant medullary thyroid cancer, where the LIBRETTO‐531 trial included weekly assessment of overall side effect bother. PRO data revealed that, across the course of the trial, patients treated with selpercatinib had fewer weeks with high side effect bother (defined as response of 3 “quite a bit” or 4 “very much”) as measured by the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy item GP5 than those receiving cabozantinib or vandetanib. Recently, inavolisib was approved in combination with palbociclib and fulvestrant for the treatment of adults with endocrine‐resistant, PIK3CA‐mutated, hormone receptor–positive, HER2‐negative locally advanced or metastatic breast cancer. In the INAVO120 trial, expected toxicities were selected from the PRO‐CTCAE Item Library along with an overall side effect bother item, and the results are described in addition to clinician‐reported safety data in Section 6 of the US Product Information. The PRO data from INAVO120 provide valuable additional detail, particularly for the symptoms of mouth sores and diarrhea. These examples demonstrate the utility of rigorously assessed patient‐reported tolerability data to complement clinician reporting of safety and tolerability and inform the FDA’s benefit–risk evaluation. The substantive increase in the relevance and quality of patient‐reported tolerability data is the result of dedicated research in this area catalyzed by regulatory interest and industry commitment to assessing the patient experience.
The FDA OCE and NCI have a shared history of dedication to advancing high‐quality patient‐generated health data over the past decade. , One of the earliest programs established within the FDA OCE was the oncology patient‐focused drug development program with the express goal of identifying rigorous methods to assess the patient experience with cancer and its treatment. The NCI Tolerability Consortium provided an opportunity for academic researchers to explore the collection, analysis, and communication of patient‐generated health data to enhance assessment of tolerability. The FDA OCE has served as a willing partner in the NCI Tolerability Consortium, with the mutual goal of improving the lives of patients being treated for cancer by applying sound methods to assess and communicate this valuable information in the clinical trial context.
Despite recent advancements, there is a need for improvement in the tolerability assessment of cancer treatments using PRO information. In this issue, each of the NCI Tolerability Consortium grant teams will share their research, describing how their work advances collection of patient‐generated health data to inform determinations of the tolerability of cancer treatments. We applaud the research teams and NCI scientific leaders for their commitment to advancing the assessment of cancer treatment tolerability and the methodologies that strengthen the measurement, analysis, and interpretation of patient‐generated health data. For our regulatory work, we hope this research enhances the type and quality of data being collected in cancer clinical trials. As we elucidated previously, we have seen a marked improvement in PRO data submitted to FDA, and this information can have a powerful impact toward the benefit–risk evaluation of oncology products. Indeed, with an increase in available therapies across cancer types, similarly efficacious agents with improved tolerability are an important opportunity for cancer drug and biologic development. In the end, an accurate understanding of cancer treatment tolerability provides essential data to inform treatment decision‐making by those who need it most: patients, caregivers, and health care providers.
