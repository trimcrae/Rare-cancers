## Abstract

## Background
The receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK)/Ras/Raf/MEK/ERK signaling pathway is one of the most tumorigenic pathways in cancer, with its hyperactivation strongly linked to the aggressive nature of glioblastoma (GBM). Although extensive research has focused on developing therapeutics targeting this pathway, clinical success remains elusive due to the emergence of resistance mechanisms.

## Objective
This review investigates how inhibition of the RTK/Ras/Raf/MEK/ERK pathway alters transcription factors, contributing to acquired resistance mechanisms in GBM. It also highlights the critical role of transcription factor dysregulation in therapeutic resistance.

## Methods & Results
Findings from key studies on the RTK/Ras/Raf/MEK/ERK pathway in GBM were synthesized to explore the role of transcription factor dysregulation in resistance to targeted therapies, radiation, and chemotherapy. The review highlights that transcription factors undergo significant dysregulation following RTK/Ras/Raf/MEK/ERK pathway inhibition, contributing to therapeutic resistance.

## Conclusion
Transcription factors are promising targets for overcoming treatment resistance in GBM, with cotreatment strategies combining RTK/Ras/Raf/MEK/ERK pathway inhibitors and transcription factor-targeted therapies presenting a novel approach. Despite the challenges of targeting complex structures and interactions, advancements in drug development and precision technologies hold great potential. Continued research is essential to refine these strategies and improve outcomes for GBM and other aggressive cancers.

Keywords: Glioblastoma, RTK/Raf/MEK/ERK inhibitors, Resistance mechanisms, Transcription factors

## Introduction
IDH wild-type glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common and lethal primary brain tumour, primarily due to challenges in achieving gross total resection and the presence of highly invasive cells that contribute to resistance to standard of care radiation and chemotherapy [ ]. Despite a highly heterogeneous oncogenetic profile, aberrant kinase signalling is nearly a universal feature of GBM [ , ]. In part, the aggressive nature of GBM and other lethal tumors has been attributed to the hyperactivation of receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) and its downstream signalling pathways, including the Ras/Raf/MEK/Extracellular signal regulated kinase (ERK) activation [ – ]. This signalling cascade directly couples signals from cell surface receptors to transcription factors that modulate gene expression. Following RTK activation, ERK enters the nucleus to regulate the activity of various transcription factors (both repressors and activators) which in turn modulate a variety of cellular functions including those implicated in tumorigenesis such as apoptosis, cell cycle progression, cell migration, differentiation, metabolism, and proliferation [ ] (Fig.  ). Aberrant activation of the RTK/Raf/Ras/MEK/ERK signaling pathway, often driven by mutations such as EGFR tyrosine kinase alterations in GBM disrupts key transcription factors and the critical biological processes they regulation [ , ] (Fig.  ). Consequently, this pathway has been extensively studied in an effort to identify potential therapeutic targets in cancer [ ]. While targeting various components of this pathway pharmacologically demonstrated encouraging results in both pre-clinical and clinical studies, acquired drug resistance has emerged as a major challenge in this therapeutic approach [ – ]. Various mechanisms of resistance have been described [ , ] and a large proportion of these mechanisms are associated with reactivation of the RTK/Raf/Ras/MEK/ERK pathway or the activation of alternative kinase signalling pathways. Additional mechanisms are coming to light including modifications in the tumour microenvironment, cell death resistance mechanisms, tumour metabolism and epigenetic modifications [ – ]. In this review, we highlight how dysregulation of transcription factors, caused by inhibition of the RTK/Raf/MEK/ERK pathway, contribute to resistance against these inhibitors. We also explore how their dysregulations can lead to resistance to radiation and chemotherapy in GBM.
 Transcription factors, essential for regulating gene expression and orchestrating biological processes, act as master regulators, integrating upstream signals to determine cellular behavior and fate. The intracellular signalling pathways that drive the action of transcription factors are complex, concomitant, and often overlapping. Here, we focus on several key transcription factors extensively studied in the GBM literature, including CIC, YAP/TAZ, c-MYC, STAT3, p53, AP-1, HIF-1α, and GLI1. The dysregulation of these factors has been shown to contribute to resistance against these inhibitors (see Fig.  and Table  ). Recent advancements in genomic technologies, including next-generation sequencing, single-cell RNA sequencing, and chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing, have significantly expanded our understanding of their roles in GBM pathogenesis and resistance to therapy. Single-cell approaches, in particular, have revealed the diversity of transcription factors-driven transcriptional programs within distinct tumor cell populations, shedding light on their roles in intratumoral heterogeneity and tumor evolution [ ]. For example, STAT3 and c-MYC play pivotal roles in phenotypic plasticity, facilitating shifts between proneural and mesenchymal subtypes and enabling tumor cells to adapt to therapeutic stress, while environmental pressures such as hypoxia activate hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha (HIF-1α), driving the mesenchymal subtype by promoting cell survival, invasiveness, and resistance to therapy [ – ]. Many of the tumor characteristics driven by these transcription factors are hallmarks of the persistent therapeutic resistance observed in GBM, making them critical targets for interventional therapies. Directly targeting transcription factors presents a promising strategy to overcome resistance to these inhibitors, offering a potentially more effective solution compared to previous approaches that have largely failed. Despite their potential as therapeutic targets, transcription factors are underutilized due to the challenges of direct targeting. However, advancements such as CRISPR/Cas9 and RNA-based therapies provide new opportunities to selectively target transcription factors and overcome resistance. Recent preclinical models in GBM show promising results [ ]. By targeting transcription factors, we may develop more effective treatment strategies capable of overcoming resistance to RTK/Raf/Ras/MEK/ERK pathway inhibitors and other therapies, potentially improving outcomes and quality of life for GBM patients. Moreover, this approach could pave the way for addressing drug resistance in other malignancies, ushering in a new era of precision oncology.
 

## Capicua (CIC)
The high-mobility group (HMG)-box protein Capicua (CIC) serves as a transcriptional repressor of RTK/Ras/ERK-responsive genes [ , – ]. While CIC is frequently inactivated in oligodendrogliomas and other cancers, it is wild-type in GBM [ , – ]. This review specifically addresses the dysregulation of CIC in the context of GBM, where CIC is wild-type. Despite this, hyperactive ERK signaling in GBM leads to the loss of CIC protein, which contributes to tumorigenesis [ ]. Importantly, CIC inactivation and protein loss—through either post-transcriptional mechanisms or acquired mutations—recently emerged as an acquired resistance mechanism to RTK/Ras/ERK inhibition in various cancers, including GBM [ , – ].
Given the significant and well-established role of CIC in the regulation of the RTK/Ras/Raf/MEK/ERK pathway as opposed to other transcription factors, alongside the 90% prevalence of aberrant RTK signalling in GBM patients [ ], CIC must be explored as a clear therapeutic option in this malignancy. Moreover, the key contribution of RTK aberrancy to the aggressive profile of GBM further establishes CIC as an important target downstream of RTK activation in GBM [ ].
The mechanism of CIC activation involves the translocation of ERK into the nucleus upon RTK activation. ERK phosphorylates CIC at Ser173, thereby decreasing the stability of CIC on the DNA and alleviating CIC/DNA binding [ – ]. The phosphorylation of CIC also tags the protein for ubiquitination by E3 ubiquitin ligase PJA1, ultimately resulting in degradation [ ]. Both of these changes allow for the expression of oncogenes, including oncogenic transcription factors ETV1/4/5. While it was believed that inhibiting MEK/ERK signalling would maintain CIC-mediated repression, it has been found that inhibition of RTK/ERK in fact promotes loss of CIC, contributing to the resistance of these inhibitors [ , ]. Specifically, we found that sustained MEK/ERK inhibition using PD98059 and selumetinib respectively failed to restore CIC protein levels in GBM [ ]. The continued de-repressed state of CIC targets may explain the desensitisation of cells to RTK/Ras/MEK/ERK inhibition [ , ]. When CIC binding is relieved, oncogenic target genes are highly expressed regardless of upstream MEK/ERK inhibition. This may suggest that the lack of efficacy of MEK/ERK inhibitors in GBM in the clinical context [ ] is due to the loss of CIC-mediated repression, thus exposing CIC as a concurrent therapeutic target. We recently found that a degradation-resistant CIC mutant, which maintains CIC’s vital repressor function, restored sensitivity to MEK/ERK pathway inhibitors in GBM [ ]. This data suggests that strategies to stabilise CIC protein may increase the efficacy of MEK/ERK pathway inhibitors and improve therapeutic outcomes.
Wang et al. demonstrated that low CIC expression through deletion of ATXN1, which reduced CIC protein, was linked to increased resistance to trametinib, a clinically approved MEK inhibitor [ ]. This finding was shown in lung, colon, pancreatic, and melanoma cancer models [ ]. Da Via et al. further validated these findings, in showing that upon CIC gene silencing, resistance to B-Raf/MEK inhibition was induced [ ]. Simon-Carrasco et al. found that inactivation of CIC from trametinib-sensitive human T-ALL cell lines using CRISPR/Cas9 makes cells resistant to MEK inhibition, suggesting that CIC inactivation or loss renders MAPK inhibition inefficient in human cancer [ ]. In human neural stem cell lines, CIC was also identified as a determinant of the effectiveness of inhibiting epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) signalling [ ].
Loss of CIC also mediates resistance to EGFR inhibitors in non-small cell lung cancer by promoting cell cycle entry [ ]. More recently it was found that CIC modulates the effect of RTK inhibitors in RTK-driven sarcomas, by an ERK re-activation mechanism [ ]. Interestingly, it was also recently shown that mutated CIC (leading to loss of function) causes increased MAPK activation in oligodendroglioma which has further implications on the response to MAPK/ERK inhibition [ ]. The results of this study suggest the use of CIC as a possible genetic marker for the use of MEK/ERK inhibitor therapies [ ]. While these results have not yet been explored in GBM, the results observed in these other cancers provide rationale for further exploration of CIC stabilisation as a mechanism to overcome resistance to these inhibitors.
Further studies should focus on gaining a deeper molecular understanding of CIC repressor function in order to overcome resistance mechanisms in GBM. It should be noted that directly targeting CIC is challenging pharmacologically, which poses a barrier to CIC-tailored treatments. For this reason, there have been no clinical applications targeting MEK/ERK re-sensitization through CIC to date. Until new strategies are developed to directly target CIC, focusing on the concomitant signalling pathways that impact CIC stability is an important research avenue that should be explored in GBM. By understanding these regulatory mechanisms of CIC, the door may also be opened to new treatments aimed at CIC stabilisation and sensitisation toward RTK/Ras/MEK/ERK inhibitors, both in GBM and other cancers. The discovery of new pathways may also lead to a stronger foundation of clinical trial data where CIC is effectively stabilized, which is a current gap in the literature for this transcription factor.

## YAP/TAZ-TEAD complex
Yes-associated protein (YAP) and PDZ-binding motif (TAZ) are transcriptional co-activators homologs downstream of the Hippo pathway [ , ], responsible for normal organ growth and development [ , ]. Neither YAP nor TAZ has any DNA-binding domains—rather, the YAP/TAZ co-complex binds other transcription factors to exert its’ effects [ ]. The major transcription factors activated by YAP/TAZ are transcriptional enhanced associate protein (TEAD) family members which promote either apoptosis or proliferation, depending on the context [ , , ]. Mechanistically, YAP binds to TEAD at the proline rich domain through two distinct α and Ω loops [ ]. The α loop forms first, followed by an order-to-disorder transition and finally Ω loop binding, which is found to be more significant in YAP:TEAD binding [ ]. Phe69 is a hotspot for binding on the α loop, and the interaction between YAP Arg89 and TEAD4 Asp272 is a critical interaction on the Ω loop [ ]. This binding is distinct from that of TAZ, which has been found to form a heterotetramer with TEAD. These two binding mechanisms are non-redundant and are both important for the assembly of the YAP/TAZ-TEAD complex [ ].
While YAP/TAZ-TEAD is responsible for organ growth and development in normal cells [ , ], the dysregulation of this co-activator factor has also been implicated in tumorigenesis in various cancers, including GBM [ , , , – ].
Emerging evidence indicates that deregulation of YAP/TAZ signalling may be a major mechanism of intrinsic and acquired resistance to various targeted and radio/chemotherapies [ ]. In the context of GBM, knockdown of YAP and TAZ in EGFR amplified/mutant GBM cells reduced EGFR expression, prevented proliferation and increased apoptosis in patient derived GBM cells and orthotopic xenograft GBM models [ ]. This is because the downregulation of YAP and TAZ prevents the described assembly of the YAP/TAZ-TEAD complex and thereby decreases the transcriptional activity of EGFR [ ]. Vigneswaran et al. found that verteporfin, which inactivates the YAP/TAZ complex, combined with the B-Raf inhibitor, dabrafenib, increased apoptosis in tumour cells compared with verteporfin alone, suggesting that YAP/TAZ inhibitors may have more effective antitumor activity in combination with drugs that target RTK pathways and that YAP/TAZ inhibition may prevent resistance to therapeutics that target RTK/MEK/ERK signalling in GBM. Interestingly, a relationship between CIC and YAP1 has been explored in the context of drug resistance in human cancer [ ]. Specifically, it was found the CIC suppresses YAP1 to limit tumorigenesis, and the silencing of YAP1 in cells that are CIC-deficient leads to re-sensitization to MAPK inhibition [ ]. This provides further support for the importance of CIC stabilisation in sensitising to MEK/ERK inhibition in GBM as discussed above.
YAP1 depletion also sensitised neuroblastoma cell models to trametinib (MEK inhibitor). The YAP/TAZ-TEAD complex upregulates c-MYC/MYC gene products, and the depletion of one element of this complex results in significant downregulation of transcriptional products and thereby enhances cell cycle arrest [ ]. Interestingly, trametinib was also found to induce the rapid nuclear translocation of YAP1 in neuroblastoma, unveiling another potential indicator of the connection between YAP1 and MEK inhibitor resistance [ ]. The above results indicate that the co-inhibition of YAP/TEAD and EGFR/MEK could enhance the anti-apoptotic effects of MEK inhibition alone to reduce oncogenesis [ , , ].
Moreover, through a chemicogenomics approach, direct interaction was observed between MAPK signalling and TEAD (major transcription factors activated by YAP/TAZ) stability [ ]. Specifically, the knockdown of YAP combined with MEK inhibition by cobimetinib, selumetinib, and PD-901 results in decreased tumour growth in Hippo dysregulated tumors like breast, liver, and lung cancer, as well as glioma—this is significant due to the resistance to chemo and molecular therapies that is seen in Hippo-dysregulated tumors [ ]. Upon sequencing after YAP1 depletion and treatment with cobimetinib, a MEK inhibitor, loss of chromatin accessibility at AP-1 and TEAD binding sites was observed [ ]. This suggests that the co-inhibition of both YAP1 and MEK may result in more effective anti-tumorigenic effects in many cancers, particularly those with Hippo dysregulation—including glioma [ ].
The implication of YAP1 in RTK/Raf/Ras/MEK/ERK inhibitor treatment resistance is also seen in many other cancers, beyond brain tumors [ , – ]. Similarly, studies suggest that increased YAP1 transcriptional activity through dysregulation of the Hippo pathway may contribute to MEK inhibition resistance [ , , , ]. Lin et al. found that YAP1 gene silencing in HCC364 and Cal-12T human non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells resulted in increased sensitivity to the MEK inhibitor trametinib, as well as a greater degree of maximal growth inhibition and tumour death in these cells [ ]. Moreover, silencing of TEAD2 and TEAD4 phenocopied the described effects of YAP1 suppression on sensitivity to MEK inhibitors in HCC364 cells. These results highlight YAP expression and its effects on gene targets as crucial to the response of MEK targeted therapies, and position YAP inhibition as a strong potential mechanism of overcoming MEK inhibitor treatment resistance [ ]. It was also found that inhibition of YAP by XAV939 and TEAD using MYF-01-37 or knockout of YAP1 in EGFR-mutant non-small cell lung cancer enhanced EGFR/MEK inhibition-induced apoptosis [ ]. In an in vivo uveal melanoma study where treatment with a MEK inhibitor resulted in the upregulation of YAP1 and oncogenic activity, the use of an histone deacetylases (HDAC) inhibitor in conjunction with MEK inhibition reduced this effect and led to better treatment results [ ].
In addition to its role in RTK/Raf/MEK/ERK resistance, YAP was linked to radioresistance in glioma mouse models, immortalised cell lines and primary stem cells from GBM patients on single cell analysis [ , ]. Zhang et al. postulated that radiation promotes the migration of YAP from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, where it promotes the expression of fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2) which in turn activates the MAPK/ERK signalling pathway to promote DNA damage repair, generate resistance to therapy, and further contribute to oncogenesis [ ]. Treatment with YAP-FGF2-MAPK inhibitors such as AZD4547 attenuated this resistance, resensitizing gliomas to radiotherapy [ ]. However, another study found that the downregulation of YAP1 is a major contributor to radioresistance in GBM [ ]. Moreover, TAZ has been shown to promote resistance to temozolomide (TMZ), a chemotherapeutic agent used to treat GBM [ ].
While the targeting of YAP/TAZ and TEAD has been challenging, new strategies involving various combinations of small molecule inhibitors, chemotherapy, and microenvironment bioengineering may lead to a stronger evidence base for the clinical significance of YAP/TAZ in GBM [ , ]. While many of these strategies have yet to be explored clinically in GBM and other cancers, some have entered clinical trials. For example, the inhibition of TEAD using small molecules is being explored in 3 Phase l trials [ ]. One small molecule, VT3989, has shown success in and safety in mesothelioma patients [ ]. The emergence of these new treatments and techniques bears promise for the GBM population as they continue to be explored.
In summary, YAP and TAZ, acting as transcriptional co-activators downstream of the Hippo pathway play pivotal roles in normal organ growth and tumorigenesis. Their involvement in RTK/Raf/MEK/ERK inhibitor resistance and radioresistance suggests that targeting this complex may overcome therapy challenges in various cancers, including GBM which should be the focus of further studies.

## c-MYC
c-MYC is a multifunctional transcription factor regulating processes such as cell growth, survival, and metabolic reprogramming [ , ]. In GBM, its dysregulation drives tumor progression, heterogeneity, and resistance, particularly in glioblastoma stem cells (GSCs), which rely on c-MYC for proliferation and survival. Loss of c-MYC disrupts GSC-driven xenograft formation and intratumoral heterogeneity, linking it to poor clinical outcomes [ – ]. Technological advancements, such as RNA sequencing and chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing, have mapped c-MYC's regulatory networks in GBM, revealing its critical role in tumor adaptability and GSC maintenance [ ]. Single-cell sequencing has further demonstrated how c-MYC drives phenotypic transitions between GBM subtypes, including mesenchymal and proneural states, complicating treatment strategies [ ]. Moreover, CRISPR-Cas9 studies have identified c-MYC as a therapeutic vulnerability, opening the door to novel targeted approaches [ ]. These advancements have deepened our understanding of c-MYC’s role in GBM pathogenesis and reinforced its potential as a key therapeutic target.
Building on its pivotal role in tumor progression, c-MYC has also been identified as a key contributor to resistance against the MEK inhibitor trametinib. Shcreck et al. examined the role of MEK and mTOR inhibitor therapy in GBM and showed that cell lines with c-MYC amplification had reduced sensitivity to trametinib [ ]. Furthermore, Gao et al. showed that c-MYC knockout restores the initial therapeutic effects of trametinib treatment hindered by the glucose metabolism gene pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) overexpression in glioma cells both in vitro and in vivo [ ].
In addition to MEK inhibition, numerous efforts have been made to target the EGFR, which is commonly dysregulated in GBM [ ]. The limited success targeting this receptor in GBM may be due to c-myc driven resistance mechanisms that are well established in lung cancer [ ] but less defined in GBM. In GBM, Chen et al. show that human primary GBM cell lines are resistant to osimertinib (an FDA-approved EGFR inhibitor) due to MAPK-interacting kinases (MNK1/2) which are known regulators of c-MYC expression [ ]. This suggests that MNK1/2-driven resistance to osimertinib is due to c-MYC overexpression, however this needs to be further explored.
High c-MYC expression, in addition to GBM, has also been shown to mediate resistance to MEK inhibitors [ ] B-Raf inhibitors [ ], and FGFR inhibition [ ] in other malignancies. In contrast to this, a recent case study by Colombo et al. found that low c-MYC expression in the brain lesions of a patient with low-grade serous ovarian cancer (LGSOC) which developed following binimetinib (MEK inhibitor) treatment is responsible for the resistance to binimetinib and metastasis [ ]. This paradoxical discovery underscores the intricate nature of c-MYC's role in resistance to RTK/Raf/MEK/ERK inhibitors, necessitating further studies to unravel its complexities, particularly in the context of GBM.
Furthermore, c-MYC has been shown to contribute to chemotherapy resistance in GBM. Zhou et al. found that the long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) PSMG3-AS1 is significantly upregulated in TMZ resistant GBM cells which directly stabilises c-MYC in the nucleus to drive TMZ resistance [ ]. Similarly, Lan et al. found that a novel c-MYC regulator SPNRG (Small Nuclear Ribonucleoprotein Polypeptide G) is upregulated in TMZ resistant patient derived GBM lines and that suppressing SPNRG decreases c-MYC expression and re-sensitizes to TMZ [ ]. Using recurrent TMZ-refractory GBM specimens, TMZ-resistant cells, and resistant-xenograft models, Luo et al. report that c-MYC inhibits miR-29c expression to drive TMZ resistance [ ].
The field of c-MYC-targeted therapies is rapidly advancing, with innovative approaches currently under investigation. Despite the challenges of targeting transcription factors, the c-MYC inhibitor Omomyc (OMO-103) is in early-phase clinical trials, evaluating its safety and efficacy in patients with advanced solid tumors [ ]. In addition to OMO-103, various small molecule inhibitors, gene editing techniques, and RNA-based therapies are being explored, each addressing different aspects of c-MYC function and its pathways [ ]. Investigating the combination of c-MYC inhibitors with RTK/Raf/MEK/ERK inhibitors may help address resistance mechanisms, particularly in GBM therapy.

## STAT3
The intracellular signalling protein, STAT3, belongs to the seven-member STAT protein family (STAT1, -2, -3, -4, -5a/b, and -6). The STAT3 gene is located on chromosome 17q21.31 and is cleaved to form two isoforms (the full-length STAT3α and the dominant negative variant STAT3β) [ ]. STAT3 is activated transiently through canonical phosphorylation of Tyr-705 at the carboxyl terminal. Further, post-translational modifications of STAT3, including phosphorylation at Ser-727, acetylation (at lysine (K) residues K49, K87), and methylation (at K140), enhance its cellular activity [ ], with phosphorylation in GBM specifically linked to radiation resistance through interactions with proteins like FOXM1 and anti-apoptotic genes like BCL-2, promoting DNA repair and evasion of cell death under therapeutic stress [ ].
STAT3 plays a pivotal role in GBM tumorigenesis, contributing to tumor plasticity, immunological regulation, tumor growth, neoplastic transformation, and treatment resistance [ ]. Emerging technologies such as single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics have identified STAT3 as a key regulator of the mesenchymal subtype and a driver of immune evasion within specific microenvironments. By interacting with pathways like NF-κB, STAT3 supports the mesenchymal phenotype, driving intratumoral heterogeneity and enabling adaptation to treatment-induced stress [ , ]. These findings highlight STAT3’s role in transcriptional reprogramming and resistance to immunotherapies, positioning it as a marker of therapeutic resistance and a promising target. Preclinical studies employing CRISPR-based editing and RNA interference further emphasize STAT3’s central role in GBM progression and potential for targeted intervention [ , , ].
Various studies demonstrate that STAT3 confers resistance to EGFR and MEK inhibition, affecting treatment outcomes and prognosis of GBM. Jensen et al. assessed in vitro growth inhibition and in vivo efficacy with afatinib (EGFR inhibitor), pacritinib (JAK/STAT inhibitor), or both [ ]. The group identified an increase in STAT3 signalling in vitro and in vivo following EGFR inhibition. As such, combined treatment using afatinib and pacritnib was deemed synergistic compared to individual treatments with each inhibitor which led to a decrease in sphere-forming abilities of brain tumour stem cells’ (BTSC’s) and reduced tumour burden and size of BTSCs tumour bearing mice [ ]. Kenchappa et al. demonstrated that the resistance to mitotic kinesin (Kif11) inhibitor ispinesib in GBM is due to suppression of apoptosis driven by activation of STAT3 (at Y705 and S727 phosphorylation sites) [ ]. Importantly, they uncovered that targeting two kinases, Src and EGFR, in vivo with clinically available inhibitors (erlotinib, an EGFR inhibitor and dasatinib/saracatinib, Src inhibitors), which inhibit STAT3 activity, synergistically and significantly also prolonged survival in ispinesib-treated GBM-bearing mice [ ].
STAT3 was also demonstrated to play an important role in resistance to tyrosine kinase inhibitors in other brain tumors. Resistance to selumetinib (MEK inhibitor) in medulloblastoma mouse models was shown to occur due to JAK/STAT3 pathway activation [ ]. As such JAK/STAT3 pathway inhibitor (pacritinib) was able to sensitise to the effect of MEK (selumetinib) inhibitor of medulloblastoma bearing mice [ ]. Interestingly, various other cancer studies, including head and neck squamous cell carcinoma and non-small cell lung cancer, demonstrate STAT3 resistance to EGFR and MEK inhibition and their detrimental effect on treatment outcomes and prognosis [ , , ].
Furthermore, beyond the inhibition of RTK/Raf/MEK/ERK pathway, STAT3 is shown to play an important role in chemo/radioresistance in GBM. Various studies demonstrate that the activation of STAT3 is associated with increased tumorigenicity and resistance to therapy both in vitro and in vivo in GBM. In a study by Lau et al., a genetically engineered mouse model of proneural high-grade glioma (HGG) was studied with a focus on the proneural-to-mesenchymal transition (PMT)—a characteristic of gliomas with poor prognosis [ ]. When isolating primary proneural HGG cells from mouse and human tumors, radiation induced the mesenchymal transition and that irradiation-induced PMT was associated with the activation of STAT3 [ ]. The group further demonstrated that inhibiting STAT3 using JAK2 inhibitors in combination with radiation decreased PMT and extended the survival of mice [ ]. Similarly, Yang et al. demonstrated that highly tumorigenic, radioresistant and chemoresistant glioma cells have elevated phosphorylated STAT3 protein levels. When treating these cell lines with shRNA-STAT3 (sh-STAT3) or AG490, a STAT3 inhibitor, decreased stem-like properties and radioresistance was observed [ ]. Ultimately, these studies demonstrate that the STAT3 transcription factor plays a significant role in the resistance mechanism of RTK/MEK/ERK inhibitors and in chemo/radioresistance.
Although the clinical exploration of STAT3 inhibitors in combination with upstream inhibitors is still in its early stages, STAT3 monotherapy has shown promising results in clinical trials. Several STAT3 inhibitors are currently being evaluated across preclinical and clinical studies. These include both direct and indirect small molecule inhibitors, as well as antisense oligonucleotides like AZD9150, which targets STAT3 mRNA and is under investigation in lymphomas and solid tumors [ ]. The STAT3 inhibitor OPB-51602 has recently completed a Phase 1 clinical trial, demonstrating a reduction in STAT3 phosphorylation and some partial responses in patients with solid tumors resistant to standard treatments [ ]. Additionally, TTI-101, an orally bioavailable selective small molecule that prevents STAT3-mediated transcriptional activation, is currently in Phase 1 clinical trials [ ]. Notably, WP1066, an oral p-STAT3 inhibitor, is also being tested for various cancers, including GBM [ ]. Given these developments, further investigation into the efficacy of STAT3 inhibition, particularly in combination with existing therapies, is warranted. This research is crucial for understanding how to overcome resistance mechanisms in GBM and improve treatment outcomes.

## p53
The TP53 gene, located on human chromosome 17p13.1, encodes the p53 protein. This protein acts as a transcription factor that is composed of various domains, specialising in diverse regulatory pathways and transcriptional activity regulation [ ]. In GBM, 80% harbour modifications of p53 mutations/deletions or inactivation caused by the amplification of the oncoproteins, mouse double minute 2 homolog (MDM2) and mouse double minute 4 homolog (MDM4) amplification which are negative regulators of p53. Advances in genomic sequencing have illuminated the prevalence of TP53 mutations in GBM, linking specific mutations to the loss of tumor-suppressive functions and the acquisition of oncogenic properties. These mutations disrupt key processes such as apoptosis, genomic stability, DNA repair, and cell-cycle regulation, promoting tumor invasiveness and therapy-resistant cell populations. As revealed by RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analyses, mutant p53 rewires transcriptional networks critical for cell proliferation, often through interactions with transcription factors like p63 and p73, driving recurrence, progression, and treatment resistance [ ].
Dysregulation of p53 has been implicated in resistance to EGFR and MEK/ERK inhibitors in GBM, highlighting its role in driving treatment resistance within RTK pathway-targeted therapies. Sato et al. found that the MEK/ERK signalling pathway regulates the expression of the repair enzyme, O-6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT), which is well documented as the clinically most relevant mechanism of resistance against TMZ (chemotherapeutic) involving the MDM2-p53 axis [ ]. This group further demonstrated that MEK inhibition decreases MDM2 expression, enabling p53-dependent downregulation of MGMT, which resensitizes otherwise resistant GBM cells to TMZ treatment. Furthermore, combining MEK inhibition with TMZ effectively reduced the tumorigenic potential of GBM stem cells [ ]. In a study investigating malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNSTs) resistance, researchers determined that p53 deficient mouse models were less sensitive to RTK, MET and MEK inhibition in vivo when compared to p53 wildtype mouse models [ ]. This pattern is also seen in other cancers, such as leukaemia and NSCLC, where the loss of p53 leads to increased resistance to EGFR and MEK inhibitors, further demonstrating the complexity of p53 function and its contribution to resistance which needs to be further examined [ ].
In addition to RTK/MEK/ERK resistance mechanisms, p53 alterations play a role in the chemo/radioresistance of GBM. Modulation of p53 activity affects apoptosis which contributes to the acquisition of radioresistance [ ]. In an earlier study, Shu et al. reported that in GBM, the failure of p53 to induce the expression of p21BAX, which is a promoter of cell apoptosis, causes radioresistance in GBM-derived cells [ ]. Halliday et al., found that p53 controls the overall expression changes after radiation to such a large extent that irradiated p53 knockdown tumors cluster more closely with unirradiated tumors than with irradiated p53 intact tumors in a mouse model of proneural GBM p53 [ ]. The importance of p53 in contributing to radioresistance in GBM has also recently been found to occur via Rho GTPase activity and subsequent p53-mediated DNA repair mechanisms [ ]. In addition to its key role in radioresistance, Forte et al. found that RITA, a molecule originally identified for its ability to restore p53 functions, synergized with TMZ and was able to decrease the expression of MGMT, which is a crucial player in the TMZ resistance in GBM [ ]. Similarly, Tong et al. found that loss of p53 contributes to YB-1-mediated TMZ resistance in glioma cells [ ]. When a p53-small molecule inhibitor was tested for its effect on TMZ antitumor activity, results demonstrate that cytotoxic activity of TMZ was largely increased when p53 wild-type GBM cell lines were treated with this inhibitor [ ]. These results were also replicated in-vivo when treating intracranial xenografts, further demonstrating that TMZ co-therapy can effectively enhance cytotoxicity [ ].
Overall, these studies underscore the critical role of p53 in mediating drug resistance mechanisms, highlighting the need for further exploration in GBM and other cancers. Interestingly, clinical trials have focused on reactivating wild-type p53 or modulating mutant p53. These include approaches such as gene therapy and small molecules like PRIMA-1, which show promise in restoring p53's tumor-suppressor function in GBM patients [ ]. Further, several other clinical trials are investigating therapies designed to restore the function of mutant p53 or inhibit its negative regulators, such as MDM2. APR-246, a drug aimed at reactivating mutant p53, has shown promise in clinical trials, particularly in combination with other treatments for myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [ – ]. Similarly, MDM2 inhibitors like idasanutlin and ALRN-6924 have demonstrated encouraging results in cancers such as AML and lymphomas, where wild-type p53 is still present [ ]. Importantly, some of these MDM2 inhibitors have progressed to clinical testing in GBM [ ]. However, their efficacy, both as standalone treatments and in combination with other therapies, has yet to be fully established [ ]. Future research should focus on evaluating these p53-targeted drugs specifically within the context of GBM, particularly regarding their potential to overcome resistance to RTK/Raf/MEK/ERK inhibitors.

## AP-1 complex
AP-1 is a heterodimeric complex of Fos (cFOS, Fos-B, FOSL1), Fos related antigen-1 (FRA1), JUN (c-jun, junB, junD), and activating transcription factor [ ]. These factors can form homodimers and/or heterodimers through their leucine-zipper domains, creating the AP-1 complex [ ]. Following RTK activation, ERK phosphorylates and activates the AP-1 complex, enabling it to regulate a diverse array of cellular processes. In GBM, the AP-1 complex is highly dysregulated through the hyperactivation of signaling pathways such as MEK/ERK, as well as genetic alterations including epigenetic changes and the loss of negative regulators [ – ]. The advent of novel technologies, such as bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing, has further illuminated the role of AP-1 in mesenchymal transformation, revealing therapy-resistant subpopulations of GBM with high AP-1 activity. Notably, FOSL1, a key AP-1 subunit, drives mesenchymal transformation by mechanisms such as NF1 loss, which activates the Ras/MEK/ERK pathway. This contributes to enhanced tumor aggressiveness, plasticity, and mesenchymal traits; underscoring the AP-1 complex’s role in GBM heterogeneity and treatment resistance [ , , ].
In GBM, AP-1 and its subunits have been shown to mediate EGFR inhibitor resistance through re-activation of ERK [ ]. More specifically, Gou et al. show that erlotinib (EGFR inhibitor) treatment induces TNF-JNK-mediated AP-1 (c-JUN) transcriptional activation which in turn stimulates another RTK, Axl, which re-activates ERK and contributes to erlotinib resistance in patient-derived GBM neurospheres and GBM cell lines [ ]. In addition to erlotinib, a similar resistance mechanism involving AP-1 (c-JUN)/Axl activation has been observed in GBM cells treated with the EGFR inhibitors afatinib and gefitinib [ ] and in EGFR tyrosine kinase-resistant lung cancer cells [ ], highlighting the critical role of AP-1/Axl activation in mediating resistance to EGFR-targeted therapies. Additional AP-1 mediated transcriptional activation mechanisms have been shown to contribute to the resistance to EGFR [ – ], B-Raf [ ], and MEK/ERK [ , ] inhibitors in other malignant tumors that need to be examined in the context of GBM.
In GBM, AP-1 has also been implicated in chemo/radioresistance. Munzo et al. found that increased EGFR activation in GBM TMZ resistant cells activates AP-1 which in turn leads to increased expression of connexin 43 (Gap junction alpha-1 protein) and resistance to TMZ [ ]. Furthermore, Gao et al. found that the AP-1 subunit JUN plays a key role in mediating TMZ resistance in hypoxic GBM cells [ ]. Similarly, Zhang et al. demonstrate that another AP-1 subunit Fra-1 drives resistance to cisplatin (chemotherapeutic agent) in GBM both in vitro and in vivo [ ]. Importantly, using single cell analysis of primary and recurrent GBM patients it was recently found that radiation induces AP-1 transcriptional activation which regulates the mesenchymal hallmarks of inflammation, invasiveness and radioresistance [ ].
These findings highlight the crucial role of the AP-1 complex in mediating resistance to RTK/Raf/ MEK/ERK pathway inhibitors, and chemoradiotherapy in GBM. Despite substantial preclinical evidence supporting the potential of AP-1 inhibitors, clinical trials specifically targeting AP-1 in GBM remain limited compared to other extensively studied targets. While AP-1 inhibitors have yet to be evaluated in combination with upstream inhibitors, preclinical studies suggest a promising therapeutic potential when used alone. For instance, MLN944, an AP-1 inhibitor, has demonstrated significant anti-tumor efficacy through DNA–protein binding in both human and murine cancer models [ ]. Notably, MLN944 has shown the ability to delay tumor growth in HT29 human colon carcinoma models [ ]. Encouraged by these results, clinical trials are now underway to assess MLN944’s dose-limiting toxicity in patients with advanced tumors. Moving forward, research should focus on exploring the potential of combining AP-1 inhibitors with other targeted treatments in GBM to enhance therapeutic sensitivity and improve patient outcomes [ , ].

## HIF-1α
Hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha (HIF-1α) is a master transcriptional regulator of cellular and developmental responses to hypoxia, playing critical roles in both health and disease [ ]. HIF-1α has emerged as a pivotal regulator of GBM progression, orchestrating tumor growth, invasion, and therapeutic resistance through hypoxia-induced transcriptional programs. These programs drive critical processes such as angiogenesis, metabolic reprogramming, and cellular survival, enabling tumor cells to adapt and thrive under the hostile conditions of the hypoxic tumor microenvironment [ , , ]. Recent advancements in genomic technologies, including spatial transcriptomics and single-cell RNA sequencing, have further illuminated HIF-1α’s multifaceted role in GBM. By mapping its functional impact across different tumor regions and subpopulations, these technologies have revealed how HIF-1α contributes to tumor heterogeneity, progression, and resistance to therapy, underscoring its potential as a key therapeutic target for addressing GBM’s aggressiveness and treatment resistance [ , ].
HIF1 α has been shown to mediate resistance to therapies in GBM. In fact, the intense vascular hyperplasia observed in GBM is mediated by HIF-1α transcriptional activation of pro-angiogenic targets including vascular endothelial growth factors (VEGF) and its receptor tyrosine kinase VEGFR [ ]. Thus, it is not surprising that HIF-1α has been shown to contribute to the resistance of anti-angiogenic therapy in GBM [ , ]. Although HIF-1α’s role in mediating resistance to other RTK’s or its downstream effectors (Raf/MEK/ERK) is not well established in GBM, its role in mediating resistance to EGFR inhibitors in other malignant tumors is well documented [ – ]. It is important to note that HIF-1α’s activity is regulated and amplified by RTK downstream oncogenic pathways both MERK/ERK and PI3K/AKT, which enhance its translation and transcriptional programs, suggesting an indirect contribution to therapy resistance [ ]. Moreover, HIF-1α interacts with the RTK pathway, with ERK amplifying its effects by upregulating hypoxia-responsive genes that sustain GSCs, drive EMT-like traits, and exacerbate intratumoral heterogeneity, promoting tumor plasticity, aggressiveness, and therapy resistance [ , , ]. Despite the lack of direct evidence linking HIF-1α to resistance against RTK(EGFR)/MEK/ERK therapies, its potential role warrants further investigation, particularly given the evidence that activation of the RTK(EGFR)/MEK/ERK pathway following radiation or chemotherapy stabilizes HIF-1α and contributes to radio- and chemoresistance in GBM [ ]. Marampon et al. show that the MEK/ERK signal transduction pathway positively regulates HIF-1α protein expression and activity, preserving GBM radioresistance in hypoxic conditions. Importantly, they found that HIF-1α inhibitor (FM19G11) reduced the radioresistance of GBM cell lines. Similarly, Kim et al. found that radiation stabilises the HIF-1α protein, even in normoxic conditions through p38 MAPK, and that targeting HIF-1α or p38 sensitises GBM cells to radiation [ ]. Wang et al. describe a positive feedback loop between EGFR ligand EGF and HIF-1α/HIF-2α that promotes chemoresistance in primary GBM cells which can be rescued by HIF-1α/HIF-2α knockout [ ]. In addition to MAPK driven expression of HIF-1α other mechanisms including reactive oxygen species [ ], chaperone mediated autophagy [ ], p21 [ ] and Beclin-1 [ ] have been shown to promote HIF-1α activation/stability and mediate treatment responsiveness in GBM.
These findings underscore the crucial role of HIF-1α in mediating resistance to both targeted therapies and conventional treatments in GBM. HIF-1α inhibitors operate through various mechanisms: some, like OKN-007, block HIF-1α at the mRNA level to prevent its transcription and expression, while others, such as EZN-2208, inhibit its mRNA translation. Additional inhibitors, including Melatonin, Curcumin, and EF-24, promote HIF-1α degradation, whereas Acriflavine, Echinomycin, and KCN1 disrupt the binding of HIFs to their hypoxia response elements (HRE) [ ]. Despite extensive research, effective HIF-1α inhibitors for GBM are still elusive. However, ongoing clinical trials offer significant promise. For instance, AFP-464, shown to inhibit HIF-1α in preclinical studies, has progressed to clinical trials in breast cancer patients [ ]. Emerging novel HIF-1α inhibitors and innovative drug delivery methods, such as nanotechnology-based approaches, are showing potential in cancer therapy. Combining HIF-1α inhibitors with upstream inhibitors represents a promising strategy to address GBM's resistance mechanisms. Future research should focus on leveraging HIF-1α as a therapeutic target to enhance the efficacy of RTK/Raf/MEK/ERK pathway inhibitors and overcome chemo- and radioresistance in GBM.

## GLI1
Glioma-associated oncogene homolog 1 (GLI1) serves as a critical transcription factor in both the canonical and non-canonical Hedgehog pathways (Hh), exhibiting precise regulation during embryonic development and tissue patterning/differentiation [ ]. However, its role extends beyond development, as it has been implicated in promoting various cancer hallmarks, including proliferation, survival, angiogenesis, metastasis, metabolic alterations, and resistance to chemotherapy [ ].
Dysregulation of GLI1, a key transcription factor in Hh signaling pathway, plays a crucial role in GBM progression, tumor heterogeneity, and treatment resistance. Elevated levels of GLI1 have been associated with worse prognosis in GBM patients [ ], and GLI1 has been shown to play an important role in the growth, maintenance, and recurrence of GBM [ ]. Aberrant Hh signaling amplifies GLI1 activity, promoting the expression of genes involved in proliferation, anti-apoptosis, and therapy resistance, while fostering phenotypic plasticity that sustains intratumoral heterogeneity [ ].
Advances in genomic technologies, such as RNA sequencing and chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing, have mapped GLI1’s downstream targets, revealing its regulatory role in stemness and tumor plasticity in GBM [ ]. Single-cell RNA sequencing has highlighted GLI1-driven transcriptional diversity, while CRISPR-Cas9 studies have demonstrated its critical role in tumor maintenance, positioning GLI1 as a promising therapeutic target in GBM [ ]. These insights underscore GLI1’s central role in GBM pathogenesis and its potential for targeted interventions.
GLI1’s role in mediating resistance to chemotherapy is well established in GBM [ ]. Urdiciain et al. show that GLI1 reduction through HDAC inhibition enhances TMZ-induced apoptosis and reverses the malignant phenotype of GBM cells [ ]. A similar effect of HDAC-mediated GLI1 expression has been shown to contribute to radiotherapy resistance in GBM cells [ ] confirming the importance of HDAC-mediated GLI1 overexpression in therapeutic resistance of GBM. In addition to HDAC inhibition, Wang et al., found that inhibition of GLI1 activity with cyclopamine (Hh pathway inhibitor) led to chemosensitization to TMZ in glioma stem cells [ ]. Similarly, Li et al. demonstrated that combining the GLI1 inhibitor GANT61 with TMZ significantly suppressed glioma cell proliferation, induced higher rates of apoptosis and DNA damage, and reduced invasive capabilities [ ]. Farheen et al. found that GLI1 promotes TMZ resistance in glioma by maintaining the expression of the drug resistance gene MRP1, and its inhibition with GANT61 enhances TMZ efficacy, emphasizing the potential of targeting the Hh–GLI1 signaling axis to mitigate drug resistance in GBM [ ]. Building on these findings, a related study demonstrated that silencing GLI1 enhanced TMZ cytotoxicity, which was associated with increased levels of wild-type p53, a critical regulator of apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, and senescence [ ]. Additionally, Melamed et al. reported that combining GLI1 silencing with TMZ treatment promotes apoptosis and significantly reduces neurosphere formation in GSCs [ ]. Collectively, these studies underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting GLI1 to enhance TMZ efficacy and overcome drug resistance in GBM, particularly in GSC populations.
While GLI1 is not directly implicated in resistance to RTK/Raf/MEK/ERK inhibitors in GBM, it is well-established that GLI1 drives resistance to EGFR inhibitors in lung cancer and B-Raf inhibitors in melanoma165. Moreover, its upregulation in GBM in response to therapy suggests a potential role in treatment resistance, warranting further exploration in this context. Although many GLI1 inhibitors have been tested in vitro, in clinical trials GLI1 is primarily targeted through SMO receptor inhibition [ ]. Vismodegib (DC-0449), a SMO inhibitor, is currently being tested in Phase II trials for recurrent GBMs (NCT00980343) to identify patient subgroups with a higher likelihood of treatment response based on predefined molecular characteristics [ ]. Other SMO inhibitors, such as sonidegib (LDE225), is in Phase II trials for relapsed medulloblastoma in children and adults [ ]. Additionally, glasdegib (PF-04449913) is being explored in select hematologic malignancies [ ]. Given GLI1’s role in chemoresistance in GBM and resistance to EGFR inhibitors in lung cancer, future studies are necessary to determine whether GLI1 inhibitors can help overcome resistance to RTK/Raf/MEK/ERK pathway inhibitors in GBM.

## Conclusion
The transcription factors outlined in this review present promising avenues for future research in overcoming resistance to RTK/Raf/MEK/ERK pathway inhibition and chemo/radioresistance in GBM (Fig.  ). Co-treatment with inhibitors targeting these pathways alongside therapies directed at transcription factors represents a compelling strategy and warrants further investigation as these therapies continue to evolve (Fig.  ). While there is significant therapeutic potential in the targeting of transcription factors to overcome treatment resistance, this approach brings its own set of challenges. Notably, many of the transcription factors we discussed are “master regulators”, which play a role influencing diverse interconnected cellular processes, making their specific targeting difficult from a pharmacological perspective [ ]. Moreover, transcription factors are often large, multi-subunit structures with complex interactions between proteins and with their gene targets, making them far less easily targeted for inhibition than other steps of oncogenic signalling pathways. Challenges posed by the blood–brain-barrier play a role in limiting drug penetration and drug efficacy when considering the use of these drugs [ ]. However, recent advances in pharmaceutical strategies are beginning to overcome these obstacles [ ]. Proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs), for instance, leverage the ubiquitin–proteasome system to degrade transcription factors and are showing early promise in clinical applications. Small-molecule inhibitors are being designed to disrupt transcription factor-DNA interactions or inhibit co-factor binding, and advancements in structural biology are making these approaches increasingly feasible. Gene-editing technologies such as CRISPR/Cas systems now allow precise modulation of transcription factor activity, offering novel opportunities to investigate their roles in treatment resistance. Additionally, targeting transcription factors indirectly through epigenetic regulators, such as BET proteins that control gene expression at super-enhancers, represents another innovative strategy. These advancements in transcription factor targeting, though still in early stages, highlight the field’s potential to significantly enhance the efficacy of RTK/Raf/MEK/ERK pathway inhibitors and overcome resistance mechanisms in GBM. Continued research will help refine these methodologies, paving the way for translational and clinical success.
