Division of Medical Oncology, Duke, Raleigh, NC, USAhttps://orcid.org/0000-0002-8674-8102StarrJason S Most commonly epNECs originate from the upper and lower gastrointestinal (GI) tract along with the pancreas. Unfortunately, patients with epNEC typically present with advanced disease (ie, metastatic) which is associated with a poor prognosis, with median overall survival typically less than 1 year. , Treatments for gastroenteropancreatic NEC are sorely lacking, with platinum and etoposide being the mainstay first-line treatment for many decades now. There are currently no molecularly targeted therapies specifically for epNEC. 
Novel targeted cancer therapy has been a burgeoning field of research, promising effective and tolerable cancer treatment heralded by the success of imatinib in chronic myeloid leukemia. Targeted therapies block biological transduction pathways and/or cancer proteins to cause cancer cell death by apoptosis and/or stimulation of the immune system. As our understanding of the molecular underpinnings of GI malignancies continues to evolve and grow, so does the prospect of targeted therapies. HER-2, KRASG12C, and FGFR2 are some examples of successes of targeted therapy in GI malignancies.
RET signaling is vital for non-neoplastic neuroendocrine cellular development. RET gene fusions are oncogenic drivers that produce chimeric, cytosolic proteins with constitutively active RET kinase domains. Thus, mutation of the RET gene can be a driver in neuroendocrine tumorigenesis; classically exhibited by the development of medullary thyroid carcinoma. Nuclear receptor co-activator 4 (NCOA4)-RET fusion has been identified as one of the most common RET fusions in NSCLC, thyroid, lung, and salivary carcinoma. , Here, the authors present a patient with pancreatic NEC with a finding of a NCOA4-RET fusion and subsequent treatment with a RET inhibitor.

## Case Presentation
A 54-year-old White male with no past medical history was evaluated for a 3-week history of right upper quadrant pain, fevers, and 15-pound weight loss. Computerized tomography (CT) of the abdomen demonstrated a 3.5-cm ill-defined mass in the pancreatic head with pancreatic duct dilation and innumerable, bilobar masses throughout the liver parenchyma, the largest lesions measuring 3.6 cm and 3.1 cm in the left and right lobes, respectively ( ). Core needle biopsies of the pancreatic mass and one liver lesion revealed poorly differentiated NEC, large cell type, with a Ki-67 > 50%. The patient went on to receive a total of 4 cycles of carboplatin (AUC 6, day 1) and etoposide (100 mg/m2, days 1-3) every 3 weeks. Clinically, the patient responded to the initiation of treatment, with the resolution of pain. Imaging demonstrated radiographic response to corroborate clinical response.
 Next-generation sequencing (NGS) via ctDNA (Guardant Health) demonstrated a NCOA4-RET fusion at a 16.5% variant allele fraction (VAF). Other notable findings from the NGS results included a G protein αs (GNAS) mutation (81% VAF), CTTNB1 D32G mutation (41% VAF), tumor mutation burden of 17.22 mutations/megabase pair, and microsatellite status stable (complete report in ). Whole exome sequencing (Tempus) was also performed at diagnosis, and summary of all NGS is in .
 After shared, informed decision-making with the patient, we pursued a phase II clinical trial with the RET inhibitor (selpercatininb 160 twice daily) as a form of maintenance therapy. The patient experienced diarrhea (grade 1) along with grade 1 elevations of alanine transferase (ALT), aspartate transferase (AST), bilirubin, and alkaline phosphatase during treatment. Patient’s first restaging imaging ( ) was 55 days after starting therapy and showed progressive disease by RECIST v1.1 with a 79% increase in target lesions and new hepatic metastases.
 Next-generation sequencing with ctDNA was repeated upon progression. This demonstrated nearly identical findings as had been seen in the ctDNA prior to RET inhibitor challenge ( ). The previously seen NCOA4-RET fusion (16.2% VAF) was again identified ( ).
The patient was then started back on carboplatin and etoposide. After 2 cycles, a CT abdomen revealed further disease progression. After an informed discussion, the patient and oncologist decided to pursue dual checkpoint immunotherapy (nivolumab 240 mg and ipilimumab 1 mg/kg). Unfortunately, the disease progressed rapidly, and the patient was enrolled in hospice and passed away 10 days after administration of immunotherapy.

## Discussion
Selpercatinib is a selective RET protein kinase inhibitor that was first approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2020 for RET fusion-positive non-small cell lung cancers (NSCLC) and thyroid cancers based on the results of the LIBRETTO-01 study. The LIBRETTO-01 study was a phase I/II, open-label, basket trial, which included cohorts of patients, possessing a RET fusion, with NSCLC, thyroid cancer, and “other” cancers. The “other” cohort included 45 patients, of which 27% (n = 12) had pancreatic cancer. The type of pancreatic cancer was not specified in the publication, so whether any of these patients had pancreatic NEC is not known. Three cases of NEC originating in the lungs were included in a trial of selpercatinib in NSCLC, however, the results of these specific patients were not reported individually. Based on positive efficacy, the FDA in 2022 granted accelerated approval for selpercatinib for adults with locally advanced or metastatic solid tumors (ie, tumor agnostic) with RET gene fusions, following prior systemic treatment or those who have no satisfactory alternative treatment options. 
Our patient with stage IV pancreatic NEC received selpercatinib in a clinical trial. Unfortunately, after less than 2 months of targeted therapy, he had progression of disease. Many possible mechanisms can explain this primary resistance. An important observation in our patient is that the tumor genome demonstrated marked complexity, with several notable genomic findings.
The first possible reason for primary resistance is related to the relatively low VAF (16%) of the NCOA4-RET fusion at diagnosis. The low VAF suggests this finding was likely a subclone as opposed to a driver mutation. A targeted therapy is much more likely to have significant clinical improvement if the target is rate-limiting in tumor growth (ie, driver mutation) and is present in most of the tumor cells, suggesting that it arose in the “founding” tumor clone. Variant allele frequency is an important consideration that may help predict clinical response to targeted therapy.
G protein αs was the most prevalent mutation seen and may represent a possible suspect in tumorigenesis and primary resistance to RET inhibition. The gene carries important interactions with the MAP kinase pathway. GNAS plays a key role in cell signaling as it is a fundamental subunit of the G protein-coupled receptor, participating in cell signaling via adenylyl cyclase and cyclic AMP. There are multiple studies implicating GNAS gain-of-function mutations in cancer development. While the mechanism remains incompletely elucidated, there is evidence of cross-communication between the GNAS protein and the MAP kinase pathway, specifically at the level of RAS signaling. Activating KRAS mutations have already been identified as a possible driver of primary resistance to selpercatinib. Therefore, it is conceivable that our patient’s gain-of-function GNAS mutation led to downstream activation of the MAP kinase pathway, bypassing the RET inhibition. Moreover, GNAS copy number variations have been seen as possible drivers of resistance to other targeted therapy, including ROS1 and ALK inhibitors in NSCLC. 
Another possible resistance mechanism is based on previous receipt of platinum-based chemotherapy (PBC). In the LIBRETTO-01 study patients with previous PBC as compared to treatment naïve patients had an ORR of 61% (95% CI, 55%-67%) versus 84% (95% CI, 73%-92%), respectively. , In the 41 patients with non-NSCLC and non-thyroid tumors, 71% of patients (n = 32) received PBC prior to selpercatinib therapy. Overall, the study demonstrated an ORR of 44% (95% CI, 29%-60%). , When considering the superior outcomes in treatment patients with naïve-NSCLC compared to patients with previous PBC use, the high proportion of PBC use in the non-NSCLC, non-thyroid cohort likely resulted in decreased ORR. Our patient received a PBC regimen and subsequently progressed rapidly through selpercatinib. Further investigation is needed to confirm the observed correlative link between prior PBC and decreased efficacy of RET inhibition to help guide the sequencing of therapy.
There are as many as 35 variants of RET fusions. The NCOA4-RET fusion, identified in our patient, has been postulated to increase growth capacity and clonogenic expansion, leading to tumorigenesis. Just as the mechanism of tumorigenesis may vary based on the RET fusion identified, there appears also to be a variable response rate to RET inhibition. In the LIBRETTO-001 study of non-NSCLC, non-thyroid solid tumors, NCOA4-RET fusion was the most common, representing 39% of cases (n = 16) and had an ORR of 31% (95% CI, 11%-59%) compared to the ORR of 44% in the overall population (95% CI, 29%-60%). 
Prior to the development of selpercatinib and its sister drug, pralsetinib, RET-altered cancers were targeted with the use of multikinase inhibitors, causing grade ≥ 3 adverse events (AEs) in up to 70% of patients. Clinical trials using selpercatinib and pralsetinib have revealed lower rates of serious AEs compared to other multikinase agents. Our patient experienced many of the common AEs (all grade 1) that are reported with RET inhibition: xerostomia, diarrhea, and transaminitis.
There is a growing body of literature to support molecular characterization (ie, NGS) of epNEC to help with treatment selection and/or predict response to targeted therapy. Sending tissue for NGS at the time of diagnosis of epNEC should be strongly considered. Additionally, it would be prudent to also send a “liquid biopsy” (ie, ctDNA) in parallel to help corroborate tissue findings. In addition to detection of the targetable genomic alterations, a liquid biopsy can also help with monitoring disease response and can detect emergent subclonal mutations (ie, resistant clones). 
Further research studying the molecular pathogenesis of epNEC is desperately needed to move the field forward. This will facilitate the development of novel therapies and/or utilization of preexisting therapies. Our case presentation highlights the importance of nuanced interpretation of genomic reports to allow for accurate selection of effective therapy.

## Conclusion
We report a case of RET inhibition in a patient with a pancreatic NEC harboring a RET fusion. Our patient demonstrated primary resistance to selpercatinib, with disease progression at first restaging scan. There are multiple proposed mechanisms for our patient’s primary resistance, including decreased susceptibility of our patient’s NCOA4-RET fusion to RET inhibition, the RET fusion representing a passenger alteration to another tumorigenic driver pathway, and/or decreased efficacy of RET inhibition after PBC. Although in this instance the discovery of the RET fusion was not helpful for this patient, molecular characterization (ie, NGS) of epNECs is warranted. Consideration for NGS (both tissue and blood) should be strongly considered at the time of diagnosis.
