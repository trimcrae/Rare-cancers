## Abstract
Synthetic lethality (SL) is a genetic interaction phenomenon in which a cell can survive when either of two genes is individually mutated, but simultaneous disruption of both genes leads to cell death. This phenomenon reveals the redundancy and complementarity among gene functions and provides a theoretical basis for precision cancer therapies targeting specific genetic defects. The SL approach achieves the selective elimination of cancer cells by targeting the synergistic survival pathways on which tumor-specific genetic defects rely. This review highlights the core molecular mechanisms of SL in DNA damage repair, cell cycle checkpoint regulation, metabolic reprogramming, and epigenetic regulation; summarizes target discovery strategies based on high-throughput functional genomics and computational biology; and discusses successful clinical translation cases exemplified by poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors, along with current challenges and future directions.
Keywords: synthetic lethality, precision oncology, tumor heterogeneity, PARP inhibitors, DNA damage repair

## Introduction
Synthetic lethality (SL) is a genetic interaction phenomenon in which cells survive when either of two genes is individually mutated, knocked down, or inhibited but die when both genes are simultaneously disrupted. This concept originated from genetic research in model organisms, which focused on revealing redundancy and complementarity among gene functions [ ]. In cancer therapy, synthetic lethality strategies achieve precise targeting of cancer cells by disrupting synergistic survival pathways dependent on specific genetic defects (such as inactivated tumor suppressor genes or activated oncogenes) while maximally sparing normal cells. In recent years, advances in functional genomics and high-throughput screening technologies have transformed synthetic lethality from a fundamental genetic concept into a promising cancer treatment paradigm [ ]. 
The concept of synthetic lethality originated from genetic studies in the early 20th century, gradually developing from observations in fruit fly hybrid experiments into a core theory for understanding genetic redundancy and complementation [ ]. In the 21st century, research revealed that tumor cells with breast cancer susceptibility gene 1/2 (BRCA1/2) mutations are sensitive to poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors because of defects in homologous recombination repair, providing the first successful example of clinical translation utilizing the SL strategy [ ]. 
The core of the synthetic lethality strategy lies in exploiting tumor-specific genetic defects (such as the loss of tumor suppressor gene function or aberrant activation of oncogenes) to create targetable “vulnerabilities,” known as “non-oncogene addiction,” thereby achieving precise killing of cancer cells [ ]. For example, tumors with BRCA1/2 mutations are highly dependent on PARP-mediated repair pathways because defects in homologous recombination repair, and inhibiting these pathways can lead to the accumulation of DNA damage and cell death. TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) kinase in KRAS-mutant tumors and myosin light chain kinase (MLCK) in MYC-driven tumors have also been identified as synthetic lethal targets. Epigenetic regulators, such as the stability of chromodomain helicase DNA binding protein 1 (CHD1) in the context of phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) loss and the dependence on DDB1 and CUL4 associated factor 5 (DCAF5) in SWI/SNF related, matrix associated, actin dependent regulator of chromatin, subfamily b, member 1 (SMARCB1)-mutant tumors, further highlight the complexity and diversity of synthetic lethal networks [ ]. The theoretical advantage of this strategy is the potential for a clear therapeutic window, wherein the drug selectively kills cancer cells with specific genetic alterations while sparing normal cells. In practice, however, achieving this selectivity is often limited by tumor heterogeneity, adaptive resistance, and on target toxicity [ ]. For instance, compared with traditional chemotherapy, the use of PARP inhibitors in patients with BRCA1/2 mutations significantly extends progression-free survival and results in lower toxicity. In addition, synthetic lethality can be used to target mutations traditionally considered “undruggable” (such as KRAS or tumor protein p53 (TP53)), thereby expanding the range of targets for anticancer drugs [ ]. Successfully translating synthetic lethal interactions requires meeting several conditions: a clear therapeutic window, highly penetrant biomarkers, pharmacological feasibility, and the ability to adapt to tumor heterogeneity. Early studies have shown that BRCA-mutant cells are highly sensitive to PARP inhibition, with sustained and profound sensitivity, supporting clinical translation. Combining functional biomarkers (such as RAD51 recombinase (RAD51) foci formation assays) with genomic scar analysis can further improve the accuracy of patient selection and therapeutic benefit [ ]. 

## Molecular mechanisms of synthetic lethality

## DNA damage repair pathways
DNA damage repair pathways are central to maintaining genomic stability, and their functional defects represent hallmarks of tumorigenesis. Moreover, these defects create targetable vulnerabilities that have been most extensively exploited in synthetic lethality. The intricate network formed by the homologous recombination repair pathway and PARP family proteins exemplifies the current understanding of synthetic lethality mechanisms.

## Core role and mechanism of the BRCA‒PARP axis
The BRCA‒PARP axis represents the first successful translation of the synthetic lethality theory into cancer therapy. Its core mechanism lies in simultaneously disrupting two fundamental repair pathways for single-strand breaks (SSBs) and double-strand breaks (DSBs). In normal cells, poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase, particularly its canonical isoform PARP1 acts as a DNA damage sensor, rapidly recognizing and binding to single-strand breaks (SSBs). It catalyzes the synthesis of poly(ADP-ribose) chains, recruiting repair proteins such as X-ray repair cross complementing 1 (XRCC1) to efficiently repair SSBs. Additionally, PARP1 plays a crucial role in replication fork stabilization. When cells with intact homologous recombination repair (HRR) function are subjected to PARP inhibition, they can still survive by accurately repairing DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) that evolve during DNA replication because of unrepaired single-strand breaks (SSBs) via the HRR pathway.
However, in tumor cells harboring pathogenic BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations, homologous recombination repair is lost. In such cases, PARP inhibitors disrupt both repair pathways simultaneously by inhibiting PARP catalytic activity and blocking SSB repair; moreover, clinical drugs such as olaparib and talazoparib “trap” PARP1 at DNA damage sites [ ]. This “PARP‒DNA complex” itself becomes a potent cytotoxic insult, impeding DNA replication and causing replication fork collapse, which in turn generates numerous double-strand breaks (DSBs). In the context of HRR deficiency, cells must rely on error-prone alternative pathways, such as alternative end joining, to repair these DSBs, ultimately leading to dramatic increases in genomic instability and cell death.
Research on PARP inhibitor resistance mechanisms provides compelling genetic evidence for this model. Genome-wide and high-density CRISPR-Cas9 screening studies have revealed that, in addition to classic BRCA1/2 reversion mutations or deletions in HRR-related genes such as p53-binding protein 1 (53BP1) and REV7 homolog, DNA polymerase zeta accessory subunit (REV7), PARP1 gene mutations conferring resistance were also detected in PARP inhibitor-resistant cells [ ]. These mutations span the DNA-binding zinc finger domain, regulatory domains, and even the catalytic domain. Their common feature is a significant impairment of the ability of PARP1 to be “trapped” on DNA by inhibitors rather than directly affecting its catalytic activity toward NAD⁺ [ ]. This finding directly demonstrates that “PARP trapping” is the key mechanism mediating its cytotoxicity rather than simple catalytic inhibition. The R591C mutation causing loss of PARP1 trapping function was identified even in the tumor of an ovarian cancer patient with primary resistance to olaparib, further confirming the importance of this mechanism in clinical resistance (Fig. ).
 

## HRR-related gene defects and PARP inhibitor sensitivity
The “BRCAness” concept has significantly expanded the application prospects of PARP inhibitors beyond germline BRCA1/2 mutations. It refers to tumors that exhibit homologous recombination repair deficiency (HRD) through other mechanisms. As detailed in Section " ", PARP inhibitors exert their synthetic lethal effect by trapping PARP1 on DNA in HR-deficient cells. Beyond BRCA1/2, the HRR pathway involves numerous genes, including partner and localizer of BRCA2 (PALB2), ataxia telangiectasia mutated (ATM), A™ and Rad3-related (ATR), checkpoint kinase 2 (CHEK2), RAD51 and its paralogs, and genes in the Fanconi anemia pathway [ , ], mutations that can also confer sensitivity to PARP inhibitors. Germline or somatic mutations in these genes impair homologous recombination repair (HRR) function to varying degrees, conferring cellular sensitivity to PARP inhibitors. For example, in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer, deletions in genes such as A™ and BRCA2 have been demonstrated to correlate with the clinical response to PARP inhibitors.
Moreover, functional assessment of the HRR status is crucial for predicting PARP inhibitor sensitivity. Genomic scar analysis can retrospectively determine whether a tumor harbors HR defects by identifying characteristic mutation patterns such as large-scale state transitions. However, this approach fails to reflect the real-time functional status of the HRR at the time of treatment [ ]. Therefore, functional biomarkers, such as the ability of RAD51 to form foci in the nucleus following DNA damage induction, as detected by immunofluorescence, have been shown to provide a more direct and dynamic reflection of HRR capacity [ ]. These biomarkers can identify tumors sensitive to PARP inhibitors despite the absence of HRR gene mutations detected by conventional genetic testing.
Notably, HRR defects are not the sole source of PARP-captured damage. Studies have revealed that ribonuclease H2 (RNase H2) deficiency leads to substantial accumulation of embedded ribonucleotides within the genome [ ]. These ribonucleotides can be cleaved by topoisomerase I, generating numerous DNA single-strand breaks that subsequently serve as “substrates” captured by PARP. Consequently, RNase H2-deficient cells exhibit heightened sensitivity to PARP inhibitors. This repair vulnerability, termed ribonucleotide excision repair (RER) defects, arises from the inability to remove misincorporated ribonucleotides from DNA. Given that the ribonuclease H2 subunit B (RNASEH2B) gene is frequently lost because of 13q14 deletions in chronic lymphocytic leukemia and metastatic prostate cancer, this discovery provides novel theoretical support for the application of PARP inhibitors to tumors with intact HRR function but ribonucleotide excision repair (RER) defects.

## Reverse synthetic lethality between POLQ and HRR
Further investigation of HR-deficient tumors revealed a novel synthetic lethal relationship beyond the BRCA‒PARP paradigm, namely, an inverse synthetic lethality between the HRR pathway and DNA polymerase θ.
Classic synthetic lethal HRR-BER/SSBR: Within the BRCA‒PARP axis, HRR and BER/SSBR are two parallel, functionally complementary pathways primarily responsible for DSB and SSB repair, respectively. When HRR is inactivated, cells become highly sensitive to disruption of BER/SSBR (via PARP inhibition). This effect demonstrates synthetic lethality targeting parallel complementary pathways.
Reverse synthetic lethal HRR-TMEJ: DNA polymerase θ (POLQ) serves as the central effector of theta-mediated end-joining (TMEJ), an error-prone pathway for DNA double-strand break repair that operates independently of homologous recombination [ ]. In HR-proficient cells, POLQ expression is low, and its inhibition does not compromise viability; indeed, suppressing POLQ can even increase HR efficiency by promoting RAD51 nuclear filament assembly [ ]. This finding indicates that the POLQ-mediated TMEJ pathway is ordinarily suppressed when HR is functional. However, in HR-deficient tumors—such as many high-grade serous ovarian cancers—POLQ expression is significantly upregulated and is essential for survival. Mechanistically, POLQ performs a dual function: its C-terminal polymerase domain executes TMEJ, while its N-terminal helicase-like domain contains a RAD51-binding motif that actively disrupts RAD51-ssDNA nucleofilament assembly, thereby antagonizing any residual HR activity that could be toxic to HR-deficient cells. By providing a critical alternative repair pathway (TMEJ) while simultaneously suppressing residual HR, POLQ becomes indispensable for the survival of HR-deficient tumors. Consequently, pharmacological inhibition of POLQ selectively kills HR-deficient cells, establishing a robust synthetic lethal interaction with significant therapeutic potential. This “reverse” synthetic lethality contrasts with the classic parallel pathway relationship: in HR-proficient cells, POLQ inhibition is harmless and may even promote more precise HR repair; conversely, HR-deficient tumors become critically dependent on POLQ for survival. Genetic evidence supports this dependency, as Fancd2⁻/⁻Polq⁻/⁻ double knockout causes embryonic lethality in mice, and POLQ knockdown in HR-deficient xenograft models significantly enhances sensitivity to PARP inhibitors. Collectively, synthetic lethal networks in DNA damage repair extend far beyond the BRCA‒PARP axis. These discoveries greatly expand the understanding of synthetic lethal molecular mechanisms, from defects in the HRR caused by BRCAness to novel PARP-captured damage sources revealed by RNase H2 deficiency and the intricate interplay between HRR and POLQ. These findings provide a robust foundation for precisely identifying beneficial populations and developing next-generation targeted therapeutics (Table ).
 

## Synthetic lethal effects on cell cycle checkpoint regulation
Cell cycle checkpoints are core regulatory mechanisms for maintaining genomic integrity. The TP53-regulated G1/S checkpoint and the ATR-CHK1-WEE1 pathway-regulated G2/M checkpoint form a dual safeguard. However, in most cancers, frequent TP53 inactivation leads to loss of G1/S checkpoint function, forcing cancer cells to become highly dependent on the G2/M checkpoint to cope with replication stress. This dependency provides a strong rationale for targeting A™ and Rad3-related (ATR), checkpoint kinase 1 (CHK1), or WEE1 G2 checkpoint kinase (WEE1) in TP53-deficient tumors, as detailed in this section. This section systematically elucidates the core mechanisms linking TP53 inactivation to G2/M checkpoint dependency, with a focus on translational advances in WEE1 and CHK1 inhibitor-based synthetic lethal strategies (Fig.  ).
 

## Core mechanism: TP53 inactivation and G2/M checkpoint dependency
As the “guardian of the genome,” TP53 plays a central role in the G1/S checkpoint by responding to DNA damage through transcriptional activation of effector proteins such as cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1 A (p21), thereby arresting cell cycle progression to facilitate repair [ ]. Loss of TP53 function (via mutation or deletion) is among the most common genetic events in cancer, occurring in approximately 50% of all tumors. This process directly results in defective G1/S checkpoint function, preventing cells from effectively arresting and repairing damage before DNA replication. Consequently, extensive DNA breaks and replication stress accumulate. To maintain survival, TP53-deficient cancer cells compensate by upregulating G2/M checkpoint activity. This adaptive dependency forms the molecular basis for synthetic lethality.
The G2/M checkpoint is precisely regulated by the ATR-CHK1-WEE1 signaling cascade [ ]. Upon DNA damage or replication stress, ATR is activated and phosphorylates CHK1. CHK1 then inhibits cell division cycle 25 (CDC25) phosphatase activity and activates WEE1 kinase activity, promoting the phosphorylation of cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1) at Tyr15. This process blocks the activation of the CDK1-cyclin B complex, arresting cells in the G2 phase. Studies have indicated significantly elevated endogenous replication stress levels in TP53-deficient cells, further underscoring the dependence on the ATR-CHK1-WEE1 pathway. Studies have also shown that endogenous replication stress levels are significantly increased in TP53-deficient cells, making them more dependent on the ATR-CHK1-WEE1 pathway. For example, under ATR inhibition, compared with wild-type cells, TP53-deficient mouse embryonic fibroblasts exhibit increased levels of replication stress, as evidenced by the subsequent expression of DNA damage markers (pannuclear γH2AX staining, which is indicative of replication fork collapse) and increased apoptosis [ , ]. In the context of oncogenes such as cyclin E1 (CCNE1) being overexpressed and exacerbating replication stress, TP53 inactivation further amplifies this dependency [ , ]. ATR inhibitors synergistically induce p53-independent apoptosis, confirming the synergistic lethal interaction of TP53 inactivation and ATR inhibition.
In addition to ATRs, the downstream effectors CHK1 and WEE1 also exhibit synthetic lethality with TP53 deficiency [ ]. Mechanistically, inhibiting CHK1 or WEE1 abolishes the G2/M checkpoint, forcing cells with unrepaired DNA damage to prematurely enter mitosis and undergo mitotic catastrophe (e.g., micronucleus formation and chromosomal fragmentation). Notably, this synthetic lethality exhibits pathway specificity: while PI3K/mTOR inhibitors target growth factor signaling and cannot directly impinge on the G2/M checkpoint, even at their maximum tolerated clinical doses (e.g., alpelisib 400 mg daily; bimiralisib 80–140 mg daily) [ , ], these agents fail to induce the selective lethality observed with ATR/CHK1/WEE1 inhibition in TP53-deficient cells, underscoring that this vulnerability is uniquely linked to G2/M checkpoint dependency. Preclinical models further validated that in TP53-deficient ovarian, colorectal, and pancreatic xenografts, WEE1 or CHK1 inhibitors combined with DNA-damaging agents (e.g., gemcitabine and cisplatin) significantly suppressed tumor growth but had minimal effects on TP53 wild-type models.
In summary, TP53 inactivation creates unique synthetic lethal vulnerability by disrupting the G1/S checkpoint and increasing the dependence on the G2/M checkpoint. Targeting the ATR-CHK1-WEE1 axis not only selectively eliminates TP53-deficient cancer cells but also provides an indirect targeting strategy for traditionally “undruggable” TP53 mutations [ ]. 

## Synthetic lethal effects of WEE1 inhibitors
WEE1 kinase serves as a core negative regulator of the G2/M checkpoint by maintaining CDK1-Tyr15 in an inactivated state through phosphorylation, thereby preventing progression to mitosis [ ]. In TP53-deficient cancer cells, WEE1 expression is frequently upregulated to counteract heightened replication stress and DNA damage burdens, making WEE1 an ideal target for synthetic lethality [ ]. 
WEE1 inhibitors (e.g., AZD1775, formerly MK1775) antagonize WEE1 kinase activity, leading to premature CDK1 activation and abolishing the G2/M checkpoint [ ]. In TP53-deficient cells, this action induces synthetic lethality; cells fail to arrest in the G1 phase and lose the G2 repair window, ultimately succumbing to mitotic catastrophe. Studies have demonstrated that AZD1775 effectively inhibits WEE1 at nanomolar concentrations (IC₅₀=5 nM) and induces selective apoptosis in TP53-deficient cervical, colorectal, and pancreatic cancer cell lines, with apoptosis rates 3–5 times greater than those in TP53 wild-type cells [ ]. Furthermore, combining AZD1775 with DNA-damaging agents (e.g., cisplatin and gemcitabine) further amplifies the synthetic lethality effect. In TP53-deficient ovarian cancer xenograft models, the combination of AZD1775 and carboplatin resulted in tumor inhibition rates of 70%-80%, whereas the rates were only 20%-30% with carboplatin alone.
AZD1775 is the most advanced WEE1 inhibitor to date. As of 2016, 34 clinical trials have evaluated its safety and efficacy in combination with DNA-damaging agents. Phase I studies have demonstrated that in patients with advanced solid tumors, the objective response rate to AZD1775 combined with cisplatin reached 30% in the TP53-deficient subgroup, which was significantly higher than that in the overall population (15%) [ ]. Dose-limiting toxicities primarily included myelosuppression and supraventricular tachycardia, although they were generally manageable. Notably, a Phase I study (NCT01748825) revealed partial responses in patients with BRCA1 mutations, suggesting that WEE1 inhibition may result in synergistic synthetic lethality with homologous recombination repair defects [ ]. 
However, the clinical application of WEE1 inhibitors faces several challenges [ ], including the off-target effects of AZD1775 (such as inhibition of YES1 and SYK), insufficient blood‒brain barrier penetration, which limits its use in brain tumors, and the lack of precise biomarkers for patient selection. Future directions include developing highly selective WEE1 inhibitors (e.g., CJM061, whose single-agent toxicity is 50% lower than that of AZD1775), optimizing dosing regimens to increase intracerebral concentrations, and integrating functional biomarkers (e.g., replication stress scores) for precise identification of beneficial populations.

## Clinical translation of CHK1 inhibitors
As a downstream effector of ATR, CHK1 plays a central role in the DNA damage response and replication fork stability. Its inhibition disrupts the G2/M checkpoint and induces synthetic lethality under high replication stress, particularly in TP53-deficient tumors.
CHK1 inhibitors (e.g., LY2606368) block CHK1-mediated phosphorylation of cell division cycle 25 C (CDC25C), leading to sustained CDK1 activation and forcing cells to enter mitosis with DNA damage [ ]. In TP53-deficient cells, the absence of the G1/S checkpoint renders CHK1 inhibition lethal, triggering a “replication catastrophe” characterized by replication fork collapse, accumulation of DNA double-strand breaks, and elevated phosphorylated H2A histone family member X (pH2A.X) levels. Nonclinical studies have demonstrated that LY2606368 has an IC₅₀ of 1 nM for CHK1 inhibition in cell-free assays and induces tumor regression via pCHK1 suppression in a Calu-6 xenograft model [ ]. PK/PD modeling predicted a minimal tumor response at an average pCHK1 inhibition rate of 49.7% within 72 h, with a maximal response achieved at 70.5%.
A Phase I study of LY2606368 demonstrated objective responses for the first time when a CHK1/CHK2 inhibitor was used as monotherapy. Among 45 patients with advanced cancer, two patients with squamous cell carcinoma achieved a partial response (PR), and 15 patients experienced stable disease (SD), with response durations ranging from 1.2 to 7.2 months. Notably, all the responders had SCC (anal SCC and head/neck SCC), suggesting that tumor types with higher replication stress levels exhibit greater sensitivity to CHK1 inhibition. Mechanistically, SCC often involves oncogene activation (e.g., CCND1 amplification) and DNA repair defects, leading to elevated endogenous replication stress and increased dependence on the CHK1 checkpoint.
In terms of safety, the primary dose-limiting toxicity of LY2606368 was transient Grade 4 neutropenia (73.3% incidence), which was short-lived (typically < 5 days) and did not result in treatment-related death. This toxicity stems from the role of CHK1 in hematopoietic stem cell cycle regulation, but its reversibility highlights the selectivity of synthetic lethality as normal cells recover through compensatory mechanisms while TP53-deficient cancer cells undergo apoptosis.
However, the clinical translation of CHK1 inhibitors remains challenging. First, reliable PD biomarkers are lacking. Follicular pH2A.X is not significantly correlated with tumor response, and circulating tumor cell counts were insufficient for analysis. Second, the association of TP53 status with treatment efficacy was inconsistent. However, some TP53 wild-type SCC patients achieved disease stabilization, suggesting potential alternative predictors (e.g., replication stress score or HR deficiency). Future research should focus on developing combination biomarkers (e.g., pCHK1/pH2A.X levels in tumor biopsies) and exploring combination strategies of LY2606368 with DNA-damaging agents (e.g., gemcitabine) to further amplify synergistic lethal effects.
Synthetic lethality in cell cycle checkpoint regulation represents a key paradigm for precision oncology. TP53 inactivation creates “G2/M checkpoint-dependent” vulnerability, offering an ideal window for targeting the ATR-CHK1-WEE1 axis. Preclinical and early clinical data on WEE1 and CHK1 inhibitors have thoroughly validated the feasibility and selectivity of this strategy, demonstrating particularly significant efficacy in TP53-deficient or high-replication-stress tumors. Future efforts to optimize inhibitor selectivity, develop novel biomarkers, and explore rational combination regimens hold promise for expanding the application scope of synthetic lethal strategies, offering more precise therapeutic options for refractory cancers [ ]. 

## Synthetic lethal effects on metabolic pathways
Metabolic reprogramming is a core feature of tumor cells. It not only rapidly supplies biosynthetic precursors but also directly influences epigenetic regulation and genomic stability by altering metabolite levels, thereby generating context-specific metabolic dependencies that can be therapeutically targeted [ ]. Metabolism-related synthetic lethality primarily arises from two mechanisms: the abnormal dependence of cancer cells on specific metabolic pathways and DNA repair defects caused by the accumulation of oncogenic metabolites. These mechanisms collectively form the theoretical basis for metabolism-directed synthetic lethality strategies.

## Core mechanism: synthetic lethal opportunities generated by metabolic reprogramming
Metabolic reprogramming in tumor cells involves adaptive alterations across glycolysis, amino acid metabolism, lipid synthesis, and mitochondrial function. While these alterations promote tumor growth, they also create specific metabolic dependencies—the phenomenon known as “nononcogene addiction”. When key metabolic enzymes undergo mutation or abnormal expression, tumor cells must upregulate alternative or bypass pathways to maintain viability. This adaptive dependency becomes an ideal target for synthetic lethality interventions.
For example, in colorectal cancer, APC loss upregulates the expression of tryptophan 2,3-dioxygenase (TDO2) by continuously activating the β-catenin signaling pathway, promoting the breakdown of tryptophan into kynurenine. The latter facilitates tumor proliferation and inhibits T-cell function through aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) signaling, thereby remodeling the immunosuppressive microenvironment [ ]. TDO2 inhibitors can selectively induce apoptosis in APC-mutant models, confirming the synthetic lethality relationship between APC loss and TDO2 inhibition [ ]. 
Another significant example is MYC-driven metabolic vulnerability [ ]. Overexpression of the MYC oncogene promotes cell proliferation and metabolic reprogramming across multiple tumors while revealing specific metabolic dependencies. Studies indicate that MYC-overexpressing tumors exhibit high sensitivity to the inhibition of mitochondrial complex I [ ]. Mechanistically, MYC drives glutamine metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation, increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels [ ]. The inhibition of complex I further exacerbates oxidative stress beyond the tolerance threshold of cancer cells, inducing nonapoptotic cell death, such as ferroptosis. This mechanism offers novel therapeutic approaches for MYC-driven tumors, particularly those with traditionally challenging MYC overexpression.
Furthermore, nucleotide metabolism presents abundant synthetic lethal opportunities [ ]. In tumors lacking methylthioadenosine phosphorylase (MTAP), cells exhibit heightened sensitivity to protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5) inhibition because of accumulated 5’-deoxy-5’-methylthioadenosine (MTA). Mechanistically, MTA competitively inhibits PRMT5 activity, forcing MTAP-deficient cells to upregulate PRMT5 expression for survival and inhibitors then synergistically induce cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. This synthetic lethal relationship has been validated across multiple MTAP-deficient tumor models, including glioma, pancreatic cancer, and lung cancer.

## ME2-ME3 axis paradigm: synthetic lethal network of metabolic enzyme cross-regulation
The metabolic axis formed by malate decarboxylase 2 (malic enzyme 2 (ME2)) and malate decarboxylase 3 (malic enzyme 3 (ME3)) provides a classic example of metabolic synthetic lethality [ ]. ME2 and ME3 catalyze the oxidative decarboxylation of malate to pyruvate, simultaneously generating NADH or NADPH, thereby linking the tricarboxylic acid cycle to the mitochondrial electron transport chain. In normal cells, ME2 and ME3 exhibit functional redundancy, together maintaining cellular redox homeostasis and biosynthetic demands.
Research has revealed that in ME2-deficient tumor cells, the mitochondrial NADH/NADPH balance is disrupted, forcing cells to rely on ME3 to maintain metabolic homeostasis. In this state, inhibiting ME3 induces severe oxidative stress and an energy crisis, selectively killing ME2-deficient cells. Mechanistically, ME2 deficiency causes mitochondrial malate accumulation and impaired NAD+ regeneration, disrupting TCA cycle flux. Simultaneous ME3 inhibition further blocks malate metabolism, leading to mitochondrial collapse. This synthetic lethal relationship was validated in glioblastoma and lung cancer models, providing a specific therapeutic target for ME2-low tumors.
Notably, the synthetic lethality of the ME2-ME3 axis is significantly influenced by tissue type and genetic background. In KRAS-mutant lung cancer, ME2 expression is typically downregulated, increasing the sensitivity of cells to ME3 inhibition. Conversely, the dependence of BRAF-mutant melanoma on the ME2-ME3 axis is weaker. This context-dependent behavior reflects the complexity of metabolic synthetic lethality, suggesting the need for patient stratification on the basis of specific molecular tumor characteristics.
From a translational medicine perspective, strategies targeting the ME2‒ME3 axis face challenges, primarily because of the current lack of highly selective ME3 inhibitors. However, on the basis of this synthetic lethality principle, researchers are exploring indirect intervention strategies, such as amplifying metabolic stress in ME2-deficient cells by modulating mitochondrial membrane potential or reactive oxygen species levels, providing alternative pathways for clinical translation.

## Cross-Talk between metabolic abnormalities and genomic stability
The cross-talk between metabolic abnormalities and genomic stability provides another critical dimension for synthetic lethality [ ]. The accumulation of oncogenic metabolites can directly or indirectly disrupt DNA repair mechanisms, creating opportunities for synthetic lethality when combined with DNA damage response inhibitors.
The accumulation of 2-hydroxyglutarate (2-HG) caused by isocitrate dehydrogenase 1/2 (IDH1/2) mutations exemplifies this mechanism [ ]. As a structural analog of α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), 2-HG competitively inhibits multiple α-KG-dependent dioxygenases, including histone demethylases and DNA demethylases. Research has indicated that 2-HG disrupts chromatin signaling at DNA damage sites by inhibiting the histone demethylase lysine demethylase 4B (KDM4B), leading to an abnormal increase in the level of histone H3 lysine 9 trimethylation (H3K9me3). Specifically, the transient peak of H3K9me3 following DNA double-strand breaks in normal cells is essential for recruiting the Tip60 (KAT5, lysine acetyltransferase 5)-A™ complex and initiating homologous recombination repair. In IDH-mutated cells, however, the baseline H3K9me3 level is abnormally elevated, masking the damage-induced signaling peak. This leads to impaired A™ recruitment, defective DNA end excision, and reduced RAD51 foci formation.
This IDH mutation-induced HR defect renders tumor cells highly sensitive to PARP inhibitors. Studies have demonstrated that compared with wild-type cells, IDH1-mutated glioblastoma cells and fumarate hydratase (FH)-deficient renal carcinoma cells exhibit 3- to 5-fold increased sensitivity to PARP inhibitors. Supplementing α-KG or overexpressing KDM4B reversed abnormal H3K9me3 patterns and repaired defects, restoring sensitivity to PARP inhibitors and confirming the causal relationship between metabolic abnormalities and DNA repair defects.
Similar mechanisms have been observed in tumors with succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) and fumarate hydratase (FH) defects [ ]. SDH mutations cause succinate accumulation, inhibiting the expression of multiple α-KG-dependent histone and DNA demethylases; [ ] FH mutations lead to the accumulation of fumarate, which not only suppresses α-KG-dependent enzymes but also directly alkylates DNA and proteins, inducing DNA damage [ ]. These metabolic abnormalities collectively increase sensitivity to PARP inhibitors, providing a theoretical basis for precision therapy in related tumors.
From a clinical translation perspective, metabolism-targeted synthetic lethal strategies hold significant value [ ]. First, metabolic abnormalities are often driven by specific mutations, providing ideal biomarkers. Second, metabolic states can be noninvasively monitored by imaging or metabolomics, facilitating treatment response assessment. Finally, drugs targeting metabolic abnormalities may exhibit synergistic effects when combined with traditional DNA-damaging agents, enhancing therapeutic efficacy while reducing toxicity.
However, metabolic synthetic lethality strategies also face challenges. Tumor metabolism is highly plastic and heterogeneous, potentially leading to resistance through metabolic reprogramming. Additionally, metabolic pathways perform critical functions in normal tissues, necessitating careful evaluation of therapeutic windows. Future research should integrate multiomics approaches to systematically decipher the interactions between metabolic networks and genetic backgrounds and identify patient populations most likely to benefit from metabolism-directed synthetic lethality strategies.
In addition to the oncometabolite-mediated mechanisms discussed above, a conceptually distinct form of metabolic synthetic lethality has recently emerged through the paradigm of synthetic dosage lethality (SDL). Unlike classical synthetic lethality, which involves loss-of-function mutations in both genes, SDL occurs when overexpression of a gene (typically an oncogene) results in a dependency on another gene that is not essential in normal cells. This concept is particularly relevant for targeting oncogenes that are difficult to inhibit directly, such as Polo-like kinase 1 (PLK1).
PLK1 is overexpressed in a wide range of cancers and drives chromosomal instability, yet direct PLK1 inhibitors have shown limited clinical success because of off-target toxicity and narrow therapeutic windows [ ]. To overcome this challenge, a genome-wide SDL screen was performed to identify genes whose inhibition selectively kills PLK1-overexpressing cells. This approach identified IGF2BP2 as a critical SDL partner—a finding validated by both in vivo and in vitro CRISPR screens at single-cell resolution. Mechanistically, the loss of IGF2BP2 disrupts mitochondrial metabolism by downregulating PLK1 expression and suppressing the expression of oxidative phosphorylation genes, leading to impaired ATP production and the selective elimination of PLK1-overexpressing cancer cells. Pharmacological inhibition of IGF2BP2 confirmed this synthetic dosage lethal interaction, demonstrating that targeting metabolic vulnerability can circumvent the challenges associated with direct oncogene inhibition. This work established SDL as a powerful framework for identifying context-dependent metabolic dependencies in tumors driven by oncogene overexpression.
This concept aligns with a broader emerging paradigm in which targeting metabolic vulnerabilities—whether through classical synthetic lethality or SDL—is increasingly recognized as a key strategy to overcome therapeutic resistance, particularly in the context of oncogene-driven metabolic reprogramming [ ]. 

## Epigenetic regulation
Epigenetic regulation precisely controls gene expression patterns by dynamically modifying chromatin states without altering DNA sequences and plays a pivotal role in tumorigenesis and progression. Recent studies have revealed that mutations in epigenetic regulators can create unique vulnerabilities by disrupting epigenetic homeostasis, which can be exploited through targeted inhibition of compensatory pathways. Dysfunction in chromatin remodeling complexes, histone modification enzymes, and reader proteins creates unique vulnerabilities by disrupting epigenetic balance. This section systematically explores synthetic lethal effects arising from chromatin remodeling complex mutations, focusing on the synthetic lethal relationships among paralogous subunits within the SWI/SNF complex, and discusses novel synthetic lethal paradigms in epigenetic regulation.

## Vulnerability of chromatin remodeling complex mutations
Chromatin remodeling complexes regulate key biological processes—including gene transcription, DNA replication, and repair—through ATP-dependent changes in nucleosome positioning and structure. Among these, the mammalian switch/sucrose nonfermentable mSWI/SNF) complex is among the most extensively studied epigenetic regulatory complexes, with mutations in its subunits detected in approximately 20% of human cancers [ ]. These mutations disrupt chromatin remodeling functions, leading to gene expression dysregulation and promoting tumorigenesis. Crucially, SWI/SNF complex mutations reveal the dependence of cancer cells on specific epigenetic regulatory pathways, offering an ideal window for synthetic lethal interventions [ ]. 
In malignant rhabdomyosarcoma (MRT) and atypical teratoid rhabdomyosarcoma (ATRT), biallelic inactivating mutations in SMARCB1 (encoding the BAF47 protein) are nearly universal. As a core component of the SWI/SNF complex, SMARCB1 deficiency leads to reduced expression of cell cycle inhibitors (e.g., CDKN2A), tumor suppressors (e.g., BIN1), and neuron differentiation-related genes (e.g., CD133 and DOCK4) while abnormally activating oncogenic pathways such as Hedgehog and MYC. Studies have indicated that SMARCB1 loss results in specific sensitivity to enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) inhibition in cancer cells, establishing a classic synthetic lethal relationship. EZH2, the catalytic subunit of the polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2), catalyzes the trimethylation of histone H3 lysine 27 (H3K27me3), thereby suppressing target gene transcription. In SMARCB1 wild-type cells, the SWI/SNF complex maintains an antagonistic equilibrium with PRC2 in gene regulation [ ]. However, SMARCB1 deficiency disrupts this balance, leading to abnormally elevated PRC2 activity, which is critically important for cancer cell survival.
Preclinical studies have confirmed that the selective EZH2 inhibitor EPZ-6438 effectively suppresses proliferation in SMARCB1-deficient MRT cells and induces complete tumor regression in mouse xenograft models [ ]. Mechanistically, EPZ-6438 competitively binds the S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) substrate binding site of EZH2, inhibiting its methyltransferase activity and leading to a significant reduction in H3K27me3 levels. In SMARCB1-deficient cells, EZH2 inhibition releases PRC2 suppression on target genes and restores the expression of cell cycle inhibitors (CDKN1A and CDKN2A), tumor suppressors (BIN1), and neuronal differentiation genes (CD133 and PTPRK) while downregulating the expression of Hedgehog pathway genes (GLI1) and MYC. Notably, EZH2 inhibition exerts minimal effects on SMARCB1 wild-type cells, indicating marked selectivity. This synthetic lethal relationship not only provides novel therapeutic strategies for MRT/ATRT but also suggests extensive synthetic lethal networks involving chromatin remodeling complex mutations and histone modifier inhibition [ ]. 
In addition to the SMARCB1-EZH2 axis, other SWI/SNF subunit mutations result in similar vulnerabilities [ ]. For example, ARID1A-mutant cancers depend specifically on ARID1B, whereas SMARCA4 (BRG1)-mutant tumors rely on its homolog SMARCA2 (BRM) for survival [ ]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that chromatin remodeling complex mutations can create a dependence on specific regulatory pathways by altering epigenetic homeostasis, thereby offering opportunities for synthetic lethal strategies [ , ]. 

## SMARCA4-SMARCA2 paralog lethality
The interaction between SMARCA4 (BRG1) and SMARCA2 (BRM) within the SWI/SNF complex exemplifies paralog lethality, in which selective inhibition of the remaining paralog becomes synthetically lethal, leading to preferential elimination of cancer cells harboring the initial mutation. Research into the underlying mechanisms and clinical translation progress offer significant insights for precision cancer therapy.
SMARCA4 and SMARCA2 are mutually exclusive catalytic subunits within the mSWI/SNF complex. Both contain conserved ATPase and bromodomain structures, which are responsible for driving nucleosome sliding or eviction through ATP hydrolysis, thereby regulating chromatin accessibility. Despite high sequence homology (approximately 70% similarity), their functions in normal cells overlap and diverge: SMARCA4 plays a critical role in embryonic development and cellular differentiation, whereas SMARCA2 is more involved in the regulation of proliferation-related genes. This functional redundancy underpins their paralogous lethality: when SMARCA4 is inactivated by mutation, SMARCA2 compensates to maintain the essential functions of the SWI/SNF complex, ensuring cellular survival. Conversely, in SMARCA4 wild-type cells, SMARCA2 function can be replaced by SMARCA4, and its inhibition does not affect cell viability.
Functional epigenetic approaches (e.g., deep-coverage shRNA screening) have revealed that SMARCA4-mutant cancers exhibit high sensitivity to SMARCA2 inhibition [ ]. A screen across 58 cancer cell lines revealed SMARCA2 as the most significantly dependent gene in SMARCA4-mutant cells (p = 2.03 × 10⁻⁷). Mechanistically, SMARCA4 loss impairs the catalytic function of the mSWI/SNF complex, promoting the maintenance of complex activity by upregulating SMARCA2 expression in cancer cells. The inhibition of SMARCA2 results in a complete loss of ATPase activity in the complex, initiating a series of epigenetic perturbations: the core subunit BAF53A (actin-like protein 6 A (ACTL6A)) dissociates, impairing chromatin binding, and genome-wide H3K9me3 levels abnormally increase, causing the silencing of oncogenes; ultimately, the cell cycle is arrested in the G₁ phase and displays a senescence-like phenotype [ ]. Notably, this effect is selective. In SMARCA4 wild-type cells, SMARCA2 inhibition does not affect proliferation. However, in lung, ovarian, and liver cancer cells with homozygous SMARCA4 loss, SMARCA2 knockdown significantly suppresses tumor growth.
In the SMARCA4-mutant NCI-H1299 lung cancer xenograft model, doxycycline-induced SMARCA2 shRNA significantly suppressed tumor growth (the T/C ratio was reduced to 15%), with no recurrence after drug withdrawal. Tumor tissue analysis revealed that SMARCA2 depletion led to elevated H3K9me3 levels, reduced expression of the proliferation marker Ki67 (MKI67), and increased positivity for senescence markers, which is consistent with the in vitro mechanism. In contrast, SMARCA4 wild-type tumors did not respond to SMARCA2 inhibition. These data confirm the in vivo validity of the SMARCA4-SMARCA2 paralog lethality relationship.
From a therapeutic perspective, SMARCA2 offers significant advantages as a drug target. First, its function in normal tissues can be compensated by SMARCA4, potentially enabling a broad therapeutic window for SMARCA2 inhibition. Second, SMARCA2 contains targetable ATPase and bromodomain structures, providing clear directions for small-molecule inhibitor development. Currently, multiple research teams are developing selective SMARCA2 inhibitors, with preclinical models demonstrating significant antitumor activity in SMARCA4-mutant cancers. Furthermore, SMARCA4 mutations occur at a relatively high frequency (10%-15%) in malignant tumors such as lung adenocarcinoma and ovarian cancer, which often lack other targetable driver mutations, making SMARCA2 a highly promising therapeutic target.
The SMARCA4-SMARCA2 paradigm reveals a general rule of allelic lethality in epigenetic regulation. Similar mechanisms have been confirmed in other subunits of the SWI/SNF complex: AT-rich interaction domain 1 A (ARID1A) and AT-rich interaction domain 1B (ARID1B), which are mutually exclusive DNA-binding subunits within the complex, also exhibit allelic lethality. In ARID1A-mutant cancers, ARID1B becomes essential for maintaining SWI/SNF complex integrity; its deletion disassembles the complex, leading to dissociation and degradation of the core catalytic subunit SMARCA4, ultimately causing cell growth arrest. Notably, ARID1A and ARID1B mutations frequently cooccur in cancer, yet all cancer cells retain at least one functional ARID1 allele, further underscoring the critical importance of ARID1 function for cancer cell survival.
The concept of collateral lethality extends beyond the SWI/SNF complex. Research has revealed that other epigenetic regulatory complex members, such as the homologous protein polycomb repressive complex 1 (PRC1) members RING1A and RING1B and histone deacetylases HDAC1 and HDAC2, also exhibit similar functional redundancy and synthetic lethal relationships. These findings suggest that targeting homologous genes of mutated epigenetic regulatory factors may become a universal strategy for treating related cancers [ ]. 

## New paradigms in epigenetic synthetic lethality
Beyond chromatin remodeling complexes, other epigenetic regulatory pathways offer novel opportunities for synthetic lethal strategies. Interactions between histone modification reader proteins and transcription factors, DNA methylation regulation, and noncoding RNA networks have been demonstrated to participate in the construction of synthetic lethal networks. Among these, the synthetic lethal effect of the interaction between bromodomain containing 4 (BRD4) and the transcription factor Twist in basal-like breast cancer (BLBC) represents a new paradigm in epigenetic regulation [ ]. 
In BLBC, the transcription factor Twist—a core regulator of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT)—undergoes dual acetylation at residues K73 and K76 by Tip60 acetyltransferase via its N-terminal “histone H4 mimetic” motif (containing the GK-X-GK sequence). The diacetylated Twist specifically binds to the second bromodomain (BD2) of the BET family protein BRD4, thereby recruiting positive transcription elongation factor b (P-TEFb) (CDK9/Cyclin T complex) and RNA polymerase II to the superenhancer region of the Wnt family member 5 A (WNT5A) gene, activating WNT5A transcription. WNT5A, a noncanonical Wnt pathway ligand, is crucial for maintaining the invasive and cancer stem cell properties of BLBC cells.
Research has revealed that disrupting Twist-BRD4 interactions induces BLBC-specific synthetic lethal effects. Mechanistically, BET bromodomain inhibitors (e.g., JQ1 and MS417) competitively bind the acetyl-lysine binding pocket of BRD4-BD2, blocking its interaction with diacetylated Twist [ ]. This prevents the BRD4/P-TEFb/RNA-PolII complex from being recruited to the WNT5A promoter/enhancer region, leading to impaired WNT5A transcription. Consequently, both classical and noncanonical Wnt signaling pathways are suppressed, reducing the expression of pluripotency markers (CD44, Sox2, and Oct4) and ultimately decreasing cellular invasion and tumor spheroid formation capacity. Notably, this effect is highly selective: JQ1 treatment has a limited effect on Twist-low or non-BLBC cells, whereas Twist knockdown or Twist K73R/K76R mutation completely abolishes the sensitivity of BLBC cells to JQ1 [ ]. 
In vivo studies further validated this synthetic lethality relationship: in the SUM1315 cell xenograft model, treatment with JQ1 (50 mg/kg) or MS417 (20 mg/kg) significantly inhibited tumor growth, accompanied by downregulation of the expression of WNT5A and the proliferation marker Ki67 in tumor tissues. More importantly, Wnt5a-knockdown BLBC cells nearly completely lost their tumorigenic capacity, confirming that WNT5A is a key downstream effector of the Twist-BRD4 axis.
In addition to the Twist-BRD4 axis, synergistic lethal effects on other epigenetic regulatory pathways have emerged. For instance, the histone demethylase lysine demethylase 5 A (KDM5A) has been identified as a synergistic lethal target in KRAS-mutant lung cancer; EZH2 inhibition exhibits synergistic lethal effects in ARID1A-mutant ovarian cancer; and DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) inhibition induces differentiation in tet methylcytosine dioxygenase 2 (TET2)-mutant myeloid tumors. Collectively, these findings broaden the application scope of epigenetic synthetic lethality [ ]. 
Research on synthetic lethality in the field of epigenetic regulation is rapidly transitioning from basic science to clinical practice. With the discovery of more epigenetic vulnerabilities and the development of specific inhibitors, precision therapy based on epigenetic profiling is anticipated in the future. However, challenges persist. For example, the highly context-dependent nature of epigenetic regulation demands precise patient stratification, the off-target effects of epigenetic inhibitors require further optimization, and tumor epigenetic heterogeneity may lead to resistance. By integrating multiomics data, developing highly selective inhibitors, and exploring rational combination strategies, epigenetic synthetic lethality approaches hold promise as novel therapeutic options for refractory cancers (Table  ).
 In terms of the diverse mechanisms discussed above—ranging from DNA damage repair and cell cycle checkpoints to metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic regulation—several common principles that govern the clinical success or failure of synthetic lethal strategies have emerged. First, the robustness of the dependency matters. Successful synthetic lethal targets (e.g., PARP1 in BRCA mutant cancers) are those where the genetic defect creates a critical bottleneck in a parallel pathway that cannot be easily bypassed by compensatory mechanisms. Second, the penetrance of the vulnerability is crucial. Strategies that target genes with high frequency alterations across tumor types (such as TP53 or ARID1A) have broader applicability but often face greater context dependence. Third, the therapeutic window is determined by the degree to which normal tissues express or depend on the target pathway. Successes such as those associated with PARP inhibitors benefit from the fact that normal cells retain intact homologous recombination repair, whereas failures often stem from on-target toxicity in essential tissues. Fourth, tumor heterogeneity and adaptive resistance represent universal barriers that can render even well-validated synthetic lethal interactions clinically ineffective if not prospectively addressed [ ]. These principles provide a framework for understanding why certain targets have translated successfully while others have faltered and underscore the need for multipronged strategies that account for context dependence, lineage specificity, and evolutionary plasticity of tumors.

## Strategies for identifying synthetic lethal targets

## Functional genomics screening
Synthetic lethal interactions are highly dependent on innovations in functional genomics screening technologies. These technologies perturb gene function across the entire genome and apply selective pressure in specific genetic contexts, providing a powerful and virtually unbiased research paradigm for revealing tumor cell-specific dependencies. From early RNA interference to mainstream CRISPR-Cas9 systems and further to cutting-edge single-cell and spatial multiomics technologies, the evolution of screening platforms continuously drives our understanding of the complexity of synthetic lethal networks and their clinical translation.

## Technical paradigms and major discoveries in CRISPR-Cas9 genome-wide screening
In the field of synthetic lethal target discovery, the central role of clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-CRISPR associated protein 9 (Cas9)-based genome-wide knockout screening has been established. The universality of this technology stems from its ability to mimic gene loss-of-function effects, directly indicating potential therapeutic targets [ ]. By delivering a genome-wide library of single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) into a pool of cells expressing Cas9 and tracking the changes in sgRNA abundance at different time points using deep sequencing, the effects of gene knockouts on cellular fitness can be systematically evaluated [ ]. 
In synthetic lethality studies, the critical design involves parallel comparisons of isogenic cell lines harboring specific driver mutations (e.g., KRAS G12V or BRCA1 deletion) with their wild-type counterparts to assess survival differences after identical genetic perturbations. Genes targeted by sgRNAs that cause significant cell viability decline exclusively in the mutant context are identified as synthetic lethal partners in that context [ ]. This strategy has been used to successfully identify multiple targets with therapeutic potential [ ]. For example, using CRISPR screening, multiple teams have independently identified the essential role of the nonreceptor tyrosine kinase TBK1 in models of KRAS-mutant pancreatic and colorectal cancers [ ]. 
However, the choice of cell culture model profoundly influences the outcome of such screens. Synthetic lethal interactions identified in conventional two-dimensional (2D) monolayer cultures may not fully recapitulate the complexities of the tumor microenvironment. Three-dimensional (3D) culture systems, including spheroids and organoids, have revealed context-dependent vulnerabilities that are masked in 2D assays. For example, a 2025 study using a MYC-driven murine liver cancer model revealed more than 600 essential genes shared between 2D and 3D conditions but also revealed context-specific fitness genes [ ]. Notably, inactivation of epigenetic modifiers, including Bcor, Kmt2d, Mettl3 and Mettl14, had opposite effects on cell fitness in 2D versus 3D culture. Most importantly, this study revealed 3D-dependent synthetic lethality with partial loss of Prmt5 resulting from reduced Mtap expression caused by 3D-specific epigenetic reprogramming. These findings demonstrate that the dimensionality of culture systems can create or abolish synthetic lethal interactions, highlighting the need for validation in more physiologically relevant models.
Furthermore, competitive coculture assays using fluorescently labeled isogenic cell lines enable direct visualization of synthetic lethal effects in a mixed population. A recent study established BRCA1 isogenic pairs from triple-negative breast cancer cells and noncancerous RPE cells, each labeled with distinct fluorescent reporters [ ]. When cells were cocultured and treated with PARP inhibitors, the preferential killing of BRCA1-deficient cells was directly observable by flow cytometry, and subtle differences between PARP inhibitor-resistant lines and BRCA1-proficient cells could be distinguished. This approach more closely mimics the competitive environment within heterogeneous tumors and provides a sensitive platform for validating synthetic lethal interactions.
Beyond target discovery, CRISPR screening has also shown great potential in dissecting the mechanisms of resistance in known synthetic lethal interactions [ ]. For example, in a PARP inhibitor-resistant model, screening not only reaffirmed the importance of genes in the homologous recombination repair pathway but also unexpectedly revealed mutations in the PARP1 protein itself as a major cause of resistance. These mutations impair the protein’s ability to be ‘trapped’ by the inhibitor rather than directly altering its catalytic activity, thereby profoundly refining the understanding of the mechanism of action of PARP inhibitors [ ]. Similar screenings have also revealed that the deletion of genes related to replication fork protection increases sensitivity to WEE1 inhibitors.

## New dimensions in single-cell and spatial functional genomics
Although pooled CRISPR screens are powerful, the population-averaged phenotypic readouts they provide cannot capture how cellular heterogeneity affects responses to genetic perturbations. This limitation is compounded by the intrinsic differences between cancer cell lines derived from distinct tissues of origin. A 2024 study investigating the histogenetic origin of malignant cells demonstrated that susceptibility to a synthetic lethal suicide gene system (TK.007/ganciclovir) varied dramatically across cell types: lymphoma and leukemia cells were significantly more sensitive than solid cancer cells were, with osteosarcoma and melanoma exhibiting intermediate susceptibility [ ]. This lineage-specific dependency highlights that synthetic lethal interactions are not universally transferable across tumor types, even when the same genetic perturbation is applied. This limitation is being addressed by emerging single-cell functional genomics technologies, such as Perturb-seq, which integrates CRISPR screening with single-cell RNA sequencing [ ]. This technology enables the simultaneous reading of sgRNA (i.e., perturbation identity) and the complete transcriptome expression profile within a single cell, increasing the resolution of genotype‒phenotype association studies to the single-cell level.
In research on synthetic lethality, this high-resolution approach can reveal unique biological insights. First, it can dissect the heterogeneous response mechanisms within cell populations subjected to the same genetic perturbation. For instance, Perturb-seq has revealed that after a synthetic lethal target is knocked out, some cells survive by activating specific compensatory signaling pathways, such as stress responses or metabolic reprogramming. These pathways themselves can serve as potential targets for combination therapies aimed at preventing or overcoming resistance. Second, this approach helps elucidate how cell states regulate synthetic lethal effects, for example, by dynamically linking phenotypic changes induced by genetic perturbations to intrinsic stem-like states or epithelial–mesenchymal transition states, thereby identifying the most vulnerable cell subpopulations.
Second, this technology aids in elucidating how cellular states modulate synthetic lethal effects. Tumor cells in distinct differentiation or cycle states may exhibit varying sensitivities to specific genetic perturbations. Perturb-seq dynamically links phenotypical changes induced by genetic disruption to intrinsic cellular states—such as stem-like properties or epithelial‒mesenchymal transition states—thereby identifying the most vulnerable cell subpopulations. For instance, recent studies have revealed that in MYC-driven tumors, cell subpopulations in specific metabolic states exhibit hypersensitivity to mitochondrial complex I inhibition, which is a state-dependent synthetic lethal relationship that is difficult to capture via conventional screening methods.
In the future, the integration of intracellular CRISPR screening with single-cell or spatial transcriptomics holds promise for elucidating synthetic lethal interactions within the intact tumor microenvironment [ ]. By analyzing the interaction networks between tumor cells and immune cells, fibroblasts, and other cell types under genetic perturbations, it is possible to identify novel synthetic lethal vulnerabilities dependent on intercellular communication, thereby opening new avenues for microenvironment-tailored precision immunotherapy or combination therapies [ ]. 
In summary, functional genomics screening technologies themselves are undergoing a profound transformation from merely providing target lists to mapping intricate mechanistic landscapes. The core role of high-throughput screening technologies, such as CRISPR-Cas9, has been established in target discovery. Moreover, the integration of single-cell and spatial multiomics technologies can be used to reveal the dynamics and context dependence of synthetic lethal interactions at unprecedented resolution, laying a solid foundation for ultimately achieving precision therapy based on individual tumor genetic backgrounds and cellular states.

## Computational biology and the discovery of synthetic lethal targets
With the exponential growth of high-throughput omics data, computational biology has become a key driver for systematically discovering and validating synthetic lethal interactions. Compared with traditional experimental screening, computational methods can integrate multidimensional data, reveal potential genetic interaction networks, and predict synthetic lethal targets with clinical translational value, significantly enhancing the efficiency and accuracy of target discovery.

## Dependency-based synthetic lethal gene prediction
The core of dependency-based prediction models lies in leveraging large-scale gene expression, mutation, and gene dependency data from cancer cell lines. Computational methods identify gene perturbations that impair cell survival specifically in the context of certain gene mutations [ ]. The fundamental assumption is that if the knockout or low expression of gene B significantly affects cell viability only in cell lines with gene A mutation but has a minimal effect in cell lines with wild-type gene A, then genes A and B form a potential synthetic lethal pair.
The Cancer Dependency Map (DepMap) released by the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard serves as a model resource for such research [ ]. This database systematically contains genomic, transcriptomic, and CRISPR-Cas9 gene knockout screening data from thousands of cancer cell lines. By calculating dependency scores for each gene across different genetic backgrounds and employing statistical models (such as linear regression and machine learning algorithms) to control for systematic biases between cell lines, researchers can infer synthetic lethal relationships between gene pairs at scale [ ]. For example, analysis of DepMap data has revealed that cancer cell lines harboring ARID1A mutations exhibit hypersensitivity to ARID1B knockout, strongly corroborating experimentally validated cross-lethality relationships and underscoring the reliability of computational predictions.
The statistical power of such analyses hinges on meticulous control design. Ideal predictive models require rigorous parallel comparisons of phenotypic differences between mutant and wild-type cell lines under identical genetic perturbations. To mitigate noise from tumor heterogeneity, advanced computational methods—such as analysis of variance (ANOVA) combined with multiple hypothesis testing correction and regularized regression models (e.g., least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO))—are widely used to identify gene pairs whose effects differ most significantly across diverse genetic backgrounds [ ]. For instance, when predicting synthetic lethal partners for KRAS-mutant cancer cells, computational models integrate CRISPR screening data from more than 300 KRAS-mutant and wild-type cell lines. This approach successfully identified the known partner TBK1 and a novel candidate gene, cyclin Y-like 1 (CCNYL1), and subsequent experiments confirmed that it specifically inhibited the proliferation of KRAS-mutant cells [ ]. However, the predictive power of dependency maps derived from 2D-cultured cell lines may be limited when these maps are applied to tumors growing in their native 3D environment. A 2023 study using chromosome-engineered hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines demonstrated that large-scale chromosomal deletions create acquired vulnerabilities that can be identified through genome-wide CRISPR screening in isogenic 3D culture systems [ ]. By engineering heterozygous loss of chromosome 8p—a frequent deletion in liver cancer—and performing CRISPR screens in isogenic cell pairs, one study identified NUDT17 as a synthetic lethal target specific to 8p-deleted cells. This approach, which combined chromosome engineering with functional genomics in a defined genetic background, illustrates how model systems that more faithfully recapitulate tumor genetics and architecture can reveal context-specific vulnerabilities.

## Biological network and pathway analysis
Biological network and pathway analyses advance synthetic lethality research from the “gene pair” level to the “pathway and network” level [ ]. By integrating known protein interactions, signaling pathways, and metabolic networks, these analyses systematically reveal the topological patterns and biological logic underlying synthetic lethal interactions.
The core methodology underlying these analyses is the “pathway complementarity” hypothesis: if two genes reside in parallel, functionally redundant pathways within the same essential biological process, a genetic defect in either pathway renders cells sensitive to inhibition of the other pathway [ ]. On this basis, researchers have computationally analyzed pathway databases (e.g., Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Reactome) and protein interaction networks (e.g., Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) and Biological General Repository for Interaction Datasets (BioGRID)) to identify functionally complementary pathway modules [ ]. For example, computational analysis has revealed extensive synthetic lethal interactions between the DNA homologous recombination repair (HRR) and nonhomologous end-joining (NHEJ) pathways, providing a theoretical blueprint for developing combination therapies targeting DNA repair-deficient tumors [ ]. 
Network centrality analysis is another critical strategy. This approach posits that genes occupying pivotal hub positions within protein interaction networks are more likely to have their functional loss amplified by mutations in other genes. By calculating topological metrics such as degree centrality and betweenness centrality, computationally prioritized fragile targets susceptible to synthetic lethality can be identified [ ]. For instance, analysis of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex interaction network revealed that its catalytic subunit SMARCA4 exhibits high connectivity [ ]. Deletion of SMARCA4 alters local network topology, inducing dependency on its paralog SMARCA2—a finding highly consistent with experimental observations.
Furthermore, computational approaches based on gene coexpression and functional similarity can effectively predict synthetic lethality [ ]. The underlying principle is that gene pairs with high functional correlation or highly coordinated expression are more likely to participate in the same biological process, thereby exhibiting functional redundancy. By calculating gene pair expression correlations (e.g., Pearson correlation coefficient) or functional similarity (e.g., based on Gene Ontology semantic similarity), functional association networks can be constructed to infer potential synthetic lethal relationships. Research integrating multiomics data from breast cancer has constructed tumor-specific functional networks, successfully predicting synthetic lethal effects between PTEN loss and KDM5A inhibition, offering new perspectives for the precise classification of breast cancer.

## Biomarker discovery and optimization
The successful clinical application of synthetic lethal therapies critically depends on biomarkers that can accurately identify responsive populations [ ]. Computational biology plays an indispensable role in biomarker discovery, optimization, and validation by integrating genomic, transcriptomic, epigenomic, and clinical data.
In the biomarker discovery phase, computational methods assess clinical relevance and estimate the potential beneficial population by correlating predicted or validated synthetic lethality relationships with large-scale cancer genome datasets, such as The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC) [ ]. During the optimization phase, considering the limited predictive power of single-gene features, more robust computational models are constructed by integrating multidimensional features. For instance, when sensitivity to PARP inhibitors is predicted, in addition to the BRCA1/2 mutation status, the homologous recombination deficiency score derived from integrated genomic scar features has been shown to more accurately identify patients who are likely to benefit.
Second, for biomarker optimization, single-gene mutations or expression levels may be insufficient for accurate treatment response prediction. Computational models can integrate multidimensional features to construct more robust predictive signatures. For example,, in addition to BRCA1/2 mutation status for predicting sensitivity to PARP inhibitors, computationally integrated “homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) scores” (quantitative metrics for synthesizing genomic scars such as loss of heterozygosity (LOH), telomeric allelic imbalance (TAI), and large-scale state transitions (LST)) have been demonstrated to more accurately identify ovarian and breast cancer patients who are responsive to PARP inhibitors. This multivariate biomarker model significantly improved patient selection accuracy.
In addition, machine learning-based classifiers can be used to extract the most predictive feature combinations from complex high-dimensional data to construct efficacy prediction models. Moreover, computational biology, through the analysis of multiomics data from samples collected before and after treatment, aids in understanding and overcoming resistance mechanisms, thereby enabling the dynamic optimization of biomarker combinations. For instance, bioinformatics analysis of tumor samples from patients resistant to PARP inhibitors has revealed multiple resistance mechanisms, including BRCA1/2 reversion mutations and 53BP1 loss. These findings have prompted the incorporation of these resistance-associated genes into next-generation biomarker panels, enabling iterative biomarker optimization.
In summary, computational biology has significantly advanced the discovery and clinical translation of synthetic lethal targets through three functions: dependency prediction, network analysis, and biomarker development. It transforms vast amounts of omics data into actionable biological insights, forming a powerful complement to functional genomics screening. Together, computational biology and functional genomics screening constitute the two main approaches of the synthetic lethal research paradigm. With further advancements in artificial intelligence and multiomics integration technologies, computational biology holds promise for revealing deeper genetic vulnerabilities in tumors and providing novel prospects for precision cancer therapy.

## Clinical translation and application

## PARP inhibitors: translation from the concept of synthetic lethality to clinical practice paradigms
The clinical translation of PARP inhibitors represents a landmark achievement in the application of synthetic lethality to oncology, yet it also highlights the challenges inherent in targeted therapy, including resistance and limited duration of benefits. The progression of PARP inhibitors from scientific breakthrough to clinical application exemplifies the core principle of precision medicine of selecting the most effective treatment strategies on the basis of the specific genetic profiles of tumors. This section systematically outlines the clinical success and expanded indications of PARP inhibitors, delves into their resistance mechanisms, and explores future combination therapy strategies.

## Clinical success and indication expansion
As detailed in Section " ", the synthetic lethal interaction between BRCA deficiency and PARP inhibition provided a rationale for targeting BRCA-mutated tumors with PARP inhibitors. Clinical translation of this concept began with BRCA1/2-mutated tumors, where loss of homologous recombination repair renders cells highly sensitive to PARP inhibition, leading to the first regulatory approval of a synthetic lethal agent.
Olaparib, the first approved PARP inhibitor, demonstrated significant efficacy in a subset of patients with advanced serous ovarian cancer in its landmark Phase III trial, highlighting both the potential and the limitations of response durability [ ]. Multiple PARP inhibitors (including niraparib, rucaparib, and talazoparib) subsequently gained approval, expanding their indications to include breast, pancreatic, prostate, and other cancers. However, the extent of benefit varies widely across tumor types and genetic contexts, and many patients do not respond or eventually relapse [ ]. 
The clinical application of PARP inhibitors has successfully progressed from subsequent-line therapy to first-line maintenance treatment and even earlier stages [ ]. Pivotal Phase III clinical trials (such as SOLO-1, POLO, and PROfound) have demonstrated that PARP inhibitors can significantly prolong progression-free survival and/or overall survival in patients with advanced ovarian, pancreatic, or prostate cancer harboring BRCA1/2 germline or somatic mutations [ ]. Notably, the PROfound study confirmed that the population benefiting from treatment could be extended to patients with mutations in other homologous recombination repair-related genes (such as ATM), reflecting the value of the ‘BRCAness’ phenotype in guiding clinical application [ ]. Despite the initial wave of approval, PARP inhibitors faced significant regulatory reversals between 2022 and 2023, with the FDA withdrawing or narrowing six indications in advanced ovarian cancer after four randomized trials showed potential detriment to overall survival. Indications for the use of niraparib, olaparib, and rucaparib in BRCA-mutated or HRD-positive advanced ovarian cancer patients were withdrawn and their use in the maintenance of recurrent ovarian cancer was narrowed to only BRCA-mutated patients [ ]. These findings underscore that long-term survival benefits are not guaranteed even for established synthetic lethal strategies.

## Primary mechanisms of resistance
Despite the significant efficacy of PARP inhibitors, the emergence of resistance remains a major clinical challenge that limits their long-term utility. Resistance mechanisms are diverse and include the restoration of homologous recombination repair function (e.g., through BRCA1/2 reversion mutations or 53BP1 loss), alterations in drug targets (e.g., PARP1 mutations that impair trapping), enhanced replication fork stability, the upregulation of drug efflux pumps, and tumor microenvironment-mediated adaptations [ ]. A detailed discussion of these mechanisms and their clinical implications is included in Sect. (Resistance Management Strategies), where we also discuss approaches to overcome resistance. In BRCA1/2-deficient cells, PARP inhibition causes replication fork collapse [ ]. However, some resistant cells survive by enhancing replication fork stability. EZH2 inhibition promotes fork reversal by increasing histone H3 lysine 27 acetylation, which is a protective mechanism. Conversely, EZH2 overexpression is correlated with fork instability and sensitivity to PARP inhibitors. Furthermore, RAD51 stability and recruitment to replication forks are critical for maintaining fork stability, and alterations in its regulatory factors may influence PARP inhibitor sensitivity.
The overexpression of drug efflux pumps such as P-glycoprotein (encoded by the multidrug resistance 1 (MDR1)/ATP binding cassette subfamily B member 1 (ABCB1) gene) is a classic resistance mechanism against multiple chemotherapeutic agents and is also implicated in resistance to PARP inhibitors. Preclinical studies have indicated that cells overexpressing P-glycoprotein exhibit reduced sensitivity to olaparib and talazoparib, with this resistance being reversible by P-glycoprotein inhibitors.
Downregulation or loss-of-function mutations in PARP1 can lead to resistance to PARP inhibitors. Specifically, mutations in the DNA-binding domain of PARP1 (e.g., p.R591C) impair the ability of PARP1 to be “trapped” on chromatin, a process considered a key mechanism for PARP inhibitor cytotoxicity. These mutations have been detected in clinically resistant samples, offering new insights into resistance mechanisms.
Hypoxic conditions within the tumor microenvironment can induce sensitivity to PARP inhibitors by downregulating the expression of genes related to homologous recombination repair. However, prolonged hypoxic adaptation may also promote the evolution of resistant clones through selection pressure. Additionally, the activation of pro-survival signaling pathways, such as phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT)/mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR), has been shown to decrease the efficacy of PARP inhibitors, whereas combining these pathways with PI3K/AKT pathway inhibitors may restore sensitivity.
In-depth investigations of resistance mechanisms have revealed remarkable plasticity in tumor cells in response to PARP inhibition pressure. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for developing novel combination strategies, reversing resistance, and rationally planning treatment sequences.

## Combination therapy strategies
To broaden the patient population benefiting from PARP inhibitors, enhance their efficacy, and overcome resistance, multiple combination therapy strategies are being actively explored. These approaches leverage insights into DNA damage response networks, aiming to generate synergistic antitumor effects through multitargeted interventions.
PARP inhibitors can be combined with DNA-damaging agents. Platinum-based drugs induce interstrand DNA crosslinking, and their combination with PARP inhibitors theoretically enhances the killing of DNA repair-deficient tumors. Clinical studies have shown the promising activity of a PARP inhibitor plus platinum chemotherapy in ovarian and lung cancers. However, overlapping toxicities such as bone marrow suppression limit their clinical application, making optimized dosing regimens a future research focus. Additionally, the combination of PARP inhibitors with topoisomerase I inhibitors (e.g., irinotecan) is under investigation, with preclinical data indicating synergistic effects in BRCA-mutant models [ ]. 
The theoretical basis for combining PARP inhibitors with immune checkpoint inhibitors is that DNA damage induced by PARP inhibitors can enhance antitumor immune responses by increasing tumor mutation burden, promoting neoantigen release, and activating the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway and may also upregulate PD-L1 expression (Fig. ). Clinical studies such as MEDIOLA and TOPACIO have demonstrated that olaparib or niraparib combined with programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors yield durable clinical benefits in patients with BRCA-mutated ovarian cancer and triple-negative breast cancer [ ]. However, not all patients benefit, making the identification of predictive biomarkers a key future focus.
 Combining PARP inhibitors with antiangiogenic agents may theoretically increase tumor sensitivity to PARP inhibitors. Antiangiogenic drugs induce tumor hypoxia, and the hypoxic environment downregulates key homologous recombination repair genes. The PAOLA-1 study confirmed that olaparib plus bevacizumab significantly extended PFS compared with bevacizumab monotherapy in first-line maintenance therapy for advanced ovarian cancer, with particularly pronounced benefits in the homologous recombination repair-deficient population. This combination strategy offers a new option for patients with homologous recombination repair deficiency regardless of BRCA status.
Rational combination strategies aim to both enhance efficacy and overcome or prevent resistance. For example, preclinical studies have indicated that PI3K inhibitors downregulate HRD gene expression and enhance the efficacy of PARP inhibitors. Furthermore, combining PARP inhibitors with cell cycle checkpoint inhibitors such as ATR, CHK1, or WEE1 can further increase efficacy in HRD tumors by abolishing the G2/M checkpoint and inducing mitotic catastrophe [ ]. 
Combining PARP inhibitors with epigenetic agents—such as histone deacetylase inhibitors and DNA methyltransferase inhibitors—can alter chromatin structure to influence DNA repair protein recruitment, thereby increasing PARP inhibitor sensitivity. Preclinical studies have indicated that HDAC inhibitors downregulate BRCA1 and RAD51 expression, thereby enhancing the efficacy of PARP inhibitors. Multiple early-phase clinical trials are currently evaluating the safety and efficacy of such combination strategies.
With increasing insights into DNA damage response networks and tumor cell adaptation mechanisms, combination strategies involving PARP inhibitors will become more precise and personalized. Future challenges lie in identifying patient populations most likely to benefit from specific combination regimens and balancing efficacy with toxicity to maximize clinical benefit.
The evolution of PARP inhibitors elegantly demonstrates that synthetic lethality is not only a powerful fundamental scientific concept but also a core engine driving the transformation of precision oncology. From its initial success in BRCA-mutated ovarian cancer to its current expansion across multiple cancer types, the story of PARP inhibitors is far from over. A more in-depth understanding of resistance mechanisms and the development of innovative combination strategies will continue to propel this field forward, bringing hope to more patients (Table  ).
 

## Emerging synthetic lethality-targeted therapies
The success of PARP inhibitors has significantly spurred the development of drugs targeting other synthetic lethal pathways. With advances in insights into DNA damage response (DDR) networks, cell cycle regulation, and epigenetic remodeling, a series of emerging targeted therapies are rapidly progressing from preclinical research to clinical practice. These therapies are intended to expand the frontiers of precision medicine, overcome resistance to existing therapies (including PARP inhibitors), and provide new options for patients with tumors lacking traditional targetable driver mutations. This section focuses on the clinical translation progress of three promising emerging synthetic lethality-targeting drugs: ATR inhibitors, WEE1 inhibitors, and PRMT5 inhibitors.

## Clinical progress of ATR inhibitors
Ataxia-telangiectasia-related kinase 3 (ATR), a central kinase in the DDR network, plays a critical role in the response to replication stress and DNA damage. The synthetic lethality strategy targeting ATR is based on two primary principles. First, in tumors with loss of G1/S checkpoint function (e.g., as a result of TP53 loss), cancer cells become highly dependent on the ATR-CHK1 axis to maintain G2/M checkpoint survival (see Sect.  ). Second, in tumors with HR defects (e.g., BRCA mutations), the ATR signaling pathway is often basally activated as a compensatory survival mechanism. Inhibiting ATR synergizes with PARP inhibitors to induce stronger synthetic lethal effects.
Preclinical studies have shown that ATR inhibitors exhibit significant activity in models with acquired resistance to PARP inhibitors, potentially through mechanisms involving the disruption of replication fork stability and the inhibition of residual ATR-dependent repair. Furthermore, ATR inhibition also results in significant synthetic lethality in the context of A™ deficiency, as A™ and ATR functionally overlap in the DDR network [ ]. 
The results of the CAPRI trial represent a milestone in the clinical translation of ATR inhibitors [ ]. This study systematically demonstrated for the first time in humans that the combination of the ATR inhibitor ceralasertib with the PARP inhibitor olaparib results in substantial activity and favorable tolerability in patients with HR-deficient, platinum-sensitive high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) who have acquired resistance to PARP inhibitors. The 50% objective response rate (ORR) and median progression-free survival (PFS) of 7.43 months are particularly noteworthy in this heavily pretreated, extremely poor-prognosis population. The study rigorously defined “acquired resistance” (requiring prior benefit from PARPi treatment), ensuring population homogeneity and validating the synthetic lethality principle and that ceralasertib can restore sensitivity to olaparib in tumors resistant to PARPis.
Combining ATR inhibitors with DNA-damaging chemotherapy is another key approach. A Phase I study (M6620 plus topotecan) has demonstrated the feasibility of this strategy [ ]. The study established the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) for the combination regimen and observed antitumor activity in heavily pretreated patients with advanced solid tumors. Particularly encouraging response rates and PFS were achieved in patients with platinum-refractory small cell lung cancer (SCLC), which is typically associated with an extremely poor prognosis. In vivo pharmacodynamic analysis (enhanced γH2AX signaling in hair follicles) confirmed the potent inhibitory effect of M6620 on ATR and its ability to potentiate topotecan-induced DNA double-strand breaks, confirming the synergistic mechanism. SCLC frequently exhibits high replication stress and TP53 mutation, making it an ideal target for ATR inhibitor combination therapy.
Currently, multiple ATR inhibitors (e.g., ceralasertib, berzosertib/M6620, and camonsertib/RP-3500) are in clinical development [ ]. The challenges for these inhibitors include the following: (1) Optimizing combination strategies and dosing regimens: Optimal combinations with PARPi, chemotherapy, or immune checkpoint inhibitors should be determined, and intermittent dosing schedules to balance efficacy and hematologic toxicity should be designed. (2) Biomarker development: In addition to TP53 mutation and HRD status, more precise predictive markers such as replication stress scores, specific gene mutations (e.g., ARID1A), or functional assays (e.g., baseline activation levels of the ATR signaling pathway) should be identified [ ]. (3) Overcoming resistance: Resistance mechanisms specific to ATR inhibitors should be investigated and the corresponding combination strategies developed.

## WEE1 inhibitor development
WEE1 kinase is another key negative regulator of the G2/M checkpoint. Its inhibition leads to premature activation of CDK1/2, forcing cells with DNA damage to undergo mitosis and triggering “mitotic catastrophe”. The most typical synthetic lethality context for WEE1 inhibitor development involves TP53 mutations. As described in Section " ", TP53 loss causes cancer cells to lose the G1/S checkpoint, making them highly dependent on WEE1-mediated G2/M control. Thus, inhibiting WEE1 selectively kills TP53-mutant tumors. Furthermore, in tumors with defective homologous recombination (e.g., BRCA mutations), WEE1 inhibition has synergistic effects with those of PARP inhibitors. These effects may stem from the dual role of WEE1 in regulating replication fork stability and HR repair processes.
Adavosertib (AZD1775) is the most advanced WEE1 inhibitor. Multiple Phase I/II clinical trials have evaluated its efficacy as a monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapy (gemcitabine, cisplatin, carboplatin, etc.), radiotherapy, or targeted agents. In TP53-mutated solid tumors such as ovarian cancer and osteosarcoma, compared with chemotherapy alone, adavosertib plus chemotherapy demonstrated superior efficacy. For instance, a Phase II study in TP53-mutated ovarian cancer patients demonstrated a 33% response rate with adavosertib plus carboplatin [ ]. However, its clinical application faces challenges, primarily dose-limiting toxicity (e.g., bone marrow suppression, diarrhea, and fatigue) and some off-target effects.
The challenges facing WEE1 inhibitors are illustrated by a recent Phase II trial of adavosertib in recurrent uterine carcinosarcoma, which is a tumor with TP53 mutations in > 90% of cases and is theoretically ideal for WEE1 synthetic lethality [ ]. Despite the strong rationale, the trial showed limited activity: the objective response rate was 22.2% (2/9 patients), the median progression-free survival was only 2.7 months, and treatment-related adverse events occurred in 88.9% of patients. The study was terminated early because of sponsor discontinuation. This setback demonstrates that even genetically defined targets can fail in translation because of insufficient efficacy or prohibitive toxicity. These disappointing results underscore the need for better predictive biomarkers and more tolerable regimens, which are being addressed through next-generation WEE1 inhibitors such as ZN-c3 and IMP7068, as discussed below.
In addition to TP53-mutated solid tumors, WEE1 inhibitors also show promise in MYC-driven or CCNE1 (cyclin E)-amplified tumors, as these oncogenes induce severe replication stress. The following combination strategies are a future focus: (1) Combination with PARP inhibitors: Preclinical and early clinical data (as referenced in the provided EFFORT trial) indicate that adavosertib combined with olaparib still achieves a 29% ORR in PARPi-resistant patients, despite significantly higher toxicity (76% grade 3/4 events) than the ATRi+PARPi combination in the CAPRI trial [ ]. (2) Combination with immunotherapy: Preclinical evidence suggests that WEE1 inhibition activates the cGAS-STING pathway by inducing DNA damage, increasing tumor mutational burden, enhancing tumor immunogenicity and potentially synergizing with immune checkpoint inhibitors. (3) Combination with DNA-damaging agents: Combinations of various chemotherapies and radiotherapy are being explored to broaden the therapeutic window [ ]. 
To overcome the toxicity and off-target effects of adavosertib, the development of more potent and selective WEE1 inhibitors (e.g., ZN-c3 and IMP7068) is a current research focus. These next-generation drugs aim to improve safety profiles and explore efficacy across broader genetic backgrounds (e.g., KRAS-mutated pancreatic cancer). Concurrently, developing reliable functional biomarkers (e.g., phosphorylated CDK1 levels and replication stress markers) for precise patient selection is critical for advancing WEE1 inhibitor translational success.

## Prospects for PRMT5 inhibitors
Protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5) catalyzes the symmetric dimethylation of arginine residues and participates in the regulation of transcription, splicing, DNA repair, and the cell cycle. Its emergence as a synthetic lethal target is closely linked to the loss of the metabolic gene methylthioadenosine phosphoribosyltransferase (MTAP).
The MTAP gene is located near cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2 A/B (CDKN2A/B), which is a critical tumor suppressor gene and is frequently codeleted with CDKN2A/B in various cancers, including glioma, pancreatic cancer, lung cancer, and mesothelioma. MTAP is responsible for the metabolism of 5’-deoxy-5’-methylthioadenosine (MTA). In MTAP-deficient cells, MTA accumulates abnormally. The structure of MTA resembles that of the PRMT5 substrate S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), enabling it to act as an endogenous competitive inhibitor that mildly suppresses PRMT5 activity. To maintain normal methylation levels and cell survival, MTAP-deficient cancer cells must upregulate PRMT5 expression or activity. Exogenous PRMT5 small-molecule inhibitors synergize with endogenous MTA to completely block PRMT5 function, leading to selective cell death. In MTAP-normal cells, where MTA levels are low, the effects of PRMT5 inhibitors are relatively minor.
This synthetic lethal relationship has been validated in extensive preclinical studies. In MTAP-deficient cell lines and animal models, PRMT5 gene knockdown or pharmacological inhibition effectively and selectively suppressed tumor growth, whereas MTAP-normal cells remained insensitive. On the basis of this robust principle, multiple pharmaceutical companies have developed PRMT5 inhibitors (e.g., GSK3326595, JNJ-64619178 and PF-06939999) and have advanced them into clinical trials.
Early clinical trials are currently evaluating the safety and preliminary efficacy of PRMT5 inhibitors in advanced solid tumors and hematologic malignancies with MTAP deficiency. Initial results have revealed some antitumor signals but also present several challenges: (1) Toxicity management: PRMT5 also has extensive functions in normal tissues, particularly those of the hematopoietic system. Dose-limiting hematologic toxicities (e.g., thrombocytopenia and neutropenia) are common issues that potentially restrict drugs from reaching the effective concentrations required for complete PRMT5 inhibition. (2) Efficacy optimization and biomarkers: Confirmation of sufficient target inhibition in patients is needed, as is exploration of optimal biologically effective doses. In addition to MTAP deficiency, additional biomarkers predicting efficacy or resistance may exist. (3) Combination therapy: Exploring combinations of PRMT5 inhibitors with other agents is key for overcoming the limitations of monotherapy efficacy and preventing resistance. For instance, coadministration with methionine adenosyltransferase 2 A (MAT2A) inhibitors synergistically reduces intracellular SAM levels, further increasing synthetic lethality. Combinations with DNA-damaging agents or immunotherapies are also under investigation.
Despite these challenges, the PRMT5-MTAP axis remains among the most compelling new targets in precision oncology because of its well-defined synthetic lethal mechanism and relatively common MTAP deletion frequency. With a better understanding of the biological functions of PRMT5, the development of superior inhibitors, and rational combination strategies and patient selection, PRMT5 inhibitors hold promise as novel targeted therapeutic options for patients with MTAP-deficient tumors across various malignancies.
ATR, WEE1, and PRMT5 inhibitors are a new generation of synthetic lethal targeted therapies beyond PARP inhibitors. They target critical vulnerabilities in the DNA damage response, cell cycle checkpoints, and epigenetic regulation. Despite facing challenges such as toxicity, biomarker identification, and resistance development in clinical translation, the treatment landscape for refractory cancers is being progressively reshaped on the basis of robust synthetic lethality principles and emerging clinical evidence. Future success will depend on more precise patient stratification, rational combination strategies, and the development of next-generation drugs targeting specific resistance mechanisms.
The clinical translation of synthetic lethality strategies faces significant challenges, most notably with respect to tumor heterogeneity and resistance. These two core interrelated obstacles not only limit the population that is responsive to initial treatment but also drive disease progression. Furthering understanding and overcoming these challenges is essential for advancing synthetic lethality therapies.

## WRN helicase: a synthetic lethal target in MSI cancers
Microsatellite instability (MSI), resulting from deficient DNA mismatch repair (dMMR), occurs in approximately 15% of colorectal, 22% of gastric, and 20–30% of endometrial cancers [ ]. While MSI-high tumors often respond to immune checkpoint blockade, 40–60% of patients do not benefit, highlighting the need for alternative therapeutic strategies.
A landmark study in 2019 identified the RecQ DNA helicase WRN as a synthetic lethal vulnerability factor in MSI cancers through genome-scale CRISPR/Cas9 and RNAi screens [ ]. WRN was selectively essential in MSI models in vitro and in vivo but dispensable in microsatellite stable (MSS) models. Mechanistically, WRN depletion induced double-strand DNA breaks and promoted apoptosis and cell cycle arrest selectively in MSI models, which required the helicase activity but not the exonuclease activity of WRN.
The clinical translation of this discovery has progressed rapidly. Two highly potent small-molecule WRN inhibitors, HRO761 and VVD-133,214 (RO7589831), have recently entered Phase I clinical trials for MSI cancers [ ]. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that these inhibitors exhibit striking activity against MSI cancer models, including patient-derived xenografts, while sparing MSS cells [ ]. Notably, WRN inhibition is a promising strategy for overcoming immunotherapy resistance in MSI colorectal cancer, positioning WRN inhibition as a valuable addition to the synthetic lethality armamentarium [ ]. 

## PKMYT1: expanding synthetic lethality in CCNE1-amplified and RB1-deficient cancers
PKMYT1, a member of the WEE family of kinases, plays a critical role in cell cycle regulation by phosphorylating CDK1 at Thr14, thereby maintaining the G2/M checkpoint [ ]. In 2022, a landmark study revealed that CCNE1 amplification, which occurs in approximately 5–10% of ovarian, breast, and gastroesophageal cancers, creates a synthetic lethal dependency on PKMYT1 [ ]. Mechanistically, CCNE1 overexpression drives replication stress, and PKMYT1 inhibition forces premature CDK1 activation, selectively leading to mitotic catastrophe in CCNE1-amplified cells but not in normal cells. The first-in-class selective PKMYT1 inhibitor lunresertib (RP-6306) has advanced to Phase I clinical trials (NCT04855656) and has demonstrated promising antitumor activity in preclinical models [ ]. 
Building on this discovery, a 2023 study revealed a synthetic lethal interaction between WEE1 and PKMYT1, demonstrating that their combined inhibition at low doses synergistically eradicates ovarian cancer cells and organoid models [ ]. Mechanistically, WEE1 and PKMYT1 coinhibition synergistically promotes CDK activation, exacerbates DNA replication stress and replication catastrophe, and activates inflammatory STAT1 signaling. Importantly, this multiple low-dose approach circumvents the dose-limiting toxicity associated with high-dose WEE1 monotherapy, offering a strategy to overcome MYT1 overexpression-mediated resistance and harness the potency of WEE1 inhibition through synthetic lethality.
More recently, a 2025 study identified RB1 deficiency as a novel predictive biomarker for combined ATR and PKMYT1 inhibition in breast cancer [ ]. In RB1-deficient breast cancer models, coinhibition of ATR (camonsertib/RP-3500) and PKMYT1 (lunresertib/RP-6306) disrupts both the S-G2 and G2-M checkpoints, leading to replication stress, premature mitotic entry, and the accumulation of DNA damage. Patient-derived xenograft models demonstrated marked tumor regression with this combination, while RB1-proficient tumors remained resistant. Mechanistically, RB1 loss impairs double-strand DNA repair by attenuating homologous recombination and nonhomologous end joining, creating context-specific vulnerability to dual checkpoint inhibition. Notably, RB1-deficient tumors comprise nearly 40% of triple-negative and estrogen receptor-positive breast cancers resistant to CDK4/6 inhibitors, positioning RB1 status as an important predictive biomarker for this combination strategy. These findings expand the therapeutic landscape of PKMYT1-targeted strategies beyond CCNE1 amplification to include RB1-deficient tumors, offering new precision treatment options for advanced breast cancers.

## Challenges and future directions

## Challenge of tumor heterogeneity

## Genetic heterogeneity: spatial and temporal
Tumor heterogeneity, defined as the differences in the genome, epigenome, transcriptome, and proteome of tumor cells within a single tumor or across different lesions, presents a fundamental challenge to synthetic lethality strategies that depend on a specific and homogeneous genetic background [ ]. 
Spatial heterogeneity implies that key driver mutations conferring synthetic lethality sensitivity—such as BRCA1/2, ARID1A, or SMARCA4—may not be present in all tumor cells [ ]. If only a subset of tumor cells carry BRCA1 mutations, PARP inhibitors will selectively eliminate these sensitive cells, whereas the intrinsic BRCA1 wild-type resistant subclones will survive and expand, ultimately resulting in treatment failure and disease recurrence [ ]. This ‘target loss’ phenomenon is equally detrimental to strategies based on “synthetic lethality.” For instance, the loss of a core subunit (such as SMARCA4) in one subclone may be offset by the wild-type state of the same gene in another subclone, thereby rendering therapies aimed at the compensatory subunit (such as SMARCA2) incapable of targeting all tumor cells [ ]. 

## Nongenetic heterogeneity: microenvironment, 3D architecture, and cellular plasticity
Beyond genetic heterogeneity, the 3D architecture of tumors and the presence of multiple cell types within the microenvironment create additional layers of complexity that cannot be recapitulated in 2D monocultures. A comprehensive 2025 review comparing 2D and 3D culture systems revealed that during organoid or spheroid formation, crucial aspects, including cancer biology, the transcriptome, the proteome, signaling pathways and drug sensitivity, undergo substantial alterations [ ]. For example, compared with their 2D counterparts, cells in 3D culture exhibit increased drug resistance, which is attributed to factors such as cytoskeletal reorganization and the overexpression of specific genes (SLC2A1, ALDOC, and PFKFB4) that drive drug resistance. These observations suggest that synthetic lethal interactions validated only in 2D systems may overestimate in vivo efficacy and that 3D models should be incorporated into the validation pipeline.
In addition to the spatial and temporal heterogeneity of genetic alterations, a more dynamic dimension of tumor heterogeneity lies in cellular plasticity and state-dependent vulnerabilities. Chromosomal instability (CIN), an emerging hallmark of cancer, generates extensive genetic diversity through persistent errors in chromosome segregation, replication stress, and defective DNA damage repair [ ]. This continuous generation of genetic variants fuels intratumoral heterogeneity and provides a reservoir of preexisting resistant clones that can be selected upon therapeutic pressure. Paradoxically, the very process that drives heterogeneity—CIN—also creates unique therapeutic opportunities. Cells with high CIN are often dependent on specific pathways that enable them to tolerate ongoing chromosomal missegregation, such as the spindle assembly checkpoint, proteostasis networks, and specific metabolic adaptations. These dependencies are state dependent, meaning that they are not hardwired by static genetic mutations but emerge dynamically from the precarious state of chromosomal instability itself. Targeting such state-dependent vulnerabilities—rather than static genetic markers—is a promising strategy to overcome the adaptive capacity of heterogeneous tumors.

## Translational challenges: context dependence and biomarker strategies
Tumor genomes evolve under therapeutic pressure [ ]. Pretreatment biopsies may fail to fully capture the tumor’s genetic landscape, particularly low-frequency, preexisting resistant subclones. When synthetic lethal drugs exert selection pressure, these initially minor resistant subclones rapidly expand. For example, prior to PARP inhibitor therapy, cells harboring BRCA1/2 reversion mutations may constitute only a tiny fraction of the tumor, making them difficult to detect by conventional methods [ , ]. This clonal evolution undermines the predictive accuracy of static biomarkers based on single biopsies [ ]. 
A critical limitation is the marked heterogeneity of treatment effects across patients with different HRR gene mutations. A 2025 meta-analysis of 13 trials in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (4,278 patients) revealed stark contrasts: Patients with BRCA2 alterations had robust responses (a PSA response rate of 3.3 per 100 person-months), whereas those with A™ or CHEK2 alterations derived minimal benefit from PARP inhibitor monotherapy (0.4 and 1.0, respectively) [ ]. Even with combination therapy, no overall survival benefit was observed for patients with A™, CHEK2, or CDK12 alterations. This result challenges the broad ‘HRDpositive’ classification and underscores the gene-specific nature of synthetic lethality.
Tumor heterogeneity imposes greater demands on biomarkers. First, liquid biopsy (circulating tumor DNA, ctDNA) has immense potential as a noninvasive technique capable of integrating information from systemic tumor lesions [ ]. Monitoring dynamic changes in the allele frequency (MAF) of mutations in synthetic lethality-related genes within ctDNA theoretically enables a more comprehensive assessment of tumor genetic composition and earlier detection of emerging drug-resistant clones. Second, the development of functional biomarkers (such as the RAD51 focus formation assay discussed in Sect. ) may partially overcome issues arising from genetic heterogeneity. This is because they reflect the real-time, integrated functional state of pathways (e.g., homologous recombination repair capacity) within tumor cells rather than the mutation status of individual genes. Finally, in terms of treatment strategies, combination therapy is considered an effective approach for addressing heterogeneity. By simultaneously targeting multiple synthetic lethal vulnerabilities or synergistic pathways, a multipronged approach can reduce the likelihood of resistance arising from single-pathway heterogeneity. For example, in HR-deficient tumors, combining PARP inhibitors with ATR inhibitors (as in the CAPRI trial) may simultaneously eliminate both drug-sensitive and potentially drug-resistant subpopulations, delaying the onset of resistance.
Beyond genetic heterogeneity, synthetic lethal interactions are profoundly context dependent, varying not only across tumor types but also across cellular states within the same tumor. A 2024 systematic analysis of synthetic lethality-driven precision oncology revealed that most clinical trials of synthetic lethal therapies involve genes in the DNA damage response pathway, and while success rates for these trials are generally higher than those for nonsynthetic lethal therapies, the majority of candidate interactions fail to translate because of insufficient consideration of lineage-specific dependencies and the dynamic nature of cancer evolution [ ]. This analysis underscores that the “one size fits all’” approach to synthetic lethality, in which a given genetic defect is assumed to confer the same vulnerability across all contexts, is fundamentally flawed. Instead, successful translation requires a nuanced understanding of how tissue of origin, epigenetic state, and microenvironmental factors modulate the expression and essentiality of synthetic lethal partners. The emerging paradigm is “contextual synthetic lethality”, in which vulnerability is defined not by genotype alone but by the intersection of genotype, lineage, and cellular state.

## Resistance management strategies
Acquired resistance remains an unavoidable challenge for synthetic lethal therapies, as with all targeted treatments [ ]. 
On the basis of extensive research, particularly with respect to PARP inhibitors, resistance mechanisms can be broadly categorized as follows:

 Restoration of target pathway function: This category includes BRCA1/2 reversion mutations that restore homologous recombination, as well as inactivation of 53BP1, RIF1, or the Shieldin complex, which promotes DNA end resection and partially restores HR in BRCA1-deficient cells.
Alterations in drug targets: PARP1 mutations (e.g., p.R591C in the DNA binding domain) impair PARP trapping without affecting catalytic activity, directly reducing drug cytotoxicity. Similar gatekeeper mutations may occur in other targeted kinases.
Enhanced replication fork stability: In BRCA-deficient cells, fork protection mechanisms can be upregulated. EZH2 inhibition promotes fork reversal and sensitizes cells, whereas EZH2 overexpression is correlated with fork instability and sensitivity to PARP inhibitors.
Drug efflux pump upregulation: Overexpression of P glycoprotein (MDR1/ABCB1) reduces intracellular drug concentrations and is a common resistance mechanism for multiple small-molecule inhibitors.
Compensatory pathway activation: Tumors may activate alternative survival pathways. For example, sensitivity to ATR inhibition in ATM-deficient contexts can be circumvented by increased DNAPKcs activity.
Resistance mediated by the tumor microenvironment: Hypoxia can downregulate the expression of HR-related genes, and prolonged adaptation to hypoxia may promote the evolution of resistant clones.

Prospective strategies for managing resistance include (1) dynamic monitoring using ctDNA to detect emerging resistant clones; (2) the development of next-generation drugs targeting specific resistance mechanisms (e.g., PARP1-selective inhibitors that bypass trapping defects); and (3) rational combination therapies that simultaneously block multiple survival pathways before resistance emerges, such as combinations of a PARP inhibitor plus an ATR inhibitor that have shown activity in patients with PARP-resistant ovarian cancer.
Prospective strategies for managing drug resistance include the following:

Prospective identification and dynamic monitoring: Continuous patient monitoring using highly sensitive ctDNA sequencing before treatment, during therapy, and during disease progression enables prospective identification of low-frequency resistant clones. This identification allows early intervention when resistant clones begin to expand, laying the foundation for “dynamic precision medicine” and real time adjustment of treatment strategies on the basis of tumor evolution.
Development of next-generation drugs: Targeting specific resistance mechanisms directly through novel drug development is key. For example, novel PARP1 inhibitors or allosteric inhibitors that bypass the “PARP-trapping” mechanism are being explored for treatment of tumors that are resistant due to diminished trapping capacity. With respect to resistance arising from collateral homolog upregulation, bispecific inhibitors that simultaneously target the initial target and its homologs may offer a solution.
Rational combination therapies: This is the most fundamental strategy for overcoming and preventing resistance. Its principle involves simultaneously blocking multiple critical survival or compensatory pathways before resistance mechanisms emerge. Combining agents at different nodes within the same pathway achieves deeper inhibition and reduces escape via that pathway. Additionally, combining agents targeting distinct yet functionally complementary or synergistic pathways enhances efficacy. Examples include the combination of PARP inhibitors with ATR inhibitors (CAPRI trial), aiming to simultaneously intensify DNA damage generation and weaken the damage response, or the combination of PRMT5 inhibitors with MAT2A inhibitors, designed to synergistically reduce intracellular SAM levels and completely dismantle methylations. Genomic instability induced by synthetic lethal drugs can increase tumor mutational burden and neoantigen release while enhancing antitumor immunity through mechanisms such as cGAS-STING pathway activation. This activity synergizes with immune checkpoint inhibitors, potentially eliminating cells resistant to targeted therapies.
Intermittent dosing and dose optimization: Sustained drug pressure remains the strongest driver of clonal selection. Exploring intermittent dosing strategies (e.g., the Day 1–7 regimen for ceralasertib in the CAPRI trial) or identifying the lowest effective dose while maintaining efficacy may help mitigate selection pressure and delay the emergence of resistant clones.
PARP1-selective inhibitors: In addition to directly targeting resistance mechanisms, next-generation PARP1-selective inhibitors are emerging as a strategy to improve the therapeutic index of PARP inhibition and potentially delay resistance. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that PARP1 inhibition alone is sufficient to induce synthetic lethality in homologous recombination repair (HRR)-deficient cells, whereas PARP2 inhibition contributes to hematological toxicity because of its essential role in erythropoiesis [ ]. Saruparib (AZD5305), a first-in-class highly selective PARP1 inhibitor and trapper, has shown a favorable safety profile in the phase I/IIa PETRA trial (NCT04644068). Among 61 patients with advanced BRCA1/2-, PALB2-, or RAD51C-mutated tumors, saruparib was well tolerated with no dose-limiting toxicity; rates of grade ≥ 3 anemia (14.8%) and neutropenia (6.6%) were substantially lower than those typically observed with first-generation PARP inhibitors (e.g., olaparib). Preclinical patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models further support enhanced efficacy, with saruparib achieving superior complete response rates (75% vs. 37%) and longer median progression-free survival (386 vs. 90 days) than olaparib [ ]. The improved tolerability of PARP1-selective inhibitors also facilitates combination strategies, as demonstrated in the ongoing PETRANHA study (NCT05367440) evaluating saruparib with novel hormonal agents in metastatic prostate cancer, which reported manageable safety and no clinically significant drug‒drug interactions. By reducing on-target hematological toxicity while maintaining potent antitumor activity, PARP1-selective inhibitors are a significant advancement over first-generation PARP inhibitors, with the potential to improve treatment adherence, enable more effective combinations, and potentially delay resistance emergence.

Tumor heterogeneity and drug resistance are two formidable barriers to the advancement of synthetic lethality therapies. Addressing these challenges requires a shift from a static diagnostic and treatment model based on single biopsies to a predictive management paradigm involving systematic and dynamic monitoring. Future success will critically depend on the development of highly sensitive biomarkers, a profound understanding of resistance mechanisms, and the design of novel drugs and intelligent combination strategies that can proactively prevent or overcome resistance on the basis of these insights. Only in this way can the precision potential of synthetic lethality strategies be fully realized, providing lasting benefits to a broader patient population.

## Future directions
Future advancements in synthetic lethality strategies will rely heavily on technological innovation and interdisciplinary integration [ ]. Advances in single-cell multiomics, novel drug modalities, immunotherapy synergy, and artificial intelligence predictive models are collectively driving the field toward higher precision, broader coverage, and greater individualization, offering new ways to overcome the heterogeneity and resistance challenges encountered in current clinical practice [ ]. 

## Application of single-cell multi-omics technologies
Single-cell multiomics technologies will advance the understanding of tumor heterogeneity and synthetic lethal mechanisms. Traditional omics approaches struggle to capture the diversity of cellular states within tumors and their differential responses to treatment. In contrast, single-cell RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and epigenomic analysis can reveal heterogeneous responses to genetic perturbations across distinct cell subpopulations within specific genetic contexts [ ]. For instance, by integrating single-cell CRISPR screening with transcriptomic sequencing, researchers can decipher the differential sensitivity of distinct cellular states to SMARCA2 inhibition in the context of SMARCA4 deficiency and identify susceptible subpopulations and their characteristic signaling pathways. Furthermore, spatial multiomics technologies reveal how cell‒cell interactions within the tumor microenvironment influence synthetic lethal effects [ ]. For instance, in immune cell-rich regions, tumor cells may become more dependent on specific DNA repair targets because of paracrine signaling. In the future, as single-cell proteomics and metabolomics technologies advance, the construction of holistic, dynamic tumor vulnerability maps is likely, laying the foundation for “single-cell-level” precision medicine.

## Development of novel drug formulations
The development of novel drug modalities is key to expanding the scope of synthetic lethal targets and overcoming “undruggable” challenges. Traditional small-molecule inhibitors face significant challenges when targeting transcription factors, scaffold proteins, and nonenzymatic functional proteins lacking clear active sites. Protein degradation-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) use event-driven catalytic mechanisms to efficiently degrade targets that are difficult to inhibit with conventional small molecules, greatly expanding the target space for synthetic lethality strategies [ ]. For instance, in ARID1A-deficient tumors, its synthetic lethal partner ARID1B—a structural component of chromatin remodeling complexes lacking an enzyme active site—has proven difficult to target with small-molecule inhibitors. In contrast, PROTAC technology can effectively induce synthetic lethality through the degradation of the ARID1B protein. Additionally, molecular glue compounds can increase the proximity between E3 ligases and target proteins, thereby enabling target degradation [ ]. These compounds have demonstrated clinical value for targets such as IKAROS family zinc finger 1/3 (IKZF1/3). Moving forward, PROTACs and molecular glues will continue to pioneer new therapeutic frontiers for traditionally “undruggable” cancer proteins such as RAS and MYC, as well as nonenzymatic subunits within transcriptional regulatory complexes [ ]. Concurrently, novel approaches such as oligonucleotide-based degradation strategies and antibody‒PROTAC conjugates will offer expanded possibilities for tissue-specific delivery and broadening the spectrum of targetable proteins.

## Synergy between synthetic lethality and immunotherapy
Synergistic effects between synthetic lethality and immunotherapy represent a critical frontier in cancer treatment. Research has indicated that defects in DNA damage repair not only directly induce tumor cell death but also activate the cGAS-STING pathway. This pathway promotes type I interferon production, enhances antigen presentation, and increases T-cell infiltration, thereby transforming “immunologically cold tumors” into “hot tumors”. The synergistic effects of PARP inhibitors with immune checkpoint blockade in ovarian and breast cancers have been clinically validated, although efficacy remains constrained by tumor immune microenvironment heterogeneity [ ]. Future research will focus on identifying biomarkers that best predict the response to this combination strategy, such as intratumoral STING pathway activity, T-cell receptor clonality, and myeloid cell composition [ ]. Furthermore, the potential synergies between emerging synthetic lethal targets and immunotherapy remain to be explored. For instance, replication stress and DNA fragmentation induced by ATR or WEE1 inhibitors in TP53-mutated tumors may further increase immunogenicity. In addition to direct cytotoxic effects, PRMT5 inhibition in MTAP-deficient tumors may also modulate antitumor immunity by regulating T-cell function and cytokine expression. Through strategically designed clinical trials that integrate synthetic lethal drugs with immunomodulators and cell therapies, a dual-precision “targeted + immune” approach may be achievable.

## Applications of artificial intelligence predictions
The application of AI prediction models will significantly accelerate the discovery and validation of synthetic lethal targets. As multiomics data scales continue to expand, traditional statistical methods struggle to efficiently reveal hidden genetic interactions within vast datasets. Machine learning and deep learning models can integrate genomic, transcriptomic, protein interaction network, and chemogenomic data to predict high-potential synthetic lethal pairs and assess their drug development potential [ ]. For instance, graph neural network-based prediction systems can learn topological features from known synthetic lethal pairs to recommend novel candidate targets across the entire genome. Generative AI can also design optimized combination therapy regimens for specific target combinations. Furthermore, AI models demonstrate strong potential in biomarker development by integrating clinical, pathological, and molecular features to construct comprehensive scoring systems for predicting individual patient treatment responses. In the future, with the accumulation of high-quality datasets and algorithmic iterations, artificial intelligence will not only serve the front end of target discovery but will also permeate the entire chain of drug design, patient stratification, efficacy monitoring, and resistance early warning. These processes will propel synthetic lethality research from a “mass screening” paradigm toward a new era of “intelligent design”.

## Conclusion
This review has systematically outlined the complete trajectory of synthetic lethality strategies, from classical genetic concepts to a core therapeutic paradigm in precision oncology. This review first analyzed the fundamental principles of synthetic lethality and its core logic in creating selective therapeutic windows for cancer treatment and then provided an in-depth review of its specific molecular mechanisms across several key biological areas, including DNA damage repair (represented by the BRCA‒PARP axis), cell cycle checkpoint regulation (such as TP53 loss and ATR/CHK1/WEE1 dependency), metabolic reprogramming (such as the ME2‒ME3 axis and MTAP‒PRMT5 pair), and epigenetic regulation (such as mutations in subunits of the SWI/SNF complex). Together, these insights paint a multidimensional interaction network based on tumor-specific genetic defects, revealing the universality of the core therapeutic logic of “nononcogene addiction.”
Currently, research in this field has moved beyond the discovery of single targets and has entered a stage of systematic target identification driven primarily by high-throughput functional genomics (such as CRISPR screening) and computational biology, successfully advancing multiple drugs, including PARP inhibitors, into clinical practice. Cutting-edge research is focused on two main directions. First, the use of single-cell multiomics, spatial transcriptomics, and artificial intelligence models to analyze tumor heterogeneity at higher resolution and predict context-dependent vulnerabilities; and second, actively exploring emerging targeted therapies in addition to PARP inhibitors (such as ATR, WEE1, and PRMT5 inhibitors), as well as rational combination strategies of synthetic lethality drugs with immunotherapies and other targeted therapies, with the goal of expanding the benefiting patient population and overcoming acquired resistance.
More broadly, despite two decades of research, PARP inhibitors remain the only class of synthetic lethal agents to achieve widespread clinical approval. The slow translation of other pairs (WEE1, ATR, and PRMT5) reflects not only drug development difficulties but also the context-dependent nature of these interactions. This gap between preclinical promise and clinical reality underscores the need for better models and patient stratification.
Looking forward, the core challenges and directions for breakthroughs in synthetic lethality research lie in addressing tumor heterogeneity and the evolution of drug resistance. Solutions will rely on technological innovation and strategy integration, such as developing dynamic monitoring systems based on liquid biopsies and functional biomarkers to enable real-time adjustments during treatment; using artificial intelligence to integrate multidimensional data to optimize patient stratification and combination therapy design; and expanding the range of targetable proteins by developing new drug modalities, such as PROTAC degraders. Ultimately, by building an intelligent precision medicine system that thoroughly integrates individual genetic backgrounds, real-time molecular profiles, and microenvironmental characteristics, synthetic lethality strategies are expected to provide fundamental solutions for achieving truly personalized cancer therapy.
In conclusion, the field of synthetic lethality has matured from a collection of isolated genetic observations into a coherent therapeutic framework, yet the gap between preclinical promise and clinical reality remains substantial. The common thread underlying successful applications—most notably PARP inhibitors in BRCA mutant cancers—is the presence of a robust, lineage-appropriate genetic defect that creates a nonredundant dependency on a druggable target. Conversely, failures often stem from three recurring pitfalls: (1) overreliance on 2D culture models that fail to recapitulate the complexities of the tumor microenvironment; (2) insufficient attention to lineage-specific and context-dependent variations in synthetic lethal interactions; and (3) underestimation of the adaptive capacity of tumors, which evolve resistance through both genetic and nongenetic mechanisms [ ]. Moving forward, integrating these lessons will require a shift from static, genotypecentric models to dynamic, multidimensional frameworks that incorporate lineage context, cellular state, microenvironmental influences, and evolutionary potential. Only by embracing this complexity can the full therapeutic potential of synthetic lethality be realized.
