## Abstract

## Background
Understanding context-specific gene dependencies is essential for uncovering therapeutic vulnerabilities and advancing precision oncology. However, existing computational methods often fail to capture sample-specific effects or provide biological interpretability.

## Methods
We developed GATDep, a graph attention network-based framework that predicts sample-specific gene dependency scores by integrating transcriptomic data with curated gene-gene interaction networks. Each sample is represented as a context-aware graph instance, where node features are derived from gene set variation analysis, enabling pathway-level encoding of cellular states.

## Results
GATDep frames dependency prediction as a node-level regression problem and outperforms state-of-the-art models across multiple benchmark datasets. The model provides interpretable attention weights and feature attribution maps via GNNExplainer, revealing biologically meaningful gene interactions. When applied to CRISPR and RNAi screening datasets, GATDep accurately identifies both known and novel dependencies and shows strong correlations with drug sensitivity. Furthermore, analysis of TCGA patient cohorts demonstrates significant associations between predicted dependencies and key clinical features, including microsatellite instability, chemotherapy response, and pathway-level perturbations.

## Conclusions
GATDep offers an extensible and interpretable framework for modeling gene essentiality at the sample level. By combining network topology with transcriptomic context, it enables the discovery of functionally and clinically relevant gene dependencies, providing a foundation for drug prioritization and precision cancer therapy.

## Introduction
Identifying gene dependencies—genes essential for cancer cell survival but nonessential in normal cells—is a central goal in precision oncology, offering critical insights for targeted therapy development [ ]. This concept is particularly powerful in the context of synthetic lethality (SL), where inhibition of a gene selectively kills cancer cells harboring specific mutations [ ]. A canonical example is the sensitivity of BRCA1/2-deficient tumors to PARP inhibitors, which exploit deficiencies in homologous recombination repair (HRR) by further disrupting base excision repair (BER), leading to lethal genomic instability [ ]. This paradigm has led to the clinical approval of PARP inhibitors, including Olaparib and Rucaparib, for BRCA-mutant cancers. SL strategies have since expanded beyond DNA repair to include cell cycle regulation and metabolic reprogramming [ , ]. For instance, ATR inhibitors (e.g., Berzosertib, AZD6738) preferentially target ATM-deficient tumors by amplifying replication stress [ ]. Likewise, TP53-mutant tumors, which lack G1/S checkpoint control, become increasingly dependent on the WEE1-regulated G2/M checkpoint; WEE1 inhibition (e.g., Adavosertib) abolishes this backup mechanism, inducing mitotic catastrophe [ , ]. Additional vulnerabilities, such as the synthetic lethality between MTAP deletion and PRMT5 inhibition, are also under investigation [ ]. These discoveries underscore the need for systematic, context-aware approaches to identify actionable gene dependencies across diverse tumor types.
High-throughput CRISPR-Cas9 screens have transformed gene dependency mapping by enabling genome-scale perturbation studies across hundreds of cancer cell lines [ – ]. Drug-perturbed CRISPR screens have facilitated the discovery of context-specific vulnerabilities, such as enhanced sensitivity to ATR inhibitors in ATM-deficient models [ ] Other efforts have uncovered targets like WRN helicase in microsatellite instability (MSI) tumors [ ], or PARP sensitivity in CDK12-deficient contexts [ ]. Complementary resources like DepMap now provide extensive multi-omic datasets for integrative modeling of gene essentiality. Yet, experimental screens remain limited by scale, cost, and tissue representativeness, motivating the development of computational approaches.
Several machine learning models have sought to infer gene dependencies in silico. Notably, graph-based frameworks have gained traction for their ability to encode biological relationships and molecular context. SLGNN [ ] and KG4SL [ ] model SL interactions using knowledge graphs and graph neural networks, while MGE4SL [ ] integrates protein-protein interaction (PPI), Gene Ontology (GO), and pathway data. PiLSL [ ] emphasizes pairwise modeling of SL interactions. However, these models largely focus on predicting binary SL interactions and often neglect quantitative prediction of gene-level dependency scores [ ]. They also lack explicit modeling of sample-specific transcriptomic context or network topology, limiting biological interpretability. Expression-based models such as DeepDEP [ ] and DepMap’s random forest predictors rely on global transcriptional similarity but fail to incorporate biological priors like gene networks. Similarly, correlation-based approaches like FIREWORKS [ ] estimate coessentiality from bulk screening data but lack granularity for patient-specific inference. These limitations highlight the need for interpretable, scalable frameworks that integrate biological structure with molecular profiles.
While large-scale CRISPR-Cas9 screening has accelerated the discovery of gene dependencies, current computational models remain limited in their ability to accurately predict gene dependencies across diverse molecular contexts and to offer insights into the underlying biological mechanisms. To address these challenges, we developed GATDep, a graph attention network–based framework designed primarily to improve predictive accuracy and generalizability across heterogeneous cancer types by integrating transcriptomic data with biologically curated gene interaction networks. In addition, we incorporated GNNExplainer to provide preliminary interpretability analyses, aiming to highlight potential gene–gene interactions that may underlie model predictions. GATDep constructs a comprehensive gene graph using data from PPIs, GO annotations, and KEGG pathways. Node features are derived from gene set variation analysis (GSVA), providing functional representations of gene expression profiles. Each sample is modeled as a graph, where dependency prediction is framed as a node-level regression task within a graph-level context. Through multi-head attention mechanisms, GATDep learns to assign adaptive weights to neighboring nodes, enabling interpretable, context-specific modeling of gene interactions. Key advantages of GATDep include: (1) Context-awareness, by incorporating sample-specific transcriptomic data; (2) Biological interpretability, through attention mechanisms and GNNExplainer [ ]; (3) Multimodal flexibility, supporting mutation, CNV, and other omics as node features; and (4) Translational relevance, as demonstrated by applications in drug response prediction, SL prioritization, and patient stratification.
GATDep offers a computationally scalable, biologically informed, and mechanistically interpretable solution to gene dependency modeling, addressing long-standing limitations in existing machine learning approaches that neglect network topology or lack sample specificity.

## Methods and materials

## Data acquisition and preprocessing
Multi-omics datasets of human cancer cell lines were retrieved from the DepMap portal (version 24Q4), including gene expression (OmicsExpressionProteinCodingGenesTPMLogp1BatchCorrected.csv), somatic mutation profiles (OmicsSomaticMutationsProfile.csv), gene-level copy number variations (PortalOmicsCNGeneLog2.csv), and CRISPR-Cas9 gene effect scores (CRISPRGeneEffect.csv). To select highly variable genes (HVGs) for model training, we computed the standard deviation of gene effect scores across cell lines and retained genes with a standard deviation (SD) greater than 0.2. Given the skewed distribution of gene effect values and their biological interpretation, we applied min–max normalization to scale all CRISPR-derived gene effect scores to the [0,1] range. The resulting values were referred to as gene dependency scores, where lower scores indicate stronger dependency of the cell on the corresponding gene. Protein–protein interactions data were downloaded from the STRING [ ] database (file: 9606.protein.links.v10.5.txt.gz), and protein identifiers were mapped to gene symbols using the biomaRt [ ] R package. These were used to construct the gene–gene interaction network. Functionally annotated gene sets were obtained from the MSigDB [ ], including terms from the Gene Ontology Biological Process and KEGG pathway collections, and were used for GSVA-based enrichment profiling and the construction of gene–gene set relational graphs. Additionally, drug response data were obtained from public pharmacogenomic repositories. Dose–response data for monotherapy were retrieved from the GDSC2 dataset (GDSC2_fitted_dose_response_24Jul22.csv), and drug combination assays were sourced from the DrugComb database (summary_v_1_5.csv).

## GSVA score calculation
To quantify pathway-level activity across cell lines, we applied GSVA [ ] to the full-transcriptome TPM expression matrix. GSVA is a non-parametric, unsupervised method that transforms gene expression profiles into sample-wise enrichment scores for predefined gene sets, such as KEGG pathways and Hallmark gene sets. The algorithm utilizes kernel density estimation with a Gaussian kernel (kcdf = “Gaussian”) to compute continuous enrichment scores, enabling pathway-level analysis without requiring prior grouping of samples. During computation, gene sets containing fewer than 5 or more than 500 genes were excluded (min.sz = 5, max.sz = 500) to ensure robustness. To improve computational efficiency, parallel processing was implemented. The resulting GSVA score matrix provides a standardized representation of functional pathway activity across all cell lines and was used as input for downstream graph-based modeling.

## Unsupervised clustering of cancer cell lines and LOOCV
To stratify cancer cell lines into molecular subtypes, we performed unsupervised clustering based on the GSVA-derived pathway activity matrix. Specifically, K-means clustering (k = 10) was applied to 1,088 cell lines using Euclidean distance as the similarity metric to capture functional differences in pathway activation. The algorithm iteratively minimized within-cluster variance to identify distinct molecular subtypes reflecting transcriptional heterogeneity. The resulting ten clusters served as the stratification basis for subsequent LOOCV, enabling robust evaluation of model performance across heterogeneous cellular contexts.

## GAT model architecture and training procedure
To enable sample-specific prediction of gene dependency, we developed a deep learning framework based on Graph Attention Networks, termed GATDep. This model integrates transcriptomic features and gene–gene functional interactions to quantitatively infer gene essentiality across heterogeneous tumor contexts. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig.  , GATDep first transforms the input GSVA-based pathway features through a linear embedding layer (512 units, LayerNorm, ReLU activation), followed by two stacked graph attention layers (hidden size = 64, heads = 2, dropout = 0.2). Node-level outputs are integrated with global graph embeddings using a mean-pooling operation, and the concatenated features are passed to a regression head to predict individual gene essentiality scores. The model was trained using Adam optimizer (learning rate = 1e-3, batch size = 5) and mean squared error (MSE) loss.

Model performance was evaluated using Pearson correlation coefficient and mean squared error, providing complementary measures of prediction accuracy and calibration.

## DNN architecture and training
To benchmark against the GAT-based model, we implemented a fully connected deep neural network (DNN) under the same input–output framework, aiming to learn a non-linear mapping from high-dimensional functional pathway activity profiles to gene dependency scores.

## Model training and optimization strategy
The DNN model was trained under the same leave-one-out cross-validation scheme as the GAT model to ensure consistent and rigorous evaluation of generalization performance across all samples. For regression tasks, the Huber loss function was employed as the optimization objective. Model optimization was performed using the Adam optimizer (optim.Adam), with an initial learning rate of 0.01. To adaptively refine learning dynamics, a learning rate scheduler (ReduceLROnPlateau) was applied, which halves the learning rate when validation loss fails to improve for three consecutive epochs. To prevent overfitting, early stopping was implemented such that training was terminated once the model’s performance plateaued on the validation set. This strategy ensured that the final model was both efficient and generalizable.

## Model benchmarking and comparative evaluation
To rigorously assess the predictive performance of different approaches, we conducted a systematic benchmarking of the GAT, GCN, DNN, and published DeepDEP models under a leave-one-out cross-validation framework. Three key metrics were used to evaluate model performance:

MSE — quantifies the average deviation between predicted and observed gene dependency scores;
Individual gene-wise correlation (ρindividual genes) — measures the Pearson correlation between predicted and actual scores for each gene across all samples;
Pooled gene-level correlation (ρpool genes) — assesses the global correlation across all gene-sample predictions.

For DeepDEP, we adopted the publicly released model and performed subsampling analysis to estimate individual and pooled correlation metrics under comparable experimental conditions. This enabled a fair and reproducible comparison across all tested models.

To elucidate the internal decision-making processes of our GATDep model, we employed GNNExplainer [ ], a widely used interpretability framework for graph neural networks that identifies subgraph structures and node feature dimensions most critical to a model’s prediction. For a given target node (representing a gene of interest), GNNExplainer seeks to identify a minimal subgraph and an associated feature mask that collectively preserve the model’s original output , thereby revealing the local topological and functional dependencies underlying the prediction.
After model training, the top20 learned edge masks are visualized to highlight the specific neighboring genes that contribute most significantly to the predicted gene dependency score. These interpretable outputs not only provide mechanistic insights into the model’s reasoning but also offer biologically plausible hypotheses for experimental validation of candidate synthetic lethal interactions.

## GEO dataset processing
We queried the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database using a combination of relevant keywords, including “CRISPR screen”, “RNA-Seq”, “Drug response”, and “Homo sapiens”, to identify publicly available datasets related to gene essentiality and treatment perturbations. Candidate datasets were manually curated by screening abstracts and metadata to retain those containing well-annotated and accessible gene expression profiles. For each selected dataset, we performed PCA to assess sample quality and exclude outliers with aberrant transcriptomic profiles. Gene expression data in either log-transformed TPM or raw count format were then subjected to GSVA using gene sets from the MSigDB collection, resulting in a sample-by-gene set enrichment score matrix. This GSVA matrix was subsequently input into the pretrained GATDep model to infer gene dependency scores for each gene within individual samples. Group-wise comparisons of dependency scores were conducted to identify differentially dependent genes between experimental conditions. Alternatively, genes were ranked within each sample to extract top-scoring candidates, denoted as strong dependency genes. Identified genes were subjected to downstream functional enrichment analysis, including over-representation analysis (ORA) and GSEA, to investigate associated biological pathways and molecular functions potentially driving the observed dependency patterns.

## Functional enrichment analysis
To elucidate the biological relevance of identified gene dependencies, we performed Gene Ontology based functional enrichment using the clusterProfiler [ ] R package. For discrete gene sets (e.g., top-ranked or differentially dependent genes), ORA was conducted using the enrichGO() function, focusing on the Biological Process (BP) category to identify enriched functional terms. For genome-wide ranked gene lists, we conducted Gene Set Enrichment Analysis via the GSEA() function in the same package. Genes were ranked based on either the differential dependency score between experimental groups or the absolute dependency value, allowing pathway-level insights under various perturbation contexts. All enrichment analyses were performed using gene sets from the MSigDB, including KEGG and GO terms. Functional terms with a p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

## TCGA data processing and analyses

## Data acquisition
Transcriptomic profiles of tumor samples were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) using the gdc-client.exe tool. Corresponding clinical annotations, including follow-up records, chemotherapy response, and MSIsensor scores, were obtained from cBioPortal.

## MSI status and gene dependency analysis
WRN gene dependency scores were predicted using the GATDep model across selected TCGA samples. Based on MSIsensor scores, patients were stratified into three groups [ ]: MSI-High (MSI-H, score >10), MSI-Low (MSI-L, 4 < score < 10), and Microsatellite Stable (MSS, score < 4). Group-wise comparisons of WRN dependency scores were performed using two-sided Student’s t-tests.

## Chemotherapy response analysis
Due to potential heterogeneity in chemotherapeutic regimens and treatment outcomes, we filtered for patients with consistent drug administration and response evaluation. Patients were categorized into a Response group (Complete Response or Partial Response) and a Non-response group (Stable Disease or Progressive Disease). Differential analysis of gene dependency scores was performed between the two groups, followed by pathway enrichment using GSEA and ORA to identify pathways associated with drug sensitivity or resistance.

## Survival analysis
To investigate the prognostic significance of gene dependencies, univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were fitted using the coxph() function from the survival R package. For each gene, patients were dichotomized based on the median dependency score into strong (score < median) and weak (score >median) dependency groups. Kaplan–Meier curves and forest plots were generated using ggsurvplot() and ggforest() from the survminer package [ ] to visualize survival differences and hazard ratios.

## Results

## GATDep model
GATDep is designed to capture complex regulatory dependencies and sample-specific functional determinants of gene essentiality. It operates on a biologically informed gene–gene interaction graph, where nodes represent genes and edges encode curated physical or regulatory interactions derived from protein–protein interaction databases, Gene Ontology, and KEGG pathways. Input features are derived from transcriptomic data using GSVA, which transforms expression profiles into enrichment scores of predefined biological processes. These GSVA scores are projected onto gene nodes based on curated gene–gene set associations, embedding each node with sample-specific functional context (Fig.  ). Each tumor sample is treated as an independent graph instance, sharing a global edge structure but differing in node-level input features. GATDep formulates dependency prediction as a node-level regression task within this sample-specific graph context. For each gene node, the model outputs a continuous dependency score representing its predicted functional importance in the molecular context of that sample.
 To enhance model expressiveness, GATDep employs multi-head attention mechanisms that assign adaptive weights to neighboring nodes, enabling the model to focus on informative regulators and pathways. This architecture allows differential modeling of gene influence based on network topology and transcriptional context. Furthermore, we integrate GNNExplainer to identify predictive subgraphs and key features driving individual predictions, offering interpretability and biological insight. In summary, GATDep combines transcriptomic-driven feature embedding, graph-based context modeling, and interpretable attention mechanisms to produce biologically grounded, context-aware gene dependency predictions. This framework supports downstream applications in target prioritization, drug response prediction, and synthetic lethality discovery, thereby advancing both mechanistic understanding and translational potential in precision oncology.

## Model training and external validation
To construct a robust and biologically informed modeling framework, we curated CRISPR gene effect scores from the DepMap 24Q2 release, encompassing 17,916 genes across 1,178 cancer cell lines. To prioritize informative features, we selected 2,456 highly variable genes (HVGs) based on a standard deviation (SD) threshold > 0.2 across cell lines (Fig.  A). Functional gene set annotations were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB), including 7,658 Gene Ontology biological process (GOBP) sets and 844 KEGG pathway sets, totaling 8,502 unique gene sets representing 18,004 genes. Of these, 2,350 genes overlapped with the HVGs and were associated with 6,561 curated gene sets (5,909 GOBP and 652 KEGG; Fig.  B and Supplementary Fig.  A). To ensure data consistency across modalities, we restricted our analysis to 1,088 cancer cell lines from DepMap for which matched CRISPR dependency scores, transcriptomic profiles, and somatic mutation data were available (Fig.  C). Raw gene effect values ranged from ~ 0 to -3, with values below − 1 classified by DepMap as indicative of gene essentiality. To minimize the impact of extreme values and facilitate regression training, gene effect scores were min-max normalized to a 0–1 scale, herein referred to as dependency scores, with lower values denoting stronger dependencies (Fig.  D). Using transcriptomic data, we computed GSVA enrichment scores for all 6,561 gene sets across the 1,088 selected cell lines, providing a sample-specific representation of functional activity. K-means clustering of GSVA profiles identified 10 transcriptionally distinct subgroups among the cell lines (Supplementary Fig.  B; Supplementary Fig.  ), highlighting the heterogeneity captured by this feature representation.
 The GATDep model was trained using a stratified 10-fold LOOCV scheme (Supplementary Fig.  C). Training was conducted for 75 epochs, during which convergence was consistently observed across both training and validation sets (Supplementary Fig.  D–H). Performance evaluation revealed a strong correlation between predicted and observed dependency scores in the training data (Pearson R = 0.93; Fig.  E), indicating that GATDep effectively captures informative patterns from the input features. Importantly, the model demonstrated robust generalization performance, achieving a Pearson correlation of R = 0.86 on held-out test samples (Fig.  F).
To evaluate external predictive validity, we benchmarked GATDep against orthogonal gene dependency estimates from the DEMETER2 project [ ], which includes RNAi-based gene knockdown data for 701 cancer cell lines. Among these, 541 cell lines had matched transcriptomic profiles and were used for prediction (Supplementary Fig. I). GATDep predictions showed significant positive correlations with DEMETER2 dependency scores (Fig. G), demonstrating that the model generalizes beyond CRISPR-derived data and captures biologically relevant gene dependencies.

## Model comparison and biological interpretability
To assess the contribution of multi-omic features beyond transcriptomic data, we extended the GATDep framework by incorporating additional molecular layers, including proteomic profiles, mutations and CNVs. The resulting variants, referred to as GAT_Proteomics, GAT_Exp_Mut and GAT_Exp_Mut_CNV, respectively, were constructed using the same training strategy as the baseline GAT_Exp model (Fig.  A–B). For comparison, we also implemented a deep neural network (DNN_Exp), a graph convolutional network (GCN) and an elastic net regression model using transcriptomic data alone, and benchmarked their performance alongside the previously published DeepDEP model (Fig.  C).
 All models were trained on the same dataset using consistent LOOCV protocols (Supplementary Fig. A-C). Specifically, the GAT_Proteomics model utilized 935 cell lines for which both proteomic and CRISPR dependency data were available. Model performance was evaluated on held-out test sets by computing per-gene prediction accuracy, denoted as ρindividual genes. We observed that all three GAT-based models achieved similarly high per-gene correlations with observed dependency scores, with no significant differences among them. However, each GAT model significantly outperformed DNN_Exp, DeepDEP and the elastic net in per-gene correlation (Fig. D, top; Supplementary Fig. D-H). Given that DeepDEP was originally trained on a considerably smaller dataset (278 samples), we next investigated whether differences in sample clustering could have affected its reported performance. To eliminate this confounding factor, we recalculated gene-level correlations across all samples without sample stratification (Fig. D, Top, grey bar). While DeepDEP achieved a mean gene-level correlation of 0.13, consistent with the authors’ originally reported benchmark [ ], our GAT-based models yielded a substantially higher mean correlation of 0.34, indicating improved predictive accuracy at the gene level (Supplementary Fig. I-J). Interestingly, the GAT_Proteomics model exhibited a slightly lower, but not statistically significant, compared with GAT_Exp, indicating that proteomic and transcriptomic features contributed similarly to gene dependency prediction. This observation aligns with previously published findings [ ] and suggests comparable predictive utility between proteomic and transcriptomic data modalities. Of note, the GCN model performed comparably to the GAT variants, implying that graph neural network architectures, whether convolution-based or attention-based, consistently outperform conventional deep or machine learning approaches. This advantage is likely attributable to the graph models’ ability to explicitly encode gene–gene and gene–gene set interaction networks. To further evaluate performance, we examined pooled correlations across all gene predictions (ρpool genes). Interestingly, DeepDEP outperformed both DNN and GAT models in this metric (Fig. D, middle). However, this may be attributed to differences in data preprocessing: whereas our models were trained on scaled gene effect scores (0–1 range) to stabilize learning, DeepDEP used raw scores, which may inflate correlation metrics due to outlier values. We next compared model accuracy in terms of mean squared error (MSE). Both the GNN and DNN_Exp models exhibited substantially lower MSEs than DeepDEP (Fig. D, bottom), indicating smaller deviations between predicted and observed values. This improvement is likely due to the normalization of gene effect values prior to model training. As there were no significant performance differences among the three GAT variants, we selected GAT_Exp as the final model for downstream analyses. In addition, we applied the GATDep model to predict gene dependency scores for 297 cell lines that had available transcriptomic profiles but lacked corresponding CRISPR dependency data. Previously, Cai et al. employed a multi-omic approach using the MOSA model to impute CRISPR dependency scores for these same cell lines [ ]. Comparative analysis between our predictions and the MOSA-imputed results revealed a strong overall correlation (ρ = 0.92), indicating that GATDep can effectively capture CRISPR dependency patterns even in the absence of direct experimental measurements (Supplementary Fig. ).
To investigate biological interpretability, we applied GNNExplainer to interpret specific gene dependency predictions. For instance, analysis of the TP53 node revealed that its predicted dependency was strongly influenced by neighboring genes such as KIF2C and KPNA2 (Fig. E). KPNA2 is a nuclear transport factor that regulates the subcellular localization of TP53 and other transcription factors. Aberrant KPNA2 expression is associated with increased proliferation, poor clinical outcomes, and resistance to therapy across multiple tumor types. Notably, p53 activity can repress KPNA2 expression, while KPNA2 overexpression may impair p53 nuclear import, creating a feedback loop that promotes tumorigenesis [ ]. Additional studies in colorectal cancer have shown that Wip1 cooperates with KPNA2 to promote proliferation in a p53-dependent manner [ ]. KIF2C is a mitotic motor protein frequently upregulated in various malignancies and implicated in chromosome segregation and genomic instability. KIF2C is negatively regulated by p53 [ ]. These findings highlight the regulatory and functional interdependence between TP53 and KPNA2, as captured by our model. Similarly, interpretability analysis of BRCA1 revealed significant attribution from genes including BRAP and BRAT1, both of which are associated with BRCA1 [ – ] (Supplementary Fig. K-L; see Discussion for further elaboration). These examples demonstrate the ability of GATDep not only to predict dependency scores with high accuracy, but also to provide mechanistic insights into gene–gene interactions underlying tumor vulnerabilities.

## Validation in synthetic lethality and drug response
The potential of GATDep to identify genes underlying synthetic lethal interactions was evaluated using the GSE272107 CRISPR screen in A549 lung cancer cells [ ]. In this study, the authors targeted 2,948 metabolism-related genes and identified PRDX1 as synthetically lethal with ATM inhibition—PRDX1 loss sensitized cells to ATM inhibitors. We retrieved expression profiles from 5 ATMi-treated and 5 DMSO-treated A549 cell lines (Fig. A), performed GSVA to generate enrichment scores for 6,561 gene sets, and used GATDep to predict dependency scores for 2,350 genes across the 10 samples. Differential analysis revealed 295 metabolism-related genes with altered dependency between groups. Notably, the PRDX1 dependency score was significantly lower in the ATMi group than in the DMSO group (Fig. B–C; Supplementary Fig. A), consistent with increased dependency upon drug treatment and corroborating the original study. PTEN, another gene highlighted in the publication, was similarly detected by GATDep as differentially dependent in the ATMi group.
 To validate GATDep at the genome-wide scale, we analyzed GSE259249, where the authors used a whole-genome CRISPR knockout library in SUM149 cells to identify genes synergistic with compound C70 [ ]. Deletion of the transcription factor ZBTB7A increased sensitivity to C70. We obtained transcriptomic data from six DMSO-treated and five C70-treated SUM149 cell lines (Fig. D), and performed GATDep-based prediction. We found that ZBTB7A dependency scores were significantly lower in the C70-treated group (Fig. E–F), suggesting enhanced dependency under drug pressure. Additionally, SCAF4, previously reported as a top C70-sensitizing gene, was also ranked highly by GATDep (Fig. E), further supporting the model’s biological relevance.
We next explored the predictive capacity of GATDep for drug response using the GDSC2 dataset [ ]. Using Nutlin-3a as an example, we identified 968 cell lines with IC50 data, of which 702 had matched expression profiles, and 529 were included in the GATDep model (Fig. G). For these 529 cell lines, we predicted MDM2 dependency scores and found a significant positive correlation with LN IC50 (R = 0.51, p < 0.05; Fig. H). Furthermore, in the remaining 173 external cell lines not used during model training, we observed a similar trend (R = 0.34, p < 0.05; Fig. I), demonstrating the model’s generalizability. Similarly, in 523 cell lines, the CDK6 predicted dependency score showed a significant positive correlation with the IC50 of Ribociclib, a CDK4/6 inhibitor (Supplementary Fig. B), indicating GATDep’s utility in guiding targeted drug prediction. Together, these results demonstrate that GATDep can identify synthetic lethal interactions and predict gene–drug sensitivities based solely on transcriptomic data, providing a powerful tool for target prioritization and rational drug discovery.

## Prediction of drug combinations
To assess the potential of GATDep in predicting synergistic effects of drug combinations, we analyzed data from the NCI-ALMANAC project in the DrugComb database, which comprises 5317 drug pairs involving 103 compounds across 60 cancer cell lines and 311,604 drug response assays [ , ] (Supplementary Fig. C). The drug targets span a range of mechanisms, including DNA, kinases, and cytochrome enzymes (Fig. A). From this dataset, we selected seven drug pairs from two cell lines with reported synergy (Fig. B), and retrieved corresponding RNA-seq datasets: GSE219938/GSE219474 (K-562 treated with Vorinostat) [ ] and GSE221475 (MDA-MB-231 treated with Gefitinib) [ ]. In the K-562 dataset, we used RNA-seq data from cells treated with Vorinostat for 4 h and 48 h (Supplementary Fig. D), performed GSVA analysis to obtain gene set enrichment scores, and applied GATDep to predict gene dependency scores for 2,350 genes. Comparative analysis revealed that most significantly altered dependency genes were enriched in the 48-hour treatment group, suggesting enhanced vulnerability to specific gene perturbations under prolonged drug exposure (Fig. C). Functional enrichment of these genes indicated significant involvement in ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation, proteasomal activity, and ribosomal pathways (Fig. D; Supplementary Fig. E, G–I, K). This implicates potential synergy with drugs targeting these pathways, such as Bortezomib (proteasome inhibitor) and Sunitinib (ribosomal inhibitor), in agreement with the DrugComb dataset [ ]. GSEA of strong dependency genes in the 48 h Vorinostat group revealed enrichment in eukaryotic translation initiation, further suggesting sensitivity to translational inhibitors such as Sunitinib (Fig. E). In contrast, weak dependency genes were enriched in the ERBB signaling pathway, which may explain the lack of synergy with Lapatinib, an ERBB2/3 kinase inhibitor, also consistent with previous reports [ ].
 In the GSE221475 dataset, we analyzed four MDA-MB-231 cell lines, including two controls and two Gefitinib-treated samples (Supplementary Fig. F). Among the strong dependency genes in the treatment group, we identified DHFR (Fig. F), the known target of Methotrexate [ ], suggesting a potential synergy between Gefitinib and Methotrexate, consistent with the results in DrugComb database (Fig. B). GSEA analysis of these cells revealed enrichment of strong dependency genes in the HDACs deacetylate histones pathway (Fig. G–H), indicating possible sensitivity to HDAC inhibitors such as Romidepsin or Vorinostat. Meanwhile, weak dependency genes were enriched in the MAPK signaling (Fig. G, I) and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways (Supplementary Fig. J), suggesting reduced sensitivity to kinase inhibitors such as Sorafenib, again consistent with previously reported combinatorial profiles [ ].
Collectively, these results demonstrate that GATDep can leverage post-treatment transcriptomic profiles to infer drug-induced gene dependency changes, enabling the identification of synergistic targets and rational drug combinations.

## Clinical relevance of predicted gene dependency
Building on these experimental validations, we next assessed the clinical relevance of GATDep using TCGA patient cohorts, examining the associations between predicted gene dependency scores and major clinical variables, including microsatellite instability, chemotherapy response, and patient prognosis. First, to assess the clinical relevance of GATDep predictions, we analyzed RNA-seq data from 1,637 tumor samples across five TCGA projects (TCGA-READ, ESCA, STAD, COAD, and UCEC) (Supplementary Fig. A). Corresponding MSIsensor scores were obtained from cBioPortal [ ] to classify samples by microsatellite instability status. Consistent with prior reports, MSI-H tumors exhibited significantly lower predicted WRN dependency scores compared to MSS tumors (Fig. A), confirming the model’s ability to capture known biological dependencies [ , ].
 Next, to explore the relationship between predicted gene dependency and chemotherapy response, we curated a cohort of 1,327 TCGA patients with consistent chemotherapy treatment and response data (Fig. B; Supplementary Fig. B). Dependency scores were significantly associated with chemotherapy non-response for the majority of genes examined (Fig. C). For instance, in TCGA-BRCA, the dependency score of NUDFS5, an electron transport chain enzyme previously implicated in chemotherapy resistance [ ], was significantly lower in non-responders (Supplementary Fig. C). Stratifying by tumor type revealed distinct dependency gene profiles associated with chemotherapy response (Supplementary Fig. D). In TCGA-COAD, strong dependency genes including MDM2, ERBB2, DDX11, and JUN were enriched in biological processes such as cell cycle regulation, DNA replication, WNT signaling, and ERBB signaling (Fig. D–F; Supplementary Fig. E). These findings suggest that patients highly dependent on these genes tend to be more sensitive to chemotherapy. Conversely, in TCGA-HNSC, most strong dependency genes correlated with chemotherapy non-response and were enriched in pathways related to energy metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, and mitochondrial function (Fig. G–H; Supplementary Fig. F), processes previously linked to chemoresistance [ , ]. Furthermore, analysis of six cetuximab-treated patient cohorts demonstrated that responders exhibited significant enrichment of dependency genes within the EGFR/PI3K signaling pathways [ ], underscoring the model’s potential in predicting targeted therapy response (Supplementary Fig. G-J).
Finally, we performed a pan-cancer survival analysis leveraging GATDep-predicted dependency scores for 2,350 genes across 32 tumor types. Univariate Cox regression identified more than 20 genes whose predicted dependency scores were significantly associated with patient prognosis across at least eight distinct cancer types (Fig. I). Notably, EGFR dependency was a risk factor in seven tumor types but a protective factor in one; patients with low EGFR expression but high dependency exhibited poorer survival outcomes (Fig. J–L). This aligns with EGFR’s known oncogenic role, where high dependency reflects advanced tumor progression [ , ]. In contrast, WEE1 dependency was protective in six tumor types (Fig. M–O), consistent with WEE1’s tumor-suppressive functions through cell cycle inhibition [ , ]. Patients highly dependent on WEE1 showed better prognosis. These results demonstrate that GATDep-predicted gene dependency scores not only recapitulate known tumor biology but also provide meaningful insights into chemotherapy response and patient survival, highlighting the model’s translational potential in clinical oncology.

## Discussion
In this study, we developed a GAT-based model that systematically integrates genomic and CRISPR screening data, leveraging established gene–gene and gene–gene set interaction networks alongside biological functional annotations. This model quantitatively predicts gene dependency in a sample-specific manner by dynamically incorporating transcriptomic profiles, thereby enabling the identification of context-specific essential genes. Such insights offer a novel framework for target discovery, drug screening, and mechanistic elucidation.
We initially curated gene effect data derived from expression profiles and CRISPR screens across 1,088 cancer cell lines. Given that gene dependency is inherently context- or sample-specific, this variability partly explains the inconsistent reproducibility of known synthetic lethal interactions across different samples [ , ]. We hypothesized that gene dependency correlates with gene function; genes integral to critical cellular processes such as cell cycle regulation, homologous recombination repair, and metabolism are more likely to induce lethality upon loss. For example, the well-characterized synthetic lethal pair TP53-WEE1 reflects cellular reliance on the G2/M checkpoint mediated by WEE1 following TP53 mutation-driven G1/S checkpoint dysfunction [ ]. To capture gene function, we represented each gene through weighted gene sets using GSVA scores calculated from gene expression data, rather than employing direct gene set fingerprints as done in DeepDEP [ ]. This approach aims to better reflect the biological relevance of genes in individual samples and thereby quantify gene dependency. Since gene effect values predominantly range from − 3 to 1 (mostly negative), to prevent bias during training and enhance model convergence, raw gene effect scores were scaled to a 0–1 range. During training, cell lines were clustered into 10 groups based on their GSVA profiles, and data were partitioned in an 8:1:1 ratio for training, validation, and testing. Model performance was rigorously evaluated using 10-fold LOOCV, yielding a high correlation of predicted versus observed gene dependency scores, 0.93 in training/validation and 0.86 in testing datasets, demonstrating effective information capture.
To comprehensively capture sample-specific genomic features at multiple molecular levels, we incorporated somatic mutation and CNV data alongside transcriptomic profiles. Mutation data for 1,088 cell lines were retrieved from DepMap, with a focus on identifying loss-of-function mutations within the 2,350 selected genes. These mutation annotations were integrated with expression-derived features as inputs for the GATDep model. Subsequently, CNV profiles indicating gene-level amplifications and deletions were appended to form a multi-modal input feature set comprising expression, mutation, and CNV data for GAT model training. In parallel, a fully connected deep neural network (DNN) model was trained using gene set scores as inputs. All models were trained and evaluated using rigorous 10-fold leave-one-out cross-validation. For benchmarking, we employed the DeepDEP model, which utilizes an encoder to reduce dimensionality and extract information from gene expression data, pre-trained on TCGA cohorts and fine-tuned on cancer cell line datasets. DeepDEP integrates expression, mutation, and CNV data with CRISPR gene effect measurements to predict dependency but was trained on a smaller dataset (278 cell lines and 1,298 genes). We applied DeepDEP to predict dependency scores for 1,088 cell lines covering 1,298 genes. Comparative analyses revealed that DeepDEP’s performance on individual gene correlation was comparable to the DNN model but significantly inferior to GATDep. Conversely, DeepDEP demonstrated higher overall gene-level correlation, likely attributable to differences in data preprocessing; specifically, we scaled gene effect scores to a 0–1 range to mitigate the impact of outliers on correlation metrics, whereas DeepDEP utilized raw gene effect values. Consistently, mean squared error was significantly lower for GATDep and DNN compared to DeepDEP, indicating reduced prediction bias, which we attribute partly to scaling. The advantages of scaling gene effect scores include: (1) improved model convergence speed; (2) emphasis on the relative ranking of gene dependency within samples rather than absolute effect values, preserving the biological ordering of gene importance; and (3) robustness against technical variations, given that DepMap updates gene effect values quarterly via the Chronos pipeline [ ], which may introduce shifts in absolute values over time. Consequently, focusing on dependency rankings rather than raw scores offers greater translational stability and utility.
To elucidate the underlying biological networks captured by GATDep, we applied GNNExplainer to interpret the model’s learned graph structures. This analysis not only recapitulated established synthetic lethal interactions but also identified putative synergistic and antagonistic gene relationships. For instance, analysis of the TP53 node revealed a strong association between TP53 dependency and KPNA2, consistent with their known functional interplay. Examination of BRCA1 highlighted contributions from genes including BRAP, BRAT, and PPIL4 to BRCA1 dependency predictions (Supplementary Fig. K). BRAP was initially identified as a BRCA1-interacting partner and is known to modulate the nuclear import of BRCA1 and other proteins. Functionally, BRAP influences cellular proliferation, migration, and differentiation through MAPK and TGF-β/PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathways. Pan-cancer analyses have linked BRAP expression to DNA mismatch repair (MMR) gene mutations and DNA methyltransferase levels, highlighting its potential role in genomic instability and tumor progression [ , ]. These associations reinforce the functional significance of the BRCA1–BRAP edge detected by our model. BRAT1 (BRCA1-associated ATM activator 1) interacts with both BRCA1 and ATM, key components of the DNA damage response. As a member of the BRCA1-associated genome surveillance complex (BASC), BRAT1 facilitates cell cycle checkpoint activation and double-strand break (DSB) repair. Functional studies have implicated BRAT1 in mTOR pathway regulation and genome integrity maintenance, aligning with its predicted role in mediating BRCA1-associated dependency [ ]. PPIL4 encodes a member of the cyclophilin family with peptidyl-prolyl isomerase activity, contributing to protein folding and angiogenesis. While a direct interaction with BRCA1 has not been fully elucidated, related cyclophilins such as PIN1 have been shown to regulate homologous recombination repair and sensitize BRCA1-proficient tumors to PARP inhibition by modulating DNA end resection [ , ]. The detection of a BRCA1–PPIL4 edge may thus reflect broader isomerase-related regulatory functions influencing BRCA1-mediated DNA repair. Further analysis of EGFR dependency revealed genes such as MUC20 and COPB1 (Supplementary Fig. K). MUC20, a transmembrane mucin overexpressed in various epithelial cancers, has been shown to potentiate EGFR-driven oncogenic signaling. In endometrial cancer models, MUC20 enhances EGF-induced cellular proliferation, migration, and invasion by amplifying EGFR–STAT3 axis activation, ultimately correlating with poor prognosis [ ]. The identification of an EGFR–MUC20 edge by GATDep suggests that MUC20-mediated amplification of EGFR signaling may represent a context-specific determinant of EGFR dependency in tumor cells. COPB1 is a core component of the COPI complex, which mediates retrograde vesicular trafficking from the Golgi apparatus to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). Recent studies have demonstrated that COPB1 is critical for regulating the nuclear translocation of EGFR through COPI-mediated retrograde trafficking. This process modulates EGFR signaling output and may influence therapeutic response, particularly in EGFR-mutant non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [ , ]. The EGFR–COPB1 interaction recovered by our model highlights the potential role of intracellular trafficking dynamics in shaping EGFR dependency, an often-overlooked layer of regulation in drug response. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that GATDep not only accurately predicts gene dependency but also uncovers complex intergenic dependency networks with biological and therapeutic relevance.
To assess whether GATDep can effectively identify synthetic lethal interactions, we evaluated the model using two independent datasets. In the GSE272107 dataset, a metabolism-focused sgRNA library comprising 2,948 genes was employed to perform CRISPR-based dropout screens aimed at identifying synthetic lethal interactions associated with DNA damage response (DDR) inhibitors, such as ATM and ATR inhibitors. The library was lentivirally transduced into A549 cells, followed by treatment with either ATMi or ATRi. In both treatment groups, PRDX1 and PTEN consistently emerged among the top 10 dependency genes, suggesting a potential synthetic lethal relationship with DDR inhibition. This interaction was subsequently validated in both in vitro and in vivo experiments. We extracted gene expression profiles from A549 cells treated with DMSO or ATMi and used GATDep to predict gene dependency scores. Remarkably, PRDX1 and PTEN were among the top-ranked genes in the ATMi-treated cells, confirming a heightened dependency on these genes relative to the DMSO control. These findings demonstrate that GATDep can infer gene dependency shifts from transcriptomic data alone, offering a scalable and transcriptome-based approach for identifying synthetic lethal targets. Similarly, in an independent genome-wide CRISPR screening dataset, GATDep successfully identified a synthetic lethal interaction between the transcription factor ZBTB7A and KDM5 inhibition, further validating its capacity to detect such interactions across diverse contexts.
We further explored the utility of GATDep-predicted dependency scores in drug sensitivity prediction. Using gene expression profiles from 702 cell lines in the GDSC2 pharmacogenomic dataset, we applied GATDep to compute gene dependency scores and investigated their correlation with small molecule responses. Notably, dependency scores for MDM2 were significantly positively correlated with LN IC50 values for Nutlin-3a, a selective MDM2 inhibitor that stabilizes p53 by preventing its proteasomal degradation [ ]. These results suggest that genes with lower dependency scores are more likely to confer increased sensitivity to their corresponding targeted inhibitors, highlighting the potential of GATDep as a computational tool for drug prioritization based on transcriptomic profiles.
Given the model’s ability to capture context-specific gene dependency, we hypothesized that GATDep might also facilitate combination therapy design. To evaluate this, we leveraged data from the DrugComb repository, which includes over 5,000 drug pair combinations tested across 60 cancer cell lines. We first analyzed Vorinostat-treated K562 cells. Vorinostat, a histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor [ ], induces tumor cell apoptosis by altering chromatin structure and gene transcription. Post-treatment, these cells exhibited resistance to ERBB2/3-targeting kinase inhibitors such as Lapatinib, but remained sensitive to proteasome inhibitors, ribosome inhibitors, and transcription initiation inhibitors. GATDep analysis of gene expression profiles from Vorinostat-treated cells revealed that weak dependency genes were significantly enriched in the ERBB signaling pathway, suggesting decreased reliance on this axis. Conversely, strong dependency genes were enriched in pathways such as ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, proteasome function, and translation initiation—mirroring the observed sensitivity to Bortezomib and Sunitinib. A similar analysis was also conducted on MDA-MB-231 cells treated with Gefitinib. Among the top dependency genes, we identified dihydrofolate reductase, an essential enzyme in folate metabolism and nucleotide biosynthesis. The strong dependency on DHFR implies increased sensitivity to antifolates such as Methotrexate. Consistently, Vorinostat and Methotrexate have been reported to exhibit synergistic anti-proliferative effects in MDA-MB-231 cells. Gene set enrichment analysis of strong dependency genes in Gefitinib-treated cells revealed upregulation of HDAC-related processes, while weak dependency genes were enriched in the MAPK signaling pathway. These results are consistent with DrugComb findings, where Gefitinib-treated MDA-MB-231 cells showed increased sensitivity to HDAC inhibitors and antifolates, but not to MAPK inhibitors such as Sorafenib. Altogether, these results underscore the capacity of GATDep to capture drug-induced rewiring of gene dependency networks, enabling mechanistic insights and combination therapy discovery based on post-treatment transcriptomic profiles.
While the analyses above were conducted in cell line models, we next sought to assess whether GATDep could be applied to patient-derived transcriptomic data. Using the TCGA dataset, we investigated the dependency of WRN in tumors stratified by MSI status. Consistent with previous reports describing synthetic lethality between WRN and MSI, we observed significantly lower WRN dependency scores in MSI-high tumors compared to MSS or MSI-low samples, suggesting that GATDep can recapitulate known synthetic lethal relationships in clinical contexts. To further examine the relationship between gene dependency and therapeutic response, we grouped patients based on chemotherapy outcomes and compared dependency scores between the two groups. In colorectal cancer, most genes showed significantly lower dependency scores in the responder group, indicating greater reliance on these genes and enrichment in pathways such as the cell cycle and key signaling cascades. In contrast, in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, non-responders exhibited lower dependency scores for genes enriched in energy metabolism, ATP synthesis, and mitochondrial processes, implicating metabolic reprogramming in chemoresistance. To explore the prognostic relevance of gene dependency, we performed a pan-cancer survival analysis across 32 TCGA cancer types using GATDep-inferred dependency scores for 2,350 genes. A total of 1,446 genes were significantly associated with patient outcomes in at least one cancer type. For example, patients exhibiting strong dependency on EGFR, reflected by lower EGFR dependency scores, had significantly poorer overall survival, consistent with EGFR’s established role in tumor progression, angiogenesis, and metastasis [ ]. Conversely, patients with high dependency on WEE1, a critical G2/M checkpoint kinase, showed more favorable prognosis, aligning with WEE1’s tumor-suppressive role in maintaining genome integrity under replication stress [ ].
While GATDep showed high predictive accuracy across diverse applications, including synthetic lethality detection, drug response prediction, combination therapy screening, and prognostic evaluation, its integration of genomic features such as mutation and CNV profiles yielded limited performance gains. This aligns with previous findings indicating that expression data alone can effectively capture functional gene dependencies and drug response [ , ]. Despite its promising performance, several limitations remain. Model design: The GAT framework assumes that local graph neighborhoods are biologically meaningful, but gene interaction networks are often incomplete or context-agnostic. This may lead to noise propagation through attention weights. Data heterogeneity: DepMap integrates multiple experimental batches and screening platforms. Despite normalization efforts, systematic bias and missing data may still affect the learned dependencies. Biological relevance: Not all co-dependencies captured by the model necessarily reflect causal or mechanistic interactions, further experimental validation is needed to distinguish correlative from causal dependencies. Additionally, computational constraints limited our current implementation to relatively shallow GAT architectures and moderate batch sizes. Systematic hyperparameter optimization and scaling could further enhance performance. Lastly, the lack of clinical treatment and in vivo validation data represents a key limitation of this study, warranting further investigation in future work.

## Conclusions
In summary, we developed a GAT-based framework, GATDep, to quantitatively infer sample-specific gene dependency from gene expression data. GATDep integrates prior knowledge from gene–gene interaction networks and gene–gene set associations, and relies on GSVA-derived pathway activity scores, which obviate the need to directly process raw expression data and mitigate platform-specific variability. The model’s design enables both single-sample and comparative analyses, facilitating the identification of context-dependent essential genes under various biological and therapeutic conditions. Overall, GATDep offers a novel and practical framework for assessing gene dependency in a context-specific manner, with broad potential for informing target discovery, drug repurposing, and mechanistic insights in cancer research.
