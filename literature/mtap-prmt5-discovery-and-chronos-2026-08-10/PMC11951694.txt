## Abstract

## Aim
To investigate the comprehensive expression levels and possible molecular mechanisms of Anaphase Promoting Complex Subunit 1 (ANAPC1) in lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC).

## Methods
Data from 2,031 samples were combined to evaluate ANAPC1 mRNA levels, and 118 samples were collected for immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis. High-expression co-expressed genes (HECEGs) associated with ANAPC1 were analyzed for signaling pathways. Clinical significance, immune computations, and Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats (CRISPR) validation of ANAPC1’s role in LUSC were assessed. Molecular docking evaluated binding affinity with potential therapeutics.

## Results
ANAPC1 mRNA was significantly upregulated in LUSC (SMD = 1.97, 95% CI [1.26–2.67]). Protein-level analysis confirmed this upregulation (p < 0.001). Most HECEGs associated with ANAPC1 were enriched in cell cycle pathways. Higher ANAPC1 expression correlated with poorer survival in LUSC patients (HR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1–1.49). ANAPC1 expression was higher in males and N1-stage vs. females and N0-stage; lower in grade I vs. II/III. Overexpression reduces immune cell infiltration and immunotherapy effectiveness, while knockdown inhibits cell proliferation. Drug sensitivity and docking analyses identified tenovin-1, carboxyatractyloside, and phycocyanobilin as potential antitumor agents targeting ANAPC1.

## Conclusion
The elevated expression of ANAPC1 might play a role in LUSC advancement and progression through its participation in cell growth-related pathways.

Keywords: Lung squamous cell carcinoma, gene expression, ANAPC1, clinical value, molecular mechanism, immunohistochemistry

## PLAIN LANGUAGE SUMMARY
This study looks at over 2000 samples and shows that a protein called ANAPC1 is found in higher amounts in lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC). The study finds that higher levels of ANAPC1 are associated with poorer survival outcomes and are more commonly observed in males and at later stages of the disease. Lower levels of ANAPC1 might help patients respond better to immunotherapy. The study also shows that removing ANAPC1 slows down cancer cell growth and identifies possible compounds that could target ANAPC1, offering hope for new therapies for LUSC.

## Introduction
Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for 80%–85% of new lung cancer cases, with lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) comprising 25%–30% of these [ , ]. LUSC is closely linked to smoking, marked by genomic instability and high malignancy, often resulting in an unfavorable prognosis [ , ]. At present, surgical resection is the favored therapy for early-stage LUSC, whereas the primary first-line strategy for advanced LUSC involves incorporates both chemotherapy and immunotherapy [ , ]. While immunotherapy has significantly improved the prognosis of LUSC patients, a substantial number of patients still do not respond to existing immunotherapies, and post-treatment adverse effects can be severe [ ]. The precise pathogenic mechanisms underlying LUSC, given its unique biological characteristics, remain to be fully elucidated. The efficacy of current targeted therapies is limited [ ], highlighting the need for future research to focus on deciphering the molecular mechanisms of LUSC to achieve more precise therapeutic breakthroughs.
Anaphase-promoting complex subunit 1 (ANAPC1), situated on chromosome 2q13, encodes the largest subunit of the anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C) [ ]. The APC/C acts as an E3 ubiquitin ligase facilitating the ubiquitination and degradation of proteins, primarily controlling the cell cycle, including mitosis and the G1/S transition [ , ]. ANAPC1 forms a scaffold complex with APC4 and APC5 within APC/C, participating in APC/C-dependent substrate ubiquitination [ ]. ANAPC1 plays a role in various cancers, and its abnormal expression may be closely related to tumorigenesis in multiple cancers. ANAPC1 is widely expressed in many tissues and cancer cell lines, with significantly elevated protein levels observed in malignancies such as hepatocellular carcinoma, bladder cancer, and melanoma [ ]. The mRNA expression of ANAPC1 in NSCLC tissues is significantly elevated compared to nonmalignant lung tissues [ ]. Nevertheless, its specific molecular mechanisms in LUSC remain poorly understood. Furthermore, it was reported that mutations in ANAPC1 could be a significant factor leading to heightened susceptibility to cancer [ ]. Genome-wide association studies and meta-analyses have revealed an association between ANAPC1 and nicotine dependence (ND), which has been validated in independent samples [ ]. Considering that LUSC is closely related to smoking and ND, we hypothesis that ANAPC1 likely contributes to the pathogenesis of lung cancer. However, no studies have directly elucidated the roles of ANAPC1 in LUSC, which was warranted further investigation.
This study initially assessed the expression levels of ANAPC1 in LUSC through the analysis of extensive multi-center data and in-house clinical samples. Meanwhile, the possible molecular mechanisms, the key clinical significance, the significant correlation with immune infiltration, and potential therapeutic strategies targeting ANAPC1 of ANAPC1 in LUSC were explored. Therein, the effect of ANAPC1 on LUSC cell growth was validated through Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats (CRISPR) screening data. Overall, this investigation was the inaugural report focusing on the role of ANAPC1 in LUSC, offering fresh perspectives on the molecular targeted therapy of LUSC.

## Materials and methods
2.1.

## Data selection and preprocessing
The high-throughput datasets associated with LUSC utilized in this research were obtained from several databases, including Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx), ArrayExpress. The data collection workflow is illustrated in Supplementary Material Figure S1.
In total, this study incorporated 26 datasets (as listed in Supplementary Material Table S1), comprising 1,036 LUSC samples and 995 control samples. The ‘limma’ package [ ] was employed for data processing, including log2(x + 1) transformation and normalization. Datasets from the same experimental platform were merged. Ultimately, 16 newly consolidated cohorts were acquired, and the ComBat function of the ‘SVA’ package was used to mitigate batch effects [ ].

2.2.

## IHC staining to assess ANAPC1 protein expression in LUSC tissues
Three tissue microarray sections (catalog numbers: LUC1022, LUC1023, LUC1504) used in this study were provided by Guilin Fanpu Biotech, a subsidiary wholly owned by Pantomics, Inc. The arrays consisted of 91 samples of LUSC and 27 non-tumorous lung tissues, both originating from the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University. All procedures were meticulously executed in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. The tissue sections were initially fixed with formalin and subsequently embedded in paraffin. They then underwent deparaffinization and antigen retrieval, utilizing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid buffer. After endogenous peroxidase was inactivated, the sections were incubated with a rabbit anti-human ANAPC1 monoclonal antibody (Thermofisher, PA5-83639, dilution ratio 1:500) and kept at 4 °C. Sections incubated for 30 min at 25 °C, stained with Diaminobenzidine, counterstained, dehydrated, and sealed. Microscopic evaluations assessed staining intensity and proportion. Staining intensity was graded as: 0 (no staining, appearing blue), 1 (weak staining, light yellow), 2 (moderate staining, yellow-brown), and 3 (strong staining, dark brown). The proportion of positive cells was scored as: 0 (<5%), 1 (5%–25%), 2 (26%–50%), 3 (51%–75%), and 4 (>75%). The immunohistochemical (IHC) score was determined by multiplying the intensity score by the proportion score. Two senior pathologists independently reviewed all scores. The raw experimental data can be found in Supplementary Material 2. Informed consent was obtained from all patients, and the research was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (2024-S203-01).

2.3.

## ANAPC1 expression and its clinical correlation in LUSC
The differences in ANAPC1 expression between the LUSC and control groups were evaluated employing the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, t-test, and standardized mean difference (SMD). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed when the combined dataset displayed skewness or unequal variance; otherwise, the t-test was utilized. SMD was calculated to perform a comprehensive analysis of ANAPC1 expression between the LUSC and control groups. A random effects model was selected to assess SMD if substantial heterogeneity existed between datasets (I2 > 50% and p ≤ 0.05). Publication bias for SMD was evaluated using Egger’s test, and a sensitivity analysis was performed to determine the robustness of the results.
To further ascertain the differential expression of ANAPC1 between tumor and control tissues, the Area Under the Curve (AUC) of both the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve and the Summary ROC (sROC) curve were utilized. The association of ANAPC1 expression with overall survival (OS: duration from diagnosis to death) was analyzed utilizing the Kaplan-Meier Plotter (https://kmplot.com/analysis/index.php?p=service&cancer=lung).
Clinical parameters and corresponding ANAPC1 mRNA expression data were obtained from the TCGA database, and were supplemented with clinical parameters and IHC protein expression data derived from clinicopathological samples to form a new matrix. Subsequently, independent sample t-tests and ANOVA were employed to investigate whether there were differences in ANAPC1 expression levels across different clinicopathological parameters.

2.4.

## Potential mechanism analysis of ANAPC1 in LUSC
2.4.1.

## Exploring potential transcriptional regulators of ANAPC1
The Cistrome Data Browser serves as a comprehensive database for chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing, encompassing a multitude of human transcription factors (TFs), histone modifications, and samples pertaining to chromatin accessibility [ ]. The Cistrome Data Browser was utilized to predict the upstream TFs of ANAPC1, and the potential TFs of ANAPC1 were filtered by taking the intersection of genes that met the criteria of both the Pearson correlation coefficient (r) and the SMD. Furthermore, to validate these predictions, chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) data pertaining to TFs within the Cistrome Data Browser is utilized, with strict adherence to ensuring that the ChIP-seq data fulfills all quality assessment standards.

2.4.2.

## Potential signaling pathways of ANAPC1 in LUSC
Utilizing LUSC microarray and RNA sequencing data, we computed the mRNA expression SMD along with its 95% confidence interval (CI) for each gene. Genes exhibiting SMD > 0, a 95% CI > 0, and a p-value < 0.05 are classified as highly expressed in LUSC. ANAPC1 co-expressed genes were identified using the Pearson correlation coefficient, with screening criteria of r ≥ 0.3, p < 0.05, and applicability in at least 12 datasets. Genes that exhibit both high expression and co-expression with ANAPC1 are referred to as high expression co-expressed genes (HECEGs) of ANAPC1. These HECEGs are employed to explore the potential mechanisms underlying the role of ANAPC1 in LUSC. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis are completed by the ‘clusterProfiler’ package [ ], and Reactome enrichment analysis is performed by the ‘ReactomePA’ package [ ].
Utilizing the ‘clusterProfiler’ package, a Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed, utilizing TCGA_LUSC_mRNA data, to investigate the relationship between ANAPC1’s expression levels and the biological pathways, along with the molecular mechanisms associated with LUSC [ ]. The subset “c2.cp.kegg_medicus.v2024.1.Hs.entrez.gmt” is downloaded from the Molecular Signatures Database. To investigate the relationships among the core genes of significantly enriched pathways, a Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) network was created utilizing the STRING database (v12.0). The species is set as “Homo sapiens”, the confidence is 0.4, and the remaining parameters remain default. CytoHubba’s MCC algorithm identifies the ten most significant hub genes within the network, whereas Cytoscape (v3.10.0) serves as the tool for visualization.

2.5.

## Immune characteristic analysis of ANAPC1 in LUSC
2.5.1.

## Immune infiltration analysis
Using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA), an evaluation was conducted to investigate the differences in immune cell scores between the high and low expression cohorts of ANAPC1 in LUSC samples obtained from TCGA_LUSC_mRNA data. A detailed analysis was conducted to examine the correlation between ANAPC1 and immune cell scores, investigating the prospect of ANAPC1 as an immune prediction index. The ESTIMATE algorithm was employed to evaluate the overall impact of immune and stromal cells within the tumor microenvironment (TME) of LUSC.

2.5.2.

## Immune phenotype score analysis
The immune phenotype score (IPS) varies from 0 to 10 and is computed through machine learning algorithms, based on the gene expression profiles of representative cell types. The IPS serves as a predictor of the patient’s response to immunotherapy. The score consists of four components: effector cells, immunosuppressive cells, MHC molecules, and immunoregulatory factors, enabling a comprehensive evaluation of immunogenicity [ ]. A higher IPS indicates a positive correlation with enhanced immunogenicity [ ]. The Cancer Immunome Atlas provided the immune phenotype scores for patients with LUSC, and a Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to evaluate the relationship between ANAPC1 expression levels and the IPS.

2.6.

## Potential effects of the ANAPC1 gene on LUSC cells
The DepMap Portal offers data derived from genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 loss-of-function screens [ ], and employs the Chronos gene effect score to evaluate the influence of gene knockout on cell viability [ ]. To elucidate the function of the ANAPC1 gene in LUSC cell growth, this study retrieved LUSC cell line data, which included the CRISPR ANAPC1 Chronos score, from the DepMap Portal to examine the impact of ANAPC1 knockout in these cell lines. A negative score indicates that the growth of LUSC cells is inhibited after knocking out the ANAPC1 gene. The cBioPortal database is rich in data, covering multiple cancer types and multi-dimensional information, providing extensive resources for cancer research. This study uses the cBioPortal database to explore gene mutations of ANAPC1 in LUSC.

2.7.

## Drug molecular docking
To screen potential therapeutic drugs targeting ANAPC1 in LUSC, ANAPC1 was mapped to the Coremine Medical database, and the association strength between ANAPC1 and drugs was quantitatively evaluated through significance values. Obtain the top ten drugs in terms of association strength. After excluding drugs that are toxic to the human body and are not drug monomers, select the remaining five drugs for molecular docking. The crystal structure of ANAPC1 (PDB ID: 5LGG) was obtained from the RCSB PDB database, with solvent molecules and co-crystallized ligands removed utilizing PyMOL 2.4. The drug structure is obtained from the PubChem database. Subsequently, molecular docking was conducted using the CB-Dock2 platform (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/php/index.php) with the selected ligand’s protein target. CB-Dock2 functions as a molecular docking tool leveraging AutoDock Vina analysis, enabling automatic identification and analysis of ligand-receptor binding sites [ ]. Finally, drugs with a minimum binding energy less than -7 kcal/mol are screened out according to the docking results, and the results of these drugs are visualized.

2.8.

## Statistical analysis
Besides the aforementioned statistical methods, Spearman’s correlation coefficient was employed in all relevant analyses to assess the immune association of ANAPC1 in LUSC. A 95% CI for SMD that excludes 0 indicates statistical significance, while p > 0.1 suggests no substantial publication bias in the SMD results. Regarding hazard ratio (HR), a 95% CI including 1 or p > 0.05 denotes a lack of statistical significance. The calculations and visualizations were conducted using R (v4.3.1), Stata (v17.0), SPSS 27.0, and GraphPad Prism 10.0. The procedures of this study are illustrated in .

## Results
3.1.

## ANAPC1 mRNA is significantly overexpressed in LUSC tissues
Among the 16 datasets analyzed in this research, a notable elevation in ANAPC1 mRNA expression levels was observed in 15 cohorts when comparing the LUSC group to the control group (excluding “GSE159857”) (p < 0.05; Supplementary Material Figure S2A). The model of random effects revealed that the levels of ANAPC1 mRNA were notably higher in 1036 LUSC samples when juxtaposed against 995 control samples (SMD = 1.97, 95% CI [1.26–2.67]; see Supplementary Material Figure S2B). Both Begg’s and Egger’s tests confirmed the absence of publication bias in the SMD results (p = 0.264; Supplementary Material Figure S2C–D). Given the notable disparity in ANAPC1 mRNA expression between LUSC and control samples, the potential utility of the marker for diagnosing LUSC was assessed through the examination of ROC and sROC curves. The ROC curve for each dataset was drawn to assess the diagnostic potential of ANAPC1 mRNA expression in LUSC. The findings revealed a significant increase in ANAPC1 mRNA expression within LUSC samples. Supplementary Material Figure S3A displays the outcomes of the ROC analysis. Moreover, sROC analysis confirmed high ANAPC1 expression in LUSC (AUC: 0.96 [0.94–0.97], Supplementary Material Figure S3B), with sensitivity of 0.92 (95% CI: 0.79–0.98) and specificity of 0.90 (95% CI: 0.81–0.95, Supplementary Material Figure S3C).

3.2.

## Verification of ANAPC1 protein level in LUSC
Immunohistochemical staining of clinicopathological samples (n = 118) was also performed to verify ANAPC1 protein level expression in LUSC tissues. Under the microscope, no ANAPC1 protein expression was observed in the control tissues ( ), but a distinct and positive staining of the ANAPC1 protein was evident in LUSC tissues, as depicted in , and the level of protein exhibited a substantial increase (p < 0.001; as depicted in ). At the protein level, ROC curve analysis demonstrated a significant difference in ANAPC1 expression between LUSC and control samples (AUC: 0.989, 95% CI: 0.976–1.000; ), further supporting its elevated expression in this cancer.
 
3.3.

## Potential mechanism analysis of ANAPC1 in LUSC
3.3.1.

## CDK8 is a potential transcriptional regulator of ANAPC1
As shown in Supplementary Material Figure S4A, nine TFs with selection scores of ≥0.95 were screened based on the prediction results of the Cistrome Data Browser. By applying the SMD calculation method and conducting a co-expression analysis, CDK8 was pinpointed as a potential TF which governs the expression of ANAPC1 and demonstrates a positive association with it. The ChIP-Seq peak signal for CDK8 is positioned within 1 kb both upstream and downstream of ANAPC1, which further corroborates this finding (see Supplementary Material Figure S5).

3.3.2.

## Potential mechanism analysis of ANAPC1 in LUSC
In this study, a total of 558 HECEGs can be screened for next-step analysis. In Gene Ontology Biological Process (GO-BP) analysis, HECEGs are significantly enriched within pathways such as “sister chromatid segregation”, “regulation of chromosome organization”, and “mitotic nuclear division” (Supplementary Material Figure S4B). In Gene Ontology Cell Component (GO–CC) analysis, HECEGs are significantly enriched within signal pathways such as “condensed chromosome”, “nuclear chromosome”, and “chromosome, centromeric region” (Supplementary Material Figure S4C). In Gene Ontology Molecular Function (GO-MF) analysis, HECEGs are significantly enriched within pathways such as “catalytic activity, acting on DNA”, “ATP hydrolysis activity” and “DNA helicase activity” (Supplementary Material Figure S4D). KEGG analysis shows that HECEGs associated with ANAPC1 are mainly enriched in signal pathways such as “Cell cycle”, “DNA replication”, “Base excision repair”, “Nucleocytoplasmic transport” and “p53 signaling pathway” (Supplementary Material Figure S4E). Reactome analysis shows that HECEGs are mainly involved in significant enrichment of “Cell Cycle Checkpoints”, “G1/S Transition”, “Mitotic G1 phase and G1/S transition”, “Mitotic Anaphase” and “S Phase” (Supplementary Material Figure S4F). These results suggest that HECEGs of ANAPC1 may affect the development of LUSC by participating in the regulation of related pathways.
GSEA analysis indicates the potential role of ANAPC1 across various signaling pathways. In the group characterized by high expression, the curve reaches its zenith, indicating a direct proportional relationship between pathway activity and levels of ANAPC1, thus increasing when ANAPC1 expression rises. Utilizing TCGA_LUSC_mRNA data, GSEA shows that ANAPC1 is related to pathways including “DNA REPLICATION LICENSING”, “PRE IC FORMATION”, and “ORIGIN UNWINDING AND ELONGATION” (Supplementary Material Figure S6A–B). Protein-protein interactions (PPIs) are employed to further elucidate the molecular mechanism of ANAPC1. Interaction analysis identified ANAPC1’s connections with core genes from the two most significant pathways (Supplementary Material Figure S6C–D), revealing the common hub gene CDT1 along with six additional shared hub genes.

## Correlation between ANAPC1 and the immune landscape of LUSC
Within the TCGA_LUSC_mRNA data, individuals diagnosed with LUSC were categorized into two cohorts: those with high ANAPC1 levels and those with low ANAPC1 levels, utilizing the median expression value of ANAPC1 as the cutoff point. Utilizing ssGSEA analysis, notable disparities were observed in the scores of 27 immune cell categories (excluding Memory B cells) between these two groups, with the high ANAPC1 expression group exhibiting lower immune cell levels (p < 0.05, see ). To delve deeper into the relationship between ANAPC1 and immune cell scores, a correlation analysis was performed. The results uncovered an inverse relationship between ANAPC1 expression and the scores of most immune cells, as depicted in . Specifically, ANAPC1 expression revealed a pronounced inverse correlation with 27 distinct immune cell categories, notably including Central memory CD4 T cells (r = −0.4, p = 2.6e − 20), Type 17 T helper cells (r = −0.37, p = 8.7e − 18), Monocytes (r = −0.36, p = 3.8e − 17), and Type 1 T helper cells (r = −0.34, p = 5.9e − 15).
 Given the crucial function of the TME in immune surveillance, evasion, suppression, and treatment [ ], and the critical importance of immune responses in anti-tumor activities, this investigation further assessed the TME. Using the ESTIMATE algorithm, the analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between ANAPC1 expression and TME Stromal Score (r = −0.28; p = 1.4e − 10), Immune Score (r = −0.38; p = 2.2e − 16), and ESTIMATE Score (r = −0.35; p = 3.1e − 16), while showing a positive correlation with tumor purity (r = 0.35; p = 3.1e − 16) ( ). A notable discrepancy was detected in the TME scores when comparing the group with high ANAPC1 expression to the group with low expression (p < 0.001, as illustrated in ).
In this study, the IPS of ips_ctla4_neg_pd1_neg, ips_ctla4_neg_pd1_pos, ips_ctla4_pos_pd1_neg, and ips_ctla4_pos_pd1_pos in the ANAPC1 low expression group was significantly higher than that in the ANAPC1 high expression group (Supplementary Material Figure S8). Revealing that patients with low expression of ANAPC1 are expected to be candidates for immune checkpoint inhibitors in LUSC patients.

3.6.

## Potential effects of ANAPC1 gene on LUSC cells
3.6.1.

## Growth of LUSC cells is strongly inhibited after knockout of the ANAPC1 gene
The Chronos score emphasizes ANAPC1’s significance in diverse LUSC cell lines. When the score falls below 0, it suggests that decreasing the expression of ANAPC1 is apt to increase cancer cell death. As shown in Supplementary Material Figure S9, the gene effect scores for ANAPC1 in 21 LUSC cell lines are below −1, indicating a strong inhibition of proliferation in these lines following ANAPC1 gene knockout, which suggests that ANAPC1 is essential for the survival of cancer cells in LUSC.

3.6.2.

## Gene alterations of ANAPC1 in LUSC and its correlation with mRNA expression
Genetic alterations are one of the key factors in tumorigenesis and progression. The findings revealed that the incidence of ANAPC1 mutations in LUSC cases amounted to 10%, and it was relatively abundant in the “mRNA High” annotation (Supplementary Material Figure S10A). Further analysis found that the top ten genes most notably upregulated in the gene alteration group compared to the unaltered group are: PIK3CA, FYTTD1, MELTF, HTR3E, ATP11B, MCF2L2, ANKRD18DP, FAM157A, RPL35A and CEP19 (Supplementary Material Figure S10B). In LUSC individuals, under the condition of copy number amplification, ANAPC1 mRNA levels are higher than those of other types of alterations in ANAPC1 (Supplementary Material Figure S10C).

3.7.

## Identification of potential inhibitor drugs for ANAPC1
This study predicted the targeted drugs of ANAPC1 from the perspective of drug treatment and performed molecular docking on the predicted drugs. According to the magnitude of the association strength, the top 10 drugs are obtained. Among them, the monomer drugs of nontoxic substances are tenovin-1, policosanol, carboxyatractyloside, phycocyanobilin, and 2,3-dihydroxybenzoic acid (see ); according to the results of molecular docking, the drugs with the minimum binding energy ranking below −7.0 kcal/mol are tenovin-1, carboxyatractyloside, and phycocyanobilin. Molecular docking shows that the ANAPC1 protein has a high molecular affinity with tenovin-1 (affinity = −8.3 kcal/mol), carboxyatractyloside (affinity = −7.6 kcal/mol), and phycocyanobilin (affinity = −8.6 kcal/mol) (the molecular docking pattern diagram is shown in ).

## Discussion
NSCLC remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [ ]. Unlike lung adenocarcinoma, LUSC has limited benefit from molecular targeted therapy due to its unique biological characteristics [ ]. It is well known that cigarette smoking tends to induce molecular mutations and acts as the predominant risk factor for LUSC development. Previous studies have found that ANAPC1 is up-regulated in various cancers (including NSCLC) [ ], and commonly alters in lung cancer [ ]. Moreover, ANAPC1 is associated with ND [ ]. All these findings suggest that ANAPC1 is likely to play a part in the carcinogenesis of LUSC. However, to date, no studies have systematically investigated the functional or clinical associations between ANAPC1 and LUSC. This study employed a multi-omics approach to comprehensively characterize the clinicopathological significance of ANAPC1 in LUSC, systematically evaluating its expression patterns, prognostic implications, immunomodulatory functions, and upstream signaling cascades through integrated genomic analyses, thereby establishing novel mechanistic frameworks to address critical knowledge gaps in LUSC.
ANAPC1 might contribute to the development and progression of LUSC. As the same as previous reports [ ], in this work, it was validated that the expression level of ANAPC1 was increased in LUSC tissues compared with non-tumorous lung tissues. Through comprehensively analyzed 1036 LUSC samples and 995 control samples, it was found that ANAPC1 mRNA levels were increased in LUSC tissues, which was corresponding to the results analyzed in cBioPortal. Consistently, with IHC staining, it was found that ANAPC1 protein was higher in 91 in-house LUSC tissues than those 27 control lung tissues. In addition, the Chronos score analyzed with the DepMap Portal indicated that ANAPC1 was essential for the survival of LUSC cell lines. Aimed to figure out the mechanism of expression change of ANAPC1, employed the Cistrome Data Browser we found that transcription factor CDK8 positively co-expressed with ANAPC1 and most likely acted as an upstream regulator of ANAPC1. Emerging as a pivotal oncogenic modulator, CDK8 critically governs tumorigenic progression through dual regulation of transcriptional reprogramming and stress adaptation [ , ]. It seemed that over-expressed ANAPC1 might be regulated with CDK8 and then promoted LUSC tumorigenesis. Furthermore, up-regulated ANAPC1 was observed in male, pathologic_N1, and pathology grade I LUSC tissues relative to female, pathologic_N0, and pathology grade II LUSC tissues respectively. And similar to T-cell malignancies [ ], the OS of LUSC patients with high ANAPC1 expression level were shorter than those with low expression level. Therefore, ANAPC1 contributed to the development and progression of LUSC.
Cancer development may be fueled by aberrant DNA replication and cell cycle progression [ , ]. In this study, it was determined that the HECEGs related to ANAPC1 are most prominently concentrated in the KEGG pathways of “Cell Cycle” and “DNA Replication”. Previous research indicates that ANAPC1 operates as an E3 ubiquitin ligase that is involved in controlling the cell cycle, and it may facilitate cancer progression by influencing mitosis and the G1 phase [ ]. GSEA analysis shows that high expression of ANAPC1 in LUSC can activate multiple cell growth pathways, especially the DNA replication licensing pathway. Activation of this pathway may lead to licensing or initiation of multiple origins, which in turn leads to replication stress and genomic instability [ ], and this instability may lead to malignant transformation of cells [ , ]. The findings indicate that ANAPC1 could potentially play a role in the cancer advancement by influencing signaling pathways related to cell growth. PPI analysis of the top two significantly enriched pathways in GSEA shows that CDT1 is the common key gene in these two networks. Other common hub genes are MCM family genes (MCM2-MCM6) and CDC6. CDT1 and CDC6 are necessary for loading MCM2-7 into the replication origin [ ], and then MCM2-7 is integrated to form the CMG complex (CDC45-MCM-GINS) to activate helicase function [ ]. CDT1 is acknowledged to be intimately associated with tumor development [ ], where its elevated levels are related to lower survival rates and poor outcomes in various malignancies, such as breast carcinoma, lung adenocarcinoma, and bladder carcinoma [ ]. These clues suggest that ANAPC1 might facilitate the progression of LUSC by participating in cancer cell growth processes and ANAPC1 should be a promising LUSC therapeutic target.
Molecular targeted therapies have significantly improved the quality of life and prolonged survival in patients with lung adenocarcinoma and other malignancies. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that combination strategies, such as integrating molecular targeted therapies with immunotherapies, exhibit synergistic effects to maximally suppress tumor growth and progression [ ]. Building on these advances, this study identified three potential ANAPC1-targeted compounds, including tenovin-1, carboxyatractyloside, and phycocyanobilin. Tenovin-1 is a selective SIRT1/2 inhibitor that stabilizes the tumor suppressor protein p53 by inhibiting the activity of the deacetylases SIRT1/2, thereby activating downstream apoptotic pathways [ ]. Furthermore, studies of Tenovin-1 in NSCLC cells have demonstrated its ability to inhibit cell growth and anchorage-independent growth [ ], further supporting its potential application in LUSC. Carboxyatractyloside induces mitochondria-dependent apoptosis by inhibiting the mitochondrial adenine nucleotide translocator, blocking ATP/ADP exchange, and leading to the collapse of the mitochondrial membrane potential [ , ]. Phycocyanobilin is a functional chromophore contained within phycocyanin that is utilized, and phycocyanin induces apoptosis in breast cancer cells by inhibiting MCL-1 [ , ]. TME is vital for cancer progression and treatment effect [ ], with immune cells constituting a significant portion of the TME [ , ]. Furthermore, the impact of ANAPC1 expression level on immune cell infiltration within the tumor microenvironment was investigated to elucidate its immunomodulatory role and therapeutic implications.
This study found that ANAPC1 was significantly negatively correlated with immune cell levels. Elevated ANAPC1 expression was linked to reductions in Stromal Scores, Immune Scores, and ESTIMATE Scores within the TME, along with an augmentation of tumor purity. IPS analysis showed that patients with low expression of ANAPC1 were more likely to be candidates for LUSC immune checkpoint therapies. To summarize, ANAPC1 exhibited a significant correlation with the immune microenvironment, rendering it a potential biomarker for assessing immune therapy efficacy.
This study still has some limitations: (1) Firstly, the current number of clinical pathological sample cohorts is limited, and it is imperative to expand them to include more clinical samples. Simultaneously, additional studies on the relationship between ANAPC1 and LUSC progression and prognosis are needed to explore whether ANAPC1 may serve as a biomarker for evaluating LUSC prognosis. (2) Although potential mechanisms involving ANAPC1 in LUSC signaling pathways and upstream transcription factors have been investigated, further extensive research remains necessary. (3) Further exploration into the molecular mechanism of ANAPC1 in LUSC necessitates a range of both in vitro and in vivo experimental studies. To overcome existing limitations, additional scientific experimentation will be required in the future.

5.

## Conclusion
To sum up, this study affirmed that due to up-stream CDK8 regulation and cell growth signaling pathway, ANAPC1 expression level was remarkable up-regulated in LUSC, which contributed to unfavorable prognosis and immune cells infiltration reduction. ANAPC1-targeted compounds combined with immunotherapy should be promising treatment for LUSC.
