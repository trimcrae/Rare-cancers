In contrast, the Surveillance of Rare Cancers in Europe (RARECARE) project sets a more stringent threshold, which defines rare cancers as those with an incidence rate below 6 cases per 100,000 individuals per year [ ]. Meanwhile, China’s National Central Cancer Registry (NCCRC) classifies rare tumors as those occurring at an annual incidence rate of <2.5 cases per 100,000 individuals [ ]. When viewed collectively, rare cancers account for ∼24% of all cancer cases in Europe and ∼25% in the United States [ , ]. This aggregated incidence surpasses that of any single common malignancy, emphasizing their combined clinical importance. Furthermore, rare cancers represent the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths in the United States annually [ ]. From a broader perspective, rare cancers are not truly “rare” but rather constitute a significant and often underestimated public health burden.
The overall survival (OS) duration for rare cancers is generally shorter than that of common cancers. For example, adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC) has an annual incidence of only 1–2 cases per million individuals [ ]. It demonstrates a five-year OS rate of <35% [ ]. This is substantially lower compared to common cancers at a similar stage, which shows a significant clinical disparity. Additionally, the development of novel anticancer drugs for rare cancers has markedly lagged behind advances made for more prevalent cancers [ ]. ACC’s aggressive behavior and high lethality often result in late-stage diagnosis, severely limiting therapeutic options and significantly reducing survival [ ]. Pharmacotherapy for ACC predominantly relies on conventional chemotherapy and surgical intervention, with limited availability and slower advancement of innovative targeted therapies compared to common cancers, such as breast cancer and lung cancer [ , ]. The key factors contributing to the poor prognosis of rare cancers include diagnostic challenges and delays, limited availability of specialized healthcare facilities, decreased effectiveness of standard treatment regimens, and insufficient funding for preclinical and clinical research [ ].
Addressing these challenges requires innovative approaches, and recent advances in bioinformatics and sequencing technologies provide promising opportunities. In recent years, advancements in high-throughput sequencing technologies have significantly increased the availability of multi-omics data in cancer research, drawing considerable interest within the scientific community [ , ]. These data have been crucial in identifying biomarkers and discovering novel therapeutic targets. With extensive accumulation of multi-omics datasets, researchers have effectively integrated these resources into bioinformatics databases, facilitating accessibility for a broader range of investigators, including laboratory biologists and clinical oncologists who might lack advanced computational expertise. Such databases typically include resources like biomarkers and therapeutic targets, providing user-friendly interfaces and data integration tools. Prominent examples of these databases are GEPIA [ ], UCSC Xena [ ], Expression Atlas [ ], and the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) [ ], etc.
While these databases are highly valuable and widely utilized, their analytical workflows predominantly focus on single-dataset investigations. However, cross-dataset validation remains fundamental for establishing reliable biomarkers, making it particularly challenging to derive high-confidence conclusions in rare tumor studies. Moreover, databases such as TIGER [ ] and BEST [ ] primarily focus on common cancers, with minimal representation of rare cancer types. This gap significantly impedes research efforts targeting rare tumors, as researchers lack sufficient integrated multi-omics information. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop a comprehensive, rare-tumor-specific database to effectively support multifaceted research on rare cancers.
To address this gap, we developed the Rare Cancer Explorer (RaCE) (https://biospace.shinyapps.io/race/ or https://hiplot.com.cn/race/), an integrated multi-omics database specifically dedicated to rare cancers. RaCE provides a comprehensive collection of 13 rare solid tumor types and 5451 individual samples from 69 independent datasets. It features meta-analysis and functional analyses through one integrated meta-analysis module and eight dedicated functional analysis modules, respectively. This database offers researchers a robust, visually intuitive, and user-friendly interface, significantly facilitating both informational and applied research. With RaCE, investigators can efficiently leverage existing rare cancer data resources and systematically explore the mechanisms of rare cancer initiation, progression, and potential therapeutic targets.

## Materials and methods

## Expression and mutation data collection
This database has 69 independent datasets, including 5451 samples across 13 types of rare human cancers ( ). RNA-seq, microarray, immunotherapy cohort data, and genomic (mutational) data were collected from publicly available sources: TCGA, TARGET, ArrayExpress [ ], GEO [ ], and PubMed. Clinical data, including pathological subtypes, gender, sample location, and clinical stage, were collected for each dataset if available. TCGA and TARGET datasets were downloaded using the UCSCXenatools (v1.4.8) [ ] package from UCSC Xena [ ], and other datasets were obtained directly from their respective platforms. Reference publications associated with each dataset are accessible through the source database.

## Expression data preparation and normalization
We referred to our previously established pipelines for bioinformatics data processing to standardize the handling of expression data and corresponding clinical information [ , ]. In general, the 53 expression datasets included in RaCE can be classified into three major categories:
(i) For six transcriptomic datasets obtained from SRA and GEO, raw FASTQ or CEL files were downloaded individually. For SRA files, we utilized the fasterq-dump tool from the SRA Toolkit (v3.1.0) [ ] (https://github.com/ncbi/sra-tools) to convert them into FASTQ format. Quality assessment of the raw sequencing data was then performed using FastQC (v0.12.0) [ ] (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc) with evaluation metrics including per-base quality scores, GC content, adapter contamination, and overrepresented sequences.
Given that modern aligners such as STAR are capable of handling adapter sequences and low-quality bases through soft-clipping, read trimming was not performed in this study. Read alignment was conducted using STAR (v2.7.11b) [ ], mapping reads to the GRCh38 reference genome with gene annotation from GENCODE release 43 [ ]. A two-pass alignment strategy was employed for each sample to enhance splice junction detection. Aligned BAM files were subsequently sorted using SAMtools (v1.20) [ ].
Gene-level quantification was performed using the featureCounts function in the Subread package (v2.0.6) [ ], counting uniquely mapped reads in exonic regions to generate raw count expression matrices. To reduce background noise from lowly expressed genes, we retained only those genes with counts >1 in at least three samples.
Next, gene length information was retrieved using the getGeneLengthAndGCContent function in the EDASeq package (v3.19) [ ], and raw counts were normalized to RPK (Reads Per Kilobase) by dividing each gene’s count by its length in kilobases. TPM (Transcripts Per Million) values were then calculated by dividing each gene’s RPK by the sum of all RPKs in a sample and multiplying by 106, followed by log2
transformation.
(ii) For datasets from TCGA and TARGET, raw sequencing data were processed using a pipeline similar to the one described earlier (see: https://docs.gdc.cancer.gov/Data/Bioinformatics_Pipelines/Expression_mRNA_Pipeline/). Since preprocessed raw counts are available for these datasets, we uniformly downloaded them using the UCSCXenaTools package (v1.4.8) [ ] from UCSC Xena [ ]. These raw counts were then converted to TPM using the same annotation file (GENCODE release 43, GRCh38) and log2-transformed as described earlier. For RNA-seq datasets that were not part of TCGA or TARGET but were only available as processed values (e.g., FPKM or TPM), and for which raw data could not be retrieved, we uniformly re-annotated the data using the GENCODE release 43 (GRCh38) annotation. The original preprocessing methods of these datasets were also documented and made accessible to users under the detailed dataset information section.
(iii) For microarray data, most datasets were directly obtained from GEO, and a simple log2 transformation was applied. Metadata from series matrix files were retrieved using the R package GEOquery (v2.72.0) [ ]. Microarray data were processed using platform-specific annotation files, such as those for Affymetrix®, Illumina®, and Agilent® platforms. Detailed formats and both pre-download and post-download processing procedures for each dataset can be found in the dataset-specific documentation within RaCE.
To ensure consistency in data interpretation and results presentation, clinical characteristics were standardized and harmonized. A comprehensive metadata table was compiled for each sample, including sample ID, patient ID, tissue type, sample type, age at diagnosis, ethnicity, race, cancer stage, OS, and other relevant clinical parameters.

## DEG analysis and PCA
Each rare cancer type has a dataset that includes two groups: primary tumor and normal, for differentially expressed gene (DEG) and principal component analysis (PCA) analysis. DEG analysis is performed using the limma (v3.58.1) [ ] package, where logFC represents fold change, P refers to the P-value obtained from the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted P-values are included. PCA was analyzed using the IOBR (v0.99.9) [ ] package.

## Gene enrichment
The analysis includes five types of enrichment: GO [ ], KEGG [ ], GSEA [ ], Reactome [ ], and Disease [ ]. DEG analysis follows a strict quality control process, allowing users to customize the adj.P-value and log2FC thresholds to generate DEG results and obtain enrichment results under different filtering criteria. Enrichment analysis was conducted using the clusterProfiler (v4.0) [ ] and ReactomePA (v1.36.0) [ ] packages in R. GO, KEGG, and GSEA analyses utilize built-in algorithms in clusterProfiler, while Reactome pathway enrichment was performed via the ReactomePA interface. All enrichment results were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method.

## Survival analysis
Survival analysis is performed using the Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival curves and the Cox proportional hazards regression model, implemented through the tinyarray (v2.4.2) (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=tinyarray), survival (v3.7-0) [ ], and survminer (v0.4.9) [ ] R packages. In KM survival analysis, samples are classified into high-expression and low-expression groups based on either the median expression level or the optimal cutoff value of the target gene, and statistical significance of survival differences between groups is assessed using the log-rank test. Since each cancer type includes multiple datasets, a unified filtering criterion is applied to KM and Cox analysis results for more intuitive data visualization: the 95% confidence interval (CI) of the hazard ratio (HR) must have a lower bound >0.1 and an upper bound <10. Survival analysis for the immunotherapy cohort was conducted using the same method ( ).

## Tumor microenvironment immune infiltration
This module provides six commonly used algorithms to estimate immune cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment (TME), including CIBERSORT [ ], EPIC [ ], MCP-counter [ ], Quantiseq [ ], TIMER [ ], and xCell [ ]. For each cancer type, an optimal dataset was selected as the test set based on data and sample quality (details can be found in the “Data” module in RaCE), and immune infiltration analysis was performed using the IOBR R package. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to evaluate the correlation between different gene pairs in specific cell types. To improve analysis efficiency, all algorithms had completed preprocessing calculations for specific datasets in the background, and the immune cell abundance scores are persistently stored in the database.

## DNA methylation
DNA methylation data were downloaded from the UCSC Xena platform using the UCSC XenaTools package (v1.4.8), comprising Illumina Human Methylation 450K array datasets from nine cancer types originating from TARGET and TCGA. Probe annotation was performed using the HM450.hg38.manifest.gencode.v36.probeMap file provided by UCSC Xena. Prior to analysis, methylation sites containing missing values (NA) were removed. Subsequently, high-throughput survival analyses were conducted based on the β-values of individual methylation sites, following a workflow consistent with the previously described “Survival analysis” in the “Materials and methods” section.
To ensure the reliability and biological relevance of the survival results, stringent filtering criteria were applied to the initial analyses: for Cox proportional hazards model results, entries were retained only if the HR was finite and non-zero, statistically significant (Cox P < 0.05), with the lower CI > 0 and the upper CI < ∞. For KM analysis, entries were preserved only if both median and optimal grouping strategies yielded upper CI < ∞, significant log-rank test results (KM_median_P < 0.05 and KM_optimal_P< 0.05), and biologically plausible HR intervals (upper CI < 10 and lower CI ≥ 0.1 for both grouping methods). Filtered survival analysis results were visualized as forest plots for intuitive interpretation. Additionally, batch Spearman correlation analyses between the β-values of methylation sites and their corresponding gene expression levels were performed, with results presented in bubble plots.

## Rare cancer cell line dependency score
The CRISPR gene dependency data (Chronos Scores) from DepMap Public 24Q4 were downloaded from the DepMap: The Cancer Dependency Map Project at the Broad Institute (https://depmap.org/portal/). Corresponding cell lines and score data for rare cancer types in this database were filtered ( ) to evaluate the essentiality of target genes in specific cell lines. Lower scores indicate stronger gene dependency in the cell line, while higher scores indicate weaker dependency. The module also provides lists of the top 10 cell lines with the highest (least dependent) and lowest (most dependent) dependency scores.

## Drug response prediction
Training set data were obtained from the Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal_v2 (CTRP2) [ ], Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer_v2 (GDSC2) [ ], and Profiling Relative Inhibition Simultaneously in Mixtures (PRISM) [ ] databases. For each cancer type, an optimal dataset was selected as the test set based on data and sample quality. Ridge regression analysis was performed using the Oncopredict R package based on cancer cell drug response and expression data. Spearman correlation analysis was used to correlate the expression levels of target genes with predicted drug sensitivity scores for each cancer type. Correlation values <−0.4 were selected, and the top 10 drugs were ranked from low to high based on correlation coefficients. GDSC2 and CTRP2 provide IC50 values, while PRISM provides AUC values. Lower IC50 and AUC values indicate stronger targeting effects of the drug on the target gene.

## Immunohistochemistry
Tissue samples from four cases each of neuroblastoma (NB) and adrenocortical adenoma used in this study were obtained from Huizhou Central People’s Hospital and approved by the ethics committee of Huizhou Central People’s Hospital (No. KYLL2023105). Prior to paraffin embedding, the samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Each tissue block was sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm, treated with 1% H2O2 to block endogenous peroxidase activity, and then incubated with normal goat serum to block nonspecific binding. The tissue sections were incubated with the primary antibody at 24°C for 2 h, followed by incubation with a biotinylated secondary antibody at room temperature for 30 min to facilitate binding. The immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining protocol was consistent with our previous study [ ]. The immunoreactive score method was used to evaluate staining, calculated by multiplying the percentage score of positively stained cells by the staining intensity score. Scoring for the percentage of positive cells was defined as follows: 1 (<10%), 2 (11%–50%), 3 (51%–80%), and 4 (>80%). Staining intensity was visually assessed and scored as: 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), and 3 (strong) [ ]. The antibody used for IHC staining was anti-SHISA3 (Immunoway, YT6626).

## Database implementations
RaCE is mainly built on Shiny using R as the backend language and HTML/CSS/Javascript as the frontend languages [ ]. The application is open-accessible for all users. A modular design was applied throughout the application to ensure the efficiency and independence of different components. This modular design makes it highly maintainable and extendable for future updates.

## Results

## Overall design
RaCE is a comprehensive analytical database specifically developed for the systematic analysis of multi-omics data in rare cancers. RaCE aims to provide researchers with an integrated one-stop solution from initial data mining to mechanistic exploration (Fig. ). The database aggregates 69 high-quality datasets, comprising 5451 samples across 13 rare cancer types. RaCE includes one meta-analysis (data) and eight key functional modules: Gene Expression Analysis (Expression), DEG Analysis, KM and Cox Survival Analysis (Survival), Rare Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (RCCLE), Cancer Mutation Signatures (Mutation), Tumor Microenvironment Immune Infiltration (TME), Cancer Immunotherapy Survival Analysis (Immunotherapy), and Drug Response Prediction (Drug Predict). Each module supports multidimensional data analysis and visualization, allowing users to customize graphical elements, including point size, color schemes, and font size. Users can conveniently download datasets and high-resolution visual outputs. Inside all the modules, detailed step-by-step tutorials are offered, enabling users to perform complex analyses efficiently.
 

## Dataset meta-analysis and clinical association (data and expression)
In the “Data” module, users can access comprehensive information on all datasets, including the availability of prognostic data and the specific modules involved in the analysis (Fig. ). Additionally, users can review KM OS curves and survival time distributions for the corresponding datasets, as well as the age at diagnosis and its distribution. Detailed patient information encompasses general characteristics (e.g., age and sex), histological features (e.g., histologic type and pathologic stage), and prognostic timelines and endpoints [e.g., OS and progression-free interval (PFI)] (Fig. ).
 Within the “Expression” module, users first select a target cancer type and then input a single gene or multiple genes. The system automatically retrieves expression values from all available datasets for the specified cancer type. Results are presented as dynamic boxplots, with the horizontal axis indicating sample information and the vertical axis representing normalized expression levels. Each boxplot includes a median line, with overlaid scatter points illustrating the distribution of individual samples (Fig. ). The multi-gene visualization function enables a comparative analysis of expression levels across multiple genes (Fig. ).

## DEG and enrichment analysis
The DEG analysis module offers users a robust suite of tools for analyzing gene expression changes, enabling the selection of representative datasets from a specific cancer type for differential expression analysis. Through an interactive interface, users can designate a target cancer type and dynamically filter DEGs based on log2 fold change (log2FC) and adjusted P-values (adj. P-value). The module includes a built-in volcano plot feature, allowing users to customize thresholds to refine results and enhance visualization (Fig. ). Additionally, it provides PCA-based sample clustering plots, offering a clear visual representation of DEG expression pattern differences across sample groups (Fig. ). Furthermore, the module integrates five enrichment analysis methods—GO, KEGG, GSEA, Reactome, and Disease—enabling users to select specific approaches for functional annotation. Results are displayed in interactive tables and various visualization formats, with options to sort by metrics such as P-value or normalized enrichment score. Users can also adjust font size, color schemes, and paragraph length to meet research and publication requirements (Fig. ). Designed with user experience in mind, this module features real-time responsive chart updates, ensuring an efficient workflow from data filtering to functional annotation.
 

## Survival analysis
The survival analysis module integrates the KM method and Cox proportional hazards regression model, enabling users to systematically evaluate the prognostic significance of target genes in rare cancers. Through an interactive interface, users can select a specific cancer type and target gene, as well as customize survival endpoints (e.g., OS, disease-specific survival, and PFI). The module automatically retrieves clinical follow-up data and gene expression profiles from the corresponding datasets, calculating HR and their 95% CI using the log-rank test (KM) or Wald test (Cox). Results are dynamically displayed in forest plots and tabular formats, with options for users to adjust the adjusted P-value (adj. P-value) threshold to refine visualization outcomes. For KM survival analysis, users can freely select either the median or the optimal value as the grouping threshold. The module incorporates a built-in data quality control process to filter out extreme HR values, ensuring the statistical robustness of the results. This functionality provides an efficient tool for formulating targeted therapeutic strategies and identifying prognostic biomarkers in rare cancers (Fig. ).

## Rare Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia
The Rare Cancer Cell Line Dependency Scoring module integrates data from the Broad Institute’s Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE), focusing on the analysis of gene functional effects in rare cancer types and their corresponding cell lines. Leveraging the Chronos dependency score derived from CRISPR gene knockout assays, this module evaluates the essentiality of target genes for cell survival in specific cell lines. A lower score indicates stronger dependency on the gene within a given cell line, while a higher score suggests weaker dependency. This allows users to assess the impact of gene knockdown or overexpression experiments on cell line proliferation based on the dependency scores of target genes. Through an interactive interface, users can select a target gene, and the module automatically generates a cross-cancer dependency score distribution plot. This is presented as a boxplot, illustrating score variations across different rare cancer types, with the y-axis annotated to indicate the number of cell lines per cancer type (Fig. ). Additionally, the module provides a ranked list of the top 10 cell lines with the highest (least dependent) and lowest (most dependent) dependency scores (Fig. ). Designed to offer reliable data support for target identification and functional studies in rare cancers, this module facilitates advancements in precision medicine research.
 

## DNA-level multi-omics (mutation and methylation)
This module employs maftools (v2.16) [ ] for standardized preprocessing of somatic mutation data from rare cancers, systematically elucidating the relationships between mutation patterns and gene expression. Upon inputting a target gene, the module automatically generates a waterfall plot illustrating genomic alterations, highlighting the top 15 most frequently mutated genes. The right panel of the heatmap further displays the proportion of genomic variations and statistical differences between groups with high and low expression of the target gene. Additionally, users can access a violin plot depicting the distribution of total mutation burden (TMB) in the corresponding rare cancer, stratified by the median expression level of the target gene, as well as an expression distribution plot comparing wild type and mutant groups for the top 15 high-frequency mutated genes (Fig. ). Integrated DNA methylation analysis enables Cox and KM survival analyses based on methylation site β-values, along with Spearman correlation analysis between methylation levels and corresponding gene expression (Fig. – ).
 

## Tumor microenvironment immune infiltration
This module incorporates six widely used algorithms to estimate immune cell infiltration within the TME. All algorithms have been preprocessed and computed in the background for specific datasets, with immune cell abundance scores persistently stored in the database. Upon selecting a target cancer type and gene, users can instantly generate a global correlation heatmap and a structured association table depicting the relationship between gene expression levels and immune cell scores. This provides an efficient analytical interface for exploring immunotherapy targets and investigating microenvironment heterogeneity in rare cancers (Fig. ).
 

## Immunotherapy survival analysis
To investigate the clinical relevance of input genes in the context of immunotherapy for rare cancers, we curated six immunotherapy cohorts with available expression data and treatment information (e.g. anti-PD-L1, anti-PD-1, and anti-CTLA4). Leveraging preprocessed standardized expression profiles and clinical follow-up data (OS and progression-free survival), this module enables users to dynamically assess survival differences between high- and low-expression groups of a target gene following immunotherapy. This is facilitated through KM survival curves and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models. Results are presented in forest plots and tabular formats. The module offers data-driven decision support for the discovery of immunotherapy biomarkers and the optimization of personalized treatment strategies in rare cancers (Fig. ).

## Drug response prediction (drug predict)
In this module, RaCE conducts drug response assessments on large-scale samples by integrating drug response and expression data from tumor cell lines sourced from the GDSC_v2, CTRP_v2, and PRISM databases. The predictive model for drug response employs a ridge regression algorithm implemented in the oncopredict package [ ]. This model was trained on transcriptional expression profiles and evaluated using default 10-fold cross-validation against drug response data from cancer cell lines, yielding satisfactory predictive accuracy. Consequently, clinical drug responses can be estimated solely using expression data from large sample cohorts. The modeling and prediction processes have been completed, with drug response evaluations for all tumor samples across the three databases precomputed and stored on the database’s backend. RaCE calculates correlations between all drugs and input variables across all cohorts. Users can select a target gene and a rare cancer cohort to generate a lollipop plot, which illustrates the top 10 drugs ranked by correlation coefficient (from low to high) with the input gene in the selected cohort (Fig. ), alongside differences in drug response scores between median expression groups of the gene (Fig. ). Drugs with higher rankings suggest that elevated expression of the target gene may predict resistance, whereas lower IC50 and AUC values indicate a stronger targeting effect of the drug on the gene of interest.
 

## Interactivity and customization
RaCE not only offers an extensive array of sophisticated analytical modules for in-depth research but also provides robust user interactivity and a high degree of customization. For instance, in the Survival module, to explore the survival information of a target gene, users can choose the gene with different combinations of survival regression methods, sampling grouping strategy from the dropdown menus step by step. Next, a forest plot with statistical metrics is generated in real time. This streamlined workflow exemplifies the database’s efficient and user-friendly interactive design (Fig. ). Similarly, in the Expression module, users are empowered with extensive customization options, including the ability to freely adjust font, point size, expression scale, and color schemes, etc. Moreover, the size of all plots in RaCE is adjustable. If one feels the plot size is not big enough, full-screen mode can be used by hovering over the plot and clicking on the full-screen button in the bottom right corner of the plot (Fig. ).
 

## Case study: SHISA3 as a new neuroblastoma biomarker
We first used the “DEG” module of the RaCE platform to perform differential expression analysis on the NB dataset and identified genes that were significantly upregulated (log2FC > 2, adj.P < 0.05) ( ). Among these upregulated genes, we found that SHISA3 (Shisa Family Member 3) was significantly elevated. SHISA3 belongs to the Shisa protein family, mainly involved in the regulation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and cell cycle control [ , ], and has been reported to play important roles in various cancers such as lung and breast cancer [ ]. However, no studies have yet revealed the specific function of SHISA3 in NB.
To systematically investigate the role of SHISA3 in NB, we performed a comprehensive analysis using multiple modules of the RaCE platform. First, in the “Survival” module, we used the prognostic endpoint information from all NB datasets to conduct KM survival analysis and Cox regression for SHISA3 expression. The results showed that, except for some endpoints in the TARGET-RNA-seq dataset, which were not statistically significant, the HR values in all other datasets were >1 and statistically significant (Fig. A and B), suggesting that SHISA3 may have good predictive power for patient prognosis in NB. To determine the expression of SHISA3 in NB, we collected clinical samples of NB and benign tumors (adrenocortical adenomas) as controls and performed IHC. Figure  shows representative IHC images of SHISA3, and the results showed that SHISA3 protein expression was higher in the NB group compared to the control group (P < .05). Next, we used the “RCCLE” module to identify the top 10 cell lines with the lowest dependency score for SHISA3. The results showed that the SHSY5Y and TGW NB cell lines were among them, with SHSY5Y having the lowest score, indicating that SHISA3 may be a key gene in these two cell lines. We then used the “Mutation” module and found that in the NB dataset, there was no statistically significant difference in mutation frequency between high and low SHISA3 expression groups ( ). In the “TME” module, results from different algorithms consistently indicated that SHISA3 expression was significantly positively correlated with the infiltration level of fibroblasts. Finally, in the “Drug Predict” module, we used the NB dataset in combination with the GDSC_v2 drug database to predict potential targeted drugs. The analysis showed that PLX4720, AZD5438, and I-BET762 had significant differences in drug response between high and low SHISA3 expression groups, suggesting that these may be potential therapeutic candidates related to SHISA3.
 

## Discussion
As noted in the Introduction, the definition of rare cancers is primarily based on incidence thresholds—such as <15 per 100,000 according to the NCI [ ], <6 per 100,000 by RARECARE [ ], and <2.5 per 100,000 in China per NCCRC [ ]. The defining feature of rare cancers lies in their low incidence and limited research resources. Collectively, rare cancers encompass ∼200 distinct types [ ]. Although each type individually contributes only a small fraction of the cancer burden, together they represent a significant global health concern.
However, in practice, the collection of rare cancer data faces the challenge of “extreme scarcity.” On one hand, many cancer types are so infrequent that they often present as sporadic cases in clinical settings, making it difficult for individual institutions or research projects to accumulate enough samples for sequencing analyses. On the other hand, even when studies are published, they frequently suffer from limited sample sizes (e.g., only 1–2 cases), incomplete clinical information (e.g., lacking follow-up data or unclassified pathological subtypes), or restricted data accessibility (e.g., due to privacy concerns), thereby precluding their inclusion in systematic databases. For instance, penile cancer accounts for <1% of male cancers in the U.S. [ ]; a PubMed search using the keywords “Carcinoma of penis” and “RNA-seq” yielded only one relevant publication, and the dataset was not publicly available. Such limitations directly hinder the inclusion of many rare cancer types in public resources.
Given these practical challenges, the selection of 13 cancer types in RaCE 1.0 was based on a principle of balancing rarity with data accessibility. Specifically, while adhering to incidence-based definitions of rare cancers, we prioritized those types with multiple publicly available datasets (a total of 69 datasets, including 53 expression profiles), relatively sufficient sample sizes (n = 5451), and well-annotated clinical information. These 13 cancer types, therefore, represent data-accessible and clinically relevant examples of rare cancers, providing a practical and systematic analytical resource for researchers under current data availability constraints.
RaCE directly addresses critical challenges in rare cancer research, such as data fragmentation and the shortage of specialized analytical tools. Combining numerous datasets and samples with extensive multi-omics data and state-of-the-art bioinformatics methodologies, RaCE streamlines target identification, prognostic evaluation, and precision therapy development for rare cancers. Apart from large public database platforms such as Expression Atlas and the HPA, RaCE includes a greater number of rare solid tumor types and rare cancer datasets compared to other databases like GEPIA3, TISCH2, and scCancerExplorer. Moreover, RaCE provides a broader and more customized suite of analytical modules specifically tailored for rare cancer research (Fig. ). While these existing platforms incorporate certain rare cancer types, their core designs primarily focus on pan-cancer analyses or specific technologies, such as single-cell analysis and immune infiltration profiling, and are not optimized to address the unique challenges posed by rare cancers, such as small sample sizes and dispersed datasets. For example, GEPIA3’s analytical tools are mainly based on TCGA data and lack integration of external datasets; TISCH2 and scCancerExplorer concentrate on TME analysis at the single-cell level but do not offer integrated analytical functions for rare cancer bulk transcriptomic data. In contrast, RaCE’s module design is fully aligned with the specific requirements of rare cancer research.
 Notably, the “RCCLE” module enables users to functionally validate the roles of candidate genes within selected cell lines, providing critical guidance for downstream experimental validation—a feature lacking in platforms such as GEPIA3. Through this module, RaCE can effectively assist users in confidently inferring whether a gene acts as an oncogene or tumor suppressor. Therefore, RaCE is not intended to replace existing databases but rather to complement them by focusing on resource integration and tool optimization specific to rare cancers, collectively offering more comprehensive support for cancer research.
To further demonstrate the functionality and application value of the RaCE platform in rare tumor research, we used NB as an example to show users how to systematically explore a potential biomarker by integrating multiple functional modules within RaCE. Interestingly, SHISA3 has been previously studied in other cancer types and is generally considered a tumor suppressor gene, with its promoter region frequently hypermethylated, leading to gene silencing and thereby promoting tumor initiation and progression [ ]. However, in this study, through analysis using the “DEG” module combined with IHC experimental validation, SHISA3 was found to be significantly overexpressed in NB and showed a strong association with poor prognosis in survival analyses, suggesting it may play a different biological role in NB. In the “RCCLE” module, SHISA3 exhibited strong dependency across several cell lines, particularly in SHSY5Y and TGW, two NB cell lines. Notably, SHSY5Y had the lowest dependency score, suggesting that SHISA3 may be a critical gene necessary for NB cell survival. However, in the “TME” module, SHISA3 expression was significantly positively correlated with fibroblast infiltration, implying a possible role in NB progression through TME remodeling. Moreover, based on the “Drug Predict” module, we found that the IC50 values for three potential targeted drugs—PLX4720, AZD5438, and I-BET762—were significantly reduced in the SHISA3 high-expression group. These drugs target the RAF signaling pathway [ ], CDK pathway [ ], and epigenetic regulation [ ], respectively, further suggesting that SHISA3-related molecular networks may offer new directions for future therapeutic interventions. In summary, this case not only reveals the possible oncogenic function of SHISA3 in NB, but also comprehensively demonstrates the powerful capabilities and practical value of various functional modules in RaCE for mechanistic exploration in rare tumors.
As the first comprehensive analytical database dedicated exclusively to rare cancers, RaCE significantly lowers research barriers through its modular design, precomputed analytical workflows, and interactive visualizations, thereby empowering clinicians and researchers with robust data-driven insights.
