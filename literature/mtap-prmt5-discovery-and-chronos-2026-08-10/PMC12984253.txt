## Simple Summary
Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cells with high levels of the epigenetic regulator DNMT3B show poor patient outcomes and appear to rely on DNMT3B-controlled DNA regulatory elements to maintain growth and survival. We found that DNMT3B overexpression is driven by changes in gene control regions rather than mutations, and that inhibiting DNMT3B selectively remodels DNA methylation and chromatin at key cell-cycle and survival genes without globally erasing methylation. This disruption induces replication stress and programmed cell death and, importantly, rewires the balance of pro- and anti-apoptotic signals to make leukemia cells markedly more sensitive to the BCL-2 inhibitor venetoclax. These findings identify DNMT3B as a central epigenetic stabilizer of leukemic fitness and support combined DNMT3B and BCL-2 targeting as a rational therapeutic strategy for high-risk AML.
Keywords: acute myeloid leukemia, apoptosis, chromatin accessibility, DNA methylation, DNMT3B, enhancer remodeling

## Abstract
Background: DNMT3B is frequently overexpressed in molecular subsets of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and is associated with poor prognosis. Unlike DNMT3A, DNMT3B is rarely mutated, suggesting dysregulation through epigenetic mechanisms. The regulatory basis and downstream consequences of DNMT3B overexpression in AML remain incompletely defined. Methods: We integrated analyses of BeatAML, TCGA, and BLUEPRINT cohorts with multi-omic profiling (RNA-seq, DNA methylation, ATAC-seq, and proteomics) in DNMT3B-high AML models. Nanaomycin A (NanA) was used as a DNMT3B-directed functional probe to interrogate cis-regulatory remodeling, transcriptional circuitry, and apoptotic dependencies. Results: DNMT3B overexpression was linked to enhancer-associated chromatin activation rather than recurrent genetic mutation, particularly in CEBPA- and NPM1-mutant AML. NanA exposure produced focal epigenomic remodeling, including 6900 differentially methylated CpGs, with 268 CpGs located within regions of altered chromatin accessibility. These changes were accompanied by coordinated transcriptomic and proteomic reprogramming enriched for cell-cycle, checkpoint, and stress-response pathways. Functionally, DNMT3B perturbation induced redistribution of cell-cycle phases with increased S-phase fraction and progressive apoptosis. Transcriptional profiling demonstrated induction of BH3-only sensitizers (NOXA, PUMA), repression of BCL2, and compensatory upregulation of MCL1 and BCL-XL, collectively reshaping apoptotic dependency. Combined DNMT3B perturbation and BCL2 inhibition produced cooperative cytotoxicity in DNMT3B-high AML models. Conclusion: DNMT3B functions as a context-dependent epigenetic regulator linking enhancer-associated chromatin organization with proliferative control and apoptotic resistance in AML. DNMT3B-directed epigenetic perturbation remodels cis-regulatory circuitry and is associated with increased venetoclax responsiveness, supporting DNMT3B-governed networks as a candidate co-targeting axis in high-risk AML.

## 1. Introduction
DNA methylation (DNAm) is a fundamental epigenetic modification that regulates gene expression, chromatin architecture, and lineage fidelity. Aberrant DNAm patterns are a hallmark of malignancy, particularly in hematologic cancers such as acute myeloid leukemia (AML), where they reinforce stemness, impair differentiation, and contribute to therapeutic resistance [ , ]. These patterns are established and maintained by the DNA methyltransferases DNMT1, DNMT3A, and DNMT3B [ , ]. DNMT1 functions primarily as a maintenance methyltransferase with high affinity for hemimethylated DNA during replication, whereas DNMT3A and DNMT3B are classically defined as de novo enzymes. However, DNMT3A/3B can contribute to maintenance methylation at selected loci, and DNMT1 can exhibit limited de novo activity, indicating coordinated rather than strictly segregated functions [ , , ].
These enzymes differ markedly in their involvement in AML pathogenesis. DNMT3A is frequently mutated in AML, most commonly at the R882 hotspot, where dominant-negative loss of function produces focal hypomethylation and contributes to clonal hematopoiesis. In contrast, DNMT3B is rarely mutated but often overexpressed, particularly in high-risk molecular subtypes, and elevated DNMT3B expression correlates with adverse clinical outcomes. Its functional role appears context-dependent. In KMT2A-rearranged leukemia models, Dnmt3b can act as a tumor suppressor by maintaining gene-body methylation and repressing stemness-associated loci [ ]. Conversely, in cytogenetically normal AML, increased DNMT3B expression predicts inferior outcomes independent of global DNAm changes, suggesting regulatory functions beyond canonical methyltransferase activity [ ]. DNMT3B is also elevated in FLT3-ITD–positive AML, a transcriptionally plastic and relapse-prone subtype, raising the possibility that DNMT3B contributes to stabilization of oncogenic regulatory networks [ , ].
Emerging evidence indicates functional interplay between DNMT family members. Catalytically inactive DNMT3B isoforms can act as accessory factors that recruit or stimulate DNMT3A activity at specific genomic loci, while structural studies of DNMT3A R882 mutants show disrupted DNMT3-family oligomerization and altered methylation targeting. DNMT enzymes also exert non-catalytic effects: DNMT1 can repress transcription through interactions with histone-modifying complexes, and DNMT3B participates in chromatin regulation through protein–protein interactions and inactive splice isoforms. DNMT3A and DNMT3B exhibit distinct chromatin-targeting preferences, with DNMT3A associated with H3K36me2/3-marked gene bodies, and DNMT3B has been reported to associate with linker DNA, repetitive elements, and repressive chromatin domains in several contexts. Consequently, shifts in their relative abundance redistribute methylation activity across regulatory regions. In DNMT3A-mutant AML, loss of DNMT3A function may not be fully compensated by DNMT3B, whereas DNMT3B overexpression may reinforce methylation at normally repressed elements and stabilize aberrant epigenetic programs [ ]. These context-dependent observations—DNMT3B acting as a tumor suppressor in certain leukemias yet marking aggressive disease when overexpressed—indicate that DNMT3B functions within a broader DNMT regulatory network shaped by mutational background and chromatin context. Enhancer-associated DNMT3B overexpression in specific AML subtypes, together with its distinct genomic targeting and accessory functions, supports a model in which DNMT3B stabilizes leukemic regulatory circuitry.
Here, we integrate DNA methylation, chromatin accessibility, transcriptomic, and proteomic profiling to define the cis-regulatory features underlying DNMT3B overexpression in AML. We show that DNMT3B activation is associated with enhancer-like chromatin remodeling rather than recurrent mutation. We further examine how pharmacologic DNMT3B-directed perturbation influences chromatin organization, cell-cycle control, and apoptotic signaling. These findings provide a mechanistic context for DNMT3B-dependent epigenetic circuitry and support its consideration as a rational co-target in molecularly defined AML subsets and potentially other DNMT3B-dysregulated malignancies [ ].

## 2. Materials and Methods

## 2.1. Genomic and Epigenetic Analysis of AML Patient Data and Cell Lines
DNMT3B expression (n = 395) and survival associations (n = 649) were quantified in BeatAML 2.0 ( ) [ ]. To provide additional context for expression differences between normal hematopoietic cells and AML subtypes, an independent microarray-based AML dataset from the MILE study (accessed via BloodSpot) was analyzed for comparative DNMT3B expression across normal bone marrow and molecular AML subgroups. DNMT family mutation profiles were assessed in TCGA (n = 143), and BLUEPRINT DHS/histone-mark profiles from 37 AML samples identified four promoter and intragenic DHS modules (DHS-I–IV) at the DNMT3B locus enriched for promoter/enhancer marks ( ) [ ]. (Full processing, Hotspot v3/MACS2 parameters, and QC details are provided in .)

## 2.2. Functional Dependency of DNMT3B in AML
DNMT3B mRNA expression from CCLE was correlated with DEMETER2 RNAi dependency scores to assess gene essentiality in AML [ ]. Protein abundance was evaluated by immunoblotting in AML cell lines and primary HSCs, and functional dependency was further tested by treating cells with the DNMT3B inhibitor Nanaomycin A (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) and assessed for concentration-dependent effects on viability. Detailed protocols, antibody information, and assay conditions are provided in the .

## 2.3. Transcriptomic and Pathway Enrichment Analysis
KG1A cells were treated with NanA at 50 nM for 48 h (selected as a sub-IC50 concentration based on viability curves), followed by RNA-seq on three independent biological replicates (details in ); interpretation emphasizes pathway-level responses and acknowledges that later time points may include secondary transcriptional effects related to stress and apoptosis. Differential expression was assessed using DESeq2 (v1.40.2) with |log2FC| ≥ 1.5 and FDR < 0.05, defining differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Pathway enrichment was performed using GSEA (v4.3.3) with MSigDB Hallmark gene sets and 1000 permutations ( ). Gene sets were considered enriched at nominal p-value < 0.05 with FDR q-value < 0.25, consistent with GSEA recommendations.

## 2.4. Integration of DNA Methylation and Gene Expression Profiles
Genome-wide DNAm profiling was performed on NanA-treated KG1A cells using the Illumina Infinium MethylationEPIC v2.0 BeadChip (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), with three independent biological replicates per condition (processing and annotation in ). CpGs were tested with limma (BH-FDR); for downstream integration, we classified CpGs with |Δβ| ≥ 0.10 relative to controls as hyper- (increase) or hypo- (decrease) methylated, and genes with average Δβ ≥ ±10% across promoter or gene body regions were defined as differentially methylated genes (DMGs). DMGs were integrated with differentially expressed genes (|log2FC| ≥ 1, FDR < 0.05), generating four categories: hyper-up, hyper-down, hypo-up, and hypo-down. Circos plots were produced using Circa (v1.0, OMGenomics, San Francisco, CA, USA).

## 2.5. Proteomic Profiling and Integration with Epigenomic Data
Mass spectrometry–based proteomics was performed on five biological replicates per condition using Orbitrap DIA (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) acquisition, with proteins identified by EncyclopeDIA and quantified via Scaffold DIA at 1% FDR. Normalization and differential abundance were analyzed using ProteiNorm and limma, with significant proteins defined as FDR < 0.05 and fold change > 2; pathway enrichment was subsequently performed. To assess epigenetic regulation, differentially methylated genes (DMGs) were integrated with RNA-seq and proteomic data, and genes showing concordant transcript and protein changes were classified as coordinately regulated. Detailed acquisition parameters, preprocessing workflows, and statistical criteria are provided in the .

## 2.6. ATAC-Seq and Integration with DNA Methylation Profiling
ATAC-seq was performed on KG1A cells treated with NanA or vehicle (n = 3 per group) to map chromatin accessibility, as previously described [ ]. Because NanA induces time-dependent apoptosis, accessibility changes are interpreted as reflecting both primary regulatory remodeling and secondary stress-related chromatin responses at the 48 h time point. Differentially accessible regions were identified using DiffBind (v3.8.0; FDR < 0.001) and intersected with differentially methylated CpGs from Illumina MethylationEPIC arrays ( ). TF motif enrichment was performed using Pscan-ChIP with the JASPAR 2018 NR database [ ] both globally (regions gaining accessibility after DNMT3B inhibition) and locally at DHS-I, the promoter-proximal DHS at the DNMT3B locus. For DHS-I, hg38 coordinates were analyzed with a promoter background against the TRANSFAC database to identify candidate regulators of DNMT3B expression.

## 2.7. Apoptosis and Cell Cycle Analysis
KG1A cells (1 × 106 per condition) were treated with NanA (25 or 50 nM) or DMSO in three biological replicates. Apoptosis was quantified using the FITC Annexin V/PI Dead Cell Apoptosis Kit (including DAPI counterstain where indicated; V13242, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) on a BD LSRFortessa cytometer (BD Biosciences, Milpitas, CA, USA), distinguishing early and late apoptotic populations. For cell-cycle analysis, DNA content was quantified by flow cytometry using Vybrant DyeCycle Violet (Thermo Fisher Scientific, V35003, Waltham, MA, USA) to determine G0/G1, S, and G2/M phase distribution.

## 2.8. Combination Treatment, Cell Viability, and qPCR Analysis of Apoptotic Regulators
M07e and KG1A cells were seeded in 96-well plates (2 × 104 and 1 × 104 cells/well, respectively) in RPMI-1640 with 10% FBS and treated with Nanaomycin-A (NanA) and Venetoclax (VEN; HY-15531, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA), either alone or in combination, across an 11-point range (10 nM–25 µM). For combination therapy, VEN and NanA were co-administered at a 1:1 molar ratio across matched concentrations. After 48 h, cell viability was measured using the CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Assay (Promega, G9242, Madison, WI, USA) on an Agilent BioTek Gen5 Reader (Santa Clara, CA, USA), with values normalized to DMSO controls. Each condition was tested in triplicate across three biological replicates. In parallel, KG1A cells were treated for 48 h with DMSO, NanA, VEN, or NanA + VEN (each at one-fold below IC50 to ensure sub-lethal dosing). Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen; 74104, Germantown, MD, USA), reverse-transcribed (500 ng) with iScript (Bio-Rad; 1725037, Hercules, CA, USA), and subjected to qPCR with SYBR Green Master Mix (Bio-Rad; 1725270) on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection (Bio-Rad; 1855195). Expression of apoptotic regulators (BCL2, MCL1, BCL2L1, PMAIP1/NOXA, BCL2L11/BIM, BBC3/PUMA, BAX, BAK1) was quantified by the ΔΔCt method, normalized to ATP5B, using technical triplicates from three biological replicates. Primer sequences are provided in .

## 2.9. Statistical Analysis
For targeted functional assays, statistical comparisons between groups were performed using Mann–Whitney U tests or one-way ANOVA as appropriate, with significance defined as p < 0.05 unless otherwise specified. Omics-scale analyses incorporated multiple-testing correction within each dataset using established analytical frameworks: DESeq2 for RNA-seq, limma for DNA methylation and proteomics, and DiffBind for ATAC-seq, with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment applied per analysis. Specific FDR thresholds and effect-size criteria used for each dataset are described in the relevant Methods subsections. All results reflect at least three biological replicates per condition. Data visualization and statistical analyses were performed using OriginPro (v2025; Northampton, MA, USA), GraphPad Prism (v10.5.0, Boston, MA, USA), and schematic elements in selected figures were created using BioRender (BioRender, Toronto, ON, Canada).

## 3. Results

## 3.1. DNMT3B Overexpression in AML Is Linked to Poor Prognosis and Epigenetically Reprogrammed Regulatory Accessibility
To assess the clinical relevance of DNMT3B, we analyzed BEAT-AML transcriptomic and outcome data. Patients with elevated DNMT3B expression had significantly reduced overall survival (log-rank p < 0.05; A). Expression was highest in AML M1 (without maturation), followed by CEBPA- and NPM1-mutant AML, but it remained low in APL (PML-RARA), t(8;21), and inv(16) ( B). Independent analysis of the microarray-based MILE AML cohort showed a similar overall pattern, with higher DNMT3B expression in several AML subtypes relative to normal bone marrow, supporting subtype-specific variability consistent with the BEAT-AML observations ( ).
 Intra-subtype Kaplan–Meier analyses in CEBPA- and NPM1-mutant AML showed that above-median DNMT3B expression trended toward poorer survival; statistical power was limited for the CEBPA subgroup (low-expression n = 2), whereas the NPM1 subgroup did not show a significant association in this dataset. These findings suggest that elevated DNMT3B expression may mark more aggressive disease behavior within genetically defined AML subsets ( ).
Somatic mutation profiling of DNMT genes in TCGA confirmed frequent DNMT3A mutations but rare alterations in DNMT1 or DNMT3B ( C), indicating that DNMT3B dysregulation arises through non-genetic mechanisms. To explore chromatin involvement, we analyzed DNase I hypersensitivity data from 37 AML samples in BLUEPRINT, identifying four discrete DHS (DHS-I–IV) across DNMT3B regulatory regions ( D). Median Hotspot v3-enrichment scores were highest at DHS-I (8.17, max 14.8), DHS-II (7.38), and DHS-III (7.40) compared with DHS-IV (6.18; ). Kruskal–Wallis testing confirmed significant inter-DHS differences (p < 0.05), with Dunn’s post-hoc test identifying DHS-I as significantly more enriched than DHS-IV (adjusted p = 0.0163) ( E). Histone profiling at DHS-I revealed an active chromatin state characterized by strong H3K4me3 (median 19.8) and H3K27ac (4.79), moderate H3K4me1 (3.83), and low repressive marks H3K27me3 (3.07) and H3K36me3 (2.82) ( F,G). Because histone-mark datasets for normal myeloid progenitors are limited, these results define AML-specific enrichment rather than disease-exclusive activation. The concurrent presence of H3K4me3 and H3K27ac within a CpG-island promoter indicates a promoter-proximal enhancer-like configuration rather than a canonical distal enhancer. Kruskal–Wallis testing (p < 0.0001), followed by Dunn’s comparisons, showed H3K4me3 enrichment exceeded all other marks (adjusted p < 0.01), and H3K27ac was higher than H3K4me1 and H3K27me3 (adjusted p < 0.01). Motif-enrichment analysis of DHS-I using Pscan-ChIP identified SRF as the top-enriched factor (p = 0.0103), suggesting a role in DNMT3B promoter activation ( ). Together, these data indicate that DNMT3B overexpression in AML is associated with promoter-proximal chromatin accessibility and enhancer-like regulatory features rather than recurrent somatic mutation, although direct comparisons with matched normal hematopoietic chromatin datasets remain limited.

## 3.2. DNMT3B Inhibition Induces Mitotic Disruption and Stress Response Pathways in High-Expressing AML Cells
We first examined the relationship between DNMT3B transcript abundance and functional dependency using the DEMETER2 RNAi dataset. DNMT3B transcript abundance was not significantly correlated with RNAi gene-effect scores (Pearson r = 0.294, p = 0.18; A), indicating that DNMT3B expression alone does not predict functional dependency. Several DNMT3B-overexpressing AML lines used in this study (TF-1, KG1A, M07e) were absent from DEMETER2. To address this, CRISPR-based Chronos scores from the DepMap 25Q2 dataset ( ) revealed minimal dependency for TF-1 (+0.018) and M07e (−0.014), reinforcing that high DNMT3B expression does not necessarily confer essentiality in vitro. Immunoblotting confirmed markedly higher DNMT3B protein levels in TF-1, KG1A, and M07e compared with MV4;11, AML-193, and MOLM-13, whereas CD34+ HSPCs lacked detectable DNMT3B ( B). These findings establish TF-1, KG1A, and M07e as representative DNMT3B-overexpressing AML models for pharmacologic interrogation.
 To probe DNMT3B-associated vulnerabilities, we treated AML cell lines with Nanaomycin A (NanA), used here as a DNMT3B-directed functional probe given reported catalytic selectivity alongside potential DNMT3B-independent cytotoxic effects. Viability assays revealed a spectrum of sensitivities (IC50 = 62–969 nM; C): TF-1 (969 nM), KG1A (334 nM), and M07e (195 nM) were relatively resistant, whereas MV4;11 (62 nM), AML-193 (74 nM), and MOLM-13 (22 nM) were more sensitive; notably, at fixed low-nanomolar dosing, NanA also elicited time-dependent stress and apoptosis, emphasizing that IC50 values should be interpreted alongside exposure duration. This pattern indicates that NanA cytotoxicity only partially tracks with DNMT3B abundance, consistent with mixed determinants of response and the known limitations of NanA as a quinone-based chemical probe [ ]. Accordingly, we used NanA to interrogate DNMT3B-linked chromatin and transcriptional responses while explicitly recognizing that viability effects may not be exclusively DNMT3B-dependent. Notably, DNMT3B-high models were not uniformly more sensitive to NanA, consistent with the lack of DNMT3B dependency in RNAi/CRISPR datasets and suggesting that DNMT3B expression alone does not determine acute cytotoxic response.
In KG1A cells, NanA exposure was associated with a concentration-dependent reduction in DNMT3B protein abundance, with partial reduction at 25–50 nM and greater reduction at 100 nM ( D,E; p < 0.001). Residual DNMT3B protein at 100 nM indicates incomplete catalytic blockade, consistent with NanA’s reported pharmacodynamic profile. RNA-seq analysis of KG1A cells treated with 50 nM of NanA for 48 h revealed 1835 down-regulated and 1129 up-regulated genes ( F; ). Gene-set enrichment analysis (MSigDB Hallmark) identified enrichment of mitotic spindle, TNFα signaling via NF-κB, p53 pathway, IL2–STAT5 signaling, hypoxia, and G2/M checkpoint programs ( G), consistent with integrated stress and checkpoint responses at this exposure duration. Among these, mitotic-spindle and checkpoint pathways were most enriched, linking pharmacologic DNMT3B inhibition to stress-response and apoptotic networks rather than direct cell-cycle arrest. The enrichment of p53 and inflammatory signaling signatures further suggests that DNMT3B-associated epigenetic perturbation initiates integrated stress programs that couple proliferative control with apoptotic priming in AML cells.

## 3.3. Disruption of DNMT3B Activity Rewires Cis-Regulatory Methylation and Protein Abundance in Cell Cycle Networks
To define the epigenomic consequences of DNMT3B inhibition, we profiled genome-wide DNA methylation in KG1A cells treated with Nanaomycin A using Illumina EPIC arrays. Circos visualization revealed widespread differential methylation with a net bias toward CpG hypomethylation ( A). In total, 6900 CpGs were significantly altered (FDR < 0.05; |Δβ| > 0.1), most within promoters (n = 3950; 3439 hypo-, 511 hypermethylated), followed by gene bodies (n = 1592; 1085 hypo-, 507 hypermethylated) and intergenic regions (n = 1358; 803 hypo-, 555 hypermethylated). Most methylation changes were modest (Δβ within ±0.25; B), consistent with partial reconfiguration rather than global erasure. To link methylation dynamics with transcriptional output, differentially methylated CpGs were intersected with significantly altered genes from RNA-seq analysis. Correlation analysis revealed weak inverse associations between methylation and expression at promoters (r = –0.12) and gene bodies (r = –0.20; C,D), indicating that DNMT3B-associated methylation changes act in concert with additional regulatory layers such as chromatin accessibility and transcription-factor recruitment rather than functioning as a sole determinant of transcription.
 Genes were classified by concordant or discordant methylation–expression patterns. Promoter-associated genes included 61 hypo-up, 117 hypo-down, 7 hyper-up, and 27 hyper-down. Gene-body-linked genes showed 12 hypo-up, 58 hypo-down, 3 hyper-up, and 33 hyper-down. The prevalence of hypo-down and hyper-up categories deviates from canonical repression models, supporting network-level rather than unidirectional control of transcriptional responses. To determine whether these transcriptional shifts extended to the proteome, RNA-seq data were integrated with quantitative proteomics from DNMT3B-inhibited KG1A cells. A subset of promoter-hypomethylated and transcriptionally up-regulated genes also showed increased protein abundance ( E). Seventeen genes—including TIAM1, CDK6, DENND1B, and KDM2A—were coordinately up-regulated at both transcript and protein levels ( F,G; ). These genes participate in cell-cycle progression, cytoskeletal organization, checkpoint regulation, and chromatin remodeling, consistent with functional themes emerging from pathway analyses ( ). Together, these findings show that DNMT3B inhibition preferentially induces hypomethylation at gene-proximal regions accompanied by selective transcriptomic and proteomic remodeling. Rather than directly “governing” transcription, DNMT3B appears to stabilize proliferative gene networks through multi-layered epigenetic interactions that integrate DNA methylation, chromatin accessibility, and transcriptional output.

## 3.4. DNMT3B Inhibition Induces Demethylation at Cis-Regulatory Elements of Cell-Cycle Genes
To assess how NanA exposure reshapes cis-regulatory architecture in DNMT3B-high AML, we intersected differentially methylated CpGs with differentially accessible chromatin regions from ATAC-seq in KG1A cells treated with 50 nM of NanA. Genome-wide annotation of differential accessibility (n = 36,942; ) showed predominant localization within intronic (34%) and intergenic (33%) regions, followed by promoters (27%) and smaller fractions in exonic, untranslated, and termination sites ( A). Integration with DNAm data identified 268 CpGs located within regions of altered accessibility after DNMT3B inhibition, a small but statistically non-random overlap (hypergeometric p < 0.05) enriched at promoter-proximal and intragenic loci ( B). Promoter-associated CpGs (n = 184) were disproportionately represented compared with gene bodies (n = 40) and intergenic regions (n = 44). Most promoter-linked CpGs (n = 166) were hypomethylated, consistent with localized chromatin remodeling at regulatory regions of transcriptionally active genes.
 IGV visualization of representative loci revealed concordant increases in chromatin accessibility and CpG hypomethylation at CDK6, CENPE, GSK3B, and SLC7A5 ( C–F). In CDK6, DNMT3B inhibition produced focal demethylation and enhanced accessibility at an intragenic DHS downstream of the TSS, displaying enhancer-like chromatin features. CENPE exhibited analogous promoter-proximal remodeling, while GSK3B and SLC7A5 showed accessibility gains within intronic, H3K27ac-positive regions defined as enhancers (>1 kb from the TSS) according to ENCODE chromatin-state maps. Motif analysis of DNMT3B-depleted accessible regions demonstrated enrichment for NRF1 and E2F1 motifs—transcription factors associated with chromatin opening and G1/S-phase progression ( ). Functionally, these genes participate in mitotic-spindle organization, checkpoint control, and proliferative signaling, paralleling pathway-level reprogramming observed in transcriptomic and proteomic datasets ( and ). Together, these findings indicate that NanA exposure is associated with selective demethylation and accessibility remodeling at cis-regulatory elements of cell-cycle genes, supporting a model in which DNMT3B maintains the epigenetic configuration of proliferative transcriptional networks in AML.

## 3.5. DNMT3B Inhibition Disrupts Cell Cycle Progression and Induces Time-Dependent Apoptosis in AML Cells
To functionally validate the transcriptional and epigenetic changes triggered by DNMT3B inhibition, we examined cell-cycle distribution and apoptosis in KG1A cells treated with 25 nM or 50 nM of Nanaomycin A. At 24 h, DNMT3B inhibition caused a significant redistribution across major phases ( A,B). Sub-G1 content increased at both concentrations, indicating early DNA fragmentation, whereas G1-phase cells declined at 25 nM and remained largely unchanged at 50 nM. S-phase fractions expanded at both doses, and G2/M increased at 50 nM, suggesting replication stress and checkpoint activation. By 48 h, these effects intensified: sub-G1 cells rose sharply, G1 populations fell markedly, and modest increases in S and G2/M persisted ( C,D), consistent with progression from S-phase disturbance toward G2/M checkpoint engagement and apoptotic accumulation.
 Annexin V–FITC/PI staining confirmed that DNMT3B inhibition elicits a canonical, time-dependent apoptotic response ( E–H). At 24 h, early apoptotic cells (Q2; PI− AnnV+) increased by ~1 percentage point at both 25 nM and 50 nM relative to control, and late apoptotic cells (Q3; PI+ AnnV+) rose by ~2–3 points, while viable cells (Q1; double negative) and necrotic cells (Q4; PI+ AnnV−) showed no significant change. At 48 h, apoptosis became dominant: Q2 and Q3 populations increased markedly, viable cells decreased, and the distribution shifted away from the double-negative (AnnV−/PI−) population toward Annexin V–positive fractions; quadrant assignments and summary values are reported as defined in the corresponding figure panel. All changes except the minor variations in Q1 at 24 h were statistically significant (p < 0.05, Dunnett’s test). Together, these data show that NanA treatment is accompanied by accelerated loss of G1 fraction, accumulation of S/G2–M stress signatures, and a robust apoptotic response, consistent with checkpoint and p53 pathway enrichment in transcriptomic analyses ( ).

## 3.6. DNMT3B Inhibition Primes AML Cells for Venetoclax Sensitization Through Apoptotic Rewiring
Because DNMT3B inhibition altered mitotic and checkpoint genes ( ), induced focal hypomethylation at regulatory elements of cell-cycle regulators ( ), and triggered progressive apoptosis ( ), we hypothesized that DNMT3B blockade may lower the apoptotic threshold and sensitize AML cells to BCL2 inhibition. To test this, we assessed the combined effects of Nanaomycin A (NanA), used here as a DNMT3B-directed functional probe, and Venetoclax (VEN), a BCL2 antagonist. In KG1A cells, single-agent IC50 values were 334 nM for NanA and 136 nM for VEN, while the combination reduced the IC50 to 40.9 nM (95% CI 37.8–44.1) and steepened the dose-response slope ( A). In M07e cells, VEN and NanA monotherapy IC50 values were 1045 nM and 195 nM, respectively, whereas the combination decreased the IC50 to 132 nM (95% CI 121–144) ( B). Across concentrations, NanA + VEN produced greater cytotoxicity than either agent alone, consistent with cooperative activity in the DNMT3B-high models tested. Because the combination was tested using a fixed molar ratio rather than a full-dose–matrix design, formal Bliss or ZIP synergy modeling was not feasible; therefore, these data are interpreted conservatively as cooperative cytotoxicity rather than definitive pharmacologic synergy. Taken together with DNMT3B inhibition–induced replication stress and apoptosis ( , , and ), these cooperative effects support a model in which DNMT3B-dependent epigenetic programming buffers apoptotic signaling; its inhibition functionally “primes” cells for BCL2 blockade. We interpret these data as apoptotic “priming” rather than direct DNMT3B–BCL2 coupling. Because apoptotic commitment is governed by the balance of pro- and anti-apoptotic BCL2-family programs, we next quantified transcriptional responses of key apoptotic regulators and interpret simultaneous induction of BH3-only genes and compensatory anti-apoptotic genes as apoptotic priming accompanied by adaptive buffering. Importantly, this interpretation aligns the combination phenotype with upstream mitotic/checkpoint disruption and apoptotic engagement following DNMT3B inhibition, supporting DNMT3B-governed circuitry as a mechanistically grounded co-targeting axis to enhance VEN efficacy in selected AML contexts.
 

## 3.7. Transcriptional Reprogramming of Apoptotic Regulators Following DNMT3B and BCL2 Inhibition
To investigate the molecular basis of VEN sensitization following DNMT3B inhibition, we profiled the expression of canonical apoptotic regulators by qPCR in KG1A cells treated with the DNMT3B inhibitor NanA, VEN, or their combination. The panel included mitochondrial apoptotic effectors (BAK1, BAX), BH3-only activators and sensitizers (PMAIP1/NOXA, BBC3/PUMA, BCL2L11/BIM), and anti-apoptotic BCL2 family members (BCL2, MCL1, BCL2L1/BCL-XL) ( C–J). BAK1 was significantly downregulated across all treatments relative to control (p < 0.0001), with no further reduction in combination, suggesting a basal reconfiguration of mitochondrial homeostasis rather than a treatment-specific effect ( C), while BAX exhibited progressive downregulation with both VEN and NanA + VEN (p < 0.01), consistent with its post-translational regulation and limited transcriptional responsiveness in AML ( D) [ , ]. In contrast, BH3-only genes showed stronger and differential responses: PMAIP1 (NOXA) was robustly induced by NanA alone and further elevated with NanA + VEN (p < 0.0001), consistent with its role in sequestering MCL1 and shifting apoptotic dependency toward BCL2 ( E) [ ]. BBC3 (PUMA) expression remained unchanged under either monotherapy but was strongly upregulated in the combination arm (p < 0.001), suggesting that DNMT3B inhibition primes chromatin for inducible PUMA transcription ( F); and BCL2L11 (BIM) increased modestly under NanA or VEN alone (p < 0.01) but did not rise further in combination, indicating saturation or compensatory feedback regulation ( G). Among anti-apoptotic BCL2 family members, BCL2 was repressed by both DNMT3B inhibition and VEN individually (p < 0.05) and further suppressed in combination (p < 0.0001) ( H), consistent with transcriptional remodeling of BCL2 regulatory elements following DNMT3B inhibition [ ]. In contrast, MCL1 and BCL2L1 (BCL-XL) were significantly upregulated in the NanA + VEN arm (both p < 0.0001) ( I,J), reflecting compensatory survival pathway activation commonly associated with acquired VEN resistance [ , ]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that DNMT3B inhibition enhances the expression of BH3-only sensitizers (NOXA, PUMA), represses BCL2, and triggers compensatory MCL1/BCL-XL induction, thereby reshaping the transcriptional architecture of the apoptotic rheostat. Notably, induction of BH3-only sensitizers, together with MCL1/BCL-XL upregulation, is not mutually exclusive and is consistent with a stress-adaptation state in which apoptotic priming is counterbalanced by compensatory survival signaling. Thus, these data indicate that DNMT3B-directed perturbation shifts the apoptotic network toward increased priming while simultaneously engaging buffering pathways that are well-recognized mediators of emerging venetoclax resistance. This DNMT3B-dependent remodeling primes leukemia cells for mitochondrial apoptotic collapse upon BCL2 blockade, positioning DNMT3B as a mechanistic co-regulator and rational therapeutic target in the context of VEN-based AML therapy.

## 4. Discussion
Our study identifies DNMT3B-associated regulatory circuitry as a context-dependent epigenetic feature of AML, with pharmacologic perturbation linked to focal methylation changes at cis-regulatory elements, altered proliferative transcriptional programs, and apoptotic remodeling. Integrative multi-omic analyses demonstrate that DNMT3B inhibition initiates coordinated reprogramming of the methylome, chromatin landscape, transcriptome, and proteome, culminating in replication stress and programmed apoptosis. Rather than functioning primarily as a global methyltransferase, DNMT3B appears to act as a regulator linking local DNA methylation with chromatin organization and transcriptional stability.
We show that DNMT3B overexpression in AML is not mutation-driven but enhancer-mediated. Epigenomic profiling revealed DNase I hypersensitive sites enriched for H3K27ac and H3K4me3 across the DNMT3B promoter and gene body, consistent with active cis-regulatory regions that are most pronounced in CEBPA- and NPM1-mutated AML datasets, suggesting subtype-associated enhancer remodeling at the DNMT3B locus; however, downstream chromatin and transcriptional consequences were examined in representative DNMT3B-high AML models rather than systematically across genetically defined AML subtypes. SRF emerged as a candidate transcriptional regulator at the DNMT3B promoter, and SRF has been implicated in transcriptional and cytoskeletal programs that overlap with DNMT3B-associated regulatory networks in leukemia models, although direct SRF–DNMT3B regulatory links remain to be demonstrated [ ]. In CEBPA- and NPM1-mutant AML, SRF motif enrichment may reflect broader enhancer rewiring involving lineage-defining transcription factors, although direct cooperative regulation of DNMT3B remains to be demonstrated and will require targeted perturbation and chromatin-occupancy studies [ ]. Together, these findings support a model in which DNMT3B overexpression is epigenetically driven through transcription factor–mediated enhancer–promoter engagement, rather than through genetic alteration.
NanA treatment was associated with a modest, dose-dependent reduction in DNMT3B protein levels in AML cells ( D), consistent with reports in hepatocellular carcinoma and other models, but differing from A549 lung carcinoma cells where DNMT3B levels remained unchanged [ , ]. Biochemical studies have reported preferential inhibition of DNMT3B catalytic activity by Nanaomycin A, although its quinone scaffold can also induce oxidative stress- and context-dependent cytotoxicity, suggesting that NanA should be interpreted as a DNMT3B-directed functional probe rather than a fully selective inhibitor [ ]. Variability in cellular redox buffering or mitochondrial stress tolerance may therefore contribute to differential NanA sensitivity independent of DNMT3B abundance, which could partly explain why DNMT3B-high AML models do not uniformly display increased NanA susceptibility. Because Nanaomycin A is a quinone-based molecule reported to induce oxidative and mitochondrial stress responses, some transcriptional or phenotypic effects may reflect DNMT3B-independent cytotoxic mechanisms; accordingly, our interpretation emphasizes loci showing concordant DNA methylation, chromatin accessibility, and gene-expression changes consistent with epigenetic regulation. DNMT3B inhibition was associated with focal rather than global hypomethylation, preferential remodeling of promoter- and enhancer-like regions, and heterogeneous transcriptional responses consistent with chromatin-context–dependent DNMT3B function; however, these associations are correlative, and we cannot exclude that accessibility changes reflect indirect effects such as replication stress, cell-cycle perturbation, or broader epigenomic remodeling rather than direct DNMT3B occupancy at these loci. Motif enrichment for NRF1 and E2F1 within DNMT3Bi-accessible regions suggests that DNMT3B influences transcription factor occupancy and cell-cycle transcriptional wiring rather than operating as a uniform gene silencer [ , ]. Consistent with this, mass spectrometry showed a trend toward increased NRF1 protein abundance following NanA (log2FC = +1.55; padj = 0.135; ). While this supports NRF1 activation, direct occupancy studies (e.g., CUT&RUN or ChIP-seq) will be required to establish causality. Because NanA has been reported to inhibit DNMT3B catalytic activity, the observed epigenomic effects are consistent with catalytic inhibition; however, DNMT3B-independent cytotoxicity and secondary stress responses, particularly at later time points, cannot be excluded. Complementary genetic DNMT3B perturbation studies, including our prior work demonstrating increased venetoclax sensitivity following DNMT3B knockout in DNMT3B-high AML models [ , ], support DNMT3B-associated epigenetic regulation while underscoring that Nanaomycin A should be interpreted primarily as a functional probe rather than a fully selective inhibitor.
DNMT3Bi produced subtle yet coordinated methylation shifts sufficient to drive transcriptional and proteomic remodeling. Genes such as CDK6, CENPE, and GSK3B exhibited enhancer demethylation, chromatin opening, and protein up-regulation, implicating DNMT3B in maintaining mitotic-fidelity checkpoints [ ]. Contrary to classical G1 arrest, DNMT3Bi caused G1 depletion with S-phase accumulation, consistent with premature G1 exit, replication stress, and G2/M checkpoint activation. Increased CDK6 expression following DNMT3B inhibition likely reflects an adaptive stress response rather than sustained proliferative signaling, as it occurs in the setting of checkpoint engagement and progressive apoptosis and parallels prior observations that epigenetic stress can induce compensatory upregulation of cell-cycle regulators without restoring durable proliferation. In this context, DNMT3B-directed perturbation appears to destabilize replication-associated transcriptional programs and checkpoint control mechanisms, rather than simply slowing cell-cycle progression [ , ]. This interpretation is consistent with broader epigenetic reprogramming following DNMT3B perturbation and does not establish these genes as direct DNMT3B targets; rather, candidates such as CDK6, CENPE, and GSK3B likely represent downstream effectors of DNMT3B-associated chromatin remodeling with potential therapeutic relevance that warrants functional validation in additional models (summarized schematically in ).
 To dissect the apoptotic circuitry, we profiled BCL2 family members under NanA, VEN, and combination treatment. NanA + VEN prominently up-regulated BH3-only sensitizers PMAIP1 (NOXA) and BBC3 (PUMA), while BCL2 was transcriptionally repressed across all arms, consistent with enhanced apoptotic priming. MCL1 and BCL2L1 (BCL-XL) were significantly induced at the transcript level in the combination group, mirroring compensatory survival programs commonly associated with evolving venetoclax resistance in AML and other myeloid neoplasms. BAX and BAK1 were transcriptionally suppressed by NanA ( C,D) but up-regulated at the protein level (log2FC = +1.29 for BAX; +2.77 for BAK1; ), suggesting post-transcriptional regulation of effector proteins downstream of DNMT3B perturbation. Collectively, these data are consistent with an apoptotic priming model in which DNMT3B inhibition lowers the apoptotic threshold through BH3-only gene induction and BCL2 repression, although direct functional confirmation of mitochondrial priming (e.g., BH3 profiling or protein-level validation across additional models) was beyond the scope of this study; early induction of MCL1/BCL-XL may represent an adaptive response that could limit durability of venetoclax-based regimens [ , , ]. These findings suggest that combinatorial approaches targeting MCL1 or BCL-XL could improve durability of DNMT3B–BCL2 co-targeting strategies; however, the observed enhancement of venetoclax sensitivity was demonstrated in DNMT3B-high AML cell line models, and evaluation in primary AML samples and in vivo systems will be required to establish translational generalizability.
Clinically used hypomethylating agents (HMAs) such as azacitidine and decitabine also modulate DNA methylation and improve outcomes when combined with venetoclax in older or unfit AML patients, and preclinical data suggest that HMAs can alter chromatin accessibility and enhancer activity, primarily through DNMT trapping following incorporation into DNA (and RNA for azacitidine) rather than targeted DNMT3B inhibition [ , ]. Our data suggest that DNMT3B-directed perturbation may involve more focal remodeling of enhancer-like elements compared with the broader effects of conventional hypomethylating agents, although direct comparative studies are needed [ ], providing a mechanistic rationale for exploring DNMT3B-selective strategies—alone or in combination with BCL2 inhibitors—as a complementary approach to pan-hypomethylating therapy [ ].
Beyond AML, DNMT3B overexpression and focal hypomethylation signatures recur in colorectal, breast, and hepatocellular carcinomas, and elevated DNMT3B or DNMT3B target-gene expression associates with adverse outcomes in several solid tumors, underscoring broader oncogenic relevance [ , ]. In TCGA breast cancer, for example, patients with below-median DNMT3B expression (RSEM < 114.28) showed significantly improved overall survival (log-rank p = 0.033; Wilcoxon p = 0.005; HR = 1.42, 95% CI 1.03–1.95; ), consistent with a context in which DNMT3B acts predominantly as an oncogenic factor. In contrast, ectopic Dnmt3b expression can delay leukemogenesis and impair leukemia-initiating capacity in KMT2A- and Myc/Bcl2-driven mouse models, highlighting a tumor-suppressive role in specific stem-cell contexts. Collectively, these observations indicate that DNMT3B function is highly context dependent, reflecting differentiation state, cooperating lesions, and chromatin landscape rather than intrinsic oncogenic or tumor-suppressive activity. Although DNMT3B is unlikely to act as a single determinant, our data support its consideration as a context-dependent prognostic biomarker and potential therapeutic target. Clinically, DNMT3B-high AML subsets could be identified using transcriptomic profiling, quantitative PCR, or immunoblot-based assays, and available cohort analyses suggest elevated DNMT3B expression in defined high-risk AML subtypes rather than uniformly across AML. The stability of DNMT3B expression during disease evolution or relapse remains incompletely characterized and warrants longitudinal study. Importantly, while CD34+ hematopoietic progenitors show low baseline DNMT3B expression in our analyses, the consequences of pharmacologic DNMT3B inhibition under hematopoietic stress conditions remain uncertain, underscoring the need for careful toxicity evaluation in translational development.

## 5. Conclusions
In summary, our data support DNMT3B as a context-dependent epigenetic regulator of chromatin-governed survival programs in AML. Its overexpression appears primarily enhancer driven rather than mutation mediated, and its inhibition reconfigures focal methylation at key cis-regulatory elements, remodels transcriptional and proteomic modules governing proliferation and apoptosis, and primes AML cells for BCL2 blockade. This DNMT3B-dependent apoptotic priming—characterized by BH3-only activation, BCL2 repression, and BAX/BAK1 stabilization with compensatory MCL1/BCL-XL induction—provides a mechanistic rationale for DNMT3B-targeted epigenetic co-therapy. These findings offer a conceptual framework for extending this therapeutic paradigm to additional malignancies exhibiting aberrant DNMT3B activation.
