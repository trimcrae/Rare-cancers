## Background
Functional genomics aims to decipher gene function by observing cellular changes when specific genes are disrupted using CRISPR technology. However, these experiments are limited by scalability, as comprehensive CRISPR screens require extensive resources, involving millions of cells and thousands of sgRNAs, making large-scale studies challenging. We propose a novel approach with “CRISPR lossy compression” to reduce the complexity of CRISPR screens by focusing on key genetic nodes that can infer genome-wide phenotypes. These condensed sets, comprising 100 to 1,000 genes, enable previously impractical genome-wide screens tractable.

## Results
To make this approach accessible to the wider scientific community, we developed goloco, an interactive web application that allows users to explore genome-scale loss-of-function phenotypes from as few as 100 pooled measurements. The tool is complemented by a wide array of analyses, including volcano plot visualizations, regression and network analyses.

## Conclusions
This tool goloco empowers researchers to conduct genome-scale functional studies with minimal experimental overhead, broadening the accessibility of large-scale functional genomics research.

## Background
Cataloging when and where genes are expressed does not tell us what those genes do in a mammalian cell. Functional genomics seeks to understand what happens in a cell when we turn off a gene with CRISPR. Collectively, experimental perturbations with CRISPR libraries can provide critical annotations of gene function, and insights into biology across diverse genetic backgrounds and experimental conditions. However, functional genomics is fundamentally less scalable than transcriptomics. You can measure 20,000 transcripts in a single cell, but only 1 CRISPR loss-of-function phenotype. This inability to scale functional genomic inquiry means that many important experiments are impossible, and any increase in experimental complexity in pooled CRISPR experiments is extraordinarily laborious. This is because a single unbiased mammalian growth regulation experiment requires ~ 80,000 unique sgRNAs and ~ 80 million cells for a single experimental condition. We propose that one way to reduce the size and complexity of genome scale screens is to shrink libraries by only including a subset of highly informative genes rather than a smaller number of sgRNAs for every gene. Orders of magnitude reductions in gene set size could empower experiments at scales that are currently impossible. To do this we identified key genetic nodes whose loss of function predicts the loss of function phenotype of a related gene(s). Lossy compression sets pack global portraits of growth regulation into tunable collections of 100–1000 genes that balance global accuracy and information loss. This makes previously impossible experiments possible because we can get genome-scale portraits of growth regulation with tiny pools of hundreds of sgRNAs. Lossy compression sets can also be added to any sub-genome scale tool to give broader genomic information. In order to bring this potential to the community we built a fully contained web application that allows a user to input any size lossy compression set, and then make testable predictions about sgRNA phenotypes in all human genes.

## Implementation
goloco is a web application that hosts a machine learning inference tool and interactive data visualizations. Its primary function is to perform genome-wide CRISPR knockout inference, achieved through a three-node architecture consisting of:Dash frontend server: Initiates user requests and handles interactive visualizations and navigation via Dash callbacks.
Celery backend worker: Executes inference tasks.
Redis key-value store: Manages communication between the frontend and backend.

## Workflow

A user initiates an inference through the Dash frontend, triggering a Celery worker.
The worker, using a boto3 client, downloads individual gene models from a secured AWS S3 bucket, stores them in memory, and performs the inference.
The worker reports progress and sends results to the Dash frontend via Redis.
Frontend tasks such as data visualization updates and navigation requests are handled by Dash callbacks.

## Deployment
goloco is containerized with Docker and deployed on Heroku (PaaS). Key challenges and their solutions include:Storage limitations: The large storage requirement (~ 18,000 gene models) exceeds typical PaaS capacities. This is mitigated by storing gene models in an AWS S3 bucket, accessible to Celery workers via boto3.
Long inference runtime: Genome-wide inference can exceed the 30-s timeout limit for frontend requests. To resolve this, the Dash frontend initiates long callbacks and offloads inference tasks asynchronously to Celery workers, with progress and results communicated via Redis.

## Data and preprocessing
goloco uses data from genome wide CRISPR screens performed by the Broad Institute and publicly available for download on the Dependency Map (DepMap) portal. The 19Q4 release of the CRISPR gene effect database is used for consistency with data that was used for training and validating our genome wide prediction models. CRISPR gene effect scores from this dataset are reported in CERES scores, which scale Log2Fold changes in growth regulation by defining the average of all common essential genes as a CERES score of −1 and all common non-essential genes as a CERES score of 0, while also adjusting for Copy Number Variants. As a result, a CERES score of 0 indicates no effect on growth regulation by the CRISPR knockout and a more negative score indicates negative growth regulation. Definitions used by our web application for essential, nonessential, and conditional essential are all consistent with those of the 19Q4 release. Data from this release is already normalized with the CERES algorithm and missing CERES scores are imputed to be the average across all other cell lines.

## Statistical methods

## CERES predictions, Z-scores, and linear regressions
goloco generates genome-wide predictions from small scale experiments using thousands of Random Forest Models, as developed in our previous work. When a CERES prediction for a gene is made, a z-score is calculated by normalizing the predicted score relative to the distribution of CERES scores for that gene across all experiments in the DepMap 19Q4 dataset. Specifically, the z-score is computed as the difference between the predicted score and the average CERES score for the gene, divided by the standard deviation of the CERES scores for that gene. An important prediction will meet two criteria: the predicted CERES score is significant relative to the overall distribution of CERES scores across all genes and experiments, and the predicted CERES score is significant within the context of the specific gene's CERES score distribution. The p-value of a prediction is calculated as the product of two probabilities. The probability of observing the predicted CERES score in the overall distribution of CERES scores, and the probability of observing the predicted CERES score within the distribution of CERES scores for the specific gene. These probabilities are determined using the two-tailed area under the probability density function (PDF) at the predicted score. The PDFs are estimated from the DepMap 19Q4 data, using kernel density estimation. z-scores and p-values are included in the downloadable prediction from goloco. We leave parameterization of filtering for hits or significant genes up to the user. goloco users can use the “Volcano Plot” and “Z-Score Hits” tabs in the “Explore > Hits” page and apply thresholds to define significant predictions to generate ranked gene lists—based on z-scores, p-values, or CERES scores—that are important for their experiment. These lists are downloadable and functional enrichment analysis on gene sets are employable directly on the web application using gProfiler2. We use ordinary least square regressions for linear models of pairwise and multiple pairwise gene comparisons of CERES scores in the “Explore > Regressions” page. We display Pearson r correlation coefficients on the multipairwise regressions.

## Louvain community, clustering and feature importance
We developed 200 Louvain communities in our previous work using the Louvain method on the network of genes, where we modeled each gene as a node and each CERES predictive feature as an edge. Each Louvain community represents a biological network, and different cell-lines may utilize this network differently. However, network utilization was never categorized. To characterize different phenotypes of network utilization for each Louvain community, we use the DepMap 19Q4 cell-line data and isolated only genes involved in the specific community. Then we use PHATE on this network limited feature space for dimensional reduction and k-means clustering to identify seven clusters for each network, where each cluster represents a phenotype of network utilization. We repeat this process for each Louvain community. Additionally, we visualize these clusters on other dimensional reduction techniques including PCA, UMAP, and tSNE. For every cluster, we generate a ranked feature importance list of genes with a semi-supervised approach using Minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance (MRMR) feature selection for classifying one cluster against the combination of all others. These feature importance scores give an indication of the most important genes in the network that define each cluster. The results of these analyses can be visualized and explored in the “Community Analysis” and “Cluster Analysis” tabs in the “Pathways > Communities” page.

## Results
The goloco application allows the user to directly use scores of the compressed list (200 genes list) from multiple experiments for genome-wide inference. The tool also supports upload of raw gene counts from CRISPR experiments for conversion into CHRONOS scores for artifact correction. The entire analysis requires no coding knowledge. After job completion, the results are provided within the application with an interactive session for immediate follow-up analyses. The session can also be downloaded and reloaded back in the future. Several visualizations are provided in the tool, including a volcano plot of predicted scores against their p-values, with significant predictions highlighted (Fig.  a), and a plot of predicted scores, ranked by extremity of z-scores, mapped against violin plots of that gene across all other DepMap cell lines (Fig.  d). Directly on the web application, gProfiler2 can be run on “hits” from the volcano and z-score plots to obtain a Manhattan plot and a table of functional enrichment scores of GO terms (Fig.  e). , The user can perform single and multiple pairwise comparisons, with linear regression analysis, on genes of interest (Fig.  f). The tool also provides a way to visualize relevant functional connections between different genes within a Louvain network for a specific cell-line or experiment (Fig.  i). In addition, there are dimensional reduction visualizations for clusters defined with PHATE, each cluster characterizing a different network utilization phenotype of a Louvain community (Fig.  g). Relevant genes which best define these clusters are also predetermined and visualized in their ranked order of importance (Fig.  h). The tool also provides a tabular list of predicted scores, distributions and probability density functions of the scores and z-scores, grouped by essential, nonessential, and/or conditional essential. 
As a typical workflow, the users can download the list of genes representing the lossy 200 gene set (L200) by navigating to the “Predict” page. A small sgRNA library for these 200 genes can be constructed as a standalone library, or from larger libraries such as Brunello or Avana. We recommend at least 2 sgRNA’s per gene. Users can perform CRISPR knockout experiments using this compressed library. Next, the user will need to use the raw data from their experiments to generate three .csv files to convert their data to gene effect scores using the “Gene Effect Calculator” tab in the “Predict” page of goloco. Examples of formatting these .csv files are provided in the web application. Additionally, descriptions of this gene effect calculator tool and the required inputs is also documented in Dempster et al. The .csv files will be input to the gene effect calculator and the raw reads for each experiment will be converted to gene effect scores. The output of this tool, which is a .csv file of gene effect scores for L200 genes (rows) for each experiment (columns), will be an input to the genome wide prediction tool. The user can input this file into “Option 1” in the “Submit Inference” tab in the “Predict” page, which will run the genome-wide prediction. Once the prediction is completed, users can download the prediction and start exploring the results.
Users can navigate to the “Explore > Hits” page and select the “Volcano Plot” tab. Here the user may adjust thresholds for CERES scores and p-values to generate a list of selectively essential gene predictions for their experiments through filtered criteria. Similarly the user may navigate to the “Explore > Hits” page and select the “Z-Score Hits” tab and modify various thresholds and criteria to identify lists of selectively essential genes for their experiments. These important gene lists can be explored directly on goloco with the embedded gProfiler2 tool by selecting the “GO” button on the queried genes of each tool. This can provide the user with an overview of potentially upregulated biological pathways involved in growth regulation requiring further investigation. Alternatively, the gene lists can be downloaded and further explored through experimentation or uploaded to other publicly available tools for functional gene annotation such as PANTHER GO enrichment analysis for more detailed results. Users can navigate to the “Explore > Regressions” page and select the “Multiple Pairwise Regression” tab to visualize correlated genetic dependencies in networked genes by selecting genes within individual Louvain communities. The users’ pairwise comparison of predicted gene effects across experiments will be overlaid with the background regression of measured effects across all cell lines in the DepMap 19Q4 data. Predicted correlation between user experiments can be compared against the DepMap data.

## Discussion
Genome-wide CRISPR screens, while powerful, face significant logistical hurdles as researchers attempt to scale screening to more diverse contexts. Our compression sets strategically select a smaller subset of genes and predict the effects of disrupting the entire genome. This approach drastically reduces the scale and cost of these experiments, opening doors to previously impossible genome scale studies across thousands of conditions, or in experimental contexts that do not allow for the introduction of a genome scale library. However, using our models was previously challenging, and this work aims to give broader access to our models. We develop a useful interface for exploring data and predictions. This gives users access to our models and to convenient tools to interpret predictions. Our models may serve as a screening tool to identify hotspots for further investigation and provide insight into genetic networks that are targetable in different contexts. This may prove valuable for investigating novel drug targets and promoting rational drug design, amongst many other applications, while minimizing costs.
However, the simplicity of compression sets also creates trade-offs. The inherent information loss in compression could mean missing subtle gene functions or context-specific effects, but the accuracy of these predictions may improve as more data becomes available, refining the selection of genes for compression sets. Alternatively, some genes may truly lack observable effects when disrupted individually, limiting the impact of potential false negatives. Finally, many genes have small observed effects in all current screens and are inherently challenging to predict because the most interesting new screens would produce a large phenotype where one has not been previously observed.
Despite these caveats, lossy compression holds immense promise. It allows users to explore genome function at experimental scales that are simply not possible with a genome scale library.

## Conclusions
We hope this tool will make the use of small sgRNA collections with genome-scale impact broadly useful to bench biologists anywhere. Beyond enabling specific experiments with only our library, it also facilitates the inclusion of these gene sets into any targeted pool of sgRNAs such that you can bolt on genome scale inference on to any functional genomics experiment with little experimental cost. We envision that this will be broadly useful for phenotyping small molecules and novel genes as well as enabling systems biology investigations of cell function at a large scale.
