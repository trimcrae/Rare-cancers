## Introduction
The advent of cancer genome sequencing coupled with functional genomics approaches to characterize the genetic events driving tumorigenesis led to the identification of novel targets and mechanisms to treat cancer. One of the strongest and most prevalent synthetic lethal interactions identified from these efforts is the selective dependence of MTAP-deleted cells on PRMT5 activity [ , , ].
Protein arginine methyltransferase-5 (PRMT5) is a type II PRMT that uses the cofactor S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) to catalyze the symmetric dimethylation of arginine (SDMA) residues on substrates that regulate DNA damage, spliceosome activity, and transcriptional activity, and other necessary cellular functions [ , , ]. The broad role that PRMT5 plays in regulating cellular homeostasis renders it an essential gene, which is supported by data from CRISPR-based cellular screens [ ]. High expression of PRMT5 has been noted in multiple tumor types including melanoma, glioma, colorectal carcinoma, leukemia, lymphoma, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), pancreatic, and prostate cancer highlighting it as a target for cancer therapy [ ]. Early efforts to develop clinical PRMT5 inhibitors focused on mechanisms of action that inhibited PRMT5 irrespective of MTAP status, such as the SAM-uncompetitive/substrate-competitive PRMT5 inhibitor, pemrametostat (GSK3326595), or the SAM-competitive PRMT5 inhibitors, onametostat (JNJ-64619178), PRT811, and PF-06939999. Though clinical activity was observed with these inhibitors, they exhibited dose-limiting hematological toxicities, likely due to the inability to selectively target cancer cells [ , , ]. More recently, a new class of PRMT5 inhibitors has been developed with a novel, MTA-cooperative mechanism of action that leverages the synthetic lethal relationship between MTAP deletion and PRMT5 inhibition [ , , , ].
As CDKN2A loss is one of the most common genetic alterations in cancer and MTAP is located with close proximity on chromosome 9p, co-deletion of MTAP with CDKN2A occurs in 10–15 % of all human cancer and with greater frequency in tumor types such as glioblastoma, mesothelioma, pancreatic adenocarcinoma, NSCLC, and cholangiocarcinoma [ , ]. MTAP is a critical enzyme in the methionine salvage pathway, a process that recycles methionine from methylthioadenosine (MTA), the product of polyamine synthesis. MTAP loss causes the accumulation of its substrate, MTA, which is a SAM-competitive PRMT5 inhibitor [ , , ]. We hypothesized that a compound that leverages the accumulation of MTA by binding to PRMT5 in an MTA-cooperative, substrate-competitive manner would achieve selective PRMT5 inhibition in MTAP-deleted cancer cells. A compound with this selectivity for tumor cells is expected to be well tolerated and efficacious with broad therapeutic potential both alone and in combination with other therapies.
TNG908 is an oral, small molecule inhibitor of PRMT5 that binds cooperatively with MTA to selectively target MTAP-deleted cancer cells while sparing normal cells, which are MTAP-proficient. Due to its mechanism of action, TNG908 drives on-target antitumor activity selectively in MTAP-deleted xenograft models across different tumor types, including tumor regressions and complete responses. TNG908, which is brain penetrant in preclinical species, is being tested in a Phase I/II clinical study to evaluate the safety, tolerability, and preliminary antitumor activity in patients with MTAP-deleted advanced or metastatic solid tumors including glioblastoma (NCT05275478). New patients are not being recruited to this study due to portfolio prioritization.

## MATERIALS and METHODS
Full experimental details are provided in the Supplemental Materials and Methods.

## Compounds
Where indicated cell culture media also contained the following additives: AGI-41998 [ ], IDE397 (Compound A from WO2022/256806), AG-270 [ ], MRTX1719 [ ], AMG 193 [ ], TNG462 [ ], and GSK3368712 [ ] were generated by custom synthesis; MTDIA and palbociclib were obtained from MedChemExpress; abemaciclib was obtained from either MedChemExpress or Eli Lilly; and GSK3326595 [ ] and GSK3368715 [ ] were either obtained by custom synthesis or from MedChemExpress.

## MTA and SAM quantification
Cells were seeded in triplicate in 6-well plates in 1.5 mL complete cell culture media. At the indicated timepoints, one well of cells was trypsinized and counted using a ViCell counter. From each of the other two replicate wells, a sample of extracellular media was collected and stored at −80 °C and the other well was aspirated completely, and intracellular samples were prepared by scraping the wells in ice cold 80 % MeOH. Xenograft samples were prepared by extracting snap frozen tumor pieces in ice cold 80 % MeOH.
Intracellular supernatant or cell conditioned media was mixed with an internal standard solution (100 ng/mL labetalol, 100 ng/mL tolbutamide in MeOH). The mixture was vortexed, centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10–15 min at 4 °C, and supernatant was transferred to a sample plate, mixed with an equivalent volume of water, and shaken at 800 rpm for 10 min. The supernatants were injected for LC-MS/MS analysis. MTA data were normalized to a d3-MTA control and SAM data were normalized to 13C5-SAM. Absolute quantification was generated from a standard curve, and the resulting data were normalized as indicated.

## Cellular admixture assay
The HAP1 MTAP-isogenic cell line pair was engineered to either stably express RFP (HAP1 MTAP WT) or GFP (HAP1 MTAP-null) under the control of a UbiC promoter. Cellular suspensions were prepared at the indicated ratios such that 500,000 cells were suspended in 1 mL DMEM + 10 % FBS. At Day 0, each cell suspension was seeded into 12-well plates at 3000 cells/well in 2 mL DMEM + 10 % FBS in duplicate. On Day 1, cells were either treated with DMSO as a control, or 20 µM TNG-0004921 (equivalent to the GI90), an MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitor tool compound. Flow cytometry analysis was conducted at the indicated timepoints. At each timepoint, single cells were isolated using FlowJo software to exclude cell debris and doublet cells. Population percentages were determined based on event counts for GFP-positive vs RFP-positive cells. The data were then graphed using GraphPad Prism software as mean ± SEM.

## In vitro drug anchor screen
A549 (MTAP-null) cell line was transduced with CRISPRa constructs (Sigma, SAMHELPERP-1KT) to stably express dCas9-VP64 as well as the MS2-P65-HSF1 to constitute the Synergistic Activation Mediator (SAM) CRISPRa system [ ]. A single cell clone was subsequently generated and tested to ensure high CRISPRa activity. A genome wide CRISPRa library based on the Calabrese design [ ] was transfected into the picked clone at multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.4 to ensure an infection rate of one sgRNA per cell. For drug anchor screen, Day 0 started at 96 hrs post puromycin selection at which point the library infected cells were divided into 4 arms including DMSO, 100 nM TNG-0240105, 300 nM TNG-0240105 and 1 µM TNG-0240105. Screens were performed for 14 days. Each arm was subcultured routinely to avoid over-confluency and a library coverage of >1000 cells/sgRNA were maintained throughout the screen. Cells were harvested for gDNA extraction and the genome-integrated sgRNA sequences were amplified by PCR using HotStart ReadyMix (Roche, KK2602). Final Kapa Pure Beads (Roche, KK8802) purified products were sequenced on Illumina NextSeq2000 systems to achieve >1000x coverage of the library.

## Results

## Homozygous deletion of MTAP causes MTA accumulation and partial PRMT5 inhibition
Homozygous deletion of the MTAP gene co-occurs with CDKN2A loss across histologies providing a large opportunity for precision oncology application ( A). The most proximal consequence of MTAP loss is the accumulation of its substrate, methylthioadenosine (MTA) ( B). In MTAP-isogenic cell lines generated by knocking out the endogenous MTAP gene using CRISPR-based technology, time-dependent accumulation of intracellular MTA occurred solely in MTAP-deleted cell lines and to a level consistent with a cell line with endogenous deletion of MTAP and CDKN2A ( C, S1A-B, and S7A-B). As expected, exogenous MTA was rapidly metabolized by MTAP-proficient (MTAP WT) cells, whereas exogenous and endogenous MTA levels appeared to reach equilibrium in cells with MTAP loss (MTAP-null) cells suggesting bidirectional flux across the cell membrane ( D). The MTA accumulation seen in MTAP-null cells was not an artifact of in vitro cell culture as a 7-fold increase in MTA was also detected in MTAP-null xenografts relative to isogenic MTAP WT xenografts ( E and S1C). Consistent with previous reports, this accumulation was specific to MTA as alteration of intracellular SAM levels was not detected ( E) [ , , ]. The accumulation of MTA in MTAP-null cells was a direct consequence of MTAP loss as pharmacological inhibition of MTAP using the small molecule inhibitor, MT-DADMe-ImmA (MTDIA), in MTAP WT cells phenocopied MTAP loss by driving MTA accumulation relative to SAM ( F) [ ]. The accumulation of MTA competes with the PRMT5 activating cofactor, SAM, to form an inactive PRMT5·MTA complex [ , ]. PRMT5 inhibition can be monitored in cells by quantification of SDMA, which is the methyl mark conferred by type II PRMT family members of which PRMT5 is the primary type II PRMT. Evidence of basal PRMT5 inhibition in MTAP-null cells was observed both in vitro and in vivo with an average 2.5-fold reduction of SDMA-modified protein levels relative to MTAP WT isogenic controls ( G-H). 

## TNG908 is an MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitor that is selective for MTAP-null cells
We designed TNG908 to selectively bind the inactive PRMT5·MTA complex ( A-B), a mechanism of action that we hypothesized would allow it to selectively target MTAP-null cancer cells while sparing MTAP WT normal cells. In cell-free assays, the potency of TNG908 improved ∼12-fold in the presence of MTA (IC50 = 21.2 ± 9.3 nM, Ki = 0.26 nM) relative to the absence of MTA (IC50 262 ± 52 nM, Ki 3.2 nM), consistent with an MTA-cooperative binding mechanism ( C). 
To determine on-target inhibition of cellular PRMT5, an in-cell western (ICW) assay was developed in the HAP1 MTAP-isogenic cell line pair to monitor SDMA, the proximal PRMT5 pharmacodynamic (PD) biomarker. With acute compound treatment, TNG908 potently inhibited cellular SDMA levels in the HAP1 MTAP-null cell line with an IC50 of 9 nM compared to 180 nM in the HAP1 MTAP WT cell line ( D and S2A). GSK3326595 (pemrametostat) and JNJ-64617198 (onametostat) are PRMT5 inhibitors with SAM-uncompetitive or SAM-competitive mechanisms of action, respectively. They both inhibited PRMT5 equivalently in MTAP-null and MTAP WT cells, suggesting that only an MTA-cooperative inhibitor can selectively inhibit PRMT5 in MTAP-null cells ( D).
The potent inhibition of PRMT5 by TNG908, which is selective for MTAP-null cells, is also specific as we do not observe modulation of asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA) marks that are conferred by Type I PRMTs ( E and S2B). In a biochemical panel containing 38 methyltransferases, only PRMT5-containing complexes were inhibited >90 % at 10 µM ( F). These data together demonstrate that TNG908 is a potent and selective MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitor that is selectively active in MTAP-null cancer cells.
PRMT5 is an essential gene, likely due to its well-described role as a regulator of transcriptional activity, cell cycle control, spliceosome assembly, and other cellular processes. As TNG908 was able to potently inhibit PRMT5 selectively in MTAP-null cells as measured by differential SDMA modulation, we next asked whether TNG908 would also maintain that selectivity in cellular viability assays.
In 7-day viability assays using MTAP-isogenic cell lines generated by either knocking out endogenous MTAP using CRISPR technology (HAP1) or by reconstituting functional MTAP in a cell line with endogenous homozygous MTAP deletion (LN18), TNG908 inhibited cellular viability with an MTAP-null potency of 100–200 nM and 15-fold selectivity over MTAP WT cells ( A, S1B and D, and S7D-E). Consistent with the non-selective PD modulation in the SDMA ICW assay, SAM-uncompetitive and SAM-competitive PRMT5 inhibitors did not demonstrate MTAP-selective viability effects ( B). Interestingly, we noted that the GI50 closely correlated with the PD IC90, consistent with the interpretation that ≥ 90 % PRMT5 inhibition is required for loss of cellular viability (compare D to A-B) [ ]. To test whether the TNG908 selectivity would be maintained across tumor types, a cancer cell line panel comprised of ∼200 cell lines representing bladder, leukemia, lymphoma, bone, CNS and PNTs, breast, head & neck, kidney, liver, cholangiocarcinoma, NSCLC (adenocarcinoma and squamous), mesothelioma, pancreatic, melanoma, sarcoma, gastric and uterine cancers was profiled. Consistent with the selectivity and efficacy demonstrated in MTAP-isogenic cell lines, TNG908 demonstrated marked selectivity for MTAP-null cancer cell lines independent of histology whereas the SAM-uncompetitive PRMT5 inhibitor, GSK3326595, did not ( C). Dose-dependent antitumor activity was confirmed in an HCT116 MTAP-null xenograft model, whereas an isogenic HCT116 MTAP WT xenograft model was unaffected at the same dose levels confirming the selectivity of TNG908 is maintained in vivo ( D and S1C). 
In D, we showed that MTAP-proficient cells can metabolize free MTA. To determine whether the presence of MTAP WT cells could affect the efficacy of an MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitor, we performed a cellular admixture experiment wherein MTAP WT and MTAP-null cells were mixed at defined ratios and then challenged for 7 days with an MTA-cooperative PRMT5 tool inhibitor, TNG-0004921 ( E and S3A). After 5 days of compound treatment, the MTAP-null cell population is abolished and a commensurate enrichment of the MTAP WT population is detected demonstrating that even when MTAP-null cells were co-cultured with 9X more MTAP WT cells, an MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitor is still able to selectively target MTAP-null cell populations ( F and S3B). By treating MTAP WT cells with MTDIA, a small molecule MTAP inhibitor, we further determined that an MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitor only requires 2X accumulation of intracellular MTA to be active and selective, with maximal efficacy and selectivity demonstrated with a 5X accumulation of intracellular MTA ( F and G). This result is specific, as MTDIA has no effect on the efficacy of the inhibitor in an isogenic MTAP-null cell line ( H).

## TNG908 drives tumor regressions in MTAP-null xenograft models representing multiple histologies including glioblastoma
TNG908 drove dose-dependent antitumor activity in the LN18 MTAP-null cell line-derived xenograft model, which is consistent with dose-proportional free plasma concentrations and PRMT5 inhibition in a 10-day PK/PD study ( A-B). Similar to the in vitro cancer cell line panel, there was no evidence of histology bias for TNG908 efficacy in MTAP-null xenograft models representing multiple histologies ( C). Terminal PD analysis demonstrated strong PRMT5 inhibition in all MTAP-deleted xenografts assayed (Figure S4). Durable tumor regressions were noted in ∼30 % of the MTAP-deleted models at the 120 mg/kg BID dose level, including models representing bladder, cholangiocarcinoma, glioblastoma, NSCLC, mesothelioma, lymphoma, and leukemia ( C-D). TNG908-driven regressions were demonstrated in MTAP-deleted NSCLC xenograft models that were derived from the same patient, either before the initiation of clinical therapy or following three lines of therapy (taxane/platinum/bevacizumab/radiation, erlotinib, and pemetrexed), showing that even heavily pre-treated tumors respond equivalently to TNG908 ( E). Sustained tumor regressions were maintained even after the discontinuation of dosing in patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models representing NSCLC and glioblastoma, suggesting single agent TNG908 treatment cured these mice ( F). 
Approximately 40 % of all glioblastomas are MTAP-deleted, and TNG908 drove strong efficacy in MTAP-null glioblastoma models both in vitro and in vivo ( A, A, A-C and 4F), so we asked whether TNG908 could be an effective treatment for patients with MTAP-deleted glioblastoma. The intrinsic properties of TNG908, which include high passive permeability and low potential for efflux by P-gp or BCRP transporters, are consistent with the ability to penetrate the blood brain barrier ( A). In single dose non-human primate pharmacokinetic studies, the free TNG908 exposure was ∼60 % in cerebrospinal fluid, which is used as a surrogate for brain tissue in survival studies, relative to plasma ( B). To evaluate whether TNG908 maintains antitumor activity in the native tumor microenvironment, we utilized the aggressive U87MG MTAP-null glioblastoma xenograft model implanted either subcutaneously or orthotopically in the brain. Strong on-target antitumor activity was observed in the subcutaneous xenograft model, including a 55 % tumor regression at the 120 mpk BID dose level ( C-D). Even though the brain penetrance of TNG908 was determined to be a fraction (∼15 %) in mice compared to higher species (eg, non-human primates), strong antitumor activity was also demonstrated in the orthotopic model by both luminescence quantification (89 % antitumor activity on Day 20) and by extension of median survival by 53 days ( E-F and S5). 

## Identification of MAT2A and CDK4/6 inhibitors as potential combination partners with TNG908
Though single agent TNG908 activity drove strong antitumor activity including complete responses in MTAP-null xenograft models across histologies, there were models for which regressions were not observed. Therefore, we questioned whether novel therapeutic combination strategies could be identified. To address this question, we employed two strategies: 1) we leveraged our functional genomics capabilities by using a CRISPR-based editing platform to conduct an unbiased in vitro drug anchor screen to identify potential vulnerabilities, and 2) we leveraged cancer genome annotations to identify genetic alterations with targeted therapies that co-occur with MTAP deletion.
An A549 MTAP-null NSCLC cancer cell line clone with stable expression of CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) components was infected with a whole genome library and cultured for 14 days in the presence or absence of TNG-0240105, an MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitor tool compound with 100X selectivity for MTAP-null cells in a 7-day viability assay (Figure S6A). Guides targeting the MAT2A and WDR77 genes were among the most significantly enriched following TNG-0240105 treatment, demonstrating that activation of these genes promotes resistance to PRMT5 inhibition ( A). MEP50, which is encoded by WDR77, is a member of the PRMT5 methylosome complex and is required for PRMT5 activity [ , ]; therefore, it is not unexpected that upregulation of MEP50 may rescue deficient PRMT5 activity. MAT2A catalyzes the formation of the PRMT5 obligate co-factor, S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) and has also been identified as a synthetic lethal pair with MTAP deletion [ ]. As we showed that MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitors leverage the accumulation of MTA relative to SAM for their activity and selectivity, we questioned whether MAT2A upregulation would affect PRMT5 activity. Upregulation of MAT2A with either CRISPRa guides in the A549 NSCLC cell line or constitutive expression of an exogenous MAT2A cDNA in the LN18 MTAP-null GBM cell line caused an increase in SDMA-modified protein levels consistent with increased PRMT5 activity, as well as an ∼3X decrease in potency of TNG-0240105 or TNG908 ( B-C, S6B-C, and S7E and F). This potency loss was limited, as the effect of MAT2A upregulation saturated and could not approach the selectivity window set by MTAP-reconstitution (Figure S6C). As expected, MAT2A upregulation did not significantly influence intracellular MTA levels, but it was associated with a > 2X increase in intracellular SAM levels and therefore a reduction in the MTA/SAM ratio ( D-E). These data are consistent with the MTA/SAM ratio contributing to the activity and selectivity of MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitors in cells. 
As exogenously driven MAT2A upregulation decreased sensitivity to MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitors, we questioned whether upregulation of MAT2A occurs as a response to PRMT5 inhibition. Following 3 days of TNG908 treatment, there is no effect on MAT2A, PRMT5, or WDR77 expression in MTAP-null cells. Consistent with a previous report, we do observe decreased PNKP, ATM and POLD1 levels, however ( F and S6D) [ ]. Though MAT2A upregulation is not an adaptive response to PRMT5 inhibition, we questioned whether combination with a MAT2A inhibitor could synergize with MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitors. In the LN18 MTAP-null GBM cell line, combination of a MAT2A inhibitor, AG-270, with TNG908 drove a strong synergistic combination benefit in a 7-day viability assay ( G). The synergy is recapitulated in additional MTAP-null cell lines, with different MTA-cooperative PRMT5 and MAT2A inhibitors, and in vivo using an MTAP-null xenograft model demonstrating that the combination benefit is robust ( H and S6E-I). Synergy was also observed between an MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitor and a MAT2A inhibitor in an MTAP WT cell line at high concentrations (Figure S6F). Though the in vivo synergy is striking and drives a complete response at well-tolerated, but sub-therapeutic, doses of the PRMT5 and MAT2A inhibitors, a similar complete response can be accomplished with an increased dose of the MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitor itself (Figure S6J). These data demonstrate that strong antitumor activity can be driven by combination of sub-therapeutic doses of a MAT2A inhibitor and an MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitor, but also suggest that the combination may not be necessary if therapeutic doses of the PRMT5 inhibitor can be achieved.
Virtually all MTAP-deleted cancers are also CDKN2A-deleted because MTAP deletion occurs as a passenger deletion with CDKN2A loss. CDKN2A loss has been reported to increase sensitivity to CDK4/6 inhibition in multiple cancer cell lines, and others have reported potential synergy between CDK4/6 and PRMT5 inhibition [ , ]. Therefore, we questioned whether TNG908 and CDK4/6 inhibition would drive a combination benefit in MTAP-null cancer models. Combination of MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitors with either abemaciclib or palbociclib, potent and selective CDK4/6 inhibitors, drove a combination benefit selectively in MTAP-null cancer cell lines independent of tumor type ( I and S6K-L). This combination benefit translated in vivo in MTAP-null xenograft models representing NSCLC and GBM. LU99 and U87MG MTAP-null xenograft models were dosed with abemaciclib or palbociclib at clinically relevant doses, and TNG908 was dosed sub-therapeutically to allow the observation of a combination benefit. In both models, a combination benefit was observed regardless of the sensitivity of the model to single agent CDK4/6 inhibition ( J-K and S6M-N). These data underscore the potential for broad applicability for this combination across MTAP-deleted cancer types.

## Discussion
One of the strongest and most prevalent described synthetic lethal interactions is PRMT5 dependence in cells with homozygous MTAP deletion [ , , ]. This relationship represents a subset of synthetic lethality termed “collateral lethality,” which occurs when therapeutic vulnerabilities arise due to co-deletion of passenger genes proximal to tumor suppressor genes [ ]. Homozygous deletion of the chromosome 9p21 locus, which includes the tumor suppressor gene CDKN2A, occurs in approximately 15 % of cancers and frequently involves co-deletion of genes proximal to CDKN2A, including MTAP. Therefore, homozygous co-deletion of MTAP occurs in up to 90 % of tumors with CDKN2A deletion [ , ]. As a result, 10–15 % of all human cancer is predicted to have homozygous MTAP deletion, generating a broad and diverse patient population. Though PRMT5 was identified as a selective dependency in MTAP-null cells, first generation clinical PRMT5 inhibitors were designed with mechanisms of action that could not selectively target MTAP-null cancer cells relative to MTAP WT normal tissue and were therefore hindered by hematological toxicities and fatigue that limited efficacy [ , , ].
TNG908 is an MTA-cooperative, substrate-competitive PRMT5 inhibitor with 15X selectivity for MTAP-null cancer cells relative to cells that are MTAP WT. The ability of TNG908 to selectively target MTAP-null cells while sparing MTAP WT normal cells is predicted to translate to a broad therapeutic index and therefore more meaningful clinical efficacy compared to those early PRMT5 inhibitors with non-MTA-cooperative mechanisms of action such as pemrametostat (GSK3326595) and onametostat (JNJ-64619178). Indeed, early reports from different MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitors, MRTX1719 (BMS-986504) and AMG 193, show objective responses are achievable without the hematological toxicities reported with the early PRMT5 inhibitors; thereby demonstrating proof of concept for this mechanism of action [ , ]. Additional MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitors are in various stages of clinical development, including TNG462, but one clear differentiating feature of TNG908 is the strong preclinical evidence for brain penetration.
Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common malignant primary brain tumor in adults, with a poor median overall survival with standard-of-care therapies. According to The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), homozygous MTAP deletions occur in 44 % of glioblastoma, suggesting that a brain-penetrant, MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitor may be particularly important for this patient population. It is also important to note that 15 % of NSCLC (20 % squamous and 11 % adenocarcinoma) and 20 % of cutaneous melanoma are MTAP-null. NSCLC and melanoma are tumor types that frequently metastasize to the brain; therefore, a brain-penetrant MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitor may also be advantageous for their treatment. TNG908 has high passive permeability and is not a substrate for P-gp or BCRP efflux transporters. Consistent with these favorable attributes, TNG908 demonstrates in vivo brain penetration in preclinical species, including non-human primates and rodents. Due to the inconsistent levels of P-gp and other transporter protein levels between human and rodent, NHP is likely the better predictor of human brain penetration relative to rodents [ , ]. However, despite a reduced Kpuu in mice (Kpuu ∼0.15) relative to non-human primates (Kpuu ∼0.6), oral administration of TNG908 still increased median survival by 3-fold in a highly aggressive GBM orthotopic xenograft model.
A prior report suggested that MTA may not accumulate in GBM due to metabolism of free MTA by the MTAP WT cells in the MTAP-deleted tumor microenvironment [ ]. We also observe that MTA can be secreted by MTAP-null cells and metabolized by MTAP WT cells; however, further data show that even when MTAP-null cells are co-cultured with a 9X excess of MTAP WT cells, the intracellular MTA levels are still sufficient in MTAP-null cells for the activity and selectivity of an MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitor. This finding is consistent with data generated with MRTX1719, another clinical MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitor [ ]. Our data further suggest that only a 2–5X increase in intracellular MTA is necessary for the full activity and selectivity of MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitors. These data combined with the strong antitumor activity observed in the orthotopic GBM xenograft model suggest that MTAP WT cells do not significantly impact the efficacy of MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitors.
TNG908 drives strong single agent activity in MTAP-null xenograft models across tumor histologies, including sustained complete responses after discontinuation of dosing in GBM and NSCLC PDX models. It is interesting to note that ∼30 % of the MTAP-deleted xenograft models achieved regressions with TNG908 treatment, which is similar to the clinical response rate reported for another MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitor [ ]. TNG908 treatment drove strong PRMT5 inhibition in all MTAP-null models assayed, which suggests that MTA levels are not limiting in MTAP-null cancer cells. The factors that contribute to sensitivity to PRMT5 inhibition, including potential additional biomarkers, are still unknown but are under current investigation.
These data strongly support the clinical development of TNG908 for single agent therapy in MTAP-deleted solid tumors; however, we also investigated potential combination strategies that can be clinically leveraged. Combination of TNG908 with the respective standards of care for each tumor indication is rational and should be investigated clinically. Instead, this work focused on strategies to identify novel combination strategies for TNG908: combinations with targeted therapies for genetic alterations that co-occur with MTAP deletion and combinations informed by biological rationale and results from in vitro drug anchor screens.
It has been reported that CDKN2A loss may sensitize cells to CDK4/6 inhibitors [ ]. As nearly all MTAP-deleted tumors are also CDKN2A-deleted, we investigated the combination efficacy of TNG908 and CDK4/6 inhibitors. A clear combination benefit was evident in vivo in xenograft models regardless of single agent sensitivity to either abemaciclib or palbociclib. Whether this combination benefit is the result of inhibition of two parallel pathways or a mechanistic interaction between the CDK4/6-Rb and PRMT5 pathways remains unclear. Abemaciclib has been reported to be brain penetrant in clinical studies, suggesting that this combination may be particularly meaningful in MTAP-deleted, CDKN2A-deleted glioblastoma patients [ , ]. Furthermore, TCGA analysis of the tumor histologies with the highest prevalence of MTAP deletion suggests that, on the whole, other driver genetic alterations are neither enriched nor mutually exclusive with MTAP deletion [ , ]. Therefore, combination of TNG908 with other targeted agents that have orthogonal mechanisms of action may drive strong combination benefit with a broad therapeutic window due to non-overlapping toxicity.
A CRISPRa-based in vitro drug anchor screen suggested that MAT2A upregulation may reduce sensitivity to MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitors due to a reduction of the intracellular MTA/SAM ratio. Though we do not observe MAT2A upregulation as an adaptive response to PRMT5 inhibition, we hypothesized that combination of an MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitor with a MAT2A inhibitor would be synergistic. Indeed, strong synergy was observed in vitro and in vivo with multiple MTA-cooperative PRMT5 and MAT2A inhibitors. However, synergy was also demonstrated in MTAP WT cells, suggesting that this combination may be limited by on-target toxicity. Importantly, though strong synergy was demonstrated in vivo in the NCI-H838 xenograft model using sub-therapeutic doses of an MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitor, complete responses were also observed with single agent PRMT5 inhibition. These data suggest that the combination of an MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitor with a MAT2A inhibitor may enhance activity of a less active molecule or allow dose reduction of an agent with off-target limitations but is unlikely to be meaningful for an MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitor with strong single agent activity and sufficient selectivity. In August 2023, Amgen and Ideaya initiated a clinical trial to investigate the safety, tolerability, and efficacy of AMG 193, an MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitor, and IDE397, a MAT2A inhibitor, in MTAP-deleted advanced solid tumors. At the time of manuscript preparation, the results of this trial were not available (NCT05975073).
The most successful example of synthetic lethality is the clinically validated dependence of BRCA-mutant tumors on PARP activity. The FDA has approved four PARP inhibitors (olaparib, rucaparib, niraparib, and talazoparib) for use in patients with BRCA-mutant cancers demonstrating the transformative power this genetic concept may hold for the next generation of effective cancer drugs. TNG908, a PRMT5 inhibitor with an MTA-cooperative binding mechanism that distinguishes it from first generation PRMT5 inhibitors, selectively targets MTAP-deleted cancer cells while sparing normal MTAP WT cells. It drives strong and durable antitumor activity, including complete responses, in MTAP-deleted xenograft models across tumor histologies strongly supporting histology-agnostic clinical development. Though additional MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitors are also in development (eg, MRTX1719, AMG 193, TNG462, and AZD3470), TNG908 is differentiated by strong brain penetration in preclinical models and in situ antitumor activity in a highly aggressive orthotopic glioblastoma model. TNG908 is being tested clinically patients with MTAP-deleted solid tumors, including glioblastoma, in a PhI/II clinical trial (NCT05275478). New patients are not being recruited due to portfolio prioritization.
