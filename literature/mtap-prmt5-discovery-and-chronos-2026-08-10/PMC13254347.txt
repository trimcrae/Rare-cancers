Here, we combine germline Structural Variants (SVs) with mass spectrometry-based proteomics on tumors from 1637 cancer patients spanning various tumor tissues of origin to determine the extent SV breakpoint patterns involve differential protein expression of nearby genes. Rare and singleton SVs disrupting protein expression of known cancer susceptibility genes collectively involve 6% of patients. About 24% of the hundreds of genes with SV-associated non-coding cis-regulatory alterations at the mRNA level are similarly associated at the protein level. Both rare and common SVs may associate with differential protein expression within a specific tumor type or across multiple tissue types, including SVs differentially represented by patient ancestry. SVs involving altered methylation of CpG Islands or enhancers are also implicated in differential protein expression. Our results emphasize the contribution of germline SVs to cancer heterogeneity at the proteome level.

Transcriptomic and proteomic data help clarify the functional effects of inherited structural variation in cancer. Here, the authors integrate germline structural variants with tumor proteomics across 1,637 patients spanning 11 tumor types and show that a subset of variants alters protein levels, contributing to molecular diversity across cancers.

## Introduction
Structural variants (SVs) are a diverse class of genetic variation that include deletions, tandem duplications, insertions, inversions, and translocations at least 50 bp in length. In a human genome, thousands of germline SVs may be present, compared to millions of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) . At the same time, germline SVs vary widely in size and therefore typically affect an order of magnitude more nucleotides than SNPs , . Historically, SVs were initially explored using microarrays, which were limited to detecting copy-number differences of sequences involving a discrete set of array probes . The falling costs of whole-genome sequencing (WGS) have enabled SV detection to the base pair resolution, whereby all genomic regions may be sequenced to enable detection of balanced SVs and complex events in addition to copy number variants . As compared to short-read WGS, recent developments in long-read WGS allow for the resolution of certain types of SVs (especially in repetitive regions), leading to the identification of additional SVs , , though to date, its application in large-scale genomic studies has been limited by challenges in sample preparation and high costs . Germline SVs can influence gene expression across individuals, e.g., through copy number variation, gene disruption, or impacting non-coding genomic regulatory elements . Recent short-read WGS studies involving large numbers of individuals have assessed the impact of germline SVs on the human transcriptome , – . Non-coding germline SVs associated with differential expression often do not involve specific functional annotations other than their proximity to genes, and existing genome annotations seem unlikely to be informative for modeling functional variant effects . At the same time, germline SVs can influence gene expression through impacting DNA methylation , , .
RNA transcript levels of a gene are typically assumed to represent its corresponding protein levels. However, in actuality, proteome and transcriptome abundances are not sufficiently correlated to act as proxies for each other, this disparity being understood as rooted in fundamental biological regulation – . Recent technological advancements in mass spectrometry (MS)-based proteomics have enabled the profiling of more than 10,000 protein features across hundreds of samples . Whereas numerous studies involving expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) analyses have associated genotypes with gene mRNA levels across various tissue and cellular contexts (focusing almost exclusively on germline SNPs , ), the impact of germline variants on the proteome has been understudied and remains poorly understood . In the context of normal tissues, one study, involving Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project samples and data, surveyed germline SNP influences on protein variation in a few individuals across 32 tissues , while a more recent study expanded this GTEx analysis to 144 individuals in each of five normal tissues . In the context of cancer, the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) recently investigated the impact of germline SNPs and small Insertions-Deletions (indels) on the tumor proteomes of 1064 cancer patients (representing ten tumor types by tissue of origin) . The global impact of germline SVs on the proteome, for any tissue or cellular context, remains to be thoroughly investigated.
Germline variants have been heavily studied in the context of cancer. A sizable fraction of cancer is heritable , . Germline variants, including SVs, could contribute to cancer susceptibility with moderate to high penetrance . Germline variants may also contribute to tumor progression as well as to cancer risk . Studies utilizing SNP genotyping arrays and next-generation sequencing have cataloged pathogenic germline mutations in cancers , , though germline SVs are unstudied in this context. One recent study of germline SVs in neuroblastoma and Ewing sarcoma, involving cases and controls, estimated that rare SVs contribute 1.1 to 5.6% of liability for these cancers . Gene expression data can provide meaningful context to the potential roles of germline SVs in human disease. WGS combined with MS-based proteomics data currently in the public domain would cumulatively involve over 1600 tumors , – , which could be leveraged to study the global influence of germline SVs on the cancer proteome. These data provide an opportunity to characterize a class of normal variation across individuals in the context of diseased tissues spanning various tumor tissues of origin. Most of the genes impacted at the proteome level by germline SVs here would represent normal phenotypic variation (e.g., involving patient ancestry), though a fraction of the SVs involved could have potential relevance to cancer. We recently surveyed the influence of germline SVs on the transcriptome and DNA methylome of human tumors , , , though an analogous study involving the cancer proteome remained to be carried out.
In this present study, we combined patient germline SV data (taken from a normal blood sample) with tumor proteomic profiling and other multi-omics data across different cancer types to determine to what extent germline SV patterns impacting genes at the mRNA level are reflected at the protein level. By design, our study considered both common and rare SVs. Using an established analytical framework , , – , we systematically cataloged gene-level associations with altered expression—by either mRNA or protein—in conjunction with nearby SV breakpoints. We also explored the influence of germline SVs on DNA methylation and its downstream influences on protein expression. Many of the germline SV-expression associations identified span tumors from various tissues of origin, while associations specific to tumor type were also identified. We could also identify rare and singleton germline SVs disrupting important gene classes and cancer susceptibility genes, as well as germline SVs impacting genes essential for cancer cell proliferation or associated with patient survival. Broadly, our study demonstrates that observed germline SV-protein associations are much more sparse than germline SV-mRNA associations and can therefore be used to increase confidence as to which associations are more likely to be biologically relevant.

## Results

## Compendium of germline SVs and protein expression
For a total of 1637 patients, we assembled a comprehensive dataset combining WGS data (from the blood normal sample) and gene expression data (from the tumor sample). We compiled these data from multiple public sources (Supplementary Data  ) , – —involving CPTAC, TCGA, Children’s Brain Tumor Network (CBTN), and Applied Proteogenomic OrganizationaL Learning and Outcomes (APOLLO). Of the 1637 tumors profiled for proteomics using MS-based methods, 1596 had mRNA expression by RNA sequencing, and 1282 had DNA methylation array data. The combined proteomics-WGS dataset involved 11 tumor types by tissue of origin (Fig.  ). For the collated proteomic and transcriptomic datasets, we normalized expression values within each tumor type, whereby neither tissue-dominant differences nor inter-laboratory batch effects would influence the downstream analyses , , – . 
The entire proteomics combined dataset comprised 15,428 genes with proteins measured in at least one tumor, including 10,742 genes with proteins represented in at least 400 tumors profiled (Fig.  ). The issue of missing values is a known limitation of MS-based proteomics . In our case, genes for which mRNA expression was significantly associated with germline SVs in our previous studies , —genes including ADGRG7, CRYBB2P1, NBPF3, and NUTM2B—had too few tumors with proteomic data to be meaningfully evaluated in our 1637-patient cohort. As anticipated , protein expression of genes broadly correlated with the corresponding mRNA levels across tumors (Fig.  ), although not with a high degree of correlation for most genes (median Pearson’s r-value of 0.41). Due to this discordance between protein and mRNA, genes with SV-associated expression at the mRNA level may not be statistically significant at the protein level, as explored below. We deposited the proteomic data into the UALCAN cancer data analysis portal (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu) , .
As an avenue for exploring germline structural variation in the context of protein expression, we assembled a germline SV call set for the 1637 patients, based on short-read WGS of the patient’s blood normal sample. Across the 1637 patients, 704,263 distinct germline SVs were detected after filtering for calls made by both of two algorithms (consistent with the practice of other studies , , , and allowing 200 bp of slop at each breakpoint , Supplementary Data  ), involving 3.3 million SV detection events in all (each event representing the detection of a specific SV in a specific patient). Most of these germline SVs had been observed elsewhere, with 73% of SV detection events involving SVs represented in the Database of Genomic Variants (DGV) curated from published studies , 58% of events represented in the gnomAD high-quality SV catalog based on WGS analysis of 63,046 individuals , 55% of events represented in TOPMed SV catalog based on 138,134 individuals , and 84% of events represented in any one of the above SV catalogs (Figures  and and Supplementary Data  ). Of the 704,263 distinct SVs, most—86%—were found in a single patient, with the remaining SVs being “recurrent”, i.e., found in more than one patient. The recurrent SVs represented 82% of all SV detection events. The frequency of germline SVs in our proteomic-WGS cohort broadly correlated with allele frequencies (AFs) as reported in gnomAD (Figure  ), with ~96% of gnomAD SVs having AF < 1%. Ancestry-enriched SVs catalogued elsewhere, e.g., in TOPMed, broadly showed similar patterns of representation by ancestry in our proteomic-WGS cohort (Figure  ). Of SVs in our cohort reported in gnomAD, a very small fraction—about 0.13%—were detected in >10% of our patients but with gnomAD AF < 1% (Supplementary Data  ); however, we make no conclusions here regarding their possible disease relevance in the absence of future case-control studies. Consistent with our previous studies , , the copy number landscape in tumors is more heavily influenced by somatic SVs than by germline SVs, where germline duplication or deletion SVs spanning genes often do not manifest as corresponding gene-level copy alterations (Figure  ).

## Germline SVs involving loss of gene function
We examined rare and singleton germline SVs in our call set, as some of these might represent mutations leading to loss of gene function. Such deleterious germline SVs may confer risk to certain types of cancer . In our proteomic-WGS cohort, we examined 114,684 germline SV events involving rare (AF < 1%) or singleton SVs (i.e., SVs detected for just one patient) having a breakpoint within a gene (each event involving SV detection in a single patient), and we evaluated which of these were predicted to result in loss of function of that gene (by SVAnnotate ). Of the 114,684 SV detection events (representing 3.4% of all events), 25,781 were predicted to result in loss of function of the associated gene, with 12,442 of these SV events involving both mRNA and protein data being available for the same tumor samples in which these germline SVs were identified. Of these 12,442 SV events, 1847 involved lower expression of the gene by both protein and mRNA (<−0.4 SD from the sample median for each analyte), representing a highly significant overlap between loss-of-function SV events and low gene expression events (3E-10, chi-squared test, Fig.  and Supplementary Data  ) and illustrating the importance of incorporating expression data loss of function predictions. 
The 1847 SV events involved 512 genes and 786 of the 1632 patients. Enriched gene categories for the 512 genes (Fig.  ) included “MHC protein complex” (eight genes), “antigen binding” (12 genes), “extracellular exosome” (102 genes), “protein transport” (55 genes), “ATP binding” (67 genes), “mitochondrial matrix” (24 genes), and “Golgi apparatus part” (47 genes). When examining 688 manually-curated Cancer Susceptibility Genes (CSGs) involving various cancer types , we found 31 of these to involve the 512 with loss of function SVs, involving 101 (6%) of the 1637 patients and spanning multiple tumor types, with most of the SVs being present in just one or two patients (Fig.  ). These CSGs included CDH13, CDKN2A, MSR1, SDHA, SMAD4, and SMARCB1. Similarly, 194 patients (12%) harbored rare SVs impacting Golgi apparatus-related genes (Fig.  ). With genes involving the mitochondrial matrix being significantly enriched within our top 512 genes (Fig.  ), rare SVs impacting 24 genes in this category involved 68 patients (4%, Fig.  ). Consistent with previous reports , we found that disrupting SVs events for the 512 genes tended to involve higher average tissue-specific expression for the given gene associated with the cancer tissue of origin (p < 1E-20 by paired t-test, Supplementary Data  and Methods).

## Gene-level SV-associated protein alterations
We set out to systematically identify genes for which the proximity of germline SVs was recurrently and significantly associated with differential expression in the tumor sample, at the protein and mRNA levels, which may involve non-coding regions and cis-regulatory alterations , , . Here, we tested for associations between germline SVs (identified from normal DNA) and gene expression levels in the corresponding tumors (such associations not involving somatic SVs or variant-containing transcripts). Using previously demonstrated integration approaches between SVs and expression , , – , we assessed gene-level associations between tumor expression—both protein and mRNA—and nearby germline SV breakpoints across the proteomic-WGS cohort. Briefly (see Methods), a gene-by-sample matrix of SV breakpoint patterns was first constructed for each of a series of genomic region windows in relation to genes (e.g., SV breakpoints falling within the region 100 kb upstream of a given gene). Then, for each gene, the correlation between the breakpoint pattern and gene expression across samples was assessed for both the mRNA and protein datasets, using linear models to incorporate relevant covariates (Figure  ).
We observed hundreds of genes showing significantly altered gene expression—at either the protein or mRNA levels (False Discovery Rate , or FDR < 10%, linear model)—in relation to nearby germline SV breakpoints (relative to patients without breakpoints). SV breakpoints associated with altered expression include those located downstream or upstream of genes or occurring in the gene body (Fig.  and Figures  - and Supplementary Data  and ). These significant SV-expression associations spanned the tumor tissue of origin, as the entire 1637-patient cohort was analyzed here, with expression data normalized within tumor type and the linear model incorporating tumor type as a covariate. At the mRNA level, the top SV-associated altered genes in our 1637-patient cohort overlapped highly with those previously identified using GTEx and PCAWG cohorts , which respectively represent a variety of normal tissues and human tumors (Figures  and , overlap p = 2.4E-7 and p = 7E-14, respectively, by one-sided Fisher’s exact test, for positive SV-expression associations). For either mRNA or protein levels, a set of 364 recurrently altered genes by SV breakpoints combined with expression differences, with FDR of <10%, were identified across the set of region windows examined relative to genes: 100 kb upstream, 100 kb downstream, within the gene, and 1 Mb upstream or downstream. The 1 Mb window was considered as genomic elements such as enhancers may impact genes as far as ~1 Mb away . Also, SVs within genes would not necessarily directly disrupt them but instead may impact introns or individual exons in ways that increase rather than decrease gene expression. 
Only a fraction of the genes significant for a given region at the mRNA level were significant at the protein level (Fig.  –c). When comparing gene-level SV associations common between mRNA and protein, we utilized relaxed statistical criteria to lower false negatives, consistent with previous studies , , e.g., so as not to exclude significant mRNA associations where the association was at least nominally significant for protein. In addition, we generated two sets of gene-level SV-mRNA associations (Supplementary Data  ), using both the complete mRNA dataset with all available expression data and a “filtered” mRNA dataset with any values for a given patient and gene that were not represented in the protein dataset filtered out. With the filtered mRNA dataset, any disparate results observed between protein and mRNA should have less to do with diminished power for some genes due to lack of detection by proteomics. Out of 10,742 genes, 755 had a significant SV association across the four gene region windows (p < 0.01, linear model) at the mRNA level using the unfiltered dataset, of which just 129 (17%) had a corresponding association for the same region window at the protein level (p < 0.05), this overlap being highly statistically significant (chance expected overlap of 16 genes, one-sided Fisher’s exact test p < 1E-30 for over-expressed genes, p < 1E-49 for under-expressed genes). When using the filtered mRNA dataset, 665 genes had SV associations at the mRNA level (p < 0.01), of which 157 (24%) were concordant by protein analysis (p < 0.05). Alternatively, using a more stringent statistical cutoff for protein, at p < 0.01, with a less stringent cutoff for mRNA, at p < 0.05 for the filtered dataset, a relatively higher percentage—33%—of the 446 statistically significant proteins were also significant by mRNA, and so significant protein-level associations were more likely to be significant by mRNA versus checking mRNA-level associations at the protein level, which may reflect in part some of the mRNAs involved not being translated into protein. Any observed discordances between protein and mRNA can stem in part from biological factors (e.g., post-transcriptional regulation) and statistical false negatives (and conceivably from measurement error or technical sensitivity in some instances), where interpreting a null statistical result is inherently difficult and would not necessarily prove that no association exists. All other mRNA-related results reported below rely on the unfiltered mRNA dataset.
For a set of 129 genes with significant SV-associations for both protein and mRNA (p < 0.01 for mRNA, using unfiltered mRNA dataset, p < 0.05 for protein, same region window), these genes were enriched for specific gene categories (Fig.  ), including “regulation of cholesterol storage” (involving CD36, CES1, MSR),’ dicarboxylic acid metabolic process’ (involving ACLY, HAAO, KYAT3, and ME3), and “monocarboxylic acid metabolic process” (12 genes). In contrast, the 627 genes with significant SV-association for mRNA but not protein (mRNA p < 0.01, protein p > 0.05) were enriched for other gene categories, including “dopamine receptor binding” and “RNA binding”. The 129 concordant genes had higher mRNA-protein correlations on average across the 1637 tumors than the 627 discordant genes (average Pearson’s r of 0.51 versus 0.37, p < 1E-15 by t-test on Fisher Z-transformed r-values). Of the 129 concordant genes, only one (USP8) was cancer-associated by COSMIC, and only one gene (SIDT2) was among the 201 genes previously involved in concordant mRNA and protein associations with somatic SV breakpoints . Other example genes with germline SV-expression associations being concordant for both mRNA and protein analytes include CA8 (carbonic anhydrase 8, Fig.  ), involving a highly recurrent SV upstream of the gene (DGV:nsv4168578 ), and PTGR1 (prostaglandin reductase 1, Fig.  ), involving a highly recurrent SV within the gene (DGV:esv3621492). As with the above genes, the observed SV-associated differences in expression may appear subtle, consistent with normal phenotypic variation, where such small molecular changes over the patient’s life may conceivably have an effect. For most of the 129 genes, the SV-expression association could not be explained entirely by patient ancestry (Figure  ), though the underlying germline SVs often had patterns enriched by ancestry, as explored further below.

## Impact of SVs on DNA methylation
Germline SVs have been associated with DNA methylation differences across individuals, thereby influencing gene transcription , . Here we examined how germline SVs associated with differential methylation of CpG Islands (CGIs) in our proteomic-WGS cohort and how the related mRNA differences of the nearby genes appeared translated at the protein level. Of the 1637 patients in our cohort, 1282 had the tumor profiled for DNA methylation (involving eight of the 11 major tumor types). At the DNA methylation level, a set of 1237 CGI probes associated with recurrently SV-altered DNA methylation (using FDR < 10%) were identified across the set of region windows examined relative to genes: 100 kb upstream, 100 kb downstream, within the gene, and 1 Mb upstream or downstream (Fig.  and Supplementary Data  ). 
For the genes involving germline SV-CGI methylation associations, only a fraction showed concordant associations with nearby SV breakpoints at the mRNA or protein levels, with an even smaller fraction of concordant proteins as compared to concordant mRNAs (Fig.  , b). We examined the overlap between top SV-associated CGI probes and the related genes with corresponding SV-expression associations (in the inverse direction) at either mRNA or protein levels (Fig.  ), which should represent classical gene silencing relationships . Using a p value cutoff of <0.01 (linear model, any gene region window), 2686 CGI methylation probes were positively correlated with nearby SV breakpoints, of which 233 probes involved genes negatively correlated between mRNA expression and SV breakpoints (p < 0.05, same region window), a significant overlap (p < 1E-12, chi-squared test), with just 28 of the 233 probes also showing inverse correlation (p < 0.05) between protein and SV breakpoints across the 1282 tumors. Similarly, of 1124 CGI probes with negative SV-methylation associations (p < 0.01, linear model), 82 had a positive SV-mRNA expression association (p < 0.05 linear model, p < 1E-6 for feature overlap), with 15 of these having a coordinate SV-protein association. Genes showing inverse relationships between CGI methylation and expression (both mRNA and protein) involving germline SVs include CES1 (carboxylesterase 1), GGACT (gamma-glutamylamine cyclotransferase), and PTDSS2 (phosphatidylserine synthase 2), the first two genes involving SV-associated lower methylation and higher expression (Fig.  , d) and PTDSS2 involving SV-associated higher methylation and lower expression (Fig.  ).
In addition to methylation at CGIs, DNA methylation at distal enhancer regions has been implicated in gene regulation – . For each of 30,292 enhancers , we determined its methylation association with nearby germline SV breakpoints within 100 kb of the enhancer. We found 109 enhancers with an SV-methylation association significant at FDR < 10% (linear model, Fig.  and Supplementary Data  ), 67 of which showed lower methylation with breakpoints. Of the 109 significant enhancers in our proteomic-WGS cohort, 41 were significant (p < 0.01) in the same direction in the CBTN pediatric brain tumor cohort of 1292 patients that we analyzed previously for germline SVs and methylation (p < 1E-22 for both higher and lower feature sets, one-sided Fisher exact test). For the top enhancers from our proteomic-WGS cohort with significant SV-methylation association (p < 0.01 by linear model, involving>2 patients), we examined the genes within 500 kb of the enhancer for SV-expression association involving the SV breakpoint pattern surrounding the enhancer (p < 0.05). About 5% of genes within 500 kb of the enhancer show SV-expression associations in the inverse direction at the mRNA level (Figs.  a and ), with 13 of these genes having SV-protein expression associations concordant with mRNA (Fig.  ). Genes with consistent and concordant SV associations at the enhancer methylation, mRNA, and protein levels included ECHDC1 (Fig.  ) and SH3GLB2 (Fig.  ), each involving higher enhancer methylation and lower expression in conjunction with nearby SV breakpoints and multiple recurrent germline SVs. In addition to the above DNA methylation associations, deletion SVs spanning enhancer regions were associated with lower expression for three genes, including MAMDC2 (Figure  ). 

## Tumor type-specific SV associations
The impact of germline variants on the regulation of specific genes is highly dependent on tissue and cellular context , . The SV-expression associations described above represent pan-tissue associations spanning various tumor tissues of origin. In addition, we conducted analyses within tumor subgroups by tumor type, focusing on tumor types with more than 100 patients and combining head-and-neck and lung squamous cancer patients into a single squamous tumor type , . A limitation with the tumor type-specific analyses is the lower number of patients, which impacted statistical power. Still, within each tumor tissue type, we identified widespread SV-expression associations that exceeded the number expected due to multiple testing (Fig.  –c). Many tissue-specific SV-expression associations identified were not significant in the pan-tissue analyses (Fig.  ). We focused on the tissue-specific SV-expression associations that were consistent for both mRNA and protein analytes (p < 0.01 for each, linear model), using the gene region window with the best p value from the mRNA analyses when integrating protein and mRNA results (Fig.  , c). For each tumor type analyzed, the top SV-mRNA associations significantly overlapped with the top SV-protein associations (Fig.  ). 
A top set of 137 genes exhibited tissue-specific SV-expression associations significant for both mRNA and protein in the same direction (with p < 0.01 for each, linear model), only 17 of these genes being similarly significant in pan-tissue analyses (Fig.  and Supplementary Data  ). Similarly, a top set of 60 CGI probes involved tissue-specific SV-CGI methylation associations (p < 0.05) with concordant SV-expression associations significant for both mRNA and protein (p < 0.01) in the opposite direction from methylation (Fig.  ). Tissue-specific SV-expression associations, involving both protein and mRNA, included CDK2 (glioblastoma), RHOH (lung adenocarcinoma), and ARIH1 (renal, Fig.  ). When considering SV breakpoint patterns for each of the four gene region windows (100 kb upstream, 100 kb downstream, within the gene, and 1 Mb up- or downstream), up to thousands of genes had SV breakpoints significantly overrepresented according to tumor type (Figure  and Supplementary Data  ), suggesting potential roles of germline SVs in delineating tumor type-specific differences. At the same time, very few genes had SV-expression associations—at both mRNA and protein levels—corresponding to these enrichment patterns, either involving pan-tissue SV-expression associations (12 genes in all, Figure  ) or tumor type-specific SV-expression associations corresponding to the enriched tumor type (12 genes in all, Figure  ).

## SV associations involving patient ancestry
Widespread genetic differences are associated with ancestry . In our proteomic-WGS cohort, unsupervised analysis of germline SV breakpoint patterns revealed an overall separation between patients based on their ancestry (Fig.  and Figure  ), involving each of the major categories of patient ancestry—African, European, and Asian—represented by our cohort. We also found widespread differences in tumor gene expression across patients based on ancestry, independent of the tumor tissue of origin, with the mRNA-level correlates of ancestry significantly overlapping with the protein-level correlates (Fig.  and Supplementary Data  ). When considering SV breakpoint patterns for each of the four gene region windows (100 kb upstream, 100 kb downstream, within the gene, and 1 Mb up- or downstream), up to thousands of genes had SV breakpoints significantly overrepresented involving each of the three patient ancestry groupings (Figure  ). We carried out the same SV breakpoint patterns enrichment analysis by ancestry in the PCAWG cohort of 1218 tumors with germline SV data , where on the order of half of the top significant genes from the proteomic-WGS cohort overlapped with the top genes of the PCAWG cohort corresponding to the same ancestral group and gene region window (Figure  and Supplementary Data  ). 
Furthermore, we found that a large proportion of the genes with concordant SV-mRNA and SV-protein associations, as cataloged above, involved SV breakpoint patterns significantly enriched in one of the three major patient ancestry groupings. Of the 69 genes with SV-expression associations at the p < 0.01 significance level (linear model) and in the same direction for both protein and mRNA, 31 genes also involving significant enrichment of SV breakpoints by patient ancestry for the same region window, as consistently identified across both proteomic-WGS and PCAWG patient cohorts (Fig.  and Figure  ). Of the 31 genes, ten—including FSCN1 (Fig.  )—had breakpoints enriched within patients of African ancestry, eight—including TIGAR (Fig.  )—had breakpoints enriched within patients of Asian ancestry, and 13—including CTSW (Fig.  )—had breakpoints enriched within patients of European ancestry. While SV breakpoint patterns involving a given gene may be enriched for a particular ancestry grouping, the breakpoint patterns for that gene can involve other ancestry groupings, as observed for the above three genes. Of the above 69 genes, only one, SIDT2, did not have a significant SV-expression association by either protein or mRNA when patient ancestry was incorporated as a covariate into the model (Supplementary Data  ), meaning that patient ancestry alone could not entirely explain the SV-expression associations for the other genes, even where SV breakpoint enrichment patterns by ancestry were involved.

## SV associations involving essential genes and patient survival
While most genes with germline SV-associated differential expression would presumably have no role in cancer, we further examined our top genes with positive SV-expression association by both protein and mRNA for genes having relevance to cancer. Here, we turned to two external gene sets: genes essential for cancer cell growth and genes with mRNA associated with worse patient outcomes in pan-cancer analysis. For the former, we examined the Cancer Dependency Map (DepMap) CRISPR assays measuring the essentiality of each gene for each of 1,070 cancer cell lines, where a low DepMap-based gene effect score (<-0.75) for a given gene in a cell line indicated that the cell line is dependent on the gene for proliferation in vitro. For the latter, accessed our previously compiled catalog of pan-cancer mRNA correlates of overall survival transcending tumor lineage across 10,271 patients, involving multiple cancer types, while correcting for cancer-type-intrinsic survival differences . When we intersected our top SV-expression correlates with each of these external cancer-relevant gene sets, we identified three genes—FABP5, NEDD1, and TOP2A—that spanned all three sets (Fig.  ). 
Neural precursor cell expressed developmentally down-regulated 1 gene, or NEDD1, has recently been found to promote the development of lung adenocarcinoma in mice . Topoisomerase IIα gene, or TOP2A, is essential for cell division, highly expressed during mitosis, and required for mitotic chromosome condensation and segregation across multiple cancer types . Both NEDD1 and TOP2A showed significant prognostic associations across multiple cancer types and external datasets (Figs.  b-d), where the risk stratifications involving NEDD1 were less pronounced than those for TOP2A, a well-established cancer-associated gene. Germline SV breakpoints within the region 100 kb upstream of NEDD1 involved just three patients, two of them involving head and neck cancer (Fig.  ), with the germline SVs not catalogued elsewhere. In contrast, germline SVs associated with higher TOP2A expression included three commonly occurring SVs with breakpoints spanning the 1 Mb region surrounding the gene and involving a quarter of patients (Fig.  ).

## Discussion
Protein data, in addition to gene transcription data, can add another dimension in assessing the relevance of non-coding germline variants. Nearly all studies to date have relied on mRNA profiling data for associating SNPs or SVs with differential expression of nearby genes. Combined MS-based proteomic and germline WGS data on appreciable numbers of human specimens have only recently become available. Here, we found that only about 20-25% of the genes associated with germline SV-associated cis-regulatory alterations at the mRNA level were similarly associated at the protein level. We see this level of overall concordance between the two analytes even when considering the issue of missing data involving the proteomic platform. For many genes, protein expression may not be detectable in most samples. Nevertheless, for most genes, the MS-based proteomic platform could greatly aid in prioritizing the thousands of associations between germline variants and mRNA expression observed in countless other studies involving various tissue and cellular contexts. Future studies involving larger patient and sample numbers will greatly facilitate our further establishing germline SV-expression associations that would be consistently observed across multiple cohorts and datasets. Here we observed SV-expression associations that span multiple tissue types as well as associations within a specific tissue or tumor type. Profiling greater numbers of samples for protein levels would provide greater statistical power, particularly for tissue-specific analyses . In addition, long-read WGS could detect additional germline SVs not detectable by short-read WGS, e.g., SVs spanning problematic regions including long tandem repeats and segmental duplications , , and additional germline SV-expression associations would likely be uncovered using long-read WGS across hundreds of individuals. Cause-and-effect relationships and the mechanisms underlying these SV-expression associations broadly remain to be established, though our findings point to DNA methylation and enhancers as playing a role for some genes.
In our patient cohort, most of the genes observed here as being impacted at the proteome level by germline SVs would represent normal phenotypic variation across individuals (e.g., involving patient ancestry). These associations remain to be explored across normal human tissues (e.g., using the GTEx data resources ). Based on our observations involving GTEx-based associations at the mRNA level, we expect a large fraction of our SV-protein associations to be represented in normal as well as diseased tissues. At the same time, some of the SV associations identified in our patient cohort may have potential relevance to cancer. For example, we could identify rare and singleton germline SVs disrupting cancer susceptibility genes and important gene classes involving mitochondria and Golgi apparatus, for which protein data could confirm loss of expression corresponding to the SV having predicted loss of gene function. Cumulatively, the gene classes impacted by rare, gene-disrupting SVs could involve a substantial fraction of patients, e.g., 6% of patients for known cancer susceptibility genes. Our catalog of SV-expression associations at the protein level serves as a resource for future case-control studies that may link a particular germline SV with cancer risk, as in the absence of an SV-expression association the potential role of any SVs significantly associated with cancer may be unclear. Interestingly, one recent case-control study of germline SVs in neuroblastoma and Ewing sarcoma did not identify any common variant as associated with disease but similarly identified rare and singleton germline SVs leading to loss of gene function in certain gene categories as being significant . Understanding the role of specific germline SVs in cancer can aid in our identifying additional genes and associated processes as relevant to the biology of cancer.
While there is interest in studying germline variants for their potential to be cancer-predisposing, emerging studies now suggest that germline variants can contribute to tumor progression as well as cancer risk . Individual germline variants involving genes with well-established cancer roles—such as CDH1, CD44, MTAP, and ERBB4—may be associated with patient outcomes . Unbiased screens may also identify associations of germline variants and patient outcomes involving genes not previously tied to tumorigenesis , , . Much of the focus here has been on SNPs within gene coding regions, though patient survival associations may also involve SVs in noncoding regions. We might expect pathogenic germline variants having high penetrance to be relatively uncommon in the human population . Examples of this from our present study would include singleton SVs within genes or rare SVs with cis-regulatory impacts on NEDD1. On the other hand, another class of germline variants may exist that would not necessarily contribute to cancer development but instead contribute to patient outcomes, including common variants. One example of this uncovered by our study is TOP2A, a gene with a well-established role in cancer cell proliferation and strong expression associations with patient outcomes, but for which the associated germline SVs involving higher gene expression collectively represented ~30% of patients in our cohort. We would anticipate that elevated TOP2A expression levels, whether arising from somatic or germline variation, could contribute to disease progression, even when the disease had an underlying cause not involving TOP2A. Common germline variants with disease-contributing roles remain an understudied area, one for which protein data, in addition to mRNA data, may offer insights into the disease.

## Methods

## Patient cohorts
The results here are based upon data generated by the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC), the Applied Proteogenomic OrganizationaL Learning and Outcomes (APOLLO) research network, the Children’s Brain Tumor Network (CBTN), and the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) Research Network. Tumor molecular profiling data and sequencing data from paired blood normal were generated through informed consent as part of the efforts of each of the above consortiums and analyzed here per their data use guidelines and restrictions. We assembled a comprehensive dataset of combined blood normal WGS and tumor gene expression data (by protein or RNA) on 1637 human patients. The cancer types represented in the proteomics combined dataset were the following: Breast Invasive Carcinoma (n = 97 patients with combined WGS and proteomics data) , colorectal adenocarcinoma (n = 43) , glioblastoma (n = 198) , , head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (n = 108) , lung adenocarcinoma (n = 171) , , lung squamous cell carcinoma (n = 105) , ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma (n = 117) , pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (n = 137) , pediatric brain tumors (n = 181) , , renal cell carcinoma (n = 258) – , and uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (n = 222) , . For some patients from the CBTN pediatric brain tumor cohort, multiple tumors taken from the same patient were profiled for gene expression , , , in which case one tumor was randomly selected to represent the patient in our present study (Supplementary Data  ). Where noted, we compared results from our proteomic-WGS combined datasets with results from combined tumor mRNA and germline SV datasets (n = 1218 patients) generated by the PCAWG consortium .

## Germline structural variant (SV) datasets
For each of the 1637 patients in our study, we compiled germline SV calls based on short-read WGS of the blood normal sample. Except for the CBTN pediatric brain tumor patients, we downloaded WGS Binary Alignment Map (BAM) files of the patient blood normal sample from the Genome Data Commons (GDC) . Using the GDC BAMs, we made germline SV calls using Delly (v.3.1.1) and SVABA(v1.2.0) , which were the SV callers used by PCAWG and our previous study of PCAWG data . For the pediatric brain tumor patients, the CBTN made germline SV calls using Manta (v1.4.0) and SVABA (v1.1.0) algorithms . The hg38 reference was used for SV calling of all patients. We used only germline SV calls that passed quality filters in the analyses. For each patient, germline SV calls from the two calling algorithms used were pairwise joined based on SV position, allowing 200 bp slop at each breakpoint. We required two algorithms to make the SV calls independently, as this would result in lower false positive SV calls for our final dataset, as demonstrated in studies involving somatic mutation calling , . For this merged germline SV call set, we used the Delly (GDC) or Manta (CBTN) SV genomic coordinates throughout the study. Delly and Manta algorithms classified each SV call as one of the following: tandem duplications, insertions, deletions, inversions, and translocations. A small percentage of germline SV calls represented translocations, which in practice are often false positives caused by misalignments of repeat expansions . Therefore, we removed all germline translocation SV calls from the analyses. We did not require the two algorithms to make same SV type calls, though the type calls made by Delly or Manta were largely consistent with the type catalogued elsewhere, including DGV (Supplementary Data  ). For the CBTN SV calls, we filtered out of the merged germline SV call set any SVs that were overrepresented in either the first CBTN cohort or the later CBTN-X01 cohort , to minimize any potential batch effects .
Rare and singleton SVs were defined in our call set as SVs with either AF < 1% (CBTN, gnomAD, and TOPMed cohorts) or occurring in a single patient. For the subset of these SVs for which one breakpoint fell within a gene boundary, we used SVAnnotate of the GATK-SV pipeline to identify SVs with predicted loss of function (e.g., the deletion overlaps any coding sequence of the transcription start site). The use of the <−0.4 SD cutoff for low expression in the related analyses is somewhat arbitrary but is in line with similar cutoffs used in our previous studies for assessing notably higher or lower expression of a gene in a sample – , , . In one analysis, we focused on 688 manually curated Cancer Susceptibility Genes involving various cancer types (accessed at csgs.sequenxe.com in May 2024).

## Proteomic datasets
We assembled a combined dataset of MS-based proteomics data of primary tumors from previous studies (Supplementary Data  ) , – . Of the 1637 tumors with proteomic data, 1551 were from CPTAC-led studies and 86 were from the APOLLO-led adenocarcinoma study . The above studies analyzed the tumors using global proteomic and phosphoproteomic profiling by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). We obtained processed protein expression data from the CPTAC Data Portal , the Protein Data Commons [https://pdc.cancer.gov/pdc/], or the associated publications’ supplementary tables. Proteomic data, as collected from the public domain, were processed at the gene level rather than at the protein isoform level; as a simplification, we did not consider different isoforms for the same protein in the present study. The colorectal proteomic dataset, obtained from the supplemental data , involved imputed values, while for the other studies we obtained non-imputed values.
Most of the proteomic data in the proteomics-WGS combined datasets of our present study were previously assembled and analyzed in our previous proteomics studies , , , with the addition here of data from the more recent studies for glioblastoma , uterine , and renal . For each study, taking the logged expression values provided in the associated data table, we normalized proteomic data for downstream analyses in the following manner and as previously described , , , , . For the profiles taken from the CPTAC data portal, we first normalized expression values to standard deviations from the median within each proteomic profile. We then normalized expression values across samples to standard deviations from the median for all profiles. For datasets where two different data centers generated values on the same tumors, we averaged normalized values from the respective data centers in instances of duplicate profiles for the same tumor sample. As intended, by normalizing expression within each cancer type and each proteomic dataset, neither tissue-dominant differences nor inter-laboratory batch effects would drive the downstream analysis results. The combined dataset of total proteins included the 15,428 unique genes by Entrez Identifier that were represented in our previous proteomics combined dataset of 2002 tumors .

## Transcriptomic datasets
For CPTAC projects utilizing tumors from TCGA, we obtained TCGA data RNA-seq data from the Broad Institute’s Firehose data portal [https://gdac.broadinstitute.org], and we obtained RNA-seq data for the other CPTAC projects from the GDC. We obtained RNA-seq data on CBTN pediatric brain tumors through the public project on the Kids First Data Resource Portal and Cavatica [https://cbtn.org]. We obtained data for the APOLLO project from links provided with the associated publication . We normalized expression values across samples to standard deviations from the median within each cancer type and dataset, as we carried out above for proteomic data.

## Copy-number alteration (CNA) datasets
All but three tumors in our study had corresponding gene-level CNA data. For CPTAC projects utilizing tumors from TCGA, we obtained SNP array-based CNA “thresholded” values (−2, −1, 0, 1, 2) from the Broad Institute’s Firehose data portal [https://gdac.broadinstitute.org]. We obtained gene-level absolute copy data (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5) for the other CPTAC projects and the APOLLO dataset from the Genome Data Commons [https://gdc.cancer.gov/]. We first normalized the absolute copy data according to ploidy (dividing gene copy value by average copy value for all genes), then thresholded to values approximating homozygous deletion (−2), heterozygous deletion (−1), wild-type (0), gain of 1–2 copies ( + 1), and amplification with at least 5 copies (2). We obtained gene-level copy data from CBTN pediatric brain tumors from Cavatica [https://cbtn.org] and thresholded similarly to the CPTAC copy data. For the APOLLO cohort, gene-level log(tumor-normal) values were obtained from the GDC publication page and then thresholded to amplification, gain, wild-type, loss, or deletion.

## DNA methylation datasets
For 1103 tumors, DNA methylation profiles had been generated by CPTAC using the Illumina Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChip array platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA), with level3 beta values being made available via the Genome Data Commons . For 179 tumors, DNA methylation profiles had been generated by CBTN on the same array platform , , for which we previously obtained and processed raw IDAT image files from the CBTN Cavatica site . Illumina EPIC probe annotation was based on hg19 coordinates. When overlapping methylation array probe coordinates to SV calls based on hg38, we first used the UCSC Genome Browser LiftOver tool to convert the probe coordinates to hg38. For CGI analyses, our study focused on the 118,906 Illumina array probes falling within CGIs. For each of 30,292 enhancers, we mapped the nearest DNA methylation probe (within 20 kb), utilizing the enhancer annotations provided by Kumar et al. , and using the UCSC Genome Browser LiftOver tool to convert enhancer coordinates from hg19 to hg38. For downstream analyses of the DNA methylation data, we corrected for differences between study and batch by linear modeling incorporating batch as a cofactor. For visualizing methylation values as scatterplots and boxplots, we normalized logit-transformed methylation values within each tumor type.

## Integrative analyses between SVs and expression
Using SVExpress , we defined genes with altered expression (by protein or mRNA) associated with nearby germline SV breakpoints. Relative to each gene, genomic region windows considered included the within-gene regions and within 100 kb upstream or 100 kb downstream of the gene. For the above regions, SVExpress constructed a gene-to-sample matrix with entries as 1 if a breakpoint occurs in the specified region for the given gene in the given sample, and 0 if otherwise. We also used SVExpress to examine a 1 Mb region surrounding each gene, using the “relative distance metric” option , whereby breakpoints close to the gene will have more numeric weight in identifying SV-expression associations, while breakpoints further away but within 1 Mb can have some influence. The 1 Mb window was considered as genomic elements such as enhancers may impact genes as far as ~1 Mb away . The genomic region windows used here were based on our previous studies that explored different window sizes (with windows beyond 1 Mb tending to yield fewer significant genes) , . If a given genomic region window relative to a given gene overlapped with the region windows of one or more other genes, each gene was tested separately. Gene-level SV-expression association analyses included 15,428 unique named genes, 10,742 of which had protein values for at least 400 tumors. The 10,742 genes were the main focus of the recurrent SV-protein expression association analyses. We could generate additional SV-expression associations for genes with at least 100 protein values involving at least one non-gynecological tumor type (Supplementary Data  and ).
Using the gene-by-sample SV breakpoint matrix, SVExpress assessed the correlation between expression of the gene and the presence of an SV breakpoint using a linear regression model, incorporating sample tumor type and gene-level copy number alteration (CNA). The linear regression model used here was therefore the following: “Y ∼ SV + CNA + tumor type”, where Y is expression of a given gene (normalized within individual cohort/tumor type), “SV” is the SV breakpoint pattern (Log2 distance for 1 Mb region, 1 vs 0 for the other regions), CNA is the copy alteration pattern, and ‘tumor type’ is a categorical variable encoding the individual cohort/tumor type (with one of these arbitrarily used as a reference though the reference selection does not affect the results). In addition, genes located on the X or Y chromosome needed to remain significant after correction for patient sex, in addition to the other covariates; the linear regression model used in this instance was the following: “Y ∼ SV + CNA + tumor type + sex”. In practice, the CNA, tumor type, and sex covariates did not show any major influence on the SV-expression associations (Figure  ). Where the SV call set was defined based on the intersection of two SV callers, as described above, SV-expression associations based on a SV breakpoint matrix generated using a single SV caller were largely concordant with the study results using the combined callers (Figure  ). For the analyses involving within-gene, 100 kb upstream, and 100 kb downstream gene regions, we only considered genes with at least three patients associated with an SV within the given region when estimating False Discovery Rate .
By SVExpress, a gene shows significant SV-expression associations if the expression and SV breakpoint patterns line up non-randomly with respect to each other across all samples analyzed, after correction for covariates. By design , our integrative analytical approach does not assume the specific mechanism of altered expression and treats SV breakpoints representing different classes (tandem duplications, deletions, and inversions) and sizes the same , where each of these classes may contribute to SV-associated higher expression or lower expression (Figure  ). If multiple breakpoints occur near the gene, the breakpoint closest to the gene start is used in the breakpoint matrix. Our analytical approach to associate SVs with gene expression was gene-centric rather than variant-centric. This aspect is in contrast with eQTL-based approaches focusing on variant-level associations . In contrast to SNPs, there were much fewer germline SVs per patient, making our gene-centric approach tractable. Also, the gene-centric approach greatly simplifies the number of tests performed, allowing more associations to survive multiple testing considerations. Our approach also allows different variants within a region to collectively contribute to differential expression. In addition, some germline SV calls representing the same variant may differ slightly in genomic coordinates, e.g., due to sequencing or mapping or calling issues, where all variant calls would be consistently captured by our approach. In this study, tumor type largely reflected the batches involved in the combined expression datasets, as each tumor type (by tissue of origin) was contained within one or two batches (as defined by separate studies), and no batches involved multiple cancer types. For the SV-mRNA integrative analyses, we generated mRNA results for both the full mRNA dataset, including expression values that were not represented in the protein dataset (e.g., protein expression not detected for a particular gene in a particular tumor), and for a filtered mRNA dataset, with any expression data values not represented in the protein dataset removed. The results for the filtered mRNA dataset would help assess how missing protein values could contribute to disparate results between the protein and complete mRNA datasets.
In addition to analyzing all patients representing the various tumor types (by cell or tissue of origin) in our combined cohort, we carried out analyses within tumor subgroups by tumor type, focusing on the tumor types with more than 100 patients (allowing for sufficient power but excluding breast and colorectal tumors). As squamous cancers, such as head-and-neck and lung squamous, have been found in pan-cancer molecular analyses to coalesce into one major tumor type , , we combined head-and-neck and lung squamous patients into one squamous cell carcinoma group for the tumor type-specific analyses. For each tumor type-specific analysis, we included proteins with at least 50 expression values across patients. SVExpress was used as described above to evaluate SV-expression associations, at both mRNA and protein levels, for each of the four genomic region windows. Linear models associating SV breakpoint patterns with expression corrected for tumor histology and gene-level CNA, as well as patient sex for genes on X or Y loci. For each gene, one region window was selected for both mRNA and protein, using the region window with the best p value for the mRNA analyses.

## Integrative analyses between SVs and DNA methylation
In our present study, we employed the same overall approach for identifying associations between germline SV breakpoints and differential DNA methylation patterns as we had carried out previously , , , . Using SVExpress, we defined genes with altered DNA methylation involving their associated CGI probe in conjunction with nearby somatic SV breakpoints. For each of four genomic region windows 100 kb upstream of the gene, 100 kb downstream, within the gene body, or 1 Mb upstream or downstream (the latter using the distance metric method), we constructed gene-to-sample SV breakpoint matrices for related gene CGI with germline SV breakpoint patterns. For the CGI methylation analyses, the corresponding gene-to-sample SV breakpoint matrices included 12376 unique genes with Entrez identifier (taken from the 15,428 genes from the proteomic dataset), with gene coordinates obtained from Ensembl BioMart (GRCh38.p14) .
CGI-level analyses focused on the 118,906 array probes annotated as falling within CGIs (and included in the 15,428 genes represented in the proteomic dataset). The genes associated with each CGI probe, according to the Illumina platform annotation, were used to construct CGI probe-to-sample breakpoint matrices, using the gene as the reference for the relative breakpoint locations and the gene-to-sample SV breakpoint matrices described above. SVExpress assessed the association between CGI methylation and the presence of an SV breakpoint using linear regression models. We used logit-transformed methylation array beta values (i.e., “M-values”  ) in the linear regression modeling for DNA methylation data to help the data better align with linear model assumptions . For CGI probes with significant SV-methylation associations considered in downstream analyses, we required significant associations to arise after correction for tumor histologic type as well as for differences between study and batch. In addition, CGI probes located on the X or Y chromosome needed to remain significant after correction for patient sex in addition to sample histologic type. For the analyses involving within-gene, 100 kb upstream, and 100 kb downstream gene regions, we only considered CGI probes with at least three patients associated with an SV within the given region when estimating FDR by Storey and Tibshirani method . When relating CGI methylation results with gene expression results, we relied on the Illumina array annotation for the gene associated with the given CGI probe; in instances where a CGI was annotated as possibly involving multiple genes, we selected one gene (the first one listed) to assign to the CGI probe, to simply the analyses and to minimize multiple testing issues. We also identified SV-CGI methylation associations by patient tumor type, for the gene region window that was significant by SV-mRNA analysis for the gene associated with the CGI probe.
For each of 30,292 enhancers, we mapped the nearest DNA methylation probe (within 20 kb) and determined its methylation association with nearby SV breakpoints (within 100 kb) by linear modeling correcting for histologic type. We used SVExpress to construct an enhancer-to-sample matrix with entries as 1 if a breakpoint occurs within 100 kb of the given enhancer in the given sample and 0 if otherwise. We used logit-transformed methylation array beta values for the linear modeling to associate SV breakpoint pattern with differential methylation of the nearby array probe. For top enhancers with significant SV-methylation association, the genes within 500 kb of the enhancer were examined for SV-expression association (at both mRNA and protein levels) involving the SV breakpoint pattern surrounding the enhancer. Genes with significant SV-expression association but in the opposite direction of the SV-methylation association for the nearby enhancer were considered as being of particular interest. Linear regression models involving the enhancer-associated SV breakpoint patterns at DNA methylation, protein, and mRNA levels corrected for tumor tissue of origin (as well as sample batch for methylation).

## Associations with patient ancestry
Of the 1637 patients represented in our proteomic-WGS cohort, 1534 were categorized according to one of the following ancestry categories: African (n = 56), European (n = 1280), Asian (n = 193), or American Indian/Alaskan Native (n = 5). The Asian category encompassed both South Asian and East Asian ancestry groupings. The remaining 103 patients in our cohort either did not have ancestry information reported or were categorized as “other” and could not be assigned to one of the above groups. For 257 TCGA patients, patient ancestry was inferred by TCGA Network using SNP arrays . For 740 CPTAC patients, patient ancestry was inferred by genomic analysis as part of the CPTAC pan-cancer studies . For 156 CBTN pediatric brain tumor patients, we could obtain self-reported patient ancestry from the PedCBioportal (https://pedcbioportal.kidsfirstdrc.org/). For the remaining patients, self-reported ancestry could be obtained from the supplementary data associated with the study publication or from the Proteomic Data Commons.
For the three most represented ancestral groups (African, European, Asian), we determined differential expression in each ancestral group relative to the rest of the patients. For this analysis, we assembled an expanded cohort of 2167 patients and 2236 patients with tumor mRNA and protein data , respectively, adding to our 1637-cohort patients that did not have WGS data but which had expression data compiled as part of our previous proteogenomic studies . Also, for each major ancestral group, we determined the enrichment of SV germline breakpoint patterns for each gene. For each genomic region window relative to genes considered (100 kb upstream of the gene, 100 kb downstream of the gene, and within the gene), we counted the patients with a germline SV breakpoint relative to the gene in the region of interest, then determined enrichment of breakpoint occurrences by one-sided Fisher’s exact test. We carried out the same analyses in an external cohort from PCAWG (n = 1218 patients, only 36 of which involved TCGA samples in the 1637-patient cohort), to determine the significant genes overlapping across both cohorts. Of the top genes with significant SV-expression associations at both mRNA and protein levels, those genes involving SV breakpoint pattern enrichment by patient ancestry was determined, as consistently identified across both proteomic-WGS and PCAWG patient cohorts.

## Essential genes in cell lines and TCGA associations
We examined genes of interest having SV-expression associations in external datasets. We examined gene effect scores (with low scores denoting essential genes) based on DepMap CRISPR assays , using the dataset as analyzed using the Chronos algorithm from Dempster et al. . We used a cutoff score of <−0.75 to denote gene essentiality in a given cell line. Pan-cancer correlates of patient overall survival at the mRNA level, were taken from our previously generated catalog based on TCGA data , using p < 0.01 by multivariate Cox incorporating tumor and FDR < 10% as cutoffs. For the rare and singleton SV events disrupting genes, we examined TCGA mRNA data for the cancer types represented in our 1637-patient cohort (TCGA projects BRCA, CRC, GBM, HNSC, KIRC, LUAD, LUSC, OV, PAAD, and UCEC). For each TCGA cancer type, we computed the average log2 mRNA expression values for each gene (using the datasets from ref. ), and we computed the average pan-cancer expression across all the cancer types. For each disrupting SV event (by both predicted LOF and low gene expression), we looked up both the average cancer-type-specific expression corresponding to the gene and the tumor sample and the average pan-cancer expression of the gene. For the 1847 rare disrupting SV events from Fig.  , we compared the cancer type-specific expression versus the pan-cancer expression using a paired t-test and found the average difference was higher for the cancer-type-specific expression patterns.

## Statistical analyses
All p values were two-sided unless otherwise specified. Nominal p values do not involve multiple comparison adjustments, while FDRs involve p values adjusted for multiple gene feature comparisons. Enrichment of GO annotation terms within sets of differentially expressed genes was evaluated using SigTerms software and one-sided Fisher’s exact tests. Visualization using heat maps was performed using both JavaTreeview (version 1.1.6r4) and matrix2png (version 1.2.1) . Figures indicate exact value of n (number of tumors or cell lines), and the statistical tests used are noted in the Figure legends and next to reported p values in the Results section. Boxplots represent 5%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 95%. Figures represent biological and not technical replicates.
We made considerations for multiple testing of gene features throughout the study. For each feature tested in the linear modeling, corresponding FDRs were computed using the Storey and Tibshirani method : [nominal p value of the feature] × [total number of features tested]/[number of features in the dataset with p value less than or equal to the given p value]. For a top feature set with FDR < 10%, the FDR would suggest that on the order of 10% of these features might have nominal significance due to multiple testing (and, conversely, some 90% of the top features would represent bona fide significant associations). FDR estimates by Storey and Tibshirani were consistent with FDR estimates by permutation testing (Figure  ). We relied on a stricter FDR cutoff for defining top genes when carrying out gene-level global molecular analyses for a single analyte (e.g., gene-level SV-protein associations or SV-mRNA associations). When overlapping different top-gene result sets across multiple independent analyses (e.g., gene-level SV-expression associations as observed in both protein and mRNA data), we used a more relaxed p value cutoff for each individual analysis to limit false negatives better and identify significant overlap patterns. As reported, the degree of gene set overlap across independent analyses was often highly statistically significant. This practice would be consistent with our previous studies identifying genes with demonstrated functional roles as originally identified using integrative analyses , . For example, when selecting genes based on both SV-mRNA and SV-protein associations, using a nominal p value of 0.01 for each, the number of intersecting features expected for a given region window would be: [total number of features tested] × [nominal p value/2] × [nominal p value/2] × [factor of 2, as we consider both mRNA higher/protein higher and mRNA lower/protein lower feature sets]. The number of actual intersecting features found for each region window (using p < 0.01 cutoffs, with the same direction of association for both) were 19, 20, 16, and 42 for 100 kb upstream, 100 kb downstream, within-gene, and 1 Mb up- or downstream regions, respectively, where the chance expected by multiple testing (i.e., genes that, by random chance, would meet the nominal p value threshold for both analytes with the same direction of association), were, respectively, 0.38, 0.37, 0.20, and 0.54. These estimates by probability theory were consistent with results of permutation testing when shuffling the samples in the 1 Mb SV matrix 100 times and computing SV-expression associations by both protein and mRNA with p < 0.01 (average of 0.6 significant genes). In addition, when using Fisher’s method to combine protein and mRNA p values for each region (computed using one-sided p values, with the direction of change required to be the same in both), the top genes significant by both protein and mRNA at nominal p < 0.01 for each were significant by Fisher’s methods with FDR < 3% (Supplementary Data  ). Furthermore, for the genes highlighted individually using scatterplots and boxplots, at least one of the analytes considered had FDR < 10% for the SV association.

## Data availability
All data used in this study are publicly available. Proteomics data are available for CPTAC studies at the Proteomic Data Commons [https://pdc.cancer.gov/]. For CPTAC, TCGA, and APOLLO studies, WGS BAM files, transcriptome data, and copy number data are available at the Genomic Data Commons [https://gdc.cancer.gov/]. TCGA transcriptome and CNA data are also available at the Broad Institute’s Firehose data portal [https://gdac.broadinstitute.org]. CBTN structural variant, transcriptome data, and copy number data are available through the public project on the Kids First Data Resource Portal and Cavatica [https://cbtn.org] and through the PedCBioPortal [https://pedcbioportal.org]. Raw genomic and transcriptomic CPTAC data can be accessed via dbGap Study Accession: phs001287.v21.p6 https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/projects/gap/cgi-bin/study.cgi?study_id=phs001287.v13.p5. Raw genomic and transcriptomic APOLLO data can be accessed via dbGap Study Accession: phs003011.v1.p1 https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/projects/gap/cgi-bin/study.cgi?study_id=phs001287.v21.p6. Raw genomic and transcriptomic TCGA data can be accessed via dbGap Study Accession: phs000178.v11.p8 https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/projects/gap/cgi-bin/study.cgi?study_id=phs000178.v11.p8. Raw data for the above may be obtained once authorized access is granted via a Data Use Certification (DUC) agreement. Protected CBTN molecular data, including raw sequencing data and germline SV calls, are available under restricted access. To gain access to protected data, one can apply to the CBTN and sign a Data Use Agreement. WGS BAM files and associated Variant Call Files from blood samples represent Protected Health Information (PHI), as these files contain patient sequence data and could, in theory, be used to identify the patient. dbGap and CBTN restrictions prohibit any sharing or public posting of PHI. One can obtain access to the raw data through directly applying to dbGap or CBTN. The combined datasets of molecular profiles for gene-level total protein, mRNA, CNA, and SV breakpoint patterns (for genomic region windows 100 kb upstream, 100 kb downstream, within gene, and 1 Mb upstream or downstream)—compiled as part of our study—are available through figshare. Each molecular dataset has a common gene and sample set, allowing one to derive correlations between SV breakpoint patterns and gene expression, e.g., using the SVExpress software . Any remaining data are available within the Article or Supplementary Information.  are provided with this paper.
