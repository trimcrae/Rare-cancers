## Abstract

## Background
The evolving molecular portrait of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) has exposed previously unrecognized cell death programs that fuel disease progression and treatment resistance—uncovering untapped therapeutic potential. Recent work has uncovered disulfidptosis—a novel form of programmed cell death (PDC) triggered by glucose deprivation in SLC7A11-high AML cells—as a potential therapeutic vulnerability. However, disulfidptosis in pediatric AML (pAML) remains largely unexplored, with no comprehensive studies assessing its biological significance or clinical prognostic value.

## Method
Here, we systematically characterize disulfidptosis in pediatric AML through multi-omics integration. Using ssGSEA, we quantified PDC patterns across bulk and single-cell transcriptomes, revealing distinct molecular subtypes via unsupervised clustering. Machine learning deciphered the biological networks underlying disulfidptosis, while in vitro experiments were performed to further validate.

## Results
In this study, we demonstrated that elevated disulfidptosis scores were associated with poor prognosis and a hypermetabolic state. Notably, patients carrying different driver mutations exhibited distinct levels of disulfidptosis susceptibility. Through in vitro experiments utilizing both cell lines and primary patient-derived cells, we found that elevated expression of SLC7A11, a key regulator of disulfidptosis, correlated with chemoresistance. Furthermore, disulfidptosis signatures effectively stratified risk subgroups in pAML, revealing a novel subtype, DSP3, characterized by prominent disulfidptosis features, an immune-desert tumor microenvironment, and an unfavorable prognosis. Additionally, our in vitro experiments identified the GLUT1 inhibitor STF-31 and the mitochondrial-targeted agent darinaparsin as potential therapeutic options for DSP3 patients, and combining with conventional chemotherapy exhibited a synergistic anti-tumor effect.

## Conclusion
In summary, this study employed multi-omics analysis to examine the characteristics of pAML in the context of disulfidptosis, identifying a novel disulfidptosis-related subtype, aiming to provide new insights for future studies on optimizing traditional regimen based on pAML pathogenesis.

## Background
According to the present global cancer statistics, leukemia is the most common type of childhood tumors worldwide [ , ] and the gradual rising incidence of pediatric AML (pAML) in recent years has posed a significant threat to the physical and mental well-being among children and adolescents [ ]. Despite notable improvements in survival time due to in-depth research into the pathogenesis of leukemia and advances in clinical diagnosis and regimens, the three-year overall survival (OS) rate for pAML stands at 62 ~ 69%, with an event-free survival (EFS) rate of 53.4 ~ 61% [ – ]. Challenges such as high relapse rates, treatment resistance, limited availability of targeted therapies and a lack of precise medicine remain prevalent.
In recent years, new patterns of programmed cell death (PCD) have been continuously discovered, such as ferroptosis, pyroptosis, and cuproptosis, shedding light on their close relationship with leukemogenesis, and providing new avenues for innovative anti-leukemia strategies [ ]. For example, Venetoclax is now widely used to treat older AML patients by inhibiting BCL2, an anti-apoptotic protein helping to evade chemotherapeutic cytotoxity [ – ]. Recently, a novel pattern of PCD triggered by excessive disulfides in the cytoplasm, termed disulfidptosis has been proposed, offering a potential therapeutic target to enrich cancer treatment strategies [ ]. In detail, disulfidptosis specifically occurred in cells with abnormally elevated expression of SLC7A11, which showed vulnerability of the cytoskeleton protein to disulfide stress, when suffering glucose deprivation [ ]. However, preliminary studies indicate that the role of disulfidptosis in AML has not been thoroughly explored, particularly in pAML patients.
This study workflow was shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. It was sought to examine the distinct expression levels, biological implications, and clinical significance of disulfidptosis-related biomarkers in primary pAML. Through clustering analyses, we identified and validated four novel subtypes across independent pAML cohorts. Our preliminary findings indicated the existence of a new pAML subtype distinguished by a unique metabolic profile, an impoverished immune microenvironment, and unfavorable clinical outcomes, which may be linked to an elevated disulfidptosis score. Furthermore, we investigated potential therapeutic agents that exhibit sensitivity to this newly identified subtype. Collectively, we aimed to elucidate the interaction between disulfidptosis and pAML to provide new avenues for understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying this disease and to develop novel therapeutic strategies that address the current challenges in treatment.

## Methods

## Data acquisition and prepossessing
Totally 2363 pediatric AML samples containing bulk transcriptome, clinical observations, and additional omics level datasets from Therapeutically Applicable Research to Generate Effective Treatments AML project and St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital cohorts were included in this study. For further analysis, we transformed the count values into transcripts per kilobase million (TPM) values, and used log2 (TPM + 1) for further analysis. And AML cell lines data including expression (Expression Public 24Q2) and CRISPR (CRISPR (DepMap Public 24Q2, Chronos Score), were downloaded from DepMap portal (https://depmap.org/portal). The Single cell RNA sequence (scRNA seq) raw fastq data including 28 patients participating in TARGET AML project were from PRJNA984284. Raw data was then prepossessing as previously reported [ ] and further analysis was conducted by using Seurat (v4.0.5). The major cell types were identified based on the criteria outlined by Lambo S [ ], which utilized canonical cell type-specific marker genes and established single-cell analysis pipelines for cell type identification. Specifically, the original study integrated scATAC-seq and scRNA-seq data to distinguish malignant cells and normal cells, using a normal bone marrow atlas as a reference, projecting AML cell differentiation hierarchies, and leveraging lineage-specific genes to assist machine learning in cell type annotation. All data were analyzed using R 4.3.2.

## Extraction of programmed cell death signature cross pAML
We conducted a Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) (v1.42.0) analysis to calculate the score of each PCD per person by using the “ssGSEA” method. Then Limma package was utilized to assess the differential expression between leukemic cells and normal bone marrow mononuclear cells, or between diagnosis and relapse or among different molecular subtypes cross pAML. The PCD signature was referred Andreas Strasser (Table ). The PCD scores were also measured in sc-RNAseq data by AUCell package and visualized by ggplot2.

## Unsupervised clustering for disulfidptosis-related signatures
We performed unsupervised cluster analysis on disulfidptosis-related signatures-based expression matrices of pAML from TARGET AML projects as the training cohort by using ConsensusClusterPlus package. The gene list of Disulfidptosis-related signatures was summarized from Boyi Gan’s study [ ]. The optimal number of clusters was determined by using the consensus cumulative distribution function (CDF) and the proportion of ambiguous clusters, incorporating prior knowledge. Verification was utilized by Nearest template prediction (NTP) analysis from MOVICS package [ ]. MOVICS package was also used to identify biomarkers for each subtype.

## Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)
To depict the distinct biological feature of each cluster including signaling pathway and metabolism, we applied ssGSEA analysis by IOBR package for calculating the interested pathway scores (https://github.com/IOBR/IOBR) [ ]. The difference among four clusters was assessed by Limma (v3.50.0) package. Significant enrichment was chosen based on p.adj < 0.05 and absolute log2(foldchange) > 1. The enriched results were presented by pheatmap package. GSEA was also implied in estimating the pathway-level difference between SLC7A11-high and SLC7A11-low groups based on the rank of log2 (foldchange) of DEGs. Significant enrichment was selected by p.adj less than 0.05 and absolute normalized enriched score (NES) more than 1, and visualized by ggplot2 [ ].

## Association between Disulfidptosis and tumor microenvironment elements in primary acute myeloid leukemia (pAML)
We quantified the degree of abundance of tumor microenvironment (TME), and scores of component-related immune cells by ssGSEA as mentioned above. We utilized CIBERSORTx to calculate the proportions of various immune cells infiltrated in TME. We utilized the IOBR and MOVICS packages to evaluate immune features.

## Drug sensitivity between clusters
The response to drug was measured by the value of half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) by using pRRophetic package based on Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC; https://www.cancerrxgene.org/) database.

## Reagents
Doxorubicin, Cytarabine (Ara-C) and STF-31 were purchased from Selleck. Darinaparsin was purchased by TargetMol. Annexin V was purchased from Thermofisher and Cell Counting Kit-8 was purchased from DOJINDO.

## Cell lines
Shanghai Institute of Hematology (Shanghai, China) supplied the THP1, MV411, AML199, OCI-AML2 and Molm13 cells involved in this work. All cell lines used in this study were free of mycoplasma contamination. Cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin (Gibco).

## Lentiviral transduction
For SLC7A11 knockdown in MV411 and Molm13 cells, shRNA lentivirus was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and 1.0 µg/ml puromycin for selection. The target sequences are listed as followed:
#shSLC7A11 (TRCN0000043123):
CCTGTCACTATTTGGAGCTTT.
#scramble shRNA:
CCTAAGGTTAAGTCGCCCTCGCTCGAGCGAGGGCGACTTAACCTTAGG.

## Detection of reactive oxygen species (ROS)
The Molm13 and MV411 infected with vector or shRNA (SLC7A11) were pretreated with blank solution or Ara-c for 24 h, respectively. Subsequently, diluent DCFH-DA (1:1000) was added to the samples along with well mixing, and then incubated in a dark environment at 37℃ for 20 min. Finally, the samples were washed by 1640 and then observed under a flow cytometry.

## Reverse transcription and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)
For mRNA expression analysis of the indicated genes in the cell lines and the validation of the indicated genes from the RNA-seq results, RT‒qPCR was performed with HiScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit and ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix Kit (Vazyme). ΔCT was obtained by normalization to the CT values of the GAPDH gene. The primer sequences are listed as followed:
#GAPDH:
Forward Primer: ACAACTTTGGTATCGTGGAAGG.
Reverse Primer: GCCATCACGCCACAGTTTC.
#SLC7A11:
Forward Primer: TCTCCAAAGGAGGTTACCTGC.
Reverse Primer: AGACTCCCCTCAGTAAAGTGAC.

## Patient samples
Bone marrow samples of de novo pediatric AML patients and peripheral blood samples of healthy children were obtained from Tongji Hospital. Bone marrow mononuclear cells (BMMCs) were isolated from bone marrow by density gradient centrifugation.

## Survival analysis
The survminer package was utilized for Kaplan-Meier analysis to assess the prognostic implications. The Pairwise_survdiff from survminer enabled pairwise comparisons, with p-values adjusted via the BH algorithm. Hazard ratios (HR) were determined using a COX regression model and visualized with ggplot2.

## Statistical analysis
Results were calculated by R software or GraphPad Prism 8.0.0 and presented as the mean ± SEM of data from three independent experiments. Continuous variables were compared using t tests or Wilcox test or Kruskal‒Wallis or ANOVA tests. CompuSyn 1.0 was used to calculate the combination index (CI) and IC50 values [ ]. The threshold for significance was p < 0.05. *, p < 0.05; **, p < 0.01; ***, p < 0.001; ****, p < 0.0001.

## Result

## Different PCD features across pediatric AML
Given the critical role of distinct patterns of programmed cell death (PCD) in cancer progression, we initially assessed the enriched levels of various PCD types in primary pAML (Table ). As illustrated in Fig.  A and B, the scores for alkaliptosis, disulfidptosis, and cuproptosis were significantly elevated in leukemic cells, whereas the scores for netotic cell death and parthanatos were comparatively lower relative to normal bone marrow cells. In terms of the prognostic implications of PCD, through analyzing the overall survival (OS) rate and progression-free survival (PFS) rate, it was observed that the scores of ferroptosis, apoptosis and disulfidptosis were risky, whereas the scores of cuproptosis, parthanatos, and necroptosis played a protective role in pAML (Fig.  C-D). Regarding the chemotherapeutic response, comparing the scores of different PCD in leukemic cells in the same patient at the initial diagnosis and at relapse, there was a significant increase in scores of disulfidptosis, apoptosis, autophagy and a decrease in the scores of cuproptosis, parthanatos upon relapse (Fig.  E, Supplementary Fig. S2A). Distinct patterns of PCD were also identified across various subtypes of pAML. In the context of molecular classification, it was observed that patients with KMT2A rearrangements exhibited elevated average levels of disulfidptosis, whereas those with core binding factor (CBF) AML demonstrated lower levels (Fig.  F, Supplementary Fig. S2B). Conversely, according to the French-American-British (FAB) classification, patients classified as M1 exhibited low disulfidptosis scores, whereas those classified as M5 exhibited relatively high scores (Supplementary Fig. S2C). Furthermore, our findings indicated that elevated levels of disulfidptosis were inversely correlated with white blood cell (WBC) count and the percentage of peripheral blasts, but exhibited no significant relationship with the percentage of blasts in the bone marrow (Supplementary Fig. S2D). Additionally, we assessed the association between hallmarks and the disulfidptosis score in de novo pAML. Elevated disulfidptosis scores were associated with enhanced metabolic pathways (glycolysis, lipid metabolism, and oxidative phosphorylation) and concurrent suppression of Wnt/β-catenin signaling (Fig.  G). We subsequently quantified the disulfidptosis levels across various cell types derived from primary pAML patients’ BM samples. Leukemic stem cells (LSCs) exhibited relatively lower levels of disulfidptosis, whereas well-differentiated malignant monocytes, demonstrated elevated levels of disulfidptosis (Fig.  H). Moreover, in bone marrow lesions of pediatric patients at initial diagnosis, the disulfidptosis levels were higher in malignant cells compared to normal cells at a similar differentiation stage (Fig.  H).
 We conducted a detailed analysis of disulfidptosis at the single-cell level across various molecular subtypes, comparing initial diagnosis and relapse stages. Consistent with bulk transcriptomic profiling, we found that more differentiated cell populations, particularly monocytes, had higher disulfidptosis levels, while LSCs had the lowest. Notably, distinct molecular subtypes exhibited characteristic disulfidptosis patterns: patients with KMT2A rearrangements had the highest levels. Core-binding factor AML cases, however, maintained relatively stable disulfidptosis levels throughout disease progression (Fig.  I). Taken together, disulfidptosis played a crucial role in primary pAML.

## Disulfidptosis-related gene dysregulation in pediatric AML
We defined disulfidptosis-related signatures in accordance with the work of Xiaoguang Liu et al. [ ]. To better understand the disulfidptosis-related signatures in pAML, we performed an extensive analysis of the features of disulfidptosis-related signatures expression, their prognostic impact, methylation patterns, and copy number variations (CNVs) within primary pAML patients from TARGET AML cohort. It seemed that in leukemic cells, the expression levels of most synergistic hits were upregulated, while inhibitory hits were suppressed, compared to normal bone marrow cells (Fig.  A, Supplementary Fig. S3A). Considering the impact of disulfidptosis-related signatures on chemotherapy responsiveness, the expression levels of synergistic hits, including PPM1K, NDUFS2 and NDUFC1 were downregulated, whereas the expression levels of inhibitory hits, such as SLC7A11, ZNF484, HOXA2, and RABL3 were upregulated during relapse in the same patient (Fig.  B, Supplementary Fig. S3B). Our in vitro studies confirmed that SLC7A11 knockdown synergized with Ara-C, suppressing proliferation while promoting apoptosis in leukemic cells (Fig.  C). Clinically, SLC7A11 expression in de novo pAML BMMCs (normalized to healthy PBMCs) correlated with post-induction MRD levels in real-world practice (Supplementary Fig. S4). At the single cell level, NDUFS2 was highly expressed on progenitor-, GMP-like malignant cells, whereas SLC3A2 was highest expressed on monocyte-like malignant cells. LSCs demonstrated relatively lower expression of disulfidptosis-related genes, and characterized as high expression of NDUFA11 (Fig.  D, Supplementary Fig. S3C). Taking into account the major driver mutations, we found CBF AML exhibited a contrasting profile compared to other subtypes, characterized by reduced expression of inhibitory hits. Additionally, the expression of NDUFA11 and SLC7A11 was elevated in non-CBF pAML (Fig.  E, Supplementary Fig. S3D). Regarding the prognostic implications of disulfidptosis-related signatures, NDUFS2, HOXA2, SKA1, and SCGB3A2 were identified as risk factors for OS (Fig.  F, left) and PFS (Fig.  F, right) based on analyses conducted using both Cox regression and the Kaplan-Meier method. We also observed that among the 25 hits, the primary CNV types were heterozygous amplification and deletion, particularly notable in the WDR44, NDUFS2, and ZNF484 genes, as well as in the subtypes sharing with other karyotypes (Fig.  G). Finally, we examined DNA methylation of disulfidptosis-related genes in primary pAML, and found that most hits, particularly SLC7A11, RPN1, and LRPPRC, showed increased expression levels that were significantly negatively correlated with methylation in malignant versus normal tissues (Fig.  H).
 

## Distinct clinical outcomes were observed among various disulfidptosis-related subtypes
Subsequently, we performed a cluster analysis on pAML to elucidate the impact of disulfidptosis, utilizing the expression profiles of the aforementioned 25 disulfidptosis-related signatures, and we determined that four was the optimal number of subtypes (Fig.  A, Supplementary Fig. S5). We observed that the prognosis for DSP3 was inferior to that of the other three types in neither OS nor PFS rate, as illustrated in Fig.  B. We then adopted the transcriptome data from St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital cohorts and utilized Nearest template prediction (NTP) to validate the cluster analysis. The results revealed that DSP3 was correlated with poor prognosis in another independent cohort, highlighting the clustering method’s applicability and robustness based on disulfidptosis-related signatures (Supplementary Fig. S6A-B). Multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted in both training and test cohorts, which consistently demonstrated its significance as an independent risk factor for predicting AML outcomes (Fig.  C, Supplementary Fig. S6C). Each cluster had distinct clinic features (Fig.  D). Compared to the other three groups, DSP3 comprised younger patients, with a higher proportion of infants and fewer adolescents (Fig.  E). Regarding FAB classification, DSP3 predominantly consisted of M5 patients, whereas DSP4 was primarily composed of M4 patients, and DSP2 had a majority of M2 patients (Fig.  F). In terms of cytogenetic events and risk stratification, the DSP3 cohort exhibited a higher proportion of high-risk patients compared to DSP2 and DSP4 groups, which demonstrated higher proportions of low-risk individuals. Besides, the DSP2 group exhibited the highest proportion of CBF-AML patients, while DSP1 and DSP3 groups showed similar proportion of patients harboring KMT2A rearranged (Fig.  G, Supplementary Fig. S6D-E).
 

## Distinctive biological and metabolism differences
The four clusters showed distinctive expression pattern (Fig.  A), we categorized DSP4 as the cold disulfidptosis subtype, both DSP1 and DSP2 as the mixed subtype, and DSP3 as the hot subtype (Figs.  A and B-C). Interestingly, within the same cluster, enriched levels of ferroptosis and disulfidptosis seemed to be mutually exclusive, as DSP4, identified as a disulfidptosis cold subtype, was ferroptosis-dominant, while DSP3 was the opposite (Fig.  B-C). In addition, at the single cell level, we observed that at initial diagnosis, DSP3 exhibited a high level of disulfidptosis, predominantly contributed by monocytes-like malignant cells (Fig.  D). Moreover, during the relapse period, there is a further increase in the level of disulfidptosis in DSP3 (Fig.  D).
 The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) specific to each subtype were subjected to enrichment analysis by utilizing GO, KEGG databases, and hallmark terms to elucidate the distinctive characteristics of each pAML subtype. DSP1 exhibited marked upregulation of RAD51-dsDNA destabilization and the TAC3/TACR3-PLC-PKC signaling pathway. DSP2 showed elevated apoptotic signaling and PI3K pathway activation, while DSP3 demonstrated hyperactive mTOR signaling, and DSP4 displayed upregulated chemokine biosynthesis. Notably, DSP1 and DSP3 shared some concordant pathway alterations, including TP53 suppression and aberrant MYC target activation. DSP2 and DSP4 exhibited some synchronized active pathways (e.g., NF-κB, IL2-STAT5, Hedgehog, and hypoxia responses), which contrasted sharply with DSP1/DSP3 subtype (Fig.  E-F).
The four subgroups exhibited markedly divergent metabolic patterns. DSP2 demonstrated a globally suppressed metabolic state, though with selective upregulation in N-glycan biosynthesis and nicotinamide adenine metabolism. DSP1 and DSP3 shared activation of core metabolic pathways including purine metabolism, glycolysis, branched-chain amino acid degradation, citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation. However, DSP1 showed particularly robust activity in purine metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation, while DSP3 displayed more pronounced activation in glycolysis, branched-chain amino acid degradation, and citric acid cycle, along with uniquely upregulated fatty acid degradation. DSP4 presented a completely distinct metabolic signature characterized by enhanced glutathione metabolism, and drug metabolism (Fig.  G, Supplementary Fig. S7).

## DSP3 exhibited an immune dysfunctional state among subtypes
The tumor microenvironment (TME) exhibited heterogeneity across the four identified clusters. Notably, the TME of DSP3 resembled a desert-like environment, characterized by the lowest TME score (Fig.  A). Further analysis of single cell data also indicated that DSP3 had reduced adaptive immune responses, characterized by the lowest proportions of CD4 + and CD8 + T cells among clusters, and heightened innate immunity, evidenced by NK cell enrichment in the TME. These findings align with CIBERSORTx analysis of bulk transcriptome data (Fig.  B-C, Supplementary Fig. S8). Consistently, we noticed that the several expressions of ligands, including IL2, IL4, IL10, and IFNG, were reduced in DSP3 (Fig.  D). Additionally, leukemic cells in DSP3 exhibited a deficiency in MHC I molecule expression, which further diminished at recurrence compared to the initial diagnosis. Moreover, during remission, MHC I molecule expression on leukemic cells resurged, although it remained lower than in other clusters. Although antigen-presenting cells (APCs) in DSP3 initially exhibited high MHC II molecule expression, a significant decrease was observed at recurrence (Fig.  E). Furthermore, we observed that the cluster DSP4 demonstrated a more robust tumor immune response compared to other clusters. This was evidenced by upregulated chemokine biosynthesis (Fig.  F), a higher proportion of effective CD8 + T cells and APCs characterized by elevated HLA and co-stimulation signatures, as well as upregulation of the interferon signaling pathway (Fig.  B-F).
 

## The drug response and potential agents for various subtypes
Given the malignant characteristics of the four subtypes, we explored various drug databases to identify agents specific to each subtype, especially for DSP3 cluster. We observed that patients in DSP3 conferred resistance to conventional AML treatments, including doxorubicin, cytarabine, azacitidine and cyclophosphamide (Fig.  A). Subsequently, we identified several potential agents for targeting DSP3 based on their estimated IC50 values. These agents included darinaparsin, the GLUT1 inhibitor STF-31, daporinad and cytochalasinB (Fig.  B). Darinaparsin has been reported as a novel mitochondrial-targeted anticancer agent for the treatment of relapsed or refractory peripheral T-cell lymphoma (PTCL) in the stage of performing clinical trials, and could trigger ROS mediated apoptosis with NAD + accumulation. Furthermore, we also speculated that DSP1 responded to the VDR agonist elocalcitol, while DSP2 was sensitive to a combination of the HSP90 inhibitor tanespimycin and gemcitabine. Patients were supposed to be sensitive to erlotinib and dasatinib (Fig.  C). After identifying cluster-related cell lines from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) with the use of the NTP algorithm (Fig.  D, upper), we proceeded to measure the effect of disulfidptosis-related signature genes in human AML cell lines by analyzing the indicated gene effect data according to CRISPR-Cas9 screening, presented in the CCLE. Our observations indicated that most disulfidptosis-related signature genes were essential for survival of the DSP3 cell lines (Fig.  D, lower panel). Our validation studies further revealed that cell lines of the DSP3 subtype demonstrated heightened sensitivity to darinaparsin and STF-31, compared to other DSP subtypes. Building on this finding, we systematically evaluated potential synergistic interactions between doxorubicin and these targeted agents in DSP3 cell lines. Quantitative synergy analysis revealed distinct combination effects: In MV411 cells, The doxorubicin + darinaparsin (DD) combination showed stronger synergy (CI = 0.80) than doxorubicin + STF-31 (DS, CI = 0.98) at the median effective dose (ED50). Similarly, in THP1 cells, the DD combination (CI = 0.85) demonstrated marginally better synergy than DS (CI = 0.88) at ED50 (Fig.  E). Mechanistically, both combination regimens effectively promoted apoptosis, with the DD combination showing superior efficacy (Fig.  G). And this enhanced therapeutic effect was probably mediated by accelerated ROS accumulation (Fig.  H).
 

## Discussion
In recent years, despite progress in pAML diagnosis and treatment, the lack of targeted research compared to adult AML remains challenging [ ]. As Supplementary Fig. S1 shown, we utilized pediatric bulk transcriptome data from TARGET AML and St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital cohorts, alongside scRNA seq data to analyze PCD patterns in pAML, noting a correlation between disulfidptosis and poor prognosis. Subsequently, we categorized de novo pAML patients into four clusters based on disulfidptosis signatures, with the DSP3 group indicating a hot disulfidptosis phenotype and the worst prognosis. We then detailed the multi-omics biological characteristics of the subtypes, covering epigenetic and genetic features, clinical traits, tumor heterogeneity, pathway activations, metabolic preferences, immune characteristics, and treatment responses. Additionally, we identified potential effective agents for each subgroup. GLUT1 inhibitor STF-31 and mitochondrial-targeted darinaparsin were DSP3 promising therapies. Both agents synergized with conventional chemotherapy, enhancing antitumor efficacy.
Our study revealed elevated disulfidptosis levels in relapsed patients compared to those at initial diagnosis, which correlated with worse clinical outcomes. Prior CRISPR-based studies classified disulfidptosis-related signatures into synergistic and inhibitory hits, with synergistic hits—particularly genes encoding respiratory chain complex I components (e.g., NDUFA11, NDUFS1, NDUFS2, and NUBPL), showing strong prognostic relevance [ ]. These genes support the high energy demands of aggressive tumors, as evidenced by their association with poor survival in breast cancer [ ], clear cell renal carcinoma [ ], and lung adenocarcinoma [ ]. In detail, NDUFS2 depletion impairs oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production, disrupts membrane integrity, and elevates ROS, ultimately inducing cell death [ ]. Consistent with these reports, we identified NDUFS2 and NDUFA11 as negative prognostic markers in our cohort (Fig.  F). Further validation revealed that the “disulfidptosis-hot” DSP3 subtype, exhibiting doxorubicin resistance demonstrated heightened sensitivity to the mitochondria-targeted agent darinaparsin relative to other subtypes. And darinaparsin could restore doxorubicin sensitivity in DSP3 cell lines. It suggested that targeting disulfidptosis-associated mitochondrial vulnerabilities may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for high-risk AML.
In addition, SLC7A11, a major role in disulfidptosis, whose elevated expression level is facilitated for triggering disulfidptosis, while shielding cells from oxidative stress-induced death, like ferroptosis [ ]. SLC7A11 also has been identified as an oncogene that facilitates proliferation of AML cells in a cysteine-dependent manner [ , ]. In our study, we also found that SLC7A11 suppression increased Ara-C susceptibility in AML cells. Clinically, elevated SLC7A11 expression is associated with higher MRD burden. Taken together, patients with higher levels of disulfidptosis shared the chemotherapy-resistant feature.
It was interesting to find the mutual exclusivity between disulfidptosis and ferroptosis in DSP3 (Fig.  B-C), which may suggest a potential metabolic switch governing cell death commitment. While both pathways are triggered by redox imbalance, their differential dependence on NADPH (for disulfide reduction) and iron (for lipid peroxidation) may create a bifurcation point. Future studies could test whether combinatorial targeting of these pathways synergizes to overcome resistance in tumors with high SLC7A11 expression.
Targeting tumor metabolism vulnerabilities has become a new strategy in cancer therapy. For example, a phase I/II clinical trial targeting mitochondrial reduction in elderly AML patients combined CPI-613, an inhibitor of pyruvate and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenases, with high-dose cytarabine and mitoxantrone. This regimen improved remission and response rates for refractory elderly AML patients compared to chemotherapy [ , ]. Another study suggested targeting cystine import in AML could repurpose sulfasalazine as a potential treatment, particularly alongside chemotherapy [ ].
Research has shown that glucose starvation induces disulfidptosis specifically in cells exhibiting high SLC7A11 expression levels. In this study, we found patients with high disulfidptosis levels often adopt a hypermetabolic state, which may act as a compensatory mechanism to mitigate the effects of disulfidptosis under conditions of nutrient abundance. However, this adaptation created a metabolic vulnerability: when glucose deprivation disrupted the balance, these cells were more prone to disulfidptosis. Consistent with this hypothesis, we found that the “disulfidptosis hot” subgroup DSP3, despite uptaking more energy substrates to promote its proliferative and drug-resistant phenotypes, become exquisitely vulnerable to disulfidptosis under glucose-restricted conditions (GLUT1 inhibitors). This duality implied that therapeutic targeting of glucose metabolism (e.g., via GLUT1 inhibition) could selectively eliminate disulfidptosis-susceptible cells, offering a novel strategy to overcome chemoresistance in this subset of pAML patients.
Additionally, we noted that Fangmin Zhong et al. [ ] explored the role of disulfidptosis in adult AML, providing an exemplary model for its clinical translation. Our study focused on pediatric AML, and given the differences between pediatric and adult AML in gene expression profiles [ , ], mutation frequencies [ ], prevalent mutation types, metabolic characteristics [ ], and treatment strategies [ , ], we observed divergent findings. For instance, in adult AML, disulfidptosis seemed to be positive correlate with the tumor microenvironment (TME), suggesting that patients with high disulfidptosis activity may benefit from PD-L1 inhibitors. In contrast, our data in pediatric AML revealed that patients with higher disulfidptosis scores exhibited a relative immune-desert phenotype. Notably, both studies consistently demonstrated that heightened glucose metabolism suppressed disulfidptosis, which may represent a shared regulatory mechanism in both pediatric and adult AML. Collectively, our findings corroborate those of Fangmin Zhong et al., collectively underscoring the critical role of metabolic status in triggering disulfidptosis. This study provided complementary insights into disulfidptosis across the AML age spectrum, with Zhong et al. ‘s work examining adult cases and our study addressing pediatric manifestations.
Lastly, while our findings provided preliminary evidence for the disulfidptosis-based clustering model, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the generalizability of this model may require further validation in larger, independent real-world cohorts. Second, while the therapeutic strategy targeting the DSP3 subtype appeared promising in preliminary analyses, its clinical translation would depend on extensive preclinical validation, including rigorous in vivo studies, followed by carefully controlled prospective clinical trials to establish efficacy and safety.

## Conclusions
We illustrated the global feature of pAML related to disulfidptosis, covering clinical traits, biological significance, metabolism and the TME. Moreover, we detailed a specific AML subtype, identified as “hot disulfidptosis,” with poor prognosis, noting its metabolic vulnerabilities and potential treatments.
