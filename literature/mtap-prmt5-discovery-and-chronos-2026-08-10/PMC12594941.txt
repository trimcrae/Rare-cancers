## Summary
In uveal melanoma (UM), the most common primary intraocular tumor, up to half of patients develop fatal metastases despite high local tumor control. Effective treatments for genetically high-risk tumors remain limited, largely due to challenges posed by cancer stem cells (CSCs) and the tumor microenvironment (TME), which sustain tumor progression and resistance. Our study evaluated stemness properties in UM tumor cells, focusing on STAT3 and ANGPTL4, markers linked to cancer aggressiveness and stem-like niches. Using patient samples, we analyzed the TME and CSCs via immune microarrays, RNA in situ hybridization, immunohistochemistry, and bioinformatics, generating comprehensive expression profiles. TNFRSF14 and IKBKE were identified as biomarkers of disease severity. In high-risk BRCA1-associated protein 1 (BAP1)-negative tumors, T cell function and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) superfamily genes were downregulated, indicating immune evasion. These findings underscore ANGPTL4, STAT3, and TNFRSF14 as potential biomarkers and drug targets, emphasizing the need to address tumor-intrinsic and tumor-extrinsic pathways to improve treatment strategies.

## Introduction
Uveal melanoma (UM), the most common primary intraocular tumor in adults, originates in melanocytes within the uveal tract and is typically treated with episcleral plaque brachytherapy (EPBT) or enucleation for larger tumors. , Primary UM has an 85% 5-year survival rate, but ∼50% of patients develop liver metastases, reducing survival to ∼15%. , 
UM typically involves GNA11 or GNAQ mutations (80%–90% of cases) and often includes BAP1 inactivation (50% of tumors), correlating with early micro-metastasis usually in the liver and poorer prognosis. Gene expression profile (GEP) stratifies tumors into two groups based on BAP1 status. Low-risk class 1 tumors have wild-type BAP1, while high-risk class 2 tumors have mutant BAP1. , 
PRAME (preferentially expressed antigen in melanoma) expression further differentiates prognosis. , , , 
Current therapies for high-risk and metastatic UM remain largely ineffective, highlighting a need for unknown biomarkers and treatments. , , , , , , , , , , , , , Key challenges include resistance to cancer treatment, as well as biological challenges in the development of in vitro and in vivo models that accurately replicate the complex biology of UM, which include cancer stem cells (CSCs) and the tumor microenvironment (TME). , , , , 
CSCs exhibit the ability for self-renewal, resistance to therapy, and long latency, driven by genetic and epigenetic changes that enable dormancy and later tumor repopulation. , , , Therefore, the long latency from primary UM tumor appearance with micro-metastasis undetectable by conventional metastatic screening tools (i.e., abdominal MRI), to the time of hepatic tumors after a prolonged disease-free period of 9.5–38 years, suggests that UM tumors may contain CSCs, although this has not been entirely demonstrated. Some cancer-stemness-associated markers found in UM cells are CD44, CD133, Nestin, OCT4, and Sox2. 
The TME encompasses all non-tumor elements, with immunological factors being particularly important. The TME, particularly the liver-specific TME in UM metastasis, complicates therapeutic development due to its unique immune cell composition. , , 
STAT3 and ANGPTL4 (angiopoietin-like 4) play critical roles in both the TME and cancer stemness, contributing to cancer development. Glioblastoma (GBM) is a therapy-resistant tumor characterized by an immunologically inactive TME and a stem-like niche, like UM. We previously identified that ANGPTL4 and STAT3 genes are overexpressed in GBM tumors and that STAT3 promotes the expression of ANGPTL4 within GBM cancer-stem-like niche. , In the present study, we show that STAT3 and ANGPTL4 are independently associated with an increased risk of metastasis in UM. UM patients with aggressive, metastatic phenotypes show a strong presence of STAT3 signaling, which promotes pathways involved in tumor cell proliferation, survival, and differentiation. , , Similarly, ANGPTL4 is induced under hypoxic conditions in the TME of UM, upregulated by HIF-1α (hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha), and contributes to both angiogenesis essential for tumor survival and metastatic spread. , , However, the association of STAT3 and ANGPTL4 with cancer stemness has not been studied in UM.
Herein, we examined the expression of STAT3, ANGPTL4, and stemness markers in UM cell lines, supplemented by cancer immune gene microarrays in patient-derived tissue from primary UM tumors and UM liver metastasis. Through rigorous multi-dimensional bioinformatical analyses of our samples and comparisons with the publicly available datasets, we identified candidate genes, which were tested by RNA-in situ hybridization (RNA-ISH). Our findings underscore the necessity of addressing both tumor-intrinsic and tumor-extrinsic components of the disease. This comprehensive methodological approach holds promise for identifying therapeutic targets and advancing drug development efforts in the challenging landscape of UM treatment.

## Results

## The gene expression of STAT3 and ANGPTL4 in UM cell lines
To determine if either STAT3 or ANGPTL4 expression correlated in UM, we analyzed The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data (Genomic Data Commons [GDC] portal, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov), which revealed a statistically significant linear correlation between STAT3 and ANGPTL4 expression (r = 0.34, p = 0.0020, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.13–0.52) ( A), with high expression linked to poor patient survival in UM ( B and 1C). Since STAT3 and ANGPTL4 are independently associated with metastasis and tumor progression in UM, we investigated their expression in three UM cell lines, OMM2.5, MEL270, and MP41, whose characteristics are described in . , , , , We then found similar expression of stem cell markers (CD44, CD133, Nestin, OCT4, and Sox2) as well as STAT3 and ANGPTL4, in the three tested UM cell lines ( A). We next examined the role of ANGPTL4 in UM by short hairpin RNA (shRNA) knockdown in MEL270 and OMM2.5 cell lines and observed a significant reduction in STAT3 expression in MEL270 cells ( B). In OMM2.5 cells, while the reduction in STAT3 was not statistically significant, the trend suggests a decrease in STAT3 expression after ANGPTL4 knockdown ( C).
 

## Pan-cancer immune profiling microarrays and comprehensive multi-dimensional analysis of cellular pathways and gene expression in primary uveal melanoma and liver metastases
Although in vitro experiments identified a link between ANGPTL4 and STAT3, cell lines do not fully reflect the complexity of UM in vivo. To address this, we performed pan-cancer immune profiling microarrays on patient-derived UM tumor tissue to analyze TME pathways in both primary UM tumors and liver metastases. We analyzed 10 enucleated eyes from UM patients with a median age of 61 years (five primary and five enucleated after plaque brachytherapy) and 14 liver samples from patients with a median age of 67 years (four metastatic uveal melanoma [mUM], five with non-tumoral liver histology, four with hepatocellular carcinoma [HCC], and one with a neuroendocrine tumor metastasis to the liver [NET]). Males accounted for 60% of the eye specimens and 50% of the liver specimens. We also considered in our analysis the timing of enucleation and nuclear BAP1 immunoreactivity in eye samples. Enucleation timing was also considered because gene expression would be expected to undergo alterations after plaque brachytherapy. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) for BAP1 was examined, since the lack of reactivity is associated with poorer survival outcomes. , , , The clinical characteristic of the patient cohorts is described in .
We conducted a microarray of pan-cancer immune genes in primary UM and liver metastases ( ). The comparison between primary and post-treatment mUM tumors identified 67 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) ( A), such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF) superfamily member TNFRSF14, nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) signaling family members (IKBKE and IRF3), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) family members (MAPK11), and PLA2G6, among others. Interestingly, PLA2G6 when mutated can lead to disorders like infantile neuroaxonal dystrophy (INAD) and neurodegeneration with brain iron accumulation (NBIA). It is well known that TNFRSF14 regulates the immune response, exhibiting both co-stimulatory and co-inhibitory functions through binding with its ligand, TNFSF14 (LIGHT). , To further understand the significance of these results, we compared the microarray data between mUM tumors in the liver to other liver tumor types, including hepatocarcinoma (HCC) and metastatic neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) to the liver as well as normal liver tissue ( B). This analysis showed that 38 genes were differentially expressed in mUM liver tumors when compared to other liver tumors and normal liver tissue ( B), suggesting that mUM patients have unique gene expression profile. More importantly, only TNFRSF14 and PLA2G6 expression were elevated in both primary UM eye tumors ( A) and UM liver metastases (mUM; B, i.e., UM-10, -11, -12 and -14). To identify signaling pathway affected in UM ( A and 3B) we used Global Significance Score (GSC) pathway analysis software, which ranks gene expression as a heatmap ( C). Metastatic UM tumors exhibited several altered signaling pathways, with complement and cell-cycle pathways most affected. Furthermore, we observed significant differences in the immunological signatures when comparing BAP1− (purple) to BAP1+ (blue) UM primary tumors ( D and 3E). Interestingly, both TNF superfamily members and a signature associated with T cell markers were found to be downregulated in only the BAP1− samples (i.e., purple colored bar) from the enucleated primary tumors ( D and 3E).
 Taken together, the results provide evidence that mUM patients with BAP1−-enucleated tumors have a unique mRNA expression pattern that is devoid of T cell infiltration genes and dysregulation of the TNF-superfamily members, including the identification of TNFRSF14.

## TCGA expression data analysis of candidate gene expression and their correlation with BAP1 expression and clinical outcomes in uveal melanoma
Based on the above mentioned results that illustrate that TNFRSF14 was elevated in both UM primary tumors and metastases in the liver ( A and 3B), we next explored pathways that may interact with TNFRSF14 expression in relation to BAP1 expression ( ). A heatmap shows the correlation of gene candidates with associated UM patient survival (gray), with or without the expression of BAP1, as follows: (1) immunological TNFRSF14, IRF4, STAT3, IKBKE, and IRF3; (2) stem cell markers CD44, NESTIN, PROM1 (CD133), and SOX2; and (3) other markers ANGPTL4, POU5FI, and PRAME ( A). PRAME is a well-known biomarker for UM. , , , We restricted our analysis to treatment-naïve ocular tumors (primarily enucleated) for outcome-expression correlations ( B). The results show a strong inverse relationship between BAP1 expression and both immunological (i.e., TNFRSF14, IKBKE, and IRF3) and stem cell-related (NESTIN) gene signatures, as well as with STAT3 or ANGPTL4. More importantly, TNFRSF14, IKBKE, and IRF3 were significantly associated with poor patient overall survival (OS) in TCGA data UM cohorts ( C–4E). The hazard ratios (HRs) for all three candidate genes indicate the power to predict OS, but in particular, increased IKBKE expression decreases OS by a factor of nine.
 

## Enhanced expression of IKBKE, IRF4, ANGPTL4, and STAT3 in UM primary tumors and UM liver metastasis
The correlation analysis ( B) showed that IRF4 was negatively correlated with IKBKE (−0.09) and positively correlated to IRF3 (i.e., +0.05; B). Yet for the stem marker PROM1 (CD133), an inverse correlation is observed, that is, IRF4 was positively correlated to CD133 (+0.06) and IRF3 negatively correlated (−0.05). To further differentiate the importance of our candidate genes, we conducted multimodal cancer data analysis on 268 UM patients utilizing cBioprtal OncoPrint analysis to determine genetic alteration for TNFRSR14, IRF3, IRF4, and IKBKE ( A). Both TNFRSR14 and IRF4 had either a deep deletion or an amplification in 5% of the cohorts. In contrast, IRF3 did not have either of the two alterations, and IKBKE had amplification in 1% of the same cohorts. More importantly, the 5-year OS was significantly impacted in the altered gene candidates, with an HR > 9 and p > 0.007 ( B).
Based on the above mentioned findings, we analyzed IKBKE, IRF4, STAT3, and ANGPTL4 expression using RNA-ISH, which visualizes gene transcripts at the cellular level ( ). Expression of these candidate genes was elevated in UM tumors compared to normal choroid tissue ( A) and in mUM livers compared to non-UM liver biopsies ( B). The findings in pan-cancer immune microarrays correlated closely with RNA-ISH data, further validating the increased expression of these genes ( C and 5D).
 

## Histopathological and cell-type-profiling reveals an immunologically “cold” tumor microenvironment
We assessed CD45 expression as a pan-leukocyte marker to provide a broad assessment of immune cell infiltration by IHC for CD45 expression, which revealed few CD45+ cells in UM primary tumors ( A and 6B). While CD45 IHC provided a basic indicator of immune cell presence, the predicted immune composition and functional profile were characterized using ROSALIND cell-type profiling of our microarray data ( A). The ROSALIND analysis of UM enucleated tumors, which profiles immune cell types present in the TME, revealed macrophages as the most abundant, followed by T helper 1 cells and cytotoxic T cells, which had lower scores as shown in .
 

## High TNFRSF14 expression is observed in tumor cells within immunologically cold primary UM tumors and metastatic UM livers
TNFRSF14 regulates the immune response, exhibiting both co-stimulatory and co-inhibitory functions through binding with its ligand, TNFSF14 (LIGHT). , To investigate whether TNFRSF14 was expressed despite a minimal inflammatory response in UM tumor tissue ( A and 6B), we performed IHC for TNFRSF14. This revealed high immunoreactivity for TNFRSF14 in primary UM tumors ( C) and liver metastasis ( D) but not in non-tumor control liver tissue ( E). Therefore, TNFRSF14 is expressed by UM cells, as evidenced by RNA-ISH in F and plotted comparatively against expression levels in UM tumors versus normal choroid within the affected eyes ( G). Our findings suggest that TNFRSF14 is a biomarker that is expressed in UM tumor cells within tumors that are immunologically “cold,” as demonstrated by IHC ( C–6G).

## Interaction plot analysis of candidate genes by our microarray data
As shown in B, interaction plot analysis of IRF4, STAT3, IKBKE, and TNFRSF14 revealed that CD44, a stemness-associated marker, was also a component of a potential interactome gene network. In contrast, CD45, a pan-leukocyte marker indicative of leukocyte infiltration in the TME, exhibited less interactions within this network as compared to CD44. This suggests that our set of candidate genes has more extensive interactions with stemness-related pathways than pathways associated with the surrounding TME, although both components were present.

## DepMap CRISPR-Cas9 in silico functional analysis
To further support the role of ANGPTL4, STAT3, IRF4, IRF3, IKBKE, and TNFRSF14 in UM, we leveraged the publicly available data from the Cancer Dependency Map (DepMap, Broad (2025). DepMap Public 24Q4. Dataset, https://depmap.org/portal/), a comprehensive resource aggregating CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screening across hundreds of human cancer cell lines, including a panel of UM-derived lines. DepMap provides gene effect scores (GESs), which quantify the impact of a gene depletion on cell viability, with more negative scores indicating a greater dependency. The mean DepMap GES for the candidate genes in both non-metastatic and metastatic UM cell lines derived from primary tumors is shown in , and the mean GES is summarized in C. Among these, IRF4 and STAT3 exhibited the strongest negative GES, suggesting that UM cells, particularly metastatic ones, rely heavily on these genes for survival. In contrast, ANGPTL4, IKBKE, and TNFRSF14 had predominantly positive mean GES, indicating that their depletion does not impact cell viability. IRF3 displayed an interesting pattern, with positive GES in metastatic cell lines but negative scores in non-metastatic ones, suggesting a potential context-dependent role.

## Discussion

## Stemness properties of UM
Herein, we conducted a comprehensive study on UM, integrating clinical data to identify significant prognostic markers associated with cancer stemness and immune pathways within the tumor and TME. Several studies have suggested some stemness properties in UM, , , , such as self-renewal, proliferative capabilities, including sphere formation, a hallmark of cancer stemness. , , , Suppression of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway significantly reduces the cancer-stem-like phenotype in UM cells, effectively inhibiting their metastatic potential and invasiveness. 
STAT3 and ANGPTL4, known for their stemness-related interactions in glioblastoma, , , , , were expressed at higher levels in metastatic UM cells compared to the non-metastatic cells, suggesting a potential role in tumor aggressiveness ( A). , , Although ANGPTL4 shRNA KD was assessed solely at the gene expression level, ANGPTL4 exhibited a proportional decrease similar to that of STAT3 ( B and 2C). This trend mirrored the significantly positive correlation between STAT3 and ANGPTL4 observed in TCGA data ( A), suggesting a potential feedback mechanism. Notably, even small changes in gene expression can impact cell viability. In silico gene effect analysis indicated that STAT3 negatively influences cell viability ( C and ), supporting the hypothesis that even minor alterations in STAT3 expression may significantly affect cell survival. 
Immune modulation and tumor progression in UM arises in the immune-privileged environment of the eye, and UM evades immune surveillance through multiple mechanisms. Primary tumors generally exhibit sparse immune cell infiltration, consistent with an immunologically “cold” profile. However, some UM tumors have high infiltration of immune cells, which portends a worse prognosis, , , , due to the presence of immunosuppressive cells such as Tregs and CD163+-tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). , , , The liver also fosters an immune-suppressive environment rich in CD68+/CD163+ TAMs that blunt T cell activity. , Better outcomes were observed in the metastatic livers of patients who had high CD8+ Granzyme B+ T cells. Checkpoint molecules (such as PD-1, PD-L1, TIM-3, LAG-3, and TIGIT) are expressed at low levels, potentially explaining poor response to checkpoint inhibitors. , 
Our data suggest that the TME in UM is dominated by macrophage-mediated immunosuppression, with a potentially ineffective adaptive immune response ( A; ). This aligns with prior findings that TAMs suppress cytotoxic T cell function in metastatic UM. , , The limited presence of Th1 and cytotoxic T cells corroborates previous observations that while CD8+ T cells infiltrate UM tumors, they often exhibit an exhausted phenotype due to immunosuppressive factors such as PD-1, LAG-3, and transforming growth factor β (TGF-β). , , , 
Other candidate genes, such as TNFRSF14, IRF4, and IKBKE, have distinct expression patterns in UM and roles in modulating the immune response. We observed that these markers were more highly expressed as determined by RNA-ISH in the tumoral regions as compared to the normal choroid intraocular counterpart and non-UM liver tissue, which correlated with our findings with targeted microarrays ( C and 5D).
TNFRSF14 plays an important role in immunity and inflammation, processes that are often altered in cancer progression. 
TNFRSF14, also known as HVEN (herpesvirus entry mediator or HVEN), is implicated in immune modulation through its interactions with T cells via LIGHT, which may allow chemo-resistant cancer stem cells to evade immune surveillance. HVEN has been thoroughly studied in viral pathogenesis. Herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) glycoprotein D (gD) binds to its HVEM, to enter activated T cells. HVEN/TNFRSF14 further interacts with herpesvirus glycoprotein D for HVEN on T cells or LIGHT. LIGHT plays a role in T cell immunity and can improve CD8+ T cell response to a target antigen(s). It is possible that TNSFRSF14 in mUM interacts with LIGHT on T cells to modulate adaptive immunity from killing the newly arrived chemo-resistant CSC. This concept can be tested in future experiments. Notably, TNFRSF14 is associated with stage IV (metastatic) pathological stages in the TCGA dataset for UM. High TNFRSF14 expression was identified here within UM cells ( ) and also correlated with poor patient survival in the TCGA dataset ( C). Co-expression of CD44 and TNFRSF14 in mUM may play a pivotal role in survival and immune regulation in liver TME ( B). , 
The interferon-related gene IRF4 was identified as a DEG across multiple comparisons and synergistically functions with TNFRSF14. , , , , 
IKBKE (inhibitor of nuclear factor-κB kinase subunit epsilon) showed strong survival correlations within the TCGA dataset ( D). IKBKE, as a member of the IKK family, phosphorylates key signaling intermediates, facilitating the degradation of inhibitor of κB (IκB) and thereby activating NF-κB to drive the expression of pro-inflammatory genes. , The TNF receptor superfamily-NF-κB axis is required for the maintenance of suppressor effector Tregs, which is mediated by IRF4 in both lymphoid and non-lymphoid tissues. 
The downstream impacts of pathways, such as STAT3/NF-κB interactions found in GBM CSCs and TNFRSF14’s immune regulatory roles, may lead to development of comprehensive therapeutic strategies for UM. An emerging hypothesis from our results is that TNFRSF14 might influence crucial cellular processes for maintaining cancer stemness, promoting cell survival and proliferation, and suppressing apoptosis by modulating NF-κB, STAT3, and IRF4 pathways. , , , The modulation of NF-κB/STAT3 signaling has been previously shown to regulate key cancer stem cell markers, including CD44 and SOX2, which are essential regulators within the CSC landscape. , Therefore, the IL-6/JAK/STAT3 pathway and TNF-α/NF-κB signaling might be central to the interplay between tumor progression, angiogenesis (likely mediated via ANGPTL4), and immune suppression in UM. , , , 
Complementary insights into BAP1 expression were derived from TCGA data, reinforcing our findings and revealing significant negative correlations between BAP1 and ANGPTL4, STAT3, and TNFRSF14 ( A and 4B). These associations underscore our candidate genes as potential robust biomarkers of tumor aggressiveness in UM.
Interestingly, the bimodal appearance of IRF3 in metastatic and non-metastatic cell lines on DepMap ( C) resembles the bimodal appearance in our microarrays where it is downregulated in liver metastases ( B) and upregulated in the primary UM tumors ( A). This bimodal expression should be examined and characterized in future studies.
Furthermore, the downregulation of TNF superfamily and T cell functions in BAP1− tumors ( D and 3E) aligns with the finding of overexpression of immunosuppressive-M2-macrophage-related markers in BAP1− tumors as compared to BAP1+ mUM tumors. Further evidence on the immunosuppressive environment in class 2 UM (BAP1−) is supported by the finding that this environment upregulates PROS1, a ligand and agonist of the receptor tyrosine kinase MERTK. This activation triggers macrophage pathways that suppress proinflammatory M1 macrophages and promote M2 macrophages, leading to suppression of the TME cytokine secretion and inhibition of T cells and other immune cells, which contribute to therapeutic resistance to tebentafusp, the only approved medication for metastatic UM. , , , , 

## Limitations of the study
While our findings provide valuable computational insights alongside limited biologically tested functional effects, further experimental validation in larger, well-matched cohorts of primary ocular tumors and liver metastases is crucial to fully define the role of CSCs and TME dynamics in UM progression. Moreover, the genetic divergence among UM cell lines and other preclinical models presents challenges in translating in vitro findings to clinical applications. Although computational analyses cannot replace direct functional assays, our in silico findings provide additional validation of gene dependencies in UM. Notably, even small changes in STAT3 expression can significantly impact cell viability, as demonstrated in DepMap analysis. Furthermore, the potential role of TNFRSF14 in immune modulation and NF-κB/STAT3 signaling warrants deeper investigation, particularly in the context of CSC maintenance, tumor proliferation, and apoptosis suppression. The variation in dependency across different UM cell lines highlights potential heterogeneity in gene reliance, which may have implications for targeted therapeutic strategies.
In conclusion, our comprehensive molecular and experimental analysis of UM highlight a convergence between intrinsic stemness-associated pathways and extrinsic immune-modulatory signals within the TME. We identified factors, particularly ANGPTL4, STAT3, IRF4, IRF3, IKBKE, and TNFRSF14, that may contribute to tumor progression by promoting a permissive, immune-evasive niche supportive of cancer-stem-like cells. Notably, TNFRSF14 expression emerged as a potential link between immune signaling and stemness properties. Our findings suggest that targeting both intrinsic (i.e., stemness pathways), and extrinsic (i.e., immune modulation) factors could hold promise for more effective UM treatments by addressing the complex interplay. Future preclinical and clinical studies centered on these dual targets may pave the way for combinatorial treatment strategies in UM.

## Experimental model and study participant details
The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) uveal melanoma analysis: survival, differential gene expression, and BAP1 expression status correlation.
RNA-seq expression and mutation data from 72 patients with primary uveal melanoma tumors were obtained from the TCGA via the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) portal. Gene expression data were downloaded as raw counts for correlation and survival analysis. Clinical annotations were retrieved for survival analyses. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated for patients stratified into high and low expression groups based on median gene expression levels, with hazard ratios (HR) calculated with a Cox proportional hazards model. Gene expression patterns were analyzed in relation to BAP1 expression status and survival outcome. A heatmap with AJCC annotation was generated by GDC portal. A correlation matrix was constructed using Spearman correlation to assess interactions between selected genes and known biomarkers (BAP1, PRAME). Positive correlations were marked in red, negative correlations in blue, and statistical significance was assessed using adjusted p-values. All statistical analyses, including correlation tests, Kaplan-Meier survival curves, and Cox proportional hazards models, were analyzed with GraphPad Prism v.10.3.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was used.

## Cell culture
The three uveal melanoma cell lines used in this study, validated OMM2.5, MEL270 from Hans E Grossniklaus (Emory University), and Dr. Sandip K Basu (UTHSC) laboratories by sequencing, and MP41 cell line purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC. VA, US), represent different tumor origins and genetic backgrounds relevant to disease progression. , , OMM2.5, derived from a liver metastasis, and MEL270, from a primary choroidal melanoma are disomic for chromosome 3, while MP41, a patient-derived xenograft (PDX) from choroidal melanoma, presents monosomy 3, a feature associated with poorer prognosis. Despite these differences, all three models share positive BAP1 and PRAME expression and display stem cell-like properties. , , A comparative chart is available in . Cell lines were cultured at 37°C and 5% CO2 in DMEM or RPMI 1640 GlutaMAX (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA), supplemented with 10% or 20% HyClone fetal bovine calf serum (HyClone Laboratories, UT, USA), and antibiotics, , , tested periodically for Mycoplasma and authenticated by STR analysis. Experiments were conducted at least three times using cells at 60–75% of confluency.

## Patient tissue preparation
FFPE tissue samples from primary tumors and liver biopsies (5 μm) were prepared on slides for staining and RNA extraction, with a pathologist examining H&E-stained slides to identify normal and tumor regions.

## Method details

## Short hairpin RNA (shRNA) gene editing
ANGPTL4 was knocked down using ANGPTL4 Human shRNA Lentivirus. Transfection involved mixing vector with DMEM and PolyFect transfection reagent (Qiagen N.V. Venlo, The Netherlands) and applying to cells pre-plated in 6-well plates. RNA was extracted 48h after transfection.

## RNA extraction
RNA was isolated from cell cultures with the Qiagen RNeasy Plus Mini Kit and from FFPE samples using the RNA RecoverAll Total Nucleic Acid Isolation Kit (Qiagen N.V. Venlo, The Netherlands). Purity was assessed via NanoDrop Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) (absorbance ratios 1.7–2.1).

## Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)
qPCR was conducted using iTaq Universal SYBR Green One-Step Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, CA, USA), with reactions including SYBR Green reagent, primers, RNA, nuclease-free water, and iScript Reverse Transcriptase. The thermal cycling protocol was specifically adapted for RNA-based qPCR analysis, normalizing to the β-actin gene as previously described. , Data are represented as mean ± range.

## Pan-cancer immune profiling
The Pan-Cancer Immune Profiling panel (NanoString) was used to quantify expression of 770 genes, including 730 cancer-related and 40 housekeeping controls. RNA was hybridized with capture and reporter probes, and signals were quantified on the nCounter digital analyzer. Data analysis with nSolver 4.0 used internal controls for normalization to ensure accuracy and reproducibility.

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
Tissue samples were deparaffinized, treated with hydrogen peroxide, and washed in PBS. Antigen retrieval used Citric Acid (pH 6.0) or Tris-EDTA (pH 9.0), with primary antibodies incubated overnight at 4°C. Secondary antibody kits included the DAB Substrate Chromogen system (Dako) and VECTASTAIN ABC-AP Kit, following manufacturer protocols. Slides were counterstained, dehydrated, and mounted for digital analysis. Antibody validation used mouse embryos and spleen samples for specificity. ## RNA in situ hybridization (RNA-ISH)
RNAScope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit v2 (Advanced Cell Diagnostics Bio-Techne; CA, USA) was used according to the manufacturer’s protocol for RNA detection. This involved deparaffinization, epitope retrieval, and sample permeabilization with Protease Plus. Target probes for ANGPTL4, STAT3, IKBKE, IRF4, and TNFRSF14 were applied and incubated. Signal amplification and chromogenic detection followed, with each probe visualized via sequential fluorophore application. Slides were mounted with Vectashield Antifade with DAPI (Vector Laboratories Inc. CA, USA) for nuclei visualization. Images were captured on a Zeiss LSM 900 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Germany), focusing on regions with high dot expression per nucleus. z stack Imaging utilized a 40x lens, with data processing via Zeiss Zen blue software to enhance image quality. Quantitative analysis was conducted using Imaris AI Viewer version 10.1.1 (Oxford Instruments plc, United Kingdom).

## DepMap CRISPR–Cas9 in-silico analysis
The functional relevance of ANGPTL4, IKBKE, IRF3, IR43, STAT3, and TNFRSF14 was determined using the publicly available Cancer Dependency Map (DepMap Release). DepMap Release aggregates Whole Genome/Exome Sequencing (copy number and mutation), RNA sequencing (expression and fusions), and Genome-wide CRISPR–Cas9 knockout screening data from Project Achilles dataset. This dataset contains genome-scale CRISPR knockout screens for Achilles (Avana Cas9 and Humagene-CD Cas12 libraries), , and Achilles combined with Sanger’s Project SCORE screens, including thousands of human cancer cell lines, including several UM models.
DepMap conducted a multi-step processing of expression data before making the CRISPRGeneEffect.csv dataset available for analysis. The resulting gene effect scores were refined using Chronos, an algorithm that integrates data across multiple libraries while applying quality control metrics and copy number correction. To mitigate confounding effect from genomic copy number variations (CNVs) inherent to the CRISPR–Cas9 essentiality screens, DepMap employs CERES, a computational model specifically designed to correct for those biases. Gene effect scores are interpreted as follows: scores below −0.3 suggest that a gene is likely essential for cell viability, as its knockout significantly reduces viability. Conversely, scores near or above zero indicate that gene disruption has little to no impact on cell survival.

## Bioinformatical analysis
Multi-dimensional analyses were conducted with nSolver 4.0 and ROSALIND software, focusing on differential expression (fold change ±1.3, p < 0.05). Pathway and gene ontology analyses used the global significance score (GSC) to assess pathway impact.

Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism v.10.3.0, using Spearman correlation coefficient (r) and two-way ANOVA to evaluate quantitative variables, with significance set at p-value < 0.05.
