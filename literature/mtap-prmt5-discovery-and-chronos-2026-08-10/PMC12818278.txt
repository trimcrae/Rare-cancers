## Highlights
•Mucinous ovarian carcinoma has a unique genomic profile compared to appendiceal and colorectal mucinous carcinoma.
•KRAS (69.4 %) and TP53 (64.3 %) gene mutations were commonly observed.
•Homologous deletion of chromosome 9p21.3, affecting CDKN2A, CDKN2B, and MTAP genes, was present in 1 out of 3 patients.
•Novel therapeutic agents can target these genetic alterations.
Keywords: Mucinous, Ovarian, Carcinoma, Genomics

## Abstract

## Objective
Mucinous ovarian carcinoma (MOC) is a rare histologic subtype of ovarian cancer. While prognosis of early stage disease is excellent, novel treatment options are needed for advanced stage and recurrent MOC, which are associated with poor oncologic outcomes. In this context, we aimed to investigate the genomic profile of MOC and characterize the prevalence of actionable genomic alterations.

## Methods
The American Association of Cancer Research Genomics Evidence of Neoplasia Information Exchange was accessed, and patients with mucinous ovarian, appendiceal and colorectal carcinoma were identified. Data from the OncoKB database, as provided by cBioPortal, were utilized to determine the presence of pathogenic gene alterations. When comparing the genomic profile of mucinous ovarian and colorectal or appendiceal carcinoma, to decrease the false discovery rate, a q-value of < 0.05 as derived from the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction procedure was deemed statistically significant.

## Results
A total of 148 patients with MOC contributing to 157 tumor samples were identified. The most commonly observed genetic alterations were in KRAS (69.4 %), TP53 (64.3 %), CDKN2A (33.3 %), CDKN2B (24 %), ERBB2 (12 %), PIK3CA (9.6 %), and ARID1A (9.0 %). BRCA1 (0 %) and BRCA2 (2.3 %) mutations were rare. Homologous deletion of chromosome 9p21.3 was present in 34.4 % of patients. Compared to mucinous appendiceal carcinoma (n = 268), MOC is characterized by a higher incidence of TP53, CDKN2A, CDKN2B, and ERBB2 and a lower rate of GNAS gene alterations (q < 0.001). Compared to colorectal mucinous adenocarcinoma (n = 358), MOC is characterized by a higher incidence of KRAS, TP53, CDKN2A, CDKN2B, and ERBB2 and a lower rate of PIK3CA, KMT2D, SMAD4, BRAF, and APC gene alterations (q < 0.001).

## Conclusions
MOC has a distinct genomic profile with molecular vulnerabilities that can be exploited using novel targeted therapies. Participation of patients with MOC in molecularly driven early phase basket trials should be encouraged.

## Introduction
Primary malignant mucinous ovarian carcinoma (MOC) is a rare histologic subtype of epithelial ovarian cancer accounting for approximately 5–10 % of all ovarian tumors ( ). While the prognosis of patients with early stage disease is overall excellent (with 5-year overall survival rates above 90 %), the oncologic outcomes of patients with advanced stage disease are dismal ( , 2020). A recent analysis of the National Cancer Database reported median overall survival of patients with stage III and stage IV disease as 15.6 and 9.3 months, respectively ( ). Moreover, a meta-analysis pooling data from seven studies demonstrated that the risk of death for patients with advanced stage MOC is almost double that of patients with serous ovarian carcinoma (hazard ratio: 1.82, 95 % CI: 1.71, 1.94) ( ). Poor oncologic outcomes in advanced stage and recurrent MOC are attributed to its relative resistance to platinum-based chemotherapy ( , ). As such, development of novel treatment options represents an ongoing unmet clinical need.
Unfortunately, research has been hampered by the difficulty of discriminating primary MOC from metastases to the ovary from other primary sites, mostly gastrointestinal origin, as well as a lack of robust preclinical models ( ). That said, rigorous histopathological techniques and recent advances in molecular tumor profiling have led to a better understanding of the pathogenesis and unique molecular signature of MOC ( , ). In the era of precision oncology, comprehensive molecular analysis can reveal unique tumor liabilities that can be translated into novel targeted therapy treatment options.
The aim of the present study was to utilize a large multi-institutional dataset to explore the molecular profile of MOC in comparison with mucinous carcinoma of the appendix and colon. We contextualize our findings by summarizing existing literature on genomic alterations in MOC and applying our results to emerging treatment strategies that can capitalize on this unique molecular landscape.

2.

## Methods
The American Association of Cancer Research Genomics Evidence of Neoplasia Information Exchange publicly available dataset version 13.0 was accessed through cBioPortal. The de-identified dataset includes somatic genomic data from patients with malignancies managed in 19 participating institutions ( ). All institutions employ next-generation sequencing assays for the detection of somatic mutations in paraffin or formalin-fixed tumor samples, using panels of varying sizes (ranging 4 to 760) following target enrichment with hybrid capture and polymerase chain reaction amplicon-based methods. Copy number alterations are also provided for a subset of samples. Further details on the methodology utilized at each institution are available in the cohort data guide ( ). Clinical stage, grade, and tumor subtype (infiltrative vs expansile) are not reported in this database.
Patients with MOC, mucinous appendiceal carcinoma, and mucinous colorectal carcinoma were identified and selected for further analysis. Given new mutations can arise at the time of tumor recurrence, multiple samples from the same patient were considered separately for analysis purposes. Data from the OncoKB database, as provided by cBioPortal, was utilized to perform functional annotation of variants, and determine pathogenic gene alterations based on the primary literature ( ). When comparing the genomic profile of MOC and appendiceal or colorectal mucinous carcinoma, to decrease the false discovery rate, a q-value of < 0.05 as derived from the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction procedure was deemed statistically significant. Since all data originate from a publicly available de-identified database, the present study was deemed exempt from Penn Medicine Institutional Review Board.

## Results
A total of 157 samples from 148 patients with MOC were identified. Median patient age at the time of sample collection was 48 years (interquartile range: 26 years). Based on available data from 123 patients, the majority were White (78.0 %). Most samples (68.8 %) were obtained from the primary tumor site, while the remainder (26.1 %) were obtained from distant metastases or recurrence.
Pathogenic mutations were most commonly observed in the KRAS (69.4 %) and TP53 (64.3 %) genes. Genetic alterations were also identified in the CDKN2A (33.3 %), CDKN2B (24.0 %), MTAP (22.0 %), ERBB2 (12.0 %), PIK3CA (9.6 %), ARID1A (9.0 %), GNAS (6.0 %), SMAD4 (5.8 %), RNF43 (5.0 %), MAP2K4 (5.0 %), and MYC (5.0 %) genes. Less frequent alterations occurred in the following genes: ERBB3 (4.0 %), KDM6A (4.0 %), PTEN (3.0 %), KEAP1 (3.0 %), BRCA2 (2.4 %), BRAF (1.9 %), and NRAS (0.9 %).
Among KRAS mutated tumors, the majority harbored missense mutations at codon 12, most commonly G12D (38.7 %) and G12V (31.8 %), whereas G12C mutations were rare (4.7 %). The PIK3CA mutation spectrum was heterogeneous (five cases of E542K, three cases of H1047R, and one each of K111E, G118D, P449R, Q546R, G1007R, M1043I). In contrast, GNAS mutations consistently involved the R201 codon.
Data on copy number alterations were available for 90 patients. The most frequently observed alterations were homologous deletion of genes CDKN2A (34.4 %, 31/90), CDKN2B (32 %, 29/90), and MTAP (27 %, 10/37), all of which were located at the 9p21.3 cytoband. Amplification of the ERBB2 gene at the 17q12 cytoband was identified in 14.4 % (13/90) of samples, while 7.8 % (7/90) demonstrated KRAS amplification at the 12p12 cytoband. MYC (7 %, 6/90) gene amplifications at the 8q24.21 cytoband were also seen in a small subset of patients. summarizes the most prevalent pathogenic genomic alterations among patients with MOC in our study.
 Compared to mucinous appendiceal carcinoma (n = 268), MOC was characterized by a higher rate of TP53 (64.3 % vs 23.5 %, q < 0.001), CDKN2A (33.3 % vs 0.4 %, q < 0.001), CDKN2B (24 % vs 0 %, q < 0.001), and ERBB2 (12 % vs 1.1 %, q < 0.001) gene alterations and a lower rate of GNAS gene alterations (6.0 % vs 45.5 %, q < 0.001). Compared to colorectal mucinous adenocarcinoma (n = 358), MOC was characterized by a higher rate of KRAS (69.4 % vs 51.1 %, q < 0.001), TP53 (64.3 % vs 42.9 %, q < 0.001), CDKN2A (33.3 % vs 2.3 %, q < 0.001), CDKN2B (24 % vs 3.7 %, q < 0.001), and ERBB2 (12 % vs 5.3 %, q = 0.02) gene alterations and a lower rate of PIK3CA (9.6 % vs 22.1 %, q < 0.001), KMT2D (8 % vs 23.8 %, q < 0.001), SMAD4 (5.8 % vs 25.2 %, q < 0.001), BRAF (1.9 % vs 21.2 %, q < 0.001), and APC (2.6 % vs 48.8 %, q < 0.001) gene alterations ( ).

## Discussion
4.1.

## Summary of main findings and comparison to existing literature
Using a multi-institutional national genomic database, the present study demonstrates that MOC has a unique molecular profile that may be harnessed for therapeutic targeting. We found frequent alterations in KRAS (69.4 %) and TP53 (64.3 %) genes in MOC samples, consistent with a high prevalence of these mutations cited across prior sequencing studies ( , , , , , , , , , ). Approximately one third of cases also exhibited homologous copy number loss of chromosome 9p21.3, affecting the genes CDKN2A, CDKN2B and MTAP, which play important roles in cell cycle control and cell metabolism. Copy number alterations in chromosome 9p have been previously reported in MOC but more often in the setting of loss of heterozygosity rather than homologous deletion ( , , ). Additionally, we identified ERBB2 amplifications (12.0 %) and mutations in PIK3CA (9.6 %) and ARID1A (9.0 %) as observed in other studies but with varying frequencies. Our study adds to existing evidence supporting MOC as a separate biological entity from mucinous carcinoma of gastrointestinal origin. With regard to MAPK pathway alterations, we identified higher rates of KRAS mutations and lower rates of BRAF mutations in MOC compared to mucinous gastric or colorectal carcinoma, in line with prior studies ( , ). Mutations in APC and SMAD4 affecting Wnt and TGF-β signaling pathways, respectively, were less frequently observed in MOC while mutations in ERBB2 and CDKN2A were more commonly harbored in MOC compared to mucinous colorectal carcinoma, also consistent with prior findings ( , ). Lastly, Feltmate et al. demonstrated more frequent loss of heterozygosity involving chromosome 9p in MOC, similar to our study, compared to mucinous appendiceal carcinoma ( ). These comparisons reinforce the genomic distinctions of MOC and highlight potentially actionable differences that could guide therapeutic strategies.

4.2.

## Genetic alterations in MOC as novel therapeutic targets
The predominance of KRAS mutations in MOC highlights MAPK pathway activation as a defining feature of MOC and a valuable therapeutic target. For decades, KRAS was considered an “undruggable” target, however a number of anti-KRAS agents are currently under development or have been recently introduced into clinical practice. KRAS-G12C inhibitors, sotorasib and adagrasib, are approved treatments for non-small cell lung cancer and, more recently, metastatic colorectal cancer in combination with anti-EGFR therapy ( , , ). In addition, a small-molecule inhibitor targeting KRAS-G12D mutations (MRTX1133), present in approximately one-third of patients with MOC in our cohort, is under development and has demonstrated strong anti-tumor activity in patient-derived xenograft models ( ). Other investigational drugs include a pan-KRAS small-molecule protein–protein interaction inhibitor (BI 1701963) that blocks the interaction between SOS1 and KRAS necessary for KRAS activation and is being studied as monotherapy or in combination with trametinib, a MEK inhibitor (NCT04111458). Downstream inhibition of the MAPK pathway using MEK inhibitor combinations in genotype-matched phase I or phase II clinical trials for epithelial ovarian cancers, including one case of MOC, has shown further promising results in terms of disease response ( ). Lastly, the absence of KRAS mutations may actually identify a subset of patients more likely to benefit from anti-EGFR monoclonal antibody therapy with cetuximab acting at an upstream target ( ).
Frequent homozygous deletion of chromosome 9p21.3, which encodes the CDKN2A, CDKN2B and MTAP genes, also introduces therapeutic opportunities. The CDKN2A gene encodes the proteins p14ARF and p16, which inhibit formation of the CDK4/6 complex, a key regulator of the G1/S transition, thereby promoting cell cycle control ( , ). CDK4/6 inhibitors are commonly used to treat hormone receptor-positive breast cancer and are an attractive treatment option for MOC ( ). The modest activity of CDK4/6 monotherapy in pancreatic and gastric carcinoma has been attributed to cell cycle plasticity ( , ). However, CDK4/6 resistance can be overcome with combination strategies, namely MEK inhibitors and MDM2 inhibitors, and should be further explored in MOC. Co-deletion of MTAP, a key metabolic enzyme involved in methionine salvage, at this chromosome region also creates metabolic vulnerability in tumors that is exploitable through synthetic lethality ( ). Large scale RNAi screens (Project DRIVE and Achilles) helped identify MAT2A inhibitors (AG-270) and PRMT5 inhibitors (TNG-908, MRTX-1719), which can act synergistically with antimitotic agents like paclitaxel in preclinical models ( ).
Interestingly, a recent study utilizing data from The Cancer Genome Atlas Program, demonstrated that chromosome 9p21 loss, regardless of tumor site, results in a “cold” tumor-immune phenotype and resistance to immune checkpoint inhibition ( ). Markers of immunogenicity are rare in MOC with only 4.2 % of samples exhibiting high microsatellite instability, 4.8 % with high tumor mutational burden, and 5.3 % expressing PD-L1 in one analysis of MOC using a commercial database ( ). Similarly, an analysis of immune cell density of 126 patients with MOC identified a “cold” tumor-immune phenotype in 86 % of samples ( ). To date, there is no evidence to support the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors in MOC, thus warranting further investigation of alterative immunotherapy strategies. For example, one phase II trial in patients with pancreatic cancer showed that administration of a CD40 agonist in combination with chemotherapy can transform a “cold” to “hot” tumor-immune environment, a strategy that could be explored in MOC ( ).
Approximately one in ten patients with MOC in our study harbored ERBB2 gene amplification, which encodes HER2, a receptor tyrosine kinase that regulates cell growth and proliferation pathways. Trastuzumab is an anti-HER2 monoclonal antibody that has been incorporated in the management of patients with advanced or recurrent uterine serous carcinoma and breast cancer exhibiting ERBB2 amplification. In 33 cases of MOC, McAlpine et al. observed an 18.2 % rate of HER2 amplification and reported dramatic disease response in one patient with recurrent, HER2-amplified MOC treated with conventional chemotherapy combined with trastuzumab, while another patient experienced recurrence after trastuzumab therapy ( ). Off label use of novel HER2/neu antibody-drug conjugates (ADC), such as trastuzumab deruxtecan, can also be considered given encouraging results from recent basket phase II trials reporting 50 % objective response rate in ovarian cancer patients with HER2 amplification ( ). The BOUQUET trial is an ongoing phase II trial (NCT04931342) evaluating the use of trastuzumab emtansine, another ADC, for the treatment of persistent or recurrent rare epithelial ovarian cancers, including MOC.
Other commonly mutated genes in MOC, each affecting approximately 10 % of cases, included PIK3CA and ARID1A. Patients with PIK3CA mutations, which lead to constitutive PI3K activation driving oncogenesis, may benefit from mTOR or AKT inhibition. As mentioned previously, a partial response to dual MEK/PI3K inhibition was observed in a case of MOC enrolled in a molecularly matched driven phase I/II trial ( ). ARID1A encodes a subunit of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex that regulates access to DNA for gene transcription. Therapies that target mutations in this gene could include epigenetic modifiers, such as BET inhibitors or EZH2 inhibitors, or ATR inhibitors, which demonstrate enhanced synthetic lethality when combined with other DNA damaging agents ( , ). The rate of BRCA2 somatic mutation in our cohort was 2.4 %, which is in line with a recent systematic review reporting a prevalence of germline BRCA mutations of 2.5 % among 244 patients with MOC ( ). That said, somatic and homologous recombination deficiency testing could potentially reveal a subset of patients with BRCA-mutated MOC that could benefit from treatment with PARP inhibitors.
A major strength of the present study is the inclusion of a large number of patients with a rare ovarian tumor from multiple academic institutions following thorough pathologic review permitting meaningful genomic analyses. However, several limitations should be mentioned. Firstly, as previously discussed, genomic data derive from multiple centers each utilizing different somatic testing platforms with different gene panel sets and tumor content criteria. In addition, data on tumor recurrence and patient survival were not available, precluding the analysis of specific mutational impact on oncologic outcomes. Lastly, given absence of central pathology review, possible tumor misclassification cannot be excluded and discrimination between expansile and infiltrative pattern tumors could not be performed.

4.3.

## Conclusions
MOC is characterized by a distinct molecular profile with multiple actionable genetic alterations for targeted therapy. Robust preclinical MOC models are urgently needed to provide strong evidence to support molecularly driven phase II trials. Participation of patients with recurrent MOC in molecularly driven basket trials should also be encouraged.
