Thus, we developed a CRISPR-based method for hit validation that tests the effect of a genetic perturbation on cell fitness. Moreover, the CelFi assay can be utilized to evaluate gene dependencies and test new hypotheses, regardless of variations in single guide RNA optimization, ribonucleoprotein concentration, and gene copy number.

## Introduction
High-throughput genetic screens are an important tool in exploratory research. Often referred to as hypothesis-generating rather than hypothesis-driven research, screens are powerful techniques for understanding cellular pathways and mechanistic relationships . Pooled screens can identify genes related to specific cellular processes such as cell survival, growth or signaling. Until the discovery of CRISPR technology, these studies were often performed using RNA interference (RNAi) – . However, RNAi screens can suffer from unpredictable off-target effects and incomplete silencing of genes – . These limitations increase the risk of both false positives and false negatives being identified by RNAi screens , .
With advances in CRISPR technology, pooled CRISPR screens have become standard practice for loss-of-function large-scale screens. CRISPR knockout (KO) screens work by generating a library of single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) that either target genome-wide protein-encoding genes or a subset of genes – . The library of generated sgRNAs is then introduced into a pool of cells via lentiviral transduction at multiplicities of infection that are low enough to ensure that an individual sgRNA is integrated into the genome of most transduced cells. Thus, a pool of cells in which each cell has a single genetic perturbation is generated. Selective pressure is then applied, which can be anything from the application of a drug to the passing of time. The result of the selective pressure should create a phenotypic difference that enriches the desired outcome. Next, the selected pool is harvested and the genomically integrated sgRNAs are amplified and sequenced using next-generation sequencing (NGS) to look for depleted or enriched sgRNA sequences. The identities of these sgRNAs are then mapped back to the corresponding target genes to produce a list of potential genes of interest or “hits”.
Many pooled CRISPR screens rely on cellular fitness or viability assays as the phenotypic readout and are often called “live/dead” screens. This type of pooled CRISPR screen is commonly utilized in drug discovery programs to identify genetic vulnerabilities, such as cancer-specific genetic dependencies that are absent in normal cells , . By understanding which genes a specific cancer cell line depends on for survival, new drug targets can be uncovered.
The Cancer Dependency Map (DepMap) is a large-scale effort that aims to plot the landscape of cancer vulnerabilities using a variety of techniques including pooled CRISPR and RNAi screens . This massive effort has led to the completion and analysis of over 1000 pooled CRISPR KO screens in as many cell lines using a near genome-wide CRISPR KO library. The scale of DepMap allows inferences about gene essentiality across different cell types and cancer subtypes. If the KO of a gene causes a significant growth disadvantage in a given cell line, the gene is considered a dependency . Genes that are annotated as ‘common essential’ are genes that are important to the fitness of most tested cell lines. DepMap can also be mined for cell type–specific gene dependencies and has proven to be an invaluable resource for the discovery of cancer-specific dependencies. However, there are limitations to any pooled screening effort. For example, gene copy number, off-target effects, gene coverage, rate of cell growth, and targeting efficiency are all confounding factors of pooled CRISPR screens. In attempts to address these limitations, several different computational methods have been developed – . DepMap uses Chronos, an algorithm that models cell population dynamics in CRISPR screens to provide a score for each gene in a given cell line . The lower the Chronos score, the more likely a given perturbation is a dependency in a given cell line. For example, a Chronos score of 0 signifies that a gene is not essential, whereas common essential genes have a median Chronos score of -1. Even with these computationally predictive algorithms, identified hits must be functionally validated. A reliable, straightforward, and accessible assay is needed to validate hits before follow-up experimentation and lead generation.
Here, we present CelFi, a facile and robust assay to validate hits identified from pooled CRISPR KO screens. This CRISPR-based method measures the effect of a genetic perturbation on cell fitness by directly editing the gene of interest and monitoring the indel profile over time.

## Results

## The CelFi assay measures gene effect on cellular fitness
The CelFi assay is a straightforward process in which cells are transiently transfected with ribonucleoproteins (RNPs) composed of SpCas9 (Streptococcus pyogenes) protein complexed with an sgRNA targeting the gene of interest. The RNP complex will bind and cleave the target gene resulting in a DNA double-strand break. These breaks will be repaired predominantly by endogenous non-homologous end joining, which is error-prone and can result in small insertions or deletions (indels) of nucleotides at the site of the double-stranded break. Because most cell lines contain two or more copies of each target gene, the RNP-transfected pool of cells will contain a mixture of cells containing: (1) only in-frame indels, (2) only out-of-frame (OoF) indels, (3) a mixture of in-frame, OoF, and 0-bp indels, or (4) all wild-type (WT) or 0-bp indels (Fig.  A). If knocking out the target gene causes a growth defect or enhancement, cells with loss-of-function indels—usually OoF indels, but sometimes in-frame indels—will either decrease or increase in number over time. This primarily changes the total number of OoF indels in the cell pool.

 

To measure the OoF indels over time, genomic DNA is collected from the pool of cells at days 3, 7, 14, and 21 post transfection and assessed via targeted deep sequencing. The sequence files are then analyzed with a modified version of our previously published CRIS.py program , which categorizes indels into three bins: in-frame, OoF, and 0-bp indels. Individual cells in the population may also contain a mixture of in-frame, OoF, and 0-bp indels. However, it is not possible to plot these mixed indels when examining the bulk pool of cells. Moreover, 0-bp and in-frame indels generally result in a functional protein, while OoF indels should result in the KO of the target gene. In some cases, such as splicing alterations or targeting a critical domain, in-frame mutations can also lead to aberrant protein function and are also tracked using the CelFi assay.
To functionally validate the CelFi assay, we tested two genes (NUP54 and RAN) for their effect on Nalm6 cellular fitness. The DepMap portal lists both NUP54 and RAN as common essential genes with Chronos scores of -0.998 and − 2.66 in Nalm6 cells, respectively (DepMap 21Q3). As a negative control, we targeted an intron of PPP1R12C known as the adeno-associated virus integration site 1 (AAVS1) locus. The AAVS1 site is commonly used as a safe-harbor site for targeted integration because disruption of the site has no identified adverse effects on cells – . As expected, the OoF indels did not change over time when the non-coding region of PPP1R12C was targeted (Fig.  B). Conversely, when RAN was targeted, there was a dramatic drop in the proportion of OoF indels between days 3 and 7, with few OoF alleles remaining by day 21. Additionally, the CelFi assay correlated well with the Chronos scores, with the more negative Chronos score showing more marked decreases in OoF indels over time (Fig.  B). To further validate the CelFi assay, we selected five genes from DepMap covering a range of Chronos scores and tested them in Nalm6, HCT116, and DLD1 cell lines (Fig.  A). We chose these cell lines as they carry a stable, diploid or near diploid karyotype, reducing variables associated with copy number variation , . Together, these parental cells represent both adherent and suspension cell states. The non-coding target (AAVS1 site) was included as a negative control. MPC1 has a positive Chronos score in all three cell lines, while ARTN, NUP54, POLR2B, and RAN have increasingly negative Chronos scores. The loss of OoF indels over time correlated well with their cell line specific Chronos scores (Fig.  A and Table ). The initial sgRNA activity measured at day 3 post-transfection varied between cell lines and loci (Fig. A–C). To normalize the results between experiments, we developed the fitness ratio to capture the magnitude of change in OoF indels over time by normalizing the percentage of OoF indels at day 21 to day 3 (Fig.  B). A fitness ratio of 1 represents no change in OoF indels between day 3 and day 21, whereas a fitness ratio less than 1 signifies a decrease in OoF indels overtime. As expected, the non-coding control and MPC1 targets cluster around a fitness ratio of 1 in all three lines. Conversely, a more negative Chronos score generally resulted in an observed lower fitness ratio (Fig.  B). We also calculated the fitness ratio using the total indels including in-frame and OoF indels and observed very similar ratios to the OoF-only fitness ratios (Fig. D–F).

 

## Evaluating cell line specific vulnerabilities
Some genes are essential to the survival of all cells while others are specific to a particular cell line. We next sought to determine if the CelFi assay could be used to demonstrate cell line specific vulnerabilities. We selected six genes which showed variability in their Chronos scores across three cell lines. We assayed each gene across all three lines and determined the fitness ratios (Fig.  A–C and Fig. S2). All genes demonstrated fitness defects in the expected cell lines (red bars in Fig.  A–C). However, we observed unexpected, notable fitness defects in all three cell lines when SLC25A19 was targeted (Fig.  A–C). To determine if this result was due to sgRNA design, we performed a follow-up assay for SLC25A19 using all four sgRNAs from the Avana library used in the DepMap screens. The CelFi assay showed that loss of SLC25A19 resulted in a fitness defect in all three cell lines with all sgRNAs tested, suggesting that SLC25A19 was a false negative in the original pooled CRISPR screens for both HCT116 and DLD1 cell lines (Fig.  D).

 

## Impact of sgRNA activity on fitness ratio
To determine if overall sgRNA activity affects the fitness ratio, we performed a dilution series to reduce the total activity of an RNP targeting POLR2B. We briefly complexed RNP at a 3:1 ratio of sgRNA: Cas9 and serially diluted before transfection and indel analysis. Although the reduction in RNP concentration resulted in a lower total percentage of indels, the fitness ratio was not significantly changed, even at a 32-fold dilution (Fig.  A and Fig. S3). This suggests the CelFi assay is an appropriate tool to evaluate sgRNAs with low activity or cell lines with low transfection efficiencies.

 

## Gene copy number can affect fitness ratio
Aneuploidy is common in cancer and immortalized cell lines. We hypothesized that cells with a higher copy number of a target gene would show a smaller reduction in cellular fitness because more cells in the transfected pool would have a mixture of in-frame and OoF indels. To assess the effect of copy number on the fitness ratio, we targeted PPIL2, which has a similar Chronos score across Nalm6, U2OS, and K562 cells (-1.51, -1.29, and − 1.57, respectively), but varying gene copy numbers in these cell lines (2, 5, and 7, respectively). PPIL2 loss negatively affected cell fitness in all three cell lines, resulting in a fitness ratio of less than 1 in all test cases. However, the effect was less marked in K562, which has the highest copy number (Fig.  B and Fig. S4). While the copy number did appear to influence the fitness ratio, all three cell lines still exhibited a decrease in fitness.

## The CelFi assay can examine gene effects on cellular fitness in response to drug treatment
We next evaluated whether the CelFi assay could be used to identify mechanisms of action. We performed a genome-wide pooled CRISPR screen in BCR-ABL + B-ALL BV-173 cells to identify genes involved in dihydroartemisinin (DHA)-mediated suppression of MCL-1 in a previous study . Following this screen, we generated a clonal EIF2AK1 KO BV-173 cell line to confirm that the loss of EIF2AK1 rendered BV-173 cells resistant to DHA-induced death. In our current study, we tested the effect of EIF2AK1 KO followed by DHA treatment using the CelFi assay (Fig.  A). We first generated EIF2AK1-edited pools in BV-173 cells and then treated the pools with DHA or DMSO (control). EIF2AK1 has little impact on cell fitness under normal circumstances and the average Chronos score is near 0 in the cell lines tested on the DepMap portal. As such, the indel profile remained constant under DMSO treatment. However, DHA treatment caused the loss of 0-bp indels and an increase in OoF indels in the EIF2AK1 edited pool (Fig.  B). The fitness ratio for the different treatments also reflected these results, with the DMSO treatment ratio at ~1.0 and the DHA ratio being greater than 1.5, showing a growth advantage of cells containing EIF2AK1 KO post-DHA treatment (Fig.  C). This further confirms that DHA-mediated killing of BV-173 cells works through the gene product of EIF2AK1.

 

## The CelFi assay can identify false positive gene hits from pooled CRISPR KO screens
As discussed earlier, the CelFi assay can identify false negative hits from pooled CRISPR KO screens. As shown in Fig.  , SLC25A19 was predicted to be a genetic vulnerability in the Nalm6 cell line, but not in the DLD1 or HCT116 cell lines based on Chronos scores (Table ). However, we discovered that SLC25A19 shows a fitness defect in all three cell lines with all sgRNAs tested, including those from the Avana library (Fig.  D). We hypothesized that finding false-positive hits would be more difficult than finding false-negative hits based on Chronos score. However, when assessing a handful of reported common essential genes, we identified OTOP1 as a potential false-positive hit. We tested the fitness of OTOP1 in two different cell lines with sgRNAs targeting early, mid, and late gene exons. In both tested cell lines, the average fitness ratio between the three sgRNAs was near 1, indicating OTOP1 has a negligible effect on fitness in these backgrounds (Fig.  A). The cutting efficiency varied at the three cut sites, however all three sites showed very little change in OoF indels between day 3 and D21 (Fig. S5). It is important to note that the Chronos score is a computationally derived prediction of genetic dependency, and these scores change with additional data and algorithmic updates. At the time of our assessment, OTOP1 had a Chronos score of -1.267 and − 0.962 in HCT116 and U2OS cell lines, respectively (DepMap 21Q3). Although the Chronos score has changed over time and has gone up in recent quarters, the score is consistently less than 0 in every quarter surveyed, and OTOP1 is still listed as a common essential gene showing the importance of functionally validating CRISPR screen hits (Fig.  B).

 

## Discussion
Pooled CRISPR screens have become an important tool for functional genomics. This high-throughput discovery method has furthered our understanding of genes and pathways and has led to the identification of many drug targets . Large scale screening efforts like DepMap are extremely valuable and yield a treasure trove of data that can be mined and validated for many years, and which have already led to potential new drug targets , . Data analysis, prioritization, and identification of high-quality hits is critical to reducing the time and cost associated with validating results from pooled CRISPR screens. Even still, the top hits from a pooled screen need functional validation. Here, we present the CelFi assay; a quick and robust CRISPR-based method to validate the effects of gene perturbation on cellular fitness.
Unlike pooled CRISPR screens, which monitor the effect of a given perturbation by observing sgRNA enrichment or depletion after applying selective pressure, the CelFi assay tracks the editing outcomes at the target gene and correlates these with a growth advantage or disadvantage of individual clones within a pool of cells. A similar strategy has been previously described to track base-editor induced point mutations after applying selective pressure . The CelFi assay works by monitoring indels in the gene of interest over time in a pool of edited cells. Cells that contain only OoF indels resulting in the KO of a gene that causes a growth defect will decrease over time, resulting in a corresponding decrease in OoF indels. The more quickly a gene product affects cell survival, the more rapidly OoF indels will decline. This is particularly evident with the example of RAN which exhibited a rapid decline in the proportion of OoF indels. This drop was expected and agrees with the DepMap data as RAN has one of the lowest Chronos scores across all tested cell lines.
The simplicity of the CelFi assay allows for the interrogation of any gene in any cell line that can be transfected and cultured in a matter of days. During our experimental trials, perturbation of the genes often showed effects on cellular fitness in as little as seven days post-nucleofection. Although cellular fitness changes were often observed by day seven, there is the potential for effects on cell fitness to appear over longer periods. As a result of this, time points were taken out to day 21. Additionally, because the timing of the editing process can vary between cell lines, the harvest times can be adjusted such that a baseline indel profile can be compared to a late time point. The flexibility of the CelFi assay allows for the extension or adjustment of the observation period with minimal additional effort or cost.
In addition to the above limitations, hit identification in large-scale screens such as pooled CRISPR screens and RNAi screens can be hindered by off-target effects – . For this reason, most CRISPR libraries include multiple sgRNAs per gene to increase the statistical confidence in a given observation. However, high off-target editing by one sgRNA can confound results. Therefore, sgRNA design is an important part of screen validation and the CelFi assay. If possible, we recommend using a newly designed sgRNA that is not found in the original screening library to ensure that a hit is not the result of a specific off-target event from a given sgRNA. Similarly, using more than one sgRNA to validate a hit using the CelFi assay can also increase confidence in each target. We do not recommend putting more than one sgRNA in the same transfection pool, as the resulting indels will be complex and difficult to assess. Rather, distinct CelFi assays should be conducted with each sgRNA.
Although presented here as a fitness ratio built upon the observance of OoF indels over time, the CelFi assay monitors in-frame indels, OoF indels, and alleles of unchanged length. The ability to monitor the frequency of all allele lengths is a strength of the CelFi assay. Although in-frame mutations are often considered non-deleterious, their occurrence in critical or catalytic domains can also lead to similar outcomes as loss-of-function alleles, whilst still being transcribed and translated. Guide design can also help tease out critical domains and be further optimized to bias toward in-frame or OoF indels depending on the desired outcome. Bioinformatic tools such as inDelphi are helpful in predicting the outcome of CRISPR editing, which can inform guide design . Moreover, if the rate of both OoF and in-frame indels goes down over time resulting in the percentage of 0-bp or WT indels increasing, this likely indicates that the perturbation is causing a fitness defect.
Throughout our study, the CelFi assay proved quite robust and was able to validate hits even when cutting efficiencies were low or when gene copy number was high. Although increased gene copy number may have muted the effect of the perturbation and the corresponding fitness ratio, the result still predicted a fitness defect based on a fitness ratio < 1 (Fig.  B). These results further demonstrate the validity of the assay, suggesting that differences in delivery, sgRNA design, and other factors that may reduce cutting efficiency should not necessarily lead to false-negative results with the CelFi assay. Moreover, although we primarily used RNP and nucleofection to deliver the CRISPR components, other delivery formats such as plasmid and lipid-based delivery methods are also compatible with the CelFi assay. Additionally, our trials were performed in both adherent and suspension cell lines to ensure that these growth requirements did not alter our findings. Although we mainly used time as the selective pressure in the trials presented here, other selective pressures can be applied and combined with the CelFi assay. For example, we demonstrated how the CelFi assay was used to validate a gene involved in BV173 B-ALL cells response to DHA treatment. Additionally, we have previously used the CelFi assay to confirm that NSD1 loss conferred resistance to an EZH2 inhibitor in SMARCB1-deficient rhabdoid tumor cell lines . The CelFi assay has also been used to confirm neo-splicing in oncogenic fusions that were computationally predicted showing that this assay can test novel hypotheses and is not completely reliant on data from pooled CRISPR screens . The CelFi assay has also shown utility when co-dependency or gene compensation of two or more genes was suspected . We also found that CelFi assays were able to identify both false-negative and false-positive hits resulting from pooled CRISPR KO screens (Fig.  and Fig. ). While we did not investigate the reasons behind the deviations between the CelFi assay and the primary pooled CRISPR KO screen, we speculate that these differences could be due to off-target editing, gRNA design, suboptimal gRNA transcription, and/or RNA stability. Additionally, large-scale screens rely on population modelling, which is constantly being improved as new datasets are made available. Improved modelling will likely reduce these discrepancies in the future. Moreover, we expected that false negatives would be likely based on the nature of a large-scale screen, but we were surprised to easily detect a false-positive hit. This further underlines the necessity of follow-up hit validation following a pooled screen. Additionally, when looking at screening results from a group of cell lines from the same lineage or subtype, it is beneficial to test resulting hits from each line in other associated lines to draw broader conclusions. This is easily achieved using the CelFi assay.
Like all assays, the CelFi assay has limitations that should be considered. First, the CelFi assay is a PCR-based assay, which means that sequence context including high GC-content and repetitive sequences can create difficulties in amplifying the region of interest. Difficult regions can typically be avoided by moving the sgRNA target site to a less GC-rich or less repetitive region. PCR-based assays also suffer from PCR-bias in which smaller amplicons are preferentially amplified. Because the CelFi assay relies on measuring the ratio of different length indels, large indels may bias the ratios toward one specific indel length. To avoid this, we recommend that indels over 40 bp be removed from all time points. By removing these large and less common indels from the total indels, the ratios of more common and smaller indels can be assessed over time. This is easily done using the CRIS.py analysis tool by adjusting the start_seq and end_seq. The assay does not require the capture of the depletion of every individual cell that dies as a result of perturbation, as such deletions that are too large to detect via short read sequencing do not fundamentally change the assay outcome. Testing multiple guides in different locations during validation can also mitigate these concerns. Another limitation of the CelFi assay as presented is that it relies on targeted deep sequencing, which might be cost prohibitive if only a few CelFi assays are needed. In general, we recommend performing all follow-up CelFi assays for a given screen at the same time, barcoding the resulting amplicons, and pooling all samples into a single NGS run. This will reduce the overall cost per assay, and when paired with our modified CRIS.py analysis program, will reduce the analysis time. With that said, we have confirmed the ability to use the CelFi assay and standard Sanger sequencing followed by a trace deconvolution method, Tracking of Indels by DEcomposition (TIDE) , to quantify and manually bin and track OoF indels over time (Fig. S6). Another potential limitation of the CelFi assay is that some cell lines are highly dependent on cell densities. If significant cell death is observed at early time points due to high levels of OoF indels with few WT or in-frame alleles remaining, the amount of gRNA added can be reduced or a less active gRNA can be used to ensure the survival of some cells. Alternatively, WT cells can be spiked in to prevent complete cell loss.
Though pooled CRISPR screens are vital for unbiased gene function analysis, follow-up hit validation is critical. The CelFi assay offers a quick and reliable validation of fitness defects and is designed to complement follow-up efforts for identified hits from pooled CRISPR KO screens. For preliminary screens yielding more than 20 hits, we recommend conducting a secondary, targeted screen to identify the most promising candidates. The CelFi assay also provides the ability to examine cellular vulnerabilities, making it a valuable asset when screening for therapeutic targets or examining microenvironment changes. One of the greatest strengths of the CelFi assay is its ability to save time and resources due to its short duration and minimal hands-on time. It can save months that might otherwise be spent validating potential cellular vulnerabilities. The CelFi assay can assess observed hits from most pooled CRISPR KO screens or test novel hypothesis and can be performed despite differences in sgRNA optimization, RNP concentration, and copy number variations.

## Materials and methods

## Cell culture and transfection for generating KO cell pools
Nalm6 cells (American Type Culture Collection, ATCC; CRL-3273) were cultured in RPMI-1640 media (ATCC, 30-2001), supplemented with 10% (vol/vol) FBEssence (Avantor), 1% (vol/vol) Penicillin-Streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 1X GlutaMAX (Thermo Fisher Scietific) at 37 °C, 5% (vol/vol) CO2 in a humidified incubator. Nalm6 KO cell pools were generated using CRISPR-Cas9 technology. One million Nalm6 cells were nucleofected (4D-Nucleofector™ X-unit; Lonza) per the manufacturer’s recommended protocol with pre-complexed RNPs consisting of 100 pmol chemically modified sgRNA (Table S2) and 33 pmol 3XNLS SpCas9 protein (St. Jude Protein Production Shared Resource) using P3 nucleofector solution and CV-104 program in 20-µL cuvettes.
HCT116 cells (ATCC, CCL-247) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium, supplemented with 10% (vol/vol) FBEssence, 1% (vol/vol) Penicillin-Streptomycin, and 1X GlutaMAX at 37 °C, 5% (vol/vol) CO2 in a humidified incubator. HCT116 KO cell pools were generated by nucleofecting one million HCT116 cells as described above using P3 nucleofector solution and CA-137 program in 20-µL cuvettes.
DLD1 cells (ATCC, CCL-221) were cultured in RPMI-1640 media, supplemented with 10% (vol/vol) FBEssence, 1% (vol/vol) Penicillin-Streptomycin, and 1X GlutaMAX at 37 °C, 5% (vol/vol) CO2 in a humidified incubator. DLD1 KO cell pools were generated by nucleofecting one million DLD1 cells as described above using P3 nucleofector solution and CA-137 program in 20-µL cuvettes.
U2OS cells (ATCC, HTB-96) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium, supplemented with 10% (vol/vol) FBEssence, 1% (vol/vol) PenStrep, and 1X GlutaMAX at 37 °C, 5% (vol/vol) CO2 in a humidified incubator. U2OS KO cell pools were generated by nucleofecting one million U2OS cells as described above using P3 nucleofector solution and CM-104 program in 20-µL cuvettes.
K562 cells (ATCC, CCL-243) were cultured with Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium, supplemented with 10% (vol/vol) FBEssence, 1% (vol/vol) Penicillin-Streptomycin, and 1X GlutaMAX at 37 °C, 5% (vol/vol) CO2 in a humidified incubator. K562 KO cell pools were generated by nucleofecting 500,000 K562 cells as described above using P3 nucleofector solution and FF-120 program in 20-µL cuvettes. Each experiment was performed in triplicate unless stated otherwise with biological replicates. Cells were cultured in 6-well tissue culture plates until the last timepoint and maintained at ~ 80% confluency. All cells were tested and monitored for mycoplasma using the MycoAlertPlus™ Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza, LT07-710) and were authenticated using the PowerPlex® Fusion System (Promega #DC2402) performed at the Hartwell Center (St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital).

## Time-point collection
Cells were collected on days 3, 7, 14, and 21 post-transfection unless stated otherwise. To harvest genomic DNA, cells were pelleted by centrifugation at > 500 × g for 5 min. Twenty-five microliters of crude extract buffer (10 mM Tris, pH 8; 2 mM EDTA; 0.2% Triton X-100; 200 µg/mL proteinase K) per 5000 cells was added to the cell pellet and lysed at 65 °C for 15 min, before they were inactivated by incubation at 95 °C for 5 min.

## DHA treatment assay
An EIF2AK1-edited pool was generated in the BCR-ABL + B-ALL cell line BV-173. BV-173 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 media supplemented with 10% (vol/vol) FBEssence, 1% (vol/vol) Penicillin-Streptomycin, 1X GlutaMAX, 1% (vol/vol) non-essential amino acid solution, 1% (vol/vol) HEPES, and 0.8% (vol/vol) BME. The CAGE410.EIF2AK1.g4 guide (Table S2) was delivered by nucleofection using P3 solution and program CA-137. Editing was confirmed via NGS before the pool was expanded. Two million cells were used per replicate, and each treatment was performed in triplicate. Cells were seeded in 2 mL media containing 25 µM DHA (in DMSO) or DMSO only (d0) for 2 days and then replaced with fresh media to wash out the treatment. Cells were cultured for an additional 5 days to monitor recovery. Samples were harvested at day 1, 2, 4, and 7 post-treatment and were analyzed by NGS.

## NGS analysis
Target-specific amplicons were generated, indexed, and sequenced via NGS as previously described . Briefly, cell pellets were lysed and used to generate gene-specific amplicons with partial Illumina adapters in PCR#1. These amplicons were indexed in PCR#2 and pooled with other targeted amplicons for various loci to create sequence diversity. Additionally, 10% PhiX Sequencing Control V3 (Illumina) was added to the pooled amplicon library before running the sample on an MiSeq Sequencer System (Illumina) to generate paired 2 × 250-bp reads. Samples with less than 100 reads were repeated and resubmitted. Primer sequences for each amplicon are listed in Table S3. Sequencing data was demultiplexed using index sequences. FASTQ files were generated and analyzed using CRIS.py .

## TIDE analysis
Following time-point collection, PCR amplification was performed using MyTaq DNA Polymerase HS Red Mix (Meridian Biosciences #BIO-25047) and repliQa HiFi ToughMix (Quantabio #740609.50 S) with the following respective conditions: (94 °C for 1 min; 35 × 94 °C for 15 s, 56 °C for 15 s, 72 °C for 1 min; 72 °C for 5 min) and (98 °C for 10 s; 35 × 98 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 5 s, 68 °C for 40 s; 68 °C for 5 min). Primers for each targeted locus are listed in Table S4. PCR amplicons were purified via NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean-up Kit (Macherey-Nagel), and PCR products were Sanger sequenced by Genewiz from Azenta Life Sciences using the forward primer. The sequence signal of each replicate was compared with a WT control. TIDE data analysis was performed as described by Brinkman et al. using the free web tool available at http://shinyapps.datacurators.nl/tide/. The percentage of in-frame, OoF, and 0-bp indels was determined by dividing the sum of each in-frame, OoF, or 0-bp shiftrange decomposition frequencies by the total shiftrange decomposition frequency using default settings.

## Statistical analysis
We obtained the percentage of in-frame, OoF, and WT length sequences via CRIS.py analysis and then generated stacked bar plots of the indel profiles at each time point. The fitness ratio was calculated as the ratio of OoF indels at day 21 over the day 3 timepoint. We obtained Chronos scores from 21Q3 from the DepMap portal unless noted otherwise. All graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism version 10. We generated schematic images using BioRender.com. Statistics, where noted, were performed using an unpaired Student’s t-test.
