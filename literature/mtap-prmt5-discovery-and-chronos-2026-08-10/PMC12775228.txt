## Abstract
Loss-of-function mutations in the TP53 gene are the most frequent genetic events in human malignancies. However, the extent to which missense TP53 mutations confer oncogenic gain-of-function (GOF) properties remains incompletely understood. Using genetically defined pancreatic cancer organoid models, we demonstrated that mutant p53 provides a selective growth advantage under acute environmental stress and drives molecular transition toward a basal-like state. Transcriptomic analyses showed that under stress conditions, organoids expressing mutant p53 adopted a basal-like gene expression program, a characteristic of a poor prognosis subtype of pancreatic cancer. Furthermore, the molecular subtype was reversed to the classical subtype once it returned to normal growth conditions, suggesting that transcriptional plasticity arises from acute changes in the tissue microenvironment. Given these context-dependent oncogenic properties, our findings suggest that targeting mutant p53 may be a promising therapeutic strategy for pancreatic cancer.

## Introduction
Mutations in the TP53 gene rank among the most common genetic alterations in human cancers. Because approximately 80% of these variants are missense mutations in the DNA-binding domain [ ], these mutations abolish the sequence-specific transcriptional activity of p53 and disrupt its ability to control cell cycle arrest, DNA repair, and apoptosis [ , ]. Mutant p53 also exhibits oncogenic gain-of-function (GOF) activity and supports tumor maintenance and progression [ , ]. Mutant p53 has been suggested to co-opt various regulatory proteins and signaling pathways, thereby promoting cancer cell survival, invasion, and therapeutic resistance.
Previous studies have identified multiple mechanisms through which mutant p53 directly contributes to carcinogenesis. In acute myeloid leukemia, mutant p53 co-opts the pluripotency factor FOXH1 to activate aberrant stem cell-like transcriptional programs, thereby enhancing the self-renewal of leukemic cell [ ]. Additionally, deletion of mutant p53 (p53R172H) in triple-negative breast tumors induces substantial tumor regression via activation of the cGAS–STING pathway, highlighting its essential role in breast cancer maintenance [ ]. Consistent with its oncogenic functions, mutant p53 has been implicated in the mediation of drug-resistant phenotypes. For instance, in ovarian cancer, mutant p53 promotes cell survival following paclitaxel treatment via the miR-130b–ZEB1 axis [ ]. Furthermore, mutant p53 interacts directly with the ETS2 transcription factor, thereby impairing the cellular responses to DNA damage [ ]. Collectively, these examples highlight the ability of mutant p53 to reprogram critical oncogenic pathways beyond its canonical tumor-suppressive role.
Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is driven by near-universal KRAS activation and frequent TP53 mutations (approximately 70% of cases) [ ]. Although the loss of classical tumor-suppressive function explains how PDAC is initiated, the extent to which TP53 missense mutations actively contribute to additional malignancy-promoting effects remains elusive. To date, Weissmueller et al. demonstrated that mouse models harboring hot-spot Trp53 missense mutations develop more invasive and metastatic pancreatic tumors than p53-null models, through the disruption of p73/NF-Y repressor complex and subsequent upregulation of autonomous PDGFRβ signaling pathways [ ]. Furthermore, most TP53 mutations in PDAC are missense mutations that produce a stable mutant protein rather than being silent or truncating [ – ]. These results strongly indicate that the retention of mutant p53 confers a selective growth advantage, suggesting the necessity for a comprehensive understanding of mutant p53 GOF mechanisms in PDAC progression.
Transcriptomic studies of human PDAC have identified at least two major tumor cell subtypes, classical (progenitor) and basal-like (squamous), which differ markedly in differentiation status and clinical outcome [ – ]. Classical PDAC tumors retain epithelial identity and highly express endodermal lineage transcription factors such as GATA6 and HNF4A [ ]. In contrast, basal-like PDAC activate gene programs associated with squamous lineage with upregulation of ΔNp63 (an isoform of TP63) and a YAP/TEAD transcriptional program [ , ]. Basal-like PDAC displays a more aggressive phenotype than the classical subtype and is associated with relatively poor patient survival [ , ]. Notably, the enrichment of TP53 mutations in basal-like PDAC samples suggested a functional link between mutant p53 and the adoption of the malignant cell state [ , ]. However, the mechanism through which mutant p53 influences subtype determination has not yet been explored.

## Materials and methods

## Monolayer cell culture conditions
Human pancreatic cancer cell lines KP-2 (Cell No. JCRB0181), T3M-4 (Cell No. RCB1021), and Panc 04.03 (Cat# CRL-2555) were purchased from the JCRB Cell Bank, RIKEN BRC, and ATCC, respectively. KP2 and T3M4 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium (RPMI, Cat# LM011-03, WelGene) containing antibiotics (Antibiotic–Antimycotic, Cat# 15240062, Gibco) and 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum FBS (Cat# S001-01, WelGene). Another human pancreatic cancer cell line, Panc 04.03, was cultured in RPMI medium supplemented with 0.1% insulin (Cat# I9278, Sigma-Aldrich), antibiotics, and 15% FBS. Organoid-derived monolayer cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Cat# LM001-05; WelGene) supplemented with 10% FBS and antibiotics. Plat-A cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 5% FBS and antibiotics. For the selection of infected cells, G418 solution (Geneticin, Cat# ant-gn-1, Invivogen) was mixed with fresh medium. All cells were regularly tested using a Mycoplasma PCR Detection Kit (REF# 25235, LiliF Diagnostics) and grown at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

## Organoid culture conditions
Organoid culture was conducted as previously described [ ]. Organoids were basically plated with Matrigel (Cat# 356231, Corning) and mouse pancreas complete medium [advanced DMEM/F-12, 10 mM HEPES, Glutamax 1 ×, 500 nM A83-01, mEGF 50 ng/ml, mNoggin 100 ng/ml, hFGF10 100 ng/ml, 10 nM Gastrin I, 1.25 mM N-acetylcysteine, 10 mM nicotinamide, B-27 supplement (1 × final), R-spondin 1 conditioned media (10% final)]. All organoids were grown at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For the growth factor-depleted condition assay, organoids were dissociated using TrypLE™ Express Enzyme (Cat# 12605028, Gibco), and 1 × 103 cells were embedded in Matrigel and cultured in reduced medium (DMEM with 10% FBS). For drug-response assays, organoids cultured in complete or reduced medium were dissociated and subsequently embedded in Matrigel containing the respective medium supplemented with chemotherapeutic agents (etoposide, 1 µM; gemcitabine, 40 nM). Then, the microscopic images were captured, and the growth of organoids was quantified using ImageJ. For the monolayer transition assay, the dissociated organoids were further seeded at densities of 3 × 103, 6 × 103, and 9 × 103 cells per well in reduced media (DMEM with 10% FBS). For the serial transition experiment, monolayer cells were thoroughly dissociated, and 5 × 103 cells were re-embedded in Matrigel with complete media.

## Virus production and application
For the knockdown experiment, 97mer short-hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting p53 were cloned into a retroviral vector (LENG, Addgene #111162). The retrovirus was prepared by cloned plasmid DNA and packaging DNAs (GPE, VSVG) using OPTI-MEM (Cat# 31985070, Gibco) and polyethylenimine (PEI, Cat# 19850, Polysciences) in Plat-A cells. Viruses were harvested for 3 days and filtered through 0.45-μm filters. Infection of monolayered cells was conducted with virus mixture with culture media (1:1) containing 20 μg/ml of polybrene (Cat# H2968, Sigma-Aldrich) for 8 h. Organoids were infected using previously described methods [ ]. For the infection of organoids, Retro-X™ concentrator (Cat# 631455, Takara) was mixed with virus-contained supernatants according to the instructor’s manual and incubated at 4 °C overnight. After centrifugation at 1,500 g for 45 min at 4 °C, the virus pellet was resuspended in organoid media. Then, dissociated organoids were mixed with the concentrated virus and polybrene (final concentration; 8 μg/mL), and spinoculated by centrifugation. The plate was centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 40 min at 24 °C and incubated at 37 °C CO2 incubator. After 5 h of incubation, the cells were collected, resuspended in Matrigel, and seeded onto 24-well plates with complete organoid media containing Y-27632 dihydrochloride (Cat# Y0503, Sigma-Aldrich). Two days after the infection, G418 (final concentration: 1 mg/mL) was added for antibiotic selection.

## In vitro assays for tumor aggressive phenotype
For a cell proliferation assay, 1.0 × 105 cells were resuspended in 2 ml growth medium and seeded in six-well plates. After 48 h of incubation, the number of cells was quantified every 2 days. For the anchorage-independent sphere formation assay, 1.0 × 104 cells stably expressing shRNA were resuspended in serum-free DMEM with B-27 supplement (Cat# 17504044, Gibco) and heparin (CAS# 9041–08-1, Sigma) and seeded onto a low-attachment plate (REF# 3473, Corning). For the wound healing migration assay, shRNA-expressing cells were seeded at 1.0 × 106 cells per well in six-well plates and incubated for 24 h. When the cells filled up the well for 8–90%, each well was scratched using a 200 μl pipette tip. To remove cell debris, the cells were washed with Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS; Cat# LB001-02; WelGene) and the medium was replaced with fresh medium. After the indicated time, microscopic images were captured, and wound sizes were calculated using ImageJ.

## Western blotting analysis
Cultured cells were washed with 1 ml PBS, and 0.5 million cells were counted. The cells were resuspended with 100 μl PBS containing 5 μl β-mercaptoethanol (Cat# M/P200/05, Fisher Chemical) and 100 μl 2 × Laemmli buffer (Cat# 1610737, Bio-Rad Laboratories) were added. Cells were thoroughly lysed by passing through a 1 ml syringe with a 26-gauge needle. The lysed samples were boiled at 95 °C for 10 min. Each sample was loaded and separated by 10% SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes at 4 °C for 2 h. The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline containing Tween 20 (TBST) at room temperature for 30 min. The blocked membrane was incubated with the appropriate primary antibodies at 4 °C for 18 h. The membrane was washed 3 times with TBST and incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies at room temperature for 30 min. The membrane was repeatedly washed with TBST and visualized using ECL solution (Cat# 1705062, Bio-Rad Laboratories). The primary antibodies used were against VINCULIN (sc-73614, Santa Cruz) and P53 (Cat# 2524, Cell Signaling Technology; or sc-6243, Santa Cruz). The secondary antibodies used were Goat Anti-Mouse/Rabbit IgG antibodies (HRP) (Cat# GTX213111-01 for mouse and GTX213111-01 for rabbit; both GeneTex).

## Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis
Total RNA was extracted using the QIAzol lysis reagent (cat# 79306, Qiagen), and DNase I (Cat# AMPD1, Sigma-Aldrich) was used to prevent contamination with genomic DNA. To synthesize complementary DNA, qScript Ultra SuperMix (cat#95217, Quantabio) was used for 2 μg of each RNA. The relative expression was analyzed by using SYBR™ Green PCR Master Mix (Cat# 4309155, Applied Biosystems) and normalized to the Tbp transcript levels. The primers used in this study have been presented in Supplementary Table .

## Cell-Titer Glo 3D assay
Cell-Titer Glo 3D assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, Matrigel of each well was crushed by 100 μl of cultural medium corresponding to each culture condition, and an equal volume of CellTiter-Glo 3D reagent (Cat# G9681, Promega) was added and thoroughly mixed with pipetting. After incubation at room temperature for 15 min, 100 μl of the mixture was transferred to an opaque-walled 96 well plate, and the luminescence was measured.

## Transcriptomic analyses

## RNA-sequencing library construction
RNA-seq libraries were constructed using a NEXTflex™ Rapid Directional mRNA-seq Kit (Cat# NOVA-5138–11, PerkinElmer). From Qiazol stocks of each sample, 3 μg of RNA was isolated into mRNA through poly-(A) selection step. Following mRNA fragmentation, cDNA synthesis, adenylation, and adapter ligation were performed. Finally, each library was constructed using PCR amplification. To align the RNA-seq data, raw reads were mapped to reference mouse genome assemblies (mm10) using the STAR tool [ ]. Cufflink tools were used to analyze differentially expressed genes [ ].

## GSEA
GSEA was performed using pre-ranked GSEA according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To employ pre-ranked gene lists, genes were ranked by log2 fold change between the experimental groups of interest. MSigDB hallmark gene sets were used for unbiased analysis of transcriptional outcomes. Gene signatures of PDA molecular subtypes were obtained from previous studies and refined in this study (see below) [ , , ].

## Integration of the PDA subtype-specific genes
To define the robust molecular subtype marker genes, previously reported subtype-related gene sets from two independent cohort studies (Chan-Seng-Yue et al. and Somerville et al.) were curated without weighting. After verifying the mRNA expression in both human and mouse PDA datasets, we identified the intersection between these two PDAC subtype-specific gene sets (classical: 143 genes; basal-like: 104 genes). The integrated subtype marker gene sets were further validated by comparing their expression in publicly available cohort datasets associated with each subtype (e.g., TCGA-PAAD and QCMG-PAAD) (Supplementary Fig. F). The final lists of subtype marker genes are provided in Supplementary Table .

## Principal component analysis (PCA)
Principal component analysis was performed to evaluate global transcriptomic variation and sample clustering. PCA was performed using ClustVis (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/clustvis/) [ ]. Briefly, principal components were computed using the pcaMethods package, primarily employing singular value decomposition (SVD) with iterative imputation to handle missing values.

## Single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA)
ssGSEA was conducted using the GenePattern web tool (https://cloud.genepattern.org/gp/pages/login.jsf) to quantify molecular subtype signatures in individual RNA-seq samples [ ]. The subtype scores for each sample were calculated based on the subtype marker gene sets defined in this study.

## Analyses of the public cohort database
Cohorts were stratified by the TP53 status of their tumor tissues, and their expression levels of subtype-related genes were compared between the groups of each TP53 status. Hierarchical clustering was conducted using the one-minus Pearson correlation metric with average linkage, based on the expression of basal-like subtype markers (n = 99) in tumor tissues, stratified by TP53 mutation status. The clinical databases (mutation profile and overall survival) for TCGA-PAAD and QCMG-PAAD were obtained from the original studies and cBioPortal (https://cbioprotal.org).

## Analyses of public cell-line database
CRISPR-based gene-dependency data were obtained from the 24Q4 DepMap Public Database. PDA cell lines were stratified by TP53 mutation status, and the average gene dependency scores (chronos) were calculated and ranked according to group. Gene dependency scores and the corresponding somatic mutation profiles are available on the DepMap portal (https://depmap.org).

## Statistics
Statistical significance for all experiments was determined using Prism 8 software. The specific statistical methods used to obtain p-values are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. The significance levels are denoted as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and n.s. (not significant).

## Results

## Mutant p53 loss does not affect pancreatic cancer cell growth under monolayer and spheroid culture conditions
Previous studies indicated that mutant p53 expression promotes pancreatic tumor growth and metastasis [ , ]. To test the oncogenic properties of mutant p53, we generated a panel of human pancreatic cancer cell lines, each carrying a different TP53 mutation. We then introduced either a control (Renilla luciferase) shRNA or a TP53-targeting shRNA to knock down mutant p53. Western blot analysis confirmed the depletion of the mutant p53 protein in all engineered cell lines (Fig.  A). In contrast to earlier reports, the loss of mutant p53 did not impair cell proliferation under standard two-dimensional (monolayer) culture conditions. The growth rates of control and p53-depleted cells were indistinguishable, indicating no significant difference in proliferative capacity (Fig.  B). Consistent with these results, gene effect scores from the Cancer Dependency Map showed that pancreatic cancer cell lines were not dependent on mutant p53 for proliferation under standard growth conditions (Fig.  C). Next, we examined whether mutant p53 is required for the more complex phenotypes associated with tumor aggressiveness. We found that anchorage-independent growth was unaffected by the loss of mutant p53, as sphere formation assays revealed no significant differences between control and p53-depleted cells (Fig.  D). Similarly, wound healing assays showed that the migration of mutant p53-depleted cells was comparable to that of control cells (Fig.  E). Collectively, these results suggest that the oncogenic GOF phenotypes of mutant p53 are not observed under conventional culture conditions and that a more specialized context may be required to detect mutant p53 GOF activity.
 

## Mutant p53 confers a growth advantage under nutrient- and matrix-deprived conditions
Previous studies have demonstrated that mutant p53 exerts oncogenic functions in response to diverse intracellular and extracellular stresses, including DNA damage, oxidative stress, and chronic immune signaling [ , ]. Based on this evidence, we hypothesized that the GOF activity of mutant p53 may become apparent under environmental stress conditions commonly encountered in physiological settings.
To determine whether mutant p53 provides a selective advantage under more physiologically relevant conditions, we employed a panel of murine pancreatic organoids. These organoids are established from either premalignant pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN) lesions or fully developed tumors [ ]. PanIN-derived organoids (mP) harbor an oncogenic KrasG12D mutation while retaining biallelic wild-type p53, thus serving as a control group. In contrast, organoids derived from malignant tumors (mM) carried the KrasG12D allele together with a monoallelic Trp53R172H mutation. Given that oncogene-driven phenotypes often emerge only under stress conditions [ – ], we hypothesized that altering the culture environment, specifically by removing exogenous growth factors and Matrigel, would unmask mutant p53 GOF phenotypes (Fig.  A and D).
 Under these stress conditions, shRNA-mediated depletion of mutant p53 in mM organoids significantly impaired their growth in a reduced medium (DMEM with 10% FBS), whereas it had little effect under standard complete organoid culture conditions (Fig.  B; Supplementary Fig. A-C). Consistent with this, mP and mM organoids grew at similar rates in complete medium; however, upon switching to reduced medium, mM organoids grew significantly faster than their mP counterparts (Fig.  C; Supplementary Fig. D-F). Moreover, the removal of the Matrigel and plating in a two-dimensional monolayer culture revealed an additional mutant p53-associated phenotype. In this assay, mM organoid cells with intact mutant p53 showed clonogenic growth, whereas mP and mM organoid cells depleted of mutant p53 rarely survived (Fig.  E and F; Supplementary Fig. G). Given the importance of 3D organoid cultures in revealing GOF phenotypes that remain undetectable, these findings demonstrate that mutant p53 provides a distinct growth advantage in nutrient- and matrix-deprived contexts.

## Mutant p53 supports basal-like transcriptional reprogramming under growth-limited conditions
Building on the above results, we investigated whether mutant p53 is involved in the molecular changes underlying its context-dependent fitness advantage. We performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to compare the global gene expression profiles of mutant p53-harboring PDA organoids grown in standard 3D culture with those grown under growth factor- and Matrigel-deprived conditions (Fig.  A). Unbiased analysis using the Molecular Signatures Database revealed that withdrawing these microenvironmental factors robustly altered multiple hallmark cancer pathways (Fig.  B and C; Supplementary Fig.  A). These transcriptional changes occurred without any alteration in mutant p53 expression, as Trp53 mRNA levels remained constant between the conditions (Supplementary Fig.  B). Of note, among the most upregulated programs in growth factor/Matrigel-deprived organoids were epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), KRAS signaling, and inflammatory responses, which are hallmarks of the aggressive basal-like PDA subtype [ , ]. Consistently, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed a significant upregulation of basal-like subtype gene signatures and a corresponding downregulation of classical subtype signatures under reduced growth conditions (Fig.  D and E; Supplementary Fig. C, D). To further substantiate this shift, we integrated molecular subtype signatures from Somerville et al. and Chan-Seng-Yue et al. and found that the overlapping signatures were strongly enriched in both our organoid RNA-seq data and public PDA transcriptome datasets (Supplementary Fig.  E-G). Using the refined gene sets for ssGSEA, we quantified the subtype scores for individual samples and confirmed that growth-limiting conditions induced a molecular subtype transition toward the basal-like state (Supplementary Fig. H).
 Next, we directly tested whether mutant p53 is required for this basal-like reprogramming. Trp53 knockdown in mutant p53 organoids (mM-shP53) markedly attenuated the induction of basal-like marker genes (Aldh1a3, Cav1, Col7a1, and Basp1) under reduced conditions, while control organoids with intact mutant p53 showed a robust upregulation of these basal markers in the same environment (Fig.  F; Supplementary Fig. I). Furthermore, under reduced growth conditions, the mP organoids failed to fully induce basal-like marker genes, whereas the mM organoids showed significantly increased mRNA expression of these markers (Fig.  G). Finally, we examined whether the transcriptomic shift toward a basal-like subtype conferred chemoresistance, a hallmark of this subtype [ ]. Notably, the basal-like mM organoids exhibited reduced sensitivity to etoposide and gemcitabine, whereas the classical mM organoids remained vulnerable to these chemotherapeutic agents (Fig.  H). These results demonstrate that under growth-limited conditions, mutant p53 actively drives PDA cells to reprogram toward an aggressive basal-like phenotype by conferring survival advantages and chemoresistance.

## Mutant p53 enables reversible subtype switching in PDA organoids
Consistent with the evidence that PDA transcriptional subtypes are not irreversibly fixed [ , ], we set out to determine whether the mutant p53-associated shift from the classical to the basal-like state was due to clonal selection or transcriptional plasticity. To distinguish between these possibilities, we designed a serial transition experiment, cycling organoids between 3D (Matrigel-rich, growth factor–complete) and 2D (Matrigel-free, reduced media) cultures, with RNA-seq profiling at each stage (Fig.  A). All organoid lines expressing mutant p53 remained viable and proliferative after repeated 3D-to-2D and back transitions (Fig.  B). Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to assess transcriptomic similarities across conditions and revealed distinct clustering of organoids subjected to serial transitions (Fig.  C). In parallel, several analyses showed that their molecular subtype profiles shifted reversibly in response to culture conditions (Fig.  D and ). Under 2D/growth factor-reduced conditions, basal-like markers were strongly upregulated, while classical markers were suppressed, whereas returning the same organoids to 3D/complete medium restored their original classical-like expression patterns. Upon a second challenge under 2D/growth factor-reduced conditions, the basal-like gene expression increased again, mirroring the initial transition (Fig.  F). This reproducible on/off pattern of basal-like and classical marker expression indicates that the PDA subtype identity is environmentally regulated rather than fixed. Cells expressing mutant p53 can dynamically switch transcriptomic states in response to external cues, rather than maintaining a stable basal-like state through permanent clonal selection. Moreover, mutant p53 is required for this plasticity, because p53-depleted organoids fail to induce basal-like gene expression under growth-limiting conditions.
 

## Analysis of clinical cohort reveals that mutant p53 correlates with basal-like subtype and poor outcomes in PDAC
To evaluate the clinical relevance of our observations, we analyzed publicly available transcriptome data from PDAC patients. By examining the relationship between TP53 mutation status and tumor molecular subtype, we observed that the gene expression profiles of the basal-like subtype were strongly associated with TP53-mutant expressing tumors, whereas classical subtype markers were predominant in TP53 wild-type cases (Fig.  A and B; Supplementary Fig.  A-D). Subsequently, we investigated whether the basal-like transcriptional state was associated with specific TP53 mutations. We performed ssGSEA using subtype marker gene sets and cohort data stratified according to TP53 mutation categories (hotspot vs. others; structural vs. DNA contact). This analysis revealed negligible differences in the enrichment scores among the mutation categories (Fig.  C; Supplementary Fig. E). In contrast, in these cohort analyses, we found that patients harboring missense TP53 mutations exhibited markedly higher basal-like signature scores than those harboring either wild-type TP53 or truncating TP53 mutations (Fig.  D; Supplementary Fig. F, G). Importantly, TP53 nonsense (null) mutation did not significantly upregulate basal-like markers, underscoring that loss of p53 alone is insufficient to drive the basal-like phenotype. These results indicate that mutant p53 endows PDAC cells with transcriptional plasticity toward a basal-like state. Finally, we assessed whether the clinical outcomes were associated with TP53 status. Patients with PDA tumors harboring mutant p53 exhibited worse overall survival compared to those with functionally intact p53 (Fig.  E; Supplementary Fig. H, I), consistent with the poor clinical outcomes of basal-like PDAC [ ]. These findings demonstrate that mutant p53 contributes to PDA aggressiveness by promoting basal-like subtype switching.
 

## Discussion
Our findings demonstrate that mutant p53 regulates molecular subtype plasticity in PDAC under environmental stress, including growth factor withdrawal or loss of matrix attachment (Fig.  F). Under these conditions, the presence of mutant p53 enables PDA cells to transit toward a more aggressive, basal-like transcriptional state. These stress-induced subtype transitions are reversible, and stress-specific flexibility, supported by mutant p53, provides a survival advantage. Consequently, the capacity of mutant p53 to drive these reversible basal-like alterations may explain the aggressive behavior and therapeutic resistance often reported in TP53-mutant pancreatic cancers [ ].
The experimental stress conditions used in this study – reduced growth factors and Matrigel deprivation – mimicked the key aspects of tumor progression and metastasis. For example, PDAC tumors exist in a hypoxic, nutrient-poor microenvironment [ , ], so nutrient fluctuations occur in vivo as tumors outgrow their blood supply. Cancer cells that survive chronic nutrient starvation acquire adaptive metabolic programs. For example, PDAC cells under dual glucose/glutamine deprivation acquire reversible alterations (e.g., glutamine synthetase upregulation and open chromatin changes) that allow cancer cells to survive and resume cancer growth when nutrients are restored [ , ]. Similarly, detachment from the extracellular matrix during dissemination imposes anchorage stress. While metastatic precursors must become anchorage‐independent to resist anoikis, recent work showed that inducing an “adhesion-to-suspension transition” (AST) in epithelial cells via the ectopic expression of four hematopoietic factors was sufficient to promote anchorage-independent growth without triggering EMT [ ]. Therefore, the in vitro stresses applied here, nutrient deprivation and loss of adhesion, may reflect selective pressures in PDAC progression and can unmask the oncogenic phenotypes that mutant p53 helps exploit.
It remains unclear how mutant p53 orchestrates transcriptional plasticity under stress conditions. One possibility is that mutant p53 interacts with lineage-determining transcription factors. For instance, mutant p53 can co-opt ETS2 to drive pro-migratory and mesenchymal gene programs [ , ], and modulate TGFβ signaling by sequestering p63 and altering Smad complexes [ ]. Another member of the ETS family, ETS1, may cooperate with mutant p53 to regulate basal-like gene expression [ ]. Consistent with this, mutant p53 selectively interacts with ETS1 to modulate gene expression, whereas wild-type p53 does not [ ]. Similarly, our results demonstrated that wild-type p53 failed to induce transcriptomic plasticity, supporting ETS1 recruitment as a mutant p53-specific gain-of-function. Another potential mechanism involves mutant p53-associated chromatin reconfiguration in basal-like subtypes. For example, mutant p53 recruits MLL/COMPASS histone methyltransferases, such as MLL4 (KMT2B), promoting H3K4me1/H3K27ac deposition at latent enhancers under stress [ ]. Comparative cohort analyses across multiple cancer types further indicated that p53-mutant tumors exhibit distinct chromatin configurations compared to their wild-type counterparts [ ]. Therefore, by remodeling the chromatin landscape and forming aberrant transcriptional complexes, mutant p53 may function as a master regulator, enabling basal-like transitions in response to environmental stress.
Our results are consistent with the broader perspective that molecular subtypes are dynamic states shaped by the tumor microenvironment rather than fixed identities. Recent studies have indicated that pancreatic tumors can reversibly switch between classical and basal-like states in response to external cues [ ]. For example, when classical subtype cells are detached from a monolayer culture, grown as spheroids, and returned to monolayer conditions, a TEAD2-driven transcriptional program is activated as a characteristic of basal-like tumors [ ]. TEAD2-dependent reprogramming establishes an “endothelial-like” enhancer landscape marked by the upregulation of pro-angiogenic factors, demonstrating how changes in adhesion and suspension conditions can trigger basal-like transcriptional networks. Consistent with previous studies demonstrating a physical interaction between mutant p53 and YAP [ ], a central co-factor of the TEAD complex, this axis may contribute to intrinsic plasticity by enabling dynamic transitions between classical and basal-like states, thereby promoting tumor progression, metastatic fitness, and therapy resistance [ , ]. Given these findings, mutant p53 is a promising therapeutic target, prompting the development of mutant p53–specific therapies, including RNA-based approaches [ ], proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) [ ], and nanoparticle-based delivery methods [ , ]. Collectively, our results underscore that subtype plasticity significantly contributes to PDAC aggressiveness, enabling malignant cells to adapt dynamically to environmental challenges during disease progression.
