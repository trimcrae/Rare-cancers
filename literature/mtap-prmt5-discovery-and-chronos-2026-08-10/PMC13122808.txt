## Summary
Multiple myeloma (MM) is a genetically complex cancer that has a higher incidence and mortality in males when compared to females. These sex differences are well documented epidemiologically, yet their biological mechanisms remain uncharacterized. We hypothesized that sex-specific genetic regulation by somatic mutations affects MM cell gene expression differently in each sex and subsequently leads to differential functional genetic dependencies between the two sexes. To test this, we first identified genes with sex-biased expression in MM, then identified sex-specific somatic regulators of these genes, evaluated their association with overall survival outcomes, and finally assessed their functional importance using CRISPR-Cas9 dependency screens in MM cell lines. Our findings provide systematic evidence that sex-differentiated somatic regulation underlies tumor-intrinsic mechanisms in MM, establishing the importance of incorporating sex-aware molecular analyses for improved biomarker discovery and precision medicine. Collectively, these results establish sex as a fundamental biological variable in MM with significant implications for disease outcomes.

## Introduction
Multiple myeloma (MM) is a hematologic malignancy characterized by the clonal proliferation of plasma cells within the bone marrow. Since 1990, cases of MM have increased worldwide by more than 120% in incidence and nearly doubled in mortality. In the United States, incidence has risen by over 40%, although mortality has declined with the introduction of newer therapies and improved clinical management. , , , Epidemiology studies have identified age, ancestry, and family history as major risk factors for MM. However, sex remains a key yet insufficient determinant in MM, as males are diagnosed more frequently and experience worse survival compared to females. , , 
Sex disparities in MM are well documented in epidemiological studies, but their underlying biological basis remains uncharacterized. Prior studies point to sex differences in immune function and hormonal signaling, but increasing evidence indicates that genetic and molecular mechanisms contribute to MM pathogenesis. , Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have identified numerous susceptibility loci, implicating genes that contribute to MM risk. In addition, germline variation has been associated with survival outcomes, suggesting that a patient’s genetic background may contribute to MM development and prognosis. , The vast majority of GWASs in MM have been conducted in a sex-combined fashion. , These studies support the role of inherited genetic factors in MM incidence and mortality, but they overlook the possibility that these factors may differentially regulate gene functions in each sex. This, along with the lack of evaluation of somatic gene regulation, led us to reason that sex-dependent regulatory mechanisms might also operate at the somatic level, which has not been systematically investigated in MM. 
To address this gap, we hypothesized that somatic mutations affect MM gene expression in a sex-dependent fashion. To test this, we first identified genes with sex-biased expression in MM, then identified sex-specific somatic regulators of these genes, evaluated their association with survival outcomes, and finally assessed their functional importance using CRISPR-Cas9 dependency screens in MM cell lines. Our findings provide systematic evidence that sex-differentiated somatic regulation underlies tumor-intrinsic mechanisms in MM, highlighting the need for sex-aware molecular analysis in biomarker discovery and precision medicine. Definitions of sex-differentiated, sex-biased, and sex-specific terms are provided in . 

## Material and methods

## Data collection
We obtained multi-omic and clinical data for patients newly diagnosed with MM from two independent cohorts. The discovery cohort was sourced from the Multiple Myeloma Research Foundation (MMRF) CoMMpass Study (IA22 release), which included 584 patients (354 males and 230 females). Data types collected from CoMMpass included bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) gene expression profiles from bone marrow aspirates, whole-genome sequencing (WGS), somatic mutation annotation format (MAF) files, and clinical outcome data, such as overall survival. The validation cohort was obtained from the Microarray Quality Control (MAQC-II) project (Gene Expression Omnibus [GEO]: GSE24080), comprising 559 patients newly diagnosed with MM (222 males and 337 females) with gene expression (microarray) and clinical outcome data, specifically overall survival. For each cohort, sample inclusion required the availability of both sex annotation and the corresponding molecular data type.
Somatic expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) summary statistics were obtained from Natri et al., based on paired tumor-normal sequencing data from the MMRF CoMMpass study (IA15 release). This analysis included peripheral blood and tumor WGS data from 519 donors and matched RNA-seq-based tumor gene expression profiles. The processed somatic cis-eQTL results are publicly available via (Figshare: https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.26307886.v1).
CRISPR-Cas9 genome-wide knockout screen data from the Cancer Dependency Map (DepMap: https://depmap.org) for evaluating MM cell line growth dependencies. Gene dependency scores were derived using the Chronos correction model, which accounts for copy-number bias, screen quality, chromosome arm bias, and batch effects. This dataset included 19 MM cell lines (9 male derived and 10 female derived).

## Gene expression: Identification of sex-differentiated gene expression in MM
For the discovery dataset, we quantified sex differences in gene expression in primary bone marrow samples from patients newly diagnosed with MM in the MMRF CoMMpass study (IA22 release). Phenotype data were filtered to include only samples labeled as primary blood-derived cancer—bone marrow with available sex annotation, race, age at index, days to sample procurement time, and International Staging System (ISS) stage. This resulted in 354 male and 230 female patients. Genes located on the Y chromosome or mitochondrial genes were removed. Gene counts were normalized using the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method and filtered for low expression, defined as requiring ≥10 counts in ≥230 samples. Gene counts were transformed using the voom method from the “limma” package, and a linear model was fit to each gene to test for sex-differentiated expression while controlling for ISS stage, age at index, race, and days to sample procurement. To address the imbalance in male and female sample sizes, the analysis was repeated across 100 sex-balanced subsampled iterations in which an equal number of male and female samples were selected. This resampling strategy mitigates bias introduced by unequal sample sizes and enables us to assess whether identified genes remained consistently significant across multiple balanced data subsets, providing an additional measure of robustness. In each iteration, differential expressions were tested using a linear model with empirical Bayes moderation. For each gene in CPM (counts per million), median log2 fold change (log2FC), median false discovery rate (FDR), and the frequency of significant FDRs < 0.05 across iterations were calculated, defining sex-differentiated genes as those with median FDRs < 0.05; genes with |log2FC| > 1 and median FDRs < 0.05 were considered sex biased.
For the validation dataset, raw microarray expression data for the validation cohort were obtained from the MAQC-II project (GEO: GSE24080), generated from CD138+ plasma cells isolated from bone marrow aspirates of patients newly diagnosed with MM (222 males and 337 females) using the Affymetrix U133 Plus 2.0 platform. Expression values for multiple probes mapping to the same gene were collapsed to a single representative value using the MaxMean method from the “WGCNA” package. This resulted in an aggregated gene-level expression matrix, with one expression value per Ensembl gene ID. Genes located on the Y chromosome and mitochondrial genes were removed prior to analysis. After removing genes located on the Y chromosome and mitochondrial genes, we excluded those expressing <5 counts in >80% of samples. Expression data were normalized using the TMM method. Differential expression analysis was performed using the limma package, fitting a linear model to each gene with sex as the primary variable of interest while adjusting for age at diagnosis. Genes (CPM) with a |log2FC| > 0.25 and an FDR < 0.05 were considered sex biased. Due to differences in the gene expression quantification platform employed and processing workflow between the discovery and validation cohorts, including cell enrichment, a lower log2FC threshold (|log2FC| > 0.25) was applied in the validation analysis to account for reduced dynamic range and cross-platform variability. Validation focused on testing the 168 sex-biased genes identified in the discovery dataset, of which 126 were represented in the MAQC-II dataset.

## Sex difference in somatic mutations
Somatic mutation data for patients newly diagnosed with MM were obtained from the MMRF CoMMpass study (IA22 release). The dataset included MAF files containing exome-wide mutations. We restricted analysis to mutations annotated as nonsynonymous (missense, nonsense, and splice site) and excluded mutations flagged for low-quality or missing gene annotations. To ensure balanced comparisons, we generated 100 sex-matched subsets of patients, sampling 220 males and 220 females per iteration from individuals with available clinical and genomic data. For each iteration, we extracted somatic mutation data corresponding to the sampled patients and retained matched phenotype metadata, including age, race, ISS stage, and days to sample procurement. Next, we focused on 33 genes previously identified as significantly sex biased in expression (FDR < 0.05). Of these, 19 genes harbored at least one somatic mutation in either sex group. For each gene, mutation frequency was calculated separately for males and females as the number of patients with a mutation in the gene divided by the total number of patients of that sex:mutationfrequency=numberofmutatedsamplesforagivengeneandsex/totalsamplesofthatsex×100.

## Sex differences in mutation burden
To quantify sex differences in mutation burden among sex-biased genes, we evaluated 19 genes that had at least one somatic mutation in either sex group. To ensure balanced comparisons between sexes, we generated 100 sex-matched subsets, each consisting of 220 male and 220 female patients with complete clinical and genomic data. For each of the 100 iterations, we summarized the number of somatic mutations per gene per sample and calculated the median mutation count per sex. A two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to assess whether median mutation burdens differed significantly between males and females across iterations. We defined genes with sex-differentiated mutation burden as those with a statistically significant difference between sexes (p < 0.05) and at least one somatic mutation observed in either group.

## Somatic cis-eQTL density
To evaluate somatic regulatory mutations across sex-biased genes, we used nominal somatic cis-eQTL summary statistics from Natri et al., derived from matched tumor RNA-seq and WGS data in the MMRF CoMMpass study (IA15 release). The original cis-eQTL mapping was performed using linear regression models within a 50 kb window of the transcription start site (TSS). Somatic mutations were aggregated into recurrently mutated loci by binning mutations within 50 kb. Bins observed in at least five tumors were retained, and the midpoint of each bin was used as the proxy coordinate for eQTL mapping in QTLtools. The associations were tested within ±50 kb of each gene’s TSS, and significant cis-eQTLs were defined at FDR ≤ 0.2. For our analysis, we filtered the nominal summary statistics to retain only associations involving 33 candidate genes previously identified as significantly sex-biased in expression (FDR < 0.05). Because genotype correlation matrices were not available, we used nominal p values to estimate the relative density of regulatory associations across genes. To approximate independent regulatory signals, adjacent somatic loci each representing recurrently mutated regions defined by Natri et al. were clustered within a 1 kb window for each gene to avoid overcounting correlated signals. Also, to account for gene size bias, we normalized the number of somatic cis-eQTLs by gene span, reporting the density of significant eQTLs per megabase (Mb) of gene length. Genes annotated with a hash symbol (“#”) denote loci under dual somatic pressure, harboring structural mutations detected in one sex and nearby non-coding regulatory mutations.

## Somatic eQTL direction
Based on findings from the mutation burden analysis, we identified 12 sex-specific genes that harbor structural mutations detected in one sex and nearby non-coding regulatory mutations. Using the somatic cis-eQTL summary statistics from the MMRF CoMMpass study, we extracted all somatic mutation-gene associations for these genes. For each gene, we calculated the median cis-eQTL slope by aggregating slope values across all associated somatic mutations. The direction of the somatic cis-eQTL slope indicates whether the presence of a somatic mutation is associated with increased or decreased gene expression.

## Somatic eQTL Z score
To evaluate the direction and magnitude of somatic regulatory signals in sex-specific genes, we computed Z scores from the somatic cis-eQTL summary statistics for the same 12 sex-specific genes. β is the regression slope from the eQTL model, and p is the nominal two-sided p value. For each gene, Z scores were aggregated as the median across all associated somatic mutation-gene pairs:Z=sign(β)×Φ−1(1−p/2).

## Somatic mutation-gene association slope distribution
For EIF1AX and TXLNG, nominal somatic mutation-gene association summary statistics from the MMRF CoMMpass dataset were binned into four significance ranges based on nominal p values: 0.05 < p ≤ 0.1, 0.01 < p ≤ 0.05, 0.001 < p ≤ 0.01, and p ≤ 0.001. Within each bin, the number of mutation-gene associations was recorded, and the distribution of regression slopes (β) was visualized to illustrate the relationship between effect size and statistical significance. Median slopes were indicated by white points, representing the central tendency of the slope distribution within each p value range. This analysis was performed for visualization purposes only to summarize the overall trend of somatic regulatory signals.

## Sex-stratified multivariate survival analysis
Cox proportional hazards regression stratified by sex was used to evaluate associations between TXLNG and EIF1AX expression and overall survival, with continuous log-transformed expression values as predictors. Our analyses were conducted in the MMRF CoMMpass IA22 dataset, with separate models fit per quartile. Survival time was defined as the interval from diagnosis to death or last follow-up, with death recorded as the event.
Models were adjusted for race; Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, categorized as low (scores 0–1) vs. high (scores 2–4); age at diagnosis (years); height (inches); weight (lbs.); ISS tumor stage, categorized as low (stage I–II) vs. high (stage III); and frontline treatment category (bortezomib based vs. IMID treatment).
For each sex, the Cox model was specified ash(t)=h0(t)×exp(β1×geneexpression+β2×covariates),where h(t) is the hazard at time t, h0(t) is the baseline hazard, β1 is the coefficient for the gene expression term, and β2 represents the coefficients for adjustment covariates. Cox p values were obtained from the Wald test of the gene expression coefficient in each model. In addition, to further assess expression-level differences, we performed a secondary sex-stratified analysis comparing within-sex expression quartile 1 vs. quartile 4 (Q1 vs. Q4) using the same covariates as above, and Wald tests were also performed to compare Q1 vs. Q4 within each sex.

## CRISPR-Cas9 knockout dependency in myeloma cell lines
We evaluated gene dependency scores from CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screens in 19 MM cell lines (male = 9 and female = 10) using data from the DepMap project (release 24Q4). , Gene dependency scores were derived from the Chronos correction model, which accounts for copy-number bias, screen quality, chromosome arm bias, and batch effects across integrated CRISPR-Cas9/Cas12 screening datasets (Broad Institute Project Achilles, Sanger Institute Project SCORE, and Broad Institute Humagne-CD). Gene-level scores were combined with myeloma cell line annotations using unique DepMap identifiers (DepMap ID; e.g., ACH-000019) as provided in the DepMap metadata files. , 
We restricted analyses to MM cell lines with complete gene-effect values for EIF1AX, then stratified by sex to evaluate differences in dependency and performed two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (female vs. male). Bar plots were ordered by dependency score and colored by sex, and boxplots included individual data points to visualize score distributions. We included a reference line at −0.5 to denote the standard threshold for strong gene essentiality, as defined in DepMap documentation and prior large-scale CRISPR screening studies. , In this scoring system, values near 0 indicate non-essential genes, values < −0.5 indicate high essentiality, and values > 1 suggest a possible growth advantage. , 

## Results

## Discovery and validation of sex-biased genes in MM patient samples
Sex differences in gene expression have been commonly reported, but their role in MM pathogenesis remains poorly defined. To investigate sex differences in primary MM plasma cells, we performed sex-differential gene expression analyses in the discovery dataset (CoMMpass) and an independent validation dataset (MAQC-II) ( ). In the discovery dataset, we identified 168 statistically significant sex-biased genes (Benjamini-Hochberg FDR < 0.05). Fifteen of them had higher effect sizes (|log2FC| > 1) ( A). In the independent MAQC-II dataset, 33 of the 168 discovery genes recapitulated the same direction of sex bias, of which 32 also retained statistical significance (17 male-biased [blue] and 15 female-biased [red], A and 2B). The 33 replicated sex-biased genes spanned both autosomal and X chromosome loci, indicating that sex-biased expression in MM plasma cells is not confined to sex chromosomes. Representative examples include PIM3 (chr22), which displayed significantly higher expression in females (TMM: 5.60 vs. 5.16; ∗∗∗p < 0.001) and encodes a serine/threonine kinase previously shown to act as an oncogenic driver in MM, and DDX43 (chr6), which showed higher expression in males (TMM: 0.43 vs. 0.33; ∗∗∗p < 0.001), promotes tumor cell survival, and suppresses apoptosis in several hematologic malignancies. 
We next prioritized examining KDM6A and KDM5C, two X-linked chromatin regulators that escape X chromosome inactivation, due to their consistent female-biased expression across datasets and their established relevance as candidate driver genes in MM. KDM6A is a known tumor suppressor, and KDM5C is frequently mutated in MM. , , 
KDM6A shows female-biased expression in the discovery set (median log2FC = 0.41, FDR = 6.5 × 10−13) with validation in MAQC-II (log2FC = 0.38, 95% confidence interval [CI]: [0.28–0.48], FDR = 3.3 × 10−10). KDM5C also exhibited female-biased expression in discovery (median log2FC = 0.25, FDR = 2.2 × 10−8) and maintained the same sex bias in MAQC-II (log2FC = 0.05, 95% CI: [–0.01–0.11]), though it did not reach statistical significance (FDR = 0.72). These results are further illustrated in our boxplots: KDM6A is significantly upregulated in females compared to males (TMM: 5.48 vs. 5.10; ∗∗∗p < 0.001), and KDM5C likewise shows higher expression in females (TMM: 6.88 vs. 6.67; ∗∗∗p < 0.001) ( C).

## Differential somatic mutation frequency and burden in validated MM genes
We hypothesize that somatic mutations may regulate MM gene expression and, therefore, sex differences in somatic mutations may be the drivers of the observed differential MM gene expression. We set out to analyze somatic mutation frequency and burden in the MMRF CoMMpass dataset. Out of the 33 reproducible sex-biased genes across our discovery and validation datasets, 19 harbored at least one somatic mutation in either sex group ( A and 3B). We next evaluated mutation frequency and observed two discrete patterns. Differential mutation frequencies were detected in both sexes at different rates, such as ANK3 (5/220 females [2.3%] vs. 9/220 males [4.1%]) and USP9X (1/220 females [0.5%] vs. 8/220 males [3.6%]), and sex-specific mutations restricted to one sex, such as ASMTL in females (3/220 [1.4%] vs. 0/220 [0%]) and HUWE1 in males (2/220 [0.9%] vs. 0/220 [0%]) ( B). 
Independent of mutation frequency, for these 19 sex-biased genes, we quantified sex-associated differences in somatic mutation burden, defined as the median number of mutations per sample across 100 iterations, and compared males and females using a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test ( C). Sex-specific mutation burden was observed, with nine genes (EIF1AX, ASMTL, CD99, EIF2S3, PIM3, KDF1, PLCXD1, UBA1, and ZBED1) presenting higher burden in females (p < 0.001) and three genes (TXLNG, P2RY8, and SYAP1) presenting higher burden in males (p < 0.001). In contrast, AKAP17A, ANK3, DHRSX, HNRNPUL2, KDM6A, and USP9X did not display significant sex-associated differences (not significant [n.s.]) ( C). While multiple genes showed sex-specific differences in mutation burden, DDX3X, an X-linked RNA helicase implicated in MM cell survival and proteasome inhibitor resistance, emerged as the only gene showing statistically significant sex-biased mutation burden (p < 0.001).

## Sex-specific somatic cis-eQTL regulation density of MM genes
To assess whether sex-biased genes are subject to differential somatic regulatory activity, we quantified the density of somatic cis-eQTLs. Somatic eQTL densities were calculated using nominal summary statistics, providing a relative measure of regulatory burden across sex-bias genes (sb-genes). Each somatic eQTL represents an association between gene expression and an aggregated somatic locus, and counts were normalized by gene length to correct for gene size bias. Using this approach, we identified genes with enriched somatic regulatory activity, including KDF1 (chr1) and UBA1 (chrX) (>8 signals per Mb), which represent both autosomal and X-linked loci, indicating that somatic regulatory enrichment is not confined to the X chromosome. In contrast, PIM3 (chr22) and P2RY8 (chrX) exhibited the lowest somatic cis-eQTL densities (<2 signals per Mb), suggesting limited regulatory influence. sb-genes marked with “#” denote loci under dual somatic pressure, harboring structural mutations detected in one sex and nearby non-coding regulatory mutations ( A). To explore the functional impact of these sex-specific loci, we next compared eQTL effect sizes (slopes) between males and females. We estimated median eQTL slopes in the sex-combined dataset for the 12 sb-genes in which somatic mutations were detected exclusively in one sex ( B). Only three genes carried somatic mutations exclusively in males, while the remaining nine demonstrated female-driven patterns of somatic regulation. In particular, EIF1AX was associated with female-driven somatic regulation characterized by a negative median slope. In contrast, TXLNG was associated with male-driven somatic regulation characterized by a positive median slope, consistent with their expression bias. 
We next quantified the magnitude of these sex-specific patterns by comparing standardized Z scores across the 12 sex-specific genes ( C). We defined sex-biased genes with somatic transcriptional regulation as those with an absolute Z score greater than 1 (|Z| > 1), where negative values indicate reduced expression associated with somatic mutations and positive values indicate increased expression. Using this criterion, negative Z scores were observed for EIF1AX (female, Z = −2.3), TXLNG (male, Z = −1.8), and PIM3 (female, Z = −2.1), while positive Z scores were detected for PLCXD1 (male, Z = 1.6) and P2RY8 (male, Z = 1.9). Together, these results demonstrate that somatic mutations contribute to both downregulation and upregulation of gene expression in MM in a sex-specific manner. EIF1AX is shown for illustration, displaying reduced expression with stronger somatic cis-eQTLs ( D).

## TXLNG expression is a male-driven prognostic marker in MM
To further determine the clinical significance of sex-biased genes that were regulated by somatic mutations, we investigated TXLNG as a representative example. TXLNG was selected from the subset of validated sex-biased genes with survival associations ( ) because higher expression was associated with poorer survival in both sexes, with statistical significance observed primarily in males. Multivariate Cox regression survival analysis ( A) confirmed that high TXLNG expression was significantly associated with worse overall survival (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.02, p = 0.003), independent of other clinical covariates. To contextualize this effect, several demographic and clinical variables were also examined. Younger (age) (HR = 1.04, p < 0.001) and high ECOG performance status (HR = 1.64, p = 0.008) were associated with increased hazard, whereas Black (race) was nominally associated with an increased hazard (HR = 1.50, p = 0.05). In contrast, female (sex) (HR = 0.49, p = 0.001) and weight (lbs.) (HR = 0.96, p = 0.002) were associated with improved survival. The additional variable (height) was not significant. Kaplan-Meier survival curves demonstrated that higher TXLNG expression was significantly associated with worse overall survival, particularly in the upper expression quartiles (Q3: Cox p = 0.007; Q4: Cox p = 0.012) ( B). These results are further illustrated in our boxplots, which highlight the baseline sex difference in TXLNG expression. TXLNG is significantly upregulated in females compared to males (TMM: 5.01 vs. 4.63; ∗∗∗p < 0.001) ( C). Survival curves stratified by expression quartiles per sex ( D) reinforced this sex-specific effect: male patients in the highest expression quartile (Q4, Cox p = 0.02) experienced significantly worse outcomes compared to those in the lowest quartile (Q1, Cox p = 0.03), whereas no survival difference between quartiles was observed in females (Cox p = 0.266) ( D). TXLNG predicts worse survival overall, but this effect occurs significantly only in males, aligning with our finding that TXLNG may be influenced by sex-specific somatic regulatory control. 

## EIF1AX is a female-driven dependency and prognostic marker in MM
EIF1AX was investigated as a representative example to assess the functional and clinical relevance of sex-specific genetic regulation. EIF1AX was selected from the subset of validated genes with survival associations ( ) because higher expression was associated with poorer survival, with statistical significance observed primarily in females, and this finding was further supported by cancer dependency data in myeloma cell lines. Gene dependency scores derived from DepMap CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screens (Chronos model) , demonstrated that female MM cell lines were more sensitive to EIF1AX loss than male cell lines, with significantly lower gene effect scores (Wilcoxon p < 0.001). Across the 20 MM cell lines profiled, several female-derived lines, including KMS-11, EJM, OPM-2, LP-1, MM1-S, and JJN-3, showed pronounced sensitivity (scores < −2), well below the biological relevance threshold of −0.5, indicating strong reliance on EIF1AX for survival and proliferation ( A and 6B). In contrast, male-derived lines such as OCI-My7, KMS-26, RPMI-8226, and INA6 showed weaker dependency, with scores closer to zero, consistent with reduced EIF1AX essentiality. 
In the clinical cohort (CoMMpass), EIF1AX expression was significantly associated with worse overall survival in females (Cox p = 0.00776) but not in males (Cox p = 0.0829) ( C). In the sex-combined analysis, EIF1AX expression was also associated with worse survival (Cox p = 8.65 × 10−4), driven primarily by female patients. Quartile-based analyses further confirmed this sex-specific prognostic association: among patients in the highest expression quartile (Q4), females experienced worse survival compared to males (Cox p = 0.0546) ( D). Together, these findings demonstrate that EIF1AX is a female-driven dependency in myeloma cell lines and a female-specific prognostic marker in patients, reinforcing the functional importance of this gene in disease aggressiveness.

## Discussion
In this study, we present a comprehensive sex-stratified analysis of MM, integrating assessment of somatic gene expression and mutations, cis-regulatory associations, and CRISPR-Cas9 dependency screens, alongside survival outcomes. Unlike prior MM studies that treated sex as a covariate to adjust for, our approach considered sex as a biological variable. , This enabled the discovery of sex-specific mechanisms that would have remained hidden in sex-combined analyses. , We identified 168 sex-biased genes in primary MM plasma cells, 126 of which were also detected in the validation cohort, with 33 reaching statistical significance and maintaining the same direction of sex bias across both datasets. For these 33 validated sex-biased genes, we observed sex-specific differences in mutational frequency and burden and somatic regulation in key drivers such as EIF1AX and TXLNG. In CRISPR-Cas9 dependency screens, EIF1AX demonstrated sex-dependent vulnerabilities in MM cell lines, supported by pan-cancer analyses identifying sex-linked dependency patterns, including EIF1AX. Finally, our survival analyses demonstrated that the expression of these genes was significantly associated with the overall survival of patients with MM, differing between sexes.
Somatic mutation burden in EIF1AX and PIM3 was observed exclusively in females, while TXLNG and SYAP1 occurred only in males. Our results coincide with a phenomenon seen across cancers, in which sex-specific mutational processes shape differences in mutation burden and spectrum. Beyond these sex-specific events, we identified a subset of genes under dual somatic pressure, characterized by the coexistence of mutations within the gene body in only one sex and nearby non-coding regulatory regions. , This pattern suggests that both coding and regulatory elements of the same locus are simultaneously subjected to selective forces, potentially amplifying their transcriptional and functional impact. Dual somatic pressure may represent a compounded mechanism of dysregulation where gene body mutations alter protein function or stability, while adjacent non-coding mutations further fine-tune expression levels. , These loci could act as molecular “hotspots” of somatic regulation, integrating multiple layers of mutational influence that differ by sex and ultimately contribute to the distinct molecular phenotypes and survival patterns observed in male and female patients with MM.
We further established the clinical and functional impact of these patterns by focusing on TXLNG and EIF1AX. Quartile-based survival confirmed that high-expressing TXLNG and EIF1AX experience shorter survival compared to their low-expressing counterparts. TXLNG expression was higher in females than males; on the other hand, its prognostic association was observed primarily in males. Taken together, we speculate that there is a sex-gene interaction when evaluating the prognostic role of TXLNG. Although TXLNG is expressed at a higher level in females, the detrimental prognostic effect is much more pronounced in males. In addition, somatic regulatory effects involving TXLNG were detected only in males. These findings represent distinct observations and suggest a sex-gene interaction. In contrast, EIF1AX emerged as a female-bias gene whose high expression was associated with significantly worse survival in females but not in males. In addition, EIF1AX with CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screens showed stronger dependency in female MM cell lines. This effect was consistent across genetically and molecularly heterogeneous myeloma models, indicating that the observed vulnerability is not restricted to a particular subtype but reflects a broader, tumor-intrinsic feature. Together, these findings suggest that EIF1AX, a gene that plays a central role in translational control pathways, differentially influences MM across sexes. EIF1AX encodes a eukaryotic translation initiation factor that promotes cap-dependent ribosomal assembly and mTOR-driven protein synthesis processes that are particularly critical in plasma cells, which continuously produce high levels of immunoglobulins. , , We hypothesize that high EIF1AX expression in females may enhance global translation and immunoglobulin synthesis. This increased translational capacity may promote tumor growth and aggressiveness, providing a rationale for the worse survival observed in female patients with elevated EIF1AX expression. In line with our results, female-derived MM cell lines displayed stronger EIF1AX dependency in knockout screens, showing their heightened reliance on EIF1AX-mediated protein synthesis for survival. Because high EIF1AX expression in females increases protein synthesis and proteostatic demand, proteasome inhibitors, such as bortezomib, may be more effective in females than in males by further overwhelming the protein-degradation system. Overall, our results provide functional and clinical evidence that sex-specific somatic regulation may directly translate into sex-specific prognostic markers and therapeutic vulnerabilities.
There are limitations associated with our work. The discovery dataset showed a sample size imbalance between sexes, which we sought to mitigate by performing sex-balanced resampling. Nevertheless, other potential confounding factors, such as platform heterogeneity (bulk RNA-seq vs. microarray) and unknown clinical covariates between cohorts, may still impact the detection of sex differentially expressed genes. Because bulk RNA-seq data from bone marrow samples capture mixed cell populations, sex-specific differences in immune or stromal composition may contribute to some observed sex-specific expression and their associations with somatic mutations and survival. Although we prioritized tumor-intrinsic regulation and incorporated plasma-cell-enriched datasets where available, we cannot fully disentangle tumor-cell-intrinsic effects from microenvironmental influences. In addition, in our work, somatic cis-eQTL analysis was performed using mutations restricted to a 50 kb window of the TSS, which may miss distal regulatory elements. Moreover, the identification of sex-specific somatic cis-eQTLs relied on cross-sectional associations between somatic mutations and gene expression, which does not establish causal directionality. Potential confounding factors such as tumor purity, clonal dominance, and sub-clonal architecture may influence both mutation detection and gene expression levels, affecting our somatic eQTL findings. Careful follow-up validation is needed to examine the causality of these candidate somatic regulatory associations. Finally, although our survival analysis attempts to adjust for major covariates, unmeasured factors may still affect outcomes.
In conclusion, we demonstrate that sex-specific somatic mutations can modulate gene expression and shape MM clinical outcomes. Our work provides mechanistic insight into sex differences in MM. These markers provide opportunities to refine prognostic models and guide therapeutic decision-making, advancing precision medicine in MM and improving survival outcomes for both sexes.
