KEGG enrichment highlighted viral protein interactions involving cytokines, IL-17 signaling, and chemokine receptor interactions. Their similar expression in CSCC and CIN 3 suggests a potential prognostic value and that CIN 3 progression may involve changes in gene expression. We propose the term “CSCC-like carcinoma,” indicating CIN 3's increased invasive potential at the molecular level.

## Introduction
Cervical cancer, primarily driven by persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) types, remains a significant global health challenge [ ]. Around 93.8 % of cervical squamous cell carcinomas and 90.9 % of cervical adenocarcinomas are linked to high-risk human papillomavirus (hrHPV) infections [ , ]. Each year, approximately 604,127 new cases of cervical cancer are diagnosed worldwide, resulting in 350,000 deaths. Unfortunately, a woman dies from cervical cancer every 2 min. In 2020, an estimated 10 million cancer-related deaths were reported making it one of the leading causes of death worldwide [ , , , ]. Globally, 291 million women are carriers of hrHPV DNA, Cervical cancer (who.int). Persistent HPV infection can disrupt the immune microenvironment, leading to chronic inflammation that promotes the progression of cervical squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC) and may affect therapeutic outcomes [ ]. Cervical intraepithelial neoplasia-grade 3 (CIN 3) is closely associated with HPV and is a severe dysplastic condition affecting the cervical squamous epithelium associated with a significant risk of progression to cervical cancer. Approximately 15 % of patients with low-grade dysplasia progress to high-grade dysplasia, ultimately advancing to CSCC. Patients diagnosed with CIN 3 have a significantly elevated risk of progression to invasive cervical cancer compared to those with normal cytology or low-grade lesions; for example, large cohort studies have reported a two-to fourfold increased risk over 10–20 years relative to women with normal screening results or CIN 1 [ , , ]. If left untreated, up to 40 % of CIN 3 lesions may progress to invasive carcinoma [ ].
The TME is heterogeneous and composed of various cell types, including cancer-associated fibroblasts, a diverse array of immune cells, and non-cellular extracellular matrix components [ ]. The dynamic interactions and continuous crosstalk between tumor cells and their surrounding microenvironment can significantly influence tumor behavior, either inhibiting growth, progression, or metastasis [ , , ]. Additionally, the TME plays a crucial role in shaping the effectiveness of cancer immunotherapy by modulating immune cell infiltration and activation [ , , ]. There remains an incomplete understanding of the HPV-associated CSCC and CIN 3 immune microenvironment, particularly concerning the interactions between inflammation, immunity, oncogenes, and tumor suppressor genes. To understand the TME transcriptome we examined the transcriptomic landscape of 168 genes associated with the interactions between tumor cells and the cellular mediators of inflammation and immunity.

## Materials and methods
2.1

## Sample collection and participant characteristics
A total of seventy formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue samples of newly diagnosed untreated cases of 37 CSCC, 20 CIN 3 and 13 nonmalignant cervical tissue were collected from two hospitals: King Fahad Medical City and Dr. Sulaiman Al-Habib Medical Group, spanning period from 2010 to 2022. All tissue samples from histologically confirmed cases of CIN 3 and CSCC. To minimize the inclusion of unaffected areas, a board-certified pathologist reviewed H&E-stained reference slides to identify and annotate areas of diagnostic. Only these tumor-enriched regions were macrodissected for molecular analysis. For each sample, 5–8 serial 10 μm-thick unstained FFPE sections. Only one slide was used for DNA and RNA co-extraction per sample, utilizing the Qiagen AllPrep DNA/RNA FFPE Kit. Quality control included assessment of nucleic acid concentration using NanoDrop2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and amplification of internal control genes (GAPDH and HMBS). The inclusion criteria for participants were as follows: (1) Confirmed cases of CSCC or CIN 3. (2) Patients aged 18 years or older. (4) Complete clinical and demographic information available ( ) and (5) Adequate quality of FFPE samples, including sufficient tissue for molecular analysis. Exclusion criteria included (1) Patients who had received prior treatment (e.g., chemotherapy, radiation): that may affect gene expression in the samples. (2) FFPE samples that were insufficient in quantity or quality for molecular analysis. (3) Patients with a history of other malignancies (4) Missing clinical or demographic information. These criteria aimed to ensure that the study population was well-defined, allowing for clear comparisons between the CSCC and CIN 3. To minimize cross-contamination during the sectioning and processing of tissues, stringent protocols were implemented throughout the study. 

2.2

## Nucleic acid extraction and first-strand cDNA synthesis
Viral DNA and total RNA were extracted using the AllPrep DNA/RNA FFPE Kit from Qiagen (Hilden, Germany), following the protocol provided by the manufacturer. The purity and concentration of the extracted nucleic acids were assessed using a NanoDrop2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). For the synthesis of complementary DNA (cDNA) and removal of genomic DNA, an RT2 first-strand synthesis kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA) was employed according to the manufacturer's guidelines, utilizing 1.5 μL of RNA for the process. The quality and integrity of the extracted nucleic acids were further evaluated using the internal housekeeping gene GAPDH. To ensure precise measurements and avoid contamination, rigorous protocols were adhered to during RNA handling, including using a biological safety cabinet and maintaining RNase-free conditions.

2.3

## Detection and genotyping of HPV
Detection and genotyping of HPV were performed using a qPCR approach, utilizing SYBR Green/ROX Master Mix as per the manufacturer's protocol. The qPCR reaction mixture consisted of 12.5 μL of 2 × RT2 SYBR Green Master Mix, 1 μL of forward primer, 1 μL of reverse primer, 2 μL of complementary DNA (cDNA), and 8.5 μL of RNase-free water. The specific primers used in the assays are provided in Ref. [ ]. Amplification was performed on an ABI 7500 Fast real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). The cycling protocol included an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles consisting of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 57 °C, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s.

2.4

## Comprehensive gene expression profiling of the cancer inflammation, immunity crosstalk, oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes via RT2 profiler PCR array
The following procedures were implemented to analyze mRNA expression. First, cDNA synthesis and the removal of genomic DNA were conducted using the RT2 First Strand Synthesis Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA), following the manufacturer's protocol. The resulting cDNA was then analyzed through RT2-PCR using two specific kits: the Cancer Inflammation and Immunity Crosstalk Array (PAHS-181Z) and the Oncogenes and Tumor Suppressor Genes Array (PAHS-502ZC-12) from SABiosciences (Frederick, MD, USA). These arrays facilitated the evaluation of expression levels for 168 genes associated with tumor cell interactions, inflammatory mediators, oncogenesis, tumor suppression, transcription factors, angiogenesis, and apoptosis. A detailed list of the genes included in both RT2 Profiler PCR Array Kits can be found on the manufacturer's website (https://geneglobe.qiagen.com/us/product-groups/rt2-profiler-pcr-arrays). Real-time PCR was performed via the RT2 Profiler PCR Array using SYBR Green/ROX qPCR Master Mix (Qiagen), as previously described [ ]. This ready-to-use master mix incorporates ROX as a passive reference dye and SYBR Green for fluorescence detection. For each 96-well plate, a total volume of 2700 μL was prepared, consisting of 1350 μL of 2 × RT2 SYBR Green Master Mix, 102 μL of cDNA, and 1248 μL of RNase-free water. Subsequently, 25 μL of the qPCR component mix was dispensed into each well of the PCR array. The 96-well array was then placed into the ABI 7500 Fast instrument (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) for amplification. The PCR cycling protocol included an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 15 s at 95 °C for denaturation and 1 min at 60 °C for annealing, during which extension and fluorescence data were collected. The relative gene expression data were analyzed using online data analysis software (www.qiagen.com). Gene expression levels were quantified using the ΔΔCt method and normalized against five housekeeping genes as previously described [ ].

2.5

## Analysis of real-time RT2 profiler PCR array
The data from the PCR Array were analyzed using the Qiagen Online Analysis Software (https://geneglobe.qiagen.com/us/analyze. Each plate included three positive PCR controls and three reverse transcription controls to ensure assay reliability. The RT-PCR arrays included control wells for the assessment of potential genomic contamination, reverse transcription efficiency, and positive PCR controls. Contamination levels of human genomic DNA were assessed following the manufacturer's guidelines. The cycle threshold (Ct) values obtained during quantification were utilized to calculate fold changes in mRNA expression, employing the 2−ΔΔCt method compared to control group. To enhance accuracy, ΔCt values were normalized against the average of five housekeeping genes.

2.6

## Investigation of the CD4+ T cell subsets in TME CSCC and CIN 3
A comprehensive investigation was conducted into the roles of various CD4+ T cell markers in patients with CSCC and CIN 3, including Th1 and Th2 cytokines and chemokines. The levels of twelve human proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines were evaluated, specifically IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12, IL-17A, IFN-γ, TNF-α, and GM-CSF, using the Multi-Analyte ELISArray from Qiagen (Germantown, MD, USA). This analysis was conducted following the manufacturer's protocol. The selection of these cytokines and chemokines is based on their crucial roles in mediating inflammatory responses.

2.7

## Cancer Hallmark enrichment analysis
The Cancer Hallmarks database (CancerHallmarks.com) was used to perform enrichment analysis on all upregulated genes identified in CSCC and CIN 3. This database offers a validated framework that outlines fundamental biological principles shared among various cancers, making it a valuable resource for understanding tumor growth and metastasis. The enrichment analysis involved integrating an established gene set with the cancer hallmark pathways described in the database.

2.8

## Pathways, gene ontology and enrichment analysis of co-expressed
Metascape (http://metascape.org) is a powerful tool for gene function enrichment and classification, facilitating the analysis of gene sets. The Gene Ontology (GO) framework encompasses three primary components: biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions, which collectively provide a comprehensive annotation of gene roles and their interrelationships. In this study, genes were analyzed using the GO function and KEGG pathway enrichment network available on the Metascape platform. The Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) algorithm was employed to identify interconnected hub genes within the networks. Each identified MCODE network was color-coded to visually represent the strength of interactions among the molecules. For the interaction enrichment analysis, default parameters for minimum and maximum network sizes were set at 2 and 500, respectively.

2.9

## Statistical analysis
All samples (CSCC: n = 34; CIN 3: n = 20; Control: n = 13) were tested individually. No pooling was performed. Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). For comparisons between two groups, Student's t-test was employed, while one-way or two-way ANOVA was used for comparisons involving more than two groups, followed by Sidak's or Tukey's multiple comparisons tests as appropriate. A correlation matrix was generated for correlation analyses. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results
3.1

## HPV genotype prevalence and distribution in CSCC and CIN 3
The distribution of HPV genotypes in 37 patients with cervical squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC) and 20 patients with cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 3 (CIN 3) was investigated. The presence of eight high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) genotypes (HPV-16, HPV-18, HPV-31, HPV-33, HPV-35, HPV-39, HPV-58, and HPV-45) was assessed in each patient ( ). HPV-35 was the most common genotype in both groups, detected in 70.3 % (26/37) of CSCC patients and 45 % (9/20) of CIN 3 patients. HPV-16 genotype was detected in CSCC (40.5 %, 15/37) and CIN 3 (35 %, 7/20) patients. HPV-18 was present in 13.5 % (5/37) of CSCC patients and 20 % (4/20) of CIN 3 patients. HPV-33 was only detected in CIN 3 patients (15 %, 3/20). HPV-39 was found in a small proportion of CSCC (2.7 %, 1/37) and CIN 3 (10 %, 2/20) patients. HPV-58 was exclusively detected in CSCC patients (2.7 %, 1/37). HPV-45 was found in 18.9 % (7/37) of CSCC patients and 20 % (4/20) of CIN 3 patients. HPV-31 was not detected in any of the patients in this study. These results highlight the high prevalence of HPV-35 as the most common genotype in both CSCC and CIN 3 patients. HPV-18 showed a moderate presence in both groups, with a higher percentage in CIN 3 patients (20 %) than in CSCC patients (13.5 %). HPV-33 was not detected in CSCC patients but was present in 15 % of CIN 3 patients. HPV-35 had the highest prevalence in CSCC patients (70.3 %) and CIN 3 patients (45 %). HPV-39 and HPV-58 had low prevalence in both groups, with HPV-39 being slightly more common in CIN 3 patients. HPV-45 had similar prevalence in both groups, with 18.9 % in CSCC and 20 % in CIN 3 patients. HPV-35 emerged as the most prevalent genotype. This finding underscores the potential role of HPV-35 in the progression from CIN to invasive cervical cancer.

## Vaccine-genotype type coverage and HPV genotype frequencies in CSCC and CIN 3 patients
A total of eight high-risk HPV genotypes were analyzed in this study: HPV-16, -18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, and 58. The distribution of single and multiple infections is summarized in . 
In the CSCC group (n = 34), 53 % (18/34) had a single HPV infection and 47 % (16/34) had multiple infections. The most frequently detected genotype was HPV-35, present in 47 % of single infections and included in 73.5 % of total cases. HPV-16 was detected in 40.5 % of cases (single or co-infection). In the CIN 3 group (n = 16), 37.5 % (6/16) had a single HPV infection and 62.5 % (10/16) had multiple infections. HPV-35 was again the most common genotype overall, detected in 56 % of CIN 3 cases, followed by HPV-16 (35 %). Among the 9 high-risk genotypes targeted by the nine-valent HPV vaccine (HPV-6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58), HPV-16 and HPV-45 were most frequently detected. However, HPV-35, a non-vaccine genotype, was the most prevalent in both CSCC and CIN 3. These findings stress the importance of regional surveillance and potential gaps in vaccine coverage. This is further discussed in the Conclusion and Public Health Implications section.

3.3

## Analysis of gene expression patterns of tumor inflammation and immunity crosstalk in HPV-associated CIN 3 and CSCC
Expression profiles of 84 genes involved in tumor inflammation and immunity were assessed using a targeted PCR array. All reported fold changes below are expressed relative to HPV-negative normal cervical tissue, unless otherwise specified. In CIN 3, multiple pro-inflammatory and immunoregulatory genes were significantly upregulated. These included CCL5 (14.3-fold), CSF2 (11.4-fold), CSF3 (12.4-fold), CD274 (PD-L1, 11.4-fold), CXCL10 (5.5-fold), and IFNG (4.3-fold). Genes associated with T cell regulation and immune checkpoint control, such as STAT1 (4.4-fold) and TGFB1 (2.0-fold), were also elevated. These findings suggest an active inflammatory and immune-responsive microenvironment in CIN 3 lesions ( ). In CSCC, pro-tumorigenic and immune escape pathways became more prominent. Genes such as CXCL8 (9.08-fold), CCL20 (7.08-fold), VEGFA (5.71-fold), FOXP3 (5.22-fold), PTGS2 (COX-2, 6.8-fold), and IL6 (2.57-fold) showed marked overexpression, indicating increased angiogenesis, immunosuppression, and pro-inflammatory signaling during progression to invasive carcinoma ( ). To explore progression-related shifts, we compared gene expression directly between CIN 3 and CSCC. Some genes remained highly expressed in both, but with distinct profiles; several genes were upregulated in CSCC vs. CIN 3: CXCL8, CCL20, IL6, FOXP3, VEGFA, all significantly higher in CSCC (p < 0.001 for most). On the other hand, CCL5, CSF2, CSF3, CD274, CXCL10, IFNG, BCL2, and STAT1 were downregulated in CSCC vs. CIN 3, suggesting a loss of immune signaling as lesions progress. These results indicate a functional transition from an inflammatory but immunologically active environment in CIN 3 to a more immunosuppressive, angiogenic phenotype in CSCC ( ). 

3.4

## Analysis of oncogenes and tumor suppressor gene expression in HPV + CIN 3 and CSCC
The results reveal relatively similar gene expression levels between patients with CIN 3 and CSCC. Due to limitations in RNA quantity from FFPE samples, gene expression analysis for this panel was restricted to a subset of the study cohort: CIN 3 (n = 11) and CSCC (n = 20). The co-expression of several key genes highlights potential biological similarities between these groups ( ). For instance, CASP8 expression was substantially higher in CSCC patients (mean: 22.47) compared to CIN 3 patients (mean: 5.70; p < 0.001). Similarly, BCL2L1, an anti-apoptotic gene, showed a dramatic increase in CSCC (mean: 16.70) versus CIN 3 (mean: 1.99; p < 0.001), suggesting a mechanism that may enhance cell survival ( ). Other notable differences included KRAS, which exhibited elevated expression in CSCC (mean: 6.53) compared to CIN 3 (mean: 1.62; p < 0.0001), This substantial upregulation may indicate activation of proliferative and survival pathways, suggesting that KRAS plays a pivotal role in tumorigenesis. Additionally, APC expression increased from CIN 3 (mean: 2.00) to CSCC (mean: 4.99; p < 0.001), while BAX expression decreased from CIN 3 (mean: 11.73) to CSCC (mean: 9.33; p < 0.001). Other genes, including EGF (mean: 6.5 in CSCC vs. 1.6 in CIN 3; p < 0.0001), RB1 (mean: 12.12 in CSCC vs. 7.42 in CIN 3; p < 0.001), and ZHX2 (mean: 17.57 in CSCC vs. 4.93 in CIN 3; p < 0.001), also displayed significant upregulation in CSCC ( ).

## Downregulation of immune function and inflammatory genes in CIN 3 and CSCC patients
The transcriptomic analysis revealed similar downregulation of immune function and inflammatory genes in patients with CIN 3 and CSCC relative to HPV-negative control cervical tissue. The most notably downregulated gene in CSCC was CXCR1 (−14.16 vs −10.70). The second highly downregulated genes in CSCC were BCL2L1 (−7.11 vs −13.134), MIF (−4.41 vs −12.45), CXCR4 (−2.43 vs −5.84), STAT3 (−2.95 vs −5.431), TP53 (−2.02 vs −3.40), MYC (−4.53 vs −6.85), SPP1 (−3.29 vs −5.45), IDO1 (−4.16 vs −6.38), CSF1 (−5.55 vs −9.49), and IL4 (−2.51 vs - 6.28). Significant decreases were also observed in genes associated with immune response and chemokine signaling, such as AICDA (−3.17 vs −6.12), CCL21 (−6.00 vs −10.31), CCR2 (−3.84 vs −6.03), CCR7 (−3.88 vs −6.04), and HIF1A (−3.62 vs −5.26). Furthermore, genes involved in antigen presentation, including HLA-A and HLA-C, also demonstrated downregulation in CSCC, with mean fold changes of −5.99 vs −7.32 and −1.96 vs −4.35, respectively ( ).

Downregulation of various oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes in CIN 3 and CSCC patients was found. shows the mean expression levels of oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes. ## Clustering analysis of gene expression of tumor inflammation and immunity crosstalk in HPV associated CIN 3 and CSCC
Gene cluster patterns of genes associated with tumor inflammation and immunity crosstalk in HPV associated-CIN 3 and CSCC were identified. 4 major gene clusters in the CSCC patients were found: cluster 1 (cytokines and Growth Factors) - this cluster included CSF2, CSF3, IL2, and IL23A; cluster 2 (chemokines) - this cluster included CCL5, CXCL8, and CXCL10; cluster 3 (transcription factors and signaling molecules) - this cluster included genes such as NFKB1, STAT1, and STAT3; and cluster 4 (interleukins and immune modulators) - this cluster included IL1B, IL6, and TNF. These results suggest an active inflammatory tumor microenvironment in CSCC. 5 distinct gene clusters were identified in CINIII patients ( ): cluster 1 (immune modulation and inflammatory response) - this cluster included CCL5, CD274, CSF2, CSF3, BCL2, CCL18; cluster 2 (T-cell regulation and activation) - this cluster included CXCR3, IL13, IL12, FOXP3, GZMA; cluster 3 (growth factors and receptors) - this cluster included EGFR, IL23A, KITLG, TGFB1; cluster 4 (chemokines and cytokines) - this cluster included XCL9, CXCL10, CCR10, IL10, CCL20; and cluster 5 (inflammatory and stress response) - this cluster included IFNG, NOS2, VEGFA, STAT1 ( ). 

3.8

## Cluster analysis of gene expression profiles of oncogenes and tumor suppressors in HPV associated CIN 3 and CSCC
Clustering of upregulated genes in CSCC patients revealed distinct gene expression patterns indicative of critical biological processes ( ). Cluster 1, characterized by elevated expression of CASP8, BCL2L1, and ZHX2, points to the activation of apoptotic regulation and cell survival mechanisms. Cluster 2 showed moderate expression of BAX, RB1, KIT, and KRAS, suggesting dysregulation in apoptosis and growth factor signaling pathways. In contrast, Cluster 3 genes, including CTNNB1, APC, FOS, MYC, and NFKB1, showed generally low expression, indicating potential downregulation of Wnt signaling and oncogenic transcription factors in CSCC. 
In patients with CIN 3, a similar clustering approach revealed that Cluster 1 genes (BAX, RB1, EGF, and KIT) were notably upregulated, highlighting alterations of apoptosis, cell cycle control, and growth factor signaling. Another cluster with moderate to high expression included genes involved in apoptotic pathways and transcriptional regulation, such as CASP8, ZHX2, TGFB1, PRKCA, KRAS, NFKB1, and NFKBIA, highlighting complex signaling cascades active in CIN 3. Cluster 3 genes (APC, BCL2L1, FOS, MYC, RET, and CTNNB1) exhibited low expression, supporting the concept of altered Wnt pathway and cell survival signaling in CIN 3 ( ).

## Gene-gene correlation analysis of gene expression in HPV associated CIN 3 and CSCC reveals key molecular interactions
Correlation analysis of gene expression in CSCC patients revealed significant gene-gene interactions across various functional groups, including cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and transcription factors. The immune response modulators group (CSF2, CSF3, IL2, IL23A, IL12, IL6) showed strong positive correlations, particularly between IL2 and IL23A, as well as between CSF2 and CSF3, suggesting a coordinated role in modulating the immune environment in CSCC. Chemokines and their receptors group (CCL18, CCL20, CCL4, CCR1, CXCR2, CXCR3) exhibited moderate to strong positive correlations. A strong negative correlation between CCL20 and CCR1 was found in CSCC. Growth factors and associated receptors group (EGFR, VEGFA, KITLG) demonstrated variable but moderate positive correlations. Transcription factors and cell cycle regulators group (STAT1, NFKB1, PDCD1) exhibited moderate correlations. Notable inter-group correlations were observed between IL6 and NFKB1 ( ). 
Correlation analysis of CIN 3 samples revealed moderate to strong positive correlations in the growth factors and receptors group (EGFR, VEGFA, KITLG, and TGFB1). The cell cycle and apoptosis regulation group (BCL2, PDCD1, STAT1, and NFKB1) showed a relatively positive correlation. T-cell regulation and activation groups, including IL2, IL17A, FOXP3, and GZMA, showed positive interactions among genes involved in T-cell activation and regulation ( ).

## Gene expression correlation analysis of oncogenes and tumor suppressors in HPV + CIN 3 and CSCC
The analysis of upregulated oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes in HPV-associated CSCC showed positive correlation of various gene pairs, NFKBIA-CASP8 (r = 0.99), NFKB1-MYC (r = 0.99), FOS-ELK1 (r = 0.98), MYC-KIT (r = 0.96) and ZHX2-RB1 (r = 0.96). The results found a weak correlation between CTNNB1 and several genes. On the other hand, we observed a strong negative correlation between many gene pairs; RB1-TGFB1 (r = −0.67), PRKCA-BCL2L1 (r = −0.97), BCL2L1-EGF (r = −0.74), MYC-TGFB1 (r = −0.64) and CASP8-RET (r = −0.80). Moreover, genes like APC exhibited negative correlations with BCL2L1 (r = −0.37) and CTNNB1 (r = −0.35) ( ). 
In CIN 3 patients the apoptosis-related cluster: BAX displayed positive correlations with BCL2L1, CASP8, and FOS. In contrast, a mix of positive and negative correlations were observed between APC and BAX and BCL2L1. The proliferation and signaling genes (FOS, NFKB1, and MYC) showed strong positive correlations with each other. Also, KRAS showed positive correlations with NFKB1 and EGF. Moreover, RB1 demonstrated strong and moderate correlations with the tumor suppressor ZHX2, NFKB1 and MYC respectively. Likewise, a strong positive correlation was shown between CTNNB1 and ELK1. Conversely, modest negative correlations were observed between APC and both BAX (r = −0.37), BCL2L1 (r = −0.37) as well as between CTNNB1 and CASP8 (r = −0.17) and BCL2L1 (r = −0.16) ( ).

## Cancer Hallmark pathway enrichment analysis
To explore the functional significance of genes co-upregulated in CIN 3 and CSCC, we performed enrichment analysis against the Cancer Hallmarks gene set (6763 genes, curated). Of the 55 genes co-upregulated across lesions, 53 mapped to at least one cancer hallmark. The most highly enriched hallmark was sustaining proliferative signaling, with 46 genes involved, followed by resistance to cell death (46 genes), evading growth suppressors (42 genes), and tumor-promoting inflammation (32 genes). Additional enriched hallmarks included tissue invasion and metastasis (37 genes), angiogenesis (37 genes), immune evasion (33 genes), and replicative immortality (17 genes). Fewer genes were associated with genome instability (9 genes) and metabolic reprogramming (13 genes), as shown in and the Supplementary Table. 
These results indicate that CIN 3 lesions already exhibit broad activation of cancer hallmark pathways, suggesting early acquisition of traits associated with malignancy. In particular, enrichment of immune modulation (e.g., PDCD1, CD274), angiogenesis (VEGFA, IL6), and proliferative control (MYC, EGFR) genes highlights overlapping molecular mechanisms supporting pre-invasive stages.

## Pathway and process enrichment analysis of Co-expressed genes in CSCC and CIN 3
The Metascape database was used to analyze and visualize gene enrichment across Gene Ontology (GO) terms, including ‘biological processes,’ ‘cellular components,’ and ‘molecular functions. The Gene Ontology (GO) of the co-expressed genes in CSCC and CIN 3 was enriched in several biological processes (Gene Ontology), including cancer pathways, inflammatory response, positive regulation of cell migration, cell development, population proliferation, peptidyl-tyrosine phosphorylation, and response to viral infection. KEGG pathway enrichment analyses revealed significant associations with viral protein interactions involving cytokines and cytokine receptors, as well as pathways related to human cytomegalovirus infection, IL-17 signaling, Hepatitis C, and Leishmaniasis. In addition, WikiPathway enrichment highlighted various cancer pathways, an overview of pro-inflammatory and profibrotic mediators, pleural mesothelioma, allograft rejection, photodynamic therapy-induced AP-1 survival signaling, gastrin signaling, and spinal cord injury. ## Discussion
The TME is critical in initiating and progressing various cancer types, making it a prominent recent research focus. However, achieving a deeper understanding of the specific components of the TME particularly the roles of immune oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes remains a significant challenge. To enhance understanding of gene expression within the TME of both HPV-associated CSCC and CIN 3, the study focused on profiling the expression of 168 genes that are involved in the interactions between tumor cells and the cellular mediators of inflammation and immunity, oncogenesis, tumor suppression, transcription regulation, angiogenesis, and apoptosis.
Our gene expression analysis identified several genes co-expressed between patients with HPV-associated CSCC and CIN 3, providing insights into molecular mechanisms potentially underlying the progression from CIN 3 to CSCC. This study also assessed the prevalence of eight high-risk HPV genotypes, which may serve as preliminary data on HPV distribution in Saudi Arabia. Notably, HPV-35 emerged as the most frequently detected genotype among patients with CSCC and CIN 3, suggesting a notable burden of HPV types not currently covered by the available vaccines. Although HPV-35 has been reported in approximately 2 % of invasive cervical cancers globally, its prevalence increases up to 10 % in certain regions, such as Sub-Saharan Africa, where a significant association with precancerous lesions and cervical cancers has been documented [ ].
Future studies with larger, more representative cohorts will be essential to more accurately define the regional HPV genotype landscape and its clinical implications.
Our findings emphasize the importance of region-specific HPV genotype surveillance, particularly in populations where non-vaccine genotypes such as HPV-35 are prevalent. While HPV-16 remains the dominant oncogenic type globally, the high frequency of HPV-35 detected in both CSCC and CIN 3 patients in our cohort reflects similar trends reported in parts of sub-Saharan Africa, where HPV-35 has been associated with increased cervical cancer risk and poorer clinical outcomes [ , ]. These data support the need for expanded vaccine coverage or next-generation multivalent vaccines that address regional prevalence genotypes not currently included in bivalent, quadrivalent, or even nonavalent vaccines. It is important to contextualize our findings in relation to local HPV vaccination practices. In Saudi Arabia, national immunization programs currently provide the Gardasil-9, which does not include HPV-35 or HPV-39. Consequently, the detection of HPV-35 and HPV-39, which are not included in currently administered vaccines, highlights the need for continued genotype-specific surveillance and may have implications for future vaccine policy.
Conversely, HPV-39 and HPV-58 were found at low frequencies, suggesting their limited role in the current patient cohort. The similar prevalence of HPV-45 in both groups (18.9 % in CSCC and 20 % in CIN) points to its importance as a contributory factor in cervical cancer development. The emergence of HPV-35 in our patient population indicates a regional variability in HPV genotype distribution. The presence of multiple infections raises questions about the vaccine's overall effectiveness in real-world scenarios, especially where non-vaccine types like HPV-35 and HPV-39 are involved. We recommend developing vaccines that cover a broader range of HPV types, including HPV-35 and HPV-39.
While we examined the distribution of genotypes included in the nine-valent HPV vaccine (HPV-6/11/16/18/31/33/45/52/58), we acknowledge that this does not represent a direct assessment of vaccine efficacy, as participant-level vaccination status was not available. Furthermore, HPV vaccination was introduced into the Saudi national immunization program in 2010, primarily using the bivalent (Cervarix) and quadrivalent (Gardasil-4) vaccines, with Gardasil-9 not yet widely adopted during the period from which the study samples were collected. Therefore, the presence of vaccine-covered genotypes should be interpreted in the context of incomplete vaccine uptake and limited historical coverage, and not as an indicator of vaccine failure or population-level impact.
Our transcriptome analysis revealed significant upregulation of several genes in the CSCC group compared to the CIN 3 group, notably CCL20, FOXP3, IL13, IL17A, IL6, CXCL8, and VEGFA, indicating a shift towards a more inflammatory phenotype of TME in CSCC. The elevated expression of these chemokines has been positively correlated with tumor size, the presence of circulating tumor cells, and the occurrence of metastases. Additionally, higher levels of these cytokines were associated with poorer survival outcomes.
The increased expression of CCL20 and CSF2 points to a notable inflammatory component in the TME. CCL20 is involved in attracting immune cells, while CSF2 supports the differentiation and proliferation of myeloid cells. The upregulation of these chemokines may contribute to a pro-tumorigenic inflammatory milieu, facilitating tumor growth and metastasis. Furthermore, the role of FOXP3, a marker of regulatory T cells, indicates that immune suppression could play a role in cervical tumor progression, potentially limiting the efficacy of anti-tumor immunity.
Conversely, members of the CCL family, including CCL18, CCL4, and CCL5, were upregulated in CIN 3 patients, highlighting the dysregulation of chemokines in CIN 3, which influences tumor growth and metastasis. Chemokines can interact with vascular endothelial cells and lymphatic endothelial cells, promoting both angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis [ ]. Previous studies have shown that elevated expression of CCL2 and CCL5 facilitates the recruitment of tumor-associated macrophages (TAM) and regulatory T cells (Treg), which further contribute to cancer immune evasion [ , , ]. CIN 3 patients showed increased expression of genes, including CSF2, CSF3, TGFB1 and BCL2. The BCL2 overexpression is linked to anti-apoptotic processes and poor prognosis. Previous studies showed that high BCL2 mRNA levels are linked to poor prognosis in acute myeloid leukemia [ ]. The elevated BCL2 mRNA expression in CSCC suggests a diminished capacity of tumor cells to undergo programmed cell death, highlighting its role in promoting anti-apoptotic processes. TGFB1 is also associated with immune suppression and aggressive behavior [ , ]. TGF-β plays a crucial role in mediating immune suppression within the TME [ , , ].
The TGF-β signaling pathway is a key regulator of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), primarily through its control of EMT-related transcription factors. Activation of the TGF-β1 has been shown to drive EMT and metastasis in lung adenocarcinoma [ ]. These findings suggest that dysregulation of the TGF-β signaling pathway may contribute to the progression from CIN 3 to CSCC, potentially by promoting EMT and enhancing metastatic potential.
We found that mRNA expression levels of CD274 (PD-L1) were higher in patients with CIN 3 compared to those with CSCC. PD-L1 inhibits the anti-tumor immune response by sending co-inhibitory signals to effector T cells, which suppress immune surveillance. Increased PD-L1 expression is associated with poorer prognosis and resistance to cancer therapies [ , , , ]. The increased expression of 14 genes in CIN 3 could be biomarkers to differentiate CIN 3 and CSCC.
Analysis of co-expression patterns indicated that 21 genes (ACKR3, CCR1, CCR10, CXCL2, CXCL9, CXCR2, CXCR3, EGF, EGFR, GZMA, IL12B, IL1A, IL23A, KITLG, NFKB1, NOS2, PDCD1, FOS, KIT, MYC, and RET) maintained similar expression levels in HPV-associated CSCC and CIN 3, suggesting their importance in tumor behavior. The presence of similar co-expression patterns between CSCC and CIN 3 suggests that CIN 3 has acquired an increased invasive ability. Further investigation into the roles of chemokines and chemokine receptors in the transition from CIN 3 to CSCC is needed.
The positive correlation between proliferation and signaling genes (FOS, NFKB1, and MYC) can play a role in promoting cellular proliferation and regulating transcription. Interestingly, KRAS positively correlated with both NFKB1 and EGF, further demonstrating the interconnected nature of oncogenic signaling pathways. The strong positive correlation between proliferation, signaling genes like FOS and NFKB1, and MYC indicates their combined contributions to cell proliferation and transcription regulation. FOS is a member of the AP-1 transcription factor complex and is induced by multiple extracellular signals, connecting growth factor signaling to the regulation of early response genes involved in proliferation. NFKB1 has a dual role in cell survival and modulating inflammatory response, and MYC is a well-characterized oncogene that can drive cell cycle progression and metabolic reprogramming in cancer cells. These genes are highly interconnected, indicative of a strong signaling network that creates an environment favorable to tumorigenesis [ , ]. Additionally, the positive associations of KRAS with NFKB1 and EGF highlight the complexity of the oncogenic interplay. KRAS is a central orchestrator of all cell signaling and is commonly mutated in many cancers, resulting in proliferating and surviving cells [ , ]. Together, the interaction of KRAS and NFKB1 indicates that KRAS contributes to mediating inflammatory responses and immune evasion in the TME [ ]. Another crucial growth factor is EGF, which acts through KRAS signaling to modulate mitogenic signaling to promote tumor cell proliferation [ ]. The interconnectedness of the signaling pathways highlights the complexity of oncogenic pathways and how they contribute to the cancer phenotype. The hallmark of cancer with co-expressed genes in CSCC and CIN 3 is sustaining proliferative signaling, while replicative immortality and reprogramming energy metabolism are associated with the fewest genes. This supports the idea that proliferative signaling is crucial in cancer development [ ].
KEGG pathway enrichment analyses revealed significant links to various cancer pathways and viral protein interactions, particularly involving cytokines and their receptors. The analysis identified several cancer-related pathways, suggesting that their dysregulation may contribute to tumorigenesis, consistent with existing literature on aberrant signaling in cancer progression [ , ]. Notably, the IL-17 signaling pathway, known for promoting inflammation, is implicated in facilitating tumor growth through pro-inflammatory mechanisms.
The Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of co-expressed genes in CSCC and CIN 3 has revealed important biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions involved in the pathogenesis of several cancers [ ]. Our study identified that co-expressed genes in CSCC and CIN 3 are linked to inflammatory responses and cell migration, suggesting that chronic inflammation may drive cervical lesion progression. The involvement of cytokine-mediated signaling pathways highlights the role of the immune response in TME. The GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis reveals the intricate relationships between immune signaling, cell migration, and survival pathways in CSCC and CIN 3. We showed co-expressed gene patterns between CSCC and CIN3, with increased levels of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines in CSCC indicating a shift towards a more aggressive tumor phenotype. Elevated expression of genes like CCL20, IL-6, and IL-13 is associated with poor clinical outcomes, suggesting their potential as prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.
Although our results revealed overlapping gene expression profiles between CIN 3 and CSCC, such as upregulation of cancer inflammation and immunity crosstalk and oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes, CIN 3 and CSCC remain histologically and clinically distinct, principally distinguished by the lack of stromal invasion in CIN 3. Our findings suggest that CSCC-associated molecular features may begin to emerge in high-grade precancerous lesions, but we do not propose reclassification of CIN 3. Previous studies reported that progression from CIN 3 to invasive carcinoma involves molecular changes such as elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines, immune evasion, angiogenesis, and altered gene methylation patterns, all present before histological invasion. For instance, increased IL-10 correlates with CIN 2/3 progression [ , , ], while elevated cytokines IL-1β and IL-8 are found in CIN samples compared to controls. Transcriptomic studies also identify early upregulation of proliferation markers and immune suppression genes in CIN 3 lesions [ ]. This accumulated evidence supports the concept that CIN 3 lesions may involve early carcinoma-like molecular alterations, particularly in pathways governing inflammation, immune evasion, angiogenesis, and proliferation. While histological criteria remain the gold standard for diagnosis, determining which high-grade lesions exhibit a carcinoma-like molecular phenotype could inform risk stratification and clinical management strategies. Our results support the idea that molecular progression precedes morphological invasion. This raises the possibility that certain molecular signatures may identify CIN 3 lesions at higher risk for progression, which could improve triage and management strategies. Future research involving longitudinal follow-up and genome-wide molecular analysis of CIN 3 lesions is needed to validate this hypothesis and to determine their prognostic utility.
The presence of multiple high-risk HPV genotypes in individual FFPE cervical tissue should be interpreted with caution. CSCC is documented to develop through the clonal expansion of a single epithelial precursor cell persistently infected with a high-risk HPV genotype, most frequently HPV-16 or closely related alpha-9 types. The detection of multiple high-risk genotypes within a single sample is unlikely to reflect polyclonal or cooperative oncogenic transformation, and probably represents the coexistence of HPV infections in adjacent, histologically distinct epithelial regions. The cervix exhibits spatial and temporal heterogeneity in HPV infection, which may result in multifocal dysplastic lesions carrying distinct viral genotypes. In this study, DNA was extracted from macrodissected FFPE tissue sections, and thus represented bulk material rather than anatomically distinct lesions. Consequently, the attribution of a specific HPV type to the invasive component, versus surrounding intraepithelial neoplasia or latent background infection, cannot be definitively determined. Given that our genotyping was performed on macrodissected, bulk tissue without spatial resolution, we cannot definitively assign viral genotype to specific histologic compartments. Furthermore, we did not perform E6/E7 mRNA expression profiling 5 in situ hybridization, which would provide more direct evidence of transcriptionally active, causally associated HPV. As such, our genotyping data should be interpreted as indicative of the HPV landscape of the sampled region, rather than proof of direct etiologic involvement of all detected genotypes. Future studies employing spatially resolved methods, such as laser capture microdissection coupled with transcriptomic or integration profiling, are necessary to accurately assign causality and disentangle background infection from oncogenic drivers.

## Limitations
This study used targeted PCR arrays that profile a predefined set of 168 genes related to cancer inflammation, immunity, oncogenes, and tumor suppressors. While this strategy enabled focused analysis of well-characterized pathways relevant to carcinogenesis, it inherently restricts the discovery of novel transcripts, regulatory networks, and emerging biomarkers and may overlook other important factors influencing the TME and tumor progression. Future studies employing whole-transcriptome approaches (e.g., RNA-Seq) or single-cell analysis will be essential to comprehensively characterize the molecular landscape of HPV-associated cancers and uncover previously unrecognized mechanisms of immune evasion or malignant transformation. A more comprehensive study, including non-coding RNAs and epigenetic modifications, is recommended. Additionally, a lack of in vitro functional validation for the identified to confirm their roles in tumor biology and their potential as therapeutic targets. Conducting longitudinal studies to track changes in gene expression over time in cases transitioning from CIN 3 to CSCC will help identify the dynamics of tumor progression.

## Future perspective
The observed similarities in gene co-expression between HPV-positive CSCC and CIN 3 may enhance diagnostic accuracy in cervical pathology. A major challenge in this field is the frequent misdiagnosis of CIN 3 lesions as non-CSCC during pathological examination. Our results support the idea that molecular progression precedes morphological invasion. This raises the possibility that certain molecular signatures may identify CIN 3 lesions at higher risk for progression, which could improve triage and management strategies. Our findings may aid in identifying high-risk lesions and inform public health strategies to improve HPV vaccine policy, particularly in regions where current formulations may not fully reflect local genotype prevalence. Future research involving longitudinal follow-up and genome-wide molecular analysis of CIN 3 lesions is needed to validate this hypothesis and to determine their prognostic utility.
