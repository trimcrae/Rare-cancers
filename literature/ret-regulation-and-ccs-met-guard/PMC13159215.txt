## Background
The Aceraceae family has ecological, ornamental and economical importance in plants. However, the genetic variations, evolution, and domestication of Aceraceae species are not well understood. Pan-genomes provide a framework for capturing genetic diversity across a genus and for a comprehensive understanding of genomic variation during evolution.

## Results
Here, we construct a graph-based pan-genome from 13 Aceraceae species, revealing evolutionary dynamics driven by long-terminal repeat retrotransposons. Sequencing of 134 cultivated Acer
palmatum accessions identifies 2,160 single nucleotide polymorphisms associated with 10 fatty acid traits through genome wide association study. We further characterize 1,064,183 structural variation loci impacting fatty acid accumulation. By multi-omics analysis, we also identify ActKCS as a key candidate gene required for nervonic acid biosynthesis. Transgenic overexpression of ActKCS in Arabidopsis thaliana and Brassica napus enhances seed nervonic acid content, with increases ranging from 0.05% to 0.12% and 0.25% to 1.05%, respectively. We find that the transcription factor ActMYB95 directly regulates ActKCS. These findings decipher the genetic architecture underlying fatty acid synthesis and enable sustainable production of high value nervonic acid rich oils for neurological therapeutics and bio industrial applications.

## Conclusions
This study provides insights into the genetic and molecular mechanisms that underpin fatty acid biosynthesis. The resources and findings will accelerate the utilization and improvement of Aceraceae genetic diversity.

## Background
Aceraceae (known as the Maple family, 2n = 2x = 26) is one of the most important tree families in the world (~ 202 species), which is divided into Acer and Dipteronia. It exhibits a classic pattern of biogeographic divergence across Asia, Europe, North America and North Africa [ , ]. Specifically, the findings have identified over 150 species in China, which is considered to be the core distribution area. This finding is of significant importance for further research on the evolutionary history of Aceraceae species. In recent years, some Aceraceae species have been extensively domesticated and utilised due to their robust adaptability, and high ornamental and economic value (Fig.  a) [ , ]. Some nutritional components, such as amino acids, mineral elements, fatty acids and some important physiologically active substances have been evidenced in previous studies [ , ]. 
Surprisingly that the rare substance nervonic acid (24:1Δ15, cis-15-tetracosenoic acid, n-9, NA) has been detected in Acer truncatum (5–6%) [ ]. NA is a very long-chain monounsaturated fatty acid that present in abundance in the brain tissue of animal, breast milk and liver [ ]. It is difficult for the human body to synthesise and it is primarily isolated from the brain of the shark [ ], which plays a crucial role in brain development and has attracted widespread research interest. It is essential for the growth and maintenance of brain physiology and peripheral nerve tissue, and it binds to sphingosine to form sphingomyelin, a necessary component of myelin [ ]. The deficiency of NAs can result various neurodegenerative diseases, including memory loss, Alzheimer's disease, etc. [ ]. Clinical studies have demonstrated the efficacy of NA in promoting the repair and regeneration of nerve fibres and myelin sheaths in damaged brain tissue (Fig.  d), which has been used to treat Alzheimer's disease, schizophrenia, psychosis, attention deficit disorder [ , ]. As demonstrated by Alzheimer's Disease International, the number of people with Alzheimer's will be rise to 78 million in 2030 and 139 million in 2050. The annual global cost of Alzheimer's disease has now surpassed US $ 1.3 trillion, and is expected to rise to US $ 2.8 trillion by the year 2030. NA is vital for the treatment of the brain-based neurological disorders.
The extraction of NA from sharks is now prohibited for conservation reasons. It has also been identified in plant seed oils, including Malania oleifera, Cardamine graeca, Tropaeolum speciosum, and members of the Brassicaceae family and Aceraceae families [ – ]. However, the viable sources for large-scale NA extraction are constrained by limited distribution, NA content, and inconsistent seed yields. It is particularly noteworthy that NA has been specifically synthesised in the seed oil of Aceraceae plants. In 2011, A. truncatum seed oil (5–6% NA) was approved as a new resource food by the Ministry of Health of the People's Republic of China due to its characteristics of rapid growth (Fig.  b and c), wide geographical distribution, and high adaptability for commercial production.
Recently, pan-genomic studies in numerous crops plants have indicated that structural variants (SVs) play a critical role in trait determination and genetic improvement [ – ]. However, pan-genomic studies of Aceraceae trees have not yet been reported. To date, seven relatively high-quality genomes of Aceraceae species have been published [ , – ]. However, the full spectrum of genetic variation, evolution and domestication in Aceraceae species, including the role of pan-genomic diversity, remains largely unknown.
In this study, six high-quality genomes were first assembled using PacBio long reads and high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) maps. Furthermore, a graph-based pan-genome was constructed, utilising the de novo assembly of six Acer species and seven other previously reported genomes to explore the evolution of Aceraceae and genomic SVs. In addition, 134 A. palmatum cultivated species were also resequenced. The genetic factors underlying the complex molecular mechanisms of NA and oleic acid biosynthesis were explored by integrating extensive information on SVs and GWAS. Furthermore, we overexpressed ActKCS (A. truncatum KCS), a critical enzyme in NA biosynthesis, and discovered that ActMYB95 (A. truncatum MYB95) plays a regulatory role in modulating its activity. Pan-genome analyses revealed numerous genetic variations, which would serve as a valuable resource of untapped genetic diversity and facilitated us to identify key candidate genes and breeding in Acer.

## Results

## Chromosome-scale assemblies of six Acer species
We selected six representative of species Acer from different distribution areas across China, showcasing a wide of phenotypes diversity and adaptations traits. Of these species, three are included in the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN): A. pentaphyllum, A. tegmentosum and A. griseum. The remaining three species were of horticultural: A. elegantulum, A. buergerianum and A. henryi (Fig.  a and b). The six Acer species were subjected to Illumina sequencing, yielding an average of 36.02 Gb of data per species for genomic feature analysis (Additional file : Table S1). To enhance genome assembly and phasing, the integration of PacBio high-fidelity (HiFi) long-read sequences with high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) data. PacBio sequencing generated 17.62–38.23 Gb of data per genome, corresponding to 22.60–70.35 genome coverages (Additional file : Table S2). The resulting PacBio reads were assembled into contigs, while Hi-C data, with ~ 170 × coverage, were utilised to order and orientate the scaffolds into 13 pseudo-chromosomes (Additional file : Table S3). The high consistency between assembled sequences and the corresponding Hi-C data indicated that these six genomes obtained high accuracy (Additional file : Figs. S1-2). The total genome sizes ranged from 543.40 Mb to 1070.63 Mb, with scaffold N50 ranging from 37.00 Mb to 79.61 Mb, and a mean contig N50 length of 23.13 Mb (Table  ). Concurrently, haplotype-resolved genomes for each accession were derived from the phased contigs, followed by independent scaffolding and manual correction (Additional file : Table S4). The genome sizes were consistent with those analysed using K-mer and flow cytometry techniques [ ] (Additional file : Figs. S1, S3 and S4). The predicted number of genes in the assembled genomes ranged from 27,377 to 34,967. On average, IUCN-listed species exhibited a higher number of genes in comparison to horticultural species. A. tegmentosum exhibited the highest gene count and the longest average coding sequence (CDS) length (Additional file : Table S5). Moreover, the average BUSCO score for gene completeness was 98.6% (Additional file : Table S6). Additionally, we predicted an average of 1898 microRNAs (miRNAs), 1062 small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs), 1034 transfer RNAs (tRNAs), and 6049 ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) was predicted per species (Additional file : Table S7). These genome assemblies outperform currently available reference genomes. 

## Genome organization and phylogeny of Aceraceae
A comprehensive gene-based synteny analysis was conducted to investigate the structural variation in the genomes of six Acer species. This approach revealed substantial genomic rearrangements, highlighting significant variation in the organisation of specific chromosomal regions across species. Notably, large-scale rearrangements were observed in chromosomes 1, 3, 5, and 10 of A. tegmentosum, A. buergerianum, and A. pentaphyllum (Fig.  c). These findings are further corroborated by the extensive syntenic blocks observed between the six Acer species, which indicate that certain genomic regions have undergone complex evolutionary transformations.
In addition to these large-scale rearrangements, multiple inversions were detected across the chromosomes (Fig.  e and Additional file : Fig. S5). These inversions, which span various chromosomal segments, reflect the complex and dynamic evolutionary history of these species. The structural diversity observed in these Acer genomes provides a valuable opportunity to study the evolutionary processes that have shaped their current genomic architecture.
To further explore the evolutionary relationships within the Aceraceae family, we estimated the divergence times among 13 Aceraceae species were estimated using 4,210 single-copy orthologues. The analysis yielded two primary clades: Dipteronia and Acer, with a divergence time of approximately 25.2 million years ago (Mya) (Fig.  d). This finding is consistent with previous studies, further reinforcing the timeline of divergence between these two major groups. The construction of a phylogenetic tree from these data provides additional context for understanding the evolutionary lineage of the Acer species within the broader Aceraceae family.
Analysis of 4dTv distributions uncovered a strikingly conserved peak at 4dTv ≈ 0.50–0.60 across all examined Acer genomes, providing strong evidence for an ancestral whole-genome duplication (WGD) that occurred prior to diversification of the extant lineage (Supplementary Fig. 6). Each species also exhibited a minor peak near 4dTv ≈ 0.00–0.02, consistent with ongoing recent small-scale duplication (SSD) activity. Cross-species comparisons using A. truncatum further resolved the timing of lineage divergence: Atru–Abue, Atru–Aele, Atru–Agri and Atru–Ayan pairs showed sharp speciation peaks at 4dTv ≈ 0.00–0.02 (Supplementary Fig.  6 A), indicating a relatively recent radiation within Acer. In contrast, the Atru–Citrus sinensis comparison displayed a much deeper divergence peak at 4dTv ≈ 0.23–0.26 (Supplementary Fig. 6B), positioning C.sinensis as a distant outgroup relative to the Acer clade. Together, these signatures delineate a phylogenetic scenario in which an early WGD event shaped the genomic architecture of Acer, followed by more recent species diversification against a background of continued duplicate gene turnover.
Overall, our findings not only shed light on the intricate genome organisation of Acer species but also provide novel insights into the deep understanding evolutionary history of this genus, highlighting the significance of ancient genomic events in shaping the current diversity of the Aceraceae family.

## Transposable elements as drivers of genome expansion in Aceraceae
The expansion of genome size in Aceraceae species is closely associated with the accumulation of transposable elements (TEs). A significant positive correlation was observed between genome size and the proportion of repeat sequences, particularly TEs (Fig.  a). TEs are a critical source of genomic plasticity, contributing not only to genome size but also to the functional and structural evolution of plant genomes. In Aceraceae species, TEs account for over 59% of the genome, with long terminal-repeat retrotransposons (LTR-RTs) representing the dominant class. These LTR-RTs are found in substantial portions of the genome in A. pentaphyllum (54.83%), A. elegantulum (56.73%), A. henryi (51.76%), A. buergerianum (53.83%), A. griseum (55.21%), and A. tegmentosum (68.38%) (Additional file : Table S8). The high proportion of TEs highlights their vital function in the genomic framework of Aceraceae species and underscores the significance of retrotransposons in the expansion and diversification of plant genomes. Notably, the Sire and Tat retrotransposon families have been identified as key contributors to this expansion, reflecting their profound influence on genome evolution (Additional file : Table S9). 
To gain a more nuanced understanding of the role LTR-RTs play in genome diversification and speciation, a repetitive k-mer-based strategy was employed to identify genome-specific k-mers and LTR-RTs and build upon methodologies [ ]. By combining the chromosome sequences from different accessions, we successfully phased six distinct ‘subgenomes,’ providing compelling evidence of the pivotal role repetitive sequences play in the differentiation of these genomes (Fig.  d and Additional file : Figs. S7-S8). This genomic phasing reveals that the repetition and rearrangement of sequences are not random but are influenced by evolutionary pressures that have shaped the genomes of these species. Our efforts identified 49,486 genome-specific LTR-RTs, revealing the scale of LTR-RT-driven genomic variability.
Further exploration of the insertion times of these genome-specific LTR-RTs revealeded striking variability in their timing and active states across the species, which are intrinsically linked to differences in genome size. For instance, A. tegmentosum, A. griseum, and A. elegantulum exhibited a recent burst in LTR-RT activity, which correlates with their larger genome sizes (Fig.  b). This burst of retrotransposon activity likely reflects a major genomic reshaping event driven by ecological and evolutionary pressures. Interestingly, the distribution of LTR insertion times across chromosomes is not uniform, with regions of the genome experiencing more recent transposition events, highlighting the uneven nature of retrotransposon activity in the genome (Fig.  c and Additional file : Fig. S9).
The insertion time profiles of these LTR-RTs reveal a fascinating temporal landscape, with estimated insertion ages of subgenome-specific LTR-RTs ranging from approximately 0.073 to 6.99 million years ago (Mya) (Fig.  e). Notably, two major bursts of LTR-RT activity were identified: a recent burst occurred around 0.713 Mya, and another more ancient burst around 1.89 Mya. These bursts correspond with significant climatic and environmental fluctuations during the Pleistocene ice ages, suggesting that these genomic events may have been triggered by or coincided with major environmental shifts, including glaciation events, that would have exerted selective pressures on Aceraceae species. Such findings underscore the connection between abiotic factors, such as climate change, and the evolutionary processes that influence genome evolution.
The phylogenetic analysis of the genome-specific LTR-RTs provides further insight into illuminates the evolutionary dynamics of these elements within the Aceraceae family (Fig.  f and g). For the Copia class of retrotransposons, the Angela and SIRE branches are the most prevalent, accounting for 74.8% of all Copia sequences. These branches exhibit notable variation across species, indicating that Copia retrotransposons have been subject to species-specific evolutionary pressures. Similarly, for the Gypsy class, the Ret and Athila clades dominate, accounting for 62.0% and 19.5%, respectively, followed by the CRM at 14.2%. This clade-specific dominance highlights the differential contributions of various retrotransposon subtypes to the genomic architecture of Aceraceae species. Furthermore, the relative abundance of specific TE subfamilies may influence the size and structure of the genomes, shaping the evolutionary trajectory of these species.
In summary, this comprehensive analysis underscores the pivotal role that transposable elements, particularly LTR retrotransposons, play in shaping the genome size, structure, and evolutionary history of Aceraceae species. The interplay between transposon activity, environmental changes, and genome diversification provides valuable insights into the dynamic and ongoing process of plant genome evolution. These findings also offer a broader understanding of how repetitive sequences contribute to genome plasticity, speciation, and adaptive evolution in response to both biotic and abiotic challenges.

## Pan-genomic variation in Aceraceae
To explore the genomic diversity within the Aceraceae family, we performed comprehensive pangenome analyses were performed using 6 newly assembled genomes and 7 published genomes from A. yangbiense [ ], A. negundo [ ], A. truncatum [ ], A. pseudosieboldianum [ ], A. palmatum [ ], D. sinensis, and D. dyeriana [ ]. In total, we identified 398,595 orthologous genes, which were subsequnently classified into 41,903 gene families across the 13 genomes. The number of gene families increased progressively with the addition of genomes, eventually reaching a plateau, which represents the core gene content of the Aceraceae family (Fig.  a). This plateau suggests that the majority of gene families have been captured within these 13 genomes, offering a comprehensive representation of the genetic diversity in the family. 
Our analysis revealed that 25.18% of gene families were conserved across all 13 genomes, constituting the core gene set (10,549 families) (Additional file : Fig. S10), while 38.21% of gene families were shared by 2–12 species (16,013 dispensable families). Notably, 36.61% of gene families were found to be unique to individual species, comprising 15,341 private families (Fig.  b). The private gene families, which are species-specific, may reflect unique biological traits or adaptations within each species. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of these private genes highlighted their enrichment in biological processes such as growth, cellular processes, cellular component organisation, and the immune system (Additional file : Table S10). Converselyt, dispensable genes, which have been identified across multiple species, appear to contribute to species-specific adaptations. GO enrichment analysis supported this, with dispensable genes being enriched in functions such as transposase activity and pollination (Additional file : Table S11), suggesting a role in local ecological adaptation and reproductive strategies.
We further examined the structural differences among the three gene categories—core, dispensable, and private genes. Core genes exhibited significantly greater lengths of both gene and mRNA sequences in comparison to dispensable and private genes. This was reflected in the longer transcript lengths, larger coding sequences (CDS), and elevated numbers of exons in core genes (Fig.  c-f). These structural differences indicate that core genes, which are essential across species, may have undergone evolutionary pressure to maintain their function, potentially contributing to their larger size and higher complexity.
Finally, we mapped the presence-absence variation (PAV) of non-redundant gene families was mapped across the 13 Aceraceae genomes. The distribution of core and dispensable genes in terms of presence or absence across species reveals significant variation in gene PAV, with differences reaching approximately 0.22% (Fig.  g). This high degree of flexibility in the Aceraceae genome underscores the dynamic nature of gene content across species, reflecting both shared evolutionary history and ongoing species-specific diversification.

## Structural variation in the Aceraceae genome
A total of 3,902,854 structural variants (SVs) were identified by realigning the remaining 12 genomes against the A. palmatum reference genome (Additional file : Table S12). These SVs were evenly distributed along the 13 chromosomes (Fig.  a-c), including 21,784 copy number variations (CNVs), 3,801,920 indels (1,904,107 insertions and 1,897,813 deletions), and 79,150 presence/absence variations (PAVs). Furthermore, an analysis was conducted of the distribution of SVs by gene position across the genome, including in coding regions, intergenic regions, upstream and downstream regions (Fig.  d and Additional file : Tables S13-S15). Across these genomes, 0.41–1.05% of SVs overlapped with genic and flanking regions, suggesting a potential role for SVs in gene regulation. SVs were mainly enriched in RNA transport, and the synthesis and metabolism of secondary metabolites, such as ‘fatty acid, galactose, and flavonoid biosynthesis,’ etc. (Fig.  e). 
Some SVs are enriched in fatty acid biosynthesis pathways. To further explore the distribution characteristics of SVs in the fatty acid synthesis gene region, a total of 1,064,183 SVs shared by all Aceraceae species were identified. Of these, 79.90% were found in intergenic regions, while only 2.46% and 3.52% were located in the upstream and exonic regions, respectively (Fig.  f-i). A detailed characterisation of the SVs in each species revealed that the highest number of SVs were found in the KCS, LACS, SAD, FATB, GPAT and PAP genes (Additional file : Fig. 11). In this study, the content of NAs varied greatly among 13 Aceraceae species (3.36%−12.83%) (Additional file : Table S16). The A. elegantulum demonstrated the highest level of NAs, with a content of 12.83%. To investigate potential causes of the observed variation in the content of NAs, a total of 23 PAVs of the fatty acid synthesis gene were identified among 13 Aceraceae species, which were located in the upstream and downstream genes (Additional file : Table S17). Interestingly, only an insertion mutation of PAVs (1381 bp) was identified within the upstream regulatory region of the KCS synthase gene in A. elegantulum, which may improve the efficiency of NAs synthesis. These indicate that some of these SVs may be involved in the transcription of activated fatty acids in Aceraceae plants.

## Structural variant-Based GWAS identifies key loci for lipid biosynthesis in Aceraceae
Structural variants (SVs) have been increasingly recognized as causative factors for agronomically important traits [ – ]. A pangenome integrates a comprehensive set of genetic variants is essential for advancing genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of complex traits. In this study, we sequenced 134 A. palmatum cultivated species (Additional file : Table S18, Additional file : Figs. S12-S13), and identified 2, 160 SNPs associated with GWAS for 10 fatty acids (FAs) and related compounds (Additional file : Table S19, Additional file : Fig. S14). Notably, a significant SNP was identified on chromosome 2, which overlaps with a 109.18 kb quantitative trait locus (QTL) (FAD2-like, LG02_G0146.t1). This locus encodes key enzymes in linoleic acid biosynthesis in plants [ , ] and FAD2-like candidate protein-coding genes were identified in this genomic region (Fig.  a, b). Analysis of the FAD2-like alleles various from individuals accessions revealed a GA insert mutation leading to a frameshift (Additional file : Table S20, Fig.  c, e). This mutation caused a premature termination, resulting in the loss of function of the FAD2-like gene. Remarkably, we observed significant differences in the oleic and linoleic acid content between accessions carrying the reference and alternative alleles. Specifically, individuals harboring the mutation exhibited higher oleic acid and lower linoleic acid content, while those without the mutation showed the opposite profile, with lower oleic acid and higher linoleic acid content (p < 0.0001) (Additional file : Fig. S15). Furthermore, several candidate genes and transcription factors were found to be significantly associated with fatty acid biosynthesis (Fig.  a, d and Additional file : Figs. S16-S18). Notably, genes such as fatty acid hydroperoxide lyase (HPL), lipid-transfer protein (DIR1) and MYB transcription factors have been identified as pivotal regulators the regulators of lipid metabolism. The SV-based GWAS provides a critical complement to conventional SNP-based GWAS, offering novel insights for the development of molecular markers that could facilitate the breeding of lipid-enhanced Aceraceae species. 

## Central role of KCS in Nervonic acid accumulation
NAs as the rare substance are specifically synthesized with Aceraceae family. The content of NAs varied greatly among 13 Aceraceae species (3.36%−12.83%). Some structural variants (CNVs, indels, insertions, deletions and PAVs) were identified and among 13 Aceraceae pangenome. These provide the crucial data foundation for research into the mechanisms of NAs. The A. truncatum, as the sole multifunctional tree wthin Acer, has been developed and utilised by China's forestry authorities. This is primarily due to its rich content of NAs (5–6%), with high seed yield, making it suitable for cultivation and development (Fig.  b and c). We first assembled the A. truncatum genome in 2020 [ ]. It clear that NAs are synthesized from oleyl-CoA (C18:1-CoA) through a series of four enzyme-catalyzed reactions (KCS, KCR, HCD and ECR) in the elongation cycle on the endoplasmic reticulum, with three additions of C2 moieties [ ]. Among these enzymes, the 3-ketoacyl-CoA synthetase (KCS) has been identified as a key rate-limiting enzyme, playing an pivotal role in NA synthesized [ ]. To further investigate the molecular mechanism of NA synthesis in A. truncatum. The results showed that the NA content showed similar pattern to the oil body observations. The pattern of NA accumulation closely parallels that of oil body formation, with critical periods observed at 85 and 110 days after fertilization (DAF) in A. truncatum (Fig.  a, b and c). 
In previous studies, multi-omics analysis suggested that the ActKCS genes are central to NA biosynthesis in A. truncatum [ ]. To further investigate the genetic role of ActKCS in NA production, we overexpressed ActKCS in transgenic Arabidopsis and Brassica napus plants. The results showed a significant increase in NA content: from 0.05% to 0.12% in Arabidopsis (p < 0.0001) and from 0.25% to 1.05% in B. napus seeds (p < 0.0001) (Fig.  c-e, Additional file : Fig. S19 and Tables S21-22). To explore the transcriptional regulation of ActKCS, we predicted three MYB binding sites were predicted in the ActKCS promoter (Additional file : Figs. S20-S21). A yeast cDNA library with an average length of the inserted fragment length of 1000 bp was constructed (Additional file : Fig. S22). ActMYB95, an MYB transcription factor, was identified as a potential regulator of ActKCS expression, with a 1.76 kb fragment upstream of the ActKCS start codon. Dual luciferase (LUC) (Fig.  j), yeast one-hybrid assay (Y1H) (Fig.  h) and electrophoretic mobility shift (EMSA) (Fig.  i) assays were conducted to confirm whether ActMYB95 can directly regulate ActKCS expression. We found that ActKCS expression can be significantly induced by ActMYB95. The Y1H and EMSA assays confirmed that ActMYB95 is able to bind to the ActKCS promoter. These assays demonstrated that ActMYB95 significantly induced ActKCS expression and bound to the ActKCS promoter. Together, these results highlight that ActMYB95 activates ActKCS, thereby promoting the accumulation of NA in A. truncatum.

## Discussion
Nervonic acid, a vital component of the myelin sheath of nerve cells, is indispensable for nerve repair and regeneration [ ]. Its presence in the seed oils of Aceraceae species marks them as a valuable source of bioactive compounds with profound medicinal and economic implications. While Aceraceae plants have traditionally been appreciated for their ornamental value, their emerging role in oil production, particularly for fatty acids like Nervonic acid, holds significant promise for both agricultural and industrial applications [ ].
In this study, the first comprehensive pangenome for the Aceraceae family is presented, combining de novo genome assemblies from six species (A. pentaphyllum, A. tegmentosum, A. griseum, A. elegantulum, A. buergerianum, A. henryi) with seven publicly available genomes. This pangenome reveals the shared evolutionary history of Aceraceae species, characterised by an ancient whole-genome duplication (WGD) event, a hallmark of eudicot evolution [ , , ]. Our findings reveal that transposable elements (TEs) are major drivers of genome expansion across these species, contributing to the substantial variation in genome size and complexity observed within the Aceraceae family [ ].
The pangenome analysis also illuminates the functional diversity within the Aceraceae genomes. A core set of genes was identified as being conserved across all 13 genomes, alongside dispensable and private genes that contribute to species-specific traits. Notably, private genes were enriched for processes associated with growth and immune function, suggesting play a crucial role role in adapting Aceraceae species to unique ecological niches. Conversely, dispensable genes were found to be associated with transposase activity and pollination, suggesting their potential involvement in local adaptation and reproductive strategies. These insights highlight the remarkable flexibility and plasticity of Aceraceae genomes, which have evolved to respond to both environmental pressures and ecological opportunities.
Further, our work paves the way for the genomic dissection of fatty acid biosynthesis in Aceraceae species. The identification of FAD2, a pivotal gene in oleic acid biosynthesis, through SV-based GWAS, and the functional verification of ActKCS in the accumulation of Nervonic acid, signifies a significant step toward understanding the molecular basis of these previous oils. The regulatory function of ActMYB95 in controlling ActKCS expression underscores the complex transcriptional networks that govern lipid metabolism in these species. This study has successfully demonstrated that ActKCS and ActMYB95 act as a key regulatory function, resulting in an increase in NA content in transgenic Arabidopsis (0.05% to 0.12%) and B. napus (0.25% to 1.05%). Nevertheless, the NA content exhibited in this study in transgenic plants is considerably lower than that observed in naturally high-yielding Aceraceae species. The content of NAs exhibited significant variation among 13 Aceraceae species, ranging from 3.36%−12.83%. The structural variants identified within the upstream regulatory region of the KCS synthase gene, ultra-strong tissue-specific promoters, or the synergistic action of multiple rate-limiting enzymes may be primary causes of variations in nervonic acid content among 13 Aceraceae pangenome.
This study not only provides a foundational pangenomic resource for the Aceraceae family but also offers novel insights into the genetic and molecular mechanisms underlying fatty acid biosynthesis. Our findings open new avenues for genomic-assisted breeding programs aimed at improving the yield and quality of Aceraceae oils, with broad implications for the development of bio-based industries reliant on high-value plant oils.

## Conclusion
A pan-genome was constructed by integrating de novo genome assemblies from 13 Acer species. Furthermore, a total of 134 A. palmatum cultivars were subjected to resequencing. The findings of the present study reveal significant structural variation (SV) associated with key biosynthetic pathways and identify genetic loci that influence the accumulation of fatty acids, including nervonic acid. We overexpressed ActKCS in transgenic Arabidopsis and Brassica napus plants and significant increase in NA content: from 0.05% to 0.12% in Arabidopsis and from 0.25% to 1.05% in B. napus seeds. Furthermore, the regulatory role of ActMYB95 in modulating ActKCS, a critical enzyme in NA biosynthesis. In conclusion, these insights provide an unparalleled understanding of the genetic architecture governing the synthesis of this bioactive compound and open up new avenues for genetic improvement strategies aimed at increasing nervonic acid production in Aceraceae species.

## Methods

## Plant materials and growth conditions
Nicotiana benthamiana and A. thaliana were grown in a growth chamber at 22℃ with a photoperiod of 16 h light (120 μE m2 s−1) and 8 h darkness, maintaining a relative humidity of 50–75% [ ]. Plants of B. napus (g0180747) were obtained from Economic Crop Research Institute of Jiangsu Academy of Agricultural Sciences (JAAS) and were grown in the experimental fields, Nanjing, China.
Six representatives of Acer species were selected from different distribution areas across China. The A. pentaphyllum was collected from Sichuan Province. The A. elegantulum, A. henryi and A. buergerianum were collected from Nanjing, Jiangsu Province. The A. griseum was collected from Beijing, and the A. tegmentosum were from Tonghua, Jilin Province. The 134 cultivated A. palmatum were collected from Ningbo, Nomura Nursery Cooperative in Zhejiang Province.

## DNA preparation and SMRT sequencing
High-molecular-weight (HMW) genomic DNA was isolated from juvenile leaves using an optimized cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) protocol [ ]. DNA fragments (15–40 kb) were generated using a Megaruptor 3 system (Diagenode) and subsequently processed to construct SMRTbell libraries following the manufacturer's guidelines [ ]. Library quantification was performed using a Qubit 4.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) Fluorometer before sequencing on PacBio RSII/Sequel platforms with 10-h movie capture times.

## Hi-C chromatin interaction mapping
Fresh leaves were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde and chromatin was digested with HindIII restriction enzyme (NEB). Biotin-14-dATP (Thermo Fisher) was incorporated during blunt-end repair using Klenow polymerase (NEB). Proximity ligation was performed with T4 DNA ligase (NEB), followed by crosslink reversal and DNA purification. Biotinylated junctions were enriched using streptavidin magnetic beads before constructing 350 bp insert libraries for Illumina sequencing.

## Genome size estimation
The genome size was estimated using k-mer frequency analysis. The k-mer distribution followed a Poisson’s distribution, characteristic of the genome. Jellyfish (v2.2.6) [ ] was used to generate the 17-mer distribution from CCS reads prior to assembly.

## Genome assembly
Genome assembly for the six accessions was performed using CCS clean reads and the Hifiasm software [ ] with the following parameters: “-l 2 –purge-cov 100 –high-het -s 0.01.” De novo assembly was completed by anchoring contigs into chromosomes using Hi-C reads with Juicer and 3D-DNA. The assembled genomes were manually corrected with Juicebox (v1.11.08) [ ]. The completeness of the genomes was assessed using BUSCO [ ] (eudicots_odb10) and LAI (full-length long terminal repeat retrotransposons). Synteny across genomes was analyzed with MCScanX [ ].

## Repeat sequence annotation
Repetitive elements were annotated using a combination of homology-based and de novo prediction strategies. Homology-based prediction involved screening the Acer genome assemblies against the RepBase database (http://www.girinst.org/repbase/) using RepeatMasker (http://www.repeatmasker.org/) [ ]. RepeatProteinMask was concurrently employed to identify coding domains of transposable elements (TEs) by searching against a curated library of repetitive protein sequences.
For de novo prediction, a species-specific repeat library was constructed using multiple tools: LTR-FINDER (http://tlife.fudan.edu.cn/ltr_finder/) was used to identify full-length Long Terminal Repeat Retrotransposons (LTR-RTs), while RepeatScout and RepeatModeler (http://www.repeatmasker.org/) were utilized to construct consensus sequences for novel repeat families. This custom library was then used with RepeatMasker for genome-wide screening. Finally, TRF (Tandem Repeat Finder, http://tandem.bu.edu/trf/trf.html) was used for the dedicated identification and characterization of tandem repeats (TRs).

## Gene structure prediction and integration
Protein-coding genes were predicted using an integrated approach that leveraged homology, de novo, and transcriptomic evidence. Homology-based prediction utilized protein sequences from six closely related plant species and Uniprot protein database (UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot, https://www.uniprot.org/uniprotkb). These sequences were mapped to the Acer genome using BLAST (http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi), followed by precise gene structure prediction with GeneWise (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/~birney/wise2/) [ ]. De novo prediction was based on the statistical properties of the genomic sequence (e.g., codon usage, exon–intron distribution), employing software including Augustus (http://bioinf.uni-greifswald.de/augustus/) [ ], GlimmerHMM (http://ccb.jhu.edu/software/glimmerhmm/) [ ], and SNAP (http://homepage.mac.com/iankorf/) [ ]. Transcriptomic evidence from RNA-seq data was aligned to the genome using BLAT (http://genome.ucsc.edu/cgi-bin/hgBlat) to support and refine gene models. The final, non-redundant gene set was generated by integrating all evidence sources using EVidenceModeler (EVM) (http://evidencemodeler.sourceforge.net/). Subsequently, the resulting EVM models were refined using the PASA (Program to Assemble Spliced Alignments, http://pasa.sourceforge.net/) software, incorporating assembled transcripts to: refine splice boundaries, extend gene models to include 5' and 3' Untranslated Regions (UTRs), and identify Alternative Splicing (AS) events.

## Functional and non-coding RNA annotation
Functional annotation was performed by searching the final protein-coding gene set against multiple public databases: SwissProt/UniProt, the NCBI Non-redundant protein (Nr) database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein), Pfam protein family database (http://pfam.xfam.org/), KEGG pathway database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/), and the InterPro integrated protein classification system (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/).
Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) were annotated as follows: tRNA sequences were identified based on their characteristic secondary structure using tRNAscan-SE (http://lowelab.ucsc.edu/tRNAscan-SE/). rRNA sequences were located by using BLAST to search against conserved rRNA sequences from related species. miRNA and snRNA were predicted using the covariance models from the Rfam database via the integrated INFERNAL software (http://infernal.janelia.org/).

## LTR analysis
The identification of intact LTR-RTs was performed using LTRharvest and LTRfinder [ ] with parameters "-L 5000 -M 0.3" to identify LTR sequences. The LTRfinder software, with parameters "-motif tgca -motifmis 1 -maxlenltr 5000 -maxdistltr 20,000 -maxtsd 6", was used to identify LTRs across the genome. LTR-RTs were annotated for essential protein domains (ENV, RNaseH, RT, INT, AP, and GAG) using models derived from the GyDB database. Family Clustering and Filtering were performed using BLAST and SILIX software [ – ]. The insertion time was estimated based on nucleotide divergence and using the formula T = K/2r, where r is the mutation rate [ ]. A phylogenetic tree was constructed to understand the evolutionary relationships between LTR-RTs across species using MUSCLE [ ] and a maximum likelihood method.

## LTR-RT composition and evolutionary relationship analysis using subphaser
To systematically analyze the composition and evolutionary dynamics of LTR retrotransposons (LTR-RTs) across different Acer species, we employed a repetitive kmer-based comparative approach using the Subphaser software. Subphaser is a powerful tool designed for comparative LTR-RT analysis, capable of detecting both genome-specific LTR-RT families and divergence signals among them. To adapt the multi-species genomic data for Subphaser's input requirements, we adopted a novel strategy: the individual genome assemblies from the different Acer species were concatenated and treated as distinct subgenomes within a single input file. This allowed Subphaser to conduct a comparative analysis, effectively identifying and differentiating LTR-RT activity and composition unique to each Acer species within the group. This method facilitates the subsequent exploration of the evolutionary relationships and differential LTR-RT proliferation events across the genus.

## Gene family clustering analysis of Acer species
To analyze the distribution and species-specificity of gene families, we employed the Orthofinder software [ ] for gene family clustering. Gene families were categorized into single-copy and multi-copy families based on sequence similarity. Species-specific gene families were identified as those that appeared only in one sample or subset of samples. The clustering process involved filtering genes with multiple transcript isoforms, keeping only the longest coding sequence, and excluding genes shorter than 50 amino acids or those with premature stop codons. An all-vs-all BLASTp comparison was performed to assess the similarity between protein sequences of different species, using an E-value cutoff of 1e-5. Orthogroups were identified based on the sequence similarity, and gene families were clustered using the Orthofinder software.

## Identification of genomic variants across Acer genome assemblies
A reference genome for A. palmatum was used to anchor the pangenome construction process. The selection of A. palmatum as the reference genome was based on its representative status within the Aceraceae family, serving as a key species for studying genetic diversity and adaptation in temperate climates. This choice ensures that the pangenome provides a comprehensive representation of core genomic features and variation across Acer species.
To obtain a more accurate set of SNPs and small InDels, two complementary methods were employed: genome-to-genome comparisons and short-read alignment to the reference genome. Short-read sequencing data were aligned to the reference genome using BWA software [ ], and variant calling was performed with SAMtools [ ]. In addition to the short-read alignment approach, a genome-to-genome comparison was conducted using the MUMmer tool [ ], with the Nucmer algorithm [ ] used to align the genomes of different individuals. Dnadiff was then applied to detect SNPs between these genomes, a method particularly effective for identifying conserved and divergent regions. PAVs and CNVs were detected using both genome-to-genome comparisons and read-to-reference alignment. SVs were identified using MUMmer [ ], Assemblytics [ ], and SyRI software [ ], while large structural variations, including PAVs, were detected using Breakdancer [ ] and BWA alignment methods. Structural variants were classified into core, dispensable, and private SVs based on their presence across the samples. This comprehensive approach ensured an accurate and detailed identification of genetic variations in the pangenome.

## Genome sequencing of 134 A. palmatum lines
A total of 134 A. palmatum lines were selected for genome resequencing. DNA was extracted from leaf tissues using a standard CTAB-based method. Library preparation was performed using the Illumina TruSeq DNA PCR-Free library kit to ensure high-quality, adapter-ligated fragments suitable for sequencing. The prepared libraries were then subjected to paired-end sequencing using the Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform, producing high-coverage (average 30x) reads. Resequencing reads from the core collection of these 134 A. palmatum lines were aligned to the A. palmatum reference genome using BWA with default settings [ ]. Alignment sorting and duplicate marking were performed using SAMtools [ ]. SNPs and small InDels were called using GATK (v3.2–2) and subjected to further filtering. Variants were excluded based on the following criteria: SNPs with QD < 2.0, MQ < 40.0, FS > 60.0, SOR > 3.0, MQRankSum <  − 12.5, ReadPosRankSum <  − 8.0, or GQ < 20.0. The same filtering parameters were applied to small InDels, with FS and SOR thresholds adjusted to 200.0 and 10.0, respectively.

## Graph-based pangenome construction and SV genotyping in the population
The reference genome for A. palmatum and its associated SVs, including insertions, deletions, and inversions, were incorporated into a variant graph using the construct subcommand of vg (v1.23.0), ensuring that all alternate alleles were preserved. The initial graph was then simplified using vg prune, and indexes in XG and GCSA formats were created with vg index, enabling the '-L' parameter. Paired-end reads from the 134 A. palmatum lines were subsequently mapped to this graph-based genome, generating alignments in GAM format. Alignments with mapping or base quality below 5 were filtered out. A compressed coverage index was calculated using vg pack, and snarls were generated with vg snarls, both using default settings. Structural variant genotyping was performed using vg call with the '-v' parameter, providing a detailed overview of SVs across the population. Gene Ontology (GO) [ ] and KEGG pathway analyses [ ] were employed to identify biological processes and metabolic pathways associated with core and dispensable genes.

## Linkage disequilibrium and selection sweep analysis
Linkage disequilibrium (LD) and selection sweep analyses were performed to identify regions under positive selection using Fst and Pi values. Candidate genes associated with adaptation were identified through these analyses, and functional enrichment of these genes was performed using GO and KEGG tools.

## Population genetic structure analysis
The phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbor-joining method, which was implemented in TreeBest software (v1.9.2) (http://treesoft.sourceforge.net/treebest.shtml). The SNPs from the population were used to calculate the distance matrix, and the TreeBest tool was applied to generate the evolutionary tree. For the PCA, we used genetic data to cluster individuals based on their SNP variation, identifying subgroups that could represent distinct evolutionary lineages within the population. PCA was performed with GCTA (v1.24.2) software [ ], and the resulting distribution of individuals was visualized with R software.

## Genome-wide association study (GWAS) and structural variant-based GWAS (SV-GWAS)
GWAS was conducted using the GEMMA software [ ], which applies mixed linear models (MLM) to correct for population structure and individual relatedness. In addition, SV-based GWAS was performed to examine the association between structural variants (SVs) and phenotypic traits. This approach utilized the identified SVs in the population and assessed their correlation with key traits. The SVs were incorporated into the GWAS framework, accounting for population structure, and the significance of SV-trait associations was determined using mixed linear models.

## Analysis of lipid and oil globules
Seed oil was extracted according to the method of our previous study [ ]. Fatty acid composition was analysed by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC/MS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). Quantification of individual compounds was analysed by the FAME peak area relative to that of the internal standard [ ]. Transmission electron microscopy was performed on a JEOL-1400 scanning electron microscope (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) as described by Chen et al. [ ].

## RNA extraction and quantitative RT–PCR
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions, and cDNA was reverse transcribed and qRT-PCR was performed as described in our previous study [ , ]. Primer sequences are listed in (Additional file : Table S23), and AtACTIN2 was used as an internal reference gene [ ].

## Subcellular localisation of ActKCS
The full-length coding sequences without stop codons of ActKCS was built into the pMDC43 vector and fused with green fluorescent protein (GFP). The resulting plasmids were transferred into GV3101 and subsequently transformed into young Nicotiana benthamiana leaves using CD3-959 (35S-ER-mCherry) as an endoplasmic reticulum (ER) marker and DAPI staining as a nuclear marker [ ]. The image was captured by confocal laser scanning microscopy (Leica, TCS SP8) [ ].

## Transgenic experiments in A. thaliana and B. napus
The full-length coding sequences of ActKCS was built into the pMDC43 vector and transferred into Columbia-O (Col-0) using the Agrobacterium (GV3101)-mediated floral dip method [ ]. And full-length coding sequences of ActKCS was also inserted into the pC2300s vector and transferred into hypocotyls of (g0180747) rapeseed plants (WT) by Wuhan Towin Biotechnology Co., Ltd., using the Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation technique according to the method previously described by Zhou et al. [ ]. The transgenic plants were verified by PCR identification using the specific primers (Additional file : Table S23). T3 generation homozygous transgenic lines were used for phenotypic analysis.

## Yeast one-hybrid assay (Y1H)
The Y1H assays were performed using the Matchmaker Gold Yeast One-Hybrid Library Screening System (Cat. No. 630491, TaKaRa). The ActKCS promoter was inserted into a pHis2 vector and the linearised constructs were transformed into Y1H Gold via PEG/LiAc. The full length region of ActMYB95 was cloned into the pGADT7 vector. Transformed Y1H-Gold containing both pHis2-ActKCS promoter and pGADT7-ActMYB95 were cultured at 30 °C for 3 days to test the interaction on SD/-Trp/-Leu/-His with 3AT. The p53 promoter and pGADT7-Rec (AD-Rec-P53) were used to co-transform Y1H gold as a positive control. As a negative control, empty pGADT7 was transformed with the pHis2-ActKCS promoter in Y1H gold [ ].

## Dual luciferase assay
Experiments were performed as described by Han et al. [ ]. Briefly, the full-length coding region of ActMYB95 was amplified and inserted into the pGreen II 0029 62-SK effect vector. The ActKCS promoter was inserted into the LUC vector (pCAMBIA1381Z-LUC). Both constructs were individually transferred into Agrobacterium strain GV3101 by the freeze–thaw method. Tobacco leaves were infected by infiltration with Agrobacterium mixtures of the ActMYB95 and ActKCS promoters and P19 (4:1:1, v/v/v).
