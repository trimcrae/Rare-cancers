Approximately half of MPNSTs are associated with neurofibromatosis type I (NF1) (Gutmann et al. ), which is a complex multi-system human disorder caused by mutations in neurofibromin. About 8% to 13% of NF1 patients will be diagnosed as MPNST in their lifetime.
MPNST is generally characterized by fascicles of spindle-shaped cells, with alternating hypercellular and hypocellular areas (Rodriguez et al. ). In about 15% of MPNSTs, epithelioid or heterologous differentiation can be observed, leading to the difficulty of differential diagnosis, especially for the heterologous differentiation-predominant cases (Guo et al. ). The rapid development of molecular testing has led to the discovery of characteristic genomic aberrations in many sarcoma types. In addition to the gene aberrations suggested by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines for the diagnosis of MPNST, including alterations in NF1, CDKN2A, EED, or SUZ12, practical experience has uncovered a range of clinically significant mutations that can further aid in the diagnostic and serve as potential biomarkers for targeted therapies.
Surgical resection remains the standard of care for early-stage MPNST, which is often hindered by the large size of tumors and high positive rate of resection margins. For unresectable tumors, doxorubicin- or ifosfamide-based cytotoxic chemotherapy is the standard treatment. Unfortunately, the overall survival of MPNST patients undergoing chemotherapy is typically less than one year (Kroep et al. ). Therefore, it is necessary to thoroughly characterize the molecular landscape of MPNST in order to identify opportunities for targeted therapies and immunotherapy.
Previous genomic studies on MPNST have evaluated actionable genomic alterations (Brohl et al. ; Hirbe et al. ). In addition, the processes involved in the differential diagnosis of MPNST based on pathological assessments and molecular testing have been well documented (Gounder et al. ). However, the molecular landscape of MPNST in Chinese patients remains unexplored. Further research on the molecular mechanisms underlying MPNST will also aid in the development of targeted therapies.
In this study, targeted sequencing of cancer-related genes was conducted to identify the molecular features of a Chinese cohort with an initial diagnosis of MPNST. The differential diagnosis of MPNST was established through an evaluation of pathological features, supplemented by immunohistochemical (IHC), fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and next-generation sequencing (NGS) analyses. Actionable genomic alterations related to targeted therapy and immunotherapy as well as prognostic factors for MPNST were evaluated.

## Materials and methods

## Patients
A total of 20 patients with an initial diagnosis of MPNST were enrolled at Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital from October 2018 to October 2021. Clinicopathological and follow-up data were obtained from electronic medical records. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (approval number: KY-Q-2022-135-01). All the patients signed an informed consent form. MPNST was diagnosed according to the World Health Organization Classification System (2017).

## Immunohistochemical staining
The following antibodies were used for the IHC analysis: MUC4 (8G7, GeneTech, Shanghai, China), HMB45 (HMB45, GeneTech), MITF (D5, GeneTech), S100 (polyclone, GeneTech), Ki-67 (MIB-1, GeneTech) and SOX10 (EP268, ZSGB-Biotech, Beijing, China). IHC was performed on BenchMark ULTRA (Roche Diagnositics, Rotkreuz, Switzerland) and Bond-III (Leica Biosystems, Buffalo Grove, IL).

## Next-generation sequencing
Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor tissues and matched paracancerous tissue were collected. DNA and RNA of each specimen were extracted using QIAamp DNA/RNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Mancheester, UK), and reverse transcription of RNA was performed. The samples were analyzed by using a hybrid capture-based YuansuS® gene panel (OrigiMed, Shanghai, China). The panel covers all coding exons and selected introns of 706 cancer-related genes, including genes rearranged frequently in solid tumors. Post-capture libraries were subsequently sequenced with a mean depth of 800 × on NovaSeq 6000 sequencers (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Experiments were performed at Shanghai OrigiMed Co., Ltd., a laboratory certified by the College of American Pathologists and Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments. Single nucleotide variants and insertion-deletions were identified using MuTect (v1.7) and PINDEL (v0.2.5), and annotated using SnpEff3.0 (Cao et al. ). Copy number amplifications and homozygous deletions were detected using Control-FREEC (v9.7), with a window size of 50,000 and a step size of 10,000 (Cao et al. ). Gene rearrangements were visualized and assessed in the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV). Gene fusions based on RNA-sequencing were identified using STAR-fusion (v1.4) (Haas et al. ). Tumor mutation burden (TMB) was determined by counting nonsynonymous somatic single nucleotide variants and insertion-deletions per megabase (Mb) of the sequence examined. TMB high was defined as ≥ 10 mutations/Mb and TMB low was defined as < 10 mutations/Mb. Tumors showing instability at more than 15% of selected microsatellite loci were classified as microsatellite instability (MSI) -high. Tumors showing instability at less than or equal to 15% of selected microsatellite loci were classified as microsatellite-stable.

## Analysis of genomic alterations in the Chinese and MSK MPNST cohorts
For the comparison of genomic alterations between the Chinese and Memorial Sloan Kettering-Integrated Mutation Profiling of Actionable Cancer Targets (MSK-IMPACT) MPNST cohorts, only genes shared by both the YuansuS and the panel used in MSK-IMPACT (Cheng et al. ) were included in the analysis. Also, genes with a mutation rate of less than 10% were excluded. MSK dataset used for comparison is available on the cBioPortal (Cerami et al. ; Gao et al. ) website (https://www.cbioportal.org/study/summary?id=sarcoma_mskcc_2022).

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis was performed using R package (version 4.1.0, https://www.R-project.org). Fisher’s exact test was used for the association analysis of categorical variables. Student t-test and Wilcoxon rank sum test were used for the association analysis of normally distributed data and non-normally distributed data, respectively. Disease-free survival (DFS) was defined as the time from study entry until disease progression or death from any cause. Kaplan–Meier curve was used to analyze survival and log rank test was used to compare the difference in survival between subgroups. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

## Results

## The clinical characteristics of patients with an initial diagnosis of MPNST
The clinical characteristics of patients with an initial diagnosis of MPNST were provided in Table  . The cohort included thirteen males and seven females with a median age of onset of 39 years. In regard to the anatomical site of tumor, eight cases (40%) were located in the extremities as well as in the trunk, and four cases (20%) were located in the head and neck. Seventeen MPNST patients underwent surgical resection, of which eight patients received adjuvant therapy including chemotherapy (5 cases), radiotherapy (2 cases) and chemotherapy plus radiotherapy (1 case). 
The diagnosis of MPNST mainly relies on histopathological assessments. For the majority of patients with an initial diagnosis of MPNST, fascicles of spindle-shaped tumor cells, geographical necrosis and conspicuous mitotic activity were observed (Fig.  A, B, C). Neoplastic cells exhibited immunoreactivity for S100 (75%, 15/20) and SOX10 (70%, 14/20) (Fig.  E, F). S100 and SOX10 staining were both negative in three patients. 

## Diagnostic refinement based on NGS and additional IHC results
According to the NCCN guideline for soft tissue sarcoma, NF1, CDKN2A and EED or SUZ12 are recurrently mutated genes in MPNST. These tumor-specific genes were altered in 60% (12/20) of patients with an initial diagnosis of MPNST (Supplementary Table 1). The pathological assessment of one case indicated spindle-shaped tumor cells with obvious cellular atypia and mitotic figures. No evidence of Schwann cell differentiation was observed. S100 and SOX10 expression was negative. Hence, a diagnosis of spindle cell sarcoma was preferred. Interestingly, NGS for this tumor revealed EED gene amplification, SUZ12-HSPA12 A gene rearrangement, NF1 gene deletion and truncation, and CDKN2A gene deletion. Combining the pathological and molecular results, the patient was finally diagnosed as MPNST.
Among the 20 enrolled patients, 3 were detected with recurrent genomic alterations related to other sarcoma subtypes. To clarify the diagnosis of these patients, pathological results were re-evaluated and additional IHC staining was conducted (Supplementary Fig. ). The clinicopathological and molecular results of the three patients were summarized in Supplementary Table 2. Case 1: a 21-year-old female presented with a cervical vertebra mass. Initially diagnosed as cellular schwannoma or low-grade MPNST. NGS detected an EWSR1-CREB3L1 fusion, and subsequent MUC4 staining confirmed a diagnosis of low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma. Case 2: a 54-year-old female had a tumor mass in her right elbow. The initial diagnosis was MPNST, but it was revised to ZC3H7B-BCOR fusion sarcoma after identifying the genomic alteration. Case 3 is a 24-year-old male with a lumbar spine mass. Initially diagnosed as MPNST, the final diagnosis was revised to clear cell sarcoma after detecting EWSR1-ATF1 fusion and MITF and HMB45 expression.

## Genomic alterations of MPNST
Somatic mutation profiles of 17 MPNST patients were evaluated (Fig.  ). The most frequently mutated genes were CDKN2A (35.3%, 6/17), TP53 (35.3%, 6/17), NF1 (29.4%, 5/17) and CDKN2B (23.5% 4/17). In regard to recurrent genomic alterations of MPNST, SUZ12 was mutated in two (11.8%, 2/17) patients, and EED mutation was detected in one (5.9%, 1/17) patient. 
Pathway enrichment of somatic mutations was illustrated in Fig.  A. The highest frequency of somatic mutations enriched in the receptor tyrosine kinases (RTK)/RAS pathway (64.7%), followed by the cell cycle (52.9%) and TP53 (41.2%) pathways. Mutated genes in the RTK/RAS pathway were provided in Fig.  B. FGFR1 (17.6%) and RET (11.8%) were the top mutated oncogenes, and NF1 was the only mutated tumor suppressor gene. Genomic alterations in the DNA damage response (17.6%) and homologous recombinational repair (5.9%) pathways were also observed. Co-mutation events are considered as a key element in cancers. In the 17 MPNST patients, 33 co-mutation types were detected (Fig.  C). The difference in mutation frequency was observed between the Chinese cohort and the MSK MPNST cohort (Fig.  D). The mutation frequencies of MPNST-specific genes, including NF1 (36.0% vs. 29.4%), CDKN2A (48.0% vs. 35.3%), SUZ12 (24.0% vs. 11.8%) and EED (16.0% vs. 5.9%), were higher in the MSK MPNST cohort. However, no mutation occurred in a series of genes, such as CALR, HMGA2, MTAP, NAB2, NOTCH1, PTCH1, RET, RICTOR and STAT6, within this cohort. 

## NF1 mutations in MPNST
A total of 15 NF1 mutations, including 5 germline mutations and 10 somatic mutations, occurred in 65% (11/17) of MPNST patients (Fig.  A). Among the patients with NF1 driven mutations, 77.8% (7/9) exhibited typical histopathological features associated with NF1. On the other hand, 100% (2/2) of patients with NF1 VUS mutations and 33.3% (2/6) of the patients without NF1 mutations showed clinical evidence of with typical NF1 histopathological features. The identified mutations included nine short variants, two copy number alterations, one long-indel and three gene fusions/rearrangements. All the three gene fusions/rearrangements were simultaneously detected in one patient, including a NF1-MYO1D gene fusion, a NF1-intergenic gene rearrangement and a MYO1D-NF1 gene rearrangement (Fig.  B, Supplementary Fig. ). The NF1-MYO1D, defined as a loss-of-function fusion (Papp et al. ), was composed of NF1 exon 1 and MYO1D exons 15 to 22. The NF1-intergenic rearrangement reserving exon 1 to 24 of NF1 may also disrupt the gene function. NF1 expression was negative by IHC in this patient, which was consistent with the NGS results. 
Patients were divided into groups with and without NF1 mutation, and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) pathway enrichment analysis of mutated genes was performed (Fig.  C, D). Both groups showed mutated gene enrichment in pathways involving cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis and metastasis, such as the PI3K-Akt and MAPK signaling pathways. Furthermore, in the NF1-mutated group, mutated genes were particularly enriched in DNA repair-related and immune-related pathways, such as the Fanconi anemia and T cell receptor signaling pathways. On the other hand, the NF1-wild group exhibited significant alterations in the Hippo signaling pathway and FoxO signaling pathway. Based on GO (Gene Ontology) analysis, mutated genes in the NF1-mutated group were enriched in the regulation of DNA metabolic processes and chromatin DNA binding (Supplementary Fig. ). These results demonstrated that genomic instability and immune system dysregulation may play a role in the development of NF1-mutated MPNST.

## Actionable genomic alterations
TMB, MSI and HED (HLA-I evolutionary divergence) scores are predictive biomarkers for immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). The median TMB of the 17 MPNST patients was 2.1 mutations/Mb. One patient was classified as TMB-high (12.6 mutations/Mb). A significantly higher TMB was observed in samples with MUC16 (Wilcoxon, p value = 0.041) and ARHGEF10 (Wilcoxon, p value = 0.028) mutations (Fig.  A, B). All patients were microsatellite-stable. The median HED-score was 5.76, and five patients were identified as HED-high (HED-score > 7.887). 
A total of 34 actionable mutations were detected. Notably, the prevalence of mutations in specific genes was remarkable, with NF1 mutations leading at a frequency of 52.94% (9 out of 17 patients), followed by alterations in CDKN2A observed in 35.29% (6 out of 17 patients). Additionally, mutations in CDKN2B and FGFR1 were each detected in 23.53% of the cohort (4 out of 17 patients), as illustrated in Fig.  D. Targeted therapeutic agents, such as selumetinib for NF1 mutations and ribociclib and ponatinib for CDKN2A/B and FGFR1 mutations, respectively, have been developed, indicating potential future use in MPNST treatment. On the whole, the detection of actionable mutations in a significant 70.6% (12 out of 17) of the patients highlights the considerable opportunity for employing targeted therapies.

## Prognostic factors for MPNST
Follow-up information was available for 70.5% (12/17) of MPNST patients who had underwent radical surgery. The median follow-up duration was 523 days. Amongst patients, 58.33% (7/12) had disease recurrence. Among those 7 patients, four patients were alive with disease at the last follow-up and two patients died from the disease or from other causes. The median DFS was 323 days (range: 28 to 1,430 days). In univariate survival analysis, age, gender, tumor location, and postoperative adjuvant therapy were not correlated with DFS (p value > 0.05, data not shown). In regard to gene mutations, CDKN2A mutations were associated with shorter DFS than CDKN2A wild (median 6.08 months vs 14.3 months, p = 0.0038) (Fig.  C), while no significant association was determined between DFS and NF1 or TP53 mutations.

## Discussion
Classification of sarcoma has mainly depended on histopathology for decades. Recently, several studies have proven that diagnosis solely based on morphological approach can lead to incorrect diagnosis of sarcoma. Because of the misleading diagnosis in up to 23% of patients, Italiano et al. ( ) recommended to perform molecular testing for any suspicious mesenchymal tumors harboring molecular abnormality.
In the NCCN guideline for soft tissue sarcoma, molecular testing of characteristic genomic aberrations has been suggested as a useful ancillary testing approach to support the morphological diagnosis of sarcoma. However, conventional molecular tests, such as fluorescence in situ hybridization, can only detect a limited number of known genomic alterations at a time, which is not ideal for clinical practice. In contrast, NGS is capable of targeting hundreds to thousands of genes. Simultaneous sequencing of DNA and RNA can additionally improve the detection performance of gene fusions. Therefore, NGS is the optimal choice for precise molecular diagnosis of soft tissue sarcoma, including MPNST.
NF1, CDKN2A and EED or SUZ12 are recurrently mutated genes in MPNSTs. In this study, a patient initially diagnosed with fusocellular sarcoma was later rediagnosed with MPNST, harboring genomic alterations in these genes. Based on NGS and IHC results, three patients with an initial diagnosis of MPNST were reclassified as low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma, clear cell sarcoma and ZC3H7B-BCOR fusion sarcoma, respectively. Although MPNST and other types of sarcomas are all types of soft tissue sarcoma, but they have different treatment approaches.
MPNST is more aggressive and may require more extensive surgery. Intensive doses of radiation therapy and chemotherapy can be used, especially for high-risk or metastatic disease. In contrast, low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma has a generally indolent pattern of clinical behaviour is not considered to be particularly radiosensitive. Its response rate to first-line chemotherapy was 0% (Chamberlain et al. ). Similarly, clear cell sarcoma displays poor chemosensitivity as well with the response rate of 4% to the conventional anthracycline-based chemotherapy and a response rate of 20% to gemcitabine-based therapy (Cojocaru et al. ; Jones et al. ; Wetterwald et al. ). Chemotherapy is not routinely used in the treatment of sarcomas that are generally considered less responsive to it. For ZC3H7B-BCOR fusion sarcoma, there is limited information on the efficacy of chemotherapy. Currently, BCOR rearranged sarcomas have been shown to respond well to doxorubicin-based chemotherapy, resulting in long-term survival outcomes. But it still requires specific therapeutic approaches (Kyriazoglou and Bagos ; Whittle et al. ). Therefore, accurate diagnosis and genetic testing are crucial for determining the most effective treatment approach.
The comparative analysis of mutation frequencies between the Chinese cohort and the MSK MPNST cohort highlighted both disparities and similarities. Consistent with previous studies, mutations in NF1, SUZ12, EED, TP53, and CDKN2A have been established as significant contributors to the pathogenesis of MPNST with additional mutations further activating the Ras pathway (Brohl et al. ). However, the study uncovered differences in mutation prevalence. Specifically, the Chinese cohort exhibited lower mutation frequencies in several MPNST-specific genes but a higher incidence of TP53 mutations. Moreover, the study of the RTK/RAS pathway in MPNST development reveals unique variants specific to the Chinese population, including the notable absence of RET and FGFR1 mutations, contrasting with the genetic findings in the MSK MPNST cohort. The findings point to population-specific molecular signatures in the development of MPNST, underscoring the urgent need for precise and individualized molecular profiling to accommodate the natural diversity observed. Consequently, it is critical to pursue in-depth research to decode the functional implications of these genetic variances and determine their impact on the clinical outcomes and treatment efficacy in MPNST patients from a spectrum of populations.
In this study, we also investigated the histopathological features of MPNST patients with and without NF1 mutations. Our findings indicate that patients with NF1 mutations generally exhibit more typical histopathological features associated with NF1. However, it is worth noting that a subset of patients without NF1 mutations (33.3%, 2/6) also displayed clinical evidence of MPNST with typical histopathological features. This finding suggests that there may be other genetic or molecular factors contributing to the development of MPNST in these patients.
In addition to the histopathological differences observed between MPNST patients with and without NF1 mutations, it is important to consider the impact of these mutations on signaling pathways. Interestingly, our findings indicate that both groups showed mutated gene enrichment in PI3K-Akt and MAPK signaling pathways. This observation is consistent with previous studies that have highlighted the crucial role of the PI3K-Akt and MAPK signaling pathway and MAPK signaling pathway in NF1-related tumorigenesis (Gregorian et al. ). Interestingly, MPNST patients without NF1 mutations also showed significant alterations in Hippo signaling pathway and FoxO signaling pathway. These results imply that mechanisms not reliant on NF1 could play a role in MPNST emergence within this subset of patients. Additionally, they underscore the intricacy and diversity of MPNST, reinforcing the necessity for more comprehensive studies to clarify how these signaling pathways distinctly influence the evolution and advancement of MPNST across varied patient demographics. Understanding these molecular mechanisms could potentially lead to the identification of novel therapeutic targets for MPNST patients, particularly those without NF1 mutations.
In the analysis of immunotherapy-related biomarkers, TMB, MSI and HED-scores were evaluated. Amongst the 17 MPNST patients, one patient with TMB-high and five patients with high HED-score were considered as potential responders to immunotherapy. Mutations in MUC16 and ARHGEF10 were significantly associated with high TMB value.
MUC16 is one of the recurrently mutated genes in gastric cancer. In a study involving the TCGA and Asian gastric cancer cohorts, MUC16 mutations were associated with higher TMB and higher response rates to immunotherapy (Kim et al. ). In another study across 30 solid tumor types, patients with MUC16 mutations exhibited higher TMB, as well as higher neoantigen load (Zhang et al. ). In the NSCLC and melanoma cohorts, MUC16 mutations were enriched in patients with complete response or partial response, suggesting that MUC16 is a potential biomarker for ICI-based immunotherapy. Unfortunately, the only TMB-high patient in our study was lost to follow-up.
ARHGEF10 encodes a Rho guanine nucleotide exchange factor and is a putative tumor suppressor in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. The loss of ARHGEF10 expression enhanced tumor growth in mouse models, and was associated with enhanced proliferation, invasiveness and in vitro motility of cell lines (Joseph et al. ). At present, evidence on the relationship between ARHGEF10 and TMB is still lacking, and further exploration is required.
In our study, NF1, CDKN2A, CDKN2B and FGFR1 were the most commonly mutated actionable genes in MPNST. Selumetinib is used for patients with NF1 mutations. Patients with CDKN2A/2B mutations may benefit from abemaciclib, palbociclib and ribociclib. At present, pemigatinib, erdafitinib and infigratinib are three pan-FGFR inhibitors approved by the FDA.
Selumetinib is the first FDA-approved targeted agent for NF1 (Casey et al. ), which can negatively regulate the activity of MEK1/2 and inhibit the over-activated RAS pathway in NF1 patients, resulting in a tumor suppressor effect. In a phase II clinical trial, the majority (56%) of NF1 patients receiving selumetinib had durable responses, with a 3-year progression-free survival (PFS) rate of 84%. Approximately half of MPNST patients arise from NF1 and their RAS pathway is also abnormally activated (Gross et al. ). Therefore, such patients may benefit from selumetinib. In our study, 64.7% of MPNSTs had pathogenic variants in the RTK/RAS pathway. In addition, a clinical trial investigating selumetinib for treating MPNST (NCT03433183) is underway.
APG-115 is a novel inhibitor that targets mousedouble minute 2 (MDM2)/p53 and can restore the tumor suppressive effect of p53 by blocking the interaction between MDM2 and p53. Updated results of a phase II clinical study (NCT03611868) of APG-115 plus pembrolizumab were disclosed in the ASCO 2022 annual meeting (Abstract: 9517). A total of 150 adult patients across multiple solid tumors with wild-type TP53 or MDM2 amplifications were enrolled. APG-115 showed encouraging therapeutic effects for MPNST, with a disease control rate of 50% (6/12). In our study, one patient had MDM2 amplification and would likely benefit from APG-115. Additionally, we conducted FISH analysis on samples obtained from this patient to confirm the presence of MDM2 amplification (Supplementary Fig. A).
Genes associated with cell cycle regulation, such as TP53 and CDK4, have been recognized as independent biomarkers predicting for poor survival in MPNST (Høland et al. ; Yu et al. ). We determined that CDKN2A, involving in the cell cycle regulation as well, was significantly associated with DFS of MPNST. CDKN2A is one of the most common recurrently mutated genes in MPNST, and inactivation of this gene generally contributes to the progression of NF1 to MPNST (Chaney et al. ). The prognostic value of CDKN2A is not clear for MPNST. Martin et al. ( ) speculated that CDKN2A and its downstream targets in the cell cycle pathway may be of clinical significance, just like TP53 (Martin et al. ). However, CDKN2A has proved to be associated with poor survival in other cancer types, such as T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, osteosarcoma and oral squamous cell carcinoma (Padhi et al. ; Wang et al. ; Zhang et al. ).

## Conclusion
Based on our research, we have found that recurrent genomic aberrations, such as gene rearrangements and fusions, can effectively assist in the differential diagnosis of MPNST. Accurate diagnosis and genetic testing are crucial for determining the optimal therapeutic options for different types of sarcomas, as treatment may vary based on the definitive diagnosis. Our study further elucidated the molecular landscape of 17 Chinese MPNST patients, unveiling the potential need for diverse precision treatment approaches due to the existence of molecular heterogeneity. This finding suggests that targeted therapy and immunotherapy may offer significant benefits to most MPNST patients. Therefore, molecular testing may aid in identifying suitable candidates for clinical trials, thereby improving their chances of successful treatment. In conclusion, our findings underscore the importance of utilizing molecular testing to determine the best approach for each individual case.
